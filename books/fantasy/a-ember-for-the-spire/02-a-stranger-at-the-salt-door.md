## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

The salt door had a name that was not its name.

Officially it was the Provision Entrance of the Royal Kitchens at Vellmarre Palace, a low archway in the palace's eastern flank through which flour and fish and the king's notoriously expensive saffron entered the building. Unofficially, among the kitchen staff who knew such things, it was called the salt door, because the tax on salt was high and the honesty of certain merchants was low, and because a man could walk through it carrying two hundred pounds of "salt" without anyone weighing so much as a spoonful.

Edmund Marchetti knew the salt door better than he knew his own face. He had stood beside it every morning for nine years, at the hour when the kitchen still smelled of last night's ashes and tomorrow's bread, waiting for the carts, checking the seals, tasting — discreetly, professionally, with a tongue that had been trained until it could distinguish between two vintages of arsenic — whatever seemed worth tasting.

It was, by any reasonable measure, a terrible job. It was also the only thing Edmund was good at, and he had made his peace with that the way one makes peace with a limp: thoroughly, quietly, and without ever quite looking at it directly.

On the morning the courier came, the fog off the river was thick enough to chew, and Cook Brenna was already shouting at someone about eels.

"—and if those eels are soft again I'll have the fishmonger's head on a platter, and I mean that *culinarily*, Tam, don't give me that look—"

Edmund stood in his usual spot, half in the shadow of the archway, hands folded, expression arranged into the particular blankness that had kept him alive and unremarked for nearly a decade. He had refined this expression over years of careful practice. It said nothing, promised nothing, invited nothing. It was the face of a man you would forget before you finished describing him. He considered it his masterwork, more laborious than anything he'd ever written or drawn, though of course he'd never written or drawn anything, because men like Edmund did not leave marks.

The cart drivers came and went. The salt came in. The eels, mercifully firm, went up to the kitchens. The fog sat on everything like an old dog too tired to move.

And then, out of the grey, a man fell against the door.

Not walked. Fell — sideways, shoulder-first into the stone frame with a sound like a dropped sack of turnips, and then slid down it in a way that no living man slides unless he has stopped caring how he lands. Edmund caught him mostly by reflex, the way one catches a falling pot, and immediately regretted it, because the man's doublet was soaked through with something warm and sticky, and there is only one thing that soaks a doublet that way at six in the morning.

"Sir," Edmund said, in the tone he used for merchants trying to pass off rancid butter, "you cannot be here."

The man laughed. It was a bad laugh, wet at the edges. "No," he agreed. "I really can't."

Up close, the courier — and he was a courier, Edmund could see it now, the road-worn boots, the lean hard frame of a man who made his living outrunning other men's tempers — was perhaps forty, grey-stubbled, with a face like a map of somewhere mountainous. There was an arrow wound in his side that someone had bound with a strip of shirt and a great deal of optimism. The binding had lost the argument some hours ago.

"You're the taster," the man said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody," Edmund said automatically. It was practically his motto. He had considered having it embroidered.

"You're Marchetti." The man's eyes were doing something alarming, flickering toward the fog, then back. Listening for something behind them. "They told me — the face, they said, remember the face, it's the only safe description — *forgettable*. Gods, they were right."

"I'm going to call for help," Edmund said, "if you'll just—"

"If you call for help, I'm dead, and then so are you, probably, and definitely a lot of other people." The courier gripped Edmund's wrist with a strength that surprised them both. Blood loss, Edmund noted distantly, the professional part of his mind already cataloguing symptoms, pallor, pulse — thready, fast — while the rest of him stood frozen like a man who has just realized the floor is a painting. "Listen to me. Listen. I don't have long, and I've ridden four days to say this, so you'll bloody well listen."

Somewhere out in the fog, a whistle sounded. Two notes. Then, after a pause, two more.

The courier heard it too. His face did something complicated — fear, mostly, but underneath it a kind of grim satisfaction, the look of a man whose arithmetic has finally come due.

"Salt cellar," he gasped. "Third crate from the left. Under the false bottom."

"That's my—"

"I know which crates are yours. I've been watching this door for three days." Another wet laugh. "You taste the king's food. You're careful. You're *invisible*. And you've got a smuggler's hidey-hole ten feet from where I'm bleeding, which makes you either the bravest coward or the cowardliest brave man in Vellmarre, and either way you're what I've got."

Edmund wanted very much to point out that he was not, in fact, what the man had got, that he belonged to no one, that being chosen was not something that happened to Edmund Marchetti, that in nine years the most anyone had ever selected him for was the sampling of a suspicious blancmange. But the courier's hand was already moving, fumbling inside his coat, and what came out was a scroll case wrapped in oilcloth, sealed at both ends with wax stamped by a seal Edmund did not recognize — a tower, and within the tower, impossibly, a small flame.

"Take it," the courier said.

"No."

"*Take it.*"

"I don't take things from dying strangers in fog, it's—" Edmund searched frantically for the rule he was violating and found, to his horror, that there wasn't one. Nine years of rules, and none of them covered this. "There's no protocol."

"The protocol," the courier said, and now his voice was going thin, stretched over the pain like cloth over a wound, "is that you keep it dry, keep it hidden, and keep yourself alive long enough to read it. That's all. That's the whole job. Easier than yours, I'd wager. No henbane involved."

Edmund went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the fog.

"How do you know about—"

"Everyone in certain circles knows about the taster who's never died." The whistles again, closer now, and beneath them the muffled rhythm of hooves. The courier's grip tightened. "Marchetti. Please. My daughter thinks I'm a grain merchant. If this ends in a ditch outside the city, fine, that's the trade, I knew the price when I took the road. But if it ends in a ditch and the map burns with me—" He coughed, and the cough was ugly. "Then it was all for nothing. Twenty years. A whole kingdom, for nothing."

"A kingdom," Edmund repeated stupidly.

"Vessany," the courier whispered.

And the word went through Edmund like a knife through warm butter — smooth, effortless, devastating. He did not know why. He had never heard it before. He was almost sure he had never heard it before. And yet some locked room deep in him, a room he had not known was there, rattled on its hinges at the sound, the way a dog stirs at a name called from another street.

He took the map.

He did not decide to take it. His hand simply closed around the oilcloth, and the courier exhaled — a whole body's worth of exhale, twenty years' worth, perhaps — and sagged against the doorframe with something that was almost a smile.

"There," he said. "Knew you would. You've got an honest face under all that nothing."

"I haven't got a face at all," Edmund said. "That's rather the point of me."

"Ha." The courier's eyes were closing. "Sera would've liked you. Terrible. She likes terrible people."

"Sera?"

"My daughter." His voice was drifting now, going somewhere Edmund couldn't follow. "Sharp as broken glass, that girl. Tell her—"

The whistles sounded a third time, and this time they were answered from the *other* side of the yard, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

Later — much later, when his hands had finally stopped shaking — Edmund would try to reconstruct exactly how he had done what he did next, and he would fail, because it happened the way panic happens: all at once, in pieces, guided by some instinct deeper than thought.

He dragged the courier through the salt door. This alone should have been impossible; the man weighed as much as a full crate and fought him the whole way, not because he was conscious but because dying men are heavy in a way that has nothing to do with weight. Edmund got him past the third crate, past the fifth, into the narrow gap between the salt stores and the wall where the kitchen's oldest and least-visited shadows lived, and laid him down on the cold flagstones in the dark.

"Hush," Edmund whispered, absurdly. As if the man needed instruction.

Boots in the yard. Voices. Edmund shoved the map down the front of his shirt, felt the wax seals press cold against his sternum like two small accusing hearts, and stepped back out into the archway wearing his blankness like armor.

Four men in grey came out of the fog.

They were not palace guard. Edmund knew every livery in the palace, had spent nine years learning liveries the way other men learned songs, and these men wore no colors at all — just grey wool, grey cloaks beaded with fog-damp, and a badge he had never seen before and would spend many sleepless nights thereafter wishing he hadn't seen: a closed eye worked in silver thread.

Grey Riders. Even the kitchen ghosts whispered about Grey Riders, though no one ever said what they rode for. They asked questions, the whispers went. And the answers always fit.

"Well?" said the leader. He had a voice like a well-maintained instrument, pleasant and utterly without warmth. "This entrance serves the provisioning of the royal kitchens. We're looking for a man. Courier, road-worn, wounded. Have you seen him?"

"No, sir," Edmund said.

The leader looked at him. Edmund had been looked at by kings, chancellors, a cardinal, and once by a woman who was quite sure Edmund had poisoned her pheasant (he hadn't; it was simply a bad pheasant). He knew how to be looked at. He let his face do its work — the mild blankness, the faint suggestion of a man slightly bored and slightly dim, the posture of someone whose entire inner life consisted of salt inventory.

"You're the taster," the leader said.

"Yes, sir."

"They say you've never died."

"It's early in my career, sir. I remain hopeful."

Something flickered across the leader's face — not quite amusement, but a cousin of it. "Hopeful," he repeated. "Yes. Well. If a bleeding man comes through this door, taster, you'll send word at once. To the Spire, not the guardhouse. Anyone who helps him will be helping a traitor, and we are currently..." he paused, choosing the word with the care of a man selecting wine, "...*thorough* with traitors."

"Of course, sir."

"And your name?"

"Marchetti, sir. Edmund Marchetti."

"Marchetti." The leader said it twice, tasting it, and Edmund felt the name leave his mouth and become a thing in the world, a thread someone else now held one end of. "A common name."

"Very common, sir. Like salt."

The leader smiled thinly, and for one vertiginous moment Edmund was certain the man could see straight through the blankness, through the shirt, to the oilcloth bundle pressed against his chest — could hear, even, the small frantic drumming of Edmund's heart, which was doing its best impression of a rabbit in a sack. Then the moment passed. The Grey Riders turned back into the fog, their boots ringing on the cobbles, and the fog closed behind them like water.

Edmund stood in the archway for a count of one hundred. Then two hundred. Then, because his mother — whoever she had been; the memory was a smudge, a warmth without details — had apparently taught him thoroughness along with blankness, three hundred.

Then he went back inside.

* * *

The courier was still alive, which surprised Edmund, and conscious, which surprised them both.

"You lied to them," the man rasped, when Edmund knelt beside him in the dark. There was wonder in it, genuine wonder, as if Edmund had performed a minor miracle instead of the single most terrifying thirty seconds of his life. "Nine years in this place and you lied to *them* like it was nothing."

"It wasn't nothing," Edmund said. His voice came out strange. Steady, but strange, like a borrowed coat. "It was the largest thing I've ever done, and I once swallowed a suspect soup in front of the entire court during a coronation."

"What was wrong with the soup?"

"Nothing, as it turned out. The cook had simply used too much nutmeg and was ashamed to admit it." Edmund was rummaging as he talked, pulling the small kit he kept for emergencies — not poison emergencies, the other kind, the kind involving cooks who sliced themselves instead of the onions. Clean linen. A needle. Spirits. "Hold still. This is going to hurt."

"Everything today has hurt. Consistency is comforting."

Edmund cleaned the wound as best he could in the dark, and bound it properly this time, with actual bandages and actual pressure, and the courier bore it with the gritted-teeth patience of a man who had clearly done this sort of field surgery on himself before, badly, in worse places.

"Why," Edmund said finally, tying off the last knot, "did you come to *me*?"

"Because you're the heir."

The words dropped into the dark between them like a coin down a well. Edmund waited for the splash. It didn't come. The sentence just kept falling.

"I think," Edmund said carefully, "that you have lost rather a lot of blood."

"I think I know exactly how much blood I've lost, I've been keeping count since the ford." The courier struggled up onto one elbow, and his eyes in the darkness were fierce and fever-bright. "The map, Marchetti. When your hands stop shaking, open the map. Touch the seal. It knows. It's been waiting twenty years for blood that knows it back." He slumped again, breathing hard. "Vessany isn't gone. Erased, yes. Burned out of every book, struck from every treaty, the name itself forbidden on pain of — well. On pain. But a kingdom written out of the world leaves ink behind. And ink remembers."

"I'm a food taster," Edmund said. "My father was a clerk. My mother—" he reached for the memory and found, as always, the smudge, the warmth without a shape, "—my mother was a woman. That's the whole of my lineage. I've checked."

"Have you?" The courier smiled, and it was a terrible, tender smile. "Or have you just never been given a reason to check harder? Nobody looks for a door when they've been told all their life the house doesn't exist."

Out in the kitchens, Cook Brenna had begun her morning aria — something about onions, and betrayal, and Tam. The ordinary sounds of the ordinary world, going on ten feet away, oblivious. Edmund sat back on his heels in the dark with a dying stranger's blood drying on his hands and a forbidden kingdom pressed against his chest, and he became aware that he was, for the first time in nine years, feeling something that was not careful.

It was terror. Of course it was terror. Terror was the sensible response, the correct response, the response of a man whose entire survival strategy rested on being no one, wanting nothing, and standing precisely where he was told to stand. Every instinct he had honed was screaming at him to put the map back in the third crate, wash his hands, and go stand in his archway until the world resumed its shape.

But underneath the terror — unwelcome, unreasonable, thrilling as stolen wine — was something else.

Someone had ridden four days through fog and arrows to find *him*. Not the taster. Not the blank-faced functionary. Him. Had watched this door for three days. Had known his name, his craft, his face — had described him, to whoever *they* were, as the one man worth finding. Edmund Marchetti, who had spent nine years perfecting the art of being overlooked, had been sought. Chosen. Trusted with something that mattered enough to bleed for.

It was the worst possible development. It was magnificent.

"You need a physician," Edmund said. "A real one. And then you need to be somewhere far from here, because those men will come back, and they do not strike me as the forgetting sort."

"Physician, yes. Far, no." The courier's breath was shortening. "Nowhere's far anymore. They've had twenty years to grow roots." He caught Edmund's sleeve. "The map. Promise me you'll read it tonight. Alone. And Marchetti — whatever you find in it, however impossible it seems — remember that I chose you. Out of everyone. I chose you."

Nobody had ever said that to Edmund Marchetti. He suspected nobody had ever said it to anyone in this palace, a building whose entire architecture was designed around the principle that people were interchangeable and feelings were a fire hazard.

"I promise," he said, and was startled to find he meant it.

The courier nodded once, satisfied, and let his eyes close. Within minutes he was sleeping — truly sleeping, the deep leaden sleep of the emptied-out — and Edmund sat beside him in the salt-dark, listening to Brenna rage about onions, feeling the map's wax seals cool slowly against his chest until they warmed to the temperature of his own skin.

Which was odd, he thought.

Wax didn't usually do that.

He told himself it was his imagination. He was very good at telling himself things; it was, after all, most of what invisibility required. But all through that long day — the tastings, the ledgers, the suspicious glower of the taster-master, Master Hobb, who noticed everything and forgave nothing — the map lay against his heart like a second heartbeat, faintly, persistently warm.

And all day, in the back of his mind, one word kept rattling the locked room's hinges.

*Vessany.*

That night, in his garret under the eaves, with the bolt drawn and the candle trimmed low, Edmund Marchetti broke the first seal.

The wax parted for him like a mouth opening to speak.
