## 4. Sera Thornfell

Sera Thornfell had been waiting in the alley behind the chandler's shop for six hours, and her left boot had leaked for five of them.

She'd chosen the spot the way her father had taught her to choose everything: with patience and a grudge. The alley ran between the chandler's yard and the back wall of a dyer's, which meant it smelled like tallow and old urine, which meant nobody lingered in it who didn't have to, which meant anyone who *did* linger had a reason, and reasons could be read like letters if you squinted hard enough. It was narrow enough that a man couldn't get past her without brushing the walls. It had one drain cover he'd have to step over, loose enough to clatter and warn her. And it sat on the route Edmund Marchetti took home from the palace every second evening, when the kitchen shift changed and the food-taster slipped out of the usurper's fortress like smoke through a keyhole.

Her father had drawn that route for her once, in charcoal, on the back of a laundry list, because Papa had never been able to keep a secret without sharing the shape of it. *This is the man I'm going to trust with the most important thing I've ever carried,* he'd said, tapping the little stick-figure he'd labeled MARCHETTI, TASTER, FORGETTABLE ON PURPOSE. *He has the most careful hands in the city, Sera-bird, and the saddest eyes. You'll see.*

She hadn't seen. What she'd seen instead was her father coming home gray-faced with excitement three weeks ago, hiding a wax-sealed roll inside his coat like a boy with stolen plums, saying only, *It's done, it's given, now we wait.* Then she'd seen him come home four nights ago with a crossbow bolt through his chest, dumped at the mouth of Tanner's Row like refuse, his hands empty, his eyes open, and no map anywhere on him or in his rooms or under the third floorboard from the window where he'd hidden important things since before Sera was born.

The guards called it robbery. Robbery, in Tanner's Row, of a courier carrying nothing. Robbery by men who'd taken nothing and left a silver ring on his finger that even the poorest cutpurse in this city would have pried off with their teeth.

Sera had done her own arithmetic since then. Her father had carried something out of somewhere dangerous. Her father had given it — *it's done, it's given* — to a forgettable man with sad eyes and careful hands. And now that thing was gone, and the men who wanted it had put a bolt through the only person in the world who loved Sera Thornfell, and the forgettable man with the careful hands was walking home every second evening as though the world were an ordinary place.

So: the alley. Six hours. One leaking boot. A knife her father had bought her for her sixteenth birthday, hilt worn smooth by two years of holding.

She heard him before she saw him — the loose drain cover clattered, exactly as advertised, and then Edmund Marchetti came around the corner of the dyer's wall with his collar up and his shoulders bent, a thin gray man doing a very good impression of a shadow that had gotten lost on its way to somewhere more interesting.

"Edmund Marchetti," Sera said, and stepped out of the doorway she'd spent all evening learning how to stand in without cramping.

He stopped. He didn't startle — that was the first infuriating thing about him. He simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and looked at her, and then looked past her, checking the alley behind her for friends, and then the rooftops, and then the drain cover, cataloguing everything the way her father said he did. His eyes were sad. She'd give Papa that much.

"You're standing in my way," he said.

"I'm told you're good at noticing things." She brought the knife up, not brandishing it — Papa would have winced at brandishing — just letting it exist between them, catching the light off the dyer's windows above. "Then notice this."

"I notice you've been here a while. Your weight's on your right leg. Your left foot is wet." A pause. "There's a puddle behind you, near the downspout. If you step back two paces you'll stop losing heat through your heel."

"I'm not here for footwear advice."

"No," he agreed mildly. "You're here because Tam Thornfell is dead, and you think I know why."

The name went through her like the bolt had gone through her father. She hadn't told anyone in this city her surname; she'd been Sera the seamstress's assistant, Sera no-particular-family, for eleven years, ever since they'd moved and started over. Hearing it in this stranger's quiet voice felt like being seen through a wall.

"How do you know my father's name?"

"He told me. The night he died, he came through the salt door in the palace kitchens, bleeding, and he pressed something into my hands and said a name, and then he made me promise things I haven't figured out how to keep yet." Marchetti's gaze settled on her face, and there was nothing mild in it anymore. "I hid him. I got him to a bone-setter on Candle Lane. I don't know if he lived long enough to be found."

"He didn't." Her voice came out flat, which surprised her; she'd expected it to crack. Four days of not cracking had apparently built up a reserve. "Tanner's Row. Crossbow bolt. They called it robbery."

"That wasn't robbery."

"No. It was a message. And the thing he gave you—" She stepped forward, and the knife stepped forward with her, its point a small cold argument in the space between them. "Give it back. It was never yours to keep. Give it back so I can burn it, and bury what's left of my family under its ashes, and maybe whoever killed him will stop following the trail."

Marchetti looked at the knife. Then, maddeningly, he looked at her again, and something almost gentle crossed his face, which was worse than fear. Fear she knew how to use.

"If I give it to you," he said, "you'll die."

"Everyone dies. My father proved the timing's negotiable."

"They're killing couriers. Do you understand? Not thieves, not informants — *couriers*. Anyone who might have touched that map, anyone who might remember a name attached to it. Your father was the fourth. There were two others before him I only heard whispers about, and one more after, a wine-merchant on Bridge Street whose cellar fire burned hot enough to melt his ledgers." He said it evenly, without drama, the way a man reports the weather, and somehow that made each word land harder. "If you take the map and burn it, they won't know it's burned. They'll assume it moved. And they'll follow the last person who had it. Which would be you."

"And what do they do now?" Sera demanded. "Now that *you* have it? They follow you. So either way someone bleeds — except you've had it for days and you're still breathing, still strolling home from the palace with your little collar up, so either they haven't noticed you, or—"

"Or I'm the one thing they can't afford to kill until they know what he gave me," Marchetti said. "Yes."

The honesty of it knocked her sideways. She'd built four days of fury on the picture of him: the coward, the thief, the man who'd taken her father's trust and hoarded it while Tam Thornfell cooled on cobbles. She had rehearsed this alley scene perhaps forty times, and in thirty-nine of those rehearsals he'd denied everything, whined, bargained. In none of them had he stood in the piss-smell and the candle-grease and calmly explained his own murder-schedule to her.

"Why haven't you run?" she asked. It came out almost accusatory. "You know what it is. You know what follows it. Any sensible man would be over the border by now."

"I tried to be a sensible man for nine years." Something flickered across his face — tiredness, old and deep, like a stain under paint. "It turns out I'm bad at it."

"Show me the map."

"No."

"I have a knife."

"It's a good knife. Your father chose well — balanced, short blade for close work, the grip's been held enough to fit your hand specifically." He glanced at it with professional approval, and she realized with a fresh surge of rage that he was *distracting* her, that the observation was a conjurer's flourish. "But you've been standing in the cold for hours and your hand is shaking, and if you intend to rob me you should do it before your fingers go too numb to hold on to what you've stolen. Also, I don't have it on me. If I'm followed, I carry nothing. It's somewhere no one will look, and no, I won't tell you where, because you've just told me aloud, in an alley, that you want to destroy it, and I haven't decided to let you."

"You haven't *decided*—"

"It isn't mine," he said, and here his voice finally cracked its even surface, just slightly, a hairline fracture in good crockery. "That's what you don't understand. Your father didn't hand me a package. He handed me a *duty*, and he made me swear, with a knife at his own throat's worth of urgency, that I'd carry it to a place called the Ember Gate. I don't know what the Gate is. I don't know why me. I know that he bled out believing I would do it, and I know that the name he gave me — Vessany — is a name I've never heard spoken in nine years of listening to everything anyone said in that palace, which means it's a name people are afraid to say. And I know that whatever it is, it cost four lives already, and the fifth life it's going to cost is mine unless I move faster than the men with crossbows. So no. I haven't decided to let you burn it. I may be many unflattering things, Mistress Thornfell, but I am not a quitter of other people's dying wishes. I have too many of those already."

The alley went quiet. Somewhere above, a shutter banged; a cat expressed an opinion about the cat situation and moved along.

Sera lowered the knife an inch. Then another inch. She hated every degree of it.

"My father talked about you," she said finally. "Not by name, at the end — he'd stopped using names, he said names were getting expensive. But before. He used to come home from palace runs and talk about the taster. How you'd caught henbane in the king's stew twice, and hemlock in the wedding wine, and nobody ever thanked you because thanking the food-taster means admitting someone tried to poison the king. He said you were the loneliest man he'd ever met, and the most careful, and that if the world ended he'd want you deciding what was safe to eat afterward." Her throat tightened. "He trusted you. That's the part I can't forgive yet. That he trusted you, and it killed him."

"It killed him because he finished the job," Marchetti said quietly. "Whatever he was carrying, he got it delivered. That was the whole point of him. He knew the risks better than I do — couriers always do. He walked into that palace knowing what the errand might cost." A pause. "I'm sorry. It's a worthless word. I've watched nine years of worthlessness from the sidelines of other people's grief. But it's the coin I have."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. The anger was still there — it would always be there, she suspected, banked like a coal in a hearth, ready to catch at any draft — but underneath it, unwelcome as a splinter, was something else. This man had hidden her bleeding father. Had carried him. Had kept a dead man's promise in a city full of knives, alone, without telling a soul, without running.

Alone.

That was the part that snagged. Nobody survived alone. Papa had taught her that too: *the mountains forgive groups and eat individuals, Sera-bird, same as cities.*

"The map leads to the Ember Gate," she said slowly. "Through the Thornfells."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "You know the range?"

"I grew up in its shadow, before we ran. Smugglers' roads, shepherd tracks, passes that don't appear on any map the usurper's surveyors ever drew — because my father helped draw the ones that don't appear. He spent twenty years keeping a network of holes alive in the world." She swallowed. "You have half a map's worth of knowledge and a target painted on your back. I have the other half and a reason to use it. So here is my offer, taster, and it's the only one I'm making: you don't give me the map, and I don't ask again. You keep it, because Papa chose you and I'm not ready to believe he was wrong. But I go with you. I plan the road, I find the safe houses, I tell you which streams are poisoned upstream and which passes are watched. And when we reach this Gate of yours — if it exists — I'm the one who decides whether whatever's inside it gets burned or buried or believed. Those are my terms. The alternative is I follow you anyway, loudly, and get us both killed out of spite."

For the first time, Edmund Marchetti smiled. It was a small, crooked, startled thing, like a man discovering a muscle he'd thought atrophied.

"Spite is a surprisingly durable travel ration," he said. "It keeps for years. Very well. Terms accepted — with one amendment."

"Of course there's an amendment."

"Boots. Dry ones. We leave within three days, before the palace finishes counting its spoons and notices mine missing, and the first week is high country, and I have watched what wet feet do to people in the high country." He nodded at her left heel, where the leak had by now achieved full swamp. "Your father would agree. He struck me as a man with strong opinions about feet."

Despite everything — despite the knife still loose in her grip and the coal still banked in her chest and the four days of grief sitting on her ribs like a stone — Sera felt the corner of her mouth betray her.

"He had *entire sermons* about feet," she said. "Fine. Three days. Dawn, the third day, the tannery gate — don't dress like a palace servant, you look like a candle that's been lit too long. And Marchetti?"

"Yes?"

"If you're lying to me — about any of it, about Papa, about the Gate, about any scrap of it — I'll finish what the crossbow started, and I'll take my time about it, and I'll enjoy the taking."

"I'd expect nothing less," he said gravely. "Your father mentioned you took after him."

And then he stepped past her — carefully, wide of the knife's arc, respectful as a man passing a sleeping dog — paused at the mouth of the alley to check both ways with that terrible thoroughness of his, and was gone into the lamplit street, gray as smoke, leaving Sera alone with the smell of tallow, her ruined boot, and the strange, terrifying discovery that she had just made a bargain with the last person alive who knew what her father had died for.

She turned the knife in her hand once, sheathed it, and went to buy boots.

She had three days to prepare, one mountain range to remember, and one promise to extract from herself: that whatever waited at the end of the Ember Road, her father's map would reach it — and that Edmund Marchetti would live long enough to learn whether trusting him had been the best thing Tam Thornfell ever did, or the last mistake of a very good man.

What she could not have known — what no one could have known — was that already, in a gray office above the inquisitors' hall, a patient man was pinning four names to a board and drawing a slow line toward a fifth: *the taster.*
