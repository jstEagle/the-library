## 5. Nine Dishes of Poison

There were nine dishes set before the king that morning, and Edmund Marchetti hated the eighth one on sight.

It was a pheasant pie, glazed with egg and arranged in a fan of pastry feathers, and it smelled of thyme and butter and something underneath—something faintly, insultingly wrong. Not rot. Not rancid fat. Something green, almost sweet, like a hedge after rain.

He had learned long ago not to flinch. Flinching was a confession. So he stood at his station beside the high table with his hands folded and his face arranged into its usual expression: pleasant, vacant, forgettable. The face of a man you would struggle to describe to a portrait painter an hour after meeting him. It had taken years to perfect, and it was the single greatest work of art he would ever produce.

"Marchetti." Taster-Master Hollis's voice came from behind him, dry as old parchment. "The pie."

"Yes, Master Hollis."

"The whole slice. His Majesty is hungry this morning, and I'll not have him waiting while you chew like a cow at prayer."

Edmund cut himself a slice. Under Hollis's eye, under the eye of the under-steward, under the eye of two footmen and a pageboy who was mostly interested in picking his nose, Edmund ate the pheasant pie.

Henbane. There it was again—the green sweetness, riding under the thyme like a smuggler under a tarpaulin. A small dose, carefully measured. Not enough to kill a king. Enough to make him ill for a week, ill enough to be bled by physicians who were probably being paid by whoever had salted the pie, and weak enough afterward that a second, larger dose might go unnoticed among all the other miseries.

Someone in the kitchens had gotten ambitious.

Edmund chewed. Swallowed. Waited.

"You may stand down," Hollis said, when the customary span had passed without Edmund collapsing, foaming, or dying. "His Majesty will hear that the dish is sound."

*The dish is not sound,* Edmund thought, watching King Aldric fall upon his breakfast with the appetite of a man who had never once considered what it cost to keep him fed. *But you are welcome to it.*

He felt the first tendrils of it within the hour—a dryness in the throat, a slight blurring at the edges of things, as though the world had been rubbed with chalk. Henbane did that. He'd catalogued its symptoms the way other men catalogued debts: dry mouth, flushed skin, a racing heart, and if you were truly unlucky, dreams so vivid you woke up arguing with people who weren't there. He had built up a tolerance over nine years the way a mason builds a wall, one careful stone at a time. Most poisons, in the doses meant for kings, only made him feel as though he'd spent an evening drinking bad wine with strangers who talked too much.

By midday he was sweating through his shirt and seeing faint halos around the candle flames, and by evening he had retired to his garret with a jug of watered wine and the map hidden beneath a loose floorboard, where it lay like a heart beating under the floor of his life.

*Vessany.*

He said the name silently, testing it, the way he tested every dish. It sat strangely on the tongue. Too large. Too warm.

Tomorrow night, then. Tomorrow night he would take the road east out of the city before the gates closed for the night watch, and by dawn he would be three miles gone, and by the week's end—

A knock at his door.

Edmund went very still. Nobody knocked at his door. In nine years, nobody had knocked at his door except the once, when a chambermaid had delivered the wrong linens and apologized for eleven minutes straight.

Another knock. Then, muffled: "Marchetti. You're wanted."

It was Pell, one of the junior tasters, a boy of perhaps nineteen with a permanent look of having just been scolded. Edmund opened the door and arranged his face into mild inquiry, which took some effort, given that his pupils were presumably enormous and his mouth was drier than a church in Lent.

"Wanted by whom?"

"Hollis. The counting room." Pell squinted at him. "You look terrible."

"I'm honored you noticed."

"He asked twice whether you'd come up from your rooms. Twice. That's—" Pell lowered his voice, as though delivering news of a plague, "—that's *interest*. Nobody gets interest from Hollis unless they're promoted or dismissed, and I've never actually seen either happen."

Neither had anyone else. Hollis had held his post for thirty years and had reportedly tasted for the previous king as well, surviving three changes of regime through some alchemy of competence and invisibility that Edmund had always privately suspected was sorcery. Hollis noticed everything and remarked upon nothing, which made the remarking upon something a genuinely alarming development.

"Tell him I'm coming," Edmund said. "I need a moment to make myself presentable."

"Presentable," Pell repeated, looking at the sweat-damp collar and the chalk-white face. "Right. Good luck with that."

* * *

The counting room smelled of ink and tallow and the particular dustiness of ledgers that had never once been loved. Hollis sat behind the great table like a spider who had grown tired of webs and moved on to accounting. He did not look up when Edmund entered. He finished writing whatever he was writing, sanded it, folded it, and set down his pen with the deliberation of a man laying down a card he knew would win the trick.

"You were absent from the evening tasting on Tuesday," Hollis said.

"I reported ill, Master Hollis. The flux."

"You reported ill through Pell, who reported it through the porter, who mentioned it to me in passing on Wednesday. An illness serious enough to keep you from duty but not serious enough to require a physician's note. Curious economy."

"The flux passes."

"It does." Hollis finally looked up. His eyes were pale grey, the eyes of a man who had spent decades watching food instead of eating it, and they rested on Edmund with the weight of a ledger line. "You were also absent from the morning tasting yesterday. And today you consumed a dish of henbane with the composure of a man drinking tea, which I confess surprised me less than it should have."

Edmund kept his breathing even. Henbane helped with that, oddly—it slowed everything, thoughts included, wrapping panic in wool. "I don't follow, Master Hollis."

"No," Hollis agreed. "You rarely do. That has always been my assessment of you. Adequate palate. Impeachable discipline. No ambition whatsoever. In thirty years of training tasters, you are the only one who never once asked me how one advances." He folded his hands. "Ambitionless men are either saints or liars, Marchetti, and you have never struck me as devout."

The candle flames had halos now, Edmund noted distantly. Pretty ones. He fixed his attention on the middle distance, where Hollis's face lived, and kept his voice level. "What is it you're asking me?"

"I'm asking nothing. I am informing you that a courier arrived at the palace nine days ago asking after the household staff by name, and was turned away at the salt door because he had no warrant, and that this same courier was found dead in the river two days later, and that the guard captain's report—which crosses my desk because poison deaths are my jurisdiction—made mention of certain papers missing from the man's coat." Hollis paused. "Papers relating to Vessany."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water.

Edmund watched himself very carefully. He watched his hands stay loose at his sides. He watched his pulse refuse to climb, thanks to chemistry rather than courage. And he watched Hollis watch him back, those pale eyes moving across his face the way they moved across a suspect dish—looking, always looking, for the flaw.

"Vessany," Edmund repeated. "The old kingdom? The one from the histories?"

"The one that isn't in the histories." Hollis leaned back. "Which is precisely why the name interests me. Names that have been forbidden do not arrive at palace doors by accident, Marchetti. Someone sent that courier, someone killed him, and someone is now walking around my household carrying whatever he died holding." He rose, slowly, joints cracking like kindling. "Now. Here is what will happen. Tomorrow there is a state banquet for the Ambassador of Cael Harrow, nine courses, and the ninth course has historically been a problem. You will serve at that banquet. You will taste flawlessly. And afterward we will speak again—you and I, alone—and you will tell me exactly what it is you've become involved in, because I am too old and too tired to watch you destroy yourself slowly, and I would prefer to know whether I need to help you or bury you."

He gathered his papers and moved toward the door, pausing at Edmund's shoulder.

"For what it's worth," Hollis said quietly, "henbane in a pheasant pie is a kitchen boy's trick. Whoever means this court harm has better tools than that, and worse intentions. Eat lightly tomorrow."

And he left Edmund standing alone in the counting room, haloed candles burning, heart hammering now that the wool had worn thin.

*He knows.* Or nearly enough. Nine days. Nine days since the courier had died in his arms at the salt door, since Edmund had hidden the body beneath the flour sacks and the map beneath his floorboards, since the whole careful architecture of his invisible life had developed its first magnificent crack. And now Hollis stood between him and the road with thirty years of quiet omniscience and an invitation to confession.

Tomorrow, then. The banquet. One last performance.

After that, no more performances at all.

* * *

The banquet was held in the Long Hall, beneath banners of the usurper's house and the stuffed heads of beasts that had done nothing to deserve their current arrangement. Nine courses. Two hundred guests. Edmund took his place among the six tasters stationed along the dais, dressed in the plain grey livery that was designed, he had always suspected, to make them look like furniture.

Hollis presided from the end of the table, and did not look at him once, which was worse than looking.

Course the first: oysters in vinegar. Clean. Course the second: a soup of white beans, clean. Course the third, venison with blackcurrant—clean, though Edmund detected nutmeg where the recipe called for mace, and filed the cook's slovenliness away for no reason except that noticing things was the only language he spoke fluently. Courses four through seven passed in the rhythm he knew better than his own heartbeat: cut, lift, smell, chew, wait, swallow, wait longer, nod.

Between the seventh and eighth course, he allowed himself one glance down the length of the hall. Two hundred faces, gold plate, laughter like breaking glass. Somewhere beyond the walls, Sera Thornfell was waiting at the tannery on Coopers' Row with two horses and a temper, and if he did not appear by the third bell she had promised—with a sincerity that had genuinely frightened him—to come find him inside the palace herself.

The eighth course was quail in a sauce of dark cherries.

Edmund lifted the spoon. And stopped.

Not henbane this time. Not hemlock, not foxglove, none of the honest poisons with their familiar signatures. This was subtler—a bitterness at the very back of the bitterness, a whisper of almond that wasn't almond, and beneath it something almost floral that raised the hairs on his arms. He knew the family. He didn't know the name. Which meant it was rare, expensive, and chosen by someone who knew the king's tasters had tolerances and had shopped accordingly.

He swallowed it anyway.

Because Hollis was watching. Because refusing a dish, flagging it, spitting it into the ceremonial cloth—any of it would summon questions, guards, inquiries, the unraveling of everything in front of two hundred witnesses. Because the dose was small, a probe rather than a strike. Because nine years of tolerance was a fortress, and tonight, for the last time in his life, he needed its walls to hold.

The bitterness bloomed. His tongue went thick. Somewhere far away, the king was saying something amusing and the hall laughed like a landslide.

"Marchetti." Hollis's voice, low, at his shoulder. "Report."

"Sound," Edmund said. His own voice sounded borrowed. "Mostly sound."

Hollis looked at him for a long moment. Then he lifted his hand, and a footman removed the quail course from the royal table entirely—an act with no official meaning whatsoever, deniable, unremarkable, the sort of thing that happened at banquets when a dish ran cold. The king complained mildly about the service. Nobody died. Nobody ever thanked anybody.

The ninth course was a custard, and it was innocent as a lamb, and Edmund tasted it with tears standing in his eyes that he attributed, correctly, to the eighth course.

* * *

Afterward, in the antechamber where the tasters waited to be dismissed, Hollis found him sitting very still on a bench, hands flat on his knees, watching the world swim gently at the edges.

"You flagged nothing," Hollis said, sitting beside him. "And I removed the dish. Between us, we saved a king who will never know, from a poisoner we will never catch, using methods we cannot explain. This is the work, Marchetti. This is what it is. Thirty years, and the sum total of my gratitude is one footnote in one steward's ledger." He exhaled. "So. Speak. Who are you, and what have you carried into my household?"

And here was the moment Edmund had prepared for all day—the lie, the half-lie, the plausible fog he'd been building in his head through eight courses and a poisoning. He opened his mouth to deploy it.

And found he couldn't.

Not because of the poison. Because of exhaustion, maybe, or the henbane still humming in his blood, or simply because Hollis was the closest thing to a father the profession had ever given him, and Edmund was so very tired of being invisible in front of the one person who could see.

"There was a courier," Edmund said quietly. "Nine days ago. At the salt door. He was dying when I reached him, and he gave me a map, and the map says I'm the heir of a country that doesn't exist anymore. People are hunting it. Hunting *me*. And at dawn tomorrow I'll be gone, and I'm sorry, Master Hollis, because you've been good to me in the only way you know how, which is to say you've never once pretended to care."

Silence. The candles burned. Down the corridor, the banquet was dissolving into drunken warmth and scraping chairs.

Hollis said, at last: "The quail. You knew it was poisoned and you swallowed it anyway, knowing I'd cover you. That wasn't discipline. That was a resignation letter written in your own bloodstream." He shook his head slowly. "You magnificent idiot."

"You're not going to report me."

"To whom? The guard captain would have you in irons within the hour, and I'd gain a commendation and lose the only taster I've ever trusted to eat a suspect dish without whining about it." Hollis stood, wincing. "No. Here is what will happen. You will leave by the salt door, because the main gate keeps records and the salt door does not. You will take the eastern road, and you will ride like the debt collectors are behind you. And I"—he drew a folded paper from his sleeve and set it on the bench between them—"will discover tomorrow morning that a tin of arsenic has gone missing from my stores, and that my ledger shows it was signed out three weeks ago against a requisition that no longer exists. A theft. My embarrassment. Nothing to do with you."

Edmund stared at the paper. It was a bill of passage for a grain cart heading east, stamped and sealed, valid for two days. "Master Hollis—"

"Don't." The old man's voice cracked, just slightly, like ice on a pond. "Thirty years, boy. Do you know what I've learned in thirty years? That gratitude in this place is a coin nobody mints. So I spend it differently." He turned for the door. "Go home, Marchetti. Go be somebody's heir. And if you ever learn who salted that quail—"

He paused, hand on the latch.

"—come back and tell me. I hate an unsolved dish."

Then he was gone, and Edmund sat alone in the antechamber with a forged bill of passage, a belly full of rare poison, and the strange, unbearable knowledge that he had just been seen—truly seen—for perhaps the first time in his adult life, by the one man whose regard he'd never thought to want.

* * *

He burned his life at midnight.

Not literally—he was too careful for fire, fire left ash, and ash could be read. Instead he dismantled it piece by piece, the way he'd dismantle a suspect dish. The spare livery went into the river off Coopers' Bridge, weighted with a cobblestone. The little hoard of coins from nine years of wages went into his boot, all of it; there had never been much, invisibility paid poorly. The tin cup, the hairbrush, the chipped saint's medallion he'd bought himself at nineteen because everyone should own one useless holy thing—all of it stayed, deliberately, arranged just so, the archaeology of a man named Edmund Marchetti who had simply stepped out one night and never returned.

Last of all, the floorboard. The map, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his body. He weighed it in his palm and thought about the courier's blood soaking into the flour sacks, and the word *Vessany* gasped against his ear like a secret the world had been keeping from him.

Somewhere south of here, a girl with her father's knife was waiting to hate him properly. Somewhere east, mountains. Somewhere past the mountains, a gate, and whatever an heir was supposed to do when he got there.

He pulled the loose board back over the empty space, and it settled with a soft knock that sounded, in the silence of the garret, horribly like a door closing.

"Well," Edmund said aloud, to the room, to the ghost of himself, to nobody. "That's the last of that, then."

The third bell rang across the sleeping city. On Coopers' Row, a grain cart with a forged seal stood ready in the dark, and a sharp-tongued girl checked and rechecked a knife she already knew was sharp, waiting for a man who had spent nine years learning to disappear—and one impossible week learning, at last, to be worth finding.

Edmund Marchetti picked up his bag, put the map against his chest, and went out through the salt door into the dark—leaving behind a tin cup, a saint's medal, and every trace of the man he had been, walking toward a name he was only beginning to learn how to carry.
