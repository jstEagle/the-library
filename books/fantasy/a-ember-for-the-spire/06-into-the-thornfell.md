## 6. Into the Thornfell

The Thornfell mountains did not care that Sera Thornfell was named for them. Her father had always found this funny — "Named you after the range, girl, and the range never sent so much as a welcome note" — but there was nothing funny in it now, three days into the high passes with frost in her boots and a soft-handed palace taster stumbling behind her like a newborn deer with opinions.

"Are we stopping soon?" Edmund called.

"No."

"The thing is," he said, catching up anyway because his legs were longer and the world was unjust, "when you say 'no' like that, it doesn't actually answer the question. It just tells me your mood."

"My mood *is* the answer."

He considered this. "Then I'll take it as a no."

Sera didn't groan. She'd made a rule about it somewhere around dawn on the second day: she would not groan at him, because groaning was a form of conversation, and conversation was a courtesy reserved for people who hadn't gotten her father killed. But it was getting harder. The trouble with Edmund Marchetti — one of the eleven thousand troubles with Edmund Marchetti — was that he kept being *useful*, in small irritating ways, and useful was dangerously close to likable.

It was he who'd noticed, an hour out of the lowlands, that her water skin had been seeping through a split seam. He'd plugged it with pine pitch warmed over their cookfire, working with those careful taster's hands, saying nothing while she glared at him. It was he who knew which of the berries on the southern slope were safe, because he'd once tasted a dish garnished with their poisonous cousin and spent four days learning exactly how they killed you from the inside of a sickroom. And it was he who had looked at the first ridge of real mountain, gone faintly grey, and said, "Right," in the voice of a man accepting a sentence — and then climbed it.

She hated that she noticed these things. Noticing was the first step toward forgiving, and forgiveness was a door she had nailed shut with her father's body on the far side of it.

* * *

The smugglers' road didn't look like a road, which was the entire point of it. To anyone following behind, it was a game trail, then a rockfall, then a stretch of scree that might have been crossed by goats or idiots or nobody at all. Only someone raised on it — someone who had run messages for her father between the free cities and the hidden places since she was nine years old — could read the signs: the cairn shaped wrong on purpose, the blaze cut into a pine at knee height where no honest traveler would mark a tree, the ford crossed diagonally where the water ran shallow over flat stone.

"This way," she said, for perhaps the fortieth time.

"You know," Edmund said, picking his way along the ledge beside her, "at court we had a saying. When someone gives directions with absolute confidence, either they're right or they're trying to get you killed, and the two are distinguished only in retrospect."

"What's the saying?"

"That's it. That's the whole saying." He paused to test a foothold. "Court wisdom is mostly just fatalism with better tailoring."

Despite herself — against her explicit internal policy — something twitched at the corner of Sera's mouth. She killed it immediately.

They walked on. Below them the valley floor had already become a memory of the world, all smoke and distance; above, the peaks wore their snow like old men wear grudges, permanently and without discussion. The air thinned. Her lungs had known this altitude since childhood, but Edmund's hadn't, and she listened to his breathing go from easy to labored to a kind of stubborn wheeze that he disguised, poorly, as humming.

"What's the song?" she asked finally, because silence had its own costs and she was rationing hers.

"I don't know. Something the undercooks sang when the master of kitchens wasn't listening. I only ever caught half the words." He hummed a bar. "I think it's about a pig."

"A pig."

"A beloved pig. Or possibly a magistrate. The rhyme scheme works for both."

That time the smile almost got away from her entirely. She turned it into a cough and scrambled up the next stretch of scree ahead of him, putting distance between them, putting the mountain between them, which was harder to do than it sounded because mountains, unlike feelings, held still.

Her father should have been the one climbing here. That was the thought that lived under her ribs like a swallowed stone. Tam Thornfell had carried messages across these passes for thirty years; he knew every smuggler's gospel verse, every hidden bothy, every story about why the seventh cairn below Wolfjaw Tor leaned left. He should have been the one leading some lost heir of a dead kingdom toward whatever the map promised. Instead he'd bled out in a kitchen doorway with his hand pressed over a stranger's, and the stranger had inherited both the map and the daughter's fury, and here they were.

"He chose you," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. The thin air loosened things.

Edmund stopped climbing. Above them a bird — some ragged brown thing — wheeled once and vanished over the ridge.

"He didn't choose me," Edmund said quietly. "He fell through a door and I happened to be standing in it. There's a difference."

"He gave *you* the map. Out of everyone in that palace he could have stumbled past — cooks, guards, ministers — he put it in your hands and said Vessany. He must have known what you were."

"I didn't know what I was."

"Yes," Sera said. "Well. That's rather the tragedy of it, isn't it."

She climbed on before he could answer, because the alternative was to keep talking, and if she kept talking she would eventually say something true, like *I want to hate you and I can't quite manage it and I hate that more.*

* * *

They stopped that night in a bothy her father had shown her when she was twelve — a dry-stone shelter tucked into a fold of the mountain, invisible until you were standing in front of it. Half the roof had fallen in sometime in the years since, but the windward wall stood, and there was an old iron hook for hanging a kettle, and Sera felt a pang at the sight of it that she mistook, deliberately, for hunger.

They had hunger too. That was genuine enough. Their provisions had been calculated for speed, not comfort: hard bread, dried sausage, a bag of oats, cheese that could survive a fall from a cliff because it essentially already had. Edmund divided portions with the same scrupulous fairness he applied to everything, weighing each share in his palm like a man dosing medicine.

"You take more," Sera said, when he pushed the larger piece of cheese toward her.

"I take exactly my share. My share is simply smaller, because I'm smaller." He was not smaller. "Also I ate at court for nine years. Consider it caloric equity."

"That's the stupidest argument I've ever heard."

"It's not an argument. It's a policy. I have many policies." He bit into his smaller portion with an air of closing negotiations.

She ate the cheese. It felt like losing.

Afterward they sat close to the small fire — closer than either of them liked, because the cold at this altitude was not a metaphorical opponent and did not respect grudges — and the stars came out in the brutal clarity of the high country, more of them than the sky seemed entitled to hold. Somewhere below and behind, the lowlands glowed faintly, the usurper's capital burning its thousand candles over everything it had erased.

"Do you remember any of it?" Sera asked. "Vessany."

"A little. A kitchen with a blue door. My mother's voice singing something I can't reproduce." His voice had changed — flatter, more careful, the voice he probably used at court. "I used to think those were dreams. Now I don't know what to think they are."

"You're going to have to decide," Sera said. "Before the Gate, whatever it is. If it wants your blood and your memories, you'll want to know which ones are real before you pay for them."

He was quiet a long moment. Firelight moved on his face and made him look younger, or maybe just less defended.

"You've thought about this more than I have," he said.

"I've thought about everything more than you have. You spent nine years tasting soup. I spent nine years learning which mountains eat people."

"And yet here I am, not eaten."

"The night is young."

But there was no venom left in it when she said it, and she suspected he heard that, and she suspected he was polite enough not to show that he'd heard it, and she was grateful for that in a way that annoyed her considerably.

* * *

The riders appeared on the fourth morning.

Sera saw them first, because seeing first was her job and her inheritance. They had camped in the lee of a granite shoulder, and she had gone back along their trail to check it — a habit her father had drilled into her until it was less a habit than a heartbeat — and there, far below where the smugglers' road dropped toward the last farmsteads, she saw movement. Not herdsmen. Not hunters. Hunters moved like weather, loose and wandering. These moved like a sentence being written: eight points of grey, evenly spaced, climbing with the unhurried certainty of men following something rather than searching for it.

Grey. Not the grey of wool or rain-cloud. The grey of official cloth, dyed to match nothing in nature, so that wherever they went they looked like a smudge on the eye, a place where color refused to happen.

Inquisitors' riders. In the Thornfell.

She was back in camp within minutes, moving fast and quiet, and Edmund took one look at her face and started rolling his bedroll without asking a single question, which was the single most intelligent thing she'd seen him do.

"How many?" he murmured.

"Eight. Behind us by maybe half a day. They're on our trail."

"They can't be. Your road is supposed to be invisible."

"Roads are only as invisible as the people walking them." She kicked dirt over the fire's remains, feeling for heat with her bare hand until there was none. "We're leaving sign somewhere — a broken branch, a boot print in the wrong mud. Doesn't matter how. What matters is they've found the rhythm of us, and Riders don't lose a rhythm."

"Can we outrun them?"

"Not with you breathing like a kettle." She said it without heat, and realized as she said it that there was no calculation in it anymore, no cruelty — just assessment, the plain arithmetic of the mountain. "We don't outrun them. We stop being a rhythm. Come on."

She led him off the trail within the hour, onto ground she knew and they couldn't: a shelf of broken rock that required hands and patience and the willingness to trust certain ledges more than seemed reasonable. Twice she had to guide his feet with her own hands, his weight brief and startling against her grip, and both times neither of them mentioned it afterward, which was becoming a language between them — all the things they didn't mention, arranged carefully in rows.

From the high shelf they looked down at the trail they'd abandoned and watched, over the course of a cold afternoon, the eight grey figures come up it. Even at that distance there was something wrong about the way they rode. Horses disliked the high scree; these horses didn't spook, didn't stumble, didn't even toss their heads, moving through country that should have terrified them with the placid obedience of animals whose fear had been managed out of them. Sera had grown up on stories of the Grey Riders. The stories had prepared her for cruelty. They had not prepared her for *patience*, which was worse.

"Why don't they hurry?" Edmund whispered. "If they know where we are—"

"Because they don't need to hurry." She watched the lead rider pause at the false cairn, dismount, and turn the stones over with deliberate care before rebuilding them exactly as they'd been. "Look at him. He's not hunting us like prey. He's reading us like a letter. Every mistake we've made, he's collecting it." Her mouth was dry. "My father said the Inquisition's first question is never *where did they go*. It's *where will they go*. They're not following our past. They're following our future."

"Which means?"

"Which means they don't have to guess which pass we'll take. There's only one road that goes where the map says we're going, and they know it as well as I do. Maybe better." She pulled back from the lip of the shelf, heart hammering a slow, heavy rhythm. "We're not running from them, Edmund. We're racing them. And they've been riding this race for twenty years."

Below them, the last of the grey figures passed the false cairn without breaking stride, and the column wound on up the trail like a thread being drawn through the mountain — patiently, inevitably, toward the same door they were.

Edmund sat back against the rock. For a moment he looked like exactly what he was: a man who had spent nine years being invisible in a palace, suddenly visible on the side of a mountain to the most patient hunters in the world. Then he did something strange. He reached into his pack, brought out the bread and the oats, and began dividing the next three days' rations into careful, equal portions, his hands steady, his face settling into that flat court calm she was beginning to understand was not emptiness at all but a kind of armor.

"If they're faster than us," he said quietly, "then speed doesn't matter. What matters is knowing things they don't." He glanced up at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before — not courage exactly, and not fear, but the strange alloy of the two that mountains forge in people whether they ask for it or not. "You grew up on these roads. I grew up in the house of the man hunting us. Between us, we might know one thing the Riders don't."

Sera looked at him a long moment — this soft, forgettable man her father had chosen, this stranger she could not manage to hate — and heard herself say the truest thing she'd said in days:

"All right, taster. Then tell me what you know about inquisitors."

And he opened his mouth to answer — but the words were never spoken, because from the ridge above them, clear across the cold thin air, came a sound that froze them both where they sat: a horn, long and low and patient, blown once, like a man politely announcing that the race was over.
