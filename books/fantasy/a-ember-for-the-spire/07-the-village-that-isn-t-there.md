## 7. The Village That Isn't There

Edmund had spent nine years learning to notice things, so it was fitting that the first thing he noticed about the village was that it refused to be noticed.

They had walked past it twice before Sera stopped swearing long enough to admit something was wrong.

"The trail says there's a hollow here," she said, jabbing at her father's notes. "Spring, shelter, a smoke-hole in the rock. Pastures." She looked up at the unremarkable wall of pine and scree and gray afternoon light, as if it had personally insulted her. "There's nothing."

"There's a smell," Edmund said.

Sera sniffed the air, suspicious. "Woodsmoke. So? Travellers."

"Travellers don't bank their fires to burn slow. That's a cooking smoke — low, wet, patient. Someone's making bread." He paused. "And someone's hiding it. You hide your smoke when you don't want to be found. Which means whoever's down there has been found before, by people they didn't like."

Sera stared at him. "You can smell *intentions*?"

"I can smell bread," Edmund said. "Intentions are just bread with extra steps."

It was meant as a joke. It landed instead on whatever tender place she kept her father, because she turned away quickly and said, "Fine. Show me your clever nose, taster. Find the door."

The door, when he found it, was a hawthorn bush that grew at an angle no honest hawthorn would choose, rooted in a seam of rock with a draft coming out of it that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and — underneath, faint as a memory of a memory — rosemary.

Edmund stood very still.

Rosemary. He didn't know why his eyes stung. He'd never grown it. He'd never cooked with it professionally; it overpowered delicate sauces. But some part of him, some part with no name and no history he could point to, leaned toward that scent the way a man leans toward a window he used to sit in.

"Edmund." Sera's voice, careful. "You've gone strange."

"There's a village," he said. "Down there. And I think—" He stopped, because the sentence forming in his mouth was insane: *I think I've been here.* "I think we should knock politely."

* * *

The knocking turned out to be unnecessary, because the door opened before Sera finished raising her fist, revealing an old woman with a face like a walnut and a rolling pin raised in cheerful readiness.

"Well," said the old woman, looking them up and down. "Two half-starved strangers out of the high passes, in autumn, off the smugglers' road. Either you're robbers, in which case you're too skinny to be any good at it, or you're pilgrims, in which case you're too rude to be any good at that either. In."

"We haven't asked to come in," Sera began.

"You were going to. I could hear you deciding." The rolling pin gestured them inside. "Wipe your feet. My name is Maren Ostrova, I keep the ovens, and if you touch my proofing bowls without asking I will break both your wrists and ask questions after."

Edmund decided immediately that he liked her enormously.

The passage sloped down into warm dark, then opened all at once into a cavern of light — a whole village folded into the mountain's bones, stone houses stacked against the walls, lanterns strung on lines, terraced gardens glowing green under glass roofs cut into the rock above. Children ran between the houses chasing a hoop. A woman beat a rug from a balcony. Somewhere a fiddle was being badly played by someone who did not care.

"It's a town," Sera breathed. "A whole town under a mountain."

"A town that isn't there," Edmund said quietly, and didn't know where the words came from until they were already out.

Maren's rolling pin lowered slowly. She looked at him — really looked, for the first time, not the assessing glance she'd given the pair of them at the door but a long, searching look, the kind you give a word written in a language you'd almost forgotten reading.

"Say that again," she said.

"A town that isn't—"

"Where were you born?" Her voice had changed entirely. Gone was the banter; what remained was taut as wire.

"I don't know," Edmund said. It was the truth, and it came out smaller than he expected. "I was told I was born in Marival. Lower Marsh district. But I've never found the street anyone described to me, and the parish records for that year are missing, and—" He stopped himself. He had never said any of this aloud to anyone. Nine years of perfect blankness, and here he was confessing to a stranger with a rolling pin. "I'm sorry. That's a strange thing to tell someone."

"No," Maren said. "That's a Vessani thing to tell a Vessani woman." She reached out and took his chin in a floury hand, turning his face to the lantern light like a buyer checking a coin. "Your grandmother's cheekbones," she muttered. "Your grandfather's jaw. Twenty years, and the mountain sends him down through my front door looking exactly like—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together hard, and when she spoke again her voice was thick. "Supper first. Then the elder. Eat before you meet destiny, boy; destiny is much easier to argue with when you're full."

* * *

Supper was served in a long hall carved from living rock, at tables worn smooth by generations of elbows, and it was the best meal Edmund had eaten in nine years, which was saying something, since he had eaten at a king's table every day of those years.

He tried to explain this to Sera and made a poor job of it.

"The king's food is perfect," he said, tearing bread. "Every dish balanced, every sauce corrected, every plate arranged by artists. And none of it is for me. I eat a spoonful of everything and taste everything and enjoy nothing, because a third of my mind is always doing arithmetic about arsenic. This bread is lopsided and slightly burnt on one edge and it's the most honest thing I've eaten since I was a child."

"Since you were a child," Sera repeated. "Which child? Where?"

"I don't know." He said it again and found it came easier the second time, like a muscle warming up. "That's the whole trouble, Sera. I've built a life on not knowing. It's the safest architecture in the world. Nobody can interrogate a blank page."

"Nobody can read one, either," Sera said, and then looked briefly appalled at herself, as though the sympathy had escaped without a permit.

But around them, the hall had begun to notice. An old man two tables over had stopped chewing. A serving girl had dropped a ladle and was gripping her neighbor's sleeve. Word moved through the room faster than the food did, and Edmund felt it happen — felt dozens of eyes turn toward him with a weight he had spent his whole life training away. He knew invisibility the way a fish knows water. This was its opposite. This was being seen by three hundred people at once, and each of them looking at him like a letter finally delivered.

"They're staring," he muttered.

"Yes."

"At me."

"Yes."

"That's the worst thing that has ever happened to me."

"You once drank poison for a living," Sera said. "Surely you can survive being looked at."

"It's different. Poison only kills you." He took a breath. "This wants something."

Before Sera could answer, the doors at the end of the hall opened and the room rose — not all at once, but in a ripple, like wind moving through wheat, and Edmund understood with a lurch that they were standing for someone behind him, and turned.

The elder was small. That was Edmund's first thought, absurdly: *he's small.* White hair braided in the old style Maren wore, hands knotted like tree roots, walking with a stick of blackthorn and no hurry whatsoever. He crossed the hall slowly while three hundred people held their breath, and stopped in front of Edmund, and looked up at him with eyes gone pale as frost but very much alive.

"Hallan Vesik," he said. "Elder of this settlement. Though 'settlement' is a polite word for what we are." He studied Edmund's face the way Maren had, but longer, and with something breaking gently behind his eyes. "My grandmother served at court. She carried me on her shoulders at the spring festivals. I remember banners." He smiled, and the smile was mostly grief. "You have the Marchetti mouth, but everything above the nose is pure Aldarine. Your great-grandmother's line. Sit down, boy. Everyone else, sit down. Bread doesn't wait and neither does an old man."

Edmund sat. His legs made the decision for him.

"You know who I am," he said. "I wish somebody would tell *me*, because I've been following that sentence around all evening like a dog hoping for scraps."

Hallan Vesik lowered himself into a chair, laid his stick across his knees, and was quiet for a moment. Around the hall, conversation died away entirely, until the loudest sound was the fire.

"Do you know what happened to Vessany?" the elder asked.

"I know the word now. A dead courier said it. Sera's father." He glanced at Sera; her jaw tightened but she nodded for him to go on. "I know it's a kingdom that doesn't appear on maps. I assumed it was conquered. Swallowed. It happens."

"No," Hallan said. "Conquest leaves ruins. Conquest leaves songs, even forbidden ones. Conquest leaves graves, and graves have names." He tapped the blackthorn stick on the floor, once, softly. "Vessany was not conquered, boy. Vessany was *edited*. Twenty-two years ago the usurper's armies came, yes — briefly, bloodily, and left again. The killing was almost an afterthought. The real work was done afterward, with ink. Our name was struck from every map, every ledger, every church roll and tax book and gravestone across four kingdoms. Treaties were rewritten. Histories were reprinted. The roads that led here were renamed so that travelers asking after Vessany were asking after a place that had never existed, and were treated as madmen or liars. Children born after the purge were taught a border that had always been there, a valley that had always been empty. It is very difficult," the elder said, "to fight an enemy who has made you a typo."

Silence. Somewhere in the hall a baby started crying and was hastily hushed.

"But you survived," Sera said. "All of you. How do you survive being unwritten?"

"Incompletely," Hallan said dryly, and a ripple of rueful laughter went through the hall, the laughter of people who had earned it. "Ink is slower than fire and misses more. Farmers who couldn't read never learned their farms weren't theirs anymore. Grandmothers," he added, with a small bow toward Maren, "are notoriously difficult to edit. We gathered. Slowly, over years, along the smugglers' roads and the passes the new maps didn't bother to draw, because you cannot rename a mountain goat track. We built downward, because upward makes a silhouette. We kept the tongue alive in the winter camps and the recipes alive in the ovens and the names alive—" his voice caught, just slightly, "—in our mouths, every night, so that our children would know what could not be written anywhere. We called ourselves the ones who aren't. It is not a bad name. It keeps us humble and it keeps us hidden."

"And me?" Edmund asked. His voice came out rough. "Why does everyone look at me like that?"

"Because a story needs more than survivors, boy. It needs a reason." Hallan leaned forward. "When the purge came, the royal house scattered its children like seed. Not to raise armies — there were no armies left. To be seeds. Lives planted in foreign soil under foreign names, ordinary, forgettable, safe. Each one carrying nothing — no signet, no claim, no papers — because papers get found. Only blood, and faces, and the hope that someday someone would come looking." The pale eyes held his. "You were one of those seeds. Planted in the usurper's own capital, of all places — and there is a poetry in that I intend to enjoy fully before I die. Raised blank. Kept small. And yet here you sit, having walked out of his palace with the map every family swore was lost, wearing a face three hundred people would know in the dark."

Edmund's hands were shaking. He put them flat on the table, and the table was smooth and warm and worn by other hands, hands of people whose names had been forbidden, and that simple fact undid him more than anything the elder had said.

"I'm a food taster," he said. "I detect henbane. I am very, very good at not being noticed. That is the entire inventory of my accomplishments."

"Yes," said Hallan Vesik, gently. "Nine years of surviving unnoticed in the serpent's own kitchen, carrying nothing, telling no one, trusting no one. Do you know what that sounds like to us?" He spread his root-knuckled hands. "It sounds like preparation."

Around the hall, three hundred people who officially did not exist were looking at Edmund Marchetti with something he had never once been given in nine years at court, and could not defend against, and did not know how to refuse.

Not fear. Not suspicion. Not the flat calculation reserved for useful tools.

Hope.

Belonging, offered like a wound — open, bleeding, waiting for him to decide whether to press his hand to it.

"I never asked for this," he whispered.

"No," the elder agreed. "Seeds rarely do."

* * *

Later — much later, after the hall had emptied and Maren had pressed a second helping of lopsided bread on them "for walking purposes" and Sera had gone to check the perimeter with a company of local scouts who moved through the dark like rumors — Edmund sat alone at the cold hearth, holding the map.

The blood-seal at its heart was still faintly warm. It had been warm since the garret; warmer tonight, now, as though it knew where it had come home to. He traced its edges with one finger and felt the heat climb up his arm like something recognizing him.

*Nine dishes of poison,* he thought. *Nine years of dying a little every day so a stranger could live. And the whole time—*

The whole time there had been a valley. An oven. Rosemary. Three hundred people keeping a language alive between their teeth, waiting for a delivery that took twenty-two years to arrive.

Footsteps. Sera came back in smelling of pine and night air, sat down beside him without asking, and looked at the map in his hands.

"So," she said. "You're a king."

"I'm a taster with a title I can't pronounce."

"You're the last heir of an erased kingdom, sitting in its last kitchen, and tomorrow the elder is going to show you the sealed archive under the east hall where they've kept everything — records, genealogies, the oath rolls. Everything your family left behind." She was quiet a moment. "My father carried this map twenty years, Edmund. He told me once it was the heaviest thing he'd ever held, and he wasn't talking about weight."

Edmund looked at the seal. It pulsed faintly under his fingertip, patient as an ember.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we'd better find out what it's been trying to tell me."

Above them, far off along the ridge line, so distant it might have been a falling star, something flared red in the darkness — and Sera was on her feet before Edmund even registered it, saying the words that turned his blood to ice water:

*"Fire. That's the direction of the lower shepherds' camp."*
