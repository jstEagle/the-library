## 8. Ashes on the Tongue

Sera had learned to read smoke before she could read letters.

Her father taught her on the smugglers' roads, when she was small enough to ride his shoulders: *That's a chimney, little bird, lazy and fat. That's a kiln. And that* — a thin black thread standing straight up in dead air — *that's trouble.*

Two days out of the village that wasn't there, camped in a fold of the Thornfell where the pines grew crooked and the wind sang through them like a badly tuned fiddle, Sera woke at dawn to smoke.

Not a chimney. Not a kiln.

Trouble.

She was on her feet before her eyes were fully open, boots half-laced, knife already in hand because some habits were older than thought. Below them, down the switchbacks they'd climbed yesterday, a column of black stood against the pale morning sky. Straight up. Dead air. The kind of smoke that came from thatch and timber and things that had been homes an hour ago.

"No," Edmund said.

She hadn't realized he was awake. He sat up in his bedroll with his hair flattened on one side like a startled bird, and he was looking at the smoke too, and his face had gone the color of old tallow.

"The Riders," Sera said. "They found it."

"They couldn't have— we left two days ago, they'd have had to—"

"Corvane's Grey Riders don't find things, Marchetti. They're told where things are." She was buckling her pack as she spoke, shoving the cold remains of last night's bread into it. "Someone talked. Someone always talks. Or someone followed us and we were too busy feeling sorry for ourselves to notice."

Edmund flinched at that, which she noted with a small, mean satisfaction that curdled almost immediately into something worse. Because it was true. They'd spent two days walking in silence so thick you could have carved your initials in it, each of them nursing their grievances like a hot stone passed hand to hand, and neither of them had once looked over their shoulder properly.

Her father would have looked. Her father had died looking over his shoulder for other people's sake.

"We go back," Edmund said.

Sera stopped lacing her boot. "We what?"

"We go back." He was standing now, pulling his coat on with hands that weren't quite steady but didn't stop moving. "There will be survivors. There are always survivors — people run, people hide in cellars, people—"

"People are dead, Marchetti." The words came out harder than she'd meant, and she watched them land, watched him take them like a man taking a blow he'd been expecting. "You don't know what a Rider purge looks like. I do. There's nothing back there but ash and accounting."

"Then we go back for the ash," he said, "and we count."

And the worst thing — the thing that made her want to throw her boot at him — was that he said it without any drama at all. No speech. No trembling chin. Just a food-taster from the usurper's palace stating a fact about corpses, as if counting the dead were simply a dish that needed tasting.

She went anyway. She told herself it was because leaving him to go alone was a death sentence and she'd promised his useless royal blood to no one yet, least of all himself.

She told herself that the whole way down the mountain. It took four hours. It did not get more convincing.

* * *

The village was still burning when they reached it.

That was the first lie the fire told — that it was quick. From the ridge it had looked almost gentle, a candle-snuff of a thing. Up close it roared. Heat rolled off the ruins in waves that made the air shimmer, and the smell — Sera had smelled a lot of terrible things on smugglers' roads, but burning homes had a smell that got under your skin and stayed, like grief with a chemistry to it.

The elder — Maren, her name was Maren, she'd pressed honey-cakes on them two days ago and called Edmund by a name that made him go white — lay in the square. Sera checked, because someone had to, and then she made herself stop checking after the third body, because the third body had been a child, and there was a line past which usefulness became just another way of bleeding.

"Here!" Edmund's voice, cracked and hoarse, from behind the collapsed granary. "Here — I've got them!"

He had them. Seven of them, wedged into a root cellar whose entrance he'd apparently uncovered by hauling aside a beam that must have weighed as much as he did — and it should be noted, Sera thought wildly, that Edmund Marchetti weighed about as much as a well-fed cat. His sleeves were scorched. His palms were bleeding. A girl of maybe six was wrapped around his neck like a terrified squirrel, and an old man was leaning on his shoulder, and behind them in the cellar dark Sera could see more shapes, coughing, blinking.

"There were eleven," Edmund said. "I think. I counted eleven when they went down. Four aren't here. I looked. I looked everywhere I could before the—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Four aren't here."

"I know," Sera said.

"We have to go. Now. They'll have left watchers — Corvane always leaves watchers to see who comes to the ashes." He said the name *Corvane* the way you'd spit out something bitter, and Sera remembered, abruptly, that this man had spent nine years swallowing bitterness for a living. Nine years of holding poison in his mouth and keeping his face pleasant.

"Give me the girl," she said, and held out her arms.

He handed the child over without argument, and that — that small, unhesitating trust — did something to the stone wall Sera had been building between them since the alley. She hated that it did something. She built the wall higher and carried the girl up the mountain anyway.

* * *

They moved fast and high, off the smugglers' roads onto goat tracks that weren't tracks so much as arguments the mountain occasionally won. Sera led. Behind her came a procession of the burned-out: seven villagers from the cellar, plus three more they'd gathered on the way through — a shepherd who'd been on the high pasture when the Riders came, and his dog, and a woman named Ilse who walked with a stick and refused, flatly, to be carried or pitied.

And Edmund Marchetti, who turned out to be full of surprises, none of them impressive-looking and all of them useful.

It started small. The six-year-old — Tamsin, she was called, though mostly she communicated through the medium of crying — wouldn't drink, and Edmund crouched next to her and did something with a dried leaf and a joke about palace soup that was genuinely not funny, and Tamsin drank. Then the old man's breathing went wet and rattling, and Edmund listened to his chest with his ear, frowning, and asked Sera if there was anything on the mountain that smelled like licorice, and there was, because Sera knew every plant in the Thornfell that could feed you, kill you, or make a customs officer sneeze, and between her botany and his medicine the old man breathed easier by nightfall.

"You were a taster," Sera said, watching him crush the root with a rock. "Not a healer."

"Tasting is healing," Edmund said, not looking up. "Mostly it's knowing exactly how much of a bad thing a body can survive. Work backwards from there." He paused. "Also I stole every book in the palace physician's library over nine years, because the nights were long and the wine was poisoned."

"That was a joke," Sera said suspiciously.

"A little bit."

Huh. The man had a sense of humor. Buried deep, like a coin under floorboards, but it was there. She filed this away with the same wary interest she'd file a new route through hostile territory.

* * *

On the third day, the weather turned, because the Thornfell weather turned the way her father used to say tax collectors arrived: inevitably, and always at the worst moment.

Snow came sideways. The refugees slowed. Ilse's stick punched through a snow bridge and she went in to her hip, and it took four of them twenty minutes to haul her out, and by then everyone was soaked and shaking and the light was dying.

They holed up under an overhang that Sera had used twice before, and she got a fire going — small, screened, the kind of fire her father had taught her to build so it gave heat and gave away nothing — and she rationed out the food, and she did the arithmetic she always did, the cold arithmetic of mouths versus miles, and the answer was ugly.

Then Edmund got up, said nothing, and went back out into the snow.

"Where is he going?" the shepherd asked.

"He does this," Sera said, before she could stop herself, and then realized with irritation that it was true. He did do this. All through the retreat he'd kept drifting toward whoever was weakest — carrying Tamsin until his shoulders shook, giving his cloak to Ilse and claiming he ran hot, sitting up nights with the old man's rattling chest. Quietly. Without asking anyone to notice. It was the most infuriating possible way to behave, because you couldn't argue with a man who never announced his own virtue; you could only watch him and feel your anger slide around looking for purchase.

He came back an hour later, white to the eyebrows, carrying a brace of snow-hares he'd snared from a warren he'd spotted — *spotted*, the palace mouse, in a whiteout — and a handful of the licorice-root, and a look of dumb triumph that made Tamsin laugh for the first time since the burning.

"How," Sera said, when the hares were spitting over the fire and the refugees slept in a heap like puppies.

"My mother hunted," Edmund said. He was turning his hare, and the firelight moved on his face, and for once he didn't look like a man reading his own eulogy. "Before. I don't remember much else about her, but I remember that. She said hunger makes liars of everyone except the person who feeds you."

Sera looked at him for a long moment.

"You talk about her like she's a story you heard," she said. "Not a memory."

"She is, mostly." He shrugged, and the shrug tried to be casual and failed. "I was young when we left. Ran. Whatever we did." He turned the hare again. "Sometimes I think the only real thing I know about myself is that I can taste henbane in a stew. Everything else is just... furniture."

And there it was. Under the competence, under the quiet stubbornness, under the infuriating habit of giving away his own warmth without accounting for it — there was the same hollow she'd heard in her own voice since the courier's office had come to tell her about her father. Two orphans of different disasters, sitting at the same fire.

"I hated you," Sera said.

"I know."

"You let me hate you. In the alley, with the knife. You could have run. You could have called the watch. You just stood there and told me the truth while I held steel on you."

"It seemed rude to interrupt," Edmund said. "You were being very thorough."

Despite everything — despite the ash still in her hair, despite the four who hadn't made it out of the cellar, despite her father's map folded against her heart like a second heartbeat — Sera laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, half sob, and she covered it by throwing a pinecone at his head, which he ducked, barely, grinning.

"I'm not done hating you," she warned.

"Take your time. I'm told I grow on people. Like moss."

"Like a fungus."

"Same family," he agreed solemnly, "botanically speaking," and she threw another pinecone.

* * *

On the fifth day, the Grey Riders' scouts found them.

Sera saw them first — two figures in grey on the ridgeline above, still as crows, and then gone. An hour later, three more, lower, closer, herding. By dusk she understood the shape of it: they weren't attacking. They were driving. Pushing the refugee column toward a saddle between two peaks where, if she knew Riders, and she did, a larger force would be waiting.

She laid it out for the group around the fire, drawing lines in the snow with a stick. The shepherd swore. Ilse said, "So we fight," in the tone of a woman ordering bread, and the shepherd said, "Ilse, you can't walk to the privy without resting," and Ilse said, "I'll rest at the top," and Sera loved every single one of these impossible people with a fierceness that frightened her.

"We can't outrun them," she said. "We can't outfight them. But—" and here she stopped, because the plan forming in her head was a bad plan, a plan shaped like her father, who had also known when running wasn't enough and had chosen instead to carry a map through a country that wanted him dead.

"But?" Edmund prompted quietly.

"But Riders drink a lot of water," Sera said slowly, thinking aloud, "and they refill at streams, and they don't check what's upstream, because nobody poisons a stream, it wastes poison." She looked up and found Edmund already staring at her with an expression she couldn't read. "How fast can you work?"

His face changed. It went still, and old, and she understood suddenly that she'd just asked a man to pick up the one weapon he'd spent nine years trying to bury.

"Fast," he said.

* * *

They did it at the ford below the saddle, in the blue hour before dawn.

The plan was Sera's; the craft was his. While she drew the Riders' pickets away with noise and shadow — a smuggler's trick involving rocks, echoes, and a truly undignified amount of goat-bleating — Edmund knelt at the stream with his kit spread out on a flat stone like a surgeon's tools, working by feel and moonlight.

What he made wasn't poison. That was the horror of it, the elegance. It was *almost*-poison: a purgative, a fever-draught, a muscle-relaxant brewed from mountain plants, dosed — she watched him measure with his fingertips, pinching amounts no scale could read — dosed precisely to the strength that would drop grown men into misery without killing them. Sick riders can't ride. Cramping riders can't hold swords. And dead men bring investigators, and investigators bring questions, and questions bring Corvane down on them like a hawk.

"He'll know it was deliberate," Edmund murmured, working. "Eventually. If he thinks hard enough. But he'll never prove it, and by then—" He sealed the last flask. "By then it won't matter."

"And if you dose it wrong?"

"Then I've killed forty men and damned us all," he said pleasantly, "so let's hope my hands remember their trade."

They poured it into the stream above the ford, into an eddy where the water pooled slow behind a stone, where any sensible rider would fill a canteen. Then they ran.

The refugees went up the eastern side of the saddle in the dark, the strong carrying the weak, Ilse refusing help and receiving it anyway through the diplomatic fiction of leaning on people "for balance." Sera took the rear. From the height, in the grey first light, she watched it happen: the Riders reaching the ford, watering their horses, filling their canteens with the easy confidence of men who had never once been the ones tasted for.

By midmorning, half of them were vomiting into the snow. By noon, the pursuit had collapsed into a shambles of groaning men and frantic officers, and the refugee column was over the saddle and descending into a valley no map remembered, and Sera sat down on a rock and shook.

Not from fear. She'd been afraid plenty; fear was an old colleague. She shook because she had watched Edmund Marchetti kneel at that stream with steady hands and do the thing that had eaten his whole life — the tasting, the dosing, the arithmetic of how much ruin a body survives — and he had done it *for other people*. Voluntarily. The curse turned inside out and made into a shield.

Her father had died carrying a stranger's inheritance through the dark. She had spent months hating that stranger for surviving where her father hadn't.

She was done.

"Marchetti," she said, when he limped up beside her, snow-burned and exhausted and quietly pleased with himself in the manner of a cat that has knocked a jug off a table with perfect precision.

"Hm?"

"When this is over — when we get them through whatever door your map is hiding — I'm going to buy you a meal. A good one. Nothing in it poisoned, probably."

Edmund blinked at her. "Is that gratitude? Sera Thornfell, are you expressing a positive emotion? Should I fetch Ilse? She'll want witnesses."

"I'll take it back," she said. "I'll take it back so fast."

But she was smiling, and he was smiling, and below them the refugees straggled down into the hidden valley like embers scattered from a fire that refused, absolutely refused, to go out.

Then Tamsin, riding on the shepherd's shoulders, pointed down the valley and said, "What's that?" and the smile died on both their faces at once.

At the far end of the valley, half-swallowed by scree and winter, something caught the low sun and threw it back — a glint, a gleam, a line of gold too straight to be anything nature had made.

Sera pulled out the map. Unrolled it. Looked from the parchment to the glinting thing and back, and felt her heart begin, stupidly, recklessly, to hope.

"Well," said Edmund, in a voice like a man stepping off a ledge. "I suppose we'd better go meet our door."
