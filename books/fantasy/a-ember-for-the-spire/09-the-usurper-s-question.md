## 9. The Usurper's Question

Inquisitor Corvane did not believe in raising his voice.

He had learned, in thirty years of asking questions, that a lowered voice was far more frightening than a raised one. A raised voice said *I have lost my temper*, and a man who had lost his temper had lost the thread. A lowered voice said *we have all the time in the world, and I intend to use all of it*. Corvane always had all the time in the world. It was, he had found, the single greatest advantage anyone could possess over another human being: patience without limit, applied like a slow, even pressure, the way snow brings down a roof not by striking it but simply by continuing to fall.

So when the taster-master was shown into his study—shown, not dragged; Corvane disliked dragging, it suggested urgency—the Inquisitor rose from his desk, offered a chair, poured two cups of wine from his own decanter, and pushed one across the polished oak.

"You will notice," Corvane said pleasantly, "that I am drinking first."

Master Hollis stared at the cup. He was sixty years old and built like a flour sack, with ink-stained fingers and the perpetually offended expression of a man who had spent four decades telling kings what they could not eat. He had been taken from his bed three nights ago in his nightshirt, and someone had finally allowed him a proper coat. His hands shook around the wine.

"I don't—" Hollis began.

"You were going to say you don't know anything." Corvane sat back down. "Of course you don't. That's why you're here. Men who know things lie to me, and lying is tedious. Ignorance is so much more honest. Drink, Master Hollis. If I wanted you dead you would already be dead, and if I wanted you poisoned you would be *wishing* you were dead, which is worse, and neither option serves me. What serves me is information, and you are going to give it to me at whatever pace your conscience requires. I have cleared my calendar."

Hollis drank. His hand steadied somewhat.

"Good," said Corvane. "Now. Tell me about Edmund Marchetti."

* * *

What Corvane knew—and he made a point of knowing everything, because everything was the only amount worth knowing—was this:

Nine days ago, the food-taster Edmund Marchetti had walked out of the palace through the kitchens, paused at the guard post to hand over a letter of resignation that cited failing eyesight and a desire to return to his cousin's farm in the south, and had subsequently vanished from the face of the earth. No cousin existed. No farm existed. The handwriting on the letter was Marchetti's own, which meant nothing, because a man who tasted poison for nine years could certainly forge a resignation. The paltry savings in his garret had been left untouched, along with every stick of furniture and a remarkably ugly ceramic dog that Corvane now kept on his desk as a paperweight. A man who took nothing and left everything was either dead or running, and there was no body.

There was also the courier.

The courier had been found dying in the Thornfell foothills eleven days ago, and had been brought to the capital for questioning, and had died before reaching the city gates, which Corvane considered a professional embarrassment. But the courier had a daughter, and the daughter had disappeared the same week Marchetti did, and two disappearances that shared a week were, in Corvane's experience, almost never a coincidence. Coincidence required chaos, and chaos was loud. These vanishings had been quiet. Quiet things were planned, and planned things had authors.

Corvane collected authors.

"Marchetti," Hollis was saying, turning the winecup in circles. "Quiet fellow. Never made trouble. Never made *conversation*, if I'm honest. Fifteen years I ran the tasting floor and I couldn't tell you one thing about him. Where he drank. Whether he drank. Whether he had a mother living or a sweetheart or a grudge against any particular noble house. Nothing. It was like employing a chair."

"And yet," Corvane said, "he survived nine years on the tasting floor. Do you know how rare that is?"

"Rare enough." Hollis's mouth twisted. "I've buried six tasters in my time. Good ones. Braver than me, God knows—I never once put the spoon in my own mouth, I'll admit that to you freely, I paid men to do it and hated myself every morning. Marchetti was the best I ever had. Not the fastest, not the cleverest. The best. He'd taste a dish and you'd watch his face and you just... knew. Henbane in the king's stew, three years back. Everyone else missed it. The cook missed it, the under-tasters missed it, I missed it. Marchetti took one spoonful, set the spoon down very gently, like it was a sleeping cat, and said, 'Perhaps we might try the pheasant instead.' Saved the king's life. Nobody thanked him. The king never even knew."

Corvane leaned forward slightly. This was interesting, though not for the reason Hollis thought.

"A poison-proof tongue," Corvane said. "A man who can detect henbane in a single spoonful. And yet he resigns by letter, takes nothing, and vanishes within an hour of a certain courier arriving in the city. Does that seem like the behavior of a chair, Master Hollis?"

"I wouldn't know what chairs get up to in their off hours," said Hollis, with the first flicker of spirit, and Corvane smiled genuinely, because he liked a little resistance. Resistance gave a conversation texture, like lumps in gravy.

"Let me ask you about his absences, then. In the last month. You noted them?"

Hollis went still. Stillness, Corvane had long ago concluded, was simply honesty wearing a disguise, and honesty could always be undressed with time.

"There were absences," Hollis admitted. "He'd begun missing shifts. Marchetti, who hadn't missed a shift in nine years, who arrived early enough to sweep the floor himself rather than wait for the sweep-boy. I docked his pay twice. I was going to dismiss him, actually. I'd drafted the letter." He looked up, and something raw moved behind his eyes. "I want it understood that I reported the absences. To the steward. In writing. Whatever he's done, I did my duty."

"You did," Corvane agreed soothingly. "Your reports are precisely what confirmed my timeline. You've been enormously helpful, Master Hollis, in ways I suspect you haven't yet grasped. Now. The last question, and then you may rest." He folded his hands. "Twenty-two years ago, you served briefly in the provincial archives at Coldharbour, cataloging seized documents. Tell me what you cataloged."

And there it was—the flinch. Small, quickly mastered, but Corvane saw it the way Marchetti presumably saw henbane: one spoonful, and he simply knew.

"I don't remember," Hollis whispered.

"That's not true, but it's a lovely attempt. Let me help you." Corvane rose, crossed to the tall cabinet against the eastern wall, and withdrew a slim folder tied in grey ribbon. He set it on the desk but did not open it. "You cataloged maps, Master Hollis. Sequestered cartography. Documents removed from circulation by order of the Crown following the Unification. Among them, you handled—briefly, carelessly—a survey map of a mountain range that officially does not exist. A map bearing, in its corner, a wax seal that should not exist. You are one of perhaps six people alive who touched that map and lived. Which means you are one of perhaps six people alive who could have told someone else it existed." He untied the ribbon with great ceremony. "Or told someone else that the seal on it opens only to royal blood."

Hollis's cup cracked in his grip. Wine spread across the oak like a wound.

"I never—" His voice broke. "I never spoke of it. Twenty years, I never—"

"No," Corvane said gently. "I believe you. You're not the sort of man who speaks of things. You're the sort of man who writes them down and hides the writing, because memory frightens you and paper feels safer." He opened the folder and turned it around. Inside lay a page of Hollis's own cramped hand, a list of archival numbers, and beneath the numbers, in ink faded nearly to brown: *the sealed range — ask the old surveyor? V-something?*

"You wrote 'V-something,'" Corvane said. "That is the most dangerous pair of syllables in this kingdom, and you wrote them down. Someone read them. Someone followed the thread from your archive list to the courier who carried such maps, and thence to whoever the seal answers to. Your carelessness, Master Hollis, has started a fire. Fortunately—" he smiled, warm as a hearth, "—I am very good with fires."

Hollis wept quietly into his ruined sleeve. Corvane let him. Tears were honest, and honesty was restful after a long day of lies.

* * *

That night, alone in his study with the ugly ceramic dog and a single lamp, Corvane permitted himself to remember.

He had been twenty-six when they gave him Vessany.

Not the name—names came later, names were the last thing killed—but the place. The Green Spire valleys between the Thornfells and the sea, forty thousand souls who had the misfortune to occupy land the Crown suddenly required and a history the Crown suddenly couldn't tolerate. His commission had come in a plain envelope with no signature: *reduce, consolidate, erase.* Three words, and he had built a career on them.

They had not burned Vessany. Burning was amateur work; burned things were mourned, and mourned things were remembered, and remembered things grew back like weeds. No—he had done it properly. He had bought the historians first. Then the priests. Then the mapmakers. Within five years, no document in the kingdom named the place; within ten, no schoolchild had heard of it; within fifteen, the refugees themselves had scattered into foreign cities where their grandchildren spoke the usurper's tongue and called the old country *the mountains over east*, if they called it anything at all. He had erased a kingdom with ink and patience and not one tenth the blood everyone assumed. People imagined erasure was violence. Erasure was mercy. He had always believed that, and believed it still. A conquered people suffered and raged. An erased people simply ceased, painlessly, the way a dream ceases at waking. He had spared forty thousand descendants a war they could not win, and given them instead the gift of being no one, which was the safest thing a person could be.

Except.

Except that erasure, done properly, required maintenance. A weed pulled badly grows back at the root. Somewhere in those forbidden valleys, someone had kept a seal alive, a bloodline unextinguished, a door unbricked—and twenty-two years later the debt of his negligence had come due in the person of a food-taster with a poison-proof tongue and a forged letter about a cousin's farm.

Corvane did not regret this. Regret was for men with shorter calendars. But he felt something adjacent to regret as he studied Marchetti's file—thin as it was, pathetically thin, a service record and a wage ledger and nothing else. Nine years in the palace. No vices recorded. No friendships recorded. No *anything* recorded. The man had made himself into exactly what Corvane's life's work demanded of everyone: nobody. And it had not saved him. It had, in fact, doomed him, because a nobody could be awakened, and a sleeping man with royal blood was infinitely more dangerous than a proud man with it.

*I made him,* Corvane thought, with something like professional appreciation. *My erasure built his hiding place. And now my erasure must go find him and finish the job properly.*

There was a symmetry to it that he found almost beautiful.

* * *

The report from the Thornfell garrison arrived at dawn, and it was bad, and Corvane read it twice with mounting, unhurried displeasure.

The hidden village in the high valleys—there had been a village, of course there had been a village, roots always found water somewhere—had been burned. By Riders acting without orders. Two days after Marchetti and the girl had passed through it, which meant the village had contained witnesses, and the Riders had converted all the witnesses into ash, and ash testified about nothing. Corvane sat very still for a long moment with the report flat on his desk.

Then he rang the small silver bell.

Captain Drewe appeared, grey-cloaked, snow in her hair, smelling of horse and cold iron. She had ridden through the night. He offered her wine; she declined, as she always did.

"Drewe," Corvane said. "Explain to me the difference between an interrogation and a bonfire."

She held his gaze. She was one of perhaps four officers who understood that these rhetorical questions had actual answers, and that providing them was survivable. "An interrogation leaves you someone to question afterward."

"Precisely. And what did Captain Ollart leave me at Thornfell?"

"Ashes, sir."

"Ashes." Corvane turned the report so she could see it, though she had certainly written half of it. "Forty houses, a shrine, a mill, and possibly the only living speakers of a language I spent two decades burying. Ollart has given our quarry martyrs, Drewe. Martyrs are *memorable*. Memory is the enemy. Every refugee in that column is now carrying a story, and stories are the one cargo I cannot confiscate at a checkpoint." He pinched the bridge of his nose, wearily, letting her see the weight of command. "Where is Ollart?"

"Suspended. Facing inquiry."

"He'll be reassigned to a coastal customs post, where his enthusiasm can be applied to inspecting barrels of herring. Have him escorted off my operation today. And double the riders on the high passes—but Drewe." He waited until she met his eyes. "No fires. No summary hangings. We do not make legends. Legends escape. We take prisoners, we take testimony, and above all we take our time. Marchetti is moving toward something. A man with nowhere to run runs *toward*—toward family, toward refuge, toward hope. Find what he's running toward and stand in front of it, politely, and wait. Patience, Captain. Erasure is mercy, but it is slow mercy, and slow is the whole virtue of it."

"Yes, sir." She hesitated at the door. "There's one more thing. The smuggler roads out of Thornfell converge on a single pass before the far slopes. Old trade route. Locals call it the Pass of Names."

"Why?"

"Folk custom. Something about the dead speaking there. The mountain people used to carry their dead up it, before the Unification. Before—" She stopped herself, gracefully.

"Before there was any such thing as Unification," Corvane finished softly. "Yes. Of course the dead speak there. Dead things speak loudest right after you try to bury them." He waved her out. "Put watchers on the pass. And send word ahead to the ford at Silverwater—I want provisions requisitioned, quietly, at every crossing south of it. If our friends are thirsty, I should like to decide what they drink."

* * *

Alone again, Corvane drew a fresh sheet of paper and began, in his beautiful clerk's hand, the memorandum he would deliver to the palace by noon. *The situation is contained.* It was not false. Contained merely meant the fire was in the stove and not the curtains, and he intended, with infinite patience, to close the stove door.

At the bottom of the page he paused, pen hovering, and then added a line he had written only once before, twenty-two years ago, at the start of his first commission:

*Recommend full erasure protocols. The alternative is remembering, and remembering, sire, is how wars begin.*

He blotted it, set the ugly ceramic dog atop the finished stack, and looked out the window at the mountains, blue and patient in the distance, hiding their one remaining door.

"Run toward your hope, Mr. Marchetti," he murmured to the glass, kindly, almost fondly. "I will be standing in front of it. And I have cleared my calendar."

Far to the east, past the passes and the fords, snow was falling on a column of refugees who carried, unknowingly, the last ember of a kingdom—in bread recipes, in lullabies, in the blood of one forgettable man. Corvane could not see them. He did not need to. Snow falls everywhere eventually. One needed only the patience to keep falling.
