## 10. Smuggler's Gospel

The smugglers made camp in a hollow that smelled of pine resin and wet donkey, and their leader received them sitting on an overturned crate with a pipe clenched in her teeth like she was daring the world to ask her to put it out.

Her name, Sera said, was Maretta Voss, and she had been running goods through the Thornfell passes since before Edmund had learned to keep his face still at a poisoned table. She was perhaps fifty, built like a barrel with opinions, and she looked Edmund up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman pricing a horse she intended to buy cheap.

"So," she said around the pipe. "This is the famous heir."

"There's nothing famous about him," Sera said quickly.

"Good." Maretta spat into the dirt. "Famous people cost money."

They were eleven days out of the burned village, eleven days of leading one hundred and forty-odd refugees deeper into the mountains on roads that weren't roads, and everyone was hungry, and two children had coughs that wouldn't quit, and winter was coming down off the peaks like a debt collector. The smuggler caravan — forty mules, thirty fighters, and a quantity of contraband brandy that could have drowned a bishop — was the first good luck they'd had since the ashes.

Edmund had learned, over nine years at court, to read a room before he spoke in it. He read this one now: the way Maretta's lieutenants stood slightly behind her shoulders, the way her eyes kept ticking toward the map case on his hip, the way the whole hollow went just a little too quiet when he sat down.

She wanted something. Good. So did he.

"I'll come to it plainly," Maretta said, taking the pipe from her mouth and pointing it at him like an accusation. "You've got a hundred and fifty mouths that need moving through my passes, past checkpoints I've spent twenty years greasing, to doors I'd have to open with bribes I can't afford. That's not charity work, taster-man. My people eat because we charge for what we do."

"Name your price," Edmund said.

"The map."

The word dropped into the fire pit between them and sat there, glowing.

Sera made a sound like a kettle coming to boil. Edmund put a hand on her wrist without looking at her — a habit now, four days old, ever since she'd nearly gutted a looter behind the refugee column — and kept his eyes on Maretta.

"No," he said.

"You haven't heard terms."

"It isn't for sale at any terms. It's the only copy of the route through the Ember Gate country. Without it, these people are wandering the Thornfell in November with a coughing child and a prayer."

"Then you've got a problem, haven't you." Maretta tapped her pipe against the crate. "Because I've got the only mules, the only guides, and the only men in these hills who won't sell you to the Grey Riders by Thursday. And my price is paper."

"Why the map?" Sera demanded. "You've never even seen what's on half of it. It's mostly riddles and dead language—"

"Paper's paper, girl. A map that opens a kingdom nobody remembers? There's a man in Corvane's own customs house who'd pay me its weight in gold just to burn it. Or—" she smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, "—there's folk in the free cities who'd pay more to keep it. Either way, I eat either way. That's all a gospel is, girl: somebody's story, priced per soul."

Edmund felt the old numbness settle over him, the courtroom calm. Nine years of swallowing poison while kings watched had taught him exactly one transferable skill: how to sit very still while the world tried to shake something loose from him.

"The map stays with me," he said quietly. "But I'll give you something better than paper. Give me a day with your quartermaster's stores and your cook. I'll find every bad cask, every watered keg, every sack of flour someone's cut with chalk dust, and everything in your inventory that would poison a man if he sneezed at it wrong. I'll clean your whole caravan, and I'll do your tasting for the rest of the journey. Nobody robs a smuggler faster than bad brandy and worse enemies. You know it. I know it. That's worth more than a map you can't safely show anyone."

Maretta looked at him for a long moment, pipe smoke curling up past her eyebrows.

"He does this thing," she said to Sera, conversationally, "where he sounds reasonable, and somehow you end up feeling like *you're* the one being difficult."

"Yes," Sera said. "He does that."

"Annoying." Maretta put the pipe back in her teeth. "Here's my counteroffer, taster-man. You keep your map tonight. Think on it. Tomorrow morning I want an answer, and if the answer's no, then me and my mules will be gone before your refugees finish their porridge, and you can explain to those children why their cough got so much worse in the snow."

She stood, joints cracking like green wood, and walked away. The audience was over.

* * *

"She's going to say yes to the map," Sera said that night, low, at the edge of the camp where the firelight didn't reach. "I watched her. She's already counting the money."

"Then I'll say no tomorrow, and we'll lose the caravan, and we'll walk." Edmund pulled his cloak tighter. The cold had found the seam under his arm hours ago and settled in like a tenant. "We did it before the smugglers came. We'll do it after."

"With the Riders three days behind us? With winter—"

"Sera. The map is the only thing standing between these people and oblivion. Not discomfort. *Oblivion.* If it goes into Maretta Voss's strongbox, it gets sold or burned within the year, and every person in this camp becomes a rumor that died in the mountains. I've spent nine years being forgettable on purpose. I'm not going to hand the whole country over to be forgotten again for the price of some mules."

She was silent a moment. In the dark, her face was just an arrangement of shadows, but he knew the shape of her jaw when it set. He'd been cataloguing it without meaning to — the set jaw, the crooked eyebrow, the way she tucked hair behind her ear right before she decided to argue.

"Maretta's not the only one who's thought about selling it, you know," she said. "Half this camp knows what you're carrying. Old Brenn told his brother-in-law, who tells everyone. You guard it like a dragon guards a hoard, and dragons get killed, Edmund."

"What would you have me do? Nail it to a tree?"

"I'd have you trust somebody once in your life!" It burst out of her louder than she meant; somewhere behind them, a baby started crying at the noise, and she winced. Her voice came back down, tight as wire. "You hide things. You hide the map, you hide the plan, you hide — I don't even know what, half the time. You taste everything before you swallow it, including words. Do you know how exhausting it is to travel beside a locked door?"

Something in the sentence caught him wrong, snagged like a fishhook below the ribs, and when he spoke, his voice came out flatter than he intended.

"You want honesty? Fine. Here's some." He turned to face her fully. "Locks are how I stayed alive. Every friend I might have made at court was a liability I couldn't afford, every kindness was somebody testing whether I could be bought, and the only person in that palace who ever chose me for anything was a dying stranger bleeding out on the kitchen floor. Trust is a skill, Sera, same as anything else. Nobody ever taught me mine. So no — I don't open easily. But I have never once lied to you, and I have never once risked this map for my own sake, and if that makes me exhausting, then I'm sorry, but I'll take exhausting over dead, because dead is what happens to careless heirs."

"And what about careless couriers?"

The words landed like a slap. Sera's breath was coming fast now, and he realized — too late, always too late, he could detect henbane in a stew at forty paces but never the moment a conversation turned to poison — that they had finally arrived at the thing they'd been circling for eleven days.

"Your father didn't die because he was careless," Edmund said carefully.

"Didn't he?" Her laugh was awful. "He left the safe house. He *ran*, through three checkpoints, with Riders on his heels, to deliver a piece of paper to a food-taster he'd never met. Because of a name somebody whispered to him. 'The heir lives. The taster.' He bet his life on a whisper, Edmund, and lost, and you want to know why I don't sleep? Because I keep doing the sum. If he'd stayed hidden another week. If he'd sent the map with anyone else. If you had never existed, or never been findable — he'd be alive. That's the arithmetic. Every night. Three in the morning. Every night since the village burned and I had time to think again."

The fire cracked somewhere behind them. Edmund found that his hands had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with November.

"You're right about one thing," he said. "It's arithmetic. But you've done it wrong."

"Don't you dare—"

"He wasn't running to me, Sera. He was running *past* every other option to reach me, because I was the last name on the list, not the first. Your father was a careful man — I saw him choose a hiding place while dying, saw him pick the one door the guards wouldn't check first. Careful men don't spend their lives on a whim. They spend them on a calculation. Which means he'd already tried everything easier, and it hadn't worked, and I was what remained." He swallowed. His throat felt strange. "I've done that sum too. Every night. Three in the morning. Why me? Why should a stranger's blood buy my inheritance? I never asked for any of this. Some nights I'd give it back if I could, just to make the ledger balance."

"Then give it back," she said, but there was no blade in it anymore. Just exhaustion. "Give it back and let us both sleep."

"I can't. Neither can you. That's the whole problem."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Sera sat down abruptly on a root, as if her legs had filed a formal complaint, and put her head in her hands, and Edmund — after a hesitation that felt, even to him, absurd after eleven days of sharing a bedroll's worth of danger — sat down beside her.

"My father used to say," she said into her hands, "that smugglers' roads were honest roads. No banners, no borders. Just: can you carry it, and will you pay. He loved that. He thought it was the only fair system in the world." She lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and dry. "And now I'm going to tell you something that's going to make you very angry, and I want you to remember that I'm telling you *before* I do it, which is more courtesy than most smugglers offer."

"Sera—"

"Last night, while you were sleeping, I talked to Maretta's second. Dario. He's soft — he has a daughter in the free cities. For half the map — a *copy*, Edmund, drawn in my hand, with the Ember Gate route left off until we're through — Maretta moves the whole camp. Everyone. Mules, guides, the passes, the works. And the original stays with you, and when we reach the Gate, you burn it yourself, in front of witnesses, so no one can ever sell what no longer exists."

The cold Edmund felt then was entirely different from the mountain's cold. It was the cold of a lock turning — the specific vertigo of discovering that someone had picked it from the outside, not to rob him, but to leave something inside.

"You went around me," he said.

"I did."

"To a smuggler."

"To a mother, actually. Dario's wife runs their stores. But yes, technically, to a smuggler." She met his eyes, chin up, braced for the blow. "Are you furious?"

He searched himself, honestly, the way he'd search an unfamiliar dish. Somewhere in him, yes, there was fury — the reflexive flare of a man whose walls had been climbed while he slept. But underneath the flare, quieter and much more alarming, was something that felt disgracefully like relief. Like a man who has carried a sack of stones for years and just discovered someone else is willing to lift one end.

"A little," he admitted. "Mostly I'm — impressed. And slightly terrified. And wondering how long you've known how to draw maps."

"Since I was six. What did you think a courier's daughter learns instead of prayers?" A corner of her mouth twitched, fighting itself. "Also I negotiated the copying clause down from a third of the map. You're welcome."

"You're impossible."

"I'm *thorough*. There's a difference. My father used to say—"

She stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, over the grave of the man it belonged to.

Edmund looked at her — really looked, the way he'd learned to look at dishes, patiently, letting the truth surface on its own — and understood, all at once, that she had not been traveling with him out of obligation, or guilt, or even hatred wearing thin. She had been doing the only thing she knew how to do with grief too large for one body: carrying something dangerous, fast, through hostile country, to deliver it alive.

"He'd be proud of you," Edmund said. "That's not comfort talking. That's assessment. I watched you hold a pass against eight armed men with a kitchen knife and a bad ankle. I've eaten at the tables of kings, Sera, and you are the most competent person I have ever met."

She stared at him. The firelight found the edge of her jaw, the twitching corner of her mouth losing its battle.

"That," she said finally, "is the worst compliment I've ever received, and I once worked for a man who praised my singing."

"Was your singing bad?"

"*Appalling.*"

Somewhere behind them the baby had gone back to sleep, and the camp breathed slow and even, and above the black teeth of the pines the stars came out the way they only do over mountains — hard, close, unhidden. Edmund realized he was smiling. It felt unfamiliar, like a coat borrowed from a larger man.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we sign Maretta's bargain. You draw, I witness, and Dario's wife checks my flour for chalk dust, apparently whether I like it or not."

"That's the spirit."

"But Sera." He waited until she looked at him. "Next time you go around a locked door — knock first. I might surprise you."

"I'll consider it," she said, in the tone of someone who had absolutely no intention of considering it, and got up to fetch the good ink from her father's satchel.

Edmund stayed by the fire a while longer, hands out to the flames, doing a new kind of arithmetic. A courier had run three checkpoints on a whisper to bring a map to a stranger. His daughter had picked a lock at midnight to keep that map alive. Whatever the ledger said about debts, he was beginning to suspect he would never balance it — and that, for the first time in nine years of careful, blank, survivable days, he didn't want to.

He was still working it out when the shout came from the northern lookout: torches on the far ridge. Not smuggler lanterns — grey, disciplined, spaced like a fence being built.

Riders. And not three days behind them after all.
