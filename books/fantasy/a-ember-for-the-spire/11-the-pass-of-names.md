## 11. The Pass of Names

The pass had no name on any map worth trusting, which was, Sera's father used to say, exactly how you knew it was a good road.

"The best roads are the ones cartographers can't be paid enough to walk," he'd told her, perched on their kitchen table at seven years old, legs swinging, while he sharpened a knife that was older than she was. "Any fool can find the King's Highway. Takes talent to find the road nobody's supposed to know."

She heard his voice in her head constantly now. On the smugglers' roads especially—the switchbacks above the treeline, the scree fields where you walked heel-first and silent, the little cairns her father had called *grandmother stones* because they marked where old women had died and been too stubborn to stop guiding people afterward. Every mile of it was a lesson in his handwriting. Every mile of it was a knife between her ribs, too.

She didn't tell Edmund that. What she told him was: "Walk where I walk, don't talk when I hold up my hand, and if the wind starts singing, don't answer it."

"The wind," Edmund said, "starts singing."

"Vessani wind. It gets into the rocks here and whistles through the cracks. Sounds like voices." She adjusted her pack and did not look at him. "The old folk called this the Pass of Names. They said the dead come up here to talk, because it's the last place before—" she waved a hand at the grey sky, the grey stone, the whole indifferent roof of the world— "before wherever dead people go instead of onward. Border country. Between things."

"And do you believe that?"

"I believe the wind sings. The rest is for people who need it to be true."

Edmund nodded slowly, in that way he had of accepting other people's beliefs like dishes handed to him across a table—politely, carefully, as though any sudden movement might spill them. He'd been doing that more since the burned village. Since the high passes. Since the night she'd watched him carry a stranger's grandmother up a slope of loose rock with his hands bleeding and his face set like a man crossing a river he already knew was too deep.

She'd stopped hating him somewhere around then. She hadn't forgiven him yet. She suspected those were two different countries, and the road between them was longer than this one.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar of stone, half a day's climb below the pass's crown, where the wind dropped just enough to let a fire live. Behind them, strung out down the mountain in a line of tiny fires, camped two hundred refugees: weavers and goatherds and one extremely opinionated cooper named Bram who had opinions about everything, including the correct way to sleep, and who was currently expressing them loudly enough that Sera could hear him from three hundred yards.

"Your people are loud," Edmund observed.

"They're not my people."

"They follow your routes and cook your food and ask you where to walk. That's generally what 'my people' means."

"They're Vessany's people. I'm just the one who knows where the rocks fall."

Edmund said nothing to that, but she felt him not saying it, which was worse. He had developed an infuriating talent for silence that weighed forty pounds. She busied herself with the fire, with the barley, with anything that wasn't his face in the firelight, which had gotten harder and harder to look away from, which was its own problem she was firmly refusing to have.

Above them, the wind found the pass's throat and began to sing.

It started low—a moan, almost—and then broke into fragments, whistles and sighs and something that was nearly words. Not quite. Never quite. But near enough that the hair rose on Sera's arms, and near enough that she saw Edmund go still the way he went still when tasting, head tilted, listening with his whole body.

"It does sound like voices," he said quietly.

"Mm."

"Like a kitchen. A long way off. Lots of people talking over each other."

Sera stared at him. Of all the things the wind might sound like to a man raised in a usurper's palace, he'd chosen a kitchen. Then again, kitchens had been the only country he'd ever lived in. Nine years among pots and poison, invisible, unthanked. Sometimes she caught him watching their refugee column the way other men watch a fire—hungry for the warmth of it but convinced it would burn him if he reached out.

"My da believed in the Pass of Names," she heard herself say, and then was annoyed at herself, because she had planned to say nothing of the kind tonight. But the wind was singing and the barley was cooking and some doors open themselves. "Not the way the elders do. He thought the dead didn't come here to talk. He thought they came here to *listen*. Said it was the one place left where anyone remembered them properly. A kingdom gets erased, the names get forbidden, and where do you go to hear yourself spoken? Up here. Where the wind keeps the records."

"That's a good belief," Edmund said. "Better than most."

"He'd have liked you." She said it flatly, to take the sting out of it, and failed. "He liked hopeless causes."

"Sera—"

"No. No, you don't talk yet. You've talked around it for weeks and I've let you, because letting you squirm was the only fun I had." She wrapped her hands around her knees and made herself look at the fire instead of him. "I'm going to tell you how my father died, properly, the way I worked it out. And then you're going to sit there and listen with that taster's stillness of yours, and you're not going to argue, because I've done the arithmetic and it comes out bad for you."

The wind sang. Somewhere downslope, Bram was still talking about beds.

* * *

"He knew about you for eleven months," Sera said. "Maybe longer. He never told me until the end, and even then he told me sideways, the way he told me everything. He came home from a run last autumn with money I hadn't seen him earn and a look on his face I'd never seen before. My da had two looks on the road: flat and watchful. This was neither. This was—" she searched— "the way he looked at me when I was born, according to my mother, who claimed he cried so hard the midwife thought the baby had died. Like something had arrived that he hadn't dared want."

Edmund's hands had gone motionless around his bowl.

"He wouldn't say what he'd found. Just asked me strange questions over the winter. Whether I thought a man could be different than what he was made into. Whether I'd ever met someone who survived by being small." Her throat tightened. "I thought he was ill. I thought he'd taken a knock on the head on some road and gone soft. I told him as much. We fought about it. One of our proper fights, where the neighbors bang on the wall." She laughed, briefly, and it hurt coming out. "Last thing I ever said to him that wasn't through a door."

The wind rose and fell. She kept her eyes on the fire.

"In spring he took the map run. Told me it was routine—salt and letters to Thornfell, back inside a week. He'd carried that map for years, I know now. Kept it under the loose brick behind the stove, and I thought it was debt papers, because what else does a man hide? He must have spent all winter learning what it was. Tracing the seal. Asking questions in port towns where asking questions gets you drowned. And then—" she made herself say it— "then he chose you."

"Sera, I never met him. I swear to you, I never—"

"I know." She looked up at last. "That's the part I couldn't forgive, at first, and then it's the part I understood. He didn't choose you because he knew you. He chose you because you were the heir, and because of *how* you lived. He'd found out the bloodline was traced, Edmund. He learned the inquisitors had it—that there was a list, and a man hunting it, and that the last name on the list was a food-taster in the usurper's own palace who'd somehow stayed alive nine years in the one place they'd never think to keep looking." She shook her head. "Do you understand what he saw? Everyone else on that list ran or hid or died fighting. You hid *inside the wolf's mouth*. For nine years. Patient as a stone. My da read that and thought: there's a man who can finish this. There's a man who knows how to wait. So he carried the map to you himself, knowing the roads were crawling, because he didn't trust it to any courier alive—including, and I quote him exactly, 'my own daughter, who has her mother's temper and none of her sense.'"

A muscle moved in Edmund's jaw. "He should have sent it with someone safe. He should have—"

"He should have what? Mailed it?" Her voice cracked upward. "There was no safe. That's the whole point. He knew the risk and he took it anyway, because that's what he *did*, that was the family trade—carrying things through places that kill couriers. And when they caught him at the ford, he did the last thing he could do. He got the map off his body and into the one smuggling door in the palace that leads to the kitchens. Your door. He'd mapped that route years ago—he told me once every palace has a salt door, because every king lies about how much he drinks." Her eyes stung and she let them. "He died ten feet from safety, Edmund, having delivered his package, and the package was *you*."

The wind filled the silence, singing its almost-words. Down the mountain the fires flickered. Edmund sat absolutely still, the way he stood when the king's spoon was in his mouth and his life hung on the steadiness of his tongue, except now his stillness was breaking apart at the edges, and Sera watched it break and did not soften, because he needed to hear all of it.

"He believed you'd reach the Ember Gate," she said. "He believed you'd open it, and get everyone out, and undo twenty years of erasure with one act. He believed it the way other men believe in gods. And the worst part—the part I've carried up this entire mountain—is that I hated you for it. Because if he'd been wrong about you, then he died for a stranger's face on a wax seal. And if he was right about you..." She swallowed. "Then he died for something real, and I lost him either way, and hating you was the only grief I could carry without dropping it."

The fire popped. Bram, miraculously, had stopped talking.

"So that's the confession," Sera finished. "My father bet his life on you. Deliberately. Knowing the odds. And I'm telling you here, in the place where the dead supposedly come to listen, because if he's listening, he ought to hear me say it: I was angry at the wrong man, and I'm not sorry I dragged you over half of Thornfell first, because you deserved some dragging." She dashed a wrist across her eyes. "But I'm not going to say sorry, and you're not going to say it's not your fault, because it isn't, and we'd both just be performing forgiveness like a mummer's show. So we'll skip it. Agreed?"

Edmund didn't answer immediately. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough in a way she'd never heard from him—not the careful court murmur, not the flat kitchen voice. Something underneath both.

"You said he looked at finding me the way he looked at your birth."

"Yes."

"Nobody's ever—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been useful for nine years. Useful isn't the same as wanted. I knew the difference very young." He turned the empty bowl in his hands, slowly, like it was something precious. "He wanted me. Before he ever saw me. He wanted me to exist." A breath that shuddered slightly on the way out. "I don't know what to do with that."

"You don't have to do anything with it," Sera said. "It's not a dish. You don't have to taste it and pronounce it safe."

That startled a laugh out of him—a real one, short and cracked, and she found she'd moved closer to the fire without deciding to, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and neither of them fixed that.

"Tell me about him," Edmund said. "Not the end. The rest. What was his name?"

"Doran," she said, and said it again, louder, toward the wind, because it seemed suddenly important that somebody say it out loud in this place: "Doran Thornfell. Courier. Better at his trade than any man alive and modest as a landslide."

The wind took the name and carried it up the pass, and whether it was only stone and air or something older keeping the records, Sera chose—for tonight, sitting next to this impossible man her father had picked out like a bridegroom chooses a farm—to believe it was listening.

They talked until the fire burned low. She told him about the time Doran smuggled a goat through a customs house disguised as a very large hatbox of pastries. He told her, hesitantly, haltingly, stories from the palace kitchens—the head cook who threw knives, the duke who required his soup to be exactly the temperature of a newborn's bath—and she realized, somewhere past midnight, that she was doing something she had not done since her father died: laughing with her whole chest, in the open, on a mountain where her enemies were three days behind her.

When they finally banked the fire and rolled into their bedrolls, Edmund said, quietly, into the dark:

"If we make it through—if the Gate opens—I'd like to find whatever's left of his grave and stand at it. If that's allowed. For someone like me."

"He'd have wanted you there," Sera said. "Front row. Probably complaining about the flowers."

Silence again, comfortable this time. Then, just as she was sliding toward sleep, Edmund spoke once more, and the ease was gone from his voice, replaced by the taster's flat attention—the tone he used when he found something in the pot.

"Sera. Wake up."

She was upright before the second word, knife already in her hand. "What?"

He was sitting rigid, staring up-slope toward the crown of the pass, where the wind sang on uninterrupted. But that wasn't what he was looking at. His eyes were fixed lower, on the dark shoulder of the ridge across the valley—where, faint as spilled pepper against grey stone, lights were moving.

Not campfires. Too steady. Too many. Moving in formation, descending toward the refugee column's flank.

"Torches," Edmund breathed. "Grey Riders don't use torches unless they want you to see them coming."
