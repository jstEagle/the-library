## 12. Grey Riders at the Ford

The river had no name that anyone would admit to, which Sera said made it perfect.

"Named fords get patrolled," she explained, crouched at the water's edge with her hood back and her breath steaming in the grey light of dawn. "Unnamed ones just get crossed. There's a lesson in that about staying quiet, if you're the sort who likes lessons."

"I'm the sort who likes surviving," Edmund said. "Lessons are incidental."

Behind them, strung out along the treeline like beads on a frayed cord, four hundred souls waited to cross. Villagers from the settlement that had burned. Smugglers with their pack-ponies and their profanity. Children wrapped in every scrap of wool the column owned, asleep upright against their mothers because sleep was the only luxury that cost nothing. The ford itself was honest enough—a wide, gravelly shallows where the river spread thin and lazy over stones—but the far bank rose into a wall of pine, and a wall of pine was exactly the sort of place where men with crossbows liked to have opinions.

Edmund crouched beside Sera and studied the water. Nine years in palaces had taught him that everything worth knowing announced itself quietly: the faint bitterness under a wine's fruit, the sheen on a sauce that meant cream added too early, the servant who smiled too much the day the king's brother came to visit. Water taught nothing. Water just ran, clear as glass, indifferent as arithmetic.

"You're doing the thing," Sera said.

"What thing?"

"The face. The one where you look at something ordinary like it's going to confess to murder." She glanced sideways at him. "It's a very unsettling face. I'd appreciate it if you saved it for things that deserve it."

"Water deserves it," Edmund said. "Everything deserves suspicion once you've worked in a kitchen. Especially kitchens." He turned his palm up in the cold. "Think about what we're about to do. Four hundred people standing in the middle of a river, hip-deep, slow as a funeral procession, visible from three ridges in every direction. If the Riders find us here—"

"They won't find us *here*," Sera said. "They'll find us wherever they find us. That's how finding works." But her mouth had gone tight, and Edmund had learned enough of her by now to know that this was how Sera Thornfell agreed that a thing frightened her: by making a joke and clenching her jaw.

He had also learned, in the weeks since the burning of the village, that she counted heads at dawn. That she gave her own rations away in increments small enough that no one noticed except him. That she still carried her father's courier's satchel, empty now, and slept with one hand on it the way other people slept holding a lover's wrist.

And he had learned—he was still learning it, the knowledge arriving in his chest like weather—that when she looked at him lately, it was not with hatred. It was with something worse. Something he could be wounded by.

*Don't*, he told himself, the way he always did. *You are a taster. Your whole art is not wanting things.*

The column began to move at first light, because Sera said a crossing should begin when the light was too flat and low for archers to see clearly across water. The smugglers went first with the ponies, cursing the cold in two languages. Then the children, hoisted onto shoulders, shrieking with what Edmund recognized with a pang as delight—the only people in the whole company who thought a river was an adventure rather than a risk.

He stood at the near bank with Sera and watched them go, and that was when the dog began to bark.

Not their dog. They had no dog. This was a shaggy, half-wild thing that had followed the column for two days and been fed by everyone precisely because no one had invited it, and it stood now on the ridge above the north road with its hackles up, barking at the trees.

"Oh no," Sera said softly.

The trees answered. Not with sound at first—with motion, the particular wrongness of branches moving against the wind rather than with it. Then the grey shapes came down through the pines like water through a sieve: riders in ash-colored cloaks, maybe thirty of them, fanning along the ridge in a line that was already, Edmund understood with a lurch, too wide.

"Not all of them," Sera breathed. Her hand found her knife. "Look. Half stay mounted on the ridge. Half come down. They want the ford. They don't need to fight us—they just need to hold the far bank until the rest arrive."

She was right. He could see the shape of it the way he could see the shape of poison in a dish: the intent beneath the presentation. Thirty Riders was not a killing force, not yet. Thirty Riders was a cork.

And behind them, perhaps half a day back, came Corvane's main host. The bottle.

*

What happened next, Edmund would afterward think of as the fastest decision of his life, which was saying something, given that his entire profession consisted of fast decisions made with other people's lives attached.

The Riders coming down toward the ford would reach the far bank within minutes. The refugees in the water were committed—turning back meant panic, and panic in a river meant drowned children. The crossing needed time. Time was the one ingredient no recipe ever provided.

But the Riders would be thirsty.

They had ridden hard—harder than the refugee column, because Riders traveled light and hated, and both make for speed. Their cloaks were dusty. Their horses' flanks were lathered even in the cold. And every rider in that grey line carried a waterskin, because every man does, because thirst is the body's most insistent ambassador—and no one, *no one*, in nine years of watching soldiers eat and drink around him, had Edmund Marchetti ever once seen a soldier examine his own canteen before drinking. Soldiers trusted their water the way priests trust their gods: completely, and until the moment it failed them.

"Sera." His voice came out strangely calm. Calm was the armor; calm had always been the armor. "How long can you keep the last of them in the water? Slow the crossing?"

"Maybe twenty minutes if I invent a mud problem. Why?" She saw his face then—the face she'd mocked an hour ago—and whatever she read there made her go very still. "Why, Edmund."

"Their canteens will be hanging on their saddles while they form up on the bank. Refill points. Horses need watering anyway; they'll have to kneel the animals at the river." He was already moving back up the bank, toward the smugglers' packs, talking fast because talking fast kept the fear in its box. "There's a plant grows on this stretch. Sera, look at me—I need your smugglers. Yellow flowers, purple stem, fuzzy leaves, grows in the shingle near water. Henbane's cousin. We called it *devil's trumpet* in the palace gardens, and the head gardener paid children a copper a root to pull it because the horses kept eating it and dying."

Sera stared at him. "You want to poison the river."

"I want to poison thirty canteens," he said. "The river's too big and too fast; dilution kills the dose. But their skins fill slow, and they fill deep." He was at the packs now, pulling them open, hands steady, heart anything but. "It won't kill them. I need you to hear that. Blurred eyes, dry throat, a heart that stumbles, confusion—a man who drinks it thinks he's dying, and some do, but most just fall off their horses and see things in the trees for a day. It buys hours. It doesn't buy graves."

"You've done this before." Not a question.

"I've *tasted* it before," Edmund said. "Professionally. Six times. Once I identified it in a plum tart from the aftertaste alone, and the cook wept, and I got a commendation and a new pair of boots." He heard his own voice do the light thing it did when the ground was dropping away beneath him. "I have never once brewed it. Which is why I need someone who knows these mountains and every weed on them, and I need her *now*."

For one heartbeat—one long, river-cold heartbeat—Sera looked at him the way she had looked at him in the alley, knife-first, certain he was a thief of maps and fathers.

Then she whistled, sharp and low, and three smugglers peeled out of the crossing line and came running.

*

They worked in the shingle below the waterline while the first Riders splashed down onto the far bank, and Edmund learned that terror is an excellent teacher of botany.

Devil's trumpet grew everywhere once you knew its face—clumps of it in the gravel, yellow blooms closed against the morning cold, roots shallow and generous. The smugglers pulled it in armfuls. One of them, a gap-toothed woman named Brey who had lost a brother to the Riders outside Thornfell pass, worked in a silence that had teeth in it, and twice Edmund caught her looking at the plant with naked hope, and knew she hoped it killed.

"It mustn't kill," he said again, kneeling on wet stone with his sleeves rolled, crushing leaves in a borrowed mortar because there had been no time for anything gentler. "Brey. Look at me. If it kills, we're murderers, and Corvane will wear that story like a banner: *the Vessani poison the wells.* We're not murderers today. We're weather. We're a bad morning."

"That's a strange kind of mercy," Brey said.

"It's the only kind I know how to make," said Edmund, and did not say: *it is my entire life, distilled. I have spent nine years being a bad morning to people who wanted me dead.*

The paste went into the waterskins—or rather, into the plan for the waterskins, which was the beautiful and terrible part. They couldn't touch the Riders' own canteens; those hung on saddles across a river full of armed enemies. But horses drank at the bank, and riders refilled skins at the bank, and so Edmund's concoction—paste cut with river water into a milky, innocent-looking slurry—went into six hollow gourds that the smugglers weighted with stones and sank in the shallows at the natural watering spots, upstream of the crossing, plugged with wax that would dissolve in ten minutes of current.

By then the first Riders had reached the water's edge.

Edmund lay flat in the shingle with Sera, forty yards downstream, watching through reeds. The grey line formed along the far bank—not charging, just arriving, settling into place with the horrible patience of men who had done this many times and never once doubted the ending. An officer's horse stamped and blew. The officer dismounted, knelt his animal at the shallows, and reached for his canteen's strap to loosen it for refilling.

Edmund realized he was holding his breath and made himself stop. Tasters didn't hold their breath. Tasters breathed evenly, swallowed cleanly, and lied with their faces.

The officer filled his skin from the shallows. Drank. Made a face—Edmund's stomach dropped—then spat, wiped his mouth, and shouted something up the ridge, and Edmund understood one second too late:

The gourd hadn't dissolved yet. The man had simply tasted river water in late autumn, which tastes of stone and rot and cold, and found it objectionable.

"He'll refill properly in a minute," Sera whispered. "They always drink twice."

They always drank twice. Nine years of watching men eat had taught Edmund that too: the first taste is courtesy, the second is appetite. The officer knelt again, upstream of the third gourd's hiding place, and this time when he drank, he drank deep, and handed the skin to the man beside him, and the skin went down the grey line the way skins do, because sharing water is the oldest soldier's sacrament there is.

Twenty minutes. That was the honest estimate Edmund had given the compound's effects—twenty minutes to first dizziness, an hour to real incapacity. What he hadn't told Sera, because telling her would have made it true in the way that spoken fears become true, was the other thing nine years had taught him: dosage was a guess, always a guess, and the guesser owed the debt personally. A taster who miscalculated died of it. That was the whole covenant. That was why the job existed. Somebody had to stand between the king and the error, and stand there swallowing.

The last refugees came out of the water ashen and silent, shepherded by Sera's invented mud problem and Brey's very genuine snarls. The smugglers took the rear with loaded crossbows, walking backward, daring the grey line to try anything. The grey line did not. The grey line was beginning, here and there, to sway in their saddles.

Then one of the youngest Riders fell off his horse entirely, and the officer—who by now was blinking like a man emerging from a cellar into noon—tried to draw his sword and dropped it in the shallows, and the grey discipline that had held for twenty years cracked all at once like ice in spring. Men lurched. Men laughed at nothing. One began to sing, high and wavering, a song Edmund did not know and suspected no one living was supposed to know. Horses, feeling their riders' wrongness, began to scream.

"Go," Sera said. "*Now.*"

The column went—up the far bank, into the pines, four hundred people flowing uphill with the desperate speed of the hunted, and it should have been enough, it *was* enough, and Edmund would have gone with them except that as he turned to run, he saw the dog.

The wretched, loyal, uninvited dog had crossed with the last of the villagers and now stood confused in the open shingle, barking at the swaying Riders as though barking were a solution to anything, directly in the path of a grey horse that had gone mad with pain and fear and was galloping blind along the bank with its rider flopping like a scarecrow.

Edmund did not decide anything. Later he would reconstruct it as a series of decisions—assess the angle, note the horse's line, calculate the intercept—but in truth there was no calculation at all, only his legs carrying him out of the reeds onto open gravel, arms wide, shouting in the big stupid voice kitchen boys used to shout across courtyards, and the horse shying, wheeling, missing him by the width of a prayer. He grabbed the dog's scruff, hurled it toward the trees—it would forgive him or it wouldn't—and turned to run, and that was when the world tilted sideways.

Not from the horse. From inside.

His own hands, he realized distantly, were cracked and raw from the mortar and the crushing. Sap under every fingernail. Sap in the split skin of his thumb, sap he'd been licking at absently for the past hour the way a man licks a hangnail, because old habits die hard and kitchen boys taste everything.

*Six times I've tasted it professionally,* he thought, with a strange floating clarity, as his knees met the gravel. *The seventh is complimentary.*

The sky went soft and doubled. The river's sound stretched like taffy. Somewhere very far away Sera was screaming his name in a tone he had never heard her use, and somewhere closer the Riders were falling out of their saddles into the shallows, singing, and Edmund Marchetti—poison-proof, forgettable, the man whose entire gift was that nothing could kill him—lay down in the shingle of an unnamed ford and finally, *finally*, tasted his own medicine.

It was bitter. Of course it was bitter. It had always been bitter. He had simply spent nine years learning to swallow it without changing his face.

Darkness took him mid-thought, and the thought was: *someone should have thanked me years ago, and it turns out the someone is me.*

*

He woke to firelight, a dog asleep heavily across his feet, and Sera sitting rigid beside him with her father's empty satchel clutched in both hands like a rosary.

"You," she said, in a voice like torn cloth, "are an *idiot*."

"How long?"

"Two days." Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious. "Two days of carrying you up a mountain on a smuggler's pony, two days of Brey brewing you antidotes from a recipe you *mumbled while unconscious*, two days of listening to whether you were breathing. The column's ahead. The Riders are behind and blind and hating their lives. And you—" Her voice broke and she hated it, visibly, and pushed through anyway. "You licked your own thumb, you absolute *fool*, after a lifetime of tasting poison for a living. You of all people. You knew better."

"I did know better," Edmund agreed. His throat felt scoured. The firelight was warm and single again, which seemed promising. "That's the joke, isn't it? I finally got to be the king."

"Don't." Sera leaned forward, fierce and close. "Don't do the light voice. Don't be the forgettable man right now. Four hundred people walked into those mountains because of what you did at that ford, and I sat by your bed for two days knowing—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter, which was somehow worse. "I already couldn't save one man I followed into the mountains. Don't make it two."

The fire popped. The dog sighed and pressed harder against his feet, having apparently forgiven him.

Edmund reached out—slowly, the way you reach toward something that might startle—and rested his hand over hers on the satchel. She didn't pull away.

"There's something you should know," he said. "Before Corvane catches up, before whatever comes next. While I was lying in the gravel, dying of my own cooking, I wasn't afraid." He almost laughed; it came out rough. "Nine years I waited to be afraid, Sera. That's what nobody tells you about being a taster. You spend your whole life rehearsing your death, and when it comes, it's almost a relief to finally attend the performance." He looked at her, at the firelight in her face, and did the bravest thing he had done since the Ember Road began: he wanted something, and let his face show it. "I wasn't afraid because for the first time in my life, I'd spent myself on something instead of saving myself for nothing."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Then, gruffly, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand in a manner that suggested she would deny it under oath: "Eat your soup. Brey made it. If you insult it after everything, she'll finish what the henbane started."

He ate. It was bitter. He drank it to the bottom without changing his face, and she saw him do it, and laughed—wetly, but it was a laugh.

Outside the little firelit hollow, far to the south, the mountains stood black against the stars, and somewhere beyond them the road bent toward a pass the old maps called by a name Sera would only say once, quietly, before sleeping:

*"The Pass of Names,"* she murmured. "We make for it at dawn. My father said..." She stopped, jaw tightening. "My father said a lot of things. At dawn I'll tell you the one that matters."

Edmund lay back, dog-warm and alive, and wondered why her voice, saying *my father*, sounded so much like forgiveness.
