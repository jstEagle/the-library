## 13. Corvane's Offer

Inquisitor Corvane had a theory about snow.

Most men hated it. It slowed horses, blinded scouts, filled boots with misery and graves with bodies before their time. But Corvane had spent thirty years learning that snow was the most honest clerk in any kingdom. It recorded everything. Every footfall, every dragged sled-runner, every place a man had stopped to relieve himself or to weep. Rain washed testimony away. Snow preserved it, in exquisite, damning detail, until the thaw or the Inquisition—whichever came first—read it aloud.

He stood at the edge of the burned village and read.

Two days old, the tracks. A column of perhaps sixty souls, moving slow, burdened with children and old people and whatever they'd managed to drag from the flames. They'd gone up, toward the high passes, which was either foolish or desperate, and in Corvane's long experience the two qualities traveled together like an old married couple.

"Riders found where they slept last night," said Lieutenant Hask, breath steaming. "Cold camp. No fires."

"They're learning," Corvane said. He crouched and pressed two fingers into a boot-print, measuring it against his hand. Large. A man's print, deep at the heel—heavier than he looked, then, or carrying more than he admitted. The same print appeared again and again along the column's edge, always on the outside, always between the refugees and the open slope.

Corvane had read enough tracks in his life to know what that meant. The man was placing himself as a wall.

"How touching," he murmured.

"My lord?"

"Nothing, Hask." He rose, brushing snow from his knee with the unhurried precision of a man who never wasted a motion, because wasted motions were how men died in corridors. "Send riders to the ford and the pass both. And have someone fetch me the taster-master's file. I find I want to reread it."

Hask hesitated. "The file, my lord? We've already—"

"We've already concluded what the file says," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "I want to know what it *means*. There is a difference, Lieutenant, and the difference is usually where the truth hides." He smiled, and Hask, who had served under him for six years and still hadn't grown used to the smile—it was too warm for the words that followed it—went to do as he was told.

Corvane remained a moment longer among the ashes.

The village had been called, according to the old survey rolls, by a name that no longer legally existed, in a province that no longer legally existed, belonging to a kingdom that had been, twenty years ago, unmade with a stroke of pens and a great deal of fire. Corvane knew this better than anyone alive, because he had held the pens. Some of them had been literal. Most had not.

People imagined the Inquisition's work was torture and terror, and certainly there was some of that—the trade required tools—but the true work, the *craft* of it, was simpler and far crueler. Erasure. You did not conquer a kingdom; conquest left martyrs, and martyrs left songs, and songs outlived dynasties. No. You unwrote it. You burned the registries, renamed the roads, reassigned the dead to other soil, and within a generation the grandchildren could not say why their grandmother wept at certain words. That was mercy, properly administered. Corvane had believed this at twenty-three, when the purge of Vessany was his first commission, and he believed it now, and the fact that he had never once lost sleep over it was, he felt, evidence of his fitness for office rather than a defect of his soul.

And yet.

And yet here was a village full of people who still sang in the forbidden tongue, still kept the old hearth-blessings, still—apparently—had been waiting twenty years for a boy who'd been smuggled out as an infant and raised, of all absurd fates, as a poison-taster in the usurper's own palace.

Corvane almost admired it. Almost. Admiration was a luxury he permitted himself exactly as often as he permitted himself sleep: regularly, but briefly, and never in front of witnesses.

He turned back toward the ridge where his tent stood white against the white world, and behind him the snow went on recording everything, patient as a god, indifferent as one too.

* * *

Edmund Marchetti came up the mountain path alone, which was the first interesting thing.

Corvane watched him from the tent flap—a thin man in a borrowed shepherd's coat, walking with the careful, forgettable gait of a servant, hands visible, head slightly bowed. Everything about him was engineered to be overlooked. Corvane had spent nine years reading reports on this man without ever once seeing his face, and now, seeing it, he understood why. It was a face built like a locked drawer. Nothing protruded. Nothing invited.

But he had come alone, into the camp of the Inquisition, and that was not the walk of a forgettable man.

They met in the middle clearing, between the cookfires and the picket line, and Corvane dismissed the guards with a flick of two fingers. Snow fell softly around them, filling the silence with a sound like pages turning.

"You're shorter than your file suggested," Corvane said.

"I'm exactly as tall as I've always been," Edmund said. "Your files were written by people who never looked at me."

"Ha!" The laugh escaped before Corvane could decide whether to permit it, and he decided, on reflection, to let it stand. Honesty disarmed. "Yes. That is rather the point of you, isn't it? Nine years tasting poison for a king who couldn't pick you from a lineup of clerks. Do you know what I admire about that, Marchetti? Patience. Most men can't wait out a winter. You waited out a decade."

"I waited out nothing. I lived my life."

"Did you?" Corvane tilted his head. "Or did you hold your breath for nine years and call it living?"

Something moved behind the locked drawer of the man's face—not anger, Corvane noted with interest. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a private thought spoken aloud by a stranger.

"Why am I here?" Edmund asked. "You could have taken me at the ford. You didn't. You let me doctor your men's water—I assume you've worked that out by now—and you let me walk away, and now you invite me up the mountain alone. That's a great deal of effort for a man you could simply kill."

"Killing you would be a waste of an afternoon and an insult to both of us." Corvane gestured toward his tent. "Come inside. It's cold, the wine is decent, and I promise you the most honest conversation you've had since you left the palace kitchens. Possibly the most honest conversation of your life. I know mine will be."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll walk back down the mountain, and my riders will harry your column through the pass all winter, and we'll arrive at the same conversation eventually, only colder and with fewer of your people alive to witness it." Corvane spread his hands. "I'm not threatening you, Marchetti. I'm showing you the arithmetic. Threats are for men who need them."

For a long moment Edmund stood in the falling snow, and Corvane watched him do the sums. He was quick—that was in the file too, buried under the deliberate mediocrity, but there if you knew how to read. Quick, careful, and lonely. Nine years of being no one leaves a specific hunger, and Corvane had built a career on knowing precisely where men kept theirs.

"All right," Edmund said.

* * *

Inside the tent it was warm, lamp-lit, and civilized, which was itself a kind of argument. Corvane poured the wine himself—two cups, and he drank first from his own, letting the gesture speak. On the table between them lay a leather folio, plain, official, sealed with grey wax.

"Do you know why Vessany fell?" Corvane asked.

"Because your master wanted its mines and its passes, and wrote it out of existence when armies proved expensive."

"That's the version the survivors tell each other around fires. It's a good story. It has villains and innocence and a stolen birthright." Corvane sat, unhurried. "Would you like to hear the boring version? The true ones usually are."

"No," Edmund said. "I'd like to hear why I'm really here."

"Straight to it. Good. I'll be straight back." Corvane set his cup down and laid one hand flat on the folio. "You are the last heir of Vessany. Not symbolically—legally, by every registry my office spent twenty years trying to burn and failing to fully burn, because people are sentimental about birth records even when they're terrified. There are perhaps four hundred souls in these mountains who carry the blood, but only one carries the *name*, and names are what doors answer to. Including, I'm told, a certain door."

Edmund's face did nothing at all, which confirmed everything.

"The Ember Gate," Corvane continued mildly. "My agents have chased rumors of it for years. A door out of the country, past the wards, into the free cities, wide enough for a nation. Your courier friend died carrying the map to it. Half these mountains are singing about it in a language they're forbidden to speak. And you, Marchetti—you're going to open it, aren't you? Feed it whatever it wants and pour four hundred people through it and consider yourself a hero."

"If I am," Edmund said carefully, "why haven't you stopped me?"

"Because I don't want to stop you." Corvane leaned forward, and now his voice changed—dropped its pleasantness the way a surgeon drops the bedside manner once the patient is asleep. "I want to hire you."

Silence. Snow ticked against the canvas overhead.

"Four hundred refugees," Corvane said, "moving through high passes in winter, pursued by three companies of Grey Riders who have orders—which I wrote—to treat every soul of Vessani blood as an enemy combatant. Do you know what those orders mean, Marchetti? They mean children. They mean the grandmother who taught you the word for bread, if she's still alive. They mean a running battle through the Pass of Names with casualties I can predict to within a dozen, because I've run this exact campaign before, twenty years ago, against people just as brave and just as doomed." He turned the folio around and slid it across the table. "Or: one signature, one public renunciation, and every one of them lives."

Edmund looked at the folio without touching it. "A renunciation."

"Public. Witnessed. You stand in the capital square and declare the line of Vessany ended, yourself a common subject of the crown, and accept a position—" Corvane smiled again, warmly, generously— "as royal food-taster. Permanent. With lands. A title, if titles matter to you; they shouldn't, they're just noise, but people like noise. Wealth. Safety. And in exchange, I burn the orders I wrote and issue new ones. Amnesty. Resettlement. The villages in the high valleys stay standing. The old tongue stops being contraband." He shrugged elegantly. "The kingdom stays erased—erasure is mercy, Marchetti, I've had thirty years to test the hypothesis—but the *people* stop dying. Isn't that what you actually want? Not flags. Not thrones. You spent nine years keeping one man alive in a palace full of people who despised you. You're not a revolutionary. You're a *keeper*. Here is four hundred souls, offered to you on a plate."

"And the price," Edmund said quietly, "is that I kneel in the square and tell four hundred people their history was worthless."

"Their history *was* worthless. That's not cruelty, it's bookkeeping." Corvane reached across and opened the folio himself, turning it to face Edmund, because the moment had come and he did not believe in flinching from moments. "Before you answer, you should know what kind of kingdom you're mourning. Read."

Edmund read.

Corvane watched him read, and this—this was the part of the work he was genuinely, privately proud of. Anyone could break a man on a rack. It took craft to break him with his own family's handwriting.

The documents were authentic. That was the beauty of it. Dispatches from the last year of the war, intercepted correspondence bearing the seal of Lord Aldric Marchetti of Vessany—Edmund's father—negotiating passage, supplies, safe conduct. Terms discussed. Borders conceded. And the final letter, dated eleven days before the capital fell, promising the crown's agents the location of the royal family's escape route in exchange for guarantees of the family's lives.

"They sold the escape route," Corvane said softly. "Your parents bought their own lives with the map every loyalist family needed. The Gate's approach. The smugglers' roads. Eleven days later the Riders swept every road out of Vessany, and the families who trusted the Marchetti seal died at the fords waiting for a rescue that had already been sold." He refilled his own cup, unhurried. "Your parents lived another six months, incidentally. Then the crown, having no further use for collaborators, closed that account too. I signed that order as well. I was younger then. My penmanship was terrible."

Edmund had gone very still. Not the stillness of shock—Corvane had seen shock, catalogued it, could produce it on demand. This was something else. This was a man holding a document very carefully, the way a taster holds a spoon, and *reading* it.

"You're lying," Edmund said at last, without heat.

"Am I?"

"These dispatches are real. The seals are right. The dates are right." Edmund laid the final letter flat and tapped one corner. "But this line here—'the eastern road, by the mill at Sorrel Ford'—is in a different hand. Look at the ink. The rest of the letter's iron-gall, ten, fifteen years faded to brown. This line is fresher. Someone added it." He looked up, and for the first time the locked drawer opened, and what was behind it made Corvane, despite himself, sit back half an inch. "You forge for a living. I taste for one. We're both professionals. Don't insult mine."

For a moment the tent was very quiet, except for the snow.

Then Corvane laughed—really laughed, short and genuine, and privately he marked the moment, because genuine laughter was rare in his line of work and ought to be savored like good brandy.

"Well," he said. "Well. Nine years of swallowing henbane and they never fed you a single forgery worth the name. Yes. The line is added. I confess it freely." He leaned back, lacing his fingers over his knee. "Do you know why I showed you a doctored document instead of a clean lie? Because a clean lie you'd doubt. A doctored truth you'll *chase*. Somewhere in your father's papers—in whatever the Gate shows you, in whatever the elders remember—is a piece of the real story, and now you'll spend your nights looking for it, and wondering, and the wondering will do my work for me. Because here is the actual truth, Marchetti, the one underneath the ink: it doesn't matter what your parents did. Collaborators or martyrs, saints or sellers—the kingdom still fell. The name is still erased. Four hundred people are still freezing in a pass while I decide, night by night, whether to send three companies up it." He spread his hands, and his voice gentled into something almost kind, which was the most dangerous thing in the room. "Despair is not the absence of hope, boy. Despair is hope, refined. Distilled. One drop, pure, aimed precisely. Your parents may have betrayed Vessany. Your king may have been a fool. Your cause may be a song sung over an empty chair. All of it might be true—and none of it changes the arithmetic on this table. Kneel in the square, and they live. Open your door, and most of them die, and the ones who survive will spend their lives in exile singing about a country that no longer exists anywhere but in their mouths. Which future does a *keeper* choose?"

Edmund stood.

He did it slowly, setting the folio down square on the table, aligning its edges with a small precise motion that Corvane recognized and, reluctantly, respected—the habit of a man who had spent his life leaving things exactly as he'd found them so that no one would ever know he'd touched them.

"You're very good," Edmund said. "The wine, the warmth, the arithmetic. The almost-kindness. I tasted poisons for nine years, Inquisitor, and the deadliest ones were never bitter. They were sweet. They were the ones that made you feel wise for drinking." He paused at the tent flap. "You're wrong about one thing, though. It does matter what my parents did. Because if they died sealing that road—if they died *keeping* the door shut to save people—then I'm not the last of a traitor's line. I'm the last of a keeper's. And keepers don't sell doors."

He pushed the flap aside, and the cold rushed in, and the snow beyond was blue with evening.

"Marchetti." Corvane did not rise. His voice was pleasant again, and beneath the pleasantness, for the first time in thirty years, ran something that a less disciplined man would have called anger. "Consider the offer open until dawn. After dawn, the arithmetic changes. I have orders, and I keep my accounts current."

"I know," Edmund said. "So do I."

He walked out into the snow, and Corvane watched him go—down the path, past the pickets, a thin forgettable figure growing smaller against the vast white dark—and Corvane found, to his mild professional surprise, that his hands were not entirely steady as he poured the last of the wine.

It was not fear. He did not permit fear. It was simply that, in thirty years of unwriting kingdoms, no one had ever refused him quite like that: politely, precisely, and with the terrible calm of a man who had already chosen, and was merely waiting for the world to catch up.

Corvane drank the wine, and set down the cup, and stepped out into the falling snow.

"Hask," he said. "Wake the companies. All three. We move on the pass at first light."

"And the offer, my lord?"

Corvane looked north, toward the black shoulder of the mountains, where somewhere above the clouds a blind warden guarded a door that answered to names, and where by morning a poison-taster would either kneel or burn.

"He made his choice," Corvane said. "Now I'll make mine. Sound the horns, Lieutenant. Let's see what his door is worth."
