## 14. What My Parents Did

The records weighed less than Edmund had expected.

Corvane had left them with him like a man leaving a coin on a dead man's eyes—there, and final. A leather folio, grey as the Rider's cloaks, tied with grey string, containing perhaps forty pages in the usurper's own chancery hand. Edmund had carried it up from the meeting place in the smuggler's hollow without opening it, the way you carry a hot stone: quickly, and at arm's length, and without looking at it.

Now it sat on the flat rock between them, and the fire Sera had built crackled its small opinion of the whole situation.

"You're not going to read it," Sera said.

"I'm going to read it."

"You're going to stare at it like it's a snake until it bites you."

"That is also a form of reading," Edmund said. "A cautious form. I was a taster for nine years, Sera. I have an entire philosophy about things that might poison you. Step one is *stare at it like a snake*."

She almost smiled. He saw it happen and then saw her stop it, the way she stopped most things that might be mistaken for kindness, but the almost was there, and he had learned to live on almosts.

He untied the string.

The first page was a summary, stamped with the seal of the Office of Inquiry—a black gate on a grey field, which Edmund supposed was meant to be reassuring and instead looked like a door that had been locked from the outside. *Subject: the Vessani succession. Re: the betrayal of the royal house of Vessany by its own stewards.*

He read the first line aloud, because his voice needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"*In the winter of the Erasure, Lord Aldric Marchetti, Warden of the Ember Road, delivered to agents of the Crown the full route of the mountain passages—*"

He stopped.

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the pass, a sentry coughed, and Edmund flinched at the small sound the way he'd been flinching at small sounds for two weeks.

"Go on," Sera said quietly.

"He's saying my father sold the road." Edmund's mouth felt strange, as though he were tasting the words rather than reading them. "The smugglers' passes. The way through the Thornfell. He handed it to the usurper's men, and that's how they found the villages. That's how—" He looked up. "That's how they wrote the country out. My parents opened the door, and Corvane walked through it."

Sera said nothing for a moment. She was good at silence in the way Edmund was good at poison: professionally, and without enjoying it.

"And you believe him," she said.

"I believe the *handwriting*," Edmund said. "It's chancery hand. It's real. The dates line up with—" He stopped again, because the dates lined up with things he didn't have words for yet. "I was four years old when Vessany ended. I don't remember the ending. I remember a kitchen, and my mother singing something I've spent my whole life trying not to hum in public, and then I remember a boat, and then I remember nothing for a long time. I have no way to argue with paper."

"Paper lies all the time," Sera said. "My father used to say the first thing a forger learns is how to age a page, and the second thing is that nobody checks the second thing."

"He was a courier. He'd know."

"He'd know," Sera agreed, and something in her voice went careful, the way it always went careful around her father, around the wound she carried like a knife she'd stopped using on Edmund and started using on herself.

Edmund turned the page.

It was worse than the first. Depositions, three of them, sworn by men with names and ranks—agents of the Crown, all, all dead now or conveniently retired. Each one told the same story in slightly different words: Lord Marchetti, cornered, his wife held, his son held, had traded the Ember Road for their lives. Each one was signed. Each one had the little flourishes that chancery clerks were trained to make, the double-crossed t's, the specific way the usurper's scribes looped their descenders.

Edmund had spent nine years in a palace. He had seen ten thousand documents cross the king's table. He knew the house style of the royal chancery the way a musician knows a scale.

That was the problem.

"Here," he said, and his voice came out flat and strange. "Here, and here, and here. The depositions are dated the third, the ninth, and the eleventh of Frostmonth." He laid his finger on the page. "But this one—" the third— "says my father delivered the route *on the road to Thornfell*, and this one—" the eleventh— "says he delivered it *at his estate*. You don't deliver the same secret in two places nine days apart unless you're a very generous man."

Sera leaned over. Her shoulder touched his. She didn't move it, and he didn't either, and both of them pretended this was about the document.

"Maybe he was being thorough," she offered.

"Nobody's that thorough about treason. And look at the ink." He tilted the page toward the firelight. "The dates were written first. See how the ink's pooled differently where the numbers sit? They wrote the frame first and filled in the events around it. It's—" He laughed, and it came out bitter, and he was surprised to find bitterness in himself; he'd thought he'd used it all up years ago. "It's exactly what I would have done. If I'd been asked, in the palace, to make a dead man confess, this is how I'd do it. Dates first, ink matched, three witnesses so it looks like corroboration. It's *competent*. It's the work of someone who's done it before."

"Corvane," Sera said.

"Corvane," Edmund agreed. "It was his first commission, the elder said. The Erasure. You don't get given a kingdom to unmake unless you're very good at documents."

* * *

They read the rest of it by the fire, taking turns, because some of the pages were heavy in a way that had nothing to do with paper, and it helped to hand the weight back and forth.

And it was Sera—not Edmund, who had spent his life among documents, but Sera, who had spent hers among maps—who found the thing that mattered.

"Wait." She had the folio nearly upside down, frowning at a page of what looked like routine logistics. "What's this?"

Edmund took it. A supply requisition, dated two days *before* the first deposition. *Provisions for the escort of Lord A. Marchetti and household, from the estate at Lowmere to the border garrison at Cael Ford.* And beneath it, in the same hand, a second line that someone had tried to scrape away: *and for the sealing of—*

The rest was gone. Scraped with a knife, the parchment thinned, but parchment remembers. Edmund held it close to the fire, at the angle that made old ink ghost up out of the surface like a body under ice.

*—and for the sealing of the Ember Road.*

"Sealing," Sera said. "Not selling. *Sealing.*"

Edmund's heart was doing something complicated. "Keep reading. There's more."

There was more. A payment record—Corvane's own expense ledger, copied into the folio, presumably by a clerk who had never imagined anyone would read it this closely, or perhaps by Corvane himself, who was so confident in his paper that he'd stopped imagining readers at all. *Disbursement: to the masons of Cael Ford, for the closing of the high passages, per the Warden's instruction.* And again, a week later: *Disbursement: to the masons of Cael Ford, second course of the closure, the Warden having refused to surrender the final key until the works were complete.*

"The Warden's instruction," Edmund said slowly. "His instruction. They were building what he told them to build."

"He wasn't selling the road," Sera said. Her voice had gone quiet and fierce. "He was *walling it up*. Behind them. He gave Corvane a route that didn't exist anymore, and Corvane's agents walked into mountains that had been closed behind them, and when it all came apart, when the Erasure happened anyway because there were other ways through that your father couldn't close—"

"—Corvane needed a traitor," Edmund finished. "You don't erase a kingdom cleanly. You need the story to have a villain who isn't you. So he made one. He made two." He looked at the fire. "He made four. My mother's in here too. *Complicit by marriage.* Four words. She sealed the last of the passages herself, did you know that? The elder said—she went in with the masons. Held the lamp. My father wouldn't let her stay behind."

The fire crackled. Sera reached over and, with great deliberation, turned the folio face-down on the rock, as if it were a rude guest.

"They died sealing the map's route," she said. "The very route we're walking. Your parents didn't open the door for Corvane. They tried to lock it, and they died doing it, and he's been carrying their confession around for twenty years waiting for an heir coward enough to believe it."

Edmund found that he was crying, a little, without drama, the way a roof leaks—just a slow seep he couldn't stop and didn't particularly care to hide. Sera, to her eternal credit, pretended to be very interested in the fire.

"I spent nine years being nobody," he said. "Nine years perfecting it. And the whole time, the reason I was nobody—the reason my parents put me on a boat with no name and no history and no *anything*—was so that I'd live long enough to be somebody. And I did it so well that when the moment came I almost took Corvane's word over theirs."

"You didn't."

"I almost did. I stood in that hollow and I felt it—the *relief*. Do you understand? Twenty years of being guilty of something I didn't do, and he was offering me the end of it. Sign the renunciation, take the pension, be a comfortable dead man in a warm palace. And some part of me—" he laughed, wetly— "some part of me that's very tired wanted to believe my parents were traitors, because traitors I could understand. Traitors fit in a world where the good thing to do is survive."

Sera was quiet for a while. When she spoke, she was looking at the fire, and her voice was rough.

"My father believed in you," she said. "Rode half the country with a map sewn into his coat, got himself killed for it, and I spent a month wanting to gut you for it. And now I find out his faith was in the son of the man who built the road he died on." She shook her head. "It's a small country, the dead. Everyone in it knows everyone."

"Sera—"

"Don't." But she said it gently. "Just—don't be a traitor's son, Edmund. Be a warden's. There's a difference, and I think you've just now worked out what it is."

* * *

Corvane came at dusk, as he had promised he would, alone but for the two Riders who stayed at the mouth of the hollow with the particular stillness of men who are not guards but witnesses. He carried no weapon that Edmund could see, which was its own kind of weapon. He carried the renunciation instead—a single page, fair-copied, with a space at the bottom for a name.

Edmund had spent the afternoon composing his refusal in seventeen different ways, most of them eloquent, one of them involving the word "viper" in a way he was rather proud of. What he actually said, when the moment came, was:

"No."

Corvane raised an eyebrow. "You've read the folio."

"I've read it *closely*," Edmund said. "That was my mistake, and it's going to be yours. You wrote the dates first, Inquisitor. Ink pools. Parchment remembers. And your own expense ledger says *sealing* where you wanted the world to read *selling*." He held up the requisition page, the ghost-ink facing the last of the light. "My father didn't betray Vessany. He died trying to save the one thing worth saving, and you've spent twenty years making him confess to it from the grave. I won't help you. I won't sign. And I'll tell you something else for free, since you value documents so much: paper is only as true as the last person who checks it. I checked."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. The fire had burned down to embers, and his face in that light was smooth and patient and utterly without malice, which was the most frightening thing about it. When he spoke, his voice was almost kind.

"Do you know what I learned in my first commission, taster? That mercy is a matter of scale. One family's truth, one village's memory—these are heavy things. Heavy enough to crush the people carrying them. I have spent twenty years lifting that weight from the world. Vessany's children wake up free, because there is no Vessany to be hunted for. I am not your villain. I am the *anesthetic*."

"You're a forger," Edmund said. "With an army."

Corvane sighed, the sigh of a man disappointed in a student he'd expected better of. He took the renunciation back, folded it with care, and turned to the Riders at the mouth of the hollow.

"Then we return to arithmetic," he said. "The offer was for the heir alone. The heir declines." He looked back at Edmund, and for the first time there was something in his face—not anger, nothing so honest as anger. Just a clerk closing a ledger. "Every soul in this column. Every village that sheltered them. Every smuggler, every courier, every child born on the road. Mark them all. When the Gate opens—if it opens—we will be there. And when it does not open, we will be there also."

He left the way he'd come, unhurried, his boots quiet on the snow.

Sera appeared at Edmund's shoulder. Above them, on the ridge, the sentries were already shouting the alarm down the line—riders sighted, many riders, grey riders—and somewhere below, in the refugee camp, a child started crying and a mother started singing, in the forbidden tongue, the song Edmund had spent his life trying not to hum.

He found he was humming it now. He didn't stop.

"The pass," Sera said. "They'll hit the pass tonight or at dawn. We need the Gate, and we need it *open*."

Edmund looked at the map in his hands—at his parents' road, at the final riddle inked in a dead man's hand at its edge—and felt the weight of it settle onto him, not like a burden.

Like an inheritance.

"Then we walk tonight," he said. "And Sera—whatever the Gate asks, whatever it costs—when we get there, someone has to remember. Whatever happens. Promise me that much."

She looked at him for a long moment, the firelight catching the sharp edge of her face, and she didn't ask what he meant, because she was already beginning to.

"I promise," she said.

Above the pass, the first grey cloaks crested the ridge.
