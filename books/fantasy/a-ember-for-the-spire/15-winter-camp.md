## 15. Winter Camp

The snow came down for three days without stopping, and by the fourth the refugee column had stopped being a column at all. It was a village now—a crooked, temporary thing of canvas and pine boughs and overturned carts, huddled in the bowl of a valley that Sera's father had once called the Kettle. He'd used it as a winter camp twice, back when smuggling had been simpler and less holy. She wondered what he'd say to see it full of two hundred souls instead of two hundred bolts of silk.

Probably he'd complain about the noise.

She stood at the edge of camp with her hands jammed in her armpits, watching smoke rise from a dozen fires and flatten against the low grey ceiling of cloud. Somewhere behind them, over three passes and a frozen river, Corvane's Riders were doing whatever Riders did in winter. She tried not to think about it. Thinking about it was like poking a bruise to check if it still hurt. It always still hurt.

"Sera!" Old Maren's voice carried across the snow, cracked but indomitable. "The Vessani woman's time has come, and she's asking for you."

"Why me?"

"Because you've got hands that don't shake, girl, and because I said so."

Sera went. It was that kind of camp—nobody asked twice, because nobody had the strength.

* * *

The birth took most of the afternoon, in a tent that smelled of pine resin and tallow and the particular animal warmth of too many people keeping one another alive. The mother was Ilse, who had been a weaver in a town whose name Sera still couldn't say properly—the old tongue sat strangely in her mouth, all soft vowels where she expected edges. Her husband paced outside until Edmund physically sat him down on a log and put a cup of something hot in his hands and told him, in the tone of a man discussing the weather, that pacing in circles would accomplish nothing whereas sitting might accomplish tea.

Sera heard this through the tent wall and thought: *he'd have made a good smuggler.* The whole trade was knowing when to move and when to be furniture.

When the baby came—a girl, furious and red and loud enough to wake every soul in the Kettle—the tent erupted. Maren cried. Ilse laughed and cried at once. And when they asked what to name her, Ilse looked around the tent, at the strangers who had become the only country she had left, and said a word in the old tongue that Sera didn't know.

"The first light," translated the elder Tomas, from his corner, where he'd been dozing through everything including, possibly, the labor. "In the old speech. *Aurelin.*"

"Aurelin," Ilse repeated, and the baby, hearing her name, screamed louder, which everyone agreed was auspicious.

That night there was singing. Sera hadn't expected it—you didn't sing when you were starving and hunted—but the fires drew people the way fires do, and someone started a song low and wavering, and then others joined, and it turned out that half the camp knew it and the other half pretended to. The words were in the forbidden tongue. Of course they were. That was rather the point; the usurper could burn the books and rename the towns, but he couldn't reach into two hundred throats in a snowy valley and make them stop remembering.

Sera sat at the edge of the firelight with her knees drawn up, not singing, listening. The melody climbed and turned back on itself like a road through mountains. She caught herself humming the third verse before she realized she'd learned it, and stopped, embarrassed, though no one was looking.

One person was looking.

Edmund had settled beside her at some point with the soundless competence he applied to everything—appearing next to people the way he'd appeared next to that cup of tea. He held two wooden bowls. He handed her one without comment. It contained a soup that was mostly barley and mostly water and entirely miraculous, because she was hungry enough that it tasted like a decision made by a loving god.

"What is it?" she asked. "The song."

He listened a moment, head tilted. "It's called 'The Loom-Song.' My mother sang it." He said it simply, without the flinch she'd learned to watch for when anyone mentioned his childhood. Something had been loosening in him since the village burned—not healing, exactly. More like a man setting down a weight to see whether his arms still worked. "Weavers sang it while they worked. Each verse is longer than the last. Like thread winding onto a spindle."

"That's why I lost my place."

"You didn't lose your place. You just haven't got to the long verses yet." He blew on his soup. Steam curled between them. "I could teach you. If you wanted."

"I don't have the ear for languages."

"You have an excellent ear for languages. You swear in four of them."

"That's different. Swearing is universal."

He smiled—that small, sideways smile that still surprised her every time, because for the first month on the mountain she'd have sworn the man was incapable of it. "The script, then. The old writing. Someone should know it besides me and Tomas, and Tomas is eighty-three and naps through sieges."

She meant to say no. She had a whole speech prepared about how letters were useless in a survival situation and her father had never needed more than a charcoal mark for *danger here* and a slightly different charcoal mark for *danger there.* What came out instead was:

"Tomorrow, then. Before the wood detail."

His smile widened a fraction, which was how she knew she'd been managed, and she found—to her considerable irritation—that she didn't mind.

* * *

The lessons happened in the mornings, in the lee of the supply cart, with a slate Tomas produced from somewhere and a stub of chalk that six children were already fighting over. Edmund wrote the alphabet across the slate in tall, strange characters that looked like ladders leaning against each other.

"This one's *ess,*" he said, tapping the first mark. "Like a snake standing up to be counted."

"It looks like a broken fence."

"Everything looks like a fence to you."

"Because fences mean *keep out* or *keep in,* and both are useful information. What does your snake mean?"

"*S.* Snake. Ssss."

"Don't patronize me, Marchetti."

But she copied it. And the next one, which was *el* and looked like a shepherd's crook, and the one after, which he claimed was called *em* and which she maintained was clearly a pair of mountains holding hands. The children had abandoned their fight over the chalk to watch, and soon there were five small heads bent over the snow, scratching shapes with sticks, and Edmund moved among them correcting grips the way he must once have corrected junior tasters, patient and unbothered, and Sera watched him do it and felt something turn over in her chest that she decided, firmly, to examine later.

Later kept not arriving.

The days found their shape. Wood detail at dawn. Lessons while the fire was fresh. Then whatever the camp needed—mending, hauling, standing watch on the ridge with her father's old spyglass, sweeping the white passes for a smudge of grey that never came, and never came, and never quite stopped being possible. In the evenings, soup and songs and the slow, enormous work of two hundred people learning each other's names.

On the ninth day of snow, Edmund taught her the word for *home.*

Not the usurper's word, the flat official syllable. The old word. He wrote it on the slate and she frowned at it.

"That's just *ess* and the mountains-holding-hands and—" she squinted, "—the broken fence again?"

"*Hem.* Home." He said it in the old pronunciation, and the vowels did that soft thing she was beginning to stop hating. "It's the same root as *hearth.* Same word, originally. A home is a fire that doesn't go out."

"That's a terrible definition. A home is walls."

"A home is a fire," he said, with the mild immovability he saved for things he actually believed. "Walls are just what you build around it so the wind can't argue."

She looked at him. Firelight, evening, the works. His face was lit gold on one side and blue-dark on the other, and there was flour on his sleeve from the bread detail—he'd taken up baking, of all things, co-opting the camp ovens with the same quiet authority he brought to everything, and the bread had gone from terrible to merely rustic in a week—and she thought, with the clarity of a knife going through cloth:

*Oh. Oh, no.*

Love, when it finally arrived for Sera Thornfell, did not announce itself. There was no moment of thunder, no grand internal declaration. It simply presented its bill, retroactive, for weeks of small charges: the way he saved her the crusty end of the loaf without being asked. The way he'd started waiting for her on the ridge at dusk, ostensibly to relieve the watch, actually because he liked hearing what she'd seen. The way he said her name—not *Thornfell,* the way he had for the first bitter month, but *Sera,* two syllables handled carefully, like something borrowed that he intended to return in good condition.

"Well?" he said, because she'd been silent too long. "Is it a fence?"

"No," she said. "It's a fire."

Something passed across his face—surprise, then pleasure, then a careful blankness that she recognized, because she'd worn the same expression for years after her father died, the look of a person closing a door gently so the hinges don't wake anyone. But before she could decide whether to pry it open, a child shrieked with joy somewhere across the camp because the snow had finally stopped, and the moment dissolved into the ordinary noise of the living.

They sat together anyway. Two cups of something hot. The fires burning low and steady, hearth after hearth after hearth, a whole valley full of the old word for home, spelled out in light.

* * *

It was Aurelin's naming-day feast—or as close to a feast as the stores allowed, which meant the bread was fresh and there was honey, actual honey, that Maren had been hoarding against precisely such an occasion. The whole camp turned out. Tomas told a story so long that three separate toddlers fell asleep during it, at intervals, like lamps going out. Somebody produced a fiddle with two working strings and made them sound like six.

Sera danced. She would deny this later, under oath, but she danced—once, briefly, with Ilse's husband and then with Maren and then, when the fiddler sped up and the circle widened, with Edmund, who danced the way he did everything, competently and without fuss, and who was laughing by the end of it, actually laughing, head tipped back, and she thought: *this is what they erased. Not a kingdom. This. This exact stupid wonderful sound.*

Afterward they ended up on the supply cart with a blanket that smelled of pine tar, watching the camp bank its fires for the night. The sky had cleared for the first time in weeks. Stars everywhere, ruthless with quantity.

"My father would have hated this," Sera said.

"Hated it?"

"Loved it. Hated that he loved it. He had a rule against hope. Said it was heavier than cargo and worth less at market." She pulled the blanket higher. "He broke the rule exactly once that I know of. The night he left with your map. He kissed my forehead and told me he'd be back by thaw, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do." She paused. "I thought afterward that it was fear. It wasn't. It was the other thing."

Edmund was quiet for a while. Snow creaked as a sentry shifted on the ridge. Below them, the fires glowed like embers raked over, holding their heat.

"He believed in you," Edmund said at last. "Both of us, maybe. But you especially. He told me—"

He stopped. Not the way people stop when they've lost their nerve. The way they stop when they've heard something.

Sera heard it too, a half-second behind him: the sentry on the ridge wasn't shifting his weight. He was running. Boots slapping packed snow, coming down toward the fires fast, and behind him, faint but unmistakable, the lookout bell—one clang, two, three, the signal they'd agreed on and prayed never to hear.

She was off the cart before the third clang faded, knife already in her hand, heart slamming into the old rhythm her father had trained into her. Edmund was beside her. Everyone was beside her, the whole camp rising out of sleep like a body waking.

The sentry skidded up, breath smoking, face white.

"Riders," he gasped. "On the far ridge. A lot of them. Grey cloaks—and they're not stopping at the pass, they're coming round the Kettle. We've got till dawn. Maybe less."

Above them, the stars burned on, cold and countless, and Sera thought of the old word written in chalk on a slate: *hem.* A fire that doesn't go out.

Someone was about to test it.
