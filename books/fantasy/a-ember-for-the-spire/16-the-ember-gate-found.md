## 16. The Ember Gate Found

The riddle, when Edmund finally worked it out, had been hiding in the margins all along—small letters in faded brown ink, tucked into the map's lower corner like an afterthought, the way his mother used to tuck notes into his lunch parcels: *Where the mountain forgets to be a mountain, knock.*

"That's it?" Sera had said, reading over his shoulder in the grey light of dawn. "That's the great final riddle? *Where the mountain forgets to be a mountain?* Your ancestors guarded a kingdom's last hope with the poetic sensibility of a drunk stonemason."

"It's precise, actually," Edmund said, and had the rare pleasure of watching her stop mid-scoff. "Mountains are made of stone. Stone remembers. That's the whole principle of the old script—memory lives in what endures. But there's one kind of stone that doesn't remember anything."

She stared at him. "Obsidian."

"Volcanic glass. Born of fire, cooled too fast to hold a single memory. The Ember Gate." He tapped the map, where the pass they'd followed for eleven days ended at a cliff face drawn not in ink but in something reddish that had flaked away at the edges like old blood or old rust. "We're looking for a cliff of black glass."

They found it three days later, and Edmund would spend the rest of his unremembering life, if anyone had asked him then, trying to describe the moment he first saw it—though of course no one ever would ask, and that was rather the point.

The pass opened like a held breath let go. One moment they were walking between walls of grey granite furred with snow, the refugee column strung out behind them in a shuffling, coughing mile of humanity—two hundred and forty souls, by Sera's count, plus a goat with opinions—and the next the granite simply *stopped*, sheared away as if a god had taken a bite, and beyond it stood a cliff of pure black glass a hundred feet high, catching the morning sun and throwing it back in long red ribbons, so that the whole face of it seemed to burn without heat.

The column stopped. Nobody spoke. Even the goat went quiet, which Sera later insisted was the true miracle.

"It's beautiful," said a woman named Marta, who had lost her husband at the ford and had not voluntarily spoken a word in nine days. She said it wonderingly, and then she began to cry, and half the column cried with her, and Edmund stood at the front feeling the blood-seal warm against his chest like a second heartbeat, and thought: *I did this. I brought them here. Whatever happens now, I brought them to a door that burns.*

"Edmund." Sera's voice, low, at his elbow. She was not crying. She was doing the thing she did instead of crying, which was gripping her knife hilt and scanning the ridgelines with the expression of a woman personally offended by the horizon. "No Riders. Yet. Whatever's behind that door, we should move."

"It's not a door yet." He was frowning. The cliff was magnificent and utterly blank—no seam, no handle, no hinge, no crack. Just glass, and firelight that wasn't fire. "The map says knock. But where—"

"Maybe you knock with your head," Sera offered. "Given the family resemblance to the stonemason."

He was still searching the glass for any imperfection at all when the old man spoke.

"You knock," said a voice like dry leaves settling, "with your blood."

* * *

He was simply *there*, which was the first impossible thing. An old man seated on a shelf of black stone at the cliff's base, wrapped in a cloak the color of ash, and he had not been there a moment before—Edmund was certain of it, and Edmund had spent nine years training himself to notice exactly the sort of thing that appears where it shouldn't. The man's eyes were closed, and had the look of eyes that had been closed a very long time: not the smooth lids of sleep but the settled, sunken calm of a face that had given up on light the way a retired sailor gives up on the sea.

"Blood," the old man repeated, when nobody moved. "The old way. A drop on the glass, and the door decides." His head turned—not toward Edmund, which Edmund expected, but toward the column behind him, two hundred faces, and the blind eyes moved across them as though reading a page. "So many. It has been so long since anyone knocked at all, and never—never—so many at once."

"You're the warden," Edmund said. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the one professional skill he'd ever had and had never managed to retire.

"I am the warden." The old man smiled, and it transformed his face into something unexpectedly kind, a hearth-fire kind of face under all that ash. "And you are the last of the blood. I can smell it. Vessany has a smell, you know. Woodsmoke and river-stone and bread. Your whole kingdom smelled of bread, did you know that? The bakers of Vessa were required by law to bake on coronation days so that the whole city would smell of it. A king who smelled of bread. There has not been a coronation in twenty years, and still the air remembers." He tilted his head. "You smell of it. Under the road-dust and the fear. You smell of bread."

"I smell of goat," Sera said.

"You smell of thorn-bushes, child, which is fitting, given the name you chose. Yes, I know the name you chose. Names chosen in grief are the loudest kind." The warden turned his blind face back to Edmund. "Come. The door is waiting, and it has been waiting a very long time, and doors that wait too long begin to dream strange dreams."

* * *

The blood-test was simple, and Edmund had imagined so many complicated versions of this moment—riddles recited, trials endured, some final monstrous gatekeeper to outwit—that the simplicity of it felt almost like a trick. He pressed his thumb to a small cut Sera made without being asked, without being gentle about it either, and touched the black glass.

The cliff *sang*.

There was no other word for it. A note too low to hear properly and too deep to ignore, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, and the red light in the glass gathered itself toward his hand like iron filings toward a magnet, and for one suspended moment Edmund saw—not with his eyes, but somewhere behind them—a door. A vast door of black glass veined with fire, standing in darkness, and behind it a warmth so enormous and so patient that his eyes stung, and a voice that was not a voice saying, in the tone of something that has waited twenty years to say anything at all:

*At last. At last. The blood returns.*

He snatched his hand back. The light settled, sulking, and the warden nodded as if Edmund had passed a very easy examination.

"The door knows you," the warden said. "It has been dreaming of you since before you were born. Your mother touched that glass when she was younger than you, and her mother before her, and hers before that. It knows the whole line. It has been keeping their warmth." He paused. "It is sorry, by the way. About your parents. It liked them."

"The door is *sorry*," Sera repeated flatly.

"The door feels a great many things. It has had twenty years and nothing to do but feel them." The warden rose, unfolding slowly, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, and faced Edmund fully. "So. You have come to open it. And you have brought the remnant with you. Two hundred and forty." His blind gaze swept them again. "Two hundred and forty-one. The goat does not count, but the child in Marta's arms counts twice, being new."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "You can open it. For them. For all of them."

"I can open nothing," the warden said. "I only keep the arithmetic. The door opens itself, according to its own law, and the law is old and simple and cruel the way all old simple things are cruel." He held up one gnarled finger. "For one soul to pass, the door asks a small price. A memory. Any memory, freely given. A coin for the toll. That is how your parents meant it to be used—a handful of exiles, slipping through one at a time, each leaving behind a day they could spare. A bad harvest. A quarrel. A toothache." The finger became a fist. "But the door is not a bucket to be dipped from, taster. It is a lung. It breathes in proportion to what it breathes out. To open it *wide*—wide enough for two hundred and forty-one in a single passing, before your grey friends come over that ridge, and they are coming, I can smell their horses' iron from here—to open it that wide, the door must breathe in something worth two hundred and forty-one lives."

The wind moved along the cliff face, and the red light rippled, and Edmund heard himself ask the question even though every part of him already knew the answer, the way he had always known, from the first moment in the kitchens when a dying man had gasped a forbidden name into his hands, that this road only ever went one place.

"What could be worth that much?"

"Vessany," said the warden gently. "All of it. Every memory of it, held by the last heir. The door is made of the kingdom's forgetting, Edmund Marchetti. It was built from the erasure itself—every burned record, every forbidden name, every map scraped blank went into the fire that made this glass, and the fire has been hungry ever since. It will open wide for the remnant of Vessany. But it must swallow Vessany whole to do it. Not the land—the land is lost regardless. *You.* Your inheritance. Your name, your line, your mother's kitchen, your father's voice, every hour of every memory in which Vessany lives in you. Given freely. Fed to the fire. Forever."

The silence that followed was very large. Behind them, two hundred and forty people waited, not knowing, and the goat ate something it shouldn't have.

"And if he doesn't pay it?" Sera's voice. Sharp. Steadied by fury, the way it always was. "If he opens it narrow? One at a time? We have time. We could—"

"You have until the Riders crest the ridge. A day. Perhaps two." The warden's blind face did not change. "And narrow is not free either, child. Each single passage still costs a memory of Vessany, and he has perhaps a few hundred to give, being young, and two hundred and forty-one to feed. Do the arithmetic. Some would pass. Most would not. And he would remain—a half-emptied heir, a man with holes where his childhood was, standing on a ridge when the iron comes." The warden spread his hands. "I do not make the law. I only keep the count. I have kept it for twenty years, and I will tell you the truth of my office, since no one has ever asked: it is the loneliest arithmetic in the world."

* * *

They walked away from the cliff to talk, though talk seemed suddenly like a very small tool for a very large thing, like trying to bail out the sea with a teaspoon. They climbed a shoulder of granite above the pass, and sat, and the black glass burned below them in the afternoon light, patient as a banked fire.

Sera spoke first, which surprised him. She usually made him go first, on the theory—she'd said once, in the winter camp, half-laughing—that if Edmund went first he'd apologize his way into agreeing with her before she'd even made her argument.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you decide quietly and then spend the whole conversation arranging for everyone else to feel okay about it. I've watched you do it for months. You did it with the ford. You did it with the smugglers. You're doing it right now with your face."

"My face is doing a thing?"

"Your face is doing *several* things, and all of them are noble and all of them are infuriating." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, and stared at the burning cliff. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't. He told you. The door's law—"

"The door's law is a door's law. Doors don't get to decide what people are worth. People decide that." Her jaw was set, and Edmund recognized the look—she wore it every time she mentioned her father, grief and fury braided so tightly he could no longer tell which was which. "He carried that map for twenty years, Edmund. Twenty years. He believed in this. In *you*. He believed there'd be a way through that didn't cost—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I didn't walk you through the Thornfell and across the ford and through one whole winter so the ending could be you feeding yourself to a *door*."

"And I didn't poison my way out of the only safe life I've ever had," Edmund said, "to save one hundred people and abandon the rest." He looked at his hands. Taster's hands. Steady hands. Hands that had been valued for exactly one thing their whole life, and here at the end, the value was the same: he could die well. He'd just never expected the dying to be a *subtraction* instead of an addition. "Sera. Listen to me. Two hundred and forty-one people. Marta's baby counts twice, apparently. The children born on the road who've never seen a city. The old ones who still sing in the forbidden tongue when they think no one's listening. If the price is my memories, then the price is *mine*, and it buys *them*, and there is no honest arithmetic under heaven in which I come out ahead of that trade."

"You don't even know what you'd be losing. That's the horror of it. You'd stand there and pay and never know—"

"I know exactly what I'd be losing." His voice cracked, and he let it. "That's the whole point. I get to know, right up until the moment I don't. I get one last evening as myself. I intend to spend it very carefully."

Sera was quiet for a long time. The wind came down off the pass and the black glass threw its red light across the snow, and far below, the refugee column had begun to set up camp, small fires blooming like the first stars, and someone was singing—low, in the old tongue, a lullaby Edmund's mother had sung him and which he had not heard in twenty years and which now he would never hear again after tomorrow, and knowing that made it the most beautiful sound he had ever heard, and he had to close his eyes against it.

"There's one thing," Sera said at last, and her voice had gone strange—careful, the way she was careful only when something mattered most. "One thing the old man didn't say."

"He said everything."

"He said the door takes *your* memory of Vessany. Your memory. It never said a word about mine." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "I remember Vessany too, Edmund. Every story my father ever told me, every name, every road on that map—I have it all in here." She touched her temple. "And I am not an heir. The door doesn't get to eat me. So here is what's going to happen: you're going to pay the price, because you're right, damn you, the arithmetic is on your side. And then I am going to stand on the far side of that door and remember it all *for* you. Every bread-scented coronation day. Every kitchen. Your mother's lullaby. Your name." Her voice finally broke, and she let it, the way he had. "You will not know what you gave. But someone will. That's not nothing. That's the whole difference between a sacrifice and a waste."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment—the courier's sharp-tongued daughter who had ambushed him with a knife in an alley and somehow, somewhere between the Thornfell and the ford and one whole winter, become the only person in the world who knew him—and felt something in his chest that was too large and too painful to be gratitude and too warm to be grief.

"Sera—"

"Don't argue. You'll lose. You always lose when I'm right and I'm *always* right."

Below them, at the base of the burning cliff, the warden had risen to his feet and turned his blind face toward the eastern ridgeline, where the last light was dying—and Edmund, following his gaze, saw what the old man had smelled long before either of them could see it: a thin grey thread of riders, moving fast, iron glinting, coming over the pass like winter coming down.

The warden's voice carried up the slope, dry and calm as falling ash.

"Heir of Vessany. The door is ready. And you have one night to decide what to forget."
