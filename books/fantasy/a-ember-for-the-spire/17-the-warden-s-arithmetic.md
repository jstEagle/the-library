## 17. The Warden's Arithmetic

Sera had thrown knives at men, argued with smugglers over tolls in three languages, and once talked a mountain bear out of their supper by sheer volume of swearing. None of it had prepared her for arguing with a door.

"It's lying," she said. "Doors don't get to make demands. Doors open. That's the whole point of doors."

The blind warden turned his milky eyes toward her with the patience of a man who had heard every objection the world could manufacture and outlived them all. "The Gate is not a door, child. It is a promise. And promises are paid for."

Behind him, the cliff face shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone, though the pass below their camp was knee-deep in snow. The Ember Gate. Nine years of her father's secret errands, four months of frozen mountains and grey riders and graves dug in hard ground, all of it ending at a wall of rock that wanted to eat someone's soul.

Well. Not someone's. Edmund's.

"There are other ways," Sera said. She'd been saying versions of this since sundown, and she was aware her voice was getting thinner each time, like cloth worn through at the elbow. "We could—break through. Pickaxes. Gunpowder. There's a smuggler in Lowmark who claims he can bring down half a mountain."

"Then half a mountain falls," the warden said mildly, "and behind it stands another half, and behind that the same nothing there has always been. The Gate does not open onto the other side of the rock. It opens onto elsewhere. Only an heir's blood can wake it, and only a price can persuade it."

"Then wake it narrow. You said it could open narrow. For one."

"For one," the warden agreed. "One soul slips through, and the Gate sleeps on. Cheap. Painless." His ruined eyes seemed, impossibly, to find hers. "But I have watched your camp from my ledge these three days. I count more than two thousand fires' worth of people down that slope. How many did you mean to save?"

Sera didn't answer. That was the trap of it, laid out neat as a snare-line: the arithmetic didn't care about her feelings. One life bought one escape. Everything else—the miller's family with the new baby, old Marta who sang in the forbidden tongue, the children who thought snowball fights were what freedom tasted like—all of it cost exactly one Edmund Marchetti.

And not even his death, which would at least be simple. His memory. His name. Every scrap of the man himself, fed into the fire, so that what walked through the Gate afterward would be a stranger wearing his coat.

She found him outside later, sitting on a flat stone at the camp's edge with a cup of something hot he wasn't drinking. Below, the refugee column lay scattered across the valley like spilled beads, cook-smoke rising thin and blue. Above, on the ridgelines, the night was very clear, and she hated how clear, because clear nights meant the Grey Riders could see clearly too.

"They told you," he said. Not a question.

"The warden told me. Then you confirmed it by looking like a man confirming it." She sat down beside him. Their shoulders bumped. Neither moved away; they hadn't moved away from shoulder-bumps in some weeks now, a development she had filed under *later* along with several other developments.

"There's no alternative," Edmund said quietly.

"I've made a list of alternatives."

"You've made a list?"

"A long list. Item one: we tell everyone the Gate's a myth and settle in the hills."

"They'll be found by spring. Corvane doesn't tire, Sera. You know that better than anyone—he chased us across a whole country because tiring wasn't in his commission."

"Item two," she pressed on, "we find another heir."

Edmund gave her a look. "I'm the last living one. The village elders were very thorough about that. It was practically a ceremony."

"Item three: we trick the Gate."

"With what? My charm?" He almost smiled. "I spent nine years cultivating the opposite of charm. I was specifically trained to be forgettable. If anything, I should owe the Gate rent."

And there it was, the awful joke, and she laughed despite herself—a short, wet sound—and then her throat closed, because the joke worked because it was true, because the man beside her had spent his whole life making himself into nothing, and now the universe had looked at that careful blankness and said *yes, let's make it official.*

"I hate this," she said.

"I know."

"You don't get to be calm about it. Calm is my job. I'm the angry one. You're supposed to be the one I argue out of things."

"I've never once won an argument with you," Edmund said. "It would ruin our whole dynamic if I started."

* * *

The warden's cave smelled of cedar smoke and old paper, which surprised her until she saw the shelves: hundreds of them, carved into the living rock, holding ledgers in a dozen hands across what looked like centuries. A library with one reader, who needed no light.

"The last heir who came through was sixty-two years ago," the warden said, running a finger along a spine without needing to see it. "A woman named Ilse Vessany. She carried the price willingly. Her people crossed in nine days, and she lived forty more years on the far side, married a fisherman, forgot how to say her own name." He paused. "Her sister wrote this ledger. So that somewhere, someone would remember Ilse existed."

Sera stared at the shelf. Row after row after row of ledgers.

"These are all—"

"Every heir. Every sacrifice. Remembered by whoever loved them enough to stay and write it down." The warden folded his hands. "That is the second part of the arithmetic, girl, the part people miss. The Gate takes the memory. But memory is not only held in the one who remembers. It is also *given*. What one person carries can be handed to another, like a flame passed from candle to candle. Dimmer, perhaps. Never quite the original light. But it burns."

"That's not good enough," Sera said.

"No," the warden agreed. "It rarely is. But it is what there is."

She went looking for Edmund and found him where she knew he'd be—in the sick tents, doing the small unnoticed work he'd been doing since the first week of their flight: checking the fever-water for contamination, sniffing the milk, quietly moving a dying man's cot away from a draft. The refugees had learned to call him *my lord* awkwardly and half-jokingly, and he flinched every time, and they kept doing it, because hope needed a shape and he was the nearest thing.

An old woman caught Sera's sleeve as she passed. "He looks tired," she whispered. "Tell him to sleep. Tell him—" She lowered her voice. "Tell him whatever he's carrying, he doesn't carry it alone. That's what we used to say. Before."

Before the erasure. Before the name became contraband. Sera nodded and didn't trust herself to speak.

Later, in the blue hour before dawn, she found Edmund awake again, because of course he was, sitting by the banked fire turning his mother's copper spoon over in his fingers—one of the only objects he owned from a life he'd learned about four months ago and was about to lose forever.

"I keep thinking," he said, "about what I actually remember of Vessany. Which sounds morbid, but hear me out. It's inventory. Tasters inventory everything." He turned the spoon so it caught the firelight. "A kitchen. Warm stone floors. A song I don't know the words to, just the tune. My mother's hands. That's nearly all of it—I was so young when they took me away. Four months of villages and elders and songs, and underneath it all, my actual inheritance fits in a thimble." He laughed, soft and terrible. "The Gate's getting robbed, honestly. It should charge me less."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make yourself small so it hurts less. I've watched you do it the whole time I've known you. You did it at court, you did it in the mountains, you're doing it now. 'My memories barely count.' 'I'm nobody anyway.'" She grabbed his shoulder and made him face her. "You are not spending a thimble, Edmund. You're spending *you*. The man who swallowed poison every day for nine years for strangers who never learned his name. The man who carried a dying courier three streets out of his way with guards on his heels. The man who doctored a river ford and nearly died of it rather than let Riders take a single family." Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "The man who taught me to read the old script badly enough that I laughed for the first time since my father died."

"Sera—"

"Shut up. I'm not finished." She hadn't meant to cry and was furious to find she already was. "If you're going to do this—and I can see it in your stupid calm face that you've already done it, decided it, sometime before dawn like a sensible coward—then you're going to hear the truth first. You are the best man I have ever known, and I did not get to say that about anyone for a very long time, and it is a crime against the entire concept of justice that you won't remember me saying it."

For a moment he just looked at her, firelight moving on his face, and she saw the armor slip—the numb, competent, invisible taster gone, and underneath him just a man, frightened, thirty-something, and unbearably dear.

"Then come with me," he said hoarsely. "Walk through while it's open. Forget it all on the other side with me. We'll start over somewhere new. Two people with no pasts."

"And leave two thousand people to Corvane?" She shook her head. "You'd never forgive yourself. And neither would I, because I'd be forgetting the person who wouldn't, and what kind of foundation is that for a fresh start?"

"So this is the plan. I go through. They go through. And you—"

"Somebody has to remember," Sera said. "The warden says it. Somebody has to hold the ember. Write it down. Carry it." She wiped her face fiercely with her sleeve. "I'm good at carrying things. Ask anyone. It's my most annoying quality."

"Sera, what he loses—what you're volunteering for—it's grief. Every day. Seeing me and grieving me, over and over, and me not knowing why you look at me like that."

"I've grieved worse for less reason," she said. "At least you'll be alive. At least I can make you tea."

Edmund looked down at the copper spoon in his hand for a long moment. Then he held it out to her.

"Keep this," he said. "When I don't know what it is anymore. Tell me whose it was. Even if I can't feel it—even if it's just a story about a stranger—I want the story to be true, and I want it to come from you."

She took the spoon. Her hands were shaking. "Anything else, your highness?"

"As a matter of fact." And he smiled—the real one, the rare one, the one that had taken three countries and one apocalypse to teach him. "Start tonight. While I still know you. Tell me our story from the beginning, so that when it's gone, some part of me is at least expecting it. Like a scar that aches before rain."

Outside, far off along the western ridge, something moved against the stars. A line of them. Slow, orderly, patient as winter.

Grey.

Sera stood up, spoon clenched in her fist like a knife, heart hammering out the oldest arithmetic she knew—*how many hours, how many minutes, how long do we have*—and beside her Edmund rose too, calm already, already arranging himself toward the Gate, and she realized with a lurch that dawn was coming for both of them at once, and only one of them would survive it knowing what it cost.

"Go wake the camp," Edmund said softly. "It's time."
