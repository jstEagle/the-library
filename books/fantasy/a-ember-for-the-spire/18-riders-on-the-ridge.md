## 18. Riders on the Ridge

The first thing Corvane saw when he crested the ridge was smoke — not the black, greedy smoke of burning, but the thin grey threads of cookfires rising from a bowl of snow below, where three hundred souls who should not exist had made camp against a cliff like children hiding under a blanket.

He reined in and let his horse blow, and he did not hurry.

"Count them," he said.

His lieutenant, a broad man named Hesk whose one virtue was that he never pretended to enjoy his work, squinted down into the valley. "Two hundred and forty. Perhaps two-sixty. Mostly old ones and young ones."

"And the fighting men?"

"Forty. Fifty. Smugglers, by their look. Mountain folk." Hesk paused. "And her."

Corvane smiled. It was a small smile, the kind he kept for private satisfactions, like finding a coin behind a loose floorboard. "Yes," he said. "Her."

Below, the camp had seen them. He could read it from this distance, the way he could read everything: the sudden stillness of people bent over fires, the scatter of figures toward the cliff face, the single knot of armed men forming up at the mouth of the pass with the unhurried discipline of people who had already decided how they were going to die. And among them, small even at this distance but unmistakable, a woman with a knife and her father's stubbornness standing on a rock so the others could see her.

"Riders," Corvane said mildly, "do not charge yet. Form line. Let them look at us for a while."

"Let them run?" Hesk asked.

"Let them *hope*," Corvane corrected, and there was something almost tender in it. "Hope is what makes them stand. If we charge now they scatter into the high passes and we spend a month picking families out of snowdrifts, and half of them die of cold before we reach them, which is wasteful and ugly and I will not have it. No. We form line. We let them count us. And then, when the door opens—" he nodded toward the cliff face, where, though no ordinary eye could see it, the air had begun to shimmer like heat off summer stone— "they will walk to us rather than away from us. Toward salvation. All I have ever done, Hesk, is shorten people's waiting."

Hesk said nothing. He had learned, over eleven years, that saying nothing was the only answer Corvane did not find interesting.

The Inquisitor drew his gloves tighter and watched the door in the mountain begin, very slowly, to wake.

* * *

"They're not charging," Sera said. "Why aren't they charging?"

"Because he doesn't need to," said Doss, the smuggler captain, spitting into the snow. His beard had frozen into a shape like a broken fence, and he spoke around it with the calm of a man who had been in bad situations so often they had become a kind of weather. "Corvane's patient. Patient as a spider with a bookkeeping habit. He'll wait for the Gate."

"Then we don't give him the Gate."

Doss looked at her. Around them, fifty-odd fighters were doing the work of looking brave — checking knife edges, tightening bowstrings, stamping feeling back into feet. Behind them, two hundred and forty refugees were moving toward the cliff face in a ragged column, and above the whole valley the air over the sealed door had begun to glow, faintly, the color of coals seen through a banked fire.

Sera had spent nine days learning what the Gate's waking looked like. First the shimmer. Then the warmth — you could feel it on your face from thirty paces, like standing near an oven. Then, the elder Vessani said, the door would open narrow, wide enough for one, and only the heir's blood could widen it, and the price of widening it was—

She didn't finish thinking about the price. She had made an arrangement with herself about the price, and the arrangement was: not yet.

"He wants us formed up and desperate," she said instead. "So we won't be. Doss. How long can you hold the pass?"

Doss considered the pass. It was a throat of rock perhaps forty paces wide, walled on the left by a drop that would kill a horse and on the right by a slope of scree too steep to climb quickly. Fifty fighters. Against — she counted the grey line forming on the ridge — two hundred riders, and whatever footsoldiers came behind.

"An hour," Doss said. "Maybe two if the gods are drunk and generous."

"Make it three."

"Girl, make it three yourself, if you've got the gods' ear. I've got fifty knives and a hillside."

"You've got fifty knives, a hillside, and me." She pulled her father's map-case strap tighter across her chest, out of habit, though the map was gone now, spent, traded through the smugglers' copy-negotiation into Edmund's hands and from his hands into whatever riddle-language the Gate's warden spoke. What she had left of her father was a knife, a temper, and a promise. She intended to spend all three well.

"The scree slope," she said. "When they commit riders into the pass, we cut it loose on their left flank. They'll bunch. Bunched men in a throat of rock can't swing swords."

"That'll bring rock down on our own left."

"Yes."

Doss grinned around his frozen fence of a beard. "I knew I liked you," he said. "Right. Somebody wake the gods and get them drunk."

* * *

Down in the camp, at the mouth of the shining door, an old woman named Maren was doing the last thing she knew how to do, which was feed people.

There was no logic to it. There was an army on the ridge and a miracle at her back and her hands shook so badly she spilled more barley than she poured, but Maren had survived twenty years of being nobody from nowhere, and in those twenty years she had learned one law deeper than any other: frightened people who have eaten can think, and frightened people who haven't cannot. So she stood over her cookfire in the shadow of the waking Gate and ladled barley porridge into every hand that passed, and to each person she said the same thing, the thing her own mother had said to her on the night the soldiers burned the records and the kingdom's name became a crime:

"*You are Vessany. Say it.*"

And each of them, blinking, steaming, terrified, said it. "*I am Vessany.*"

A boy of six said it with porridge on his chin. A grandfather said it and wept without noticing. A young mother, carrying a child born on the road in the winter camp, said it twice, once for herself and once in the baby's ear, so that the name would be the first thing the child owned.

Behind her, the door glowed brighter. The warmth reached the cookfire and mingled with it, and for a moment the steam rose straight up, unbothered by wind, as if the mountain itself were breathing gently.

Maren did not look at the door. She had decided she would look at it when she walked through it, and not one heartbeat sooner. Until then there was porridge, and there were names, and there was time — however much of it the people on the ridge with the knives would buy her.

* * *

On the ridge, Corvane waited, and the waiting itself was a weapon.

He had done this arithmetic before. Twenty years ago, in another winter, in another country that had needed to stop existing, he had stood on a similar height and watched a similar column of the condemned, and he had learned then the lesson that had made his career: armies break doors, but patience breaks *people*. Give the desperate an hour to imagine escape and they will fight like demons for it. Give them three hours, and hope goes soft and rotten in their hands, and they begin to bargain.

At the second hour, as if summoned by his expectation, a rider came down from the pass under a scrap of white cloth, walking his horse carefully through the snow. Smuggler's garb. Doss himself, by the fence-shaped beard.

"Inquisitor." Doss stopped at a respectful distance and did not dismount, which Corvane noted and forgave; a man negotiating for his life likes to keep his horse between himself and the arithmetic. "You've got two hundred riders and all day. We've got fifty knives and a door. Nobody here wants to spend blood today."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Nobody ever does. That is rather my point."

"Terms, then. You let the old ones and the little ones walk through that door — I don't care what's behind it, that's not my business — and the fighting men lay down arms and come with you. Prisoners, trials, whatever your law does. You get your criminals. They get their grandmothers."

Corvane regarded him for a long moment with genuine, almost scholarly interest.

"Do you know," he said, "that is the fourth such offer I have received in my career, and the first from a smuggler. Usually it is priests." He let the silence sit. "No."

"No?"

"There is no version of today, Captain Doss, in which some of these people live and some die, and I choose which. That arithmetic is precisely what I am here to erase. A crime is not divided by mercy; it is ended or it is not. Either the door opens for all of them and closes behind them forever — in which case I have failed, and I do not fail — or it opens for none." He smiled his small private smile. "But thank you for coming down. It saved me sending for you, and I confess I wanted to see whether courage had frozen off you yet. It hasn't. That must be painful."

Doss sat very still. Then he said, quietly, "You're going to kill all of them."

"I am going to *end* them," Corvane said, with the gentleness of a man correcting a translation error. "There is a difference, and someday, if you are very lucky, someone will explain it to you." He waved a gloved hand, almost courteously. "Go back down. Tell them I'm coming at dusk. Let them eat."

Doss turned his horse. At ten paces he stopped, and without turning around said: "My father sold salt on the Vessany road. Before. He said it was the only country where a smuggler paid full tolls, because the king said honest men shouldn't need tricks." He nudged the horse on. "I remember it. Whatever you erased, some of it stuck in the cracks."

Corvane watched him go and felt, briefly, the old familiar ache — the one he had learned to call certainty, because calling it doubt had nearly killed him once, twenty years ago, in the snow, in front of a woman who would not say the forbidden name even with a sword at her throat.

"Dusk," he told Hesk. "Not before. Let them finish their supper."

* * *

They came at dusk, and the pass drank.

Later, the survivors would tell it different ways — everyone always did — but the shape of it was this: the grey riders came down into the throat of rock in columns of four, torches lit, banners high, because Corvane wanted them to see the banners, wanted the erasure to wear its own face. And for the first hundred paces nothing happened at all, which was worse than anything, until the first arrows came off the scree slope like a sudden thought, and the front rank folded, and Doss's voice roared something obscene and magnificent, and the hillside itself came down.

Sera had cut the scree herself, with Doss and six others, ropes and levers and swearing, in the hour after the parley. Now she crouched behind a boulder with her father's knife in her fist and felt the mountain shudder and heard the riders scream — horses first, always horses first, and she hated that she noticed — and then the pass was a tangle of grey and red and falling stone, and the second column couldn't advance past the first, and for eleven minutes, she counted them, eleven minutes belonged to the fifty.

Eleven minutes is a long time. Eleven minutes is two hundred people walking through a doorway.

She fought in the gaps between boulders, quick and low, the way her father had taught her — never the duel, girl, always the arithmetic; make them pay more for you than you're worth — and somewhere in the seventh minute a sword opened her sleeve and the skin beneath, and she barely felt it, because ahead of her, up the pass, the Gate had begun to blaze.

It was no longer a shimmer. It was a door of light standing open in the cliff face, tall and narrow, wide enough for one — and the refugees were funneling toward it in a line that moved with terrible, orderly patience, grandmother after child after grandfather, each stepping through into a brightness none of them could see into, each saying, as they went, the words the old woman at the cookfire had taught them.

*I am Vessany. I am Vessany.*

And at the threshold, holding the door steady with both bloody hands, stood Edmund.

Even from sixty paces, even in battle-smoke, Sera saw it: he was grey-faced, swaying, spending something no blade could touch, and the Gate drank from him visibly, light running off his fingers like water off a wheel. The warden stood beside him, blind eyes lifted, murmuring the old words. Narrow, the door stayed. Narrow, because the price of wide had not yet been paid.

Because *he* hadn't paid it yet.

"Sera!" Doss grabbed her arm, bleeding from the scalp, half-laughing, half-weeping. "Minute twelve! They're regrouping! Minute thirteen, they come again!"

"Then we buy minute thirteen," she said, and spat blood into the snow, and went back to the rocks.

* * *

Corvane entered the pass himself at minute fifteen.

He did not often do this. But he had watched eleven minutes of his careful arithmetic burn, and he had seen the door — truly seen it, its light striking his face like a memory he had spent twenty years burying — and something in him had snapped taut as a wire. He walked his horse past the wreckage of the first column, past the moaning and the dead, and forward, and the sight of him steadied the grey ranks the way a keel steadies a boat.

"Hold the pass," he said, and his voice carried, cold and clear. "Every minute they hold that door costs them a piece of the man paying for it. Look at him. We do not need to win quickly. We need only let him keep giving."

And that was true, and cruel, and it might have worked — except that at minute nineteen, Sera Thornfell climbed onto the fallen rock at the throat of the pass, in the open, in the arrow-light, and shouted with a voice that cracked like ice going out in spring:

"EDMUND! THE RIDGE IS LOST IN AN HOUR! OPEN IT WIDE OR ALL OF THIS WAS FOR NOTHING!"

And the man at the door turned, and found her across the smoke and the snow, and though his face was already going strange and smooth, already forgetting, something in it recognized her — recognized her the way a hearth recognizes a hand — and he smiled.

Then he turned back to the Gate, and began, deliberately, to pay.

The light behind him deepened from coal-orange to white.

"Oh no," said Corvane softly, and for the first time in twenty years, the Inquisitor sounded afraid. "Stop him. STOP HIM — *now*."
