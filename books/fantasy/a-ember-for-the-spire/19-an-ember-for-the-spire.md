## 19. An Ember for the Spire

The warden had told him the price at dusk. Now it was nearly midnight, and the snow came down in fat, unhurried flakes that seemed to have all the time in the world—the one thing Edmund Marchetti did not.

He sat on a flat stone before the Gate and tried to take an inventory of himself.

This was, he reflected, the strangest thing he had ever done, and he had once eaten a suspect oyster while maintaining eye contact with the taster-master to prove a point.

The Gate itself was not what he had expected. He had imagined something grand—a spire of black iron, perhaps, or doors of bronze taller than trees. Instead it was simply a shape in the cliff face, a doorway drawn in lines of faint ember-light, like a fire remembered rather than seen. It did not open onto rock. It opened onto *elsewhere*—a shimmer, a warmth, a suggestion of green hills under a kinder sky. The blind warden knelt beside it, hands on the stone, listening to it the way Edmund listened to a spoon against a cup.

Behind him, down the slope, the refugee camp glowed with cookfires. Nine hundred souls, give or take the children born on the road who no one had thought to count yet. Sera was down there somewhere, arguing with smugglers about wagon axles, because Sera argued with everything when she was frightened, and she was very frightened tonight.

Edmund pulled his coat tighter and began, quietly, to practice.

"Mother's kitchen," he said aloud, testing the words. "Blue tiles behind the stove. The chipped bowl she kept lemons in. The way she hummed when she kneaded bread—badly, off-key, always the same three notes."

The Gate flickered. Just slightly. A candle noticing a draft.

"Well," Edmund said. "That's ominous."

* * *

He had spent nine years being forgettable on purpose. There was an art to it. You kept your shoulders rounded but not slumped—you didn't want to look memorable even in your posture. You wore colors that matched walls. When nobles spoke to you, you answered in a voice pitched exactly between interesting and dull. He had once spent an entire banquet being addressed as "the other fellow" by a duke who had interviewed him personally that morning, and Edmund had considered it his finest performance.

Forgetting had been armor. He had built it brick by brick, and now the Gate wanted the whole fortress, and he found—to his genuine surprise—that part of him wanted to give it.

Because here was the arithmetic the warden had laid out, patient as a schoolmaster: the Gate could open narrowly, for one, for free. Or it could open wide, wide enough for nine hundred people and their wagons and their goats (there were eleven goats; Sera had counted them twice, angrily), and for that it needed fuel. Not blood. Not gold. Memory. An heir's entire inheritance of Vessany—every stone of it, every song, every name, down to the heir himself. The kingdom would pass through the door, and the man who opened it would remain on this side of his own life, forever.

"There are no alternatives," the warden had said, when Sera had produced alternatives for four hours straight. She had suggested, among other things: another heir (there were none), a partial payment (the Gate did not haggle), burning the Gate down (the warden had smiled at that one, which was somehow worse), and going through one at a time over several generations ("The Riders arrive tomorrow," Edmund had pointed out gently).

So. No alternatives. Only inventory.

He closed his eyes and walked through his mother's kitchen one more time, deliberately, like a man watering a garden he knew would be paved over by morning. The blue tiles. The lemon bowl. Her humming. His father coming in with cold cheeks and pretending not to be delighted by whatever was cooking. The smell of rosemary, which Edmund had chased for twenty years through every kitchen in the usurper's palace and never quite found again, because it wasn't the rosemary he missed—it was the kitchen.

"You're doing it wrong."

Sera's voice. He hadn't heard her climb up. She dropped onto the stone beside him, snow in her dark hair, her breath steaming. She had a blanket over her arm and the expression of someone who has lost an argument with herself.

"Doing what wrong?"

"Giving it away." She threw the blanket around both their shoulders without asking, which was very Sera. "You're sitting here rehearsing your memories like a speech. That's not how you should spend your last—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Your last night with them."

"How should I spend it?"

"I don't know." Her voice cracked on the honesty of it. "But not alone, and not like a man reading a ledger."

They sat in silence for a while. Down the slope, someone was singing—one of the old songs in the forbidden tongue, the ones that had survived twenty years of erasure by living in kitchens and lullabies where the law couldn't reach. The Gate's ember-lines pulsed faintly in time with it, as if it were listening too.

"Sera," Edmund said. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to actually answer, which I know is difficult for you."

A wet laugh escaped her. "Ask."

"When it's done. When I'm standing there and I don't know my own name." He kept his eyes on the campfires, because looking at her would make this impossible. "What will you do?"

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "I'll tell you."

"Tell me what?"

"Everything. One piece at a time, however long it takes. Your mother's kitchen. The henbane in the stew. The way you held my knife by the blade instead of the handle when I first met you, like a man who'd never been threatened before and didn't want to seem rude about it." Her hand found his under the blanket. Her fingers were freezing and gripped like a vice. "You asked me to remember for you. I'm telling you how. Every evening, if I have to. Until some of it sticks."

"That could take years."

"Good," said Sera Thornfell. "I've got years."

* * *

Dawn came grey and slow, and with it came the warden, tapping her way up the path with a stick that made no sound on the snow.

"It is time," she said, "if it is ever to be time. The Riders crossed the lower ford in the night. By midday they will see this valley."

Edmund stood. His legs felt like borrowed things.

Down at the camp, the refugees were already moving into line—not because anyone had ordered it, but because word travels fast in a column of the desperate, and everyone had understood overnight what the strange glowing door in the cliff required. As he walked down through the camp, people turned to watch. An old woman pressed a heel of bread into his hands. A child—he thought her name was Mirele, though he'd learned forty names on the road and feared he was mixing them up—reached up and solemnly shook his hand, as if he were a dignitary.

"They shouldn't thank me," he murmured to Sera. "I haven't done anything yet."

"They're thanking you for what you're about to do," she said. "Which is either very wise or very cruel of them. Probably both. It's a Vessani trait."

"You say that like you're not one of us now."

"I carried the map," she said. "That makes me Vessani enough to be bossy at you. Come on."

At the Gate, the warden took his hands and turned them palm-up. "It will take what you offer, in the order you offer it. Do not fight it when it comes. Fighting only makes it hurt." A pause. "Mostly."

"Mostly," Edmund repeated. "Wonderful. Is there anything else? A pamphlet, perhaps?"

"The Gate does not write pamphlets."

"A shame. It would have excellent margins."

But his humor was a thin coat over shaking, and the warden knew it, and kindly pretended otherwise. She guided him until his palms rested flat against the stone between the ember-lines. It was warm. Warm as bread. Warm as a kitchen.

"Begin," she said, "with what you can spare."

And Edmund, who had spent his whole life deciding what to swallow and what to refuse, took a breath and offered up the palace kitchens first.

* * *

He had thought it would feel like losing. It did not. It felt like *giving*—like unclenching a fist he hadn't known he'd made.

The palace went gently. Nine years of copper pots and knife-work and the taster-master's suspicious squint; the taste of arsenic, bitter-almond-sweet, that he'd learned to identify before it touched his tongue; the particular loneliness of eating beautifully prepared meals alone at a side table while the court dined without him. He gave it away piece by piece and felt lighter with each one, the way a pack feels lighter at the end of a mountain crossing even though you know you'll miss the things inside it.

The Gate drank. The ember-lines brightened from candle-glow to hearth-fire.

Then his garret above the chandler's shop, with its drafty window and its one good chair. Then the alley where Sera had held a knife to his throat and he had offered her the truth instead, and she had looked at him as if truth were the strangest weapon she'd ever been handed.

"Don't," Sera whispered beside him. He hadn't known she'd stayed. Her hand was on his shoulder now, gripping hard. "Not those. Save those."

"I don't get to save any," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "That's the whole price, love. All of it, or none of it works."

The Gate flared. The warden murmured, "Steady."

His mother's kitchen next. He gave it slowly, savoring the spending of it—the blue tiles, the lemon bowl, the three off-key notes of her humming—and as each detail left him he felt it land somewhere beyond the stone, felt the Gate grow brighter and warmer and more *real*, the doorway deepening from suggestion to certainty. Somewhere on the other side, he understood, a country was being reassembled out of what he remembered. Every memory given was a stone set back in a wall. His mother's kitchen was becoming someone's kitchen again, on the far side of nowhere.

His father's face. The rosemary smell. The village elder's stories of the kingdom written out of existence. The Pass of Names, where the dead speak. The winter camp, the songs, the children born on the road. The map, and the courier bleeding out at the salt door, gasping a name that had once been Edmund's own—

*Vessany.*

He reached for the word and found the place where it had been, smooth and blank as a wall with the picture taken down.

"Almost," the warden said softly. "Now the hardest part. Yourself. Your name. Who you are."

And here Edmund paused, because there was one thing left he wanted to keep, just for a moment longer, and he was allowed this much—he was almost sure he was allowed this much.

Sera's face in firelight.

The night at Winter Camp when she'd fallen asleep mid-lesson, her head tipping onto his shoulder while he taught her the old script, and he had sat perfectly still for two hours rather than wake her, and had understood, with the calm of a man tasting a dish and knowing instantly, finally, *this one*, that he loved her. He held that memory up to the light one last time. Turned it over. Memorized the memorizing of it, which is a fool's trick and he knew it, because the Gate would take the trick along with the treasure.

"Edmund," Sera said. His name. She said it like she was planting a flag in him.

"That's the one I'll miss most," he told her. "I want you to know that. Whatever I am in a minute—I won't know why I trust you. But I will trust you. Build on that."

"Every evening," she whispered. "I told you. Every evening."

He gave her face in firelight to the Gate.

It went like a coal leaving a fire—bright, then gone, then only warmth where it had been. And with it went Edmund Marchetti: the poison-proof taster, the invisible man, the last heir of a kingdom erased. He felt his own name loosen and lift away like snow off a branch. He felt the shape of himself go hollow and then fill with something else—not grief, not fear, but a vast quiet kindness, as if all those memories, having passed through him into the stone, had left their warmth behind as change.

The Gate blazed.

Light poured up the cliff face like dawn arriving sideways, and the doorway that had been a shimmer became a road—green hills, real air, the smell of rain on far-off fields, a whole horizon waiting. The sound it made was not thunder. It was a great indrawn breath, the sound of a held population exhaling at once.

Edmund stood before it, swaying, empty-handed and light as ash.

He turned to the woman beside him—a stranger, fierce-eyed, crying without any apparent awareness that she was doing it—and found, in the wreckage of everything he had been, one small stubborn ember that the Gate had somehow not taken, or perhaps had left deliberately, the way you leave a lamp lit for someone coming home.

"I know you," he said slowly, wondering. "I don't know how. But I know you."

Sera laughed through her tears. "Yes," she said. "Yes, you do. And I'm going to spend the rest of my life explaining the rest."

Down the slope, horns sounded—the Riders, early, crueler than the warden's arithmetic—and the refugees surged toward the blazing door, and Sera's hand closed around his like a promise.

"Come on," she said. "Walk me home."

* * *

He went. Of course he went. He was a man who had just given away everything he owned except one ember, and the ember was her hand in his, and the door was open, and behind them the snow filled in the footprints of nine hundred people as if the mountains themselves had agreed to forget—everything forgotten, everything forgiven, everything carried forward through the fire by the one person who had refused to let the price be paid in vain.

The last thing the kingdom of Vessany ever did was hold his hand.
