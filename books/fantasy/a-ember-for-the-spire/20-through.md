## 20. Through

The Gate sang.

Sera had no better word for it. It was not a sound so much as a feeling in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the fillings of old teeth she'd lost years ago—a low, golden thrum that rolled out of the cliff face and down into the snow like a bell struck somewhere inside the mountain's heart. The door that was not a door stood open at last, and through it was light. Not sunlight. Not firelight. Something older and kinder than either, the color of the inside of a held breath.

And through it poured her people.

They came stumbling and weeping, carrying children and chickens and one indignant goat that had survived three mountain passes on pure spite. Old Marta went first, because of course she did; she'd been telling anyone who'd listen for two days that she intended to be first through any door worth opening, and by all the forgotten gods, she managed it. Then the Vessani families, then the smugglers' folk who'd decided halfway up the pass that wherever this crowd was going beat freezing on a ridge, then the wounded, carried on litters of lashed-together skis.

Sera stood at the mouth of the pass with an axe she'd taken off a dead Grey Rider and did not let herself look at the Gate yet. There would be time. There had to be time.

Because behind her, down the valley, Corvane had broken the pass.

She'd seen it from the ridge an hour ago—the grey line finally punching through the barricade at the narrows, where Bren of the Salt Road and eleven others had held for four hours against impossible odds. Four hours. She'd promised them three, and they'd given her four, and when the line broke, the survivors came running uphill through the snow with their heads down and their weapons gone, and Sera had counted them twice and made herself stop counting.

Bren wasn't among them.

"Go," he'd told her that morning, grinning through a split lip, reloading a crossbow with fingers too frozen to feel the bolts. "Tell your taster if he gets that door open, I want it noted I held a pass against the Inquisition with a kitchen knife and bad intentions."

She'd tell him. She'd tell everyone. That was the whole point of surviving, as far as she could figure—someone had to carry the arithmetic of the dead, or else they died twice.

"Sera!" Tam, the smuggler boy with the burned hand, skidded up beside her, breath ragged. "Riders are maybe twenty minutes back. Fifteen if they've got horses in the narrows. We need everyone through *now*."

"Then get them through." She shoved him toward the light. "You next. Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were winding up to it. Go."

He went. And Sera turned at last to look for Edmund.

* * *

She found him ten paces from the Gate, standing in the churned snow where the refugees' passage had trampled everything to grey slush, and her heart stopped in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

He was just—standing. Facing the door. His hands hung loose at his sides, and his face was empty. Not calm. Empty. She knew every weather of that face by now: the careful court-blankness he wore like armor, the wry almost-smile he used when he was frightened, the fierce set of his jaw when someone suggested the refugees slow down. This was none of those. This was a house with the lamps out.

"Edmund." She said it loud, over the singing of the Gate.

Nothing.

She crossed to him fast, boots crunching, and grabbed his shoulder, and he turned his head and looked at her, and Sera Thornfell—who had held a knife to this man's throat in an alley and meant it, who had walked nine days of frozen hell beside him, who had learned to read the old script from his patient finger tracing letters in spilled flour—felt something crack straight down the middle of her.

His eyes were brown. They had always been brown. But there was nobody home behind them. He looked at her the way you look at weather. At a wall. At nothing.

"Do you—" Her voice came out wrong, thin. She tried again. "Edmund. It's me. It's Sera."

He blinked, slowly, politely, the way a man blinks at a stranger who has addressed him in a language he doesn't speak. "I'm sorry," he said, and even his voice was wrong—flatter, uninflected, a well with the bucket rope cut. "Do I know you?"

The words went through her like a bolt through a shield. She'd known. Gods help her, she'd known this was coming; she'd sat across the fire from him last night while he asked her, quietly, to remember for him, and she'd said yes, and she'd thought she was ready.

She had not been ready. There was no being ready for a man to look at you with your own name missing from his eyes.

"You knew me," she said, and her voice steadied because fury was always available to her, fury was the oldest tool she owned. "Nine days ago you didn't know me either, and now you do, and that's how it works, so you'll just have to take my word for it. You're Edmund. You're a food-taster, which is a stupid job, and you're very good at it, which is worse. You put too much pepper on everything. You hum when you cook. Badly. You taught a woman who can gut a fish in four seconds how to read, and you never once made her feel stupid about it, which is more than most people manage."

He watched her deliver this inventory with the mild, baffled attention of a man listening to a street preacher. "That sounds like a lot of trouble to go to," he said, "for someone you don't know."

"It is," Sera said. "It's the worst trouble I've ever been in. Come on."

She took his wrist. He didn't resist—he didn't do anything, and somehow that was the most frightening part, this man who had once drunk poison on purpose rather than let a stranger die, letting himself be led like furniture.

Behind them, down the valley, the first horns sounded.

* * *

The rear of the column was chaos wearing the mask of order. The last fifty refugees pressed toward the Gate in a shuffling river, and above them, on the ridge to the east, grey shapes were moving—not many yet, a scouting line, but moving with the terrible unhurried confidence of people who know the trap is already sprung.

Sera got Edmund to the threshold and stopped.

Up close, the Gate was worse. Better. Worse. The light pouring through it moved like water and felt like standing near a hearth after years in the cold, and the singing in her bones resolved, if she let herself listen, into something almost like voices—thousands of them, far away, saying something she couldn't quite catch. Welcoming, maybe. Or counting.

"What is it?" Edmund asked beside her. Flat. Curious the way a child is curious about a locked box.

"It's a door," Sera said. "It's the only door, and it's ours, and we're going through it before those grey bastards get here, and then it's going to close in their faces forever. That's what it is."

"That sounds important."

"It is important." She turned and caught the arm of a passing refugee—an old man, one of the Ashford villagers, hauling a sack of seed grain. "How many left?"

"Twenty, maybe thirty of us stragglers." He squinted past her at Edmund, and his face did something complicated—recognition, grief, gratitude, all arriving at once. "Is that... the heir? Is he—"

"He's through in a minute," Sera said, harder than she meant to. "Everyone goes. That was the price. Everyone goes."

The old man looked at Edmund a long moment, then bowed—actually bowed, in the snow, with Riders on the ridge—and shuffled through the light and was gone.

The horns again. Closer. And now she could see them clearly on the eastern ridge: a dozen riders, then twenty, fanning out along the crest like ink dropped in water. Below them, further down the valley, torches. So many torches.

"Tam!" she bellowed, and the boy materialized out of the crowd, singed and snow-crusted and alive, thank all the gods there weren't anymore. "Get the stragglers moving. Anyone who stops to pray, pray *walking*. Anyone who stops to argue, push them through. I don't care how."

"And you?"

"I'm last."

Tam looked at her, then at Edmund, and whatever he saw in her face made him swallow whatever he was going to say and go. Smart boy. She'd write him a reference someday, if free cities did references.

The last refugees funneled past—a mother with twins, a smuggler dragging the goat, who went through the Gate with its ears flat and an expression suggesting it found the entire enterprise beneath its dignity. Sera counted them out loud under her breath, an old habit from her father, who'd counted heads at every campsite, every ford, every dawn: *never lose one, little bird, the count is the promise.*

Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen.

Then the snow at the tree line below the Gate erupted with grey.

* * *

There were only six of them—a forward party, Riders who'd pushed ahead of the main force, dismounting and coming up through the trees with swords drawn. They must have been ordered to take the Gate, not the people; they ignored the fleeing refugees entirely and came straight for the door, and for Sera, and for the empty-eyed man standing in front of it.

Sera put herself between them and picked up the Rider's axe she'd been carrying since the pass.

"Edmund," she said, without turning around. "I need you to walk through that door."

"Why?" Flat. Honest. A question, not a refusal.

"Because I'm asking."

"All right," he said, and didn't move.

Of course. Of course he didn't. Why would he? She was a stranger shouting at him in a snowstorm while armed men charged out of the woods. She wanted to scream. Instead she did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the alley with the knife: what does this man cost me, what is he worth, what will I pay?

Everything. The answer kept being everything.

The first Rider reached her. He was young—young enough that she hated him a little for it—and he swung his sword like a boy who'd learned on dummies. She caught the blade on the axe haft, stepped inside his reach, and drove her knee into something soft and essential. He folded. The second one was better; he nearly took her arm off, and only the fact that she'd spent nine days learning exactly how tired arms fail in deep snow saved her—she gave ground instead of holding it, let him overextend, and put the axe head into the side of his knee. He went down screaming, and she felt no triumph at all, only the clock ticking in her chest.

Third Rider. Fourth. They were smarter, circling, and her arms were lead, and the horns on the ridge had stopped because the horns were no longer necessary—

A shape hit the third Rider from the side.

Edmund. Empty-eyed, flat-voiced Edmund, who should have been standing in the light waiting for instructions, had picked up a fallen sword and thrown himself bodily at a trained killer, and it was ugly and graceless and utterly without technique, and it worked, because the Rider had expected nothing at all from the hollow man by the door. They went down together in the snow. The fourth Rider hesitated—one heartbeat of confusion—and Sera's axe found him.

Then the fifth and sixth simply stopped.

They stood ten feet away, swords up, staring past her—at the Gate, at the light pouring out of it, at the singing that Sera realized had changed pitch, deeper now, resonant, the sound of a door preparing to shut. One of them took a step backward. Inquisitors were many things, but they were men of an erased kingdom's bureaucracy, and some animal part of them understood, looking into that light, that they were standing on the wrong side of history with their swords out.

"Through," Sera snarled at them, jerking her chin at the Gate. "Or stay. Stay's fine. Stay's permanent."

They stayed. They actually stayed—turned and ran, down into the trees, away from the light, and Sera would think about that later, the two men who chose the dark they knew over the door they didn't, and she would not find it in herself to judge them.

"Edmund." She spun. He was kneeling in the snow beside the unconscious Rider, sword still in his hand, looking at it with faint puzzlement, as though trying to remember why his hands knew what to do when the rest of him didn't. "Are you hurt?"

"No," he said. Then, after a pause, with the ghost of something flickering behind the emptiness—there and gone, a fish surfacing: "Were you worried about me?"

"Always," Sera said, before she could stop herself. "Constantly. It's exhausting. Get up."

He got up. And together, axes and swords abandoned in the bloody snow, they walked toward the light.

* * *

At the threshold she stopped one last time and looked back.

The valley was full of torches now, hundreds of them, the whole grey army come up the pass at last, and at their head, on a black horse, a figure in a coat the color of ash. Even at this distance, even through falling snow, Sera could feel him looking at her. Looking at the door. Corvane, who had written a kingdom out of the world and chased its last ember across a country that officially did not exist, and who had arrived, at the end of everything, ninety seconds too late.

She raised her hand—not a wave. She showed him the axe scar on her palm, the one she'd gotten holding the pass, and held it there until she was sure he saw.

Then the light took them.

Going through felt like stepping into a bath drawn by someone who loved you. Warmth everywhere at once, and the singing swelling up around them until it wasn't sound anymore but meaning, vast and gentle, and for one suspended moment Sera felt every person who had ever passed this way—all the Vessani dead, all the exiled living, thousands of hands briefly resting on her shoulders, saying *you carried us, well done, well done*—and she was crying without any shame about it, and Edmund's hand had found hers in the light, gripping hard, though his face stayed smooth and unknowing, some part of him reaching for her across the gap where himself used to be.

And then they were through, and the Gate closed.

It didn't slam. It sighed. The light drew inward like a tide going out, the singing faded note by golden note, and the great stone face of the cliff sealed itself seamless and silent, leaving nothing but rock and snow and, carved small and new above where the door had been, a single line of the old script that Sera could now read:

*An ember for the spire.*

On the other side of the stone, muffled to nothing, an army arrived at a wall that was only ever a wall.

* * *

The free side of the mountains was green.

That was the thing that undid her, in the end—not the victory, not the grief, but the stupid grass. It came up to their knees in a valley that smelled like spring, and the refugees who'd come through ahead of them were scattered across it, laughing, weeping, pulling off boots, and the goat was eating something, and the sun was warm, genuinely warm, the first warm day in months.

Sera stood in the grass with Edmund's hand still in hers and cried until her ribs hurt.

He waited. Politely. The way he'd waited through everything, these nine days and nine years. When she finally ran out, he said, "You knew me. Before. Didn't you."

"Yes."

"And I don't know you now."

"No."

He considered this. Snowmelt dripped from his hair. Somewhere behind them a child shrieked with laughter, and the goat screamed its opinion of freedom, and Edmund Marchetti—last heir of Vessany, the man who had paid everything and couldn't remember what currency—looked at her with those empty brown eyes and said:

"Will you tell me? Who I was?"

And Sera laughed, wet and cracked, because of course. Of course that was the question. Not *who am I*, not *what did I lose*—but *will you tell me*, as if the answer could ever, under any sky, in any world, be anything but yes.

"Every night," she said. "For as long as it takes. Starting with the time you poisoned an entire patrol's water canteens and nearly killed yourself doing it, because honestly, it's the best story I have, and you're going to love it."

"I sound like an idiot," he said.

"The biggest," Sera said, and pulled him toward the green.
