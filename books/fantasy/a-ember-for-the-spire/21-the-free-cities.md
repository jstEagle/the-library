## 21. The Free Cities

The city of Maravel had a smell, and the smell was salt and frying fish and tar, and after four months of snow and pine and fear, Sera Thornfell thought it was the most beautiful thing she had ever breathed.

She stood at the harbor rail with her hands wrapped around a chipped cup of tea that cost her three coppers and was worth every one of them, and she watched the ships come in. Behind her, up the hill in the warehouse district, six hundred people who had walked out of a country that no longer existed were learning how to live in one that did.

They had given themselves a name on the second night, when someone asked — gently, the way you ask about a wound — where they were from. An old woman named Brannit, who had lost her husband somewhere in the Pass of Names and never said his name aloud again, had looked at the fire for a long time before answering.

"We're the ones who came through," she said. "That's all we are now. And it's enough."

It had stuck. It spread through the camps of the free cities the way good news spreads, quietly and then all at once. The ones who came through. No kingdom required. No law could forbid it, because it wasn't a place; it was a fact. You couldn't burn a fact out of a map.

Sera took another sip of tea and let the harbor wind push her hair around. Somewhere below, a fishmonger was arguing with a gull over a herring with the passion of a man defending his homeland, and she found herself smiling, which still surprised her sometimes. Smiling. She'd have to relearn it, like walking on a leg that had been asleep.

Her father would have liked Maravel. He'd always said he'd retire to a harbor town someday, sit by the water, tell lies to fishermen. She let herself think that for exactly as long as she could bear it, and then she put it away in the room inside her where she kept him, next to the map and the wax seal and the last thing he ever said to her, which was *go with them, little thorn* — because even dead men got to be right, apparently, and she hated that.

She finished her tea. The bell above the harbor office rang eight. Time for work.

* * *

The archive of the Free Cities occupied a former granary near the Temple Quarter, which meant it smelled faintly of old grain and older dust, and its head archivist was a round, cheerful man named Odo Pell who wore his spectacles pushed up on his forehead so often that Sera suspected they had fused there.

"You want to keep records," Odo said, looking her over the way a horse trader looks at a mare. "What records?"

"Old ones."

"Everyone says that. Nobody means it. Old records are damp, dull, and full of other people's arguments about drainage ditches." He leaned forward. "Why?"

Because my father carried a map through mountains and died for it. Because a whole country was written out of existence and I watched the last door close on it while a man I love stood blank-eyed in front of me and asked me who I was. Because somebody has to remember, or the erasure wins.

"Because I'm good at it," Sera said instead.

Odo studied her a moment longer, then shrugged. "Fair answer. Most people lie worse." He handed her a key on a string. "Third floor. Anything older than the founding of Maravel is yours if you can make sense of it. Half of it's in scripts nobody reads anymore. There's a boy who does deliveries, he'll show you the stove. Don't trust the stove. Trust nothing in this building except the stone walls, and only mostly those."

She started the next morning.

The third floor was hers alone: long tables under high windows, shelves sagging under ledgers, scrolls, bundles of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth. She worked methodically, the way her father had taught her — catalog first, read later, despair never until after supper. Most of it was drainage ditches, as promised. Some of it was trade disputes from cities that no longer traded. But here and there she found things that made her sit very still.

A marriage contract in a hand she recognized from the village elder's book. A child's copybook, practicing letters in the old Vessani script, with a teacher's corrections in red. A shipping manifest from a port called Vessany-on-the-Sea, dated sixty years back, listing cargo of *ember-glass, uncut*.

She touched that one for a long time.

Then she copied it into her own ledger, in her own careful hand, and wrote beneath it: *Recovered from Granary Archive, Maravel. Found by Sera Thornfell, of the ones who came through.*

That became the ritual. Every fragment of the old tongue, every scrap of the erased kingdom, she copied and dated and signed. Not restoring anything. Just making sure it existed in more than one place. Her father had believed in redundancy the way other men believed in gods.

By midwinter she had a corner of the third floor stacked with her own volumes, forty-two of them, and Odo Pell had stopped calling it "the damp corner" and started calling it "the Thornfell collection," and once, when a visiting scholar from Corissa sneered that it was a lot of fuss over a country nobody had heard of, Odo had drawn himself up to his full unimpressive height and said, "Madam, archives exist precisely for countries nobody has heard of," and Sera had decided she would work for him for free if it came to that.

It didn't come to that. He paid her badly and fed her well, and both suited her fine.

* * *

Edmund came to the archive on a Tuesday.

She knew it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays were delivery days, and she was down on the ground floor arguing with the stove — which, as instructed, she trusted not at all — when Odo appeared beside her and said, in the too-casual voice of a man delivering news he finds delightful, "There's a young fellow upstairs asking odd questions. Says he doesn't know why he's here. I told him everyone says that, but he seems to mean it."

Sera went cold and warm at the same time, which she was learning was simply what happened now.

"Odd questions," she managed.

"About old scripts. Whether we have any. He picked up a page off my desk — a Vessani fragment, mind — and read it. Read it *fluently*, madam, like breathing. Then he looked at his own hands as if they'd betrayed him." Odo's eyes twinkled behind their perched spectacles. "I told him we had a specialist. He's waiting. Shall I send him up?"

"Yes," Sera said. "Yes. Send him up."

She climbed to the third floor faster than dignity allowed and arrived slightly breathless just as Edmund reached the top of the stairs, and there he was, and there he wasn't.

He was taller than she remembered — no, he was the same height, it was the months of feeding that had filled him out; the free cities were kind to refugees with appetites. His hair was longer. Someone had shown him how to shave properly. He was wearing a dockworker's coat, because he'd taken a job hauling crates, because of course he had; give that man a palace and he'd find the kitchens, give him freedom and he'd find honest work. He looked healthy. He looked rested. He looked like a stranger wearing a face she loved, and he smiled at her — politely, warily, the smile of a man meeting a new colleague —

and none of it was for her, and all of it broke her heart, and she smiled back anyway, because that was the deal. That had always been the deal. She remembered so he didn't have to.

"The archivist said you work with old scripts," he said. His voice hadn't changed. That was somehow the worst part and the best part. "I'm sorry to bother you. I don't — I don't actually know why I'm here. I was passing the building and I just..." He made a small helpless gesture. "Walked in. Like something pulled me. That sounds mad, doesn't it."

"It doesn't sound mad," Sera said. Her voice held. She'd had practice holding it. "Buildings remember things. Sometimes they reach out."

He laughed, uncertain. "You sound like the warden of a—"

He stopped. Frowned. Put a hand briefly to his temple, the way he did, the way he'd done every day since the Gate, some ghost of pressure behind the eyes that the healers in Corissa had examined and pronounced nothing wrong with him at all.

"I do that," he said apologetically. "Say things I don't know where they come from. Words. Names. Yesterday I told a fisherman his nets were rigged wrong and then explained *how* to fix them, in detail, and I've never touched a net in my life." He shook his head. "The healers say my mind is perfectly sound. They say whatever I lost, I lost clean."

"What do you say?" Sera asked quietly.

He considered that. He was good at considering; she'd forgotten — no, she'd *remembered*, that was the point, she kept everything — that he was good at sitting with a question instead of fleeing it.

"I say I'm doing well," he said at last. "Better than I deserve, probably. I have work, and a room, and there are people from—" he paused, searching, "—from up north, in the mountains, who look at me strangely, kindly, like they know me. One of them calls me 'my lord' when she thinks I can't hear. I keep telling her I haul crates for a living. She keeps curtsying." He smiled ruefully. "And I say most mornings I wake up feeling like I've left something somewhere important, and I spend the rest of the day not knowing what. But the feeling gets smaller. Or I get used to it. Probably both."

"That's how grief works," Sera said. "For everybody. Even people who know what they lost."

Something moved across his face — recognition without memory, an echo answering an echo.

"Do you know me?" he asked. "From before? Everyone keeps asking me that. Do you remember me?"

*Every day,* she thought. *Every hour. I remember your mother's kitchen and the way you read maps upside down without noticing and the taste of henbane you took so a king could eat safely, and I remember snow in the pass and your hands shaking around a cup of tea you made for me because I was crying and wouldn't say why, and I remember you deciding, before dawn, without telling anyone, because you never could stand to make people watch you be brave.*

"I know of you," she said. "Through mutual friends. We should — I could use help, actually. With the collection. Most of it's in a script nobody in Maravel reads."

"I read it," he said, puzzled at himself. "I don't know how, but I do."

"Would you like to learn why? Properly? Page by page?" She nodded at the long tables, the sagging shelves, her forty-two volumes. "It's slow work. Damp work. The stove is a liar and the pay is terrible."

He looked around the archive — at the windows full of grey sea-light, at the fragments of a country he had bought with himself — and something in his shoulders eased, the way it used to ease when he entered a kitchen. A man finding the room he belongs in, even if he doesn't know why.

"Yes," he said. "I'd like that very much."

* * *

He came every day after that.

He learned fast, which surprised no one but him. He handled the fragile pages like they might wake up angry. He alphabetized her chaos within a week and apologized for it, twice. When he found the copybook with the teacher's red corrections, he sat with it for an hour and then said, softly, to no one in particular, "Whoever taught this child loved them," and Sera had to leave the room and stand at the window until her eyes behaved.

Odo Pell adored him. Within a month the two of them had instituted Tuesday arguments about cataloguing philosophy that could be heard three floors down, and Odo reported to Sera, delightedly, "Your crate-hauler has opinions, Thornfell. Strong ones. About *filing*."

"He's not mine," Sera said.

"No?" Odo polished his spectacles, which he did when he was being wise, which was often. "Funny. He asked whether you took supper at regular hours. In my experience, men don't ask that about archivists they're not mine-ing."

Sera threw a dust rag at him.

But it was true that things had begun to happen in small ways. Edmund saved her the window seat without thinking about it. She made tea for two without thinking about it. He started staying past closing, reading aloud in the old script just to hear it, he said, because it sounded like rain on a roof he half-remembered. Once, sorting a bundle of letters, he laughed suddenly — a real laugh, startled out of him — at some joke in a dead woman's handwriting, and then looked stricken, because he hadn't known he remembered how to laugh at her language, and Sera said, "Don't apologize. Things live in you. Let them."

He looked at her for a long moment then, the sea-grey light on his face, and said, "You talk like you've done this before. Like you've lost something and built a life around the losing."

"I have," Sera said. "We all did. All of us who came through."

"And it gets better? The missing-it?"

She thought about lying to him, and rejected it, because he had spent nine years eating poison for strangers and deserved better than comfort.

"It doesn't get better," she said. "It gets *lighter*. The weight stays the same but your shoulders get stronger. And some days you set it down and dance, and then you pick it up again, and that's not failure, that's just carrying."

He nodded slowly, like a man writing it down somewhere safe.

"Will you teach me to carry like that?" he asked.

"You're already doing it," Sera said. "You just think the weight is empty. It isn't. Somebody's been keeping it full for you."

He didn't understand. He smiled anyway, and went back to his pages, and outside the gulls wheeled over the harbor screaming their ancient grievances, and the stove lied about the temperature, and it was, against every odd and every arithmetic, a good afternoon.

* * *

On the eve of spring, Sera opened a new volume and began the first line.

*To Edmund,* she wrote, though he would not read it yet, maybe not for years, maybe not until she was ready and he was steady and the ember in him had grown strong enough to survive knowing what it had been. *My father used to say a courier's real cargo isn't the letter. It's the reason the letter matters. So here is yours. Here is everything.*

Below it, she wrote the date, and the place — *Maravel, Free Cities, in the year the Gate closed* — and then, because her father had also taught her that every record needs a witness line:

*Dictated by history. Kept by Sera Thornfell. Of the ones who came through.*

Downstairs, the door banged open and Edmund's voice rose, arguing cheerfully with Odo about whether a certain manifest belonged in the Thornfell collection or the general stacks, and the argument drifted up the stairs along with the smell of the fish pies he'd brought for supper, and Sera dipped her pen, and began to write it all down — the stew and the salt door, the mountain and the ford, the pass and the Gate and the snow, every dish of poison, every borrowed warmth, every name —

starting, of course, with a taster who could taste tomorrow in a spoon,

and ending, someday, if she did her work right, with two cups of tea and a man learning to be someone new.

She wrote until the light went.
