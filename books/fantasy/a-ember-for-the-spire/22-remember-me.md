## 22. Remember Me

*Edmund —*

*I don't know if you'll read this. I don't know yet how much of you is left to read anything, and I won't know until I climb the stairs of the archive house tonight and find out whether the man who lives above my workroom still flinches at the smell of henbane.*

*So I'm writing it all down instead. Every evening, one page, the way I promised. If you never ask, these pages will keep it anyway. Paper is patient in a way people aren't. My father taught me that — he was a courier, which is a fancy word for a man who trusts paper more than his own memory, because paper doesn't bleed when it's caught.*

*Well. Sometimes it does. Ink bleeds. But you take my meaning.*

*Here is what I remember, so that someone remembers:*

* * *

**The first thing you ever did that mattered to me was steal my father's map.**

That's not fair, and I know it's not fair, and I've had two years to make peace with it, but I'm starting where the truth starts, not where it ends up being polite.

You didn't steal it. He gave it to you with the last strength in his body, in a doorway that smelled of tallow and old fish, while men who wore grey hunted him through the capital he'd spent twenty years pretending meant nothing to him. He chose you. Out of every face in that palace kitchen, he looked at the forgettable one — the taster nobody bothered to learn the name of — and he thought: *him. He's the one who can carry it.*

I hated you for that for a long time. Not because I believed you'd stolen it, exactly, but because if you hadn't existed, then Papa would have come home. That's the arithmetic grief does. It's bad arithmetic. It only balances if you refuse to count certain things.

Here's what I refused to count, back then: that my father had been carrying Vessany inside him for twenty years like a coal banked under ash, waiting for a hand steady enough to pass it to. That he'd told me stories about a kingdom that wasn't on any map and made me swear to memorize mountain roads I'd never walked, "just in case," and I'd rolled my eyes and sworn anyway because he asked. That when he finally found the heir — half by accident, half by the kind of stubborn detective work he pretended was luck — he must have felt the way I feel now, sitting down to write to you. Terrified. Certain. Both at once.

He died three days' ride from safety, having delivered a map to a stranger. And the stranger turned out to be the last heir of a country that officially never existed, and the stranger turned out to be — well. You know what you turned out to be. Or you will, by the time I finish telling you.

One evening at a time. That's the rule I set myself. Tonight is just the map, and the alley, and the knife I held on you, and the look on your face when I said *my father is dead because of you* and you didn't argue, didn't run, didn't even reach for the door. You just said, "Then tell me how to make it not have been for nothing."

Nobody had ever asked me a question like that before. Everybody else in my life had asked me questions like *where are you going* and *how much do you charge* and *are you the Thornfell girl, the courier's daughter.* You asked me the first question anyone had ever asked that assumed I wanted the world to be better than it was.

I put the knife away. I want that recorded too, since you'll never remember putting it there yourself: I held a knife on you in a rain-soaked alley behind a chandler's shop, and you disarmed me with honesty, which no fencing master teaches, and I have been trying to get even ever since.

* * *

**The second thing I remember is the mountains.**

You were hopeless in the Thornfell. I mean this with love, Edmund, or with whatever this is that I haven't got a better word for yet. You walked like a man crossing a ballroom — careful, upright, deeply offended by mud. You complained about the cold in complete sentences, which is a court habit if I ever saw one. When I showed you which lichen was safe to eat you looked at it the way you used to look at suspect dishes, like it might be lying to you.

But here's the part I didn't say at the time, because I was busy hating you: you never once quit. Nine days of wet wool and hard bread and my very worst company, and you kept walking. I've guided smugglers, deserters, one fugitive tax collector, and a man who claimed to be a bear-wrestler (he was not). They all talked about turning back. You never did. You'd just tighten your pack straps and say, "Which way?" like the answer couldn't possibly matter as long as we were moving toward it together.

And when the grey riders came over the ridge at the ford — I remember that too, though you won't — you didn't fight them. You poisoned their water. Calmly. While they watched the refugees, you knelt at the stream like a man washing his hands and doctored four canteens with something bitter and slow, and then you drank a sip of your own concoction to test the dose, because apparently nine years of tasting poison for a tyrant teaches a person nothing about self-preservation.

You nearly died doing it. I dragged you behind a rock and yelled at you while you shivered and went grey around the mouth, and you apologized to *me*, Edmund. For worrying me. As if I hadn't spent the whole ride from the village rehearsing ways to forgive you and finding none of them adequate.

That was the day I stopped hating you. I want it written plainly, because you'll never remember it happening: somewhere between the burning village and the ford, between watching you carry a stranger's grandmother uphill and watching you poison a river for people you owed nothing, my hate ran out of fuel, and what was underneath it turned out to be something I had no plan for.

* * *

**The third thing is the snow camp, and the songs.**

The refugee column wintered in the high valley — forty-one souls, when we counted, which we did every morning like a superstition, and once, briefly, forty-two, because little Maren arrived three weeks early and loudly, in a smuggler's wagon, with the whole camp holding lanterns in the snow. Her mother named her after a river in Vessany. She'll grow up in the free cities never having seen that river, and she'll know its name anyway, because names are the one cargo no rider can confiscate.

You taught me the old script that winter. I'd grown up mouthing the sounds without the letters — Papa's stories, sung more than spoken — and you sat across the fire from me with a stick and a patch of cleared snow and drew the characters like a man introducing me to old friends. "This one means *hearth*," you said. "This one means *return*. This one has no translation; the scholars argued about it for a hundred years. It's the one they carved over doors."

"What did it say?" I asked.

"Welcome," you said. "It just says welcome."

I learned to read that winter by firelight, spelling out the fragments of Vessani we'd salvaged — a recipe, half a song, a child's prayer against nightmares. And I remember thinking, with the particular stupidity of a woman falling in love without admitting it: *he carries a whole country in his head, and he's spending it teaching me the alphabet.*

I didn't know yet what that would cost. Neither did you. Or maybe you knew earlier than any of us. Maybe that's why you were so generous with it — a man spending coins he already knows are promised to someone else.

* * *

**The fourth thing is the Gate, and I will tell it plainly, because you deserve plainness even if you can't ask for it.**

There was a blind warden, and she tested your blood, and she named the price, and there were no alternatives — I checked, I argued, I offered her everything I had and several things I didn't, and the Gate is not a merchant and does not haggle. One heir's whole inheritance of memory, poured out like wine, to open a door wide enough for thousands.

You decided before dawn. You woke me — I was on watch — and you didn't wake anyone else, and you said, "Sera. I need to ask you something, and I need you to say yes."

"Anything," I said, which should have frightened me, because I meant it.

"Remember it for me. All of it. The parts I lose — keep them. Don't let the country die twice."

And I said yes. And then I sat with you while the sky went from black to iron to pale gold, and we didn't talk much, because what is there to say to a man on the last night he owns himself? I remember you ate most of the bread. I remember you laughed at something — I've forgotten what, isn't that strange, I kept everything of yours and lost my own small moment — and I remember thinking: *this is what it looks like when someone is brave in the boring way, the way that's just getting up in the morning and doing the terrible necessary thing without theater.*

At the Gate itself, you fed it your memories one by one. I stood close enough to see your face change with each one — your mother's kitchen going out of you like a lamp being carried room to room and finally snuffed. The palace kitchens. Your childhood home, drawn at the heart of a map. Your own name. Last of all, near the end, you looked straight at me, and your eyes went through me like light through a window, and I understood that you were giving up my face in firelight along with everything else, and I did not look away, because someone had to witness it, and witnessing was the whole of my job now.

The Gate opened. It blazed like the title of a story too large to tell in one sitting. And the people came through, forty-one and then hundreds and then more, out of a country that had been erased into a world that would have to make room for them, and you stood aside from your own miracle like a servant at someone else's wedding.

Corvane came over the ridge an hour too late. The Gate sealed in his face. I hope he lives a long time with that. I hope it sits in his chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

**The fifth thing is this morning.**

Because here is where the letter stops being history and becomes news, Edmund, and I've been circling it for three pages like a moth at a lamp.

You came to the archive today. You do that most days now — drift in around midmorning, apologetic, hands full of some small errand you invented as an excuse. Today you brought bread. You set it on my table next to the open atlas and you frowned at the map spread there — the free cities, the coast, the mountain wall — and you said, "This range. Why do I always stop looking at it here?" and tapped the place where the Thornfell passes are drawn, the place where the cartographers leave a blank space and write, in small embarrassed letters, *here be nothing.*

"There's nothing there," I said. Which is true. Officially.

"That's what I thought," you said. "But I keep coming back to it. Like a word on the tip of the tongue." And then you smiled at me — that easy, unburdened smile of yours, the smile of a man with no country and no debts and no past heavier than last week — and you said, "You always look sad when I ask about maps. Have I told you that? You look like someone keeping a promise."

"I am," I said.

"To whom?"

And I almost told you. Right there, over the bread, with the afternoon light coming through the archive windows and the dust turning gold above the shelves where the salvaged records live — the recipe, the half-song, the child's prayer. I almost said *to you.* But a promise kept in one breath is a promise half-kept, and I made you a different kind of vow at a door in a cliff face: one evening at a time. So I said, "To a friend. I'll introduce you sometime."

"When?"

"Soon," I said. "Tomorrow, maybe. Come for tea."

* * *

So: tomorrow.

I've decided how to begin. Not with the palace, not with the blood and the riders — those can wait; grief keeps, it's the one food that never spoils. I'll begin the way Papa began his stories, the way his mother began hers, back in a country that isn't on the maps: with a hearth, and a welcome, and the character carved over doors that has no translation.

I'll draw it for him in the ashes of the fire, the way he drew it for me in snow. I'll say, *this one means hearth. This one means return. And this one — this one just says welcome.* And maybe the shape of it will snag on something deep in him, some splinter the Gate missed, some grain of ash that wouldn't blow away. Maybe not. Maybe he'll simply listen, the way he listened in the snow camp, patient and attentive, a man learning a language he doesn't know he was born to.

Either way, I'll be there. Two cups of tea. The atlas open to the blank space in the mountains. And a story with no ending yet, because the ending is the part we're still writing, one evening at a time, the both of us —

— or rather, me for the both of us, and him learning to be someone new, and the two of those things turning out, night after night, to be the same work.

Come for tea, Edmund. There's a country I want you to meet.

It begins: *once, there was a kingdom that the maps forgot — but I remembered.*

*Sera*

* * *

He came early.

She heard his step on the archive stairs — she knew it now, the unhurried tread of a man with nowhere he was expected — and she closed the letter, folded it into the drawer where the others slept, thirty-one evenings of them, and set out the second cup before he knocked.

"You're here already," he said, pleased, holding up a paper bag like a trophy. "I found honey cakes. The baker swears by the recipe. Says it came from over the mountains, from a place nobody's heard of."

"Did he now." She took the bag, and their fingers touched, and neither of them hurried.

"He says the woman who sold it to his grandmother wrote the recipe in letters he'd never seen anywhere else." Edmund settled into the chair across from her, in the lamplight, beside the open book of maps. He frowned faintly at the blank space where the mountains should be, the way he always did, like a man hearing music through a wall. "Sera. Can I ask you something strange?"

"You can ask me anything," she said, and meant it, and this time the meaning didn't frighten her at all.

He leaned forward. "Teach me the word over the door. The one you mentioned. The one that just says welcome."

Outside, the free cities went about their evening — carts and gulls and children shouting in six languages, the ones who came through and the ones who were always here, indistinguishable now, which was rather the point. Inside, Sera took the poker and knelt at the hearth, and drew the old character slowly in the soft grey ash, stroke by stroke, the way it had been drawn in snow, in stone, in the margins of forbidden books, in the patience of everyone who had ever refused to forget.

"This one," she said, "means hearth."

His eyes followed her hand. Something moved behind them — far off, deep down, like a lamp carried from room to room.

"And this one," she said, drawing again, "means return."
