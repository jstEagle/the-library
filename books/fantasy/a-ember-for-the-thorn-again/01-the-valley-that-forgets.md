## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with living in a valley where magic cannot follow is that you learn to be quiet in ways most people never have to think about.

Theo Thornbury had been quiet for twenty-six years. Not shy-quiet, not sullen-quiet—Hollowmere quiet, which was a different discipline entirely. It meant never humming while sharpening a blade, because a hum could become a rhythm and a rhythm could become the shape of an oath. It meant never letting a word like *wake* or *answer* or *remember* pass their lips within a hand's breadth of steel. It meant, on the bad days, counting syllables the way other people counted coins.

Most mornings it was just habit. This morning it was habit too, but the fog was thick on the terraces and the fog always made Theo's skin itch, as if the valley's silence were a coat that didn't quite fit and never had.

They came down from the loft over the smithy before dawn, as they did every morning, and banked the coals, and set the kettle on. Below, the village of Hollowmere was arranging itself out of the dark the way it did every day: lamplight in the baker's window, the clatter of the well-chain, old Marta's goat complaining about the weather as though the weather were a personal insult. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Theo had spent their whole life learning to love them, and mostly they succeeded.

Mostly.

They ate bread and honey standing up, the way their mentor Aldous had always scolded them for. *Sit down and eat like the day deserves you*, he used to say. Aldous had been dead eleven months now, buried up on the north slope under a stone Theo had carved themselves, and Theo still caught themselves turning to say something and finding only the empty chair, the empty hook where his coat used to hang, the empty everything.

They took their knife down from the shelf over the hearth.

It was a good knife—good steel, honest balance, the kind of blade a smith's apprentice makes a dozen times before getting one right. Theo had made this one at fourteen, and Aldous had helped with the hilt, wrapping the grip in leather and setting a small brass fitting at the pommel. Theo had never thought much about that fitting. It was just a shape. A curling line, like a flame seen sideways, or a tongue of fire folded into itself.

They wore it every day. Everyone in Hollowmere wore a knife. You needed one for bread and rope and stubborn bootlaces. Nobody thought anything of it.

That was the whole art of it, really. Being nobody's thought.

* * *

The morning's work was a scythe for the Halloran farm and a hinge for the chapel door, and Theo did both with the easy, mindless pleasure of hands that knew their business. Hollowmere had one smith—had only ever needed one—and old Bram had taken Theo on at nine years old because Aldous had asked him to, and Bram had never once asked why Aldous, who was no kin, had made the request. Bram was a man who understood that some questions were like poking a bruise: the answer didn't change the hurt, and the asking made it worse.

"You've got ash on your face," Bram said around midday, not looking up from his ledger.

"I've got ash on everything. It's a smithy."

"You've got ash on your face and you've been somewhere else all morning. Wherever it is, come back. The Hallorans pay for a scythe, not a sculpture."

Theo grinned and came back. That was the other thing about Hollowmere: it noticed you. It noticed whether your chimney smoked, whether your gate was latched, whether you'd been crying or hadn't slept. A valley that forgot the rest of the world did not forget its own people. Theo had always found that both comforting and, in some small chamber of the heart they kept locked even from themselves, suffocating.

Because here was the thing Theo could not say, not to Bram, not to the Hallorans, not to anyone under this wide gray sky: they loved these people, and these people did not know them. They knew a smith's apprentice with steady hands and a dry joke. They did not know that when Theo held a blade too long, it seemed to lean toward them like a plant toward light. They did not know that once, at thirteen, half-dreaming by the forge, Theo had whispered something half-remembered and half-invented over a cooling poker, and the poker had glowed like a held breath, and Aldous had come through the door like a storm and gripped their wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *never, never, never*.

They did not know that Theo had spent every day since being two people: the one who lived here, and the one who kept the other one folded up small and hidden, like a letter never sent.

The peddler came at the end of the week.

* * *

His name was Odo Fen, and he came through Hollowmere twice a year with salt, needles, dyed thread, news of dubious quality, and a cart that squeaked in three keys. He was a round, cheerful man with a laugh like a hinge himself, and the children adored him, and the adults tolerated him, and Theo had sold him a new axle bracket two visits running.

This visit, the fog had lifted and the sun was doing its best, and the whole village had a loose, market-day feel. Odo had set up by the well and was holding court, selling ribbons to the Weaver girls and a dubious cure for gout to old Marta, who bought one every visit and never used it. Theo came down with the axle bracket under one arm and a fresh loaf from the baker's, and joined the edge of the crowd the way you do when you're delivering something but the something can wait.

"And what's the news from over the pass?" asked Widow Cale, who asked every time, and Odo gave his usual answer—roads, taxes, a wedding in some town whose name meant nothing here—and then, because he was in a good mood and the crowd was warm, he added something extra.

"Soldiers on the roads, though. Crown men. Witch-hunters, some say, though they wear no livery that says so. Asking questions in the river towns. About the old gifts, if you follow me." He winked broadly. "Course, there's no witches in Hollowmere. Never have been. That's what the valley's for, eh?"

There was a comfortable ripple of laughter. The valley's for. That was the phrase. Hollowmere was where magic couldn't follow—every child learned it before they learned their letters. The valley forgot, the world forgot, and everyone lived.

Theo laughed too. It came out sounding almost real.

Odo's cart was by the well, and Theo set the axle bracket on the tailboard and dug in their satchel for the loaf to trade, and in leaning over, in the flat noon light, with their knife at their belt swinging forward as they bent—Odo Fen looked down at the hilt.

Theo saw it happen. Saw the cheerful round face go still, the way water goes still before it freezes. Saw his eyes fix on the little brass fitting at the pommel—the curling flame, the folded fire—and something move behind them, quick and cold, like a fish turning under ice.

Odo knew the shape. Theo understood that with a certainty that dropped through them like a stone through deep water. Somewhere out there, beyond the pass, beyond the forgetting, there were people who had been taught what that sigil meant. People who had been taught to fear it, or to hunt it, or both.

Odo looked up. Met Theo's eyes. And smiled.

It was the worst smile Theo had ever seen. It was a smile with all the warmth taken out of it and something else put in its place, something careful, and Odo Fen—hinge-laughed, ribbon-selling, gout-cure-peddling Odo Fen—said, "Fine work, that bracket," in a voice pitched perfectly, deliberately, for no one else to hear, and then, louder, cheerful as ever: "Well! Long road yet, and the light's wasting. Best be off."

He didn't haggle. Odo Fen had never once in his life not haggled. He took the loaf, tossed the bracket in the cart, pressed a coin into Theo's hand—too much coin—and climbed up onto the bench, and the cart squeaked off down the valley road in three discordant keys, faster than it had come in.

The crowd dispersed, murmuring pleasantly about ribbons and gout. The sun kept shining. Marta's goat complained. Nothing in Hollowmere had changed, and everything had.

Theo stood by the well with a coin too heavy in their palm and did the arithmetic they had been dreading without knowing they were dreading it. The pass road was two days to the river towns. Odo traveled fast when he traveled scared, or traveled paid. Which meant that somewhere between six days and a fortnight from now, someone might mention, in some river town, to someone who asked questions for a living, that in a valley where magic cannot follow, there lived a smith's apprentice who wore a swordspeaker's sigil on their belt every day of their life.

*Never*, Aldous had said, gripping their wrist, all those years ago. *Never let them see what you are.*

They hadn't. They had been so careful. They had been quiet for twenty-six years, and it had taken one bent back, one flat noon light, one swing of a knife hilt, and it was done.

Theo walked home through the village they loved, past people who loved them back without knowing them, and every greeting felt like something being taken from them in advance. Bram called out about the Halloran scythe and Theo answered and heard their own voice from very far away. The valley held its fog and its silence and its forgetting, and for the first time in their life Theo understood that the forgetting had never been a wall around Hollowmere at all.

It had been a wall around them. And someone had just found the door.

* * *

That night Theo climbed the north slope to Aldous's grave, because there was no one else to talk to and there never had been, not really. The stone was plain, the way Aldous would have wanted, and the moss was already taking the letters back, which Aldous would have found funny.

"I think I've been sold," Theo told the grave. "For the price of a loaf of bread and a bad gout cure."

The wind came up the valley, smelling of pine and cold stone, and said nothing, the way the valley always said nothing. Theo sat with their back against the stone until the stars came out, and thought about running—really thought about it, for the first time. Packed bags. Night roads. A life of being no one anywhere, instead of someone only here.

But the valley was their whole shape. Leaving it meant leaving Bram, and the Hallorans, and Marta and her goat, and the well-chain's morning clatter, and every small ordinary thing they had built their quiet life out of, one careful day at a time. Leaving meant admitting there was something to run from.

And maybe—this was the thought they kept circling, the splinter in the mind—maybe Odo had seen nothing. Maybe a brass fitting was a brass fitting. Maybe the world was not about to come through the pass looking for them.

Three days later, the answer came up the valley road.

Theo was at the forge when the lookout boy came pelting down from the ridge, breathless, shouting the one sentence Hollowmere had not heard in living memory:

"Riders! Riders at the ford—and they're wearing the crown!"
