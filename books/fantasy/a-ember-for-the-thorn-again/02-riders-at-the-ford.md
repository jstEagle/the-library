## 2. Riders at the Ford

The bells told us before anyone did.

Hollowmere had three of them—cast from valley copper, tuned to a pitch that carried the length of the vale and no farther—and in my whole life they had rung twice: once for the harvest, once for Aldous's funeral. Now they rang a third time, fast and ugly, and every hand in the mill field went still around me.

*Riders at the ford,* the pattern said. *Riders at the ford.*

I set down the grain rake with a care that fooled nobody. Marta Ellery, whose family had shared our well for four generations, looked at me the way you look at a door you're not sure is locked.

"Theo," she said. Just my name. Just that.

"I'll go home," I said. "I've been fixing fences all morning."

We both knew I hadn't been near a fence.

* * *

There were eleven riders, and they crossed the ford in single file like they were rehearsing it. That was the first wrong thing. Crown soldiers ride like weather—they come loud and scattered and full of their own noise. These came quiet, spaced evenly, hooves finding the stones without splashing. The gray water barely noticed them, and something about that made my stomach turn over. In Hollowmere we believed the valley forgot what passed through it. Watching those riders cross, I understood for the first time that forgetting could be survived. It could also be walked through.

They dismounted at the green by the meeting house, eleven of them in charcoal-and-white livery, and they did not spread out to search or post sentries or do any of the things soldiers do when they expect trouble. They simply stood in a line and waited, which meant they already knew there would be no trouble. Which meant they already knew exactly what they'd come for.

She came forward alone. Tall, unhurried, her hair bound back so tight it looked painful. She wore no plume, no braid of rank, nothing ornamental—only a captain's plain gorget and a longsword worn low on the hip, the way old soldiers wear them, like an arm rather than an accessory. Her eyes moved across the gathering villagers the way an auditor's pen moves down a ledger. Not hostile. Just counting.

"Good afternoon," she said, and her voice carried without effort, warm as a hearth and about as dangerous as one if you didn't think about what hearths burn. "My name is Captain Vess Arden, in service to the Crown. My writ authorizes inquiry into matters of arcane regulation within the realm's territories." She produced a document with a seal heavy enough to anchor a boat, and held it up, and did not pass it around. "I want to be clear from the start: this is not an accusation against any of you. Hollowmere has been a loyal and peaceful community for a very long time. The Crown remembers its friends."

Nobody spoke. In Hollowmere we are not a talkative people with strangers; the valley taught us early that words drift out over the ridgeline if you let them.

Captain Arden smiled at our silence like it confirmed something pleasant.

"I'm looking for one item," she said. "One person, really. A practitioner of the old blade-oaths—a swordspeaker. They would have lived among you quietly. Perhaps unknowingly, even to themselves." A pause, delicate as a scalpel laid on cloth. "Perhaps knowingly. We understand how attached people become to their neighbors' secrets."

Old Tam Wicke, who has chaired the village council since before I could walk, stepped forward with his hands folded behind his back and his chin up, and I watched a good man prepare to lie badly.

"There's no such thing here, Captain," he said. "No magic comes into the valley. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone does," Arden agreed pleasantly. "It's why we're here, actually. Places where magic cannot follow are rare, and interesting, and occasionally load-bearing. But you've answered a question I didn't ask, Elder, which suggests you may have answers to questions I will ask. So let's begin." She unfolded a small square of paper between two fingers. "Three days ago, a licensed peddler named Corvan Hale crossed the western ridge out of your valley. At the toll station at Marrow Bridge he reported a sighting. A knife hilt, he said. Silver, worn smooth, worked with a sigil—a thorned rose wrapped in a sword. He said it belonged to a young person who ran the ferry rope and mended scythes and never once, in all his visits, let him touch the blade." She looked up. "He drew it for us. From memory. He was quite precise."

The bottom dropped out of my chest. Corvan. Corvan with the crooked smile and the chestnuts in winter and the way he'd gripped my wrist on the lane two days ago and gone white and left his apples spilled in the road. I'd thought—I'd told myself—he was frightened *for* me. Frightened men run. I hadn't understood until that moment, standing in the crowd with Marta's shoulder hard against mine, that frightened men also sell. That the same fear could go either direction, and I'd never get to know whether he'd hesitated at Marrow Bridge. Whether he'd sat there with the drawing half-made and hated himself. Whether it had mattered at all.

"He must have been mistaken," Tam said, and his voice cracked on *mistaken*, and Captain Arden's eyebrows rose with genuine kindness, which was the worst thing yet.

"Elder," she said gently, "you're doing that again. Answering questions I haven't asked." She turned the little drawing toward the crowd, though at forty paces none of us needed it. I had polished that sigil every week since I was nine years old, because Aldous told me a neglected oath-sigil was worse than none. A thorned rose wrapped in a sword. The mark of the last sworn line of Thornbury. My name, practically, carved in silver.

"Here is what happens next," Arden said, folding the paper away. "Nothing, today. I am not going to kick over flowerpots or drag elders into the mud; you've been gracious hosts to a stranger's questions and I return courtesy for courtesy." She let that sit. "But my writ permits me to remain in Hollowmere for as long as the inquiry requires, and inquiries require lodging, provisions, and cooperation. My company will billet at the meeting house. Your council will provide a guide familiar with every holding in the valley." Her gaze swept us one more time, unhurried, ledger-patient. "And whoever holds that knife should consider, tonight, in the privacy of their own conscience, what one object is worth against the comfort of everyone they've ever known. Because I promise you—" and here the warmth left her voice entirely, briefly, like a cloud crossing a window, "—I will find it. The only variable is how many people are standing near it when I do."

She thanked Tam for his time. She complimented our barley on the way past the mill field. She asked after the health of the ford, of all things, as if she hadn't just read it like a page.

Then she saw me.

Not dramatically. She didn't point or call out or reach for a weapon. She simply looked at me—one beat longer than the ledger required, the way you pause at a number that doesn't sum—and inclined her head with perfect courtesy, the way you greet someone whose name you know and whose arrest isn't scheduled until Thursday.

Then she walked into the meeting house and closed the door.

* * *

The council met in Tam's kitchen, and I wasn't invited, and they met anyway knowing I'd be listening through the wall because they left the shutter cracked for me, which told me everything about what they'd decided before they'd decided it.

"—can't give her anything, she has no proof—"

"She has a drawing, Tam. She has a peddler's sworn statement and eleven crown swords and a writ that says she can stay till midwinter if she likes—"

"So we say the knife was sold. Say it went over the ridge years ago—"

"Lie to a crown captain. In writing. With the sigil still sitting in the owner's kitchen drawer not twenty feet from her billet—"

"It's *Theo*," Marta said, and her voice was low and fierce and broke me more cleanly than any threat could have. "It's Theo, Tam. They mended my son's leg brace without charging me. They pulled the Hollis girl out of the flooded quarry. Whatever it is they are, whatever it is they're carrying—"

*I'm not carrying anything,* I almost said through the wall, and stopped, because I didn't know it was true anymore. There had always been something. The way steel warmed under my hands when I hummed certain syllables under my breath. The way Aldous had flinched, near the end, whenever I spoke a promise aloud. The way the valley itself had been chosen for me before I could choose anything at all.

They argued until dark. In the end they gave Arden her guide—Marta's boy Joren, sixteen, terrified, sworn to lead the hunters in wide useless circles—and they gave her nothing else, and every adult in that kitchen knew it would not be enough, and did it anyway.

I stood in the cold with my back against the wall of a house full of people risking charges of obstructing a crown inquiry, exile, worse—for a secret I never chose, for a sigil I polish weekly because a dead man told me to, for an ember I did not even know I was carrying.

That was the part that curdled, sitting there in the dark while the hunters' watch-fires bloomed orange in the meeting house windows. Not the fear. Fear I could carry; I'd carried it my whole life like a stone in a pocket. It was the arithmetic. Eleven riders, one child hostage to a guide, a whole kitchen of good people spending themselves like coin—and all of it spent on *me*, and I hadn't even known the price of the thing I was guarding.

I went home and took the knife from the drawer and held it over the cold hearth. One throw into the fire. Let the silver slump, let the sigil blur, let Arden find a dead thing and a live liar and nothing more.

My hand wouldn't open.

The blade lay against my palm, warm as a sleeping bird, and somewhere under my ribs something answered that warmth—an ember-deep pulse, patient and old, turning over in its sleep like something that has been waiting a long time to be asked.

A knock at the door. Three raps, uneven, hurried—not a soldier's knock.

Through the window I saw Wren Calloway, the disgraced royal mapmaker who kept to the far cottage and owed Aldous debts nobody talked about, glancing over his shoulder at the fires across the green.

"Open the door, Thornbury," he hissed. "Before those torches finish burning, I need to show you exactly how much trouble you're in—and how little of it you've seen."
