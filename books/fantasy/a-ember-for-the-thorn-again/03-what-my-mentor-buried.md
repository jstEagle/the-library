## 3. What My Mentor Buried

Aldous's cottage smelled the way it always had: lamp oil, dried mint, old paper. It should have been a comfort. Instead it felt like walking into a room where someone had just stopped speaking mid-sentence.

Theo closed the door behind them and stood in the dark a moment, letting their eyes adjust. The hunters were still at the ford. Vess Arden had promised to return within three days—she'd said it with a courtesy so precise it felt like a blade laid flat against the throat—and Theo had spent one sleepless night watching the village lie for them, badly, and decided that if they were going to be the reason Hollowmere burned, they would at least know why.

The answer had to be here. Aldous never told me anything without leaving it written down twice.

They started with the desk. Drawers of chalk stubs, spare knife-hones, thread wound on wooden spools. A tin of peppermints gone cloudy with age. Aldous had kept everything the way he'd kept his students—loosely, patiently, as if all of it might someday be needed.

The false back was behind the third drawer, exactly where a younger Theo had once found a cache of sweets and been sworn to secrecy. Theo pressed the panel, felt the click, and pulled.

No sweets. Letters. Dozens of them, bound in twine, and beneath them a folded scrap of heavy vellum marked with a sigil Theo knew better than their own heartbeat.

A sword crossed by a tongue of flame. The swordspeaker's mark. Burned into the vellum with a hot iron, not drawn—the way you'd brand livestock.

Theo sat down on Aldous's stool because their legs stopped being interested in holding them up.

* * *

He found her at dusk, the summer Theo turned seven, when magic still came to Theo like birdsong—unasked, unremarked, ordinary.

*You're certain,* Aldous said, not a question really. He crouched at the edge of the tree line where the woman waited, travel-stained, one arm cradled against her ribs. Theo remembered being hoisted onto the fence rail nearby, told to hold very still, told this was a game. They remembered the woman turning to look at them and going pale—not at what she saw, but at what she apparently didn't.

*It's already waking itself,* she said. *Listen to her hands. She speaks to things that aren't there and they listen. Captain, you have days, not weeks.*

*I'm not a captain anymore.* Aldous's voice had that flatness Theo would only learn to recognize decades later: the sound of a man bracing. *And I won't cut it out of her, Sera. That's not removal. That's butchery.*

*Then bury it. Deep enough that even she can't find it. You swore you'd take the last ember out of the war's reach. Here is your chance—no better vault ever forged than a living oath-holder inside a valley where the art cannot follow.*

*A child is not a vault.*

*No,* the woman said, and something in her voice cracked open. *But she is the only one left, and the men coming for her will open her like a letter. Choose, Aldous. Quickly.*

* * *

Theo read by candlelight with hands that would not stay steady.

The letters spanned thirty years. Most were from the same hand—Sera Vael, the script loosening over time from military precision into something hurried, hunted. The earliest were formal reports. The latest were pleas.

*The ember is the last seed of the war-magic,* she had written, in a letter dated the winter after Theo's eighth birthday. *Everything else we broke or buried. What sleeps in the girl will wake only if spoken to by an oath of full binding—and no such oath has been spoken since the field at Carrowmere. Keep her wordless, Aldous. Keep her small. A swordspeaker who never swears is only a person.*

Keep her small. Theo read the line four times. Their whole life arranged itself around it, ugly and neat, like a solved puzzle they'd never consented to be.

Every lesson Aldous had given on silence. Every time he'd caught Theo mouthing words at a dulled blade and gone white as bone, gripping their shoulder hard enough to bruise, saying *not that, never that*. Every winter festival where Theo had bitten their tongue bloody rather than speak the toast over the ceremonial sword. They'd thought it was discipline. They'd thought it was protection.

It was containment. They were the thing being contained.

* * *

Another memory, older, half-dissolved: fever, age six. Lying in this very cottage, in the bed upstairs, while Aldous held their hands and talked steadily through the night about nothing—fences, weather, the names of clouds. And underneath his voice, felt more than heard, a low repeated murmur that wasn't words yet was all words, pressing down and down until the fire in Theo's blood went quiet and stayed quiet.

They had thought it was a dream. A kind man talking a sick child to sleep.

It was a burial. He'd buried the ember in them that night—or buried it deeper, after Sera first placed it—and then he'd spent forty years standing guard over the grave, calling it mentorship.

* * *

There was one more letter, separate from the rest, sealed and addressed in Aldous's own careful hand: *For Theo, if the valley fails.*

Theo almost didn't open it. Almost set it back with the others and walked out and let whatever was coming come. But Vess Arden's courtesy was waiting at the ford, and the elders were lying for Theo with sweat running down their necks, and cowardice, they found, had a shorter reach than curiosity.

*If you are reading this, then the peddlers' talk has caught up with us, or the ember has begun to stir on its own, and either way my long cowardice is spent.*

I want you to hate me a little, and then I want you to hear me out.

Yes, they put it in you. Sera carried it out of the burning of the Collegium with half her company dead, and every power left in the world wanted it—crown, covenant, carrion-birds all. A vault can be cracked. A mountain can be mined. But a child in a valley where magic cannot follow, guarded by an old man whose whole art was patience—that, they could not touch.

I chose Hollowmere before I chose you. Know that first, because it is the truth and it is shameful. Then the woman died, and you were seven years old and already speaking to door hinges, and the choice stopped being abstract.

Was it wrong? I have turned it over ten thousand nights. I hid a weapon in a beloved thing and called it safety. I watched you grow around the weight and told myself the weight was why you were kind—because you knew, without knowing, what it costs to carry what could kill everyone you love.

I taught you to be silent because your silence was the wall between the world and the war. If I stole anything from you, child, it was the version of your life where you got to be ordinary. I have regretted it every day and I would do it again tomorrow. Both of these are true. Hold them together if you can; I never managed it.

The sigil enclosed is proof of what you carry, if anyone comes asking, though I pray no one ever does. If hunters come, run to Wren Calloway. I saved his life once and he owes me a route out of anywhere. Tell him the debt is called due.

Do not speak the ember awake. Whatever they threaten, whatever you feel it wanting—there is no version of that fire that leaves the world as it found it.

You were never my weapon. You were my family. Forgive the difference I made you carry.

—A.

Theo finished reading and found they had no tears. That frightened them most of all. Grief they knew how to carry. This was something with grief's face and a stranger's hands.

* * *

The candle had burned to a thumb's width when Theo finally moved. They went through the cottage room by room, seeing it new. Not a home. A keep. The mint drying in bundles—his nerves, dosed nightly against discovery. The practice yard out back with its deliberately blunted blades—a fencing master's tools, or a jailer's. The seat he always took at the tavern, back to the wall, facing the door, which everyone had laughed about for years. *Old soldiers*, people said. *Habits.*

He was a warden. Forty years of it. And I was the cell.

They should hate him. Part of them did, cleanly and without apology. But another part kept doing arithmetic Theo didn't want done: without the burial, the hunters come when you're seven. Without Hollowmere, you're already ash. He bought you a life with your own body and paid for it with his whole one, and never once, not once, asked you to thank him.

*Betrayal and shelter from the same hand.* Theo pressed the heels of their palms against their eyes until colors bloomed. *He warned me about this. Hold them together if you can.*

They couldn't. But they could carry both, which had apparently always been the family talent.

Outside, the light had gone long and amber. Down the lane, someone was ringing the well-bell—the alarm rhythm, two and one, two and one—which hadn't sounded since before Theo was born.

They were moving before thought caught up, letters stuffed inside their coat, the branded sigil clenched in one fist. At the door they paused, one breath, looking back at the shape of the life Aldous had built around them like a wall.

Three days, Arden had said.

The bell said the riders hadn't waited.

Theo stepped out and began to run toward the ford, where Wren Calloway—the disgraced mapmaker who owed Aldous a debt he likely didn't know he owed—was about to become the most important man in Theo's remaining life.
