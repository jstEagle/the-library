## 4. The Cartographer's Debt

Wren Calloway had three rules for surviving in a valley that wanted him forgotten, and he'd broken all of them by noon.

Rule one: don't get involved. Rule two: don't let anyone see your work. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, draw a map for free.

He was breaking rule three right now, hunched over his table in the back room of the Drowned Lantern with a sheet of good vellum and a memory that wouldn't leave him alone. The map taking shape under his pen was the old smuggler's road out of Hollowmere—the one that wound up through the slate quarries and over the Widow's Tooth before dropping into the king's highway on the far side. He'd walked it twice in his first year here, back when walking it was still a hobby and not a contingency. No one else in the valley knew it whole. Aldous had. Aldous had known everything, the insufferable old man, and now Aldous was dead, and Wren was drawing maps for a dead man's student because some debts didn't die with the debtor.

The ink was barely dry when the knock came—two soft raps, then nothing, like whoever it was had already lost their nerve.

Wren slid the vellum into the loose floorboard under the table and opened the door on Theo Thornbury, who looked exactly the way Wren expected: like someone who'd spent the night reading letters they were never meant to read.

"You found them," Wren said. Not a question. "Aldous's letters."

Theo's jaw tightened. They stood in the doorway with rain beading on their shoulders, refusing to come in, which was very Theo. "How did you know about the letters?"

"Because I helped him write half of them." Wren stepped back from the door. "Get inside before someone sees you standing there looking guilty. You have a face made for confessions."

"I'm not here to confess."

"Everyone who comes to my door is here to confess or to buy something, and you've never once bought anything." He poured two cups of the tavern's terrible cider and pushed one across the table. Theo sat, finally, but held the cup like it might be evidence. "You're here because you went through his things, you learned what's sleeping inside you, and you realized the valley won't hold much longer. Am I close?"

"You knew," Theo said quietly. "You knew what he put in me."

"I knew he chose Hollowmere for a reason. I knew you weren't an accident of placement, you were the entire point of it." Wren drank. The cider tasted like regret fermented in apples. "I didn't know the specifics. He kept those closer than his own pulse. But I'm a cartographer, Thornbury. My whole life is knowing where things are hidden."

"And your debt?"

Something cold moved through Wren's chest at that. Of course they'd told him. Aldous, you sentimental ghost. "Twelve years ago I falsified a survey for the Crown. Moved a river on paper so a lord could claim land that fed four hundred people. Aldous found out—not the Crown, not the magistrates, Aldous, because the man could find a lie the way water finds a crack. He didn't turn me in. He made me redraw every map I'd ever corrupted, and he paid for my silence with his own, and I've been hiding in this damp valley ever since, drawing honest maps no one will ever see." He set down the cup. "So yes. I owe him. And he's dead, which means I owe you, which is the worst possible arrangement, because dead men collect interest."

Theo almost smiled. Almost. It died somewhere on the way. "There are hunters at the ford."

"I know. Six riders came through yesterday asking about knives and sigils. Very polite. The polite ones are always worse." Wren pulled up the floorboard and laid the vellum between them. "This is your way out. Slate quarries to the Widow's Tooth, down the far side to the mill at Corren's Ford. From there, if you can pay, the river traders will take you anywhere the Crown's writ runs thin. Three days' walk if the weather holds. Two if it doesn't and you're motivated."

Theo studied the map the way they studied everything—like it might lie to them. "And you're coming with me."

It wasn't a question either, and Wren hated that. "I'm pointing at the road. That's the extent of my heroism."

"The captain took the sigil off my knife hilt to the capital. She'll be back. And when she comes back—" Theo's voice caught, just once, and Wren pretended not to notice, "—she'll want to know why the elders lied for me. Bramble. Old Marta. The ferryman's boys. They put themselves between me and her, and I never asked them to."

"No, you just existed near them for twenty years. Terrible of you."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." Wren rolled the map and tied it with cord. "Fine. Here is what I'm offering, and hear all of it before you argue. I walk you as far as the quarries. I show you the cut-through at the second marker stone that isn't on any royal map because I erased it from every royal map, which is the only clever thing I've done in a decade. After that you're on your own, because I am not brave, Thornbury. I'm accurate. There's a difference, and the difference is that accurate people stay alive."

Theo looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the tavern noise rose and fell like breathing.

"You erased roads," Theo said. "For twelve years you've been unmaking the kingdom's maps, one honest line at a time."

"Someone had to balance the books."

"Aldous would have liked hearing you say that."

"Aldous liked hearing himself talk. I just provided the geography." Wren stood, shouldered his satchel, and felt the strange lightness of a man about to do something stupid. "We leave tonight, after the ford watch changes. Bring nothing that jingles."

* * *

They left at moonrise, and the plan survived until dawn, which for one of Wren's plans was practically a golden age.

The quarries were exactly as he remembered—terraces of pale stone stacked against the mountainside like the ribs of something enormous and long dead. Theo moved well, quiet and sure-footed, and said almost nothing, which Wren had decided was their natural state. They reached the second marker stone as the sky went grey, and Wren was just pointing out the cut-through—a seam in the rock face narrow enough to miss if you weren't looking for the absence of it—when he heard the dogs.

Not hunting dogs. Road dogs. The kind the Crown's wardens bred for following a man's ink-stained hands across open country.

"They're not following us," Theo said, head cocked, listening. "They're going down. Toward the village."

Wren climbed the quarry rim and looked back at Hollowmere, small and grey in the valley floor, smoke rising from its chimneys in thin, orderly columns. At the ford, the six riders had become twenty. And in the square outside the meetinghouse, Captain Vess Arden's soldiers were doing something that turned Wren's stomach even at this distance: they were lining people up against the wall. Bramble the smith. Marta, who was seventy and had never raised her voice in her life. The ferryman's boys, still in their nightshirts.

Hostages. In a valley whose only crime was forgetting.

"We go back," Theo said.

Wren closed his eyes. "No."

"They're there because of me."

"They're there because a peddler sold your name for coin and a captain followed it. Going back doesn't fix that. Going back means three prisoners instead of a dozen." He heard his own voice doing the thing it did—flat, reasonable, the voice of a man who had learned that sentiment was how empires got built. "Thornbury. Listen to me. I have watched good people walk toward fires to prove they cared, and I have drawn the maps of the villages those fires left behind. The arithmetic never changes."

Theo turned and looked at him, and there was something in their face that Wren recognized with genuine horror: certainty. Not the loud kind. The quiet kind, the kind that had already finished arguing with itself.

"You said you owed Aldous," Theo said. "He used to say a map is a promise about where things are. So tell me, Wren—where does your promise end? At the quarries? At your own doorstep?" They started back down the slope, sliding on the wet stone, catching themselves, not stopping. "I'm going back. You can draw the route I take. Or you can walk it with me."

Wren stood on the quarry rim with his beautiful map in his satchel and his three rules in ruins around him, and watched the only person in Hollowmere more stubborn than a dead cartographer march toward twenty armed riders to surrender to a woman who collected swordspeakers the way other collectors collected coins.

"Damn it," he said, to the mountain, to Aldous, to the entire concept of decency. "Damn it, damn it, *damn* it."

Then he went down the slope after them, because apparently rule one had been optional all along.

They were halfway to the village when the first bell began to ring—and it wasn't the alarm bell. It was the chapel bell, rung slow and steady, the way Hollowmere rang it for a funeral. Below them, in the square, Vess Arden turned her horse toward the sound, and Wren saw her lift one hand, and the soldiers stopped moving.

Someone had come out of the crowd. Someone small.

A child, standing in front of the hostages with her arms spread wide, silent as stone, while the whole valley held its breath.
