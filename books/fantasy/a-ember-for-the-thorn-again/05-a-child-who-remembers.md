## 5. A Child Who Remembers

**Theo**

The girl was waiting at the mill when they came down through the barley, sitting on the overturned wheel like she'd been placed there by someone with an opinion about composition. She had a slate hanging from a cord around her neck and charcoal stubs in both fists, and she did not look surprised to see them.

Theo stopped at the fence line. "You're the cooper's orphan."

Pip nodded.

"You can't speak."

Another nod. Then she held up her left hand—four fingers, then pointed at Wren, then made a walking motion across her palm.

"She's counting us," Wren said. "Gods. She's counting whether we're enough."

"We're not," Theo said. "But we're what's here." They looked at the girl a long moment. Behind them, smoke stood over Hollowmere in three thin columns—the elders' houses, searched. Somewhere back there, Vess Arden was asking questions in that soft voice, and neighbors who'd known Theo since before they could walk were answering badly on their behalf.

"Come on, then," Theo said, and held out a hand.

Pip didn't take it. She hopped off the wheel, slung her slate around front, and drew something quick and certain: a stick figure with a knife, another figure with a crown, and between them a bridge, which she crossed out with two hard strokes.

Then she took Theo's hand anyway.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had smuggled things out of the capital for eleven years—maps mostly, sometimes men—and he'd learned that every escape lived or died on the first hour. The first hour told you everything: who panicked, who lied, who slowed down. He watched the swordspeaker and the mute child move through the bracken north of the valley and revised his estimate upward, then downward again when Theo stopped for the fourth time to look back.

"We don't look back," Wren said. "That's the whole religion. We are people who do not look back."

"They held the ford for me. Old Marta stood in the water and told Vess I'd gone south years ago. She's seventy. She shook the whole time and she did it anyway."

"Yes. And she'll keep doing it better if you're not standing behind her breathing." Wren pulled his coat tighter against the drizzle. "I knew Aldous thirty years. He never once mentioned you carried anything but a grudge and bad posture."

"He didn't know about the ember either. Not until near the end."

"That's worse, actually."

They walked. The road narrowed into the old war-cut, where the hills had been carved away by engines no one alive could name, and the stone on either side ran strange and glassy, fused in places, like the land itself had been made to hold its tongue. Pip ran her hand along it as they passed. When Wren glanced back he saw she'd stopped at one stretch of the wall and was pressing her palm flat against the glassed rock, eyes closed, mouth working around nothing.

"Pip," Theo called softly.

She opened her eyes. Looked at Wren. And Wren felt the look go through him like a nail—because there was nothing childlike in it. It was the look of someone checking whether a wall would hold.

They made camp that night under a leaning shelf of stone, no fire. Wren divided hard bread three ways and watched the girl eat with a soldier's economy, crumbs saved, nothing wasted, and thought: *someone taught her hunger before Hollowmere did.* He'd assumed she'd been born in the valley. He was beginning to suspect he was wrong about nearly everything connected to this job.

Afterward she took out her slate and drew.

Wren meant to glance. He ended up staring. She worked fast, without hesitation, lines laid down like she was tracing something already there—a field of pikes, a hill crowned with a tower, ranks of figures rendered not as children draw soldiers but as a quartermaster draws them, in blocks, in columns, with banners marked. And above it all, drawn twice its proper scale, a single sword upright in the earth, and light coming off it in strokes so hard she'd nearly pushed through the slate.

"The Battle of the Red Ford," Wren said slowly. "That's the Red Ford. That's—" He counted the banner marks and felt his neck prickle. "There were twelve companies on the loyalist side. Twelve. It's in maybe four surviving documents, and none of them agree on the number, and none of them are pictures. There are no pictures. Nothing survived from that day but ash and argument."

Theo had come to stand over them. In the dark, their face was unreadable. "How old are you?" Theo asked gently.

Pip held up small fingers. Seven, perhaps eight.

"And you drew a battle that ended two hundred years ago," Theo said, "down to the company count."

Pip looked up at them. Then she did something Wren never forgot as long as he lived: she put her hand flat against her own chest, over the heart, and pressed until her fingers whitened. Her face didn't change at all. That was the worst part. No fear in it, no tears. Just pressure, patiently applied, from inside.

*A memory is a wound,* Aldous had written once, in a letter Wren had carried and never delivered. *Some folk are born remembering things that happened before them, and there is no medicine for it, only witness.*

Wren looked at the swordspeaker. The swordspeaker looked at the child. Nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't be a promise, and promises, in Wren's experience, were how you got people killed in an orderly fashion.

He banked his cynicism for the night and took first watch.

* * *

**Theo**

On the third day they reached the Sere, the brown river that marked the edge of the old war-lands, and the bridge that crossed it—an ancient thing, black stone, older than any kingdom, one of the few structures the war had failed to kill. Theo smelled the smoke before they saw the riders.

"They're ahead of us," Wren breathed. "How are they ahead of us?"

"Because Vess doesn't chase," Theo said. "She reads. She knows the only road out goes over this bridge, so she's making sure the far side belongs to her before we ever get there." Theo went flat against the ridge, and Wren and Pip folded down beside them, and below, half a mile off, the bridge came into view—and so did the fire.

It wasn't the hunters burning it. It was already burning, from the far end, and the flames were wrong—too steady, too white, fed by something poured and packed at the joints of the stones. Alchemist's fire. The hunters had decided that if they couldn't hold the crossing, no one would cross it at all, and the fire was eating toward the near end with the patience of something being paid by the hour.

"There's a ferry two leagues down," Wren said. "Two leagues back the way we came, with riders on our trail, toward a ferry that may or may not still exist—"

"No." Theo's voice had gone quiet, which Wren was learning meant the opposite of calm. "Look at the wind. The fire's moving slower than they think. The stones are wet from the rain two nights ago. If we run the span now, now, we make it. If we wait, we die in the reeds."

"If we run the span and you're wrong, the child dies."

Theo turned. For the first time since the valley, they looked afraid—not of the fire, or the riders, but of the sentence Wren had just said, and of how true it was. Then Theo looked at Pip, and Pip looked back, and held up her slate:

A bridge. A stick figure running. Underneath it, in letters too controlled for a seven-year-old: *I have been across worse.*

"You've been across worse," Theo repeated.

Pip nodded once.

"Right," Wren said. "Wonderful. I flee with a prophet and an optimist." He was already unbuckling his map case to sling it tight. "Run when I say. Middle of the span, do not stop, do not look at the water, do not look at the fire. The middle is where bridges fail and where runners hesitate. We do not hesitate. Agreed?"

They agreed.

The bridge was longer than it looked, the way terrible things always are. The heat reached them at the first arch, a dry breath against their faces, and by the middle of the span the white fire was roaring somewhere behind them and the stones underfoot were ticking, expanding, singing faintly in a register Theo felt in their teeth—and deep beneath their sternum, the ember stirred, turning over in its sleep like a dog hearing its name.

*Not yet,* Theo thought, and did not say it, because saying things aloud had consequences they were only beginning to understand. *Not yet, not here, not for this.*

They came off the far end into wet grass and kept running until the bridge was a line of orange behind them, and then the fire found the last dry joint of the near span and went up all at once, a column of white that stood for a moment against the gray sky like a struck match the size of a church.

Behind them, across the burning river, riders had arrived at the near shore. Small figures. One of them did not dismount. One of them stood at the broken edge looking straight across the water—at Theo, Theo was somehow certain of it—and did not move for a long time.

Then she raised one hand. Not a threat. Something almost like acknowledgment. *Well run,* the gesture said. And turned her horse away.

* * *

**Wren**

They made the tree-line by dusk, all three, and collapsed among the roots of a beech older than the war. Wren lay on his back listening to his own heartbeat argue with him, and beside him the child sat drawing by the last gray light, and the swordspeaker sat sharpening a knife that never seemed to need sharpening, and Wren understood, with the particular dread of a man who has spent his life traveling alone, that something was being built around him without his permission.

Nobody had said the word family. Nobody was going to. It was being assembled anyway, quietly, out of shared bread and shared flight and the specific intimacy of having run across a burning bridge together and lived.

Pip tugged his sleeve. Held up the slate.

She'd drawn the three of them. Stick figures, but careful ones: himself with his map case, Theo with their knife, herself with the slate hung round her neck. And above them, where a roof should be, she'd drawn nothing at all—just an empty outline, waiting.

"What's that, then? The house we're going to buy with the money we don't have?"

Pip shook her head. Tapped the empty outline. Tapped each of them in turn. Then she erased nothing, added nothing—just looked at him with those old, old eyes until he had to turn away and pretend to check the horizon.

"A roof," he said finally. "You're saying we're the roof."

She nodded.

"Well," Wren said, to the darkness, to the beech, to nobody. "That's the most optimistic cartography I've ever seen."

Far off to the east, past the burned river, a signal fire answered another signal fire, one after another after another—Vess Arden's web, drawing tight, calling in every rider she had. Whatever she wanted with the ember, she had stopped being patient about it.

And Theo, watching the lights, said quietly, as if to the knife, as if to the sleeping thing beneath their ribs: "We need to reach the Oathbreakers' Road before she does."

Pip was already drawing the road.
