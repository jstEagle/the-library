## 6. The Oathbreakers' Road

The old war-road didn't appear on any map I ever drew, which was, I'll admit now, a professional failure and a personal salvation.

I'd traced it once, years ago, from fragments—a survey here, a smuggler's confession there—enough to know it ran beneath the spine of the realm like a vein nobody wanted to acknowledge. The crown had let it go to ruin on purpose. Roads remember where they've been, and this one remembered too much. So we walked its broken spine for three days, through gorse and fog, while the hunters' fires flickered somewhere behind us like a fever that wouldn't break.

Theo walked like someone carrying water in both hands—careful, level, terrified of spilling. Pip rode my shoulders with her heels hooked under my arms and her chin on top of my head, and she pointed at things: a kestrel, a cairn, a stone marker so old the letters had worn into suggestion. She never pointed at the road itself. I think the road frightened her, though she'd never say so. She never said anything at all.

"She knows where we're going," Theo said, on the second evening. Not asking. Watching Pip's finger track a line along the horizon, dead straight, toward nothing anyone sensible would walk toward.

"Then one of us does," I said. "Because I'm navigating by rumor and spite."

"You have a map."

"I have a *memory* of a map, which is worse." I shifted Pip's weight. "There's a community out here. Off every ledger I ever touched at court—which is why they're still alive. Mages who gave it up. Renounced their gifts, swore themselves hollow, went dark. If anyone can tell us what's burning a hole in your chest, it's them."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Fog gathered in the hollows below the road like spilled milk.

"And if they can't fix it?"

"Then they can hide us. That's the other thing oath-breakers are good at."

I expected an argument. Theo had argued about everything since the ford—the route, the rations, whether it was acceptable to eat Wren Calloway's last strip of dried plum. But Theo just nodded and kept walking, and I realized, with a small unpleasant jolt, that they were past arguing. When you find out you've been a hiding place your whole life, you stop debating the terms of the journey.

* * *

We found them on the third morning because they wanted to be found.

One minute there was a sheep track; the next there were people standing along it—six, seven, emerging from the gorse with the unhurried certainty of weather. No weapons drawn. No weapons at all, I noticed. Their belts hung empty, and the emptiness had a polished look, like a wound that had healed into an ornament.

A woman stepped forward. Sixty-odd, silver-cropped hair, hands like a stonemason's. She looked at me, dismissed me, looked at Pip, softened—and then looked at Theo and stopped moving entirely.

"You're carrying," she said.

"I'm sorry?" Theo said.

"Don't be clever, child. It doesn't suit you and it won't help you here." Her voice wasn't unkind. It was the voice of someone who had buried the unkindness deliberately, like a blade, and knew exactly which field it lay in. "You're carrying something that should have died with the war. We felt you two miles out. Like hearing a bell under water."

Behind her, the others had gone very still. Not afraid, exactly. More like people hearing a door open in a house they'd boarded up.

"We're not looking for trouble," I said. "We're looking for a roof, a meal, and possibly a miracle, in ascending order of desperation."

"Cartographer," she said, without looking at me. "Royal, once. You smell of ink and disgrace."

"Calloway. Wren Calloway. And you have me at a disadvantage, madam, which I'm told is traditional."

"Maren." She finally took her eyes off Theo, and it cost her something. "You can shelter. One night. But whatever walks in with the child and the liar—" her mouth twitched, so I knew it was almost affection "—does not walk among us as a friend. Do you understand?"

"Theo," I said. "Their name is Theo Thornbury."

"No," Maren said softly. "That's what they were called in the valley that forgets. Out here, they're the thing the bells hear."

* * *

They'd built their village inside a river bend, half-sunk into the hillside, roofs turfed green so that from above it would read as meadow. There were maybe forty of them. Every age, every origin—I counted three accents I recognized from opposite ends of the realm. And not one weapon anywhere, which in my experience meant either profound peace or profound discipline, and the way they watched Theo suggested discipline.

They fed us, first. Barley stew, flatbread, honey that tasted of clover. Pip ate with the methodical intensity of a child who had learned never to trust the next meal, and one of the younger women kept sliding extra bread onto her plate without comment until Pip finally, cautiously, leaned against her arm.

"They renounced," Theo said quietly to me, watching the settlement work. Baskets, looms, someone mending a wall with the easy competence of long practice. "Their gifts?"

"Formally. With witnesses. There's a rite." I turned my cup. "I mapped a delegation once, years ago. Court sent me to chart their boundaries so the tax collectors knew where not to go. They stood in a circle and spoke their oaths backward, syllable by syllable, until the magic came out of them like a splinter. Took hours. Some of them screamed."

"Does it work?"

"It worked on them." I looked at Theo—at the careful stillness of them, the water-carried-in-both-hands of them—and didn't finish the thought.

Theo heard it anyway. "And if it doesn't work on me?"

"Then we'll know something new about you. That's how knowledge works. Painfully."

It got a breath of a laugh out of them, quickly smothered. Around us the settlement hummed with evening—no bells, no horns, nothing raised in voice or iron. It was, I realized, the quietest place I had ever been, quieter even than Hollowmere, because Hollowmere's silence was enforced from outside and this one was chosen from within, stitch by stitch, by people who had each decided, alone, that the gift wasn't worth the burning.

Theo looked around at all of it with an expression I couldn't name at the time. Later I would call it homesickness for a place they'd never been.

* * *

Maren came for them after dark.

She brought a lantern and two elders, and she asked Theo—not ordered, asked—to sit by the central fire. The whole settlement drifted near, drawn the way moths are drawn but slower, sadder, like they resented themselves for coming.

"There's a test," Maren said. "Old. From before the renunciations. We use it to make sure the gift is truly gone." She held out her hand, palm up, over the flames. "Hold your hand beside mine. The fire will tell us what the fire hears."

Theo glanced at me. I shrugged, which was cowardly, but I've found honesty often arrives dressed as a shrug.

They sat. They put their hand out beside Maren's, close to the flame but not touching it.

For a moment, nothing. Firelight on two hands, one weathered and one not, and forty people holding their breath, and Pip pressed against my side with her eyes enormous.

Then the fire bent.

Not toward Theo's hand. *Away* from it. The whole flame leaned back like a man flinching from a struck match, and the heat came off it wrong—not warmer but *denser*, pressing outward in rings, and the lantern Maren had brought guttered and went out, and every ember in every firepit in that settlement flushed red-gold at once, in unison, like a heartbeat answering another heartbeat across a crowded room.

Maren snatched her hand back. She was on her feet before I'd finished blinking, and she had put three feet between herself and Theo, and her face—

Her face was the worst part. Not hatred. Recognition. The look of someone who has smelled smoke and knows, down in their bones, whose house is burning.

"What," Theo whispered, "what is it? What did it hear?"

Maren's mouth worked twice before sound came out. "That's not a gift," she said. "Gifts sit in a person. This doesn't sit. It *waits*. Child, what you're carrying—it isn't sleeping. It's listening. For the word. For the oath that wakes it." She wrapped her arms around herself, and I saw that her hands were shaking. "We renounced everything. We swore ourselves hollow. And you come walking out of a valley that forgets with the last ember of the war-magic tucked under your ribs like a coal in a lunch pail."

The settlement drew back, all of them, a slow tide of withdrawal that left Theo sitting alone by a fire that leaned away from them, and I have never in my life hated a group of peaceful people more, or understood them better.

"Theo," I started.

"Don't." They stood. Their face had gone very controlled, the way it did at the ford, the way it did whenever the world reminded them what they were. "It's fine. Wren. It's fine."

It was not fine. Nothing about a child of Hollowmere standing alone in a ring of empty space, in the one place they'd hoped might hold them, was fine.

* * *

Pip found them at the edge of the settlement, where the turf roofs gave way to dark hillside, and I followed her because following her had become, without my permission, a habit of the heart.

Theo sat on a boundary stone with their knees up. Pip climbed up beside them and did not lean against them, which she always did, which she had done since the bridge—she sat a deliberate hand-width away, facing forward, and I understood that even Pip had felt the fire flinch, and was choosing, consciously, at six or seven or however old she was, to sit next to the thing everyone feared anyway.

"Pip," Theo said hoarsely. "You should be careful around me. They're right. I don't know what's inside me. I don't know what it listens *for*."

Pip looked at them. Then she reached into the little sack she carried everywhere, and took out a stub of charcoal, and reached up, and instead of drawing on her slate or on the ground or on any surface at all, she drew on Theo's forearm—one line, then another, slow and certain. A hilt. A blade. A small sword, drawn on the skin above the veins, and then, at its base, a tiny figure standing guard. Holding it *down*, I realized. Holding the blade into the earth.

"That's not what I want," Theo said, barely audible.

Pip frowned at the drawing. Erased the figure with her thumb. Drew it again, bigger.

And then she opened her mouth, and out of a child who had not made a sound since the day her world burned came a word, cracked and rusted from disuse, quiet as a coal settling:

"*Theo.*"

Just the name. Just their name, spoken like it was the answer to a question someone had asked her long ago.

I watched Theo come apart and reassemble in the space of a single breath. Whatever the ember was, whatever it waited for, in that moment it lost. Pip had spoken her first word and given it to them—not to the fire, not to the war, not to the thing under their ribs. To *them*.

"Oh," Theo said. And then, wrecked and laughing and crying all at once: "Oh, *Pip*."

* * *

Maren found us there, an hour past midnight. She sat down on the cold ground across from us like a woman twice her age, which was saying something, and studied Theo for a long time.

"There's a story we tell," she said. "About the ember. That when the war ended, the last speakers broke it apart so no hand could wield it, and hid the pieces, and the greatest piece they hid where magic could not follow—because nothing hides like forgetting." She rubbed her face. "We thought it was a story. Then you crested the ridge sounding like a struck bell."

"Can you unmake it?" Theo asked. "Like your rite. Backward, syllable by syllable."

Maren was silent for a long time. Long enough that I started counting her breaths.

"No," she said finally. "What's in you isn't a gift, Thornbury. Gifts can be renounced. An ember can only be spent, woken, or carried." Her eyes were ancient and sorry. "But there are texts. From before. In our keeping, because we keep everything the crown wants burned. If you insist on understanding the thing that's riding you, we can teach you its name. Its grammar. What words it will answer."

"That's the opposite of what I need," Theo said.

"That," said Maren, "is what worries us."

She rose, brushed off her skirts, and paused.

"One more thing. Before dawn, one of ours goes down to the crossroads to trade at the market town—flour, salt, news we can afford to hear." She didn't look at me when she said the rest, which meant it was for me. "He came back an hour ago. Says there's a company camped at the ford town. Crown colors. And their captain's been asking after three travelers—a liar, a child, and a bell."

Behind us, in the settlement, every banked fire flushed gold at once—involuntary, instant, like a body flinching—and Theo closed their eyes, and I watched them understand that the ember already knew.

It wasn't waiting for Vess Arden.

It was listening for *them*.
