## 7. Vess Arden's Truth

The camp at Ashford Rise smelled of wet canvas and horse and the particular staleness of men who had marched too long in armor that no longer fit them. Vess Arden stood at the edge of it, watching the valley below through a spyglass she did not need. She could have drawn Hollowmere from memory. She had been drawing it for eleven years.

"Report," she said, without turning.

Lieutenant Sorrel came to attention behind her, boots crunching on frost-killed grass. "Scouts lost them at the Oathbreakers' Road, Captain. The old war-road. Tracks split three ways and none of them honest."

"They'll go north." She lowered the glass. "Toward the ruins. That's where the ember will pull them. It wants to go home."

"You sound certain, Captain."

"I am certain." She handed the glass to Sorrel and finally turned to face him. He was young—too young to remember the last flare of the war, young enough to still believe the crown's seals meant something. "Have the men make a cold camp tonight. No fires above knee-height. And Sorrel—if any man so much as sharpens a blade after dark without orders, put him on the picket line until his hands bleed."

He hesitated. "Captain, with respect, the men are saying—the stories about what we're hunting. A swordspeaker. They say a waking can turn a whole armory against its—"

"I know what they say." Her voice did not rise. It never rose anymore; there had been a time when it did, and then a night when it stopped forever. "I also know that fear travels faster than truth and cuts deeper. Go."

He went. Vess remained alone at the camp's edge, and after a while she took out the letter she had read perhaps two hundred times, unfolded it despite knowing every crease, and read it again anyway.

*Vess. The mill's fine, Da fixed the west wheel, Marisol says the apples came in sweet this year. Come home before first frost or she'll give your share to the pig.*

Her brother Tam's handwriting. The loop of the *V* leaning right, always right, like he was hurrying toward something. First frost had come four days after that letter left Redbridge village. She knew because she had been riding toward home when the sky over the eastern hills turned the color of a forge, and the ground beneath her horse's hooves hummed like a struck bell, and every blade within thirty miles woke at once.

* * *

People who had not felt a waking always asked her what it was like, and she had learned not to answer, because the truth sounded like madness. The truth was that she had felt it from twenty miles away—a great voice made of steel speaking through every sword and knife and scythe in the county at once, a chorus of edges, and underneath it, thin as a wire pulled to breaking: *someone's oath.* Someone had spoken the old words and meant them, and the war-magic that had slept since her grandmother's grandmother's time had opened one eye.

She had spurred toward home anyway. Of course she had. Everyone rode toward home.

What she found, she would not describe even now, even to herself, in full sentences. Fragments only. The smithy door standing open. The smith himself sitting in the road with his hands pressed flat to the dirt as if holding the world down, weeping without sound. Her father's sickle embedded six feet up an oak tree, and the tree itself bleeding sap from a cut no branch could explain. Marisol—she thought of Marisol in fragments too, deliberately, because whole was impossible—Marisol's kitchen knife on the floor of a house whose walls had been peeled open like fruit, the blade clean, which was somehow worse than blood. The coroner's report said the family of Tam Arden had died when their own tools turned in their hands during what the scholars called a resonance event. Resonance. As if her family had been bells and someone had merely rung them.

The swordspeaker responsible had been found three days later, dead of his own gift, half a county away, killed by a rival in the last spasm of a war that had supposedly ended forty years before. Too dead to question. Too dead to hate properly, though Vess had tried. She had stood over the corpse and discovered something that frightened her more than any waking ever had: nothing. No satisfaction. Grief does not settle debts; it only learns their interest rate.

So she had gone to the crown instead, because grief needs somewhere to go, and the crown is very good at giving it direction.

* * *

The King's Minister of Inquiry had received her in a room with no windows, which she later understood was a courtesy. What they discussed in windowless rooms was not meant for daylight.

"The last swordspeaker," the Minister had said, steepling his soft white hands. "Or nearly the last. There are records, Captain Arden. Incomplete records, but records. A lineage unbroken since the war. And a rumor older than the lineage—that the final working of the war-magic, the seed of it, the *ember*, was hidden inside a child. Placed there, sworn silent, carried into hiding." He had watched her the way a surgeon watches. "You want the person responsible for Redbridge."

"I want the gift dead," she had said. "Every word of it. Every waking, every oath, every child born able to speak steel awake. I want it burned out of the world like a disease."

"And if the crown decided instead that such a weapon might be useful? Held by loyal hands?"

"Then I would burn it out of the crown."

He had smiled at that, and she had understood she had said exactly what he hoped she'd say, and she had hated him for it, and accepted the commission anyway.

Eleven years. Eleven years of cold trails and false sigils and villages where old women looked at her boots and lied with the fluent courage of people protecting their own. Then, eight months ago, a peddler in a market town three valleys east of nowhere had sold her a knife for two coppers more than it was worth, and she had turned it over in her hand and seen the hilt-work—and her heart, which she had believed was finished making sounds, had lurched like a girl's.

A swordspeaker's sigil. Faint. Deliberately defaced. But alive.

And now here she stood, above the valley that forgot, hunting a person who had, by every account her scouts gave her, done nothing. Hidden nothing less than themselves. The Thornbury child—no, not a child anymore, but that was how the villagers spoke of them, *the Aldous boy's ward, quiet, decent, good with the little ones*—had lived twenty-some years without waking a single blade, as far as anyone could tell. Had held the ember inside them the way you hold a coal in cupped hands and let it go dark rather than let it light anything.

It should have made the hunt easier. Instead it made everything harder, because Vess had built her whole life on a simple architecture: the gift was the murderer, the gift must die, whoever carried it was kindling. And now she kept catching herself watching the fugitives' trail the way you watch a wound for signs of healing, hoping and dreading in the same breath.

* * *

Sorrel returned at dusk with the day's tally and a folded paper bearing the crown's seal. He offered it with the careful neutrality of a man delivering something that smelled wrong.

She broke the seal and read it twice.

*Captain Arden. His Majesty's position regarding the asset has evolved. Should recovery prove feasible, the Ministry directs preservation over destruction. The ember, once woken under controlled conditions—*

*Controlled conditions.* She almost laughed. There were no controlled conditions. That was the entire point, the whole of her eleven years distilled: there was no controlling a waking, no aiming it, no leash for it. The scholars could write *asset* and *preservation* in their beautiful clerical hands, but she had seen the asset peel a farmhouse open like fruit with everyone inside it.

"What is it, Captain?" Sorrel asked, because her face must have done something.

"A change of instructions." She fed the letter to the brazier beside the command tent and watched the seal curl and blacken. The fire took the word *asset* last, holding it alight like a small vindictive star.

"Shall I prepare new orders, Captain?"

"Yes." She looked north, toward the ruins of the first battlefield, where the ember would be pulling its carrier the way a river pulls everything loose toward the falls. "We move at dawn. Full pace. And Sorrel—" She paused, hearing her own voice and not recognizing the thing it was about to say. "When we take them. The carriers. They're to be treated gently. No restraints that bruise. No roughness."

He blinked. "Captain?"

"They've never used it." She heard how it sounded and said it anyway. "Twenty-odd years carrying that fire and never once letting it catch. Do you understand what discipline that takes? What it costs?" She thought of Marisol's kitchen knife, clean on the floor, and made her voice flat again. "I want them alive and unharmed. That is an order, and it supersedes anything in any letter with that seal. Anything the Ministry says from this day forward comes through me first."

Something flickered across the lieutenant's young face—wonder, maybe, or the beginning of doubt about who precisely was commanding whom. He saluted and withdrew.

* * *

Vess did not sleep. She sat outside her tent with her father's old sickle across her knees—he had no use for it now, and she had taken it from the ruin of Redbridge herself, had carried it through eleven years of campaigns, unwoken, unsharpened, its handle worn smooth by her grip and his. It had been in the house when the waking came. It had not turned in her father's hands fast enough to matter, or slow enough to spare him. It was just a tool again now, bent and rust-flecked, and some nights she talked to it, and she was not ashamed, because grief that cannot find its dead will make do.

Here was the thing she admitted to no one, not even the sickle: she no longer trusted the crown. She had watched the Ministry's language shift over the years, *destruction* becoming *recovery* becoming *asset*, and she understood what she was being turned into. Not an avenger. A retriever. A dog sent to fetch a live coal for hands that wanted to hold it.

But her grief—her grief she trusted completely. It had never lied to her. It told her the same true thing every morning: someone spoke words, and steel answered, and her family paid the toll. Whatever else happened on the old battlefield ahead, whatever the Ministry wanted, whatever the Thornbury person had or hadn't chosen—Vess Arden would stand where her family's killers' magic had been born, and she would end it. One way or another. Even if ending it meant ending herself alongside it.

Far to the north, past the picket fires burning low and lawful, thunder rolled over hills with no clouds—a long grinding note, metallic, like a thousand blades drawn slowly from a thousand scabbards.

The ember was waking.

She stood, slung the sickle across her back, and called for horses.
