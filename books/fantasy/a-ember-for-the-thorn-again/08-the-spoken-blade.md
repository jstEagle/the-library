## 8. The Spoken Blade

The ruins of Harrowfield did not look like the birthplace of a war. They looked like grief that had been left out in the rain for three hundred years—broken walls softened into hills, a shattered archway wearing ivy like a bandage, and everywhere the stones themselves faintly wrong, as if they remembered being burned and had never quite forgiven the fire.

Theo felt it the moment their boots crossed the old boundary stone.

The ember turned over in their chest.

They had lived with it for twenty years without knowing its name—a warmth behind the breastbone they'd mistaken for their own heartbeat's echo, a restlessness that flared whenever steel was near. Aldous had taught them to call it nothing. To breathe past it. To never, ever speak with intent while holding a blade.

Now Theo knew what it was: an ember of the war-magic, buried alive inside a child to keep it from the world. And here at Harrowfield, where swordspeakers had first spoken steel awake and the world had learned what waking steel could do, it strained against its cage like a dog scenting home.

"Stop looking at it like that," Wren said. He was limping badly—the cartography road had not been kind to his knees—and he said everything now as if someone might be listening, which, given the smoke rising over the eastern ridge, someone probably was. "It's rocks, Theo. Old ones. It can't hurt you."

"It's not the rocks I'm worried about."

Pip tugged Theo's sleeve and pointed.

She didn't speak—hadn't yet, not once in all the miles since Hollowmere—but she'd developed a whole language of hands, and her hands were saying *down*. She flattened herself against a fallen lintel and pulled them both after her, and a breath later two hunters rode through the gap where the western gate had stood, close enough that Theo could hear the saddle leather creak.

Vess Arden's riders. Not all of them. A patrol, sweeping ahead of the main column like fingers feeling for a pulse.

"They're not searching," Wren breathed. "They're herding."

He was right. The patrol rode straight across the ruin field without so much as glancing at the collapsed well or the half-buried shrine, straight to the one structure still standing—a broken watchtower on the field's far side—and took position around it. Within minutes, Theo spotted the second pair of riders coming through the southern breach. Then a third group, north.

Every route out of Harrowfield, closing like a hand.

"She read the drawings," Theo said. Pip's sketches had been stolen once, outside Thornwick; Vess had returned them, deliberately, along with a note offering clemency for the child. *The first battlefield,* the note had ended. *You already know where this ends.* "She's been steering us here since Thornwick."

"Then we should have been harder to steer." Wren's jaw worked. "There's a cellar under the shrine. Aldous told me about it—if we can reach it before they finish the circle, we might—"

Pip grabbed Wren's wrist hard enough to leave marks.

She was staring south, toward the breach, and her whole small body had gone rigid. Theo followed her gaze and saw the rider coming through it alone, unhurried, gray cloak immaculate despite nine days of pursuit, and felt every hair on their arms rise.

Vess Arden rode into the ruins of the first battlefield like a woman returning to a grave she'd dug herself.

* * *

"I know you're tired," Vess called, dismounting, her voice carrying across the broken field with terrible gentleness. "I know you're hungry. I've marched men to exhaustion before, Master Thornbury. It makes them honest. So let us be honest with each other."

Theo stood up out of cover. There was no point anymore—the circle of riders was complete, and staying low would only make Pip feel more hunted than she already did.

"You want the ember," Theo said. "You don't want me dead. You've had ten chances to put an arrow in my back."

"And taken none." Vess walked toward them across the rubble, sword sheathed, hands loose at her sides. Up close her face had the terrible calm of deep water. "Because I watched a swordspeaker wake steel when I was eleven years old. I watched it unmake my village in the time it takes to recite a poem. Four hundred people, and afterward there wasn't enough of some of them to bury." She stopped ten paces away. "I don't want you dead, Thornbury. Dead is easy. I want it *out of you*, and then I want it broken on the anvil where it was forged, and then I want to sleep for the first time in twenty-five years."

"The crown wants it awake," Theo said quietly. "Wren found the orders. You're carrying a weapon, Captain, not a funeral."

Something flickered behind her eyes—there and gone, like a fish under ice. "The crown's wants are not mine."

"That's what everyone says right before they do the crown's wanting anyway."

Pip made a sound. Theo had never heard her make it before—a thin, cracked syllable of warning—and they turned in time to see the crossbowman on the watchtower lose whatever patience Vess had been imposing on him. The bolt leapt off the string toward Pip, because Vess's men had decided days ago that the child who *remembered too much* was the true prize, and the captain's mercy had always been a leash with slack in it.

There was no time to think. That was the mercy of it, and also the horror.

Theo drew their knife—Aldous's knife, plain iron, worn smooth by forty years of a dead man's grip—and spoke.

"*Steel, remember.*"

Three words. A fragment of an oath, a splinter of the full rite, and it should have done nothing—it should have been a whisper into a well. But the ember surged up through their chest like water finding a crack in a dam, and the knife woke.

Theo had woken blades before, in secret, in the dark, careful as breathing. It had always felt like striking a match: brief, bright, contained.

This was the sun deciding to notice them.

Light poured up the blade—not firelight, not any light Theo had words for, the light of the old engravings come alive—and the air itself rang like a struck bell, and every rusted shard of iron in three hundred feet of ruin *answered*. Theo felt them all. Every broken spearhead in the soil. Every sword dropped by dying hands three centuries ago, sleeping under the grass. They stirred, straining toward the surface like drowning things, and the ember howled in Theo's ribs with something that was either joy or agony, and a single thought cut through the roar:

*I could say the rest. I could wake all of it.*

The crossbow bolt stopped.

Not deflected—stopped, hanging in the air a foot from Pip's chest, trembling, as if the world itself had been asked to hold still and hadn't dared refuse. Then it fell, spent, ordinary, and the knife's light guttered down to a thread, and Theo was on their knees in the grass gasping, and somewhere very far away Vess Arden was shouting orders in a voice that had lost all its calm.

The ember settled slowly, grudgingly, like a banked fire resenting its ashes. Theo's hands wouldn't stop shaking.

That was when they heard Wren cry out.

* * *

He had thrown himself between Theo and the tower.

Theo never learned exactly why—one of the hunters had loosed a second quarrel, wild, panicked by the waking-light, aimed anywhere at the source of the impossible thing they'd just seen. It didn't matter. What mattered was Wren Calloway, who had spent nine days insisting he was only paying a debt, lying in the grass with a quarrel buried under his collarbone, pressing both hands around it as though he could hold himself together through stubbornness alone.

"Don't—" His voice came out wet and thin. "Don't use it again. Theo. Look at me. Don't you dare heal it with that thing, I've seen what it costs—"

"I'm not going to heal it." Theo was already beside him, tearing their shirt into bands, hands moving with a steadiness they didn't feel. "I'm going to keep you alive, and you're going to complain about my technique, because that's what you do."

"Is it bad?" Wren asked Pip, who had taken his other hand in both of hers.

Pip looked at the wound for a long moment. Then, deliberately, she shook her head no—and lied, Theo was almost certain, with her whole honest face, because she had seen enough wounds in whatever memory she carried to know exactly how bad it was.

"There," Theo said, and their voice cracked in the middle of the word. They tied off the bandage. Blood was already seeping through. "There. See? Cartographers are impossible to kill. Too much of you is already lost in other places."

Wren laughed, which turned into coughing, which frightened Theo more than the blood. "Listen to me. Listen. If I slow them down tomorrow—when we get to the camp—you don't stop. Whatever the ember offers you, whatever it says in your blood, you don't take the deal. You scatter it. Promise me."

"Wren—"

"Promise me, swordspeaker."

Across the field, Vess Arden was walking toward them alone. Her men held their positions, but her sword was drawn now, dragging lightly in the grass, and her face had gone from deep-water calm to something rawer. She had seen the light. She had seen the bolt stop in the air. Twenty-five years of certainty, and it had just met its proof.

"Theo," Wren whispered. His eyes were losing focus. "Whatever you decide—decide fast."

Pip climbed into Theo's lap, small and warm and shaking, and pressed her mouth close to their ear.

"*Théo*," she said.

Her first word. Given now, in the blood and the grass, like a coin placed in the palm of someone about to spend everything.

Theo closed their eyes. Felt the ember turn toward the sound of her voice like a flower toward sun.

Then they opened them, gathered Pip close, and rose to meet the captain coming across the ruins of the first war—while behind them, over the eastern ridge, the horns of the crown's siege camp began to sound, and the last light drained out of the sky like a blade being sheathed.
