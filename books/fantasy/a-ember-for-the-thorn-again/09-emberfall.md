## 9. Emberfall

The siege camp smelled of wet canvas, horse dung, and fear dressed up as discipline. Theo came through the picket line with their hands open and empty, the way you'd approach a spooked animal—except here the animal was four hundred soldiers, and it had pikes.

They'd taken the sword off Theo at the treeline, which was fine. It had never really been theirs to take.

Wren walked at their left shoulder, arm still bound from the ruin of the old battlefield, his mapmaker's stride doing its best to look casual and failing in a way that would have been funny under any other sky. Pip walked at their right, small hand fisted in the hem of Theo's coat, her eyes moving over the camp like she was reading it—the tents, the banners, the faces—and remembering all of it for reasons none of them fully understood yet.

"Three hundred pikes," Wren murmured. "Give or take. In case you were wondering."

"I wasn't."

"Liar."

"Also true."

Ahead, on a low rise above the valley's mouth, the command tent stood with its flap pinned back, and Captain Vess Arden stood beneath the banner of the crown with her helmet off and her hair braided tight, looking like a woman who had already decided how this ended and was merely waiting for everyone else to catch up.

She watched them come the whole way. She didn't call for the pikes to lower. She didn't need to.

When they reached the foot of the rise, Vess descended alone. Up close there were lines around her eyes that hadn't been there at the ford, and Theo understood—somehow, without any magic at all—that she had not slept. That grief keeps its own hours and doesn't care whose it borrows.

"Theo Thornbury," she said. "Of Hollowmere. Last swordspeaker." Her voice carried; she let it. "You've cost me two hunters, a bridge, and six weeks of my life. I hope whatever you're carrying in that chest of yours was worth it."

"It isn't in my chest," Theo said.

"No," Vess agreed softly. "I know where it is. I've always known where it is." Her gaze dropped, briefly, to Theo's breastbone, as if she could see straight through cloth and rib to the coal Aldous had buried there before Theo could walk. "That's what the crown pays me for. Knowing where things are."

"And what do they want done with it?"

Vess's jaw tightened. For a heartbeat she looked past Theo, toward the assembled ranks, toward the valley behind them—the valley that had forgotten how to hold magic, and was about to remember.

"That depends," she said, "on the day you ask them. Destruction or delivery. The ember of the old war-magic, pulled whole out of a swordspeaker's body and set into a blade the realm can point wherever it likes." A muscle moved in her cheek. "They haven't decided which story serves them better this season."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"You've had weeks to take it. You could have cut it out of me at the ford, or at the ruins. You didn't. Why?"

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes—not softness, exactly. Recognition. One mourner seeing another.

"Because I know what waking feels like," she said quietly. "I stood in a house that was burning from the inside of a man's voice. My mother's blade sang when he spoke, and then everything I loved was smoke." Her hand flexed at her side. "The crown thinks I want revenge. Let them think it. What I want is for no one else to stand in a doorway listening to steel sing." She looked up. "So. Here we are. Before all of them. Say your piece, swordspeaker. Convince me."

Theo turned. Not just to Vess—to the ranks. Three hundred faces under helmets, three hundred blades sheathed but present, every one of them a door waiting for a voice to open it.

"My mentor put something inside me when I was too young to say no," Theo said, and the camp went quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet—leaning. "An ember of the war-magic. The last one. Everything the old oaths burned with, packed into a coal and hidden in a child, in a valley where magic couldn't follow, because the people who hid it were afraid of what they'd made."

Someone in the front rank shifted. A sergeant hissed at him to hold.

"The crown wants it destroyed," Theo went on. "Or worn. Either way, it stays *one thing*. One ember. One blade. One hand. And one hand is all the old war ever needed."

"You're going to talk," Vess said flatly. "You're going to stand there and make an oath, right now, in front of my army."

"No," Theo said. "I'm going to unmake one."

They drew a breath, and let go.

Not of the sword—they'd lost that hours ago. Of the holding. All those years of clenching down on the heat behind their sternum, of swallowing words half-spoken, of living so carefully quiet that even Hollowmere had learned to look through them. Theo opened their hands at their sides and stopped gripping.

The ember rose like a swallow.

Heat bloomed under their ribs, up through the chest, into the throat—and this time Theo didn't swallow it. They opened their mouth and gave it somewhere to go.

"*Steel of my hand*—" The voice came out doubled, theirs and something older underneath, a sound like a forge exhaling. "*No more yours than mine.*"

Every blade in the camp answered.

It started as a hum—a thin silver thread of sound from the front ranks, then the second rank, then back through the tents where cooks' knives sat in satchels and farriers' tools hung on racks. Steel singing. Not the waking-song of the old oaths, which had been a shout; this was softer, stranger, a chord spreading outward instead of gathering inward. Soldiers stumbled back from their own belts. A pikeman dropped his weapon as if it had bitten him, and the pike head glowed faintly against the trampled grass, warm as a held hand, singing its one small note.

"*What was buried in one,*" Theo said, and their voice cracked along the seam of it, human again, only human, "*let it fall as rain.*"

The ember broke.

There was no explosion. There was an exhalation—the longest breath Theo had ever taken leaving their body all at once—and light came up out of them in a slow gold flood, not striking anything, just *going*, pouring out along the singing lines of steel the way water finds channels. It passed through three hundred pikes and a thousand knives and the farrier's hammer and the buckle of Wren's map-case and did not stop, did not gather, did not ask anyone's permission. It scattered itself thin. Thinner. Thinner than thin, into every edge in the camp, and then—Theo felt it, a tug at the base of their spine—outward, beyond the camp, following steel across the whole sleeping valley and down the roads of the realm, a little warmth falling into ten thousand blades like embers from a bonfire caught by wind.

And then it was gone. All of it. Everywhere at once and nowhere in particular.

Theo's knees hit the mud.

The silence afterward was enormous. No blade in the camp was singing anymore—but none of them were silent either. Every soldier who touched a hilt found it warm. Faintly, evenly, permanently warm, like a stone kept near a hearth. Nothing any oath could wake. Nothing any oath could *own*.

"Captain." The sergeant's voice, hoarse. "My sword—it's not dead, it's—"

"I know," Vess said.

Theo pushed themselves upright, mud on their knees, and found their chest hollow in a way that wasn't pain. Just space. Room where the coal had been. They pressed a palm below their sternum and felt—heartbeat. Breath. The ordinary machinery of an ordinary body.

Grief hit first, absurdly: they mourned it. Even after everything—even after the loneliness and the hiding and the hunters—an ember of grief for the thing that had made them dangerous enough to matter.

Then Pip's arms came around their waist, and grief had to wait its turn.

* * *

Vess Arden stood very still among her unsettled soldiers, one hand resting on the hilt of her own sword, and felt nothing happen.

That was the miracle of it. Nothing happened. The blade at her hip—the blade she'd worn through the last flare of the war, the blade that had been in her family for three generations—sang no songs and woke no fires. It was only warm. Warm as a living thing, warm as a hand, and utterly, permanently free.

Her knees wanted to bend. She kept them locked, because captains don't kneel, but gods, she wanted to.

She thought of her mother's face. She thought of the doorway, and the smoke, and the singing. She thought: *it can't happen now. Not like that. Not ever like that again.* Somewhere in the realm other mothers' children were standing in other doorways, and the steel on their walls would never answer a voice again.

The crown would call this treason. She could already write the report—she'd have to write something, some account of why the ember was unrecoverable, dispersed beyond gathering, its power divided past any threshold of use. Some clerk would read it and understand that the weapon was gone and the hunt was over, and some minister would be furious, and eventually someone would decide it had been unavoidable.

Let them. She had spent years being the crown's instrument of finishing a war, and she had finally understood, watching a hollow-eyed child from Hollowmere scatter fire like seed, that she had never once been asked what *she* wanted finished.

She walked down the slope to where Theo knelt in the mud between the cartographer and the girl.

"Thornbury."

Theo looked up. Their face was gray and shining with sweat, and their eyes were clear in a way she hadn't seen at the ford—clear of something. Unburdened, or emptied; from the outside it was hard to tell the difference.

"You should know," Vess said, "the crown will want a story. Someone to blame, or someone to thank, whichever polls better in the capital."

"Take both," Wren muttered. "We're flexible."

"I'll tell them the truth," Vess said. "That the ember fell and couldn't be gathered. That no blade in this army can be woken, and neither can any blade anywhere, and that hunting the last swordspeaker is now hunting a person with no more gift than a farmer." She paused. "Which is what they always were. That part, I think, I knew from the start."

"Then it's over," Theo said. Not a question. Needing it to be one anyway.

Vess considered the question honestly, because she had spent her whole life considering things honestly and it had made her terrible at court and good at everything else.

"There will be clerks," she said. "There are always clerks. But the war—" She looked out over her army, over the warm steel and the frightened faces slowly settling into something else, wonder maybe, or relief. "The war has been over for a century. Today it finally noticed."

She unbuckled her sword belt, wound the strap once around the scabbard, and held the whole thing out to Theo, hilt first.

"I won't need it where I'm going," she said. "And it seems wrong to carry a blade that warm into what comes next."

Theo took it. Their hands shook. Vess noted, with something adjacent to respect, that they took it anyway.

"The girl draws," Vess said, gruff suddenly, turning to go. "The way the old scouts drew. If the crown is smart, they'll leave her alone. If the crown is smart." She climbed the slope toward her tent, then stopped, halfway, not looking back.

"For what it's worth, Thornbury," she said. "I'm glad it was you."

Then she was gone into the canvas dark, and Theo Thornbury sat in the mud of a disbanding war with a captain's sword in their lap, a cartographer bleeding gently at their side, and a child's chin hooked over their shoulder, watching the army's cookfires come alight one by one down the valley—ordinary fires, needing ordinary fuel, asking nothing of anyone's voice.

Pip tugged their sleeve. Pointed at the fires. Then at Wren. Then at herself. Then, with great deliberation, at Theo.

"Home," she said.

Wren laughed—one startled, hurting bark of a laugh, hand pressed to his bandaged side. "The kid's got opinions about real estate now."

Theo looked east, toward the pass, toward the valley that had spent a lifetime forgetting and was about to learn what it remembered. The road home was two days long and they had no horse and almost no coin, and Theo's chest held nothing but the future.

"Home," they agreed, and began, slowly, to stand.
