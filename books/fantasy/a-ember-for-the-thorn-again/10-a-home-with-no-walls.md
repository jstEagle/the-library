## 10. A Home With No Walls

The road into Hollowmere used to end at a ford where the river forgot your name. Now it ends at nothing at all.

I felt the change before I saw it—felt it as an absence, the way you feel a tooth that's been pulled. For twenty-six years the valley had pressed against me like a hand on my chest, that old ward humming in the stones, in the water, in the marrow of everyone born here. Magic could not follow. Neither, it turned out, could I carry it across the line. Aldous had built his hiding place well: he'd hidden me inside it.

Now I walked through where the ward had been and felt only air. Ordinary air. The kind everyone else had been breathing their whole lives.

"You're doing the face," Wren said beside me.

"What face?"

"The one where you're cataloguing everything wrong with the world and deciding not to say any of it."

"I don't have a catalogue."

"Chapter four," Pip said.

Wren barked a laugh that pulled at the bandages under his coat—he still moved like a man carrying a full cup of water he refused to spill—and Pip grinned up at him with the shameless delight of a child who has recently discovered her voice and intends to use it on all of us without mercy.

Three weeks since Emberfall. Three weeks since I stood before Vess Arden's army and spoke a new oath into the oldest steel in the realm, and felt the ember come apart in my chest like a knot finally untied—not destroyed, never destroyed, but *given away*. Scattered into every blade at the siege camp, every blade within reach of my voice, which by then was every blade in the valley and most of the ones beyond it. No single hand would ever hold the old war again. That had been the oath's whole architecture. It cost me mine.

I didn't miss it. That surprised people when I told them, and I told them often, because honesty was the other thing I'd sworn to practice. What I missed was stranger: the certainty. For one year I had known exactly what I was—the last swordspeaker, the vessel, the target. Grief is simple. Rebuilding is not.

The village came into view around the bend of the river, and I stopped walking, because it took a moment to understand what I was seeing.

There were carts on the road. Travelers' carts, painted wagons, a mule train strung out along the bank—and no one stopping them. The ford was busy. An old woman sat on the flat stone where I'd once sat alone for entire summers, selling apples to a tinker whose accent came from three valleys over. Children who weren't Hollowmere children were racing Hollowmere children down to the water, shrieking, and nobody's mother was calling them back behind the ward-line, because there was no line anymore.

"It's smaller than I imagined," Wren said. "The famous invisible valley."

"That's the point of invisible."

"Mm." He shaded his eyes. "Your elders are going to have opinions about this."

"They've been having opinions about me since before I could walk. At least now they'll be new opinions."

Pip tugged my sleeve and pointed—at the forge, where smoke rose in a thin gray thread, and at the training yard behind it, where a dozen children stood in ragged lines holding sticks, and where Marta Ellery, who had taught half the village its letters, stood looking lost among them.

"They tried," Pip said. "While you were gone. They tried to do what you did."

"And?"

She made a face of magnificent diplomacy. "They hit each other."

* * *

We stayed first at my own cottage, which the neighbors had kept swept and stocked against my return, which undid me more than the siege had. There was bread wrapped in cloth on the table and a jar of honey with a note in Tam Hollis's blunt hand: *For the hero. Don't let it go to your head.* Underneath, someone else had added in a child's scrawl: *and come teach us the sword thing.*

Wren took the room that had been Aldous's study. He said it was for the light. I think it was because the desk there faced the door, and a man who'd spent years mapping escape routes liked knowing where the exits were. His wound was healing slow—a hunter's blade had found him below the ribs at the ruins, and the healers said the scar would ache in cold weather forever, and Wren had thanked them with such sincerity they'd checked him twice for fever.

Pip slept in the loft, in the bed I'd made for a child I hadn't yet met, back when Aldous was alive and I'd still believed the future was a place I'd simply arrive at rather than something I'd have to build with my hands.

On the third morning, Marta Ellery knocked on my door with the stick-children assembled behind her like a small mutinous army.

"We can't do it," she announced. "The swordwork. Bram nearly took Fennel's eye out. Twice."

"Twice," Bram confirmed proudly.

"So you'll do it. You owe the village a debt, Thornbury, and we've decided this is how you'll pay it."

"I saved the realm."

"The realm isn't here. These children are." She thrust a bundle into my arms—practice blades, wooden, sanded smooth, made by the cooper while I was away. "Mornings. Before chores. And Theo—" her voice dropped, and for a moment she wasn't the woman who'd lied badly to witch-hunters for me, just tired and fond, "—it's good you came home."

* * *

So I taught them.

Ordinary swordwork, first: stance, grip, the geometry of distance. How to fall without breaking. How to stand up slower than you want to and faster than your opponent expects. The children learned quickly, the way children do, which is to say messily and then suddenly all at once.

But it was the second lesson that mattered, and I made them take it as seriously as footwork.

"Say what you mean," I told them, walking the lines while they held their stances. "In a fight and everywhere else. If you're afraid, say so. If you're sorry, say so. A lie is a blade you'll cut yourself on eventually—I promise you I know."

"Why?" asked a girl named Sorrel, who questioned everything, whom I already loved.

"Because words have weight. Even ordinary ones. Especially ordinary ones." I crouched to her height. "There was magic once that lived in spoken oaths. Steel woke when the right person spoke to it. And the people who had that gift learned to be very careful, because a promise made carelessly could burn a country down." I looked down the line of upturned faces. "You don't have that magic. But your promises are still real. Your words still land somewhere. So throw them like you know where they're going."

Bram raised his hand. "Did you really scatter the war-magic into every sword in the world?"

"I did."

"Does that mean every sword is magic now?"

I considered lying, out of habit, out of twenty years of habit. Then I remembered my own lesson.

"No. It means every sword is *ordinary* now. That was the point. Nobody gets to be the special one ever again. Not even me."

Sorrel frowned. "Then why do they call you the last swordspeaker if there's no more speaking?"

"Because endings need names too," Pip said from the fence rail, where she'd taken up permanent residence as self-appointed witness to my lessons. "Aldous's letters say so."

"Aldous's letters," I said, "say a great many things."

That was true. I'd spent the quiet evenings reading through what remained of his papers—the ones I hadn't burned, the ones that turned out to be less betrayal than terror, a man writing to a dead friend about the child he'd hidden and the thing he'd buried in her and how much he hated himself for both. *I chose the valley because it could keep her safe from herself,* he'd written, in the last letter, the one dated two days before his death. *If she ever reads these, tell her I was wrong about almost everything except loving her.*

I was getting there. Forgiveness is swordwork too: slow, repetitive, mostly learning how to fall without breaking.

* * *

Vess Arden came in autumn.

I saw her at the ford before I recognized her—the crown had stripped her of rank after Emberfall, or she'd stripped herself; the stories disagreed and she declined to correct them. She stood on the flat stone in traveling clothes with her sword slung plain across her back, unornamented, and watched the travelers cross, and when she saw me she didn't reach for the blade. Just lifted one hand, an acknowledgment between soldiers.

"You're not hunting me," I said, when I reached her.

"No."

"The crown might object."

"The crown," she said, "has discovered it no longer knows what to hunt. Every blade in the armory woke for a heartbeat the night you spoke, and then went quiet, and the king's scholars can't find the fire anywhere. They've decided it was never real. It's easier that way." She looked out at the water. "I came to see the valley. Everyone always said nothing got in or out. It seemed important to check whether that was another story we told ourselves."

"And?"

"And it's full of people." Something moved through her face—grief finding a door it hadn't known was open. "My family died in a market town like this one. I spent eleven years thinking safety was a wall." She turned to me. "You tore yours down and put a school in it. I wanted to see if that was madness or wisdom."

"Which is it?"

"Both. Everything worth doing is both." She shouldered her pack. "I'm not staying, Thornbury. But I'll be north, in the hill country, if the world breaks again. Someone should be watching the roads who remembers what the last war cost."

"Vess." She paused. "Thank you. For choosing, at the end."

Her jaw tightened. "Don't thank me. I didn't choose mercy. I chose to stop making my family's deaths into orders." She walked to the ford, and stopped, and said without turning: "The child. The one who remembers. Tell her the war is finished. She'll know what I mean."

Then she crossed, and was gone, and that evening I sat with Pip on the doorstep and passed the message along, word for word.

Pip was quiet a long time. Then she nodded, once, the way old soldiers nod, and leaned her head against my shoulder, and said, "It's lighter. In here. Since you spoke."

* * *

Winter came early that year, soft and white, and the valley wore it like a garment it had never been allowed to try on. Strangers wintered in the empty barns. Tam Hollis complained about it constantly and fed all of them anyway. Wren began drawing maps again—not royal maps, not smuggler's routes, but a great careful chart of Hollowmere itself, streets and springs and the names of everyone who lived there, hung in the tavern where anyone could add to it. "First honest map I ever made," he said. "No secrets. Terrible for escaping. Wonderful for staying."

And on a bright cold morning in the deep of the season, with the training yard packed snow-slick and the children stomping and breath steaming, Sorrel raised her hand during the honesty lesson and asked the question I'd been waiting a year for someone brave enough to ask.

"If the swordspeakers are all gone," she said, "what happens to their oaths? Do the old promises just... sit there? Waiting?"

I opened my mouth to give the answer I'd prepared—that the old oaths were scattered, spent, sleeping in ordinary steel forever.

And then I heard it.

Faint, far off, past the ford, past the hills—one blade, somewhere out in the wide world, ringing softly against another. Not waking. Not burning. Just singing, the way a struck bell sings, the way a promise sings when someone, somewhere, keeps it.

The children stared at me. Pip smiled.

"Well," I said, picking up my practice sword, feeling the weight of it, plain wood, no magic at all, and finding it enough. "Let's find out."
