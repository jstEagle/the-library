## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The mountain spoke first, before the mules did, before the wind, before the men who thought themselves in charge of this caravan had finished their argument about the best line through the scree. Felix Galloway heard it the way she heard everything the Thornfell chose to say — a low, grinding syllable far below the range of ordinary ears, a word made of stone and patience.

*Loosening,* the mountain said. *Loosening.*

She held up one hand and the column stopped, twelve mules and nine men going silent behind her with the obedience of people who had learned that the guide's instincts were worth more than their opinions. Dawn was still an hour off. The pass ahead of them was a black seam between shoulders of rock, the kind of place where a smuggler's cargo and a smuggler's bones went to the same unmarked grave.

"Rest the mules," she said. "We wait for light."

"We paid for speed," said Corvin Hale, the caravan master, a soft-bellied man with a merchant's smile and a smuggler's ledgers. "The toll road closes at first bell. If we're not through the Narrows by—"

"The mountain is moving," Felix said. "You can argue with it if you like. It's had longer to practice."

Hale's mouth opened, closed, opened again. He was not a stupid man, which was the tragedy of him; stupid men could be told anything and believe it, but Hale only half-believed, and the half that didn't believe made him cruel. "You've a strange ear for stone, Galloway. My grandmother said the same of her midwife, and they burned the woman for it two winters back."

"Your grandmother," Felix said, tightening the girth on her lead mule, "should have been more careful about her comparisons."

She walked the line of the caravan while they waited, checking loads with hands that knew the work. Salt pork and iron nails, dyed wool, a crate of unlicensed lantern-oil, and — she had made herself not ask, and so did not know — whatever lay in the three long boxes Hale had paid extra to have handled gently. The Thornfell had a hundred trails and the crown knew perhaps thirty of them, and the difference was the whole of Felix's living. She had walked these paths for eleven years, since the last of the old roads closed and the last of the people who remembered why they'd been closed were scattered or dead or quiet like her.

Quiet. That was the word she'd chosen for herself. Not *silent* — silence was a thing done to you, a lid pressed down. Quiet was a choice, a discipline, a room you kept swept. She had been quiet for eleven years, and in eleven years she had spoken the old speech exactly four times, and each time she had hated herself a little more, and each time she had been right to do it.

The sky went from black to iron to the color of a bruise healing. Felix stood at the mouth of the Narrows and listened with everything she had.

*Loosening,* the mountain said again, and beneath it, fainter, a second voice — the scree slope above the pass, two hundred feet of shattered slate balanced on nothing, muttering to itself like an old man in his sleep. *Heavy. Heavy. The rain was heavy and the frost got into my joints and I am tired of holding.*

"I know," Felix murmured, so low it was barely breath. "I know. Hold a while longer."

It would not. She could hear that too. The slope was going to come down within the hour, and it was going to come down on the Narrows, and the Narrows was the only way through this shoulder of the range, and behind her were nine men and twelve mules and a cargo that someone, somewhere, wanted badly enough to kill for.

She could turn the caravan back. A day lost, maybe two; Hale would dock her pay and mutter about strange ears and burned midwives, and the story would travel down to the low towns, and stories were how the witch-hunters found their work. Or she could take them through fast, before the slope finished deciding, and pray the mountain's arithmetic was slower than her feet.

Or.

Felix closed her eyes. The wind came down the pass and it smelled of snow and old iron, and somewhere far below, in the valley she'd left at dusk, the village of Hollowmere was waking — the bakery smoke, the bell on the shrine, the children who called her *Galloway* and nothing more because she'd asked them to, kindly, years ago, and they'd obeyed because children are kind in the way adults forget to be. Hollowmere had sheltered her without asking what she was. She owed the village nothing she could name, and everything she couldn't.

And eleven years of quiet was worth exactly nothing if it bought her a mass grave in the Narrows.

"Corvin," she said. "When I give the word, you drive them hard. No stopping, no matter what you hear. You understand me?"

"Depends what I'm going to hear."

"Nothing," Felix said. "You're going to hear nothing at all."

* * *

She went up alone.

The trail to the scree slope was a goat-path at best, switchbacking through juniper and frost-cracked stone, and she climbed it in the grey half-light with her breath smoking and her hands bare because gloves muffled the feel of the rock. At the top the slope spread before her like a held breath — slate and shale, grey on grey, all of it poised. Below, small as toys, the caravan waited in the mouth of the Narrows.

Felix knelt. She put her palm flat against the stone, and she let herself listen the way she had not let herself listen in years — fully, without the lid, without the sweeping of the room.

The slope's voice was a chorus of small voices, ten thousand shards of stone each muttering its own grievance, and beneath them the deep voice of the mountain itself, slow as centuries. She had learned the speech as a child, the way other children learned lullabies, from her mother's mouth against her ear: *the blade and the stone and the door are one tongue, little ember, and the tongue is a country, and the country is gone, but the tongue remains.* Swordspeech, the lowlanders called it, because the sword-masters of old Thornfell had spoken to their blades and the blades had answered, and the crown had spent a hundred years making sure no one spoke it again.

But it was never only swords. That had always been the crown's mistake, or perhaps its fear. The tongue was older than blades. It was the tongue of things that endured — stone and steel and the bones of the earth — and everything that endured could be *asked*.

"Listen to me," Felix whispered, and the words came out in the old shapes, the vowels like struck iron, the consonants like water finding its way down through rock. Eleven years since she had spoken them aloud and her mouth remembered everything. That was the cruelest part. Nothing was forgotten. Nothing faded. The gift sat in her like a banked coal, patient, waiting, and every year of silence had only made it hotter. "You are tired. I know you are tired. But there are lives beneath you, foolish lives, small lives, and I am asking you to hold."

The slope groaned. A shard the size of a shield skittered loose and rang off the stones below.

"Hold," Felix said, and poured the word full of everything she had — the weight of Hollowmere's bakery smoke, the children's voices, the memory of her mother's mouth against her ear, the whole unspent treasury of eleven silent years. "Hold. Hold. *Hold.*"

And the mountain — the old, indifferent, patient mountain — held.

She felt it settle, felt ten thousand small voices subside from grievance into something like rest, felt the deep voice below them shift its slow attention toward her with something that was not gratitude, because stone does not feel gratitude, but was perhaps recognition. *You are one of hers,* the mountain said. *You speak like the ones who built the doors.*

Felix snatched her hand back as if the rock had burned her.

She was down the goat-path and past the caravan before her heartbeat slowed, waving them through, walking the Narrows at the head of the column with her face set in the flat mask she'd worn for eleven years. Behind and above them the scree slope sat still as a held breath, and Corvin Hale kept glancing up at it and then at her, and saying nothing, which from Hale was the loudest thing she'd ever heard.

They were through the Narrows by full light. By mid-morning they had descended into the pine-shadowed country beyond, where the trail widened and the air lost its edge, and the men began to talk and joke and pass a skin of wine, because men were like that — the mountain had nearly killed them and already it was a story they'd tell in the low towns, *the pass that almost took us, and old Galloway with her strange ears.*

Old Galloway. She was thirty-one.

* * *

It was at the noon halt, in a hollow where the trail dipped beneath a ridge, that she saw it.

She was checking the mules' feet, kneeling in the pine duff, when the passenger she'd been paid not to look at shifted his weight on the far side of the fire, and the morning sun caught something at his collar.

A badge. Silver, small, worn on a chain inside his shirt — she saw it for perhaps two seconds before his hand covered it, smooth and unhurried, the motion of a man who had made that catch a thousand times. But two seconds was enough. She knew the shape. Everyone in the borderlands knew the shape now, the way they'd once known the shape of Thornfell's crown: the crossed blade and flame of the Office of the Hunt, the witch-hunters' mark, new-minted this year by a crown that had decided the old gifts were a disease and the gifted were an outbreak.

The man was maybe forty, lean, with a traveler's weathered face and a soldier's stillness. He'd boarded at the last waystation with Hale's papers in order and coin enough, and Hale had taken him without question, and Felix had looked at him once and looked away, because that was the arrangement — she was paid to guide, not to ask. She had told herself he was a fugitive, maybe, a man fleeing the hunt rather than serving it. She had told herself a great many convenient things in eleven years.

He was looking at her now. Not staring — he was too good for staring — but aware of her, the way a man is aware of a door he means to go through later. His eyes went, briefly, to her hands. To the stone she'd knelt on. Back to her face.

Felix rose, dusted off her knees, and made herself walk past him to the lead mule, unhurried, bored, a tired guide with tired feet. Her heart was a drum.

*He saw nothing,* she told herself. *There was nothing to see. You spoke to a mountain in the dark, alone, and the mountain kept your secret better than you keep your own.*

But the mountain had said *you speak like the ones who built the doors,* and the man by the fire wore the crossed blade and flame, and the crown's new hunt had begun this spring with lists — she'd heard the whispers in Hollowmere, names read out in the shrine, neighbors' neighbors gone quiet in the night. *Swordspeakers first,* the whispers said. *The crown wants the swordspeakers first.*

She looked at the fire, at Hale laughing at his own joke, at the lean passenger warming his hands with a soldier's economy of motion, and she did the arithmetic she'd been doing all her life: how many days to Hollowmere, how many ways out of the mountains if the arithmetic went wrong, how long it would take a man on a chain to send a signal — a mirror-flash, a drake-whistle, a fire on a peak.

Three days to Hollowmere. She'd be home in three days, behind the village's quiet loyalty, among people who called her Galloway and asked for nothing.

The passenger caught her looking and smiled — a mild, pleasant, nothing smile — and touched two fingers to his collar, straightening it, the silver vanishing again beneath the wool.

Felix turned back to her mules and began, very calmly, to plan how a woman travels three days with a hunter and arrives with no trail behind her at all.
