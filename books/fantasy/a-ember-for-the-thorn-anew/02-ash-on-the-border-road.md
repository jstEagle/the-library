## 2. Ash on the Border Road

Felix smelled the smoke before she saw the village.

It drifted down the pass on a north wind, thin and wrong—not woodsmoke from a hearth, not the sweet resin of the pine kilns Hollowmere ran in autumn. This was char and tar and something beneath it, something that made her back teeth ache: the reek of burned warding-herbs, the kind hunters smeared on their torches to strip a gifted soul's protections before the fire ever touched them.

She crested the ridge at dusk and stopped walking.

Below her, where Hollowmere had been, there was a black field. Forty rooftops, gone. The mill with its turning wheel, gone. The chapel where old Mother Cass had rung the bell for every birth and burial since before Felix was born—only its stone arch remained, standing over ash like a rib picked clean. Embers still moved in the ruins, small red eyes opening and closing in the dark, and the snow at the village's edge was trampled into gray slush by the passage of many horses.

The caravan behind her halted. Tam, the lead drover, came up beside her and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. He took off his cap and held it against his chest like a man at a graveside.

Felix counted. She had learned to count instead of weeping, years ago, in another burned place. Forty rooftops. Three days since the caravan had passed through, since Marta had pressed a wheel of cheese into her hands and told her the winter turnips were good this year, since the miller's boy had chased her mule down the lane laughing. Three days. Long enough for the hunt to come and go. Long enough for everyone she—

She stopped the thought the way she'd stopped the rockslide in the pass: with pressure, with will, with the old discipline of silence.

"I have to go down," she said.

Tam looked at her. "There could still be hunters."

"Then I'll go quietly."

"Felix." He used her name the way he'd use a hand on a shoulder. "Whatever you're thinking of doing down there—don't. Whatever it costs."

She was already moving.

* * *

She went down through the orchard, where the apple trees stood bare and blackened on one side, scorched by heat that had rolled uphill. The village well was the center of everything now; that was where they'd built the pyres. She could see the stakes. She could see the chains, still hanging from iron rings driven into the wellhead, and she made herself look, because looking was a debt she owed.

Five stakes. Five.

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs and made herself breathe, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the way she breathed before speaking to a blade—though she hadn't spoken to one in eleven years, not aloud, not where anything living could hear. The swordspeech rose in her throat anyway, unbidden, a word of mourning in a language made for war, and she strangled it in her chest. Grief was no excuse. Grief had never been an excuse. That was the first law of her order, the one that had died with everyone else in it: *the gift is a blade, and a blade drawn in sorrow cuts the wrong throat.*

She was still standing there, chained to her own silence, when she heard the singing.

It came from beneath the chapel arch—a sound too small to be a voice and too deliberate to be wind. A high, wavering tone, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. Felix had heard that sound once before, in another life, on the day her order put a shard of a singing blade into a child's hands to see if the gift would wake. It had woken in hers. She had been six years old.

She crossed the ash without deciding to. Her body knew the way before her mind agreed.

Under the arch, in the shadow of the fallen bell, a girl sat with her knees drawn up. Eight, maybe nine years old, in a singed wool dress, her dark hair matted with ash and something darker. Clutched to her chest with both arms was a length of metal the size of a forearm—a broken blade, its edges worn smooth as sea glass, and it was the shard that sang, a thread of sound Felix felt in her molars and behind her eyes.

The girl looked up. Her eyes were the gray of old iron, and they were dry, and that frightened Felix more than tears would have. A child who had cried herself empty.

"Hollowmere's gone," Felix said softly. "I know. I came too late."

The girl said nothing. Her mouth opened, shaped a sound that never came, and closed. She pressed the shard tighter and the singing climbed, thin and desperate, and Felix understood: the girl couldn't speak. Had never been able to, or couldn't now. But the blade could. The blade was speaking *for* her.

Felix knelt in the ash, heedless of her cloak, and held out her hand.

"My name's Felix," she said. "I'm going to get you out of here. Will you come with me?"

The girl stared. Then, slowly, she lifted the shard and held it out—and the singing changed, dropped, resolved into something almost like a word. Felix's skin prickled. Eleven years of silence, and the metal was speaking to her as if no time had passed at all, as if it had been waiting.

*Found you,* the shard sang. *Found you, found you, found.*

"Later," Felix whispered, and was horrified to hear her voice crack. "We'll talk later. Right now we run."

The girl took her hand.

* * *

They were two streets from the orchard path when the voice came out of the smoke.

"Stop. Don't run. I heard the shard—I heard it, and if I heard it, the others will too."

A man stepped from behind a collapsed byre. Big, gray at the temples, moving with the deliberate care of someone favoring cracked ribs. He wore a hunter's coat—black oilcloth, silver-buckled—but the coat was torn open at the chest and the badge was gone, ripped away, leaving a pale rectangle of undamaged cloth like a scar. In his hand was not a sword but a shovel, its edge stained with ash.

Felix put herself between him and the girl and drew her knife.

"Easy." He raised the shovel, then let it drop, point down in the ash—a surrender, or as close as a man like him could make. "I'm not going to touch her. I'm not going to touch either of you."

"Hunters don't surrender," Felix said. "Hunters don't dig. Hunters burn and ride."

"This one dug." His voice was rough as a rasp. "All day. I got four out of the cellars before the roof fell in on the fifth. Four." He said it like a man reciting a debt he'd never repay. "The girl was under the chapel. I couldn't reach her. I was about to try when you came."

Felix didn't lower the knife. "Your badge."

"Ripped it off myself. Before the torches were lit." He touched the pale rectangle on his chest. "Aldric Voss. Eleven years a hunter. I helped write the lists, Galloway. I helped draft the orders for this province. Your name was on one of them."

The world went very still and very cold. The knife in Felix's hand did not waver, because her hands never wavered, but something behind her ribs did.

"You know my name."

"I've known it since the spring. I sat in a room in Caer Dain with a list of forty-one names and I read yours and I said nothing, and then I came to Hollowmere with the men who burned it, and I stood there, and I did nothing." His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "So kill me if you want. I'd have earned it three days ago. But not while she's watching." He nodded past Felix's shoulder, at the girl. "She's seen enough men do what's easy."

The shard was singing again, low and urgent. *Danger. Danger.* But underneath it, threaded through, something else—something Felix had never heard a blade say before, because no blade had ever had cause to. *Sad. He is sad.*

Felix looked at the girl. The girl looked at Felix, then at Aldric, then—deliberately, the way a child makes a decision too large for her—she stepped out from behind Felix's legs and stood in the ash between them, the shard held against her chest, and shook her head. *No.* Not to the knife. To the killing.

"She can't speak," Aldric said quietly. "But she's the clearest speaker I've met all year."

Felix lowered the knife. Not because she trusted him—she did not, might never—but because the girl had ruled, and some part of her, the part that had been six years old in a circle of swordsingers, recognized a voice of judgment when she heard one, even a voice that never made a sound.

"The cellars," she said. "You said you got four out. Where are they?"

"Old Brannoc's root cellar, past the millrace. Two of them are children." He hesitated. "One of them won't stop talking about a door. Says a door in the mountain is the only way out of the province now. Says the maps are wrong and always have been."

Felix felt the cold go through her all over again, different this time. Sharper.

"Who told them that?"

"A woman. Passed through two days before the hunt. Sold the miller a chart, he was so proud of it. Then she went up into the high country." Aldric's mouth twisted. "She'll be caught. The drakes are flying the passes."

Felix looked north, where the peaks stood black against the last gray light, and thought of a door she had sworn never to speak of, never to seek, never to open—and of a name she had buried so deep that even she sometimes couldn't find it.

"Get the survivors," she said. "All of them. We move tonight, before the drakes come back on the morning wind."

"And then where?" Aldric asked. "There's no way out of Thornfell province that the hunt doesn't watch."

The girl looked up at Felix. The shard in her arms sang one long, clear note, and Felix, who had spent eleven years teaching herself that silence was safety, heard the truth in it and could not unhear it.

"There's one way," she said. "And it's been waiting for me a long time."
