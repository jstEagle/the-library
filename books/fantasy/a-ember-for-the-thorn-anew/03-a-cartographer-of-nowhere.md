## 3. A Cartographer of Nowhere

Isolde Vane had spent eleven years drawing the world for people who intended to own it, and she knew the look of a hunted woman the way she knew the curve of a coastline: instantly, and with a professional's cold appreciation.

Felix Galloway wore that look like a second cloak.

They met in the wreck of the Wayfarer's Rest, three miles below Hollowmere, where Isolde had gone to buy a hot meal and silence with equal coin. The inn's common room stood half-empty, its windows shuttered against rumor. Rumor traveled faster than drakes these days — *the crown burns villages now, they hush children in the mountain, the last swordspeaker runs loose in Thornfell* — and Isolde, who had once been paid to know where every road in the borderlands ended, kept her ears open and her ink dry.

Then Felix walked in out of the sleet with a girl on her hip and a man limping behind, and Isolde set down her cup very slowly.

She knew the face from the lists. She had drawn those lists herself, or rather she had drawn the maps that made them possible — the passes, the hollows, the smugglers' ways only someone who charted for the crown could have rendered so lovingly. Somewhere in Serah Dain's campaign tent there hung a map of the Thornfell highlands bearing the signature *I. Vane, Royal Cartographer* in the corner, and under that signature, faint as a watermark, were names. Villages. People.

Hollowmere had been on her map. Now Hollowmere was ash, and the woman responsible for putting it there was walking toward Isolde's table like an executioner who hadn't read her own warrant.

"You're Vane," Felix said. No greeting. Her eyes went to the travel-roll slung across Isolde's shoulder, to the brass calipers clipped at her belt, and narrowed. "The cartographer."

"I'm a mapmaker," Isolde said. "The crown revoked the rest of the title." She kept her voice level and her hands visible, an old habit from negotiating with men who measured worth in fingers. "Sit down before you draw the whole room's attention. You've brought a child into a border tavern during a hunt."

It was the wrong thing to say — she saw it land, saw Felix's shoulders square like a blade coming up on guard — but it was also the true thing, and the true thing bought her three more seconds than flattery would have. Long enough for the child to turn her head. Long enough for Isolde to see the sword-shard hanging at the girl's throat, a finger of dark metal that seemed, when the lamplight crossed it, to hum.

Long enough for Isolde to make the decision that would either save or damn whatever was left of her life.

"There's a back stair," she said quietly. "Behind the taproom curtain. It lets out into the woodshed, and the woodshed has a door that hasn't been taxed or catalogued by anyone, because I bribed the excise man myself two winters ago. Go. I'll follow. You want what I have, and you'll want it more in ten minutes, when the drakes come over."

"Why would drakes come over?" Aldric asked. The ex-hunter's voice was gravel; his hand hovered near a hip bare of any weapon he was permitted to carry.

"Because," Isolde said, rising and dropping coins on the table with the unhurried grace of a woman who had learned that hurry killed more fugitives than soldiers did, "I sent a message to the garrison at Cael Ford yesterday asking after road conditions north of the pass. Signed with my old seal, before I remembered I no longer have one. They'll send wings to fetch me for questioning." She smiled, and found to her surprise that it wasn't entirely a mask. "I've been expecting company all evening. I'm glad it's yours."

* * *

The woodshed smelled of pitch and mouse droppings, and through its gaps they watched the drakes come.

There were three of them, riding the storm-front the way fish ride a current, their saddle-lamps swinging green against the low clouds. The sound reached them first — a pressure, a wrongness in the ears, like a held breath the size of a cathedral. Wren pressed herself against Felix and put both hands over her ears, though Isolde doubted sound was the problem. Children who heard swords heard other things too. She'd read the confiscated reports. She'd filed the confiscated reports.

"They're turning," Aldric murmured. "They've marked the inn."

"They've marked the *message*," Isolde corrected softly. "They think I'm still inside. Which gives us—" she counted the lamp-swing, did the arithmetic of wind and descent that lived in her bones like scripture, "—perhaps four hundred heartbeats before riders confirm the shed is occupied. Less if the wind drops."

Felix looked at her then, really looked, and Isolde understood she was being weighed on scales far less forgiving than the crown's.

"You said you have something I want."

"I have a map." Isolde unslung the travel-roll with hands that wanted very much to tremble, and did not permit it. "Not the ones I sold. Those are burned, or should be — I burned my originals the night the edicts came down, and I've regretted nothing so slowly in my life. This one is different. This one I drew from memory, and memory, Mistress Galloway, is the one archive the crown cannot confiscate."

She spread the sheet across a split log. She had drawn it in charcoal and iron-gall ink over three sleepless nights: the high passes, the drowned valley, and at its center, rendered in lines fine as hair, a ring of standing stones around a darkness that her own pen had seemed reluctant to enter.

"The Door of Aember."

The word landed in the shed like a dropped lantern. Even Wren went still, and the shard at her throat gave off a thin, clear tone, one note hanging in the cold air like a struck glass.

"That's not possible," Felix said. But her voice cracked on *possible*, and Isolde, who had spent her career reading terrain in faces, read the truth beneath: not disbelief. Hunger. Grief with nowhere to go.

"Thornfell sank beneath the oath-memory nine generations ago," Isolde said. "Every record burned in the Consolidation, every witness 'hushed,' every road to it struck from the royal surveys — I know, because I helped strike some of them. My predecessors' sin and mine. But the Door isn't *in* Thornfell. It's the seam between here and there. And seams, Mistress Galloway, appear on no survey because no surveyor believed in them." She tapped the ring of stones. "Except one. A monk at Greywater Priory, a hundred and forty years dead, who drew it in the margin of a psalter with a note in Old Thorne: *the door remembers whoever remembers the country*. I found the psalter in the confiscation vaults two years ago. I was meant to catalogue it for destruction."

"And instead?"

"Instead I memorized it, and let the vault fire take the original, and told myself that knowledge in a living head isn't treason." Her laugh came out bent. "It is, of course. It's the worst kind. It just takes longer to hang you."

Above them the drake-lamps wheeled, and somewhere down the valley a horn sounded — short, twice, the signal for *found sign, not found quarry.* Four hundred heartbeats, she had said. Perhaps half that now.

Felix stared at the map for a long moment. Then at the child. Then, last and longest, at Aldric, whose face had gone the color of tallow, because he of all of them knew precisely what the confiscation vaults were and what came out of them.

"If this is a trap," Felix said, "if you've drawn us a road to a gallows—"

"Then I've drawn it for myself too." Isolde began rolling the map, quick and careful, her calipers clicking. "That's the bargain, if you'll hear it. Passage — my guidance, my routes, my knowledge of every blind fold in these mountains, which is better than anyone alive's, including yours, because you guide by gift and gifts leave traces, and I guide by mathematics, which leaves none. In exchange: protection. Not your swords. Your *presence*. Where you walk, the hunt looks hard, and where the hunt looks hard, no one troubles a tired middle-aged woman with a satchel." She met Felix's eyes and made herself hold them. "I am worth nothing to the crown anymore except as an example. I am worth everything to you if half of what that monk wrote is true. I'm offering you a bad bargain from a compromised position, and I'd think less of you if you took it without saying so yourself."

Silence, but for the wind worrying the shed boards and, faintly, that impossible hum from the shard at Wren's throat — rising now, almost eager, as if the metal could smell the name *Aember* on the air.

It was Wren who decided it, in the end. The girl slipped her hand free of Felix's coat, crossed the straw-strewn floor, and set her small palm flat on the rolled map, looking up at Isolde with eyes that had seen a village burn and had not yet learned to look away from adults afterward. She didn't speak. She never spoke. But her fingers curled once, deliberately, around the edge of the parchment, and the shard sang one soft ascending note.

Felix closed her eyes as if the sound had cut her.

"She trusts maps," Felix said quietly. "Swords and maps. Both things that tell you where you are." She opened her eyes, and something in them had shifted — not warmth, not yet, but the end of active wariness, which in a hunted woman was nearly the same thing. "Stay between me and Aldric on the trail. If we're followed, you run ahead and don't look back, and if I tell you to leave us, you leave us. Those are the terms."

"Accepted," Isolde said, and was astonished by how much the word cost her, and how much lighter she felt for having paid it.

* * *

They went out through the woodshed's back wall of brush into weather that had teeth. The storm had turned to sleet, then to sleet with ambition, and the high pass above them showed itself only in lightning-strokes: a white staircase to a black sky, the kind of route Isolde would once have marked on no chart at all, labeled simply *impassable winter.*

Now it was the only road, and she led them onto it by a shepherd's path that existed in exactly one head in the world, and that head was hers.

Guilt climbed with them, patient as the altitude. Every switchback had a memory attached — here a hamlet she'd surveyed for the tax rolls, there a defile she'd recommended sealing to choke the smuggler trade, which had choked the food trade instead, which had emptied three villages into the crown's work-houses. She had done it all with a steady hand and a clean nib, and told herself that maps were innocent, that ink was only ink, that the men who read her lines bore the weight of what was done along them.

Hollowmere had been along her lines. She had drawn the ford, the goat-track, the treeline where the hunters could come down unseen. Some clerk with her map open on his knee had circled it, and passed it upward, and the village had become a lesson.

And now the last swordspeaker walked behind her trusting her, and a mute child carried a singing shard, and somewhere south Serah Dain's drakes were combing the dark for all of them, using — Isolde would have staked her remaining reputation on it — her older maps. Her beautiful, accurate, murderous maps.

"Left," she called back over the wind. "The right-hand track avalanches above the second bend. Trust me; I've drawn this ground enough to hate it."

They trusted her. That was the unbearable part. The ex-hunter steadied her elbow at the ice-patch without being asked; the child kept pace at her heel like a small determined shadow; and Felix Galloway watched her the way one watches a bridge one must cross — with full attention and suspended judgment — and even that was more faith than Isolde had earned in a decade of royal service.

Near midnight they sheltered beneath an overhang, and Isolde dug the last of her honey-bread from her satchel and divided it five ways without letting herself count who got the largest piece. Wren ate hers leaning against Isolde's side, and the shard hummed contentment against the mapmaker's hip like a cat.

It was while the girl slept that Isolde noticed it. She had the map half-unrolled by firelight, checking tomorrow's route against her memory of the monk's marginalia, when Wren's sleeping hand drifted across the parchment — and the ink *moved*. Just slightly. Just the ring of stones seeming to lean toward the touch, the way flowers lean toward a window.

Felix saw it too. Isolde watched her go very still, watch her look from the child to the map to the dark notch in the northern peaks where, by everything a dead monk had written and everything Isolde had risked, a drowned kingdom waited behind a door that answered only to a spoken name.

"It knows her," Felix said. Not a question.

"It knows something," Isolde whispered. And beneath relief, beneath the first real hope she'd permitted herself since the edicts, guilt turned over like a body in shallow water — because she had studied that psalter for two years, and there had been a second line of the monk's note, the one she had not yet spoken aloud, the one written in a different, later hand:

*A door that opens to one name can be forced by another.*

Far to the south, over the black spine of the mountains, a fourth drake-light rose where no patrol had any business flying at night — climbing fast, and heading straight for the pass.
