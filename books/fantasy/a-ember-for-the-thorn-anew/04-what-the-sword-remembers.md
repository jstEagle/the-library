## 4. What the Sword Remembers

The watchtower had no name anymore. Whatever it had been called when Thornfell still stood — and Felix suspected the stones remembered, even if no one living did — that name had gone with the kingdom, worn away by four hundred winters of wind off the high crags.

It rose from a shoulder of granite like a broken tooth, half-swallowed by ivy gone brown with frost, its upper floors collapsed into a tangle of beam and slate. But the ground level held. The walls were thick as a man is tall, and there was a hearth in the east wall big enough to warm them all, and for tonight, that was enough of a miracle to ask for.

Felix got the fire going while Isolde spread her charts across the flagstones, weighting the corners with loose rock, muttering calculations under her breath like prayers to a god of margins. Aldric carried water up from the snowmelt stream without being asked, without speaking, moving through the ruin like a man trying to take up as little of the world as possible.

And Wren sat by the fire, knees drawn up, the sword shard cradled against her chest.

The girl hadn't let go of it since Hollowmere. Not while they ran the border road, not while they hid in the culvert beneath the old toll bridge while drake-shadow slid over the water, not while she slept, curled against Isolde's side on the cold ground. The shard was no bigger than a hand's length — a splinter broken from some greater blade, its edge still bright where centuries should have dulled it — and when Felix came near, it *sang*.

Not aloud. Not in any sound the ear could catch. It sang the way hot iron sings before it touches water, the way a struck bell sings after the sound has stopped. Felix had spent thirty years pretending she couldn't hear it. She was tired now, bone-tired, grief-tired, and she found she could not pretend anymore.

She crouched across the fire from the child and looked at her properly for the first time since the burning. Wren was maybe eight years old, small for it, with soot still worked into the creases of her knuckles that no washing would fully lift. Her eyes were dark and very steady. She had not made a sound since Felix pulled her from the smoke of the tannery row — not weeping, not screaming, nothing — and Felix did not yet know if that was shock or nature or something the fire had taken from her.

But the girl was looking at the shard the way other children looked at their mothers' faces.

"You hear it," Felix said quietly. Not a question.

Wren's gaze lifted. She nodded once.

Isolde's pen stopped scratching. Across the ruin, Aldric went very still over his bucket of water, the way prey goes still.

Felix turned her hands palm-up over the fire, feeling the heat press against her skin. Thirty years of silence. Thirty years of swallowing every word the blades offered her, of letting bridges fall and passes close and men die because speaking was death and silence was only loneliness, and loneliness you could survive. She had sworn it to herself over her mother's grave: *never again, never aloud, never where anyone can hear.*

Hollowmere had burned anyway. The silence had bought nothing but more ash.

"Come here," she said to Wren.

* * *

The girl crossed the space between them without hesitation, shard hugged tight, and sat down cross-legged in front of Felix with the grave patience of someone waiting to be taught something important. Felix set her own blade across her knees — the smuggler's blade, plain steel, unremarkable, the one lie she'd told with her body for three decades. Under her palms it trembled like a held breath.

"Swordspeech isn't words," she said. "Not the way you and I talk. It's older than words. The first smiths didn't forge steel — they *persuaded* it. They asked the metal to remember what it wanted to be, back when it was ore in the mountain, back when it was star-fall. Every blade remembers being something else. Swordspeech is just... reminding it."

Wren's brow furrowed. She pointed at the shard in her arms, then at Felix's blade, then tilted her head.

"Yes," Felix said. "It's already talking to you. I know. I've heard it since I was smaller than you." She hesitated, then did something she had never done in front of another living soul. She laid two fingers flat against the flat of her blade and spoke — not loudly, barely above the crackle of the fire, in a language that had no country left to be spoken in:

*"Vessah kel. Vessah kel, torren-mai."*

*Remember. Remember what you were.*

The blade woke.

There was no better word for it. The steel flushed faintly gold along its edge, like sunset caught under skin, and a hum filled the tower — low, sweet, mournful, a note that seemed to come from everywhere at once and settle behind the breastbone. Isolde dropped her pen. Aldric set down the bucket and did not pick it up again. Snow dislodged somewhere far above and sifted down through the broken floors, glittering in the firelight like falling sparks.

Wren's mouth opened. No sound came out, but her whole face changed — wonder breaking over it like dawn over water — because she could *hear* it, Felix realized. The child who could not speak could hear the speech of steel as clearly as if it were her own name being called.

Tears slid down the soot-streaked cheeks, and Wren laughed — silently, shoulders shaking, one hand pressed to her sternum as though holding the note inside her chest where it belonged.

Felix's own eyes burned. She looked at this small, smoke-marked girl hearing music no one else alive could hear, and felt something crack open in her that thirty years of silence had kept welded shut. *This is why they burn us,* she thought. *Because of this. Because of exactly this.*

She took the girl's hands and guided them to the blade's flat. "Now you," she whispered. "Don't try to say it. Just... ask. Ask it to remember being loved."

Wren closed her eyes. Her lips moved around shapes she couldn't voice — and the shard in her lap answered anyway, flaring soft gold, singing back a harmony to Felix's blade until the two notes braided together and filled the ruined tower with a sound like a choir heard through deep water.

Isolde was crying openly, pretending she wasn't, failing completely.

And Felix broke her vow a second time, and a third, teaching the child the asking-words, the listening-words, the word that meant *I am here, you are not alone*, until the fire burned low and Wren fell asleep mid-lesson with her cheek against the warm steel, both blades glowing faintly beside her like banked coals.

That was when Felix looked up and saw Aldric watching from the shadows by the stair, and remembered, all at once, that he was still among them.

* * *

He'd built the fire up and cooked the last of the smoked hare without being asked. He'd checked the perimeter twice. He'd done everything right, quietly, competently, the way he'd done everything since Hollowmere — and Felix had almost, *almost*, let herself forget what he was.

"What are the lists?" she asked him, while Isolde slept by the charts and Wren slept by the blades.

Aldric didn't pretend not to understand. He came and sat across the fire from her, elbows on his knees, and for a long moment he watched the flames instead of her face.

"The hunt keeps registers," he said. "Every gifted soul the crown suspects. Names, villages, family lines, the evidence against them. Someone has to compile them. Cross-reference the informants' reports. Decide which names rise to the top." His jaw worked. "I was good at it. That's the worst part. I could read a report and know — *know* — whether the person in it was real or imagined, guilty or just unlucky, and I flagged the real ones anyway, because the alternative was my own name on a different list."

The fire popped. Felix said nothing. She let the silence do what silence does.

"There's a page," Aldric said, "with your name on it. Felix Galloway, of Hollowmere. Last oath-bound swordspeaker. I wrote the entry myself, six years ago. I described your gift in detail I had no right to have. I recommended surveillance, then escalation." He finally raised his eyes to hers. "Hollowmere burned because of a chain of paper that started with my handwriting. So did the prison we're running from. So did—" he glanced at Wren's sleeping shape, and couldn't finish.

Felix stood up. The movement was slow, controlled, and more frightening for it. She crossed the space between them and stopped close enough that he could see the fire reflected twice in her eyes.

"I buried everyone I loved twice," she said, very quietly. "Once when I hid, and once today. And you're telling me you *wrote my name down.*"

"Yes."

"Say you're sorry. Say it, and mean it, and I'll tell you why it doesn't matter."

"I'm sorry." No hesitation. No flinch. "It doesn't cover it. I know it doesn't cover it."

"It doesn't," Felix agreed. Her hand drifted toward her knife and stopped. "Here's why it doesn't matter. Because Serah Dain commands that hunt, and Serah knew me before you ever read my name. She'd have found me with or without your ink. You didn't light the fire, Aldric. You just wrote down where it would land." She exhaled slowly. "What matters is what you write next."

He looked at her, and she watched him understand — really understand — that forgiveness wasn't being given. It was being *loaned*. At interest. Repayable in blood, or loyalty, or both.

"I'll carry the lists," he said hoarsely. "Every name I ever put down. If we reach the Door — if there's anything left of Thornfell to remember — I want their names remembered too. All of them. Mine at the bottom of the page, where it belongs."

From the hearth, half-asleep, Wren stirred. Both blades glowed a little brighter in the dark, as if they had been listening — as if they always listened — and far to the north, beyond the pass, a horn sounded once, long and low, and did not sound again.

Felix went to the broken window and looked out at the black teeth of the mountains.

Drakes, she thought. Or worse. They had until dawn, maybe less.

"Wake Isolde," she said. "We move tonight."
