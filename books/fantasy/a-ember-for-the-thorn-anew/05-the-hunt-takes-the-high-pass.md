## 5. The Hunt Takes the High Pass

Aldric Veyra had spent eleven years learning to read the signs of an ambush, and every one of them was screaming now.

It was the silence that did it. The high pass above the snowline should have been loud with wind through the scree, with the complaint of mules, with Wren's wordless humming — a sound she made constantly, like a kettle deciding whether to sing. Instead the wind had died, the mules had stopped flicking their ears, and Wren had gone still as a held breath, her hand pressed flat against the singing sword shard at her throat.

He knew what that meant. He had taught it to recruits himself, back when he'd worn the badge and believed the badge meant something. *When the mountain goes quiet, the mountain is listening for someone else.*

He let his hand drift to his belt, to the empty loop where his badge had hung for three days now. The skin beneath it felt bare and wrong, like a missing tooth. Then he let his hand keep drifting, past the badge loop, to the hunting knife at his hip — the only weapon they'd let him keep, because Felix Galloway did not trust him with a sword, and she was right not to.

"Felix," he said quietly. "Left-hand ridge. There's no birds."

She didn't look up from the mule's harness. "There's never birds up here."

"There's never *this few*."

That got her. She turned, and he watched her do what he'd watched her do a hundred times on the smuggling trails: go absolutely still, let the mountain speak to her. Her eyes moved along the ridge line, counting rocks the way a banker counts coins. Then her gaze snapped to the sky, to the far southern gap where the clouds had been sitting all morning like fat white sacks of wool.

The clouds were moving. Against the wind.

"Drakes," she said. "Isolde — how far to the cut?"

Isolde Vane stood at the edge of the trail with her map case strapped across her chest like a breastplate, the way she'd worn it since the prison break, since the rows of hushed children they'd left screaming behind them in the dark. Her knuckles were white on the strap. "The cut's a mile. Maybe more. The switchbacks slow us down."

"Then we don't take the switchbacks." Felix's voice had gone flat and quick, the smuggler's voice, all business. "We cut straight down the scree field and take the riverbed. It's ugly but it's fast."

"It's suicide," Aldric said. "Loose stone on a slope like that, with drakes overhead — they'll pick us off one at a time."

"Then you'd better pray they don't see us, hunter. It's the one prayer your order still answers."

The words were meant to cut, and they did. He let them. Three days ago he had told her the truth — that he'd sat at a table with ink-stained fingers and helped draft the lists, that *Felix Galloway, swordspeaker, Hollowmere* was in his own handwriting somewhere in the crown's archives, and that the hunt had burned her village with his arithmetic in its mouth. She had not forgiven him. She had simply stopped looking at him the way you look at a snake, and started looking at him the way you look at a snake that might still be useful.

He'd take it. He'd earned worse.

Wren tugged his sleeve. She pointed at the shard at her throat, then up at the southern sky, then made a sound — a thin, urgent note through her teeth, a note that was almost a word. The shard was singing. He could hear it now that he listened, a high silver thread of sound, and he remembered what Felix had told him in the watchtower, the night she'd broken her vow of silence to teach a mute child the first words of a dead language: *the blades remember what men forget. They sing when their kin come near.*

Their kin. There were blades on that ridge.

"Steel," he said. "Not just drakes. They've got swordspeakers' iron up there — warded, or bound, something. Wren's shard knows it."

Felix's face went very still. "How many?"

Wren held up four fingers. Then, after a moment, a fifth, grudgingly, like the fifth finger had been hiding.

"Five blades and two drakes." Felix looked at the scree field, at the riverbed below, at the mile of open trail between them and the cut. Aldric watched her weigh it all, and he saw the moment the arithmetic failed her — saw it in the way her jaw set, in the way her hand closed around the hilt of the plain smuggler's blade she carried and never, ever spoke to.

"There's another way," Isolde said.

They all turned. The cartographer had unrolled a scrap of vellum against a boulder, weighting the corners with stones, and her finger was on a line of ink that Aldric recognized with a sick lurch: it was his own survey work, ten years old, the crown's official map of the high pass. But Isolde had drawn over it. Beneath it. Through it.

"There's an old quarry gallery under the shoulder of the ridge. Pre-conquest. It's not on any map the hunt has because I never sold it to them." Her voice caught slightly on the word *sold*, and he understood — she was thinking of all the maps she *had* sold, all the doors she'd opened for the crown with ink and vellum. "It comes out below the switchbacks. But the entrance is a climb, and it's narrow, and if they seal it behind us—"

"They won't seal it," Aldric said. "They'll wait at the far end. It's what I'd do."

"Then it's fortunate," Felix said coldly, "that you're the only one of us qualified to be wrong about that."

It was almost a joke. It was the closest thing to a joke she'd made in his direction since the confession, and he felt it land somewhere under his ribs like a coal.

They moved. That was the thing he was learning about Felix Galloway — she did not deliberate the way officers deliberated, weighing and second-guessing. She chose, and then the choosing was done, and the world simply had to arrange itself around her decision. They moved along the trail at a smuggler's pace, fast and quiet, Wren riding the lead mule with the shard clutched in both fists, Isolde navigating by memory and scrap of vellum, and Aldric walking rearguard because it was the position of least trust and therefore the one they gave him.

The drakes came over the southern gap while they were still a half-mile from the quarry climb.

He heard them before he saw them — a sound like wet leather tearing, like the sky itself being unzipped — and then they were there, two shapes against the white, long-winged and wrong-jawed, the crown's hunting drakes with their riders flat along their necks. He knew the riders' silhouettes. He'd trained beside them. One of them was Serah Dain's outrider, a woman named Pell who used to cheat at cards and cry when her hawks died, and now she was leaning out of the saddle with a glass, sweeping the slope below, looking for five fugitives and a child.

"Down," Felix breathed. "Off the trail. Now."

They went down the scree. There was no choice anymore; the quarry climb was forty yards of exposed rock and the drakes were already circling. Felix went first with Wren, then Isolde, then the mules — poor terrified beasts, driven down a slope of loose stone by a woman making sounds Aldric had never heard a human make, low herding notes from deep in the chest. He came last, and the slope betrayed them exactly as he'd known it would.

A stone the size of a skull broke loose under Isolde's boot and went clattering down through the field, and the sound of it was a bell tolling.

The nearest drake folded its wings and dropped.

Aldric had seen drake-dives before. From the ground, from the target's side of it, it did not look like hunting. It looked like the sky deciding you were guilty. He was twenty feet below Isolde and he could not reach her and she knew it — he saw her know it, saw her do the cartographer's arithmetic, one life against four, and start to turn *away* from them, toward the open slope, to draw the dive.

She never got the turn finished. The drake's rider stood in the stirrups and loosed a weighted net, and it spread in the air like a black flower, and it took Isolde Vane off the slope and into the air with her map case screaming against her chest.

"NO—" Wren's voice. Wren's actual voice, cracked and rusted from disuse, one word torn out of her like a tooth.

And Felix Galloway stopped being a smuggler.

Aldric would spend the rest of his life trying to describe what happened in the next three seconds, and he would never manage it. She drew her plain blade — the unremarkable, well-worn smuggler's sword she'd carried out of the ashes of Hollowmere — and she spoke to it. Not a word. *Words.* The old language, the oath-language, the speech the crown had spent a generation burning out of the world, and the blade *answered*. Light ran up the steel like dawn coming down a valley. The sword woke. He could feel it wake, feel the air change the way it changes before a storm, and the shard at Wren's throat sang out loud and clear and joyful, and Felix threw the blade.

She threw it forty feet up a scree slope, spinning, blazing, and it took the net's trailing line and sheared it clean, and Isolde dropped — dropped and rolled and lived, tumbling down the scree in a clatter of stone and vellum while the drake banked, shrieking, its rider hauling the empty line in with the expression of a man who has just watched a legend become true.

The silence afterward was total. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

Five witnesses, Aldric thought. Five blades on the ridge, two riders in the sky, one disgraced hunter, one mute child, one cartographer sprawled in the scree. Every one of them had seen it. The last swordspeaker, spoken awake, in the open, in the light.

The rumor was dead. The rumor was now simply *fact*, and facts had armies.

Felix walked up the slope and took her sword out of the stone it had buried itself in, and when she turned around her face was ash-white and her eyes were wet and furious and she looked, for one unguarded moment, like a woman standing in the ruins of her own life's work. Eleven years of silence, spent. She looked at Aldric — at him, specifically, and he understood why. He was the only one on that slope who knew exactly what it cost, because he had spent eleven years on the other side of the cost.

"Run," she said, to all of them. "Now. Before they regroup."

They ran. Down the scree, into the riverbed, along the frozen water with the drakes circling above and screaming their position to the ridge, and Aldric ran at the rear with his knife out and his heart hammering and something in his chest that was not fear. He had worn a badge for eleven years and it had never once made him feel what he felt now, running away from his own former brothers-in-arms toward a quarry mouth in a dead country's border.

Isolde stumbled. He caught her arm without thinking, hauled her upright, kept her moving.

"You could have run," she gasped. "Back there. When the drake came for me. You could have used the chaos and been gone. No one would have—"

"Stop talking," he said. "Breathe. Run."

But she was right and they both knew it, and when they reached the quarry mouth — a black seam in the mountain's shoulder, breathing cold air that smelled of old stone and older iron — Felix stopped them all with a raised hand and turned to face him. Below them, faint but closing, came the sound of hooves. Riders coming up the riverbed. The five blades from the ridge, descending. Serah Dain's hunt, converging.

Felix looked at him, and for the first time since the confession, since the burned village, since the ink-stained lists, she looked at him the way you look at a person.

"They'll follow us in," she said. "The gallery is narrow. One fighter can hold it against ten, for a while." Her eyes went to the dark. "I'm taking Wren through. Isolde knows the way out. Someone has to stay and hold the door."

Aldric looked at the black seam in the mountain, and at the woman who had every right to leave him on this slope for the crows, and he unbuckled his hunting knife and held it out to her, hilt-first, the way a soldier surrenders and the way a man kneels.

"I'll hold the door," he said. "I've been on the wrong side of enough doors, Galloway. Let me stand at a right one."

She studied him for a long moment, this man who had written her name on a death list, and behind her Wren pressed close to Isolde's side, and the shard at the child's throat sang low and steady, like an ember refusing the wind.

Then, somewhere behind them in the riverbed, a voice he knew better than his own — Serah Dain's voice, carrying up the frozen water, clear and cold and terribly patient:

"Felix. I know you can hear me. We have your cartographer's maps and we have all morning. Come out, sister, and the child walks free."

* * *

Aldric turned to face down the slope, and drew his knife, and chose.
