## 6. Bargains Below the Snowline

The snowline came down on them like a held breath released.

Felix felt it first in her boots, then her lungs — the thin, mineral cold of the high country where even smugglers stopped pretending to be anything else. Behind her, Wren rode strapped to Aldric's back with a sword shard wrapped in wool against her chest, and the shard hummed. Not a sound anyone else could hear. Felix heard it anyway, a thread of song under the wind, and she hated how much comfort she took from it.

Three days since the pass. Three days since Isolde Vane had turned her horse around and ridden back into the drakes' shadow so that the rest of them could live, and every hour since, Felix had been doing arithmetic she couldn't finish: what a mapmaker was worth alive, what a swordspeaker was worth dead, and where those two numbers met.

Maren Ost would know.

* * *

The smuggler-queen kept court beneath the snow itself, in a hollowed-out glacier mouth the maps called nothing at all. Torches of rendered fat burned blue-green in the ice, and the light made everyone look drowned. Her people took their weapons at the door without asking, which was its own kind of message, and Felix let the sword go because refusing would have told Maren exactly how afraid she was.

Aldric's hand brushed her elbow as they passed under the ice arch. "You don't have to do this alone."

"Yes," Felix said, "I do." She didn't look at him. Looking at him was dangerous lately; he had this way of standing like a man who had already decided something, and she couldn't afford to be someone else's decision. "Keep Wren back. If I'm not out by moonrise—"

"You'll be out."

"Aldric."

"If you're not out by moonrise," he said, quiet as snowfall, "I'll come in after you. Write your arithmetic around that."

Wren tugged Felix's sleeve. The girl couldn't speak, but she didn't need to; she pressed Felix's palm flat against the wrapped shard, and it sang one bright note against her skin, a note like a name almost remembered. *Come back*, said the sword, in the only language swords had ever truly spoken. Felix closed the girl's fingers back over it.

"I always do," she lied gently, and walked into the ice.

* * *

Maren Ost sat on a throne of packing crates and anchor chain, drinking something dark from a tin cup, and she looked at Felix for a long time before she said anything at all.

"The last swordspeaker," Maren said at last, "comes into my house unarmed. Either you're very brave or you've finally decided to stop being the last of anything."

"I want information."

"Everyone wants something. That's what my house is *for*." Maren gestured with the cup. "Sit. Drink. You look like a woman who's been paying for things all week and hasn't noticed the bill yet."

Felix didn't sit. "Isolde Vane. Taken at the high pass by the hunt. I want to know where they're holding her."

"Serah Dain's holding her." Maren smiled without warmth. "You knew that. You want to know *where*, and you want me to tell you knowing I'll be selling the same answer to Dain's people by spring. Do you understand what you're asking? Information about the crown's prisons is the most expensive thing on this mountain. Blood is cheap next to it. Gold is dust."

"I'll pay."

"With what?" Maren leaned forward, and the fatlight caught the old knife-scars on her jaw like rivers on a map. "You have no gold worth the name. You have no army. You have a mute child, a hunter who lost his badge, and a reputation you've spent ten years burying. What do you imagine you have that I want?"

And there it was. The moment Felix had been walking toward since the pass, since Hollowmere burned, since the first night she'd whispered a rockslide still and felt the gift sing through her bones like a bell being rung inside a locked room. She had known, walking down into the ice, what Maren Ost would ask. She had hoped, the way people hope against avalanches.

"My secret," Felix said.

Maren went very still. In the trade, secrets were currency, but there were secrets and there were *secrets*, and both women knew the difference. A secret was something you'd done. The other kind was something you *were*.

"Speak carefully," Maren said softly.

"The Door of Aember," Felix said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else's, like a voice carried up from under water. "It opens to a true name. Freely given. Mine is the name that anchors it. My name is—"

"*Stop.*" Maren was on her feet. For the first time since Felix had known her, the smuggler-queen looked afraid, and it was somehow worse than any threat. "Not here. Not shouted in an ice hall full of my people, half of whom would sell their mothers for a pardon." She snapped her fingers, and the hall emptied of everyone but the two of them, torches guttering as bodies pulled the warm air away. Then, quieter: "Do you understand what you're offering? A name isn't coin, Galloway. Coin gets spent and gone. A name given away is a name *gone*. If I take it—"

"If you take it, you can open the Door yourself someday, sell passage into a kingdom that doesn't exist, and die rich." Felix's hands were steady. She'd been surprised by that. All week her hands had shaken over maps and meals and Wren's hair, and now, at the edge of the cliff, they were steady. "Or you can keep it and never use it, and hold the only thing in these mountains worth more than blood. Either way, you'll never sell it, because whoever holds my name holds the last door into Thornfell, and selling it means the crown comes down on this glacier before the thaw. You'd be guarding a secret worth killing for. Your whole life, you wanted to be indispensable. Here it is, gift-wrapped."

Maren laughed, low and unwilling. "They told me you were just a guide."

"They told me you were just a smuggler."

For a long moment the only sound was the glacier settling, deep groans of moving ice, like some enormous animal turning in its sleep. Maren set down her cup. When she spoke again, all the performance had gone out of her voice, and what remained was older and harder and strangely kind.

"Ask yourself one question before you pay. Not whether you can afford it. Whether the person you'll be afterward will thank you." She paused. "Because the woman who walks out of here won't be quite the one who walked in. Names are load-bearing, swordspeaker. Pull yours out and see what falls."

Felix thought of Wren's hand pressing the singing shard into her palm. Of Aldric saying *write your arithmetic around that.* Of Isolde turning her horse toward the drakes without a word, because that was what family did — it paid bills other people never saw.

She thought of the Door of Aember, waiting at the end of everything, demanding a true name freely given. And understood, with terrible clarity, that she was not spending it here. She was only telling Maren where the treasure lay, because a secret shared was still a secret kept, so long as the keeper had reason to keep it — and Maren Ost would keep it, because Felix was about to make the keeping worth more than the selling.

"The name stays mine until I give it," Felix said. "What I'm selling is the knowledge of what it's worth, and my oath that when the day comes, you'll have first claim on whatever the Door pays out. That's my price. Take it or leave it."

Maren studied her for a long, long time. Somewhere above them, the wind changed pitch, and the blue torches bent.

"You're not selling the name," Maren said slowly. "You're selling the *map* to it. Clever. Cruel, too — you know I'll spend the rest of my life wondering if you'll honor that oath." She sighed, and reached into her coat, and drew out a scrap of vellum covered in her own cramped cipher. "The Vane woman is at the Prison of Quiet Bells. Old hushing-house, three days east, under the Whitefriar crags. They don't execute the gifted there, Galloway. They *quiet* them. Ring bells until there's nothing left to ring against." Her eyes flicked up. "There are children there."

The floor tilted. Or perhaps it was only Felix.

"How many?"

"More each season. The hunt stopped burning them when someone figured out fear leaves scars and silence leaves *room*." Maren pressed the vellum into Felix's hand and closed her fingers over it, the way Wren had closed hers over the shard. "Whatever you're planning — and I can see you're planning something that will get us all killed — do it before the winter bells finish their work. Quiet doesn't kill. It just makes people into places where someone used to live."

* * *

Outside, the moon had risen white and hard over the ice fields. Aldric stood where she'd left him, Wren asleep against his back, and he read Felix's face the way Isolde read maps — quickly, completely, and with growing dread.

"What did it cost?"

"Not the name." She handed him the vellum. "But I showed him where it sleeps. Quiet Bells. Three days east."

He unrolled the scrap, and she watched his face change as he read — watched the hunter's knowledge surface in him like something drowning finally allowed to breathe. He knew the place. Of course he knew the place. He'd helped draft the lists, and lists became prisoners, and prisoners became rows of small silent beds.

"Felix." His voice cracked on it. "There are children there."

"I know."

"Wren will—" He stopped. Looked down at the sleeping girl, whose fingers, even in sleep, were curled around a sword that sang.

"I know," Felix said again, softer. Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and vast, and below them the glacier groaned like a thing dreaming of doors. She had gone in to buy a location and had instead sold a piece of the future, and the terror of it hadn't hit her yet — she could feel it coming, a slow avalanche of everything she'd promised. But underneath the terror, steady as a banked coal, was something simpler.

Isolde had bought their lives with her capture. Felix could spend a secret. It wasn't even a fair trade. It was *cheap*, and she'd have paid ten times it, and that was the part that frightened her most — not the losing of herself, but how little she now hesitated to lose.

"We ride at dawn," she said.

Aldric nodded. Then, quietly: "The shard's been humming since you went in. Same note, over and over." He looked at her. "Felix — it sounds like it's *waiting* for something."

Down in the ice behind them, faint through fifty feet of frozen stone, Felix could have sworn she heard the first far-off tolling of bells.

Three days east. And counting.
