## 7. The Prison of Quiet Bells

Isolde Vane had drawn maps for the crown for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once charted the place they called the Quiet Bell. It was not that she hadn't known. Every cartographer knew. It was simply that some places were left blank on purpose, and the blankness was a kind of mercy — or so she had told herself, ink-stained and well-paid, while the mountain swallowed people whole.

Now she stood at the treeline below it, breath fogging in the blue hour before dawn, and understood that the blank space on her maps had been the most honest thing she'd ever drawn.

The prison did not look like a prison. That was the first obscenity of it. It looked like a monastery: pale stone walls, slate roofs furred with frost, a bell tower rising against the grey sky. No battlements. No drake-perches. Only the bell itself, green with age, hanging silent in its arch. The hunters called the gift *the noise*, Isolde had learned. They called this place a place of quiet. The children inside were said to be at peace.

She knew better now. She had known since the pass, since the moment she'd turned her horse to buy the others time and woken in a cell where her own thoughts felt muffled, wrapped in wool, as though someone had put a hand gently over the part of her that screamed.

"They rotate the watch at dawn," Aldric murmured beside her. He crouched low, his former badge long gone but his hunter's bearing still carved into him, every line of him angled toward the walls like a compass finding north. "Two bells. The eastern gate opens for the supply sledge from Hollowford. Four minutes, no more."

"You're certain."

"I helped design the rotation." He said it flatly, without flinching, and Isolde gave him that much: he never flinched from what he'd done. "Third shift is Dain's own veterans on the wall, but the gate crew are locals. They'll look at papers, not faces."

Felix said nothing. She stood a little apart, hood back despite the cold, watching the bell tower with an expression Isolde couldn't read — not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like a woman looking at a grave she'd been told about all her life.

"Wren stays with me," Felix said finally.

"No." Wren's voice was small but immovable. She could speak — Isolde had learned that much in the weeks since the tower; the girl simply chose silence most hours, hoarding words like coins. Now she spent three of them and planted her feet in the snow. "I'm coming."

Felix knelt to her level. "It's not safe."

"Neither was Hollowmere." Wren's dark eyes didn't waver. At her belt, wrapped in cloth, the sword shard hummed — a sound only Felix and Wren seemed to hear, though sometimes, watching them, Isolde thought she felt it too, like a toothache of the soul. "The other children are in there. I heard them. In my sleep. They're singing without any sound at all, and nobody's listening."

The wind moved through the pines. Isolde saw Felix's jaw tighten, saw the war behind her eyes — the smuggler's arithmetic, one child against many, risk against risk — and then saw it lose. Saw love win, which was, Isolde was beginning to understand, the whole tragedy and glory of Felix Galloway.

"All right," Felix said. "You stay between us. You touch nothing that sings unless I tell you."

Wren nodded once, solemn as an oath.

Isolde pulled the forged supply manifests from her coat — her own handiwork, her own seal-work, skills the crown had once praised. "Then let's go spend four minutes well."

* * *

The sledge trick worked because Aldric knew exactly which lies the gate crew wanted to hear. Isolde played the quartermaster's clerk, all apologies and paperwork, while Felix and Wren slipped beneath the canvas among barrels of salt fish. The guards barely glanced at her. That was the second obscenity: how easy it was. Eleven years of her maps had made the crown's borders smooth as glass, and now the glass cut everyone but her.

Inside, the monastery-smell hit her first — tallow, lye, cold stone — and beneath it something else. A hush so complete it pressed on the ears. Not silence. Silence was empty. This was *suppressed*, the way a held breath is suppressed.

They moved through service corridors Aldric mapped from memory, past kitchens, past a scriptorium where clerks copied out lists. Isolde caught a glimpse of one page as they passed: names in neat columns, ages beside them. Seven. Nine. Eleven. Her stomach turned over.

The cells were in the east range, behind a door marked with the crown's sunburst and, beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic: *TRANQUILITY*.

Aldric picked the lock in nine seconds. Inside, the corridor stretched away under lamplight, lined with doors, each with a small barred window and each window showing the same thing: a child. Sitting. Still. Not sleeping — their eyes were open — but motionless as waxwork, hands in laps, faces smooth.

"Oh," Isolde said, and the word came out broken.

At the far end, iron-bound and unmistakable, sat Isolde Vane in just such a room — except hers was a proper cell, and when Aldric wrenched it open she looked up from the cot with ink still smudged on her fingers, because they'd let her keep her charts, thinking paper harmless.

"Took you long enough," Isolde whispered, and then, because there was no dignity left worth keeping, she crossed the cell and gripped Felix's arm hard enough to bruise. "There are forty-one of them down here. Forty-one children. I counted the ration ledgers they made me audit. I thought—" Her voice failed. She had thought she was buying escape. She had not known she was walking into the nursery of a slaughterhouse.

"We know," Felix said quietly. "Wren heard them."

Everyone looked at Wren. The girl had stopped at the first barred window and laid her palm flat against the cold iron. From somewhere deep in the corridor — from everywhere, from the stones themselves — came a sound Isolde would remember until she died: a single high note, thin as wire, rising and falling. The silenced children, answering. Singing through whatever cage had been built around their gifts, faint as starlight, and Wren's shard singing back.

"They can't hold it much longer," Wren said. "It hurts them to be quiet this deep."

Felix closed her eyes. When she opened them, the smuggler was gone and something older stood in her place. "Keys," she said to Aldric. "All of them."

* * *

They freed eleven before the bells rang.

It went like this: Aldric opened doors, Felix touched each child's brow and spoke one soft word — Isolde heard it only as a shiver in the air, a taste like struck iron — and the child woke, gasping, weeping, blinking up at strangers as though surfacing from deep water. Some cried. One laughed, a terrible bright laugh. None of them could walk well at first; their legs had forgotten. Isolde carried a boy of maybe six on her hip and felt him tremble like a bird, and thought of every map she'd ever sold, every border she'd smoothed, every blank space she'd left out of cowardice dressed as professionalism, and made herself a promise in the dark: *never again a blank space. Never again.*

The alarm came not as a shout but as the bell — the great green bell in the tower ringing at last, after years of silence, and its voice rolled down through the stone like the mountain clearing its throat.

"East stair's cut," Aldric reported, breathless at the junction. "Dain's veterans. We have the west cloister and maybe two minutes."

"Two minutes isn't enough for thirty children," Isolde said. Her mind, traitorous, brilliant, was already working — corridors, sightlines, the laundry chute, the sledge. "But it's enough for these eleven if we move *now*, and I know a route your rotation schedules don't cover, because I drew it and then erased it."

Felix stared at her. "You erased a route into the Quiet Bell?"

"I erased a lot of things." Isolde lifted the boy higher. "Come on."

They went through the wash-house, through steam and screaming linen, Isolde calling turns from memory — *left at the copper, down the slop-passage, mind the drain-grate* — while behind them boots thundered and the bell kept tolling. Wren refused to run ahead. She stayed at the rear, hand on the shard, and twice the pursuing footsteps faltered, slowed, tangled — as though the passage itself had grown reluctant — and Isolde did not ask what the girl was doing. Some debts you pay without auditing.

They came out through the tannery sluice into grey morning light and birch scrub, eleven children and four adults, and Isolde thought, absurdly, *we've done it* —

— and then the treeline across the stream filled with white-cloaked hunters, and Serah Dain rode out of it like winter given a uniform.

Behind them, the prison bell fell silent. Ahead, thirty more children remained behind those quiet walls, and Wren's small hand found Isolde's sleeve and gripped it, and the girl said, in a voice like a blade being drawn:

"We have to go back for the rest."
