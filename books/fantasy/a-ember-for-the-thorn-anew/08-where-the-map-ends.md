## 8. Where the Map Ends

The snow came down like a verdict.

Felix had climbed the Thornfell passes in every season a body could survive them, and she had never seen winter arrive with such intent. It did not drift or flurry; it fell in sheets, thick as wool, erasing the trail behind them within minutes of each footstep. Somewhere below and behind, torchlight flickered along the switchbacks — Serah Dain's column, climbing hard, closing the distance with the patience of people who knew exactly where their quarry must go.

Because there was nowhere else to go. Isolde's map ended here.

"Left fork," Isolde called from the front, her voice half-swallowed by wind. She held the chart against her chest like a shield, one gloved thumb tracing a line that existed on no other map in the world. "The pass narrows past the cairns. If we can reach the shelf before the snow seals it—"

"If it doesn't seal *us*," Aldric said. He carried two of the hushed children bundled against his chest, their small faces slack and dreaming beneath his cloak. Wren walked beside him with the third, a boy who could not speak but whose hand gripped hers with desperate, waking strength. The singing shard at Wren's throat hummed faintly through the storm — Felix felt it in her teeth, a thin bright thread of sound that the mountain itself seemed to lean toward.

"They're gaining," Felix said. She turned once, deliberately, and looked back down the pass.

Torchlight. Perhaps forty points of it, strung along the switchbacks like a necklace of embers. And above them, wheeling against the bruised sky, the silhouette of a drake — wings wide, rider small, patient as death.

*Serah.*

The name sat in Felix's chest like a swallowed stone. Twenty years ago they had sworn sisterhood on the border stones of Hollowmere, blood and salt and the old words, before the crown decided that the old words were heresy. Before Serah traded her sword-oaths for a hunter's commission and Felix traded her name for silence. They had been two halves of the same vow once. Now one half was hunting the other across a dying mountain, and Felix could not decide which of them had broken first.

"Felix." Wren tugged her sleeve. The girl's dark eyes were fixed on the descending drake, and her free hand rose, fingers shaping something urgent — not words, not yet, but the grammar of warning that Felix had spent weeks teaching her. *It comes. It comes fast.*

"I see it, little wren."

She drew steel.

Not her smuggler's blade — the other one, the one she had carried wrapped in oilcloth for fifteen years, the one she had sworn never to wake again. She peeled the wrapping back as she walked, letting the wind take the cloth, and the naked metal drank the storm-light and gave nothing back. The children whimpered. Aldric flinched; he had seen what she'd done at the high pass, and he knew what it cost.

"You don't have to," he said quietly. "I can fight."

"You'll be fighting drakes with a crossbow in a whiteout. Keep walking." She flexed her fingers around the grip. "I only need to buy us the shelf."

The drake came screaming out of the snow like a knife through silk.

Felix spoke.

One word — low, almost tender, the way you'd greet an old friend across a market square — and the blade woke. Light ran up its length like dawn breaking inside the steel, and the air around her changed pressure, went taut, went *listening*. The drake's rider saw the glow and shouted something that the wind tore apart, and then Felix swung, not at flesh but at the mountainside above the switchbacks.

The word she spoke next was older than the crown, older than the hunt, older than the grief between her and Serah Dain. The cliff answered. A curtain of snow and shattered rock came down across the lower path with a roar like the world clearing its throat, and the torchlight vanished beneath it, and the drake banked hard, rider clinging, rising out of reach into the murk.

Felix let the light die. Her knees nearly followed it.

"That's twice now," Aldric said, catching her elbow. "Twice you've shown them what you are."

"Then they already know." She steadied herself against him, breath ragged. "There's no hiding left, Aldric. There's only arriving."

* * *

They reached the shelf at dusk, if dusk could be said to exist inside a storm that had eaten the sun whole.

Isolde stood before a wall of stone that looked, to Felix's eyes, like every other wall of stone in the Thornfells — grey, wind-scoured, indifferent. But Isolde was weeping without seeming to know it, tears freezing on her cheeks as she pressed her palm flat against the rock and read it the way she read all her maps: with her whole self.

"It's here," she whispered. "Gods forgive me, it's actually here. I charted this from fragments, from oath-songs the crown made me copy and burn, and I told myself it was only lines on vellum—" Her voice cracked. "I sold them a hundred doors that didn't exist so they'd stop asking about this one. I never thought anyone would walk it."

"Can you open it?" Aldric asked.

"No." Isolde laughed, wet and broken. "No one opens it. That was always the point. It isn't a door for hands." She turned, and her eyes found Felix across the howling white. "It's a door for names."

Wren stepped forward. The shard at her throat sang louder now, a clear silver note that cut through the storm, and where its sound touched the stone, hairline seams of light appeared — veins of gold spreading through the rockface like frost blooming in reverse. The children stirred in their blankets. Even the mute boy lifted his head, mouth open, drinking the sound.

"The Door of Aember," Felix said. Fifteen years of silence, and still the words came easily, the way a mother's tongue comes back. "My grandmother sang me its threshold-song when I was smaller than Wren. I thought I'd dreamed it."

"Did she tell you how it opens?" Isolde asked.

"She told me it opens for a true name, freely given." Felix's throat tightened. "I thought she meant knocking politely."

Behind them, down the pass, the mountain groaned. The avalanche had bought hours, not days. Serah Dain did not lead forty hunters over a buried road; she led them *around* it, and Serah knew these mountains too — had known them, once, with Felix beside her, learning every goat-track and hidden shelf. There was no map advantage left. There was only speed.

"We open it now," Felix said. "Whatever it costs."

* * *

Serah Dain came out of the storm alone.

That was the cruelty of it. Felix heard the hunters first — dozens of boots, crossbows racked and ready, fanning across the mouth of the shelf with the drilled silence of the crown's best — and then the crowd parted, and there she was. Grey cloak rimed white. Sword sheathed. Hands empty and open, the way you approach a wounded animal, or someone you once loved.

"Felix Galloway." Serah's voice carried through the wind without effort, pitched the way commanders pitch things, but underneath it Felix heard the girl from Hollowmere, the one who used to steal honeycakes and blame the gulls. "Or whatever name you're wearing these days."

"You look well, Serah." Felix stepped forward, putting herself bodily between the hunters' line and the children. Behind her, the golden veins in the stone pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. "The commission suits you. All those badges. All that ash."

"Don't." Something moved behind Serah's eyes — quick, quickly buried. "Don't do that. I burned nothing at Hollowmere. I arrived after. You want to hate someone, hate the order that gave it to Dain's cousin to carry out. Hate me for what I've actually done." She paused. "I've come to offer terms."

"There are no terms for children."

"There are pardons." Serah spread her empty hands. "For you. For Vane. For Verrick — yes, I know he's alive, Felix, I signed the list that condemned him and I've regretted it every day since, and this is the only amends the crown will let me make. Full pardon. Clean papers. You walk out of these mountains free, and the hunt ends tonight." Her gaze slid past Felix, to the bundled shapes in Aldric's arms, to Wren standing with her hand pressed flat against the singing stone. "In exchange, the gifted children come south. To the sanctuaries."

"To the bells." Felix's voice went flat and cold. "You mean to the prison where you *hush* them. Where you hollow them out until they're quiet enough to be safe. I've seen your sanctuary, Serah. Rows of little beds and rows of little silences."

"They'd be *alive*."

"So would they be here!"

The words tore out of her rawer than she intended, and the shard answered — a flare of silver light, the stone veins brightening, the whole shelf humming like a struck bell. The hunters shifted nervously. Serah didn't move. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she let it, like a woman standing in a church.

"You were there," Serah said softly. "At the prison. I know you were — the reports say the cells were opened by a voice. So you've seen them, the ones we've already taken. Tell me honestly, Felix. Are they suffering? Or are they finally sleeping without screaming?" She took one step closer. "I have knelt beside children whose gift was burning them alive from the inside because no one taught them to hold it. The bells stop the burning. That's all they do. I have to believe that's all they do, or I couldn't put on the badge in the morning."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then I'm damned either way, and I'd rather be damned warm." Serah's jaw tightened. "Last offer, sister. Pardon for the adults. Sanctuary for the children. Or I take them, and I bury you and everyone who loves you under this shelf, and I spend the rest of my life telling myself it was mercy."

Felix looked at her — really looked, past the badge and the frost and the twenty years, at the face of the person who had known her first and longest, who had sworn the same oaths under the same stars, who had chosen the crown when Felix chose silence. Old love sat in Felix's chest like an ember under ash, still warm, still stubbornly alive, and it hurt worse than any wound the hunt had ever given her.

"You called me sister once," Felix said quietly. "On the border stones. Blood and salt."

"I remember."

"Then hear me. I won't give you the children. Not one. Not for a pardon, not for my life, not for yours." She turned her back on the drawn crossbows — a mad thing, and she did it anyway, because some truths needed the whole body to say them — and faced the glowing door. "And I won't run anymore, either."

The stone waited. The golden veins pulsed. And Felix understood, suddenly and completely, what her grandmother's song had been telling her all along: the door was listening. It had always been listening. It wanted what the oldest magic always wants.

A true name. Freely given.

Her name — the real one, the one buried under *Galloway*, under *smuggler*, under fifteen years of careful silence. The name that anchored the Door of Aember to the world. The name Maren Ost had bought from her in the dark below the snowline, and the name that, spoken aloud here, at the threshold, freely offered—

Would unmake her. She felt it the way you feel a stair missing in the dark. Speak it, and the door would open — and the speaker would pour herself into the opening, identity and oath and history spent like coin, leaving behind someone new. Someone nameless. Someone the door remembered so the country behind it wouldn't have to be forgotten.

Or she could refuse. Seal it forever, as her grandmother's song warned was possible — close the way home for good, keep herself intact, keep Wren safe and ordinary and silent, and let Thornfell stay drowned in memory until even the memory drowned too.

Snow settled on her shoulders. Serah's voice came soft behind her: "Felix. Whatever you're about to do — don't."

But it wasn't Felix the door was looking at.

It was Wren — standing at the stone with her palm flat against the gold-lit veins, the shard blazing at her throat, her dark eyes wide and certain, her lips parting around words she hadn't learned yet but somehow already knew.
