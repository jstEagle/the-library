## 10. An Ember Carried Forward

The door did not close so much as it finished speaking.

Felix felt it in her teeth first—a last syllable of stone settling into stone—and then in the place behind her ribs where her name had lived for thirty years and now lived nowhere at all. The light along the Door's seams dimmed like a hearth banked for the night. Frost crept across the threshold. And the mountain, which had held its breath since the beginning of the world, let it out.

She stood on the near side of it, the side that was still the world, with snow gathering in her lashes and Wren's small hand buried in hers.

The girl was crying without sound. She had been born mute; she would die mute; but the shard of singing sword at her throat hummed a low, sweet note that Felix had not heard from it before—not grief, not warning. Something closer to *home*. Wren pressed her free palm flat against the frozen door, and beneath her fingers the frost bloomed outward in patterns no cartographer could chart: the rivers of drowned Thornfell, the terraced orchards, the long road to a harbor city that existed now only because one child would carry its memory.

*It's safe,* the swordspeech said, though no blade had moved. It came through Wren's shard, through the girl herself, into the hollow where Felix's gift used to sit. *I have it. I have all of it.*

Felix knelt in the snow and pulled her close. "I know you do," she said aloud, and her voice cracked down the middle like river ice in spring.

Behind them, Isolde made a sound that might have been a sob or might have been laughter. The cartographer had her journal open against her knee, sketching by the last of the doorlight with hands that shook too badly for good lines—recording, recording, always recording, as if paper could be a second keeping-place for what they'd just given away. Aldric stood apart, facing back down the pass the way they'd come, one hand resting easy on the hilt of the blade Felix had woken. Watching for hunters. There were always hunters.

There was one hunter now, and she was very close.

* * *

Serah Dain came up the last stretch of the pass alone.

That alone told Felix everything. A commander of the crown's hunt did not ride alone unless she had decided, somewhere in the dark hours before dawn, that whatever happened on this ridge would never be written in any report. Her drake circled once overhead—a gray shape against gray cloud—and then folded itself onto the scree a hundred yards off and went still, folding its wings with the deliberate patience of an animal whose rider had taught it when not to listen.

Serah stopped ten paces away. Snow gathered on the pauldrons of her black coat, on the badge she no longer wore—the badge she had torn off somewhere between the prison of quiet bells and here, though Felix couldn't say when. Her face was the face Felix had loved across twenty years and a burned border: sharp-browed, wind-chapped, set now in an expression Felix had never seen on it before.

Doubt looked strange on Serah Dain. It fit her badly, like borrowed armor.

"Give me your name," Serah said.

The old reflex rose in Felix like a drawn blade: *the name is mine, the name is the door, the name dies with me.* But there was no name left to give. She reached inward and found only smooth emptiness, the way a healed wound feels when you press it—whole, and strange, and yours.

"I don't have one anymore," Felix said. It should have been terrifying to say aloud. It was terrifying. It was also, underneath the terror, the freest breath she had ever taken. "You're standing in front of someone who doesn't exist. Check your lists."

Something flickered across Serah's face—hurt, maybe, or its twin. "Felix—"

"That's the point." Felix stepped forward, putting herself squarely between Serah and the children huddled behind Isolde's cloak. Four of them had come out of the prison; four of them stood now in the snow, silent as their own held breaths, watching the two women the way children watch a storm decide which tree to strike. "That was always the point. You can't hunt what has no name. You can't burn a village for a woman who was never born. I gave it away, Serah. All of it. The oath, the gift, the country—it lives in them now." She tilted her head toward Wren, toward the shard glowing softly at the girl's throat. "In *her*, mostly."

"And what does that make you?" Serah's voice was low and rough. Twenty years of friendship, one year of war, and this was the question she chose: *what are you, if not what you were?*

Felix thought about it honestly, because Serah deserved honesty even now, especially now.

"Free," she said.

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere below, a hawk cried once and gave up on the weather.

"You know what they'll do if I go back empty-handed." Serah's jaw tightened. "They'll send Dain's replacement. They'll send worse than me. There are men under my command who think the children should burn simply for what they heard in that prison."

"Then don't go back empty-handed." Isolde spoke up from behind Felix, and Felix heard the tremor in the mapmaker's voice and the iron underneath it both. "Tell them the Door sealed itself. Tell them Thornfell is gone beyond reaching—that the passage collapsed, that no name exists that can open it again. Because it will be true. You watched it close. You'll swear it closed forever." Isolde's pen was already moving, scratching across a fresh page. "And here—" she tore the sheet free and held it out, "—is a survey of the pass showing the avalanche scar from the winter slide. Officially witnessed. By me. My maps still carry weight, Commander, however disgraced I am. Use me one last time. Let it be for something clean."

Serah stared at the offered page the way a starving woman stares at bread she suspects is poisoned. Then, slowly, she took it.

"The children," Serah said quietly. "The ones you pulled out of the bells. If anyone asks—"

"They're war orphans from Hollowmere," Felix said. "Refugees of the fire. You saw the village yourself. You know how many children the crown owes that ash."

A long silence. Snow ticked down through the windless air, fat and slow.

When Serah finally spoke, her voice had gone somewhere Felix had never heard it go—not commander, not hunter, not even sworn sister. Just tired. Just human. "I sat outside that prison for three days while they hushed those little ones," she said. "I told myself it was mercy. Quieter than burning. Kinder than the pyre." Her hand drifted to her bare chest, to the ghost of the missing badge. "And then I watched a woman with no name walk through a wall of winter and give away the only thing keeping her safe, so that a mute child could keep a dead country alive instead of letting us dig up its bones for weapons. So." She exhaled, long and unsteady. "So I don't know what I am either, anymore. Maybe that's fair. Maybe we traded."

"Serah." Felix said her name gently, the way you set down something breakable. "Go home. Plant something. Burn your lists. That ember you're carrying—" she touched her own sternum, over her heart, where the warmth of the morning still glowed, "—it doesn't need much. It just needs not to be smothered. That's all embers ever ask."

Serah looked at her for a long moment. Whatever passed between them then, no words were spent on it; some debts are settled better in silence. Then the commander of the crown's hunt turned, walked to her drake, and mounted. At the top of the pass she paused, half-turned against the gray sky, and raised two fingers to her brow—not a salute to a superior, not quite a farewell to a friend. Something older than either. Something soldiers do for the dead, or for the saved.

Then she was gone, downhill and southward, carrying her small fire home into the dark of the empire.

* * *

They walked out of the mountains the ordinary way, because the extraordinary way had closed behind them forever.

It took six days. They followed a smuggler's trail Maren Ost's people had cut years ago and abandoned—Isolde found it by reading the land the way other people read scripture, noting where the scree had been piled rather than scattered, where a cairn marked water. Aldric carried the smallest child on his shoulders for most of the second day and never once complained, though Felix saw him wince at every switchback. He had stopped asking whether she trusted him. Trust, she was learning, wasn't a thing you granted. It was a thing you built, plank by plank, in the ordinary carpentry of shared meals and shared watches and someone else's child asleep against your shoulder.

On the third night they made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's hut, and Wren sat beside Felix at the fire and drew shapes in the ash with a stick. Rivers. Terraces. A harbor with little ships. The drowned kingdom, sketched from memory—a memory that was not Wren's own and was entirely Wren's own now, both at once.

Felix watched her draw and felt the strangeness settle over her, gentle as snowfall: she could look at those shapes and feel nothing but love. Not longing. Not the ache of the exile who remembers. The country was gone from her, and in its absence there was room—room for the fire, for the girl's stick scratching in the ash, for Isolde humming off-key over her maps, for Aldric's snores, for tomorrow, which was no longer a thing to survive but a thing to meet.

"What do I call you?" Wren wrote in the ash, and underlined it twice.

Felix considered the question seriously, the way you consider a riddle that guards a treasure.

"Galloway works fine," she said at last. "It was never my true name anyway. It was just the one I wore so I could stay alive long enough to find you lot." She nudged the girl's shoulder. "Names made of the people around you hold better than names carved in doors. Ask any mapmaker."

Isolde, without looking up from her charts: "I heard that."

On the sixth day they came down out of the high country into a valley green despite the season, where a river ran loud and unfrozen over brown stones, and a market town smoked gently from a hundred chimneys—woodsmoke, bread-smoke, honest smoke. Nobody in that town knew what a swordspeaker was. Nobody would check Felix's hands for the calluses of oath-grip, or ask why a mute child wore a broken blade at her throat, or wonder why an ex-hunter flinched at church bells.

At the edge of town, where the road widened, they stopped.

"Well," said Isolde.

"Well," said Aldric.

Wren tugged Felix's sleeve and pointed—at the inn, at the bakery, at a wooden post by the bridge where notices were nailed. One notice, newer than the rest, fluttered in the valley breeze. Even at this distance Felix could read the crowned seal at its head, and beneath it, in letters meant to travel far:

**BY ORDER OF THE REGENT: ALL PERSONS POSSESSING KNOWLEDGE OF THE LOST KINGDOM OF THORNFELL ARE HEREBY REQUIRED TO PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR QUESTIONING. REWARDS OFFERED.**

The hunt was not over. It had only changed its shape—no longer hunting the gifted, perhaps, but hunting the *remembered*, the witnesses, the keepers of what the crown had drowned and now, too late, wanted back.

Wren looked up at Felix. In her eyes was the whole drowned kingdom, patient as seed under snow, waiting to be planted somewhere new.

Felix Galloway—who had no true name, no oath, no country, and more family than she had ever once dared want—rolled her shoulders, felt the old familiar weight of the blade at her hip, and smiled.

"Well," she said. "We've never been required to present ourselves anywhere in our lives. Seems a shame to start now."

She took Wren's hand, and they walked into the town together, five shadows and one small song, carrying their ember forward into whatever waited past the bridge.
