## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fence post had rotted through at the base, and Emrys Iversen was grateful for it. Grateful the way a man is grateful for any small, honest problem — one that could be solved with a spade, a new post, and an afternoon of sweat that meant nothing more than sweat.

He dug around the old post in the grey light before noon, the air smelling of wet earth and coming rain. Thornvale's weather always announced itself hours early, rolling down from the northern ridge in slow grey banners, and the valley had taught him to read its moods the way he'd once been taught to read — well. The way he'd once been taught other things.

He did not think about those things.

Two years, and he had become very good at not thinking. It was a discipline like any other; Maeve would have appreciated the irony of that, if he'd ever let himself say her name even inside his own skull. He mostly didn't. He thought of her as *the cottage*, or *the books*, or *the life*, as though the woman herself could be dissolved into the furniture of what he'd abandoned. It was cowardly. He knew it was cowardly. Cowardice, practiced daily, begins to feel like peace.

He tamped the new post into its hole, packed the earth down with the flat of the spade, and straightened his back with a groan that belonged to a much older man. From the slope above, Tam Osgood's sheep watched him with their flat yellow indifference. Below, past the fence line, the village of Netherby straggled along the river — forty roofs, a mill, a chapel whose bell rang for weddings and funerals and nothing in between. Nobody in Netherby knew what he had been. He'd told them he was a clerk's son fallen on hard times, and they'd believed him because the truth was too strange to suspect: that he had been apprentice to a hedge-witch of considerable and uncomfortable power, and that he had run from her in the night with nothing but a change of clothes and a terror he still could not fully name.

Magic did not cross into Thornvale. That was the valley's whole virtue, the reason he'd chosen it out of a dozen valleys on a map he'd stolen from a carter. Old stories said the first settlers had bargained something away to buy that silence — a tithe, a name, a child — but the stories were thin as winter broth and nobody in Netherby cared to thicken them. What mattered was that here, a headache was only a headache. Here, no voice whispered out of hearth-smoke. Here, Emrys Iversen could mend fences until his hands were ruined and callused, and die an unremarkable man, and that was the whole of his ambition.

He was carrying the spade back toward the shed when the storm broke.

It came all at once, the way mountain storms do — a wall of rain marching up the valley, swallowing the far fields, then the near ones, then him. He made the shed, then made the house across the muddy yard, a low stone cottage he rented from Widow Crane for almost nothing because nobody else wanted to live so close to the treeline. He got the door shut behind him, dripping, and was reaching for the kettle when he heard the knock.

Three small knocks. Barely a knock at all.

He stood with his hand on the kettle's handle and did not move. Nobody walked to his door in this weather. The nearest farm was half a mile downriver, and no child — and it *was* a child's knock, he was certain of that, small knuckles rapping twice more — no child should be abroad in a storm like this one.

He opened the door.

Rain came in sideways, cold as well water. On his step stood a girl of perhaps eight or nine years, barefoot, soaked to the skin, her dark hair plastered flat against a face so pale it seemed borrowed from the moon. Her dress was fine once — he could see the ghost of embroidery along the hem, silver thread worked in patterns he almost recognized — but it was torn at the shoulder and black with mud. She looked up at him, and her eyes were the strangest thing about her: not frightened, not pleading. *Waiting.* As though she had knocked on many doors and expected exactly this one to open.

"Child," he said, "where in God's name—" and then she spoke.

Not the valley tongue. Not the king's speech, nor the river dialect, nor anything sold in any market between here and the coast. The sounds that came out of her were older than all of those — vowels that bent in the middle, consonants like stones turning under water — and Emrys felt them land in his body the way a struck bell feels its own ringing. His knees went loose. The kettle, forgotten, began a thin whine on the stove behind him.

He knew the language. He had never spoken it, never dared, but he knew it — from the books in Maeve's locked chest, the ones she'd let him dust but never open, the ones whose spines bore titles in just such letters. She had read aloud from one of them exactly once, on a night he'd spent two years trying to forget, and her voice had changed when she did it. Deeper. Older. As though something were speaking *through* her rather than from her.

The child said perhaps six words. He understood none of them and all of them, the way a man understands a wound without needing it translated.

"No," he heard himself say. "No, you can't — you can't be—"

She tilted her head. Rain ran off her chin. And then, slowly, deliberately, she reached up and pressed one small hand flat against the doorframe — against the wood of his threshold, the boundary of the valley that magic could not cross — and where her fingers touched, the grain of the wood darkened and curled, like paper held too near a flame. Not burning. *Writing.* Thin characters surfaced in the oak, glowing faintly gold, then fading, then surfacing again, patient as breathing.

Emrys stumbled backward into his own kitchen and sat down hard on the bench by the stove, and the kettle screamed, and outside the thunder rolled down the valley like a barrel falling downstairs.

*It followed me,* he thought, and the thought arrived already knowing it was wrong. Nothing had followed him. Magic could not cross into Thornvale; that was the whole architecture of his safety, the load-bearing wall of his two quiet years. But the child was *in* the valley now, standing on his step, and whatever moved beneath her skin had crossed with her — or had been carried, or had *come home*. Because there was something in the writing on his doorframe that snagged at him, some half-remembered shape, and when he leaned forward despite himself and looked properly, properly, the way Maeve had taught him to look —

He stopped.

One of the characters was an Iversen mark. His family's mark. The sign his grandmother had scratched into the lintel of every house they'd ever owned, the sign he'd assumed was a farmer's superstition against the evil eye. It was not a superstition. It was a word. And it was part of whatever sentence the child was writing into his door.

The knocking came again. Three small knocks, polite, inexorable.

Emrys looked at the fire, at the dry socks on the line, at the fence he'd mended that morning — the small honest problems of a small honest life — and felt all of it tilt, gently and completely, like a table losing one leg. Somewhere south of here a woman was probably cursing his name, and somewhere further south than that, a king wore a crown, and none of it was any of his business, and all of it was coming through his door whether he opened it again or not.

He got up. He opened the door. He lifted the shivering child over the threshold, and the moment her bare feet touched his floorboards, the storm outside went suddenly, utterly silent — not ended, he realized, but *listening*.

The girl looked up at him, and for the first time her waiting face changed. She smiled, small and sad, and spoke one more word in the old tongue.

This one, he understood. It was the word for *home*.
