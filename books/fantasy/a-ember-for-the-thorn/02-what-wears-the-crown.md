## 2. What Wears the Crown

They left Thornvale before dawn, when the mist still lay in the valley like a held breath.

Emrys had packed nothing but bread, a skin of water, and the iron knife he used for mending fences — a tool, not a weapon, though he carried it now like a talisman against everything he had spent two years pretending not to be. The girl walked beside him without being told to. She had simply appeared at his elbow as he stepped over his own threshold, barefoot on the frost-brittle grass, and looked up at him with that patient, waiting face, and he had understood that wherever the seal went, he was already going too.

The border of the valley was a line no one could see and everyone in Thornvale knew by heart — a ridge of grey stone where the pines grew crooked, leaning away from something invisible. Emrys had crossed it twice in his life: once going in, fleeing Maeve's cottage and everything she'd tried to make of him, and once now, going out, with a mute child's hand curled small and cold in his own.

He felt it the moment they passed the stones. It was like stepping out of still water into a river's current — magic everywhere, ambient and indifferent, pressing against his skin. The girl shuddered. The words she'd spoken in his kitchen, the ones that had ached in his bones, seemed to hum louder now, as if the whole waking world were an instrument and she its tuning fork.

*You are a living seal,* he thought, looking down at her. *And I am taking you straight toward the thing that wants to break you.*

But there was nowhere else. He had asked the valley elders — old Marta, who remembered the last king's coronation; the priest of the low shrine, who kept books no one read. They had all said the same thing, in the flat voices of people reciting a truth worn smooth by generations: *When the seal speaks, it must be brought before the crown. That is the law older than law.* Thornvale had been founded for exactly this purpose — a place where magic could not cross, where a child like this one could grow up unremarked, until the day she couldn't.

The road east took them nine days. Through farm country gone thin and hungry, through a market town where soldiers in the king's black-and-gold watched every traveler with eyes that lingered too long on children. Emrys taught himself not to flinch at the sight of them. He taught the girl to keep her mouth closed, her hands still, her silence absolute. She learned quickly. She learned everything quickly, which frightened him more than the soldiers did.

On the ninth evening, the capital rose out of the plain like a mountain that had decided to be a city — white walls, green roofs, and above it all the spire of the palace, crowned with gold that caught the dying sun and burned.

Emrys stood on the last hill and felt the ache in his bones sharpen into something almost like pain.

"Almost there," he said aloud, though she didn't need telling. Her grip on his hand had tightened miles ago.

* * *

Wren did not have words for what she was. She had words for everything else — the Old Tongue lived in her the way blood lives in a body, filling every part of her whether she willed it or not — but for herself, for the thing inside her that made kings' priests go pale and storms fall silent, she had only images.

She was a door. She knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat. Somewhere behind her was a room, and the room was full of dark, and her whole self — her ribs, her breath, the small warm engine of her — existed to stand shut in front of it.

For two years the door had been safe. The valley was a wall around a wall around a wall, and the dark behind her had slept.

Now the walls were gone, and the dark was awake, and it was *pulling*.

She felt it first in the market town, a tugging behind her navel like a fishhook set in her center. It grew stronger every mile east. By the time they reached the capital it was all she could feel — a slow, patient drawing-out, as if someone were unraveling her thread by thread from very far away. She had begun to see the threads themselves at night: silver filaments rising off her sleeping body, drifting east through the shuttered windows of whatever inn or stable they'd found, streaming away like smoke that had somewhere to be.

The man — Emrys, though she thought of him as *the sad one who mends things* — did not know yet how much of her had already gone. She could tell because he still looked at her with worry instead of grief.

Tonight they were close enough that the pulling had a face. When Wren closed her eyes she could see it: a hall of pale stone, a throne, and on the throne a man wearing a crown that was not a crown but a *mouth*. The man beneath it flickered — sometimes hollow-eyed and slack, sometimes briefly, terribly present, a drowning sailor surfacing just long enough to scream before the water took him again.

The mouth-crown drank. Thread by silver thread, it drank.

And far away, in a loft above a chandler's shop, Wren opened her eyes in the dark and pressed her hands over her sternum, where the ache was deepest, and spoke into her palms the only word that had ever helped:

*Hold.*

* * *

They gained their audience through desperation rather than design. On their tenth morning in the city, Wren collapsed in the street outside the palace gates — folded up like a puppet with cut strings, silver light leaking visibly from her skin now, threads of it drifting east through the air where anyone might see. A crowd gathered. Someone shouted *witchcraft*, and Emrys did the only thing left to do: he picked her up and walked straight at the guards, shouting that he bore a message for the crown itself, that the kingdom was in danger, that the child was dying — and the word *dying* did what reason could not. The gates opened.

What he saw in the throne room he would spend the rest of his life trying not to dream about.

King Aldric sat upon the throne of pale stone, and he was both more and less than a man. His body was gaunt, gray-skinned, the body of a starving saint; but his eyes were the worst — twin wells with nothing at the bottom, reflecting light without holding it. Gold light pulsed faintly beneath his skin, moving in slow rivers, and Emrys understood with sick certainty that what he was seeing was stolen light. Wren's light. The king's jaw hung slightly open, and from it came a sound that stopped Emrys's heart: two voices speaking at once, one deep and resonant as a bell struck underground, the other thin and cracked and human, layered atop each other like a hymn sung by a choir of one.

"—closer," said the bell-voice. "Closer, little door. Come and be opened."

"No," whispered the other voice. Aldric's voice. "No — run — boy, take her and *run*—"

The crown moved. Not the man — the crown. It settled lower on the brow like a spider adjusting its stance, and the silver threads streaming from Wren's unconscious body quickened, pouring across the marble floor in a glittering river, vanishing into the gold band as into a drain.

Emrys ran forward anyway. He got three steps before the pressure hit him — not physical, but a weight of *presence* that drove him to his knees, that filled his skull with the taste of cold iron and the sound of his own name being pronounced correctly for the first time in his life, in the Old Tongue, by something that should not know it.

"You are Iversen's get," the bell-voice said, almost fondly. "How convenient. How *very* convenient."

Then the doors of the throne room boomed open behind him, and a voice he had spent two years dreading and dreaming of cut through the dark like a blade through silk.

"Get away from my student, you *thief*."

Maeve stood in the doorway with frost crawling up the pillars on either side of her, her gray hair loose and wild, her staff blazing with a light that hurt to look at. Older than he remembered. Angrier than he remembered. And alive — impossibly, gloriously alive, here, at the end of the world.

"Maeve," Emrys breathed.

"Don't you *Maeve* me." She strode down the aisle, and the entity on the throne actually recoiled, the crown flattening like a threatened animal. She knelt beside Wren, pressed two fingers to the girl's throat, and her face went grim. "Two years, Emrys. Two years I searched. And now you crawl back dragging the last seal in the world, half-drained, into its open mouth." She looked up at him, and beneath the fury in her eyes was something worse: fear. "I hope you're proud."

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it past all saying.

"Sorry won't hold a binding." She rose, planting her staff, and the frost spread. "We have until moonrise, maybe less, before it takes the rest of her. So you can kneel there drowning in guilt, boy — or you can stand up, remember what I taught you, and help me save her life."

Emrys looked at Wren, small and silver-fading on the marble. At the king, whose cracked human voice was whispering *please* over and over like a prayer. At the crown, drinking.

He stood up.

"What do I do?" he asked — and the moment the words left him, he felt the truth settle over him like a mantle, heavy and inevitable: this duty had been waiting for him since before he was born, and running had only ever delayed it.

Maeve began to answer — but the bell-voice laughed, low and delighted, and the laughter shook dust from the rafters.

"Oh, *yes*," it said. "Let us begin."
