## 3. The Unbinding

Maeve had bound things before. Foxes to their dens, fevers to their courses, a grieving widow's tongue for three days so she might sleep. Binding was the first craft she'd learned and the last she trusted, because every binding was a bargain struck with something that did not wish to be held.

The throne room of Caer Aldric smelled of old smoke and older stone. Torches guttered in iron rings though no wind moved; the light bent strangely toward the dais, toward the man slumped in the high seat with a crown of black iron sitting on his brow like a wound that refused to close.

"Emrys." She said his name quietly, the way one speaks near a sleeping animal. "The circle. Now."

He knelt at the northern point without argument, chalk dust on his fingers, his jaw set in that particular stubbornness she remembered from his boyhood—the look he wore when he was about to do something foolish and call it duty. Two years he'd hidden from her. Two years of mending fences while the world outside Thornvale's borders frayed like rope left in weather. And now here he was, kneeling in a king's house, about to pay for a debt he hadn't known he owed.

She took the southern point herself. Between them, Wren sat cross-legged inside the inner ring, small hands folded, eyes closed. The child's silence had never seemed like absence to Maeve. It seemed like a vessel kept deliberately empty—a cup shaped to hold something vast.

"Begin," Wren said, in the Old Tongue, and the word went through Maeve like a bell struck behind her ribs.

They began.

* * *

The counter-binding was not a spell so much as a conversation conducted in grammar older than kingdoms. Maeve spoke the anchoring lines; Emrys answered with the yielding ones; and between their voices Wren's silence deepened until it rang. The torches steadied. The strange pull toward the dais slackened, then snapped taut, like a fisherman's line finding its catch.

And out of the dark beneath the crown, something laughed.

It was not a sound so much as a pressure—laughter felt in the teeth, in the fillings of old cavities, in the mortar between stones. King Aldric's head lifted. His eyes were still hollow, still rimmed with the gray of a man long drowned, but now they *focused*, and what focused them was not the king.

"Hedge-witch," the thing said through his mouth, in a voice layered over Aldric's like rot over wood. "And her runaway. How quaint. Do you know what you kneel inside?"

"A throne room," Maeve said evenly, keeping the cadence of the binding unbroken.

"A cage." The king's body rose from the seat, joints moving wrong, too smooth, as if the flesh were a glove worn by a larger hand. "Built by your betters. Sealed by—" the mouth curved, savoring it, "—an Iversen."

Maeve's voice caught on the next syllable. Beside her, Emrys went white.

"You lie," he said.

"Do I?" The entity drifted down the steps, robes pooling around feet that barely touched stone. "Ask your mistress. Ask the books she burned and the books she didn't. The first seal was forged in blood willingly given, and the name written into its heart was Iversen. Your line made the lock, boy. Your line alone can remake it. Why do you think the valley you hid in has no magic? You were living inside the shadow of your own inheritance and never knew it."

Maeve had known. Gods forgive her, she had known since the night Emrys fled, when she opened his abandoned trunk and found the birth-token wrapped in his swaddling cloth—an iron ring stamped with a thorn and a river, the sigil of the first seal-wright. She had let him run because running was the only mercy she could offer, and because some cowardly part of her hoped the blood had thinned enough to be useless.

It had not thinned. It had only waited.

"Emrys," she said, and her voice cracked despite forty years of practice holding steady. "Don't listen. Keep the line."

But the entity turned its stolen face to him, and softened, horribly, into something almost kind.

"I offer you better than listening," it said. "I offer you the door. Walk away. Take the witch, take the child, walk back to your valley of forgetting. I will unravel this seal slowly—years, decades. You will live out your little life among your fences and your quiet neighbors, and you will die warm. The kingdom will burn after. Not during. I am patient. I have been patient under crowns longer than your people have had names."

The torches dimmed. In the half-dark, Maeve saw the temptation move across Emrys's face like weather—not greed, never greed, but exhaustion. The longing of a man who had already run once and knew exactly how sweet the running tasted.

"And if I refuse?" Emrys asked.

"Then you take the seal yourself. Alive. Bound. Forever aware, forever holding, forever *here*." The entity spread Aldric's arms wide, encompassing the throne, the walls, the weight of a nation pressing down. "Your predecessor chose it gladly. Let us see if two generations diluted the courage along with everything else."

* * *

The binding lines flared. Somewhere beneath the entity's dominion, the true king surfaced.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next: the hollow eyes flooded with a desperate, human blue, and Aldric's own voice broke through—ragged, real, a man shouting up from the bottom of a well.

"*Kill me.*" He was on his knees now, his own knees, shaking. "Witch—boy—whoever you are—listen to me. Don't renew it. Don't feed it another soul. Cut my throat and let the seal fail and let me *end*. Twenty years I've screamed inside my own skull. Twenty years watching my hands sign laws I hate, my mouth kiss my wife with its filth in it. Death is a door. This is a wall. Please. I am begging you as one servant of the realm to another. *Let me die.*"

Maeve's hands trembled on the anchor-line. Every craft she possessed, every instinct honed across four decades, told her mercy when she heard it. This was a man in torment asking for the only kindness left.

Beside her, Emrys whispered, "Maeve—"

"I know," she said. "I know what he's asking."

"And I know what I'm answering."

She looked at him then, really looked—at the fence-mender's calluses, the apprentice's ink stains that had never quite washed away, the fear and the resolve tangled together in his face like thorn through ivy. He was not brave. That was the thing she had always loved about him, even in fury: he had never once pretended to be brave. He simply did the necessary thing while visibly, honestly terrified, and somehow that was worth more than all the fearless heroes in the songs.

"The entity offered you escape," she said. "Aldric is offering you death. Everyone in this room is offering you a way out except the truth."

"The truth is mine," Emrys said. "It's been mine since Wren knocked on my door speaking the Old Tongue. I just spent two years pretending otherwise."

He stepped forward, out of the circle, breaking the pattern—and Maeve's heart seized—only to kneel before the broken king and take Aldric's trembling hands in his own.

"I won't kill you," Emrys said quietly. "I won't abandon you either. Both of those are just different ways of walking away, and I've done enough walking." He looked up at the entity, at the darkness coiling behind the crown. "You want an Iversen? Here's one. But hear me—I don't take your bargain, and I don't take his death. I take the seal. On my terms. Freely given, freely held, and *witnessed*—by her, by the child, by the Old Tongue itself. So that no crown ever again holds a secret seal. So that someday someone smarter than me finds a way to end this without a prison at all."

The laughter came again, but thinner now. Uncertain.

"Foolish," it hissed. "Foolish, foolish boy—"

"Yes," Emrys agreed. "My mistress will tell you the same."

Maeve wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, lifted the chalk, and began drawing the new circle around them both.

"Witnessed," she said, in the Old Tongue, and beside her Wren opened their eyes at last—dark, ancient, gentle—and spoke the word that would carry it down through years:

"*Witnessed.*"

The torches went out. In the dark, Emrys's hand found hers and squeezed once, and Maeve held on, because that was the whole of her craft in the end—to hold on—and began to speak him into the stone.
