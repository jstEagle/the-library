## 4. An Ember Kept

Wren learned the palace by its sounds before its shapes.

The throne room had a sound now that it had not had before—a low, patient hum beneath the flagstones, like a hearth banked for the night. The servants said the stones were settling after the storm of the unbinding. Wren knew better. She pressed her bare palm flat against the cold floor each morning when she came to visit, and she felt him there: Emrys Iversen, hedge-witch, fence-mender, coward-turned-keeper, standing in the heart of the room where no one could see him and everyone was kept safe by his standing.

Alive but bound. That was what Maeve had said, her voice rough as a whetstone, the night it was done. *He is alive, girl. Remember that when you grieve.*

Wren did remember. But remembering did not stop the grief from sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She had been mute for six years. The Old Tongue had lived in her mouth all that time with nowhere to go, filling her like water fills a cracked jar, leaking out only in dreams. It had taken Emrys—frightened, stubborn, kind Emrys—to teach her that the words were not a curse but a key. Now she spoke to him every day, kneeling at the center of the throne room where the light fell through the high windows in long amber columns, and told him everything.

"The baker's boy stole a honeycomb again," she said this morning, her voice small in the vastness. "Aldric laughed. Really laughed. Maeve says he hadn't laughed in three years, though she said it like she was angry about it." She smoothed her hand over the floor. "I'm learning to bake. Badly. You'd tell me I measure wrong. You always said measuring was just magic too lazy to commit."

The floor did not answer. But the hum deepened, just slightly, the way a sleeping man's breathing changes when someone sits beside his bed. Wren chose to believe it was him listening. She had chosen to believe a great many things since the night the crown came off, and believing had kept her upright.

* * *

King Aldric knelt to no one. Everyone in the capital knew this. He had knelt to ambassadors out of courtesy, to priests out of politics, but never—not once in his reign—to a subject, freely, without ceremony or witness arranged in advance.

On the ninth morning after the unbinding, he came to the throne room while Wren was there, dismissed his guards at the door, and knelt on the flagstones above the place where Emrys stood bound inside the binding itself.

"I am told you can carry words to him," Aldric said. His voice was his own again—one voice, worn and human, with a rasp at the bottom of it like a boot dragged over gravel. For two years Wren had heard it doubled, layered with something old and hungry speaking through the king's throat, and hearing it singular now still made her want to weep with relief. "I am told the Old Tongue carries farther than any whisper."

"It does," Wren said.

"Then tell him what I could not say while that thing wore my mouth." The king bowed his head. His crown lay on a cushion across the room; he had not touched it since. "Tell him I begged it for death, near the end, and it would not grant me even that. Tell him that when your friend stood in my place, he gave me back more than my body. He gave me back the right to be ashamed of what was done in my name—and shame, girl, is a king's most honest garment."

Wren's throat tightened. "He didn't do it for you," she said, because Emrys would have wanted the truth carried, not comfort. "He did it because he couldn't bear to walk away. He tried to walk away his whole life. This time he couldn't."

"Then he is braver than every knight in my guard," Aldric said, "and I will spend the rest of my reign making certain his sacrifice is not a secret kept by three people and a witch." He rose, stiffly, an old man's knees. "The court will know. The histories will know. And the seal will be honored, not hidden. That is the least of my debts."

That evening, Wren knelt on the warm flagstones and spoke the king's words into the Old Tongue, syllable by syllable, letting them sink down through stone and silence into whatever country Emrys now inhabited. When she finished, the hum swelled—once, twice—like a heartbeat remembered from outside.

Maeve, watching from the doorway with her arms crossed and her silver-streaked hair escaping its braid, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief deciding against itself.

"He always did like being thanked," she said. "Rude little apprentice. Never once swept my hearth properly."

* * *

They gave Wren a room in the east tower, with a window that caught the morning sun and a bed with more blankets than she had ever owned in her life. She had arrived at Thornvale barefoot and voiceless, carrying nothing but a language that burned. Now she had shoes lined with fleece, a cloak dyed the blue of the royal house, and a desk stacked with paper.

It was at that desk that the true work began.

"Say it slowly," she told the chamberlain's daughter, a solemn girl of ten named Ilse who had appointed herself Wren's first student. "Not *vael-ith*. *Vae*-lith. The breath comes first. The word is mostly breath."

"*Vae-lith,*" Ilse repeated, and the candle between them guttered and steadied, and the girl's eyes went wide as coins. "It *did* something."

"Every word does something," Wren said. "That's why they were hidden. That's why I was—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, gentler. "That's why we're going to learn them properly, all of us together, so that no one ever has to be made into a secret again."

Because that was the truth she had pieced together from Maeve's bitter lessons and Emrys's halting explanations and the thing the entity had snarled in the throne room before it was bound: seals were made in ignorance. Some frightened Iversen, generations ago, had woven a child—or a child-shaped thing—into the Old Tongue's deepest knot, and had told no one, and had died, and the knowledge had rotted into ritual, and ritual into cruelty, until a mute girl wandered out of the mountains wearing a binding she never consented to, and a king sat hollow-eyed under a crown that ate him by inches.

Secrets were how it happened. So Wren would end the secrets.

By midwinter she had seven students: Ilse, two scribes, a guardsman with surprisingly careful pronunciation, the court physician, a kitchen boy who turned out to have an ear for cadence, and—most improbable of all—the king himself, who sat among the children on a plain wooden bench and practiced his breath-words like any other pupil, and did not once pull rank when Ilse corrected him.

"They'll write songs about this someday," Maeve said, observing a lesson from the back of the hall. "The Mute Girl Who Taught the Crown to Speak."

"I wasn't mute," Wren said. "I was unheard. There's a difference."

Maeve looked at her for a long moment, and something in the old witch's weathered face shifted—softened, almost, before she wrestled it back behind her scowl. "Hmph. You sound like him." She jerked her chin toward the direction of the throne room. "Come on, then. Evening visit. He gets sulky if we're late, and yes, before you say it, I know he can't sulk. Let me pretend."

* * *

The winter visits became a ritual of their own. Maeve brought complaints—about the court, about the cold, about students everywhere mispronouncing her life's work. Wren brought stories, and sometimes bread, and once a drawing Ilse had made of the whole strange family of them: the witch, the girl, the kneeling king, and a shape of golden light in the middle of the floor that Ilse had insisted was "the man who lives in the castle's heart."

And every evening, before they left, Wren knelt and spoke the same closing words into the stone, the ones Emrys had murmured to her their first night in Thornvale, when she had been too frightened to sleep and he had sat up with her by the fire:

*Vae-lith, vae-soren, immaeth.* Rest, and be warm, and stay.

Each time, the hum answered. Each time, she let herself believe.

But on the last night of winter, as she rose from the flagstones, the answer changed.

The hum did not fade as it always did. It held—longer, deeper—and then, beneath it, faint as a finger drawn in frost, Wren felt something she had not felt since the night of the unbinding: a pressure against the edges of the seal. Not the old hunger. Nothing like the thing that had worn the crown. Something smaller. Wearier. Almost... questioning.

Almost like a voice, testing whether it still remembered how to speak.

Wren went very still, her hand flat against the warming stone, her breath clouding in the cold air of the great empty hall.

"Maeve," she called softly, not taking her eyes from the floor. "Maeve, come here. Quickly."

Far beneath her palm, patient as an ember kept alive through the longest night, something waited to be heard.
