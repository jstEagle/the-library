## 1. Ink That Will Not Dry

The trouble began, as most troubles do, quietly—on an ordinary morning, in a room full of ordinary women bent over their desks, with nothing louder than the scratch of quills and the sigh of wind against the temple's high windows.

Emrys Kavanagh had been copying the *Litanies of the Sealed Border* for eleven days. She knew the text by heart now, which was dangerous, because knowing it by heart meant her hands could wander while her mind wandered elsewhere, and wandering was the one thing she could never afford. She kept her eyes down. She kept her breath even. She kept the warmth in her fingertips pressed flat against the desk's cold oak, as she had done every day since she was six years old and had first learned that some things about herself were not to be shown, not to be named, not even inside her own head.

*The veil is mercy,* the litany went. *The veil is sleep. What burns beyond it shall not cross; what wakes beyond it shall not keep.*

Her quill moved through the familiar phrases. The ink flowed black and obedient from the well. And yet.

She had noticed it three days ago: the ink on her own pages did not dry at the same rate as everyone else's. Sister Maren's copies were matte within the hour, settled and dead as ash. Emrys's pages stayed faintly lustrous, holding the light a half-second too long, as if the letters were still deciding something. She had taken to fanning them discreetly, or turning her drying-sheets early, or angling her desk so the morning sun fell across the wet lines and burned the shine away. No one had noticed. No one ever noticed. That was the whole architecture of her life—a building with no visible doors, built one careful stone at a time over nineteen years.

She pressed her fingertips harder into the oak. The warmth answered, faint and patient, like a cat that had waited by a door for so long it no longer expected it to open. It was always worse in autumn. Something in the turning of the year, in the valley's amber light slanting through the scriptorium's eastern windows, made the warmth rise in her like sap in a tree that had been told, on pain of death, that it was furniture.

"Emrys." Sister Maren's voice, dry as the chalk she mixed her ink-ash with. "Your margins drift."

Emrys looked down. Her left margin had curved, very slightly, inward—following some line only her hand could feel. She smoothed the page flat and gave the small, apologetic smile that was expected of her, the one that said *youngest scribe in the room, still learning, still humbled.*

"My apologies, Sister."

"Mm." Maren's gaze lingered a moment. Not suspicious—Maren had never once been suspicious in the fourteen years Emrys had copied under her—but thorough, the way a woman is thorough who has spent her life checking other people's work for flaws. "Mind your lines. The Litanies forgive nothing."

*No,* Emrys thought, returning her quill to its groove. *They certainly don't.*

Around her, the scriptorium worked in its ancient rhythm. Nine desks, nine sisters, nine pools of lamplight that would be replaced by candles come dusk. The Veiled Valley's temple held perhaps sixty souls in all—scribes, gardeners, the lay-brothers who kept the water cisterns, Mother Sable in her tower with her ledgers and her silences. Their work was copying: warding texts, border hymns, the genealogies of families who had lived inside the valley for nine generations without producing so much as a flicker of unlicensed gift. That was the covenant. That was why the valley existed. Beyond the borders, the crown hunted the gifted with escalating fervor; within the veil, magic simply *did not happen*, and so no one needed to be hunted, and everyone needed only to be quiet, and useful, and ordinary.

Emrys had built her entire self around being ordinary. She was good at it now. She had a reputation for steady hands and a mild disposition, for remembering which sister took honey in her tea and which preferred her bread stale. She was the one they asked to write out the fair copies for weddings, because her letterforms were lovely. She was nobody special. She had spent two decades making sure of it, and if there were nights when she lay awake in her narrow cell beneath the eaves and felt the warmth pulsing in her palms like a second heartbeat, she reminded herself: *the veil holds. The warmth has never once crossed my skin. It can't. This is the safest place in the world for what I am.*

She believed that the way you believe in floorboards. Every morning she stepped out onto it and it held, and every morning the belief grew another layer thick.

It was nearly noon when the bells changed.

Not the noon bell—this was the gate bell, the deep one rung only for arrivals at the temple doors, and rung wrong: three strikes instead of the usual single toll, then silence, then three more. Emrys's quill stopped mid-word. Around the scriptorium, eight other quills stopped too, in a cascade of small hesitations, because every woman in that room knew the pattern. Three-and-three was the summons for assembly. Three-and-three had not sounded since Emrys was twelve.

"They're early," whispered Sister Odile, who was old enough to remember the last time. "The couriers from the pass. They're not due till the equinox."

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. They were already rising, setting down quills, blotting pages with the unconscious tidiness of women whose whole religion was order. Emrys blotted her Litanies, capped her inkwell, and felt the warmth in her fingertips flare once—sharp, sudden, like a dog scenting weather—and settle.

*Nothing,* she told it firmly. *This is nothing.*

She was already learning, though she didn't know it yet, how lies of that size feel in the mouth.

* * *

The temple's great hall had been built to hold processions, and held a crowd of sixty the way a cupped hand holds rain. Emrys found a place near the back among the younger scribes, standing on the worn flagstones where centuries of feet had polished a shallow basin, and looked up at the dais where Mother Sable sat beneath the carved motto of the valley: *WITHIN THE VEIL, PEACE.*

Mother Sable was old the way river stones are old—not fragile, but worn into a shape the water had chosen. Her eyes, pale as winter lettuce, moved across the assembled faces and rested, for exactly one heartbeat too long, on Emrys. Then they moved on. Emrys's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

At the foot of the dais stood a stranger.

He wore the crown's gray-and-scarlet, and he wore it badly—or rather, he wore it correctly, and the correctness itself was the insult: the stiff collar, the riding boots still dusty from the pass road, the sealed scroll held out before him in both hands like a relic. A royal courier. His voice, when he spoke, had the flat rehearsal of a man delivering words he did not intend to be responsible for.

"By decree of Aldric, fourth of that name, King of the Nine Provinces and Warden of the Marches." He let the titles fall like coins on a table. "Be it known to all persons within the demesne called the Veiled Valley, and to their religious and civil officers, that the Crown, having determined that the practice of unlicensed gift constitutes a clear and present peril to the realm, does hereby command: that all persons possessing, suspected of possessing, or harboring any such gift present themselves for registration at their provincial seat within forty days of this proclamation."

He paused. The hall's silence had a texture now, dense as wool.

"That those who register shall be assessed, licensed, or bound as law provides. That those who do not register shall be accounted felons, and hunted according to the statutes of the Purification. That any community sheltering an unregistered gifted person shall be liable to search, seizure, and sanction. So says the King." He extended the scroll toward Mother Sable. "Witness and seal, Reverend Mother."

For a long moment, no one moved. Emrys became aware of her own heart, of the warmth in her fingers spreading up into her wrists, and she clasped her hands behind her back where no one could see them tremble.

Mother Sable descended the three steps of the dais with the unhurried care of a woman whose knees had opinions, took the scroll, and broke the seal with her thumb. Her eyes traveled the text. Emrys watched her face and saw—nothing. Perfectly, deliberately nothing, a stillness so complete it could only have been practiced. When the old woman looked up, her voice carried to the back of the hall without effort.

"The valley acknowledges the Crown's proclamation. It will be read aloud at evening prayer, and posted at the gates." A beat. "You've ridden hard, courier. There is bread and soup in the refectory before you return."

"I'm instructed to wait for acknowledgment in writing, Reverend Mother."

"Then you'll eat while your ink dries." She turned to the hall. "Sisters. Friends. Return to your work."

They dispersed the way water disperses around a stone—too smoothly, everyone finding somewhere else urgently to be. Emrys drifted with them, one calm face among many, and might have made it all the way to the corridor if Mother Sable's voice hadn't found her like a hook finding cloth.

"Scribe Kavanagh. A moment. My eyes are tired, and the fair copy of the acknowledgment wants writing."

There was no reason for alarm. None. Elder scribes were forever being pulled aside for such tasks; Emrys had written a dozen acknowledgments for a dozen routine dispatches. She followed the old woman up the spiral stair to the tower room with her hands folded and her breathing measured, past shelves of ledgers going back four hundred years, into the small round chamber at the top where the valley's oldest secrets were kept in locked boxes that Emrys had never once been tempted to open.

Mother Sable closed the door. Then she stood with her back against it for a moment, and Emrys watched—the way you watch a wall crack—as the practiced stillness fell off her face like a mask set down at the end of a long performance. What was underneath was old, and tired, and afraid.

"You'll want to sit," Mother Sable said.

"I don't understand, Reverend Mother. The proclamation doesn't concern the valley. No one here—"

"No one here," Mother Sable agreed, moving to her desk, "is gifted. That has been the valley's word for two hundred years, and it has kept us safe, and it is true in every way that matters." She drew out a chair with her foot. "Sit down, child."

Emrys sat. The warmth in her fingertips had gone very quiet, the way small animals go quiet when the hawk's shadow crosses the grass.

Mother Sable lowered herself opposite her and folded her hands on the desk, and for a moment just looked at Emrys—that long winter-lettuce gaze that seemed to count things in a person the way a shepherd counts sheep. When she spoke again, her voice was lower.

"There are things you should know about this valley, and I find I don't have the luxury anymore of waiting until you're older. You're nineteen. You deserve the truth while there's still time to use it." She took a breath. "The veil that keeps magic out of this valley—it isn't a natural thing. Borders of that kind don't make themselves. Someone made ours. Two centuries ago a woman named Ysolt Corrigan, who had gifts enough to drown a province, poured everything she had into sealing these hills. She bought our peace with her own power, and when it was spent, the Crown escorted her out through the western pass, and she was never seen again. The valley has forgotten her. The Crown has not."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the proclamation you heard downstairs—" Mother Sable's hands tightened on each other, knuckles like river pebbles under skin. "I've received letters from outside, child, through channels the Crown doesn't watch. This hunt is not like the others. It isn't taxes dressed as piety, or a king flexing at his barons. Something has changed at court. The registries aren't for counting the gifted. They're for *finding* them. And the searches that follow the registrations—" she stopped, and started again, quieter. "Forty days. That's what the law gives. But the riders won't wait for the law. There are already companies on the roads, and the pass road is the only road, and I am an old woman with sixty souls to protect and a lie two hundred years deep."

Emrys's mouth was dry. "A lie?"

"That nothing gifted lives here." Mother Sable's gaze did not waver, and did not soften, and Emrys understood with a lurch that they were no longer speaking in generalities at all. "That is the lie, Emrys. And it has only ever been as strong as the weakest secret inside it."

The warmth rose in Emrys's fingertips so fast and so hard that she had to press her palms flat against her thighs to hold it down. In the little round room, in the light falling amber through the tower's single window, the two women regarded each other across four hundred years of ledgers—one who had guarded a secret her whole life, and one who, Emrys suddenly understood with terrible clarity, had known about it all along.

"You knew," Emrys whispered. "All these years. You knew what I—"

"I knew a six-year-old who made a dying sparrow breathe again in the herb garden," Mother Sable said gently, "and then cried for an hour because she thought she'd broken the rules, and I decided that day that no one would ever make her pay for a kindness. Yes, child. I knew. I've known nineteen years." She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the locked boxes along the wall, selecting a small iron-bound chest Emrys had passed ten thousand times without seeing. She set it on the desk between them but did not open it. Her hand rested on the lid.

"And I'm telling you now, today, because yesterday the bell at the pass watch rang twice—which means riders, not couriers. Riders in gray and scarlet." The old woman's eyes were terribly calm. "The Crown's hunt-captains don't send riders ahead of a proclamation unless they mean to see it enforced. They'll be at our gates within days. Perhaps hours."

Outside, far below, the valley lay gold and green in the autumn light—the orchards, the cisterns, the terraced gardens where Emrys had spent her whole careful life. Everything perfectly ordered. Everything exactly as it had always been.

Except that the ink on the acknowledgment she hadn't yet written was already, somewhere in her imagination, refusing to dry.

"What do I do?" Emrys asked. Her voice came out very small.

Mother Sable's hand came down on the chest's lid, and the lock clicked open under her thumb.

"First," she said, "you listen. Because we haven't much time—and there is a story I should have told you years ago, about the last child I smuggled across the border, and what it cost her."

Far to the west, faint as a struck match, something on the pass road caught the light.
