## 2. The Valley Keeps Its Secrets

The bell for evening vespers had not yet rung when Emrys climbed the narrow stair to Mother Sable's cell, and the stone under her palms was cool in the way that only old things are cool—patient, indifferent, holding the memory of every hand that had gripped it before hers. She had counted the steps a thousand times in her life. Forty-two. On the forty-second, she always paused, because beyond that threshold lay the one room in the valley where Emrys Kavanagh had never once had to pretend.

Tonight, her knuckles hovered over the door longer than usual.

She could hear, faintly, the scratch of a quill within—the same sound that filled her own days—and beneath it the low murmur of the elder's voice, though no words came through. Mother Sable talked to herself the way other people prayed. Emrys had long suspected there was very little difference between the two.

She knocked.

"Enter, child, before you wear a groove in my door."

The cell was small and cluttered and smelled of lamp oil, dried feverfew, and ink gone slightly sour in its pots. Shelves crowded the walls from floor to ceiling, bowing under the weight of books whose spines had been mended so many times they looked like quilts stitched from leather scraps. A single window, tall and narrow as an arrow-slit, let in the last copper light of the day, and in that light Mother Sable sat hunched at her desk like a bird of prey at rest—a small woman, thin as a winter branch, with white hair cropped close to her skull and eyes the color of river stones: gray, flecked, impossible to read twice the same way.

She did not look up. "You've brought me the Ashford psalter. You've also brought something else. Sit down."

Emrys set the wrapped manuscript on the desk and lowered herself onto the stool by the window, her hands folding in her lap before she realized they were trembling. She pressed them flat against her skirts.

"I don't know how to say it," she said.

"You never do. Say it badly. I'm old; I've learned to translate."

Emrys laughed—a small, startled sound, half-strangled—and then the laugh collapsed into something else, and to her horror her eyes stung. She stared hard at the floorboards until the sting receded.

"The proclamation," she managed. "From the capital. It was read at the gate this morning. All gifted persons are to present themselves for registration." Her voice thinned. "Registration, or—" She couldn't finish it. The crier's voice still hung in her ears, flat and official, reciting the word *hunted* as easily as if he were naming the price of barley.

Mother Sable set down her quill with great care, aligning it parallel to the desk's edge, and only then lifted her gaze.

"And you thought," she said quietly, "that this concerned you."

"It concerns everyone."

"It concerns *you*, Emrys. Let us not be delicate about it, we haven't the time." The old woman's voice held no cruelty—it never did—but neither did it hold comfort, not yet. Comfort, Emrys knew, was something Mother Sable dispensed like a physician's dose: precisely measured, only after the wound had been cleaned. "How long has the warmth been back?"

Emrys's breath caught. "I never told you about—"

"You never had to. I've watched you copy warding texts for eleven years, and your fingers have glowed like banked coals on exactly four occasions that I know of, and you hid each one so poorly that a blind woman would have noticed." She rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and crossed to the shelf. "The first time was when you were nine. Brother Aldous dropped a vial of lye in the scriptorium and it splashed toward little Maren's face, and before anyone could blink, the splash stopped. Hung in the air. Curved away from her like water remembering which way is downhill. And you stood there with your hands out and your eyes enormous, and Brother Aldous said, 'Well. That's fortunate,' because Brother Aldous was either a fool or a saint, and possibly both."

"He never spoke of it again," Emrys whispered. "None of them did."

"No. Because this valley keeps its secrets. That's what it's *for*." Mother Sable pulled down a book Emrys had never seen before—plain covers, no title, its leather worn soft as cloth—and carried it back to the desk. "But secrets kept too long begin to rot their keepers. So. Tell me plainly. What happened today, besides the proclamation?"

Emrys's hands had gone cold. "Nothing happened. That's just it. Nothing ever happens. And now they're coming, and I thought—I thought the valley would protect me. Us. All of us. Isn't that what it does? Magic can't cross the border. It can't find me here. It's been dormant since I was a child, it barely even—"

"*Barely.*" Mother Sable snorted. "Child, last month you re-inked the entire eastern ledger in a single afternoon without smudging a line, and the ink dried before it touched the page. Do you imagine that's diligence? That's your gift, leaking through the seams of you like light through a shuttered lamp. It isn't dormant. It's *waiting*. There's a difference, and the difference matters more now than it ever has."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Emrys gripped the edge of the stool.

"Then the wards—"

"Ah." Mother Sable sat back down, and for the first time Emrys saw something in the old woman's face that frightened her more than the proclamation had: a weary, ancient guilt, the kind that has been carried so long it has worn a groove of its own. "Yes. The wards. You should hear this from me, and you should hear all of it, because you have earned the truth by being afraid of the right things."

She opened the plain book. Its pages were dense with handwriting in three different inks, three different centuries of a single story.

"Do you know why the Veiled Valley exists?" Mother Sable asked. "Not the temple's version. Not 'a sanctuary blessed by the Nine, where magic cannot follow.' The true version."

"The temple says the founders were blessed—"

"The temple says many convenient things." She turned the book around so Emrys could see. The page showed a map, faded almost to nothing, of the valley itself—but drawn differently than any map Emrys had copied. The border wasn't a line. It was a ring of names. Hundreds of them, written in a spiral, circling the valley like a wall made of words. "A warding is a bargain, Emrys. Always. Power does not build walls for free. Something must be given, and something must be bound. The founders didn't receive a blessing. They performed a binding—and the price of it was paid by a person."

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. "What person?"

"A gifted woman named Ysolde Marran. Stronger than any of the founders themselves—they say she could call rain from a clear sky and speak to the dying across miles of distance. When the witch-burnings began, three hundred years ago, the founders begged her to weave a sanctuary. She agreed. But a veil strong enough to hide every gifted soul who would ever shelter here needed an anchor. A living one. Someone whose own power could be woven into the border itself, forever."

"She gave herself," Emrys said slowly. "To the ward."

"She gave herself *into* it. Her body lies beneath the standing stone at the western gate—you've walked past it ten thousand times and called it the Old Marker. And her power holds the veil to this day. Nothing magical crosses that border uninvited. Nothing magical *wakes* inside it, either—not fully. Which is why your gift has slept so quietly all these years, child. It isn't mercy that's kept you safe. It's Ysolde Marran, holding the door shut from underneath the ground, for three hundred years, without thanks and without rest."

The lamp flame guttered, though there was no draft. Emrys found she could not look away from the spiral of names.

"But the founders," she said. "They wrote the temple histories. They founded the sanctuary. They lived here, honored—"

"They lived here *safe*, while she lay in the ground. Yes." Mother Sable closed the book gently, as one closes the eyes of the dead. "Peace is never free, Emrys. Someone always pays, and someone always forgets to ask who. This valley's peace was bought with her exile—from life, from name, from memory. We live inside her sacrifice and call it our blessing." She looked up, and her river-stone eyes were steady. "I tell you this now for two reasons. First, because you deserve to know what you're living inside. And second—because the veil is old. Three hundred years old, anchored to a will that ended three hundred years ago. It has frayed before. Twice, in my lifetime alone, I have patched it. And a crown that sends riders to hunt the gifted will not be deterred by a frayed veil for long."

Emrys sat very still. Somewhere below, the vesper bell began to ring, and the sound rolled up through the floor and into her bones.

"Mother Sable," she said at last. "Have you ever smuggled anyone across the border?"

The old woman went quiet. Outside, the bell finished its peal, and the silence afterward was enormous.

"Three children," she said finally. "In the famine years, before you were born. Gifted children whose families feared them—or worse, loved them too much to watch them burn. I carried them out through the shepherd's path at night, past the Old Marker, and handed them to a tinker who owed me his life." Her jaw tightened. "Two survived. I don't know what became of the third. I think about him every day, and I expect I shall continue to until I die."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I am deciding whether to do it a fourth time." Mother Sable folded her hands atop the plain book, and her voice softened into something Emrys had rarely heard from her: tenderness, unarmored. "Not tonight. Not yet. But soon, perhaps. If the riders come, and the veil fails, and your gift wakes beyond the border—" She shook her head. "It will be loud, Emrys. Whatever sleeps in you, it will wake *loud*, and things in the wide world will feel it waking. Hunters. Worse than hunters. You must understand that leaving this valley is not escaping danger. It is choosing which danger you can bear."

"I understand."

"Do you? Then tell me what you understood, in your own words, so I know you weren't merely agreeing."

Emrys almost smiled despite everything. Eleven years of copying texts had taught her that Mother Sable trusted nothing so little as easy agreement. She took a breath.

"If I stay, I gamble on a fraying veil and a crown that's run out of patience. If I go, I gamble on a world where my gift announces me like a struck bell." Her hands tightened in her lap. "And either way, I stop pretending I'm only a scribe."

"There's hope for you yet." Mother Sable reached across the desk and covered Emrys's hands with her own—dry, warm, strong as roots. "Sleep tonight. Copy nothing. Tomorrow we'll—"

The knock came hard and sudden, three blows that rattled the door in its frame.

"Mother Sable!" Brother Tam's voice, breathless, cracked with alarm. "Mother Sable, come quickly—riders at the gate! Crown riders! A dozen of them, with a warrant sealed in red wax, and they're *demanding entry*!"

For a moment, neither woman moved. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if listening.

Then Mother Sable rose, and Emrys saw her do something she had never seen in eleven years: the old woman squared her shoulders like a soldier, and the guilt drained out of her face, replaced by something harder and older than fear.

"Well," Mother Sable said, taking up her staff. "It seems the deciding has been done for us."

She opened the door. Beyond her, in the corridor, Emrys could hear the whole temple stirring—doors, footsteps, voices rising like startled birds—and far below, faint but unmistakable, the iron clang of the valley's gate bell ringing for the first time in her lifetime.

* * *

They descended together, Mother Sable's staff striking the stones in a rhythm too steady for the pace, and Emrys felt the temple rearranging itself around her—the scribes pouring from the scriptorium, the novices pressed to the walls, old Brother Aldous standing in the courtyard with his hands folded and his face gone pale as parchment. Above the eastern wall, the sky had gone the color of a bruise, and against it the watchtower torches burned small and stubborn.

The gate stood at the end of the processional road, two leaves of black oak banded with iron, older than the temple itself. Emrys had passed through it a thousand times on market days, on herb-gathering walks, on the annual walk to the Old Marker. She had never once seen it closed.

It was closed now. And beyond it, in the failing light, she could see them: riders in the crown's dark gray, motionless as carved figures, their horses steaming in the cold. At their center, a man in a half-helm held up a scroll, and even at this distance the seal at its foot caught the torchlight—red wax, stamped deep, glowing like a wound.

"Open in the name of the Crown!" the herald called, and his voice carried flat and clear across the courtyard, bouncing off stone. "By warrant of the Grand Huntress, this sanctuary is required to surrender for inspection all persons bearing unregistered gifts! Compliance preserves your charter! Refusal voids it!"

Voided charter. Emrys heard the words land among the villagers like stones dropped into still water. She saw the miller's wife clutch her son's shoulder. She saw the ferryman turn his face away. She saw, everywhere, the same calculation beginning behind every eye: *who among us, who, who.*

Beside her, Mother Sable's grip found her arm—not gentle now, but firm as a shackle, guiding her back from the front of the crowd, into the shadow of the archway.

"Not one step closer," the old woman murmured, barely a breath. "Whatever happens at that gate tonight, you are a scribe, nothing more, and you stand where I can see you. Do you hear me, child?"

"Yes, Mother."

"Good." A pause. The herald called again, and the riders did not move, and the torches spat, and somewhere a horse snorted and stamped. Mother Sable's eyes stayed fixed on the gate, but her voice, when it came, was for Emrys alone, quiet and terrible and calm:

"Now we learn whether three hundred years of peace was ever real—or only borrowed. And either way, girl, I fear the bill has come due."

Beyond the wall, in the deepening dark, the lead rider raised one gauntleted hand—and the red seal on the warrant began, softly, to glow.
