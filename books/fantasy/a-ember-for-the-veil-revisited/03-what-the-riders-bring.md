## 3. What the Riders Bring

The valley did not want them.

Ilsa Vane felt it the moment her company passed beneath the first of the boundary stones—a pressure like a hand laid flat against her chest, gentle but immovable, the valley itself saying *no further* in a voice older than any crown. Her horse shied and blew foam. Behind her, six riders muttered charms they only half believed in, because witch-hunters were required to believe in very little except warrants and rope.

She had known this place as a girl. Everyone in the northern provinces had known it, the way you knew the names of saints or the shapes of clouds: the Veiled Valley, sanctuary of the sanctuary-less, where magic went to die at the border and never crossed. She had stood on the rim of it once, nineteen years old and newly sworn, and looked down at the green quilt of fields and thought: *there is the one place in the world we are not needed.*

Now she rode into it with a writ sealed in red wax and forty men who called themselves hunters because the alternative word was too honest.

"Captain," said Dree, riding at her left, a young man with a face like a closed ledger. "The wards. You feel them?"

"I feel them."

"They'll hold?"

"That's rather the point of them." She did not look at him. The writ in her saddlebag weighed more than iron had any right to weigh. *By order of His Majesty, all persons possessed of unlawful gift shall present themselves for registration. Those harboring the gifted shall be counted as gifted themselves.* She had read it eleven times on the road south, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something she could live under. They had not.

The boundary stone passed on their right, mossy and indifferent, and the pressure released—not all at once but like a held breath let out slowly, and Ilsa understood with cold clarity that the ward had examined her and found her wanting. Found her *empty*, which was what the temple folk would call unblessed and what her own trade called clean.

If only emptiness were the same as innocence.

* * *

The village met them in silence.

Not hostility—Ilsa had ridden into hostile towns and knew their texture, the slammed shutters, the dogs turned loose, the old women spitting where the horses would step. This was something else. The people of the valley came out into the lane and simply *watched*, farmers with dirt still on their hands, a baker with flour ghosting her forearms, children peering around their mothers' skirts with open, unafraid curiosity. As if the riders were weather. As if they had seen crowns come and go and expected to see this one go too.

It unnerved her company more than thrown stones would have.

"The temple," Ilsa said to the eldest of the farmers, an old man with a spine bent like a question mark. "We've business there."

"There's always business at the temple," the old man said mildly. "Mostly it's about forgiveness." But he gestured up the hill, and Ilsa saw it: a long, low building of grey stone roofed in slate the color of rain, its bell tower crowned with a weathervane shaped like an open book. Smoke rose from its kitchens. Bees worked the herb garden along its south wall. It looked like nothing so much as a grandmother's house grown enormous, and Ilsa hated her commission with a purity that surprised her.

They climbed the hill. The temple doors stood open—of course they did; the valley had no locks, she remembered that much—and inside, the scriptorium stretched away in shafts of dusty gold light, rows of desks where scribes bent over their work with the hushed diligence of monks. The smell hit her first: ink and beeswax and old vellum, and beneath it something green, like crushed leaves, like the memory of summer.

Forty years of her life she had spent learning to read rooms. This room read like a psalm.

"Welcome, riders."

An elderly woman came toward them between the desks—broad-shouldered, white-haired, wearing the plain grey of the temple over boots that had clearly done fieldwork. Mother Sable, the village headman had told them on the road, though he'd said it the way other men named mountains: a fact of the landscape, older than any of them, unlikely to move.

"You're expected," Mother Sable said. "Though I confess we hoped expectation was all you'd bring."

Ilsa dismounted and produced the writ. "By order of King Aldric, second of his name—" The words tasted like coins. "All persons possessed of unlawful gift are to be registered. My commission extends to a search of the temple and surrounding holdings."

"And how does one search for a gift, Captain?" Mother Sable asked. Not challenging—genuinely curious, the way a scholar is curious, and that was worse. "Does it glitter? Does it leave tracks?"

"It leaves *people*," Ilsa said. "People who've been strange their whole lives. People neighbors remember. We search records, ask questions, watch hands." She heard herself and stopped, disgusted. *Watch hands.* As if the gift lived in the fingers, as if she hadn't seen what the gift actually lived in—the spine, the breath, the terrible wanting heart.

Mother Sable studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw there made her nod slowly. "You may have your day, Captain. The scriptorium will keep at its work—we do not stop copying the warding texts for anyone, not even kings, because the texts are why your writ means nothing here. But you may walk among us and ask your questions."

"One day," Ilsa said. "Then my men begin in earnest."

"One day," Mother Sable agreed, and Ilsa had the sudden, unshakeable sense that the old woman was granting her a mercy and both of them knew it.

* * *

She began, because beginning was all duty ever offered her, in the records room: a low-ceilinged archive smelling of tallow and dust, shelves of baptismal rolls and tithe ledgers going back two centuries. Her men fanned through the village with their question lists—*who keeps to themselves, who was born in a storm, whose crops come in too generous*—and Ilsa sat with the ledgers and let her finger travel down columns of names while her conscience sat across the table like a prisoner awaiting sentence.

The valley's records were immaculate. That itself was suspicious; honesty this thorough was either innocence or very good acting. Births, deaths, apprenticeships, harvests—all in the same careful hand until the hand changed, generation after generation, each scribe training the next. She found herself tracing the newest hand, the most recent column of entries, ink still sharp-edged in places. A beautiful script. Disciplined, but with flourishes the writer probably didn't notice, small upward reaches at the ends of certain letters like grass leaning toward light.

The name signed at the bottom of the latest page: *E. Kavanagh.*

She should have stopped there. She was paid—well paid, in coin she never quite managed to spend—to stop exactly there and mark the name for questioning. Instead she kept reading, and reading became a kind of trespass she could not explain: twenty years of a stranger's diligent life recorded in a stranger's beautiful hand. Emrys Kavanagh, entered as apprentice at eight years. Orphaned—the record noted it without comment, mother deceased, father unknown. Promoted to full scribe at fifteen. Nothing strange. Nothing marked. Just a life laid down in ink like a path through safe country.

And yet.

When she finally emerged into the late afternoon, she told herself she wanted water and air. What she wanted, she realized too late, was to put a face to the handwriting.

She found the youngest scribe in the temple garden, kneeling between rows of feverfew with a basket of cut stems, and the world did the thing the world occasionally did to her—the thing that had made her a hunt-captain by thirty-five, the uncanny instrument she had never learned to talk about. Something *rang*. Faintly, at the very edge of perception, the way you can hear a harp string through a wall when someone in the next room touches it, hardly plucking at all.

The girl looked up. Ordinary face, ordinary brown hair coming loose from its braid, ink stains on her fingers even in the garden—ink soaked deep in the whorls, permanent as sin. And her eyes, meeting Ilsa's, went wide and then carefully, deliberately, carefully blank, in the way of someone closing a door before you'd seen what room it opened onto.

"You're the captain," the girl said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, gathering feverfew, were not.

"Captain Vane." Ilsa stayed at the gate; entering the garden felt like it would mean something, and she was suddenly superstitious about meanings. "You're the temple's recording scribe."

"Emrys Kavanagh. Yes." The basket trembled, just barely. Feverfew nodded against its rim. "Have I—have I written something wrong, Captain? Is that why you've come to the valley?" A joke, badly made, offered the way drowning people offer things to the surface.

"No one has written anything wrong." *Yet.* "I'm reviewing the rolls. Routine." Both lies, and the girl knew at least one of them; Ilsa watched the knowledge land behind those careful eyes and settle there like sediment.

They stood a moment in the green hush, bees working, the bell tower ticking its shadow across the wall, and Ilsa understood that this was the moment the writ existed for—that somewhere behind her ribs, the machinery of her office was grinding out its verdict: *mark her, question her, take her name south.* Emrys Kavanagh knelt among her herbs with her door-closed eyes and her ink-stained fingers and waited to hear which world she lived in.

"Your hand is remarkable," Ilsa said. "The warding texts. I saw them in the roll."

"Thank you." Bewildered. Off-balance. "We—they must be exact. Mother Sable says the letters are the walls."

"The letters are the walls," Ilsa repeated softly. Yes. That was precisely what her whole profession feared: that words were walls, that names were handles, that a gift could be copied out letter by letter and locked behind exactness. She turned to go. Then, because conscience is a coward that only speaks when consequences have already been arranged elsewhere, she heard herself say:

"The search resumes tomorrow morning. Today my men will review records and speak with elders only. If anyone in the valley has business they've been meaning to attend to—" she met the girl's eyes, and let the mask drop for one bare second, let her see the thing underneath the uniform, whatever the girl needed to see— "today would be the day for it."

Emrys Kavanagh went very still. Then, so quietly it was almost lost under the bees: "Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me." Ilsa walked back through the gate, through the garden's warmth, out into the cooling evening, and hated every step of it. *A day.* She had bought a stranger a single day with her own honor, and the arithmetic was obscene: tomorrow the machinery resumed, and whatever the girl did with her borrowed hours would either save her or hang them both.

* * *

That night the company camped on the village green, forbidden by temple custom from lodging within walls, and Ilsa sat apart at her fire and took out the writ and read it a twelfth time.

Somewhere north, in a capital she had visited twice and loved never, a king she had never met sat behind a crown that had ordered all this. She tried to picture him—an old man, probably, gouty and remote, signing papers fed to him by chancellors—and failed. There were rumors about the king. There were always rumors about kings. But lately the rumors had teeth: councilors dismissed overnight, old friends' names struck from guest lists, hunts commissioned with a fervor no chancellor would dare originate. *Flush them out,* the writ's preamble ran, in language unusually poetic for law. *Bring the gifted into the light, that they may serve.*

Serve whom? she had asked in the capital, and been told to mind her rank.

Dree approached her fire with two cups of the temple's rough cider, offered one, and sat when she didn't refuse. For a while neither spoke. Across the green, the valley lay dark except for one lit window high in the temple—the scriptorium, someone working late over their exact letters, their walls.

"You delayed the search," Dree said at last. Not accusing. Worse—neutral, the voice of a man laying facts on a table.

"I scheduled it."

"Captain." He looked into his cup. "In the Fenlands, three years ago, we took a woman whose gift was finding lost sheep. Sheep. We took her in front of her children." He turned the cup in his hands. "I've wondered since what we were hunting. Tonight I watched a valley full of people who don't lock their doors look at us like we were bad weather. And I find I don't know anymore which side of the veil the sickness is on."

Ilsa drank her cider and said nothing, because anything she said would be treason or a lie, and she had spent her whole career rationing both.

But later, banked fire, sleeping camp, she lay awake and listened to the valley breathe around her—the bees asleep in their hives, the temple bell silent, the one window still burning gold against the dark. Someone down there was awake, and afraid, and running out of borrowed hours. And far to the north, something wore a crown and grew hungrier, and had sent *her*—her conscience, her warrant, her tired hands—as its fingertips in the dark.

She slept at last near dawn and dreamed of a garden she had never seen, and woke with the certainty, arriving like weather, that she had just made the largest mistake or the only right choice of her life, and that within the week she would know which.

Across the green, the scriptorium window went dark.
