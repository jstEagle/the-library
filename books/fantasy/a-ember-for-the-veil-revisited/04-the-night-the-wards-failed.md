## 4. The Night the Wards Failed

The bell rang wrong.

Emrys had copied out the valley's alarm codes three times in her novitiate year—*one toll for weather, two for travelers, three for sickness*—and the sound that rolled down from the temple tower that evening matched none of them. It was a single stroke, then silence, then a single stroke again, as though the ringer's arm kept failing halfway through its arc. Or as though whoever pulled the rope could not decide what the danger was called.

She set down her pen. The ink on the page before her—the warding text she had been copying for Mother Sable these six weeks past, the one whose letters never seemed to dry no matter how fine the sand she dusted across them—bled a little at the margin, a dark bloom spreading like a bruise into the paper's grain. She had learned not to stare at that. Some of the oldest texts did this, the brothers said; ink remembers being important, and importance weeps.

The scriptorium was nearly empty at the evening hour. Only Brother Callum remained at the far table, bent over his ledger of tithes, and he did not look up when she rose. Her fingertips were warm. They were always warm lately, ever since the proclamation, ever since she'd read those words in a crown courier's voice—*all gifted persons to be registered or hunted*—and felt something under her breastbone turn over in its sleep and mutter.

*Not here*, she told them, flexing her fingers inside her sleeves. *Not in the valley. Never in the valley.*

The valley did not permit it. That was the whole of the covenant, older than the temple, older than the village: within the Veiled borders magic could not cross, could not wake, could not so much as whisper. Gifted children born here grew up ordinary or left here ordinary. Emrys had spent nineteen years telling herself that the warmth in her hands was only warmth—a quirk of blood, a trick of a body that ran hot—that it would be nothing, anywhere, because nowhere was it less than nothing than here.

But the bell rang wrong, and her fingertips burned like she'd held them over a candle, and outside the scriptorium windows the light was failing in a way she had never seen it fail, gold going thin and gray-green all at once, as though the sunset itself were being strained through cloth.

She went out into the close. The whole village was gathering in the square below the temple steps, and their faces were all tilted upward, toward the northern ridge where the veil-stones stood.

"Emrys." Mother Sable's hand closed on her shoulder from behind, and the old woman's grip was stronger than her years, harder. "Come away from the open. Come with me."

"What is it? What's happening to the light?"

Mother Sable did not answer. She was looking at the ridge, and her face—in thirty years Emrys had never once seen Mother Sable look frightened, not at funerals, not at floods—her face was the face of someone watching a door they had nailed shut begin, slowly, to open.

"The wards," Mother Sable said quietly. "Someone is unmaking the wards."

* * *

They gathered in Mother Sable's cell behind the reliquary, a handful of elders and a handful of others whom Emrys had never thought to question the company of—the miller's wife, old Tamsin, who walked with a stick; the quiet ferryman, Dell; Brother Callum, who had left his ledger open on his desk for the first time in forty years. A room full of ordinary people, Emrys would have said yesterday. Tonight she looked at them and understood, with a lurch, why they were all in this room.

"How long?" Dell asked. His voice was flat, a man conserving everything but words.

"Since sundown," Mother Sable said. "It's a relic of some kind. Crown work. The veil isn't broken—it's being *thinned*, drawn up like netting from a pond, and I haven't the strength to push it back down alone." She looked around the circle, and her eyes rested last on Emrys, and something passed over her expression that Emrys could not read—grief, maybe, or apology. "We have until morning. Perhaps less."

"Then we do what we have always done," Tamsin said. "We are ordinary. We are farmers and scribes and ferrymen. We let them search. There's nothing here to find."

"There is *me*," Emrys said, and her own voice sounded strange to her, too loud in the small stone room. Every face turned to her. "If that's why they've come. If they can pierce the veil now, then—" She stopped. Swallowed. Nineteen years of suppression sat in her throat like a swallowed stone. "Then there is something here to find. In me. Isn't there? Even here, even inside the valley—it's not gone. It's only sleeping."

No one contradicted her. That silence was worse than any argument.

Brother Callum cleared his throat. "The covenant was never that the valley cured us, child. Only that it kept us quiet. Kept us *safe*." He said the second word carefully, as if testing whether it still bore weight.

"Then I should go to them," Emrys said. "Before anyone else pays for me. Before—"

"You will do nothing of the kind." Mother Sable's voice cracked across the room like a whip, and then softened just as fast, and she took Emrys's burning hands in her cool dry ones. "Listen to me, Emrys Kavanagh, and listen well. When the veil thins fully—and it will, before dawn—your gift will rise in you like water finding its level. You will not be able to stop it. Do you understand? All these years you have been holding a door shut against a tide, and tonight someone is taking the door off its hinges. Whatever happens tonight, whatever rises in you—you must not fight it in the open. You hear me? Get to the undercroft. Get to stone and earth. And *hold*."

"Hold what?"

Mother Sable's thumbs pressed into her palms, hard, an anchor's pressure. "Yourself. Whatever else the night asks of you—hold yourself."

Outside, far off on the ridge, came a sound none of them had heard before and all of them understood at once: a long, tearing note, like a harp string breaking somewhere beneath the world. The lamps guttered. The ink in the pot on Mother Sable's desk rose a quarter-inch, trembling, and settled.

The veil was failing.

* * *

It failed at moonrise.

Emrys felt it happen from the undercroft, where she had gone at Mother Sable's command, kneeling among sacks of grain and dusty casks with her arms wrapped around herself. It came as a shudder through the floor, through the bones of the mountain itself, a great slow sigh of release—as if some vast creature had been holding its breath for three hundred years and finally, gratefully, exhaled.

And the tide came in.

She had no other word for it, then or after. Warmth poured up through her hands, her wrists, her arms—not the candle-flame flicker she had suppressed all her life but a flood, a river in spate, and every sense she owned opened like eyes snapping wide in the dark. She could feel the temple above her, every joint and beam of it, could feel the villagers in their houses like embers banked in gray wool, could feel the *threads*—the world beyond the veil was threaded, strung, humming like a plucked instrument, and the sound of it was so beautiful she began, without deciding to, to cry.

That was when the screaming started.

It came muffled through stone—voices first, then a bell, then a crack of timber that she felt more than heard, a groan of wood giving way somewhere above and to the east. Toward the market square. Toward the inn, where half the village had gathered to argue about what to do, where families stood with children on their shoulders in the lantern-light—

Emrys was moving before she knew she had decided anything. Up the undercroft stair, through the vestry, out into the night—and the night hit her like a wave.

Everything was *loud*. The stars sang. The stones of the temple path murmured under her feet in voices a thousand years dead. And ahead, around the corner of the tithe barn, the market square blazed with panic and with light that wasn't firelight—cold white radiance spilling from something in the hunters' hands, from the relic, and the veil hung overhead like torn gauze, and through the tear she could see them: riders in crown black, perhaps twenty, torches and crossbows, spreading through her village like ink through water.

And the inn's gallery was coming down.

Later she could never say whether the relic had done it or the riders' crowbars or simply the night's terrible luck—but the great beam that carried the inn's upper walkway groaned, split, and swung down in a long black arc toward the crowd below, toward a small figure standing frozen in its shadow. Tam Broadfoot's girl. Wren. Six years old, a gap in her smile, forever leaving wildflowers on the temple altar because she liked the way they made the saints smell nice.

Wren looked up at the falling beam. She did not even scream. There was no time.

There was time for Emrys.

The gift did not ask her permission. It rose out of her like light through a shattered lamp—she flung out both hands and *knew*, in one instant of perfect terrible clarity, every thread in the beam's grain, every fiber, every knot, and she *pulled*, and the air between her palms and the timber turned silver and thick as honey. The beam stopped.

It hung there. Four hundred pounds of ancient oak suspended a hand's breadth above a child's head, groaning, creaking, caught in a web of silver light that streamed from Emrys's outstretched fingers and lit the whole square like a second moon.

The screaming stopped. Everyone was looking at her.

She felt it happen in her body before her mind caught up—the moment of exposure as a physical thing, cold flooding in where the warmth had been, every secret she had ever kept ripped away in a single breath. Twenty riders. Two hundred villagers. Mother Sable's face, stricken, at the edge of the crowd. Wren staring up at the impossible light. And Emrys standing in the center of the square with her gift blazing visibly from her hands for the first time in her life, undeniable, unmistakable, *named*.

"*Gifted*," someone whispered, and the word traveled the crowd like contagion. "Gifted. Gifted. Look at her hands—"

A rider raised a crossbow.

"No need."

The voice came from the inn steps, and Emrys knew it before she found its owner—the hunt-captain, Vane, the tall woman with the gray-streaked braid who had walked the temple cloister yesterday with her disgust worn openly as a badge. Captain Ilsa Vane descended the steps slowly, hand raised, and the crossbow lowered.

She looked at Emrys. Emrys looked back, shaking, the silver web beginning to fray at its edges as her terror drained the strength from her arms. The beam trembled. A rider darted forward and dragged Wren clear, and only then—with the child safe, with witnesses everywhere and her orders ringing in the air like the wrong-sounding bell—did Captain Vane make her choice.

She turned away. Deliberately. Fully. She addressed her lieutenant about the disposition of the south lane, her back to the scribe, her eyes on anything but the glowing girl in the square, and for the space of ten heartbeats—Ilsa Vane, crown hunter, oath-bound, watched nothing at all.

In those ten heartbeats the web failed. The beam crashed down into empty cobblestones. The silver light winked out, and Emrys folded to her knees in the sudden dark like a puppet with cut strings, and when the captain turned back around there was nothing to see but a fainting girl and a broken beam and a village full of people who would each swear afterward to slightly different stories.

Almost nothing to see.

* * *

They did not take her that night. That was the mercy, and Emrys spent the long hours of it in the temple cells trying to understand its size.

Captain Vane declared the square evidence of "structural failure due to relic-induced resonance"—Emrys heard the words repeated, incredulous, through the wall of the vestry where she sat wrapped in Mother Sable's cloak—and ordered the village confined to quarters pending a full inquiry at dawn. An inquiry. As if what had happened needed study. As if everyone had not seen exactly what they had seen.

"They'll take me in the morning," Emrys said. Her hands lay in her lap like someone else's hands, still warm, still humming faintly, traitorous things. "You know that. She only delayed."

Mother Sable sat across from her, very straight, hands folded. Old Tamsin had gone; Dell had gone; only the two of them and one guttering lamp. "Ilsa Vane looked away," Mother Sable said. "I have smuggled gifted children past worse eyes than hers, child, and I will tell you what thirty years taught me: a hunter does not look away by accident. Not once. Not precisely at the moment that mattered." She leaned forward. "Which means that either the captain's conscience has teeth, or the captain has her own reasons for wanting a gifted scribe alive and unregistered. Either way, it is a door. And we are going through it tonight."

"Tonight," Emrys repeated dumbly.

"The dawn inquiry will have a new man running it—Vane's superiors are sending a verifier from the capital, she said so to her lieutenant while your village eavesdropped shamelessly through every shutter. A verifier reads gifts out of your skin like a cook reading a goose. You will not survive being verified." Mother Sable rose, stiff-backed, and took the lamp. "So it cannot be dawn. It must be now, in the dark, while the captain's men are confined and the captain is—conveniently—inspecting the north wall alone."

Emrys stared at her. "You've done this before."

"I told you the valley bought its peace with someone else's exile." Something moved behind the old woman's eyes, old grief wearing its familiar groove. "I never told you how many times." She held out her hand. "Up, Emrys. There's a travel pass in my desk that has waited nineteen years for a name, and I find I already know whose name I wrote on it. Come. The border is a mile north, and your gift is singing so loudly in my old ears that I fear the whole world can hear it."

Far off beyond the temple walls, at the torn edge of the veil, something answered—a low harmonic hum, patient and vast, like a listener leaning closer.

Emrys took Mother Sable's hand, and stood.
