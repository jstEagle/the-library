## 5. Flight Across the Border

The travel pass smelled of tallow and another woman's name.

Emrys turned it over in her hands in the dark of Mother Sable's cell beneath the temple, where the elder kept the things that could not be burned and the things that should have been. The parchment was soft at the edges, worn by a pocket not her own. *Maren Cotter, wool-merchant's daughter, licensed to travel to Thistlemere and return.* A stranger's face had been inked over with careful strokes, and beneath the forgery, in fresh sepia, Emrys's own description: *brown hair, ink-stained fingers, twenty years, quiet manner.*

"You stole this," Emrys said.

"I borrowed it from a woman who no longer needs it." Mother Sable's voice was flat as slate. She had not slept—Emrys could see it in the gray beneath her eyes, in the way her hands, usually so steady over the incense coals, kept closing and opening as though testing their own weight. "Maren Cotter died in the fever winter, six years ago. The pass was never returned to the registry. The registry, thank the quiet gods, is a shambles. It has been a shambles for a very long time, because I made certain of it."

"You've been forging papers." Emrys heard the words leave her mouth and still could not make them fit the woman before her—the woman who had taught her the proper slant of a capital *T*, who smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, who had never once raised her voice in thirty years of temple service. "For how long?"

"Longer than you've been alive." Mother Sable lowered herself onto the cell's single stool with a sound like an old door settling. The candle between them guttered and steadied. "Child, you have lived your whole life inside a lie so large you mistook it for the sky. Sit down. There is little time, and what I have to tell you will need all your years to understand, and I can only give you the length of this candle."

Emrys sat on the cold flagstones. She did not trust her legs.

* * *

"The valley was not always a sanctuary," Mother Sable began. "It was a prison. Do you know what the Veil actually is?"

"A blessing. The founders' covenant. Magic cannot cross the borders, so the gifted who come here are safe from—" Emrys stopped. She had recited the catechism a thousand times in a thousand careful hands. She heard it now the way one hears a bell after years of silence: too late, and wrong.

"Safe," Mother Sable repeated, without inflection. "Or contained. The wards that keep the hunters' relics out also keep everything else in. Including you, Emrys. Including the warmth in your fingers that you have spent twenty years pretending not to feel." Emrys flinched, and the elder raised a hand—not unkind, but immovable. "Did you truly think I did not know? I have watched you since you were brought to the temple steps with your mother's blood on your cloak. I have watched you flinch from candle flames. I have watched you copy warding texts with hands that ached, and I have said nothing, because inside the Veil your gift sleeps, and a sleeping thing is a safe thing. That was the bargain this valley made. Not with the gods. With the crown. Two hundred years ago."

"The founders—"

"Were exiles," Mother Sable said. "Gifted women and men who fled the first purge, who found this valley and its strange dead air, and who made a deal with the crown of their day: we will gather here, we will be visible, we will be *counted*—and in exchange, the crown leaves us alone, because nothing that wakes inside the Veil can ever leave it. The valley's peace was bought with a promise. Every gifted person who crossed that border and stayed agreed to be a bird in a gilded cage, so that the crown would never again have to wonder what the birds were doing." Her mouth twisted. "I broke that bargain forty years ago, child. There was a winter, early in my service, when the hunters came the way they came last week. I smuggled three children out through the old quarry road before the wards were resealed. Two survived. One was your Bree Ashdown's mother, if the letters I still receive are true. The third—" she paused, and the candle flame bent as though bowing its head— "the third I think of every day."

Emrys's throat had gone tight. "And my mother? Was she—"

"Your mother knew what you were. She crossed the border knowing the Veil would hold you quiet, knowing that inside these borders you would be safe and *small*, and she chose it anyway. She chose your safety over your wholeness. Whether that was love or fear or both, I cannot tell you. She did not leave a letter. She left you." Mother Sable leaned forward, and the candlelight carved deep lines into her face, and Emrys saw suddenly how old she was, how many secrets she had carried like stones in her pockets for decades. "Now hear the rest, because this is the part that will save your life or end it. The Veil does not only hold your gift in, Emrys. It holds it *asleep*. The moment you cross that border, everything you have smothered since childhood will wake at once. Not gradually. Not gently. It will come up out of you like a flood through a broken dam, and you will not be able to stop it, and worse—" the elder's voice dropped to almost nothing, "—worse, you will be *heard*. The land beyond the Veil is not dead air. It is full of power, full of old things and hungry things, and they listen the way spiders listen to a web. When you wake, they will feel you waking. Some of them will be curious. Some of them will be kind. And some of them will come to see what is shining in the dark."

"Then how am I supposed to survive?"

"You aren't, alone." Mother Sable rose, joints cracking, and went to the cell's far wall where a loose stone hid a niche Emrys had never known existed. From it she drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: a knife with a bone handle; a small tin of salt; a folded letter sealed with plain wax; and a dried sprig of something silver-leaved that Emrys did not recognize. "The letter is for a woman named Bree Ashdown, if you can find her—she keeps a cottage outside Fenmoss, three days east, and she owes me a debt she will hate repaying. The knife is iron-cold and salt-blessed; keep it against your skin. The sprig is ash-thistle. Chew it if your gift rises too fast and you feel yourself drowning in it. It will not stop the flood, but it will slow it long enough for you to breathe." She pressed the bundle into Emrys's hands and held them closed around it with both of her own, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "And the pass. Memorize the name. Maren Cotter. You are a wool-merchant's daughter going to visit an aunt in Thistlemere. You are bored. You are unremarkable. You are *not* a scribe with ink on her fingers—wash before you cross, and wear the gloves I've packed, and for the love of the quiet gods, Emrys, do not heal anyone. Do not touch anyone's pain. The first thing your gift will want to do is the thing it has always wanted to do. Starve it until you reach Bree."

"And if I can't reach Bree?"

"Then you run east until you find someone who looks at you the way I looked at your mother," Mother Sable said, "and you pray."

The candle had burned to a thumb's width. Emrys sat in the dark of the elder's cell with a stranger's name in her pocket and her whole life in her arms, and found that the only words she had were the smallest ones.

"I'm frightened."

"Good," said Mother Sable. "Fear is the only honest prayer left to us." She cupped Emrys's face in her hands, and her palms were dry and warm and smelled of lavender, and for a moment Emrys was eight years old again, being corrected gently on the slant of a letter. "You were the best of my students. Not the most talented—though you are that too. The best. You copy a text the way it deserves to be copied, with care for what it *means*. Go and mean something, child. Go and be the ink that will not dry."

* * *

She left at the hour before dawn, when the valley is at its most honest—when the mist lies in the fields like spilled milk and the temple bell is still, and even the watchful windows of the village are dark.

Emrys wore the gray traveling cloak Mother Sable had packed, the gloves, the bundle against her spine. She had washed the ink from her fingers in the cold spring until her nails were pale and clean, and she had looked at her own face in the water and not recognized it: a wool-merchant's daughter, bored, unremarkable, going to visit an aunt. Beneath the borrowed name, her heart beat so hard she could feel it in her teeth.

She did not go through the village. Mother Sable had drawn her a map to the old quarry road, the smugglers' path, a goat-track that wound up through the birch woods north of the temple grounds and over the shoulder of the hill where the wards were oldest and, the elder had said, thinnest. Emrys climbed it in the dark, breath smoking, boots slipping on frost-slick stone, and did not let herself look back until the trees opened at the top of the ridge.

Then she looked.

The valley lay below her like a cupped hand. Mist in the fields, the dark shapes of the temple roofs, the village huddled at its edge with its chimneys cold and its people—she hoped—asleep. Somewhere down there was Mother Sable, who would light the morning's first candle and pretend, for the hunters' benefit, that nothing had ever left. Somewhere down there was the scriptorium, her desk, the half-finished copy of the *Litanies of Warding* that someone else would have to finish. Somewhere down there was her mother's grave in the temple garden, a flat stone with a name on it, and Emrys pressed a gloved hand to her mouth and stood in the freezing wind and grieved in advance—not for what she had lost, but for what she was about to lose, which was the whole shape of her life, the small warm room of it, the known walls.

*Goodbye,* she thought at the valley, at the grave, at everything. *I'm sorry. I had to.*

The border was invisible. That was the terrible, beautiful thing about it: a line drawn through the world that no eye could see, marked only by the oldest ward-stones, squat and moss-eaten, standing in a ragged arc along the ridge like teeth. Mother Sable had told her which stone to pass—*the one split by lightning, walk through the split*—and Emrys found it in the graying light, a granite tooth cloven neatly down its center, and she stood before the gap with her heart hammering and her whole body remembering, all at once, twenty years of flinching from candle flames.

*Outside the valley, your gift will wake fully. And things will feel you waking.*

She stepped through.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The wind, the frost, the gray east turning the color of old pearl. She almost laughed—almost let herself believe it would be gentle, that the elder had exaggerated, that she could simply walk into the wide world and be a wool-merchant's daughter after all—

And then the Veil let go of her, and Emrys Kavanagh woke.

It was not a flood. It was a *sunrise*. It came up through her from the soles of her feet, from the ground itself, as if the whole world had been waiting on the other side of the border with its hands cupped around a flame, and the moment she crossed, it leaned in and lit her. Warmth poured down her arms into her fingers—her poor smothered fingers—and the numbness she had carried since childhood, the faint perpetual ache she had never once questioned, *broke*, and sensation flooded in so fierce and sweet that she cried out loud. She could feel everything. The sap moving in the birches, slow and silver. The frost dying on the grass blade by blade as the light touched it, each small death a tiny bright note. The heartbeat of a rabbit somewhere under the gorse, quick and terrified and *audible*, as if the animal were pressed against her ear. The land was not dead air. The land was a choir, and every stone and root and running thread of water was singing, and Emrys stood on the ridge with her arms half-raised and tears freezing on her cheeks, laughing and weeping at once, because she had spent her whole life in a silent room and had not known there was music.

*So this is what I am,* she thought, dizzy with it. *This is what I was, all along.*

She saw them, then—saw the threads. Faint as heat-shimmer, everywhere: pale lines of power running through the earth like veins through marble, converging, branching, pooling in hollows where old trees stood. One ran straight beneath her feet, humming, and where it passed through the split ward-stone it *frayed*, and she understood with a scholar's sudden clarity that she was looking at the Veil itself, the great woven ward, and that she could see it because she was, now, the kind of thing that could see.

The thrill lasted as long as the sunrise. Then the sun cleared the hills, the light went ordinary gold, and Emrys became aware—slowly, the way one becomes aware of a change in the weather—of a silence in the choir. A listening quality. The threads around her had gone still, or rather, they had turned. She could not have said how she knew; she knew the way the rabbit under the gorse knew, the way prey knows. Something far to the east had felt a light kindle in the dark, and had turned its attention toward it, and was now looking at the place where she stood with an attention she could feel on her skin like the sun's first heat.

*Things will feel you waking. Some of them will be curious. Some of them will be kind.*

She did not wait to learn which kind this was. She pulled the gloves tight, clutched Mother Sable's bundle, and ran east along the ridge with the wind at her back and the whole singing world pouring through her, down the far side of the hill, off the smugglers' path, toward the low country and the fen roads and the three-day journey to a cottage she had never seen, belonging to a woman she had never met, who owed her elder a debt she would hate repaying.

Behind her, on the border ridge, the frayed thread of the Veil shivered like a plucked string.

And far away, in a direction Emrys could not have named but her new-woken senses could feel the way a compass feels north, something old opened one eye—and began, unhurried, to move.
