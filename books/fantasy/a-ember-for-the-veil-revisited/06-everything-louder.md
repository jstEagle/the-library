## 6. Everything Louder

The border was a line of standing stones older than any kingdom, and Emrys crossed it at dawn the way one steps off a cliff—knowing, all the while, that the fall is the whole point.

She had imagined, in the sleepless hours before, that she would feel *something*. A shudder. A bell tolling somewhere in her chest. But for the first mile past the stones, there was only the road: rutted mud, frost silvering the gorse, her breath hanging in ragged clouds. The Veiled Valley fell away behind her in folds of grey mist, and she did not look back, because looking back would have meant stopping, and stopping would have meant weeping, and weeping would have meant going home to be registered like a head of cattle or hunted like a fox.

So she walked. And then, as the sun cleared the eastern hills and struck the open moor, the world *woke*.

It began as a sound—or what she first took for a sound. A hum, deep and vast, rising out of the ground itself, out of the stones and the peat and the slow dark water of the fens. Not heard with the ears. Heard with the same place behind her sternum where she had always kept the warmth pressed down, folded small, denied. That place unfolded now like a fist opening after twenty years, and Emrys stopped dead in the middle of the road because she could not, physically, take another step.

The land was *singing*.

No—not singing. Singing implied intention, melody, something with a beginning. This was more fundamental than song. It was the sound of things being alive: the patient thrum of roots drinking, the bright chatter of frost melting on a bramble, the low continuous note of the earth turning over its own deep thoughts. In the valley it had been silence—blessed, suffocating silence—and she had believed that silence was simply how the world sounded. She had believed she was quiet inside because she had made herself so.

Now every lie she had told herself came due at once.

She could see it too. That was worse. Light ran through everything in threads—silver-green filaments lacing the soil, climbing the hawthorn hedges, pooling in the hollows where last night's rain stood. When a crow lifted from a fencepost, it trailed a ribbon of pale radiance that hung in the air long after the bird itself was a speck against the clouds. Emrys raised her hand and watched light run beneath her own skin like ink dropped in water, veining up from her fingertips along her forearms, answering the hum, joining it.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one, to the moor, to whatever was happening to her. "Oh, you were here all along."

The warmth in her fingers—the warmth she had spent her whole life smothering—was not a flaw. It was not a sickness. It was an ear, and an eye, and a mouth, and she had spent twenty years holding all three shut in a room where nothing made a sound.

She laughed. It came out of her wild and cracked, half sob, and she laughed again anyway, spinning once on the empty road with her satchel thumping against her hip, arms out, feeling the threads of the world lean toward her like grass toward sun. The frost melted in a ring around her boots. She didn't notice. Everything was too loud, too bright, too *much*, and she loved it, and it was already beginning to hurt.

By midday the wonder had curdled into something closer to agony.

There was no dial on this. No valve. In the valley she had learned to press the warmth flat, but pressing required something to press against, and out here everything pushed back—every living thing broadcasting, every thread of power tugging at her like a hook set in her skin. The hum of the moor became a roar. The threads became a lattice so dense that looking at anything meant looking through it and beyond it and into the burning heart of it. Her head pounded. Her teeth ached. When she passed a shepherd's hut, she felt the sheep within it as a warm murmur of dim contentment, dozens of small minds brushing hers like moths at a lamp, and she stumbled away with her hands over her ears even though her ears were not what was hearing them.

She tried the old discipline. She sat beneath an elder tree and did her breathing the way Mother Sable had taught the novices—four counts in, four held, eight out—and it helped the way a cupped hand helps against a flood. The threads dimmed. The roar receded to a roar. But the moment her concentration slipped, the world crashed back in, and each crash was louder than the last, as though the land had noticed her attention and grown eager for it, hungry for it, leaning in.

*Tired,* something seemed to say, in the voice of wind over wet grass. *You are tired. Let us carry some of it.*

"No," Emrys whispered, and got up, and walked on.

The inn appeared at dusk like an answered prayer: a squat stone building crouched at a crossroads, windows bleeding candlelight, a sign creaking on its bracket depicting a boar she was too exhausted to read properly. She had coin—Mother Sable had seen to that—but more than coin she needed walls, a door, an hour in which the world might consent to be merely the world again.

Inside, the common room was smoke-thick and loud with ordinary human noise: dice clattering, a fiddle wheezing, arguments about grain prices and a wedding and someone's lame horse. Emrys stood in the doorway and nearly wept with relief, because human noise was just noise. People, ungifted people, were blessedly mute—warm shapes without voices in that other hearing. She took a stool in the corner near the fire, ordered stew and ale with what remained of her composure, and let the ordinary racket wash over her like a blanket.

For perhaps a quarter of an hour, she was almost happy.

Then she felt him.

At first it registered as a wrongness in the room's texture—a cold thread where all the others ran warm, a silence shaped like a man. He lay on a pallet by the hearth, an old drover wrapped in blankets, grey-lipped, breath rattling in a chest full of fluid. The innkeeper's wife hovered over him with a cloth and water, and the innkeeper muttered about fever camps and whether the man ought to be moved out before he brought the pox down on all their heads. Nobody expected him to see morning.

Emrys saw him differently.

She saw the thread of his life fraying—literally fraying, the silver filament that ran through him unravelling strand by strand, wisping off him like smoke off a doused candle. She saw the sickness as a knotted blackness sitting heavy in his lungs, dense as tar, choking the bright thread where it passed. And she felt—this was the terrible part, the part that undid her—she felt how *easy* it would be to fix.

Her hands were warm. Her hands had been warming since the moment she looked at him. The hum of the land had narrowed to a single clear note running up her arms, and the knot of darkness in the old man's chest pulled at her like a splinter pulls at a thumb, like an offense, like something intolerable.

*I shouldn't.* The thought arrived distant and thin. *I don't know how. I've never—*

But another part of her, the part that had healed a sparrow's broken wing at age seven and buried the memory of doing it, the part that had warmed Mother Sable's aching hands through a winter night two years ago and sworn afterward it was nothing, that part already knew. It had always known. It was simply waiting for permission she had never dared to give.

The old man coughed, a long wet terrible sound, and did not finish it.

Emrys was kneeling beside him before she decided to move.

"Let me," she said—to the wife, to the room, to no one. "Please. Let me try."

"What—" The innkeeper's wife drew back. "Girl, he's past—"

Emrys laid both hands on the drover's chest.

The world went silent.

Not quiet—silent, the way the valley had been silent, a held-breath hush in which the only thing that existed was the thread under her palms. She could feel the black knot now from inside, feel its weight and shape, and her gift rose through her arms like water finding its level, warm and sure and utterly without instruction. It didn't ask her how. It simply *knew*, the way her hands had known how to form letters before she understood words. Warmth poured out of her. It poured through the blackness and the blackness screamed—in some register below sound—and broke apart, unravelling into wisps that the silver thread caught and burned away like cobwebs in a flame.

The old man gasped. His back arched off the pallet. Colour flooded into his face so fast and so completely that it looked almost violent, and his eyes flew open—clear, brown, astonished—and fixed on her with the naked wonder of a man looking at the sun after a lifetime underground.

"Bless you," he whispered. "Bless you, child. I was in the dark. I was in the dark and you—"

The world came back all at once.

Sound crashed in—fiddle, dice, the fire, the rain that had begun against the windows—and with it, silence of a different kind. Emrys became aware, slowly and then all at once, that the common room had gone still. The dice had stopped. The fiddle had stopped. Every face in the room was turned toward her, and every face wore the same expression, and she knew that expression intimately, had seen it directed at others across the temple refectory whenever word came of some poor soul carted off by the crown's riders.

Fear.

"I—" Emrys rose. Her hands were still glowing. Faintly, unmistakably, threads of silver-green light visible beneath the skin, in the firelight, in front of everyone. She tucked them into her sleeves like a girl hiding stolen fruit. "He was dying. I only—I only—"

"You're one of *them*," said a man near the door. Not shouting. Worse than shouting: flat, certain, the tone of a verdict.

"She saved Tam's life!" the innkeeper's wife cried, but her voice shook, and her eyes when they met Emrys's asked a different question entirely.

"Registered?" someone else called out. "You registered, girl? Got papers?"

Emrys had papers. Mother Sable's forged travel pass burned against her ribs like a coal, but it was a pass, not a registration, and everyone in that room knew the difference, and everyone in that room knew what happened next, because everyone in that room had heard the proclamation read from every market cross in three counties: *all gifted persons are to present themselves for registry; those who do not will be hunted.*

The man by the door reached for the latch.

And that was when the outer door opened ahead of him, letting in a gust of rain and a woman.

She was short, broad, weathered, maybe fifty, with iron-grey hair escaping a wool hood in wet curls and a walking staff in one hand and a basket in the other. Mud to the knee. The kind of woman who belonged to crossroads inns the way the inn belonged to the crossroads. She looked around the frozen room, read it in a single sweep the way one reads a page, and said, in a voice like a comfortable armchair:

"Well, don't all stop on my account. Bertie, is that your fiddle? You've been threatening to learn it for eleven years."

A nervous ripple of laughter. The woman ignored it. Her gaze found Emrys—found her glowing hands, her white face, the old drover sitting up on his pallet breathing like a man reborn—and something in the woman's expression softened and sharpened at the same time, which should not have been possible, and was.

"There you are," the woman said quietly, to Emrys alone. "Been following your light since the ford, little moth. Brightest thing on this moor in years. Do come with me before these good folk remember their duty to the crown."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. The man at the door still had his hand on the latch, and Emrys saw him weigh it—saw the fear in him wrestling with something else, shame perhaps, or simple reluctance to be the one who did it.

The woman set down her basket, drew from it a small stoppered bottle, and set it on the nearest table with a click that somehow carried through the whole silent room.

"Fever-tincture," she announced pleasantly. "Which I brewed, which makes me also unregistered and also hunted, so unless someone means to fetch the riders for two of us, I'd suggest we all pretend this evening went differently." She smiled around the room, and it was a warm smile, a mother's smile, and underneath it was steel enough to shoe a horse. "Stew smells lovely, by the way. Bertie, play something."

Bertie, after a long moment and a longer swallow, played something.

The room exhaled. Dice resumed. The moment broke like ice at thaw—gradually, then suddenly—and the innkeeper's wife was pressing a bowl into Emrys's hands and muttering that the corner table by the hedge-witch was free, free of course it was, and no, she hadn't seen anything, nobody had seen anything.

Emrys sat where she was told. Her hands, tucked in her lap, would not stop trembling. The old drover—Tam—caught her eye from his pallet and pressed his fist to his heart, and mouthed *bless you* again, and she managed something that was almost a smile before the tears blurred him away.

The woman settled across from her, close, shoulder to shoulder, a wall between Emrys and the room. Up close she smelled of rain and rue and woodsmoke, and her eyes were brown and fierce and kind.

"Bree Ashdown," she said. "Hedge-witch, exile, maker of excellent nettle soup. And you're the valley girl who's been lighting up my second-sight like a festival bonfire for two days." She slid the stew closer. "Eat. Then you'll tell me why a scribe with ward-ink still under her fingernails crossed the veil alone with no idea what she was carrying." Her voice gentled, and gentleness, Emrys was learning, could be the most alarming thing of all. "Because child, whatever woke when you stepped over those stones—it isn't finished waking. And things out here can feel it. Things with crowns."

Outside, far off across the dark moor, a horn sounded. Once. Long. Listening.

Bree's head turned toward the window, and her jaw set.

"Eat faster," she said.
