## 7. The Hedge-Witch's Kitchen

Bree Ashdown had learned long ago that the difference between a house and a home was the smell of bread.

The cottage at the edge of Ashen Fen had been a house for eleven years—two rooms and a lean-to, a garden gone half to weeds, a chimney that smoked when the wind came from the east. She had lived in it the way a hermit lives in a cave: present, functional, unremarked. But on the morning she carried in an armful of split birch and found the girl from the inn sitting up in her bed, pale as milk and staring at her own hands like they belonged to a stranger, Bree decided it was time to bake again.

"You're awake," she said, setting the wood by the hearth. "Good. You've slept two days, and I was starting to take it personally."

The girl—Emrys, she'd said her name was, though she said it the way people say names they've borrowed—flinched at the sound of her voice. Her eyes went around the room: the bundles of dried yarrow hanging from the rafters, the copper pots, the jars of rendered fat and honey and something darker that Bree kept for emergencies of the soul rather than the body. Then they came back to Bree's face, and there was a question in them so large it couldn't be spoken.

"Go on," Bree said. "Ask it."

"Am I—" Emrys's voice cracked. She swallowed and tried again. "Did I do something? To you? At the inn?"

Bree crouched at the hearth and began arranging the kindling, giving herself something to look at while she answered. "You pulled a fever out of a dyer's son that should have killed him inside the hour. You did it without knowing what you were doing, which is either impressive or terrifying, I haven't decided which." She struck flint to steel. "And then you dropped like a sack of turnips, and I carried you out the back before the innkeeper's wife could decide whether to be grateful or greedy."

"Greedy."

"Aye. Gifted folk are worth coin to some people. Worth more to others, and not in a way you'd enjoy." The kindling caught; Bree fed it a few sticks of birch and watched the flame take hold. "So here we are."

Emrys swung her legs off the bed. She was wearing one of Bree's old shifts, sleeves rolled three times at the wrist, and she moved like someone testing whether the floor would hold. "I should go," she said. "I've already been too much trouble, and if the hunters—"

"Sit down."

"I can't—"

"*Sit down.*" Bree didn't raise her voice often; she saved it, the way she saved her strongest tinctures, for occasions that warranted it. Emrys sat. "You'll eat first. Nobody makes decisions on an empty stomach, least of all decisions about running. And you're not going anywhere until you learn not to burn your own fingers off every time you get startled, because right now you're less a witch than a lamp about to tip over."

Emrys looked down at her hands again. In the firelight Bree could see the faint shimmer beneath the skin—not visible, exactly, more *felt*, the way you feel heat off a stone long after sunset. The girl's gift sat close to the surface, poorly housed, badly fed. It had been starved for years behind whatever veil she'd come from, and now it was gorging itself on open air.

"What were you, before?" Emrys asked quietly. "Before... this?"

Bree stood, dusted bark off her skirts, and let herself smile—a real one, crooked and tired. "A hedge-witch. Which is a polite word for what the College calls a criminal. I healed people. That was my whole crime, love. No curses, no hexes, no stolen relics. A miller's daughter with a broken arm, a ferryman coughing blood into his nets. I fixed them, and I never asked the College's permission, because the College charges a year's wages for a healing license and the ferryman had four coppers and a bad winter ahead of him." She opened the flour crock. "Eleven years ago they took my name off the rolls and put it on the hunt registry instead. So." She measured flour into a bowl with the ease of long habit. "Now I'm a hedge-witch. Same work. Worse pay. Better company, lately."

* * *

The kitchen became their schoolroom by the second day, mostly because it was the only room with a table.

Bree taught the way her grandmother had taught her: hands first, words after. She set a bowl of water between them on the scarred oak and told Emrys to make the surface still.

"It already is still," Emrys said.

"It's thinking about rippling. Everything's thinking about rippling. Your job is to convince it otherwise." Bree folded her arms. "Magic isn't force, girl, whatever the College textbooks say. Force is for soldiers and fools. Magic is *attention*. You don't push the water quiet. You listen to it until it forgets to be nervous."

Emrys frowned at the bowl. Bree watched her try three different ways—the hard way, jaw clenched, knuckles white, which made the water shudder and climb the sides like something trying to escape; then the desperate way, which did nothing at all; then, finally, when frustration wore her down into exhaustion, the right way. Her shoulders dropped. Her breath slowed. And the water went glass-still, so perfectly still that it reflected the rafters, the hanging herbs, Emrys's own astonished face looking back at her.

"Oh," Emrys breathed, and the bowl shattered.

Water sprayed across the table, across Bree's apron, across the cat—who had appeared from nowhere and now sat dripping and deeply offended on the windowsill. Emrys went white. "I'm sorry—I didn't mean—"

"Course you didn't. That's the whole lesson." Bree fetched a cloth and mopped the table without any particular urgency. "Your gift answers your feelings faster than your thoughts. Fear, joy, grief—it doesn't care which. It just hears the shout and does something loud." She nodded at the girl's trembling hands. "So we teach it manners. Again."

They did it again. And again. Eleven times that morning, and eleven times the next, and on the fourth day Emrys held the water still for a full minute and then, weeping quietly with effort and triumph in equal measure, let it go gently, the ripples spreading soft as breath.

"There," Bree said, and heard how rough her own voice had gone. "There it is."

* * *

It wasn't all lessons. That was the thing Bree hadn't planned for, and couldn't have planned against even if she'd tried.

Emrys had a way of inserting herself into the workings of the cottage without ever quite asking permission—gathering eggs with a carefulness that suggested she'd never touched a hen before and found the experience profound; weeding the herb beds and asking the name of everything twice until she had it; standing at Bree's elbow while patients came through the door, watching with hungry attention as Bree splinted a poacher's wrist and lanced a swineherd's boil and talked the widow Marsh out of a wasting sickness with willow bark, bone broth, and sheer bloody-minded company.

"You charge nothing," Emrys said one evening, after the widow had left with a jar of tonic and a loaf under each arm. "For any of it."

"The widow pays in onions. The poacher pays in rabbits. It evens out."

"But if you had a license—if you worked in a town—you'd have coin. A proper house. You wouldn't be—" Emrys stopped, hearing herself.

"Hiding in a fen?" Bree finished mildly. She was rendering fat at the stove, and she kept her eyes on it. "Aye, well. There's a question buried in that question, love, and I'll answer the one underneath it instead. You're wondering why anyone would choose this. Cold winters, thin purse, a name you daren't speak above a whisper." She stirred the pot. "Because every person who walks out that door walks out *whole*. Because no College clerk gets to tell me whose pain is worth my time. Because the law says what I am is a crime, and every morning I wake up and commit it anyway, cheerfully, and the world is measurably kinder for it." She glanced over. "That's not exile, girl. That's just living somewhere the law hasn't thought to look yet."

Emrys was quiet a long moment. "My valley was like that," she said finally. "Safe. Kind. Everyone looked after everyone." Her hands curled in her lap. "But it was safe because it was walled. And the walls were bought with somebody else's suffering, and I didn't know, and I copied my warding texts and told myself the world outside was just... weather." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "You chose yours. That's the difference. You looked at what it cost and stayed anyway."

Bree turned back to the stove before the girl could see her face do anything foolish. "Eat your soup," she said.

* * *

On the sixth night, rain came down like the sky meant it personally, and neither of them could sleep for the drumming on the roof. They ended up at the kitchen table with a candle stub and Bree's second-best bottle of sloe gin, and somehow—they got there the way conversations do, sideways, by accident—Emrys started talking about her mother.

"She sang," Emrys said. She had the cup cradled in both hands but wasn't drinking. "In the temple garden, after evening bells. Old songs, valley songs, ones nobody wrote down because writing them down felt like pinning butterflies." A small, helpless smile. "She had a voice like—like the moment just before rain. Everything held its breath. I used to pretend I'd forgotten my satchel on purpose so I'd have an excuse to stay and listen."

Bree said nothing. Some stories you tend the way you tend a fire: you keep the space around them clear and you let them breathe.

"She died when I was fourteen. Fever. The temple healers did everything right and it didn't matter." Emrys turned the cup slowly. "After that I threw myself into the scriptorium. Ink and vellum and rules. Rules are comfortable when everything else has fallen down. I told myself the warmth in my fingers was nothing. I sat on it for ten years, Bree. Ten years of holding my breath." She laughed, short and bitter. "And now I'm sitting in a fugitive's kitchen learning to make water sit still, and it's the freest I've ever felt, and I hate that I feel free, because feeling free means home wasn't—"

She stopped. Bree reached across the table and covered one of those restless, glowing-at-the-edges hands with her own rough one.

"Home was real," Bree said. "And it wasn't enough. Both things, love. You're allowed both things. Anyone who tells you grief comes in one piece at a time has never carried any."

Emrys looked at their hands. When she spoke again her voice was very small. "Why are you doing this? For me. You don't know me. I'm a risk sitting at your table. Every hour I'm here, the hunters draw closer, and you've built something here, something that matters, and I'm—"

"Stop." Bree squeezed the hand once, firmly, and let go. "You want to know why? Fine. Eleven years ago, when they struck my name off the rolls, one person stood up in the College hall and said *this is wrong*. One. An old woman named Maren Ostrey, who owed me nothing, who ruined her own standing saying it, and who walked out with me afterward and pressed six coppers into my hand and told me to plant something." Bree took a slow drink of gin. "I've spent eleven years repaying a debt to a woman I never saw again. Not to her—to the *shape* of it. Somebody stood up for me when standing up cost something. So now I stand up. It's not complicated. It's just the only arithmetic that's ever made sense to me."

Outside, the rain went on. The candle guttered and steadied. And Emrys Kavanagh, scribe of the Veiled Valley, fugitive, accidental miracle-worker, put her head down on her arms on the kitchen table and cried—for her mother, for her valley, for the girl she'd been behind the walls—and Bree Ashdown sat with her the whole night through, one hand resting light between her shoulder blades, saying nothing, needing to say nothing, while the storm wore itself out against the roof.

When Emrys finally lifted her head, hollow-eyed and wrung-out and somehow lighter, she said, "Thank you," and Bree said, "Don't mention it. Ever. To anyone," and Emrys laughed—a real laugh, cracked and surprised out of her—and something settled into place in the little cottage that had been a house for eleven years and was now, unmistakably, something else.

* * *

Three mornings later, Bree went out to check the eel traps and came back fast, moving low along the hedgerow, and found Emrys in the garden with her hands full of chamomile and her face full of peace.

"Inside," Bree said. "Now. Don't run—walk."

Emrys read her correctly, thank the stars; she set down the flowers and came without a sound. Once the door was barred, Bree crossed to the window slit and looked out through the gap in the shutter.

Down the fen road, half a mile off, moving at an unhurried walk that fooled nobody: riders. Six of them, grey cloaks, and at their head a woman on a dun mare with a sword worn like an apology—Captain Vane, if Bree's memory of the hunt rosters served, and it usually did. They weren't searching the fens. Not properly. Their line bent away from the cottage, deliberate as a drawn curtain, sweeping ground they plainly had no interest in finding anything on.

Which meant one of two things. Either the captain was incompetent—and Bree had heard enough about Ilsa Vane to doubt it—or she was looking for something else entirely, and using the search for it as cover.

Then Emrys made a small sound beside her, and Bree turned, and the girl had gone the color of tallow. She was staring at nothing—at the middle air—with her gift rising off her skin like heat-shimmer, and when she spoke her voice came out flat and strange, as though it were being borrowed:

"He's calling for help," she whispered. "The king. Something is eating him alive, and he's calling for help, and I'm the only one who can hear him."

Far off across the fen, thunder rolled from a cloudless sky.
