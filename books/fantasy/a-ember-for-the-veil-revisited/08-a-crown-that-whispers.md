## 8. A Crown That Whispers

The dreams always began the same way: with the sound of his own voice.

Aldric Veyran, third of that name, King of Caelmor and the Marchlands, Warden of the Nine Rivers, would find himself standing in the throne room of his childhood—not the throne room as it was now, with its banners gone gray and its gold leaf flaking like dead skin, but as it had been when he was seven years old and the world was still a place where doors opened onto kindness. And in the dream he would be speaking. Giving a speech, perhaps, or singing a song his mother had taught him, or reciting the names of all the rivers in order from source to sea, the way his tutor had made him do until the words wore grooves in his mind.

He never knew what he was saying. That was the first horror of it. His mouth moved; the sound came out rich and certain and entirely foreign; and somewhere beneath it, like a man drowning under river ice, he could feel himself pressing upward against a surface that would not break.

Then he would wake, and the crown would be on the table beside his bed, and it would be humming.

* * *

He had taken to sleeping in the old nursery wing.

It was an eccentricity the court had learned to forgive—or rather, to stop remarking upon, which was the same thing in a palace where eccentricity was currency and madness was simply a coin no one wished to count aloud. The nursery sat at the end of the east corridor, past the apartments that had been Seraphine's, past the schoolroom with its chalk ghosts still faintly legible on the slate boards if one held a candle at the right angle. It was a small room. Low-ceilinged. The window overlooked the inner garden where the queen—Elira, whose name he had not spoken in four years except in prayer, and lately not even there—had once kept her roses.

There were no roses now. There was nothing now. He had ordered the garden closed after the funeral and had never reopened it, and the gardeners, understanding something without being told, had let it go to seed and bramble until it became less a garden than a green argument about what happens to tended things when they are abandoned.

He slept there because the crown could not follow him there. Not physically—it sat in its case on the table by the door every night, exactly where the Lord Chamberlain's men deposited it each evening after the day's formalities—but its *voice*, its reach, its long cold fingers reaching into the warm rooms of his mind, grew thinner across that distance. In the state apartments, in the council chamber, in the throne room itself, it spoke constantly. Here, in the small room with the low ceiling and the ghost of chalk dust, it had to strain. It had to *want*. And wanting, Aldric had learned over six bitter years, was the one thing it did grudgingly, the way a miser parts with coin.

So he slept badly in a child's bed that had been widened twice to accommodate a grown king's frame, and he counted the hours of silence the way a starving man counts crumbs, and he told no one.

* * *

"You look tired, Majesty."

Chancellor Ondrey said it in the tone he used for everything—mild, unhurried, the voice of a man who had spent forty years making other people's urgency seem vulgar. He stood at the council table with the morning's dispatches fanned before him like a hand of cards he had already won with.

"I slept," Aldric said.

"Of course." Ondrey's eyes flickered, briefly, toward the crown resting on its cushion at the table's center—the crown attended every council session; protocol demanded it; the entity had seen to the writing of that protocol years ago, threading its requirements into the law so smoothly that no clerk questioned why the King of Caelmor must wear his coronation regalia to discuss grain tariffs—and then returned to his papers. "The hunt reports are encouraging. Fourteen registrations this week in the river towns alone. The Grand Huntress writes that the border provinces will be fully surveyed by autumn."

"Fourteen." Aldric turned the number over. Fourteen people who had walked into a garrison office and written their names in a book because a proclamation nailed to their church door had promised that registration meant protection. Protection. The word sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. "And how many unregistered?"

Ondrey's pen paused above the page—a hesitation so slight that anyone else would have missed it, but Aldric had spent six years learning to read the micro-expressions of frightened men. "The Grand Huntress estimates several hundred, Majesty. Perhaps more. The valley districts remain... resistant."

"The Veiled Valley." He said the name carefully, tasting it, because he had begun to suspect that when he spoke of the valley he was not entirely the one choosing the words. "What is our position on the valley?"

"Our position, Majesty?" Ondrey looked up, and for a moment—just a moment—his face did something complicated. Pity, perhaps. Or fear wearing pity's clothes. "Your position. You decreed the survey of the valley yourself. Last spring. You were... quite insistent."

Had he? He searched backward through the months and found the memory waiting for him, whole and vivid: the council chamber, the maps spread out, his own hand tracing the mountain passes, his own voice explaining—with unusual passion—that no sanctuary could be permitted to harbor the gifted while honest citizens registered and suffered. He remembered saying it. He remembered meaning it, or something adjacent to meaning it, or the shape of meaning it, the way a glove holds the shape of a hand long after the hand is withdrawn.

That was the thing about the memories now. They were all gloves. He could put them on and they fit perfectly and they were warm and they were shaped like his life, but when he pressed his fingers into them he felt nothing inside.

"Yes," he said. "I recall."

* * *

The worst hours were the ones just before dawn, when the palace slept and the candles burned down to their collars and the whispering started.

It did not speak in words, not usually. Words were crude instruments, and it was not a crude intelligence. What it did was older and more intimate than speech: it *inclined*. Like a great weight shifting in a dark room, like the pressure change before a storm, it leaned against the walls of his mind and let him feel what it wanted him to feel. Despair, mostly. A vast, patient despair, the despair of deep water, of geology. *You have been fighting me for six years,* it communicated, without language, without cruelty even—cruelty would have been almost comforting, cruelty would have implied an opponent worth hating—*and look what you have left.*

And he would look. That was the trap of it, the mechanism he understood too late and too well: it only needed him to look. Because when he looked he saw the ledger of his own diminishment, and the ledger was accurate.

His sister Maren—he had not thought of Maren in three years, and when he tried now he found the memory smooth and featureless, a river stone where a woman should have been. He had loved her. He was nearly sure he had loved her. There were letters in his desk in his own hand addressed to her, warm letters, funny letters, and he read them sometimes like an archaeologist reading the grave goods of a stranger, trying to reconstruct the man who wrote them from the artifacts alone.

His daughter. Seraphine. Her name still cut, which meant she was still partly his, which meant he still had time, or had had time, or—

No. He would not finish that thought. Finishing thoughts was how it found the doors.

Because that was the true architecture of the siege, the thing no healer or priest or scholar had ever been able to see from outside: the entity did not take. Taking required force, and force met resistance, and resistance—even his worn, threadbare, six-years-battered resistance—was apparently more than it cared to spend itself against. Instead it *waited at the thresholds*. Every memory was a door, and behind every door was a room where something he loved was kept, and the entity stood in the corridors of his mind like a courteous invader, hands folded, patient as rot, and it waited for him to open the doors himself.

Because he always did, eventually. Loneliness opened them. Grief opened them. The simple animal need to remember his wife's face opened them, and he would push open the door to the room where Elira laughed in the garden with flour on her hands, and he would stand in that room drinking in the memory like a dying man drinks water, and he would not notice—at first, never at first—the thin cold thread that ran from the doorway back into the dark, back into the crown, back into the thing that fed.

It ate love. That was the discovery of the fourth year, the one that had finally driven him to start sleeping in the nursery, the one he had written in cipher in the margins of his astronomy tables because he dared not trust it to paper in any script a reader might stumble upon. It did not eat fear or pain or hatred, though it drank those too, the way a man drinks wine between meals. What it *ate*—what it had been eating, course by course, year by year, a banquet laid out over decades—was love. Cherished things. The warm rooms. And the witch-hunt, the great machine of proclamation and registry and pyre that ground on in his name across three kingdoms—

He had understood it two winters ago, sitting alone in the map room at three in the morning, and the understanding had come with the particular sick clarity of a man realizing the fever he has been treating has been the disease all along. The hunt was not policy. The hunt was *fishing*. Somewhere out in the world were people whose gifts burned bright and whose hearts, presumably, held treasures as bright as his had once been—and the entity wanted them gathered up, wanted them close, wanted whatever feast lived inside them the way a glutton wants a kitchen he can smell from the road.

He had tried, that winter, to stop it. He had drafted the revocation edict himself, in secret, in cipher, intending to read it to the full council with the crown locked in its vault three floors below.

He did not remember what happened next. He remembered waking in his bed with ink on his fingers and the draft gone from his desk and the fire stoked high, and he remembered Ondrey's mild face the next morning saying, *You withdrew the proposal yourself, Majesty. You said the timing was premature.* And he remembered the crown humming on its cushion, softly, contentedly, like a cat after feeding.

After that he had stopped drafting edicts. After that he had begun keeping the small ledger of himself—what remained, what was missing, what was smooth river stone where a person used to be—and the ledger only ever grew in one direction.

* * *

Tonight the whispering came early.

He was in the nursery, in the chair by the window, watching the moon move through the tangle of the dead garden, when he felt it lean. Not the usual diffuse pressure but something focused, something with intent behind it, and for the first time in months he caught an actual word forming in the back of his skull, a word rising through the water like a bubble:

*Soon.*

"Soon what?" he said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how his voice had changed—thinner than it used to be, frayed at the edges, a king's voice worn down to a beggar's thread.

The answer was not a word. It was an image, and it arrived whole, the way its gifts always did, generous and poisonous: a girl. Young, dark-haired, ordinary-looking, running through a landscape he did not recognize—fens, maybe, or marshland, gray water and gray sky—and around her the air itself was *lit*, threaded with light the way a spiderweb is threaded with dew, and the light poured off her like heat off a forge, and the entity's attention fixed on her with a hunger so vast and so ancient that Aldric felt his own body respond to it, his mouth watering, his heart lurching, as though hunger were contagious.

It showed him the girl for perhaps three heartbeats. Then it took the image away, deliberately, the way one draws a bone back from a dog, and left behind only the wanting.

"No," Aldric said. His hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the arms of the chair, against the wood Elira had chosen for this room when Seraphine was expected, oak from the royal forests, plain and honest. "Whatever she is. Whoever she is. No."

The pressure receded, satisfied, unhurried. It did not need his consent. It had never needed his consent. His refusal was, he understood dimly, part of the meal—resistance seasoned the meat, or perhaps it was simply entertainment, the way a court enjoys the struggles of a bull brought to the ring. It would find the girl. It was already finding her; he could feel the long attention of it turning westward, toward the borders, toward the valleys and the fens, feel the machinery of proclamations and hunters and burning registries beginning to turn faster, like a mill wheel when the sluice gates open.

And he would sit here, in a child's room in a palace he no longer governed, and watch it happen through eyes that were still, technically, his own.

He rose. He crossed to the desk—Seraphine's old schoolroom desk, small, scarred with her childhood carvings, a lopsided sun scratched near the corner when she was six—and he took out the astronomy tables and turned to the blank pages bound at the back, and he wrote, in cipher, in a hand that shook:

*Saw her tonight. The one it wants. A girl, dark-haired, west. If I am ever found, if anyone ever reads this: it is not the gifted who are dangerous. It is the thing on my head. It is fishing. Do not let it catch her. Whatever else you do—do not let it catch her.*

He stared at the words a long time. Then, because he could not remember whether he had written the same entry last month or had merely dreamed writing it, he flipped backward through the pages, checking.

The entries went back four years. Hundreds of them. The handwriting deteriorated, slowly, steadily, from a king's firm script to something cramped and slanting, and the dates grew closer together, and the entries grew shorter, and somewhere around the middle of the book the sentences stopped being about strategy and resistance and became, instead, lists—lists of things he was afraid to forget, written over and over, like prayers: *Elira laughing in the garden. Flour on her hands. Seraphine's tooth gap. The sun she carved. My sister Maren's laugh, low, before she speaks. The smell of the library at Veyran House. My mother's hands. My mother's hands. My mother's hands.*

The last entry was dated nine days ago. He had no memory of writing it. It said only:

*I don't know how much longer I am the one holding the pen.*

Outside, the moon slid behind cloud, and the dead garden went dark, and far away to the west—though he could not have known it, though he knew it anyway, the way one knows a wound is bleeding—a girl was crossing a border at dawn, and the land was beginning to hum.

Aldric closed the book. And in the case by the door, soft as a cat settling in, the crown began, very quietly, to sing.
