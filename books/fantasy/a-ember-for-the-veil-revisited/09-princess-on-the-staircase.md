## 9. Princess on the Staircase

The letters began arriving in autumn, and Seraphine noticed them the way one notices a slow leak in a roof—by the stain they left behind.

She had come to the capital in the ninth month, summoned by no one and welcomed by less. Her father's letter had been four lines long, written in a hand she almost hadn't recognized: *The court expects your presence for the winter session. Come if it suits you.* No warmth in it. No apology either, though an apology was owed for six years of silence, and both of them knew whose fault that silence was—or each believed it belonged to the other, which amounted to the same thing.

So she had come, because pride was a luxury she could no longer afford and because some stubborn root in her still wanted her father back, even the diminished version of him the court whispered about. *The king is tired,* they said. *The king keeps his own counsel these days.* *The king is not what he was.*

She took rooms in the east wing, far from the royal apartments, and told herself the distance was deliberate on both sides.

It was on the third evening of her stay, descending the great staircase toward supper, that she saw him: King Aldric Vane, standing alone in the gallery above the entrance hall, speaking to no one.

He stood very straight, as he always had—a soldier's posture worn like armor over a scholar's frame—and he was talking. His lips moved. His head tilted at intervals, the way a man's does when he listens. And there was no one there. The gallery was empty but for a pair of candelabra guttering in the draft from the doors below, throwing his shadow long and wrong across the marble.

Seraphine stopped halfway down the stairs, her hand on the banister, and watched.

He gestured once—an impatient, cutting motion she remembered from her childhood, from councils she'd been permitted to observe before she'd been deemed too much her mother's daughter to be trusted with statecraft. Then he went still, listening again, and whatever he heard made his shoulders draw inward, made him look suddenly smaller inside his fine coat, a man being scolded in a room where no one could see the whip.

Then he turned, saw her, and everything about him reassembled itself—the spine, the chin, the flat courteous mask of kingship—as though a puppeteer had snatched the strings back up.

"Seraphine." Her name came out slightly wrong, as if he'd had to fetch it from a shelf he rarely visited. "You arrived safely, then."

"I arrived three days ago, Father."

"Of course." He descended the stairs toward her, and up close she could see what the distance had hidden: he was thinner. The skin under his eyes had gone the color of old pewter, and there was a tremor in his left hand that he suppressed by gripping the banister as he passed it. "We should speak. There is—I have meant to—"

"Yes," she said, when the sentence died. "There is much we've meant to say to each other for six years."

Something flickered across his face—not guilt exactly, but its exhausted cousin—and then, behind his eyes, something else. A wariness. As if he were checking, quickly and fearfully, whether she was real.

"You look well," he said. "Your mother's coloring."

"My mother has been dead eleven years, Father. You might mention her more gracefully than that."

"I beg your pardon." He said it automatically, a courtier's reflex, and she realized with a small cold shock that he had not actually heard her—that some part of him was elsewhere entirely, attending another conversation, in another room, with someone who was not there.

"The hour grows late," he said. "We will sup together tomorrow. I give you my word."

He gave her his word the way he gave pardons and patents of nobility—freely, formally, and without any expectation of keeping it. She watched him go down the hall toward his apartments, walking too carefully, like a man crossing ice, and she stood on the staircase until the candles burned low and the footmen began to glance at her with that particular blend of concern and discretion that palace servants perfected.

*Speaking to empty rooms*, she thought. *Burning letters.* Because that was the other thing. The second stain on the roof.

* * *

She had seen the burning first, actually—two nights before the staircase. Walking in the west garden after dark, unable to sleep in a bed that smelled of someone else's lavender, she had looked up at her father's study window and seen firelight moving strangely against the glass. Not the steady glow of a lamp but the restless leap of paper catching flame, sheet after sheet of it.

She had gone up, of course. Knocked. Received no answer, tried the door, found it locked—which was itself remarkable, because her father had never locked that door in his life. He used to leave it open deliberately, so that any minister or petitioner or bored child might wander in and interrupt him, and he'd claimed it kept him honest.

Through the keyhole she had smelled smoke and tallow and, beneath them, something sweeter and stranger—like hot metal, like a forge in a closed room.

"Father?"

A long pause. Then his voice, muffled and oddly distant: "Not now, Sera."

*Sera.* He hadn't called her that since she was nine years old. It slipped out of him like something escaping through a cracked door, and then, as if embarrassed by its escape, he added, formal again: "I am occupied. Good night."

She had stood in the corridor a while longer, listening to the soft, industrious sound of pages being fed to the fire, and wondered what a king burns at midnight. State secrets, perhaps. Old correspondence. Love letters—though whose, after all this time?

In the morning, the hearth in his study had been raked clean, and the study itself had been unlocked and tidied, and her father had greeted her at breakfast with perfect composure and asked whether she found the capital's autumn agreeable.

But servants talk. Servants always talk, if you know how to listen, and Seraphine had grown up learning to listen in a court full of people who spoke only when overheard. So she listened.

The valet, Tam, a gray-templed man who had dressed her father since before her birth, mentioned—over wine she bought him, in a corner of the kitchens he trusted—that His Majesty had taken to burning things "most every week now." Letters, mostly. "Old ones, though. From before. Friends of his, if you follow me, Highness. Men he rode with, women he—" Tam coughed into his cup. "Correspondents. He reads them through first, every page, like he's saying goodbye to them. Then in the fire they go."

"Does he read anything new?" Seraphine asked. "Reports? Dispatches?"

"Oh, aye, those too. But it's the old ones he lingers over." Tam turned his cup slowly. "And there's another thing, begging Your Highness's pardon. Some nights he calls out. Names. Names I don't know, and names I do." He hesitated. "Yours, sometimes, Highness. But not—" He struggled. "Not quite yours. Like he's calling for a child, and you're grown, and he can't make the two of you fit."

Seraphine set down her cup with great care.

"And the Grand Huntress," she said. "Maren Voss. She writes to him often, does she not? The hunt reports come to his desk directly."

Tam's face did something complicated. "They do."

"And does he burn those?"

"No, Highness." The valet looked at his hands. "Those he signs."

* * *

The hunt registry was not a secret document, precisely. That was the horror of it—it hung in the entrance hall of the Ministry of Justice, a great vellum roll renewed each season, listing every gifted person registered, questioned, or condemned in the crown's name. Schoolmasters brought their pupils to see it. Priests cited it from pulpits. It was meant to be seen; terror only works in daylight.

Seraphine had walked past it a hundred times in her girlhood without reading a word. Now she stood before it in the gray light of a late October morning, hood drawn up, attended by a single maid, and read.

The registry was organized by season and province. The current season's section was longer than any she remembered—longer than the last three combined, if the faded entries above were any measure. Names, parishes, gifts alleged, dispositions. *Registered. Questioned and released. Fled, whereabouts unknown.* And, in a cramped clerk's hand, the disposition that appeared more and more often as her eye traveled down:

*Condemned. Executed.*

She told herself she was looking for nothing in particular. She told herself she had come out of general unease, a daughter's worry wearing the borrowed clothes of investigation. She was lying to herself, and some part of her knew it, because when she found the name she had known she would find it, her whole body went cold, as if she'd walked through a door into winter.

**MAREN COLE, of the Palace Household. Gift alleged: hedge-craft, healing. Condemned upon confession. Executed the 14th day of September.**

Her nurse. Her Nanny Cole, who had carried her on her hip through the palace gardens and taught her the names of herbs and let her stir the honey-and-thyme draughts she brewed for the kitchen children's fevers. Who had hummed to her through scarlet fever. Who had been dismissed from service twelve years ago—quietly, without explanation, one of the many small cruelties of the estrangement between her parents that Seraphine had never fully understood—because someone had whispered that she kept charms in her apron pocket, and her mother, frightened and rigid in her last illness, had believed the whisper.

Hedge-craft. Healing. That was all. That was the whole of Maren Cole's crime: she had spent forty years making sick people well, and the crown had put her name on a list and then put her in the ground.

Seraphine read the entry three times. The clerk's hand did not change. The date did not change.

"Highness?" her maid murmured. "Shall I fetch you some water? You've gone white as the wall."

"When," Seraphine said, and her voice came out strange and level, "was this entered?"

"The fourteenth of September, it says, Highness."

"September." Two months ago. Two months ago she had been at Wrenholt, her little estate in the south, pruning roses and answering her steward's letters, while in the capital her childhood nurse had been condemned and killed in her father's name. "Who signed the condemnation?"

The maid, wisely, did not ask why she wanted to know. "The commission sits with the Grand Huntress, Highness. But the warrant—the warrant bears the king's seal."

Of course it did. Everything bore the king's seal. The king sealed what was put before him; that was what kings were for, in the machine of the court. And yet—

And yet her father had been dismissed from service to his own mind for months now. Speaking to empty rooms. Burning the letters of old friends like a man destroying evidence of a life. Calling for a child who no longer existed. Signing, with a steady hand, the death warrants of herb-women and hedge-healers, of gentle, harmless, *useful* people, while somewhere behind his eyes something else did the choosing.

She thought of him on the staircase, flinching from a conversation she couldn't hear. She thought of the sweet metallic smell through the study keyhole. She thought of Tam's careful voice: *those he signs.*

"Sera."

He had called her that through the door. Not-quite-hers. Or perhaps—she turned the thought over slowly, examining it from underneath—perhaps it had been the truest thing he'd said in six years, the last unguarded syllable of the father she'd lost, calling out from wherever he was trapped.

"Highness?" the maid prompted gently.

"We're done here," Seraphine said. She looked at the registry one final time—at Maren Cole's name, small and permanent among the hundreds—and felt something in her chest close like a fist. Not grief, or not only grief. Grief was passive. This had teeth.

For twelve years she had stayed away from her father, nursing her grievances like a garden she tended out of spite, telling herself the estrangement was principled, that his coldness excused hers, that time would settle accounts between them eventually. Time had settled them. Time had executed her nurse and hollowed out her father and left a thing signing papers in his place, and she had spent those same months arranging cut flowers.

No longer.

"Marren," she said to her maid, using the woman's proper name for once instead of the diminutive, "send word to the palace archivist. I want copies of every hunt warrant bearing the king's seal for the past year—every one, Marren, and I want them tonight, and I want no one to know I asked. And then find out for me who delivers the Grand Huntress's dispatches to His Majesty, and whether anyone—anyone at all—is ever present when he reads them."

"And if someone asks why Your Highness wants such things?"

Seraphine pulled her hood back. Somewhere beyond the ministry windows, the autumn sun was breaking through, gilding the vellum roll, lighting up all those condemned names like lamps.

"Tell them," she said, "that the princess is taking an interest in her father's health."

She walked out of the Ministry of Justice with her spine straight and her mind already moving—warrants, dispatches, the sweet smell of a forge behind a locked door, a voice through a keyhole saying *Sera*. Pieces, not yet a picture. But she had spent six years learning how a court hides things, and she knew the first rule of concealment: nothing stays hidden from someone willing to look at what everyone else has agreed not to see.

That night, in the east wing, she lit a candle for Maren Cole and sat down at her desk to wait for the archivist's copies.

They arrived just past midnight, borne by a trembling junior clerk who would not meet her eye. Forty-one warrants in twelve months. She spread them across the floor of her sitting room and knelt among them, reading by candlelight, and it was near dawn when she found it—the pattern she had half-dreaded, half-hoped for.

Every warrant signed in the king's own hand. Every one. Through illness, through grief, through six years of estrangement and two months of obvious decline, the king had missed not a single signature.

Whatever wore her father's face, she thought, ink drying under her pen as she wrote a single line in her journal—

*It is very diligent.*

—she thought, it works tirelessly. And it was hunting. And tomorrow, she decided, she would begin to find out why.
