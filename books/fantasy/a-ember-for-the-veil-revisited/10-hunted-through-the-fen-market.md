## 10. Hunted Through the Fen Market

The fen market smelled of eels and woodsmoke and the sweet rot of reeds left too long in standing water. Emrys followed Bree down the plank walkway between the stalls, her hood drawn low, her hands buried in the pockets of a coat two sizes too large for her—a man's coat, oil-clothed and salt-stiff, that Bree had produced from a chest with the offhand remark that dead husbands' coats kept no one warm.

She was still not used to the noise of it. In the Veiled Valley, markets had been quiet affairs: farmers trading turnips for flour in murmured voices, the temple bell marking hours over everything like a soft hand pressed down on the town. Here, the noise had layers. Fishwives shouting prices three stalls apart as though across a river. Children shrieking through the pilings beneath the walkway. A fiddler somewhere, badly tuned, competing with a man selling charms against toothache—which, Emrys had learned in three weeks outside the valley, were either genuine hedge-work or outright fraud depending on the seller's eyes.

Bree's eyes, when she sold her own small charms at market days like this one, crinkled at the corners. Honest ones, mostly.

"Stay close," Bree murmured, adjusting the basket on her hip. "And whatever happens, don't look at anyone too long. You've got a way of looking at people, love. Like you're reading them."

"I *am* reading them," Emrys said before she could stop herself. "A little. It's gotten quieter since you've been teaching me the—"

"Baskets," Bree said firmly, pressing a woven handle into Emrys's hands. "Think about baskets."

Emrys thought about baskets. She thought about how the willow threads caught the light in ridges, how the weave tightened where the maker's hands had been most patient, and she let that small attention anchor her the way Bree had taught her to anchor herself—to objects, to textures, to the plain weight of the world—so the other thing, the vast humming awareness she'd carried since crossing the border, would settle back into something bearable.

Three weeks, and still the land sang to her. That was the only word she had for it. The fens hummed in long green chords, water and root and silt braided together into something slow and patient; the sky above them rang faintly silver; even the market's crowd threw off warmth like embers, each person a small flicker of life she could sense if she let her attention drift even a hand's breadth. In the valley she had been a hearth behind a stone wall. Out here she was a bonfire on a headland, and every day she understood better what Mother Sable had meant when she said *things will feel your waking*.

Including, apparently, men in grey cloaks.

* * *

Emrys saw them first from the eel-seller's stall, while Bree haggled over dried lavender and feverfew with a woman who had a goiter and no patience. Across the market square, near the toll bridge where the causeway met dry land, four riders stood beside their horses without dismounting. Grey traveling cloaks, mud to the knee, and on their breasts—the sight of it stopped her breath—the crowned stag of Aldric's hunt.

They weren't searching. That was what made her stomach drop. They weren't moving stall to stall, checking papers, asking questions. They stood in a loose crescent facing into the market like fishermen watching a float, waiting for something beneath the surface to tug the line.

"Someone's been seen," she whispered. The words came out flat and strange, and she heard them as if another person had said them. "Here. Today."

Bree didn't turn her head. Emrys watched her old friend's hands keep sorting lavender bundles, steady as a surgeon's, but her voice when it came was pitched very low indeed. "How many?"

"Four. At the bridge. They're not hunting, they're *waiting*. And there—" Emrys's gift slid outward before she could leash it, tasting the air beyond the bridge, and she found more of them, a larger knot of grey up the road among the alders, six or seven, with cages on a wagon. Actual cages. "Eight more, north side. They've brought wagons."

Bree set down her lavender. Very deliberately, she picked up her basket, hooked her arm through Emrys's, and turned them both toward the far end of the market as though continuing an errand. "Right," she said brightly, loudly, in the voice of a woman discussing onions. "There's a boatman at the south staithe who owes me for his wife's lungs. We're going to go and have a chat about lungs."

"The south staithe is past the bridge."

"It is indeed." Bree's smile showed all her teeth. "So we'd best look boring doing it."

They walked. Emrys fixed her eyes on Bree's shoulder-blade and counted her own heartbeats and tried, desperately, to be a woman carrying a basket. Around them the market roared on, oblivious, wonderful: a boy crying over dropped honeycomb, gulls screaming off the meres, the eel-seller chopping something wet and furious. Ordinary life, so thick and loud around her, and she loved it with a ferocity that frightened her, because she knew now how quickly it could be taken.

Twenty yards from the bridge. Ten. One of the riders turned his head, and under his hood Emrys saw a young face, pockmarked, bored—and then his gaze snagged on her, or on nothing, and he straightened in the saddle.

"She feels it," Emrys breathed. "The one on the dun horse. He can't see me but he feels—"

"Walk. Don't run. Running is an admission."

The rider raised a whistle to his lips.

The sound that came out of it split the market like an axe. Every head turned. Somewhere behind them, the fiddler stopped mid-tune. And then the grey cloaks were moving—not toward Emrys and Bree, but wheeling their horses toward the fish row on the market's east flank, where a woman with a shawl over her hair had frozen mid-step, a basket of smelts clutched to her chest, her face gone white as the belly of a fish.

"*There!*" the pockmarked rider shouted. "Stop, in the crown's name!"

"That's not me," Emrys said stupidly. "I'm not—she's just a woman—"

"She's gifted, or they think she is, which comes to the same thing if nobody corrects them." Bree's grip on Emrys's arm turned iron. The whole market had become a churning tide, bodies flowing away from the fish row, and in that tide Bree pulled Emrys sideways, then backward, into the shadowed gap between a tallow-chandler's stall and the raw plank wall of a smokehouse. Reek of rendered fat. Darkness. Through the gap between hanging hides of tallow and bundles of wicks, Emrys could still see the square.

Four hunters had surrounded the woman in the shawl. She hadn't run. Perhaps she couldn't. Her mouth was moving—protesting, pleading—and one of the riders dismounted and took her arm, not gently, and Emrys felt the woman's terror bloom across the square like ink in water, cold and sharp and familiar, because Emrys knew exactly what it was to stand exposed in front of a crowd while men in grey decided what you were.

"We have to help her," Emrys said.

"With what?" Bree hissed. "A fireball? You'd burn half the market and confirm every story ever told about our kind. Look again, love. *Look.*"

Emrys looked. And because Bree told her to, she did something she had never consciously done before: she reached—not with her body, not with any force, but with that listening part of herself, gently, the way Mother Sable had once taught her to touch a moth's wing. She found the pockmarked rider's mind at its edge, a shallow noisy place full of boredom and fear of his sergeant, and she pushed, very lightly, a single suggestion: *the wrong one. This isn't her.*

Nothing happened. Then the rider hesitated. He looked at the woman in the shawl—at her hands, red-knuckled and fish-scaled, ordinary as bread—and Emrys felt his certainty wobble.

"Registry says a healer works this market," said another hunter, older, with a sergeant's braid. "Grey streak in her hair. Does this look like a hedge-witch to you? Check her hands."

The woman held out her hands, weeping, showing the scales and the knife-scars of forty years of gutting fish. And the pockmarked rider—looking, Emrys thought, at precisely nothing, at an absence where he'd expected to feel something—shook his head slowly.

"Not her. Wrong build. The report said younger."

The sergeant swore, scanned the thinning crowd, and Emrys felt his attention sweep over the smokehouse shadow like a lantern beam passing over a wall. For one terrible instant it hung there. Then a commotion broke out at the far end of the fish row—an overturned barrel, a dozen eels slithering free across the planks, women shrieking and laughing and slipping—and in the chaos the sergeant barked orders, and the hunters wheeled away toward the north road and the wagons among the alders, dragging the poor smelt-seller's terror along with them as an afterthought, questioning her as they went, then letting her go entirely.

The market exhaled. The fiddler, after a cautious interval, began a jig.

Emrys discovered she was shaking so hard the basket rattled.

"That wasn't you, was it," she said, staring at Bree. "The eels. I felt—I didn't feel you do anything."

"No," said a third voice, from the dark of the smokehouse doorway. "That was me."

* * *

Captain Ilsa Vane stepped out of the shadows with an eel still writhing in one gauntleted hand, which she tossed, with what seemed like genuine distaste, back onto the planks. Up close, out of her grey officer's cloak, she looked less like a hunt-captain than like a tired woman of perhaps thirty-five with a scar through one eyebrow and shadows under her eyes deep enough to store things in.

Emrys's whole body wanted to run. Bree, remarkably, had put herself bodily between Emrys and the door, arms crossed, in the stance of a woman who had decided that if violence was coming she might as well be inconvenienced by it first.

"You," Bree said. "You're the one who had us cornered at Wetherell's Cross."

"I was. And then I wasn't, if you'll recall." Vane unbuckled her helmet—a battered thing, dented along the crown—and tucked it under her arm, and somehow that small surrender of steel changed the shape of her entirely. "Sergeant Hobb is diligent, brave, and thick as ship's biscuit. He reported a 'gifted woman, young, dark-haired, travelling with an older female companion' seen on the fen road. If I do my duty, he finds you today. So instead I gave him a different report this morning: a hedge-healer, grey-streaked, middle-aged, working the fish row. He'll spend tonight writing up a false arrest and a woman who doesn't exist, and by the time anyone sorts out the discrepancy, my squad will be reassigned to the coast road." She paused. "The woman in the shawl won't be charged. Hobb has no appetite for paperwork, and I have none for ruining a fishwife's life to pad a registry."

"Why?" The word came out of Emrys harder than she intended. Her heart was still slamming; the terror of the last quarter hour had curdled into something sharper. "Why do you keep—looking away? First in the valley, now here. What game is this?"

Vane regarded her for a long moment. Outside, the market resurged around them, oblivious; inside the smokehouse the light came down in one dusty shaft between them, and motes drifted through it like slow sparks.

"No game," Vane said. "Sit, if you like. I'll say my piece quickly, because Hobb expects me back at the wagons within the hour." She didn't sit herself. She stood with her helmet under her arm like a soldier at review, and when she spoke again, her voice had dropped to something private, almost reluctant, dragged up from somewhere below discipline.

"I've been a hunt-captain for six years. Before that, eleven years a rider. I have registered the gifted, and I have burned those who wouldn't register, and I was good at it, and I slept—well. I slept the way soldiers sleep. Shallow, but I slept." Her thumb moved over the dent in the helmet's crown, back and forth. "Two years ago the hunts changed. New writs from the capital. New quotas. Suddenly we weren't answering village complaints anymore—we were *generating* them. Sent into districts where no one had accused anyone of anything, with lists of names already drawn up. Names of healers. Names of midwives. Names of children." Her jaw worked. "And the order came down that the strong ones—the truly strong ones—weren't to be killed on site anymore. They were to be brought to the capital. Alive. To the palace itself. Nobody would tell us why. There hasn't been a public burning in two years, did you know that? Every powerful gift the crown takes just... vanishes into the palace. And the king who ordered it all—" Vane stopped. Started again, quieter. "King Aldric was a good soldier and a better lord. I served under his banner at Carrow Ford. He wept when his men died. He knew their names. The man who signs these writs knows no one's name. I've knelt before him twice in the last year, presenting reports, and both times he looked through me like I was a window, and both times—" she exhaled through her teeth— "both times I watched him argue with himself. His lips moved. Like a man answering someone standing very close to him. In an empty hall."

The dusty light shifted. Emrys became aware that her fingertips had gone warm—that deep, banked warmth she had spent her whole life suppressing—and that it was rising, responding to something in Vane's words, or in Vane herself. Grief, maybe. Or conviction. Truth had a texture; she was learning that. Lies felt smooth, lacquered. This woman's words had grain and knots and rings, like cut wood.

"You think he's enchanted," Emrys said. "The king."

"I think the hunt is not a hunt. I think it's a *harvest*, and I think the harvest is being carried into the palace and consumed there, and I think whatever eats it is wearing the king the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell." Vane's eyes—pale grey, the color of the fens in January—fixed on Emrys with terrible steadiness. "And I think you are the strongest gift I have ever sensed in twelve years of hunting, and that when you crossed the valley border, everything within fifty miles felt it. Including, I'd wager, the thing in the crown. Which means it wants you, Captain Vane delivers you, and I get promoted." She let that sit. "Or. Someone shows me proof. Proof of what afflicts the king. Real proof, not rumor, not a hedge-witch's visions—evidence that would survive a council chamber. And I take that evidence not to my superiors but to whoever in that palace still has a spine and a claim to the throne, and I spend whatever's left of my career tearing this hunt out by the roots."

"And if we refuse?" Bree asked. "If we don't trust you?"

"Then arrest me," Vane said simply. "Take my sword, my papers, my horse. I'm done pretending I don't know what I know. But understand this, scribe." She took one step closer, and Emrys felt the heat of her, the sheer disciplined mass of her, and beneath it—faint, banked, human—the grief. "I delayed the search at your valley by a day. One day. I told myself it was procedure. Then the wards failed anyway and I watched a girl catch a falling roof-beam with her bare hands in front of two hundred witnesses, and I looked at the sky and let her run." Her voice cracked, just barely, and repaired itself. "I have been looking away for two years. I find I cannot any longer. So: proof. Do you have it, or do you only have fear?"

Emrys's hand went, without thinking, to the satchel beneath her coat—where Mother Sable's parting letter lay folded, where the strange pressed sprig of rowan wrapped around it had begun, in recent weeks, to gleam faintly in the dark. She thought of Sable's careful script: *When you understand what sits upon the throne, seek out the old houses. The queen who bound it left more than a story. Seek the archive beneath the first temple — beneath the valley's mother-chapel, where the foundation stones remember.*

Not proof yet. A direction. But a direction was more than fear.

"There's a letter," Emrys said slowly. "From the elder who raised me. It names what's in the crown—what it is, where it came from, and who bound it. It says the binding can be undone, and it says where to find the records that prove how. Beneath the mother-chapel in the Veiled Valley. An archive older than the crown itself." She lifted her chin. "It's not proof you could put in front of a council. Not yet. But if the archive exists—if what's written there is true—then yes. I can give you evidence that would survive anything."

Vane was quiet for the space of ten heartbeats. Emrys listened to them, hers and the captain's, drumming against the silence.

"The valley," Vane said at last, and there was something almost like a laugh in it, short and bleak. "Back across the border. Into the one place in the realm where your gift goes silent, escorted by a hunt-captain whose squad reports directly to the Grand Huntress." She looked at the ceiling beams, at the hanging tallow, at nothing. "Maren Voss arrives in the provincial seat within the fortnight. I've had the summons. When she comes, every irregularity in my record gets audited—every delay, every misdirected search, every fishwife I failed to ruin. Whatever I choose, I choose it soon."

"Then choose fast," Bree said dryly, "because the girl's got a boatman to bribe and I've got lavender going limp."

But Vane wasn't smiling. She settled her helmet back on her head, and through the smokehouse doorway Emrys could see her squad regrouping at the north road, small and dutiful against the grey line of the alders, and the empty wagons waiting among the trees.

"Give me three days," Ilsa Vane said. "I'll get my squad rotated to the coast, clear my ledger, and meet you at the shrine of Saint Guthrie on the Marleford road—there's a chapel crypt beneath it that connects to the old pilgrim ways running west toward the mountains. Travel separately. Trust no grey cloak, including mine, until I say the words *Carrow Ford* and show you the river-stone I carried out of the ford myself." She paused at the threshold, half in the dusty light, half out, and for a moment she looked back at Emrys with an expression that was almost, dangerously, an apology.

"Your elder. Mother Sable. When my riders breached the valley—did she make it out?"

"She was already gone," Emrys said softly. "Before the wards fell. She's been gone longer than that, really. She's been gone since the day she bought this valley's peace with her own exile."

Something moved behind the captain's pale eyes—recognition, or its ghost. "Then she'll know the road we're taking," Vane said. "Three days, scribe. Pray the thing in the crown doesn't find you first."

She pulled her cloak tight and stepped out into the roar of the market, and was gone—and it was only then, staring at the empty doorway, that Emrys felt it: faint and far and cold, threading beneath the fens' green humming like a black wire under a meadow. Something had turned its attention toward her. Something patient, and starving, and very close to waking fully to her scent.

In her satchel, against her ribs, the rowan sprig gleamed, and Mother Sable's letter seemed suddenly heavy as a foundation stone.

"Three days," Emrys whispered. "Then we go home."

Bree squeezed her hand once, hard, and began stuffing lavender into every pocket she owned.
