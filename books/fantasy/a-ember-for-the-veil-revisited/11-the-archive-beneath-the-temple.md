## 11. The Archive Beneath the Temple

The letter had been waiting for her all along, Emrys realized—folded into the lining of her travel cloak, sewn there by Mother Sable's own careful hands, and she had worn it across half the kingdom without knowing.

Bree found it first, actually. She had a habit of taking Emrys's cloak down from its peg and brushing it clean each evening, muttering about road dust and moths, and on the third night in the hedge-witch's crooked little house outside Marrowfen she went still with her needle halfway through a seam.

"Emrys." Her voice had gone very quiet in the way it did when the kettle was about to boil over or when a stranger's shadow crossed the window. "Come look at this."

The paper was temple vellum, soft as moth wing, and the handwriting was Mother Sable's—the same patient, unadorned script that had copied out ten thousand warding verses over forty years.

*Emrys. If you are reading this, you have left the valley, and the valley has failed to keep you safe. Forgive me for sewing this where you would not find it too soon. Some doors should only be opened by someone already running.*

*You will hear things, out there. Whispers about what sits on the throne. The elders called it the Hunger Below the Crown, though that was never its true name—we lost the true name before my grandmother's grandmother was born. What I can give you is where to look. Under the old temple in Corvane's Reach, beneath the third step of the sunken stair, there is a door that opens only to ink freely spilled.*

*Do not go alone. Do not go at all, if you can help it. But I know you, child, and I know you will go.*

*One thing more, and hold it close: whatever they tell you about how the binding was made, remember that every binding is also a bargain, and every bargain has a price that someone loved enough to pay.*

*Your mother would be proud of you. So am I.*

*M.S.*

Emrys read it four times. On the fourth reading her hands were shaking hard enough that Bree took the letter from her and set it flat on the table between the bread board and the jars of dried nettle, weighting the corners with two river stones and a spoon, as if it might otherwise fly away.

"Corvane's Reach," Bree said. "That's six days east, if the ferries are running. It's where the old temple used to stand, before the crown built the new one over its bones." She tapped the line about ink freely spilled. "That part I like. That part sounds like something a scribe could manage."

"Bree." Emrys heard her own voice come out thin. "A starving god under the crown of the king. Bound by—" She couldn't finish it. The last lines of the letter kept turning over in her mind like a stone in a stream: *a price that someone loved enough to pay.* "Bound by what?"

Bree looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the fen frogs had begun their evening argument, and the light through the smoke-yellowed window was going the color of weak tea.

"Well," Bree said finally, reaching for her shawl. "I expect we'd best go find out."

* * *

Corvane's Reach had been a holy city once. Now it was a market town with pretensions, its old processional avenues given over to fish stalls and farriers, but the bones of its sanctity still showed through everywhere—in the way the streets curved inward toward a center no longer marked by anything except a fountain, in the door lintels carved with faded sunbursts, in the way people still lowered their voices without quite knowing why when they passed the great blank wall where the old temple's west face had been bricked up three centuries ago.

The new royal temple stood above it on the hill, white and smug, hung with banners bearing the crowned sun of Aldric's house. Emrys looked at those banners a long time from the ferry deck, and something in the base of her skull ached, a deep toothache feeling she had learned to distrust.

"It hears us," she said quietly. "Doesn't it. When we talk about it."

Bree followed her gaze up the hill. "Everything loud enough gets heard," she said. "So we'll whisper. And we'll hurry."

They lodged that night in a rooming house run by a widow who asked no questions because Bree paid in dried feverfew tinctures and the widow's knees were bad. Emrys slept badly, dreamed of a garden she could not quite see, of a voice singing a lullaby whose melody dissolved whenever she reached for it. She woke with wet cheeks and no memory of why, and lay staring at the ceiling beams while dawn came gray through the shutters, wondering—not for the first time since crossing the veil—what else her gift was doing to her besides waking.

*Things will feel your waking,* Mother Sable had warned. But nobody had warned her that waking worked both ways.

* * *

The old temple was not entirely dead. Its crypts had been sealed, its relics dispersed or melted down during the Purges of the Interregnum, but the sunken stair Mother Sable described still existed—a flight of steps behind the bricked-up west wall, descending into a flooded cellar that local children dared each other to enter and always turned back from at the third step. The third step. Of course.

It was raining, which helped; rain emptied streets and filled silences. Bree carried a shuttered lantern and a satchel of things Emrys chose not to inventory. Emrys carried the letter, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, and an inkpot, stoppered tight, borrowed from Bree's kitchen shelf.

The stair descended past the first step into cold air that smelled of stone and standing water. Past the second step, the sound of the rain died as though a door had closed on it. At the third step, Emrys felt it—a pressure against her skin like walking into spider silk, invisible and fine, stretched across the whole width of the passage.

She stopped. Beside her, Bree had gone rigid, the lantern trembling slightly in her grip.

"You feel that?" Bree whispered.

"Yes."

"That's a warding older than anything I've ever stood inside. Older than the veil itself, maybe." Bree swallowed audibly. "Ink freely spilled. You're sure you want to—"

"No," Emrys said honestly. "But Mother Sable said I'd go anyway, and she knew me better than I know myself right now."

She knelt on the third step, uncorked the inkpot, and poured it out onto the stone.

The ink did not pool. It ran—uphill, impossibly, threading along grooves in the step that Emrys had taken for natural wear, filling them, tracing lines that spiraled outward and downward and met in a design too large and too intricate to take in from so close: a circle within a circle within a circle, and at the heart of it, characters in a script older than any Emrys had been taught to copy, though her fingers twitched with the strange recognition of a hand that almost remembered how to write them.

The pressure of the warding did not break. It *opened*—the way a hand opens—and beyond the silk-touch of it, the darkness ahead exhaled. Cold air moved up the stair, carrying a smell Emrys had known all her life in the scriptorium: old vellum, lamp oil, dust, patience.

"Oh," Bree breathed. "Oh, it's real."

At the bottom of the stair, where the floodwater should have been, there was dry stone, and lamplight that burned without flame in niches along the walls, and shelves—shelves rising into the dark farther than the lantern reached, loaded with books and scrolls and tablets of baked clay, an entire library buried alive beneath the feet of a city that had forgotten it.

* * *

They walked among the shelves like trespassers in a cathedral. Neither spoke. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been an interruption.

Emrys had spent her whole life in the Veiled Valley's scriptorium, and she had thought she understood what a collection of texts was: order, repetition, safety, ten thousand copies of the same warding verse in ten thousand identical hands. This was not that. This was chaos preserved. Arguments. Contradictions. Whole histories written and then annotated by later hands who disagreed violently in the margins. Here a tablet recording grain shipments from an empire whose name Emrys had never heard; there a scroll of star-charts with constellations drawn wrong—or drawn as they had been before some slow catastrophe rearranged them. Here a shelf of children's primers. There a codex bound in iron, chained shut, its lock rusted open long ago by some earlier reader who had either finished with it or fled it.

"The elders knew about this," Emrys said at last, and her voice came out rough with disuse. "Mother Sable knew. They've always known. The valley, the veil—it wasn't just hiding gifted children. It was keeping people away from *this*."

"Or keeping this away from people," Bree said. She ran her fingers along a shelf edge, leaving a line through the dust, and looked at it the way she looked at symptoms. "Somebody built this place to be found by exactly one kind of person. Ink freely spilled, Emrys. Who pours out their ink? Scribes do. People who love the work enough to waste it."

The letter again. Emrys drew it out and read it by the flameless lamplight, and this time she noticed what grief had blurred before: the way certain phrases sat in the sentences like stones in a stream—*third step*, *ink freely spilled*, *a price that someone loved enough to pay*. Not instructions. A cipher-key. Mother Sable hadn't just told her where to come; she had told her how to read what she found.

They found the heart of the archive by following the lamplight, which grew denser as they walked until all the little flames converged on a round chamber at the center of everything, walled floor to ceiling with a single continuous book—an enormous tome, taller than a man, its pages each the size of a table top, mounted on some mechanism of brass and stone that allowed them to be turned one at a time, like the pages of a saint's life illustrated for pilgrims. Only one page was currently displayed, and it was very old, the vellum stained amber, the illumination flaked to ghosts of gold.

Upon it, in a hand that shook with either age or terror, was written:

*Herein is set down the truth of the Crowned Hunger, as witnessed by me, Alarice, fourth of that name, Queen of Corvane, in the year the sky wept ash.*

And below it, an illumination that had survived the centuries better than most: a figure robed and crowned, kneeling before a second figure that had no shape anyone would call kindly—a mass of reaching, hollow forms, mouths and hands and eyes crowded together in a hunger with no bottom to it, drawn in inks that seemed, even now, even faded, to shift when Emrys was not looking directly at them.

"Turn the page," Bree whispered.

"I don't think we get to turn it," Emrys said slowly, studying the brass mechanism. "I think it turns for us. When it decides we're ready. Or—" she touched the page's lower corner, gently, "—when it decides we've earned it."

The page stirred under her fingertip. Just barely. Like something sleeping, dreaming of movement.

They read what was legible of the displayed page for a long time, piecing the faded text together, and piece by piece the horror assembled itself in front of them.

It had come from outside the world—not from beyond the veil, which was younger, but from somewhere the old cosmologies didn't name, a place of pure appetite. It had fallen on the kingdom in the years of the ash-sky like winter falling on a field. It did not raze cities; it *emptied* them. It fed on minds—on memory, on love, on the bright interior furniture of being a person—and it grew fat on kingdoms until a queen named Alarice found, carved into the ruins of an older civilization, the terms of a binding.

Not a prison. A bargain.

The entity could not be destroyed; destruction would only scatter it into a thousand smaller hungers. It could only be *bound*—held fast in a vessel small enough to contain, and held there by a tether woven from the one thing it could never generate for itself and therefore coveted above all things: love freely given, memory freely surrendered. The binder must offer up their dearest recollection of love—not lose it to accident or age, but *give* it, knowingly, willingly, at the height of its sweetness—and the act of giving became the chain.

Queen Alarice had done it. The text recorded it in eleven surviving words, and those eleven words were worse than all the rest:

*She gave the memory of her son's first laughter.*

Below it, in a later hand, someone had added a note, cramped and furious:

*The vessel must be borne by a living wearer. The binding renews only while the bearer lives and the gift remains unpaid. Every bearer dies still holding it. Every bearer hears it. Every bearer, in time, begins to feed it small things—scraps, leavings—until it learns the taste of the mind that carries it. There is no record of any bearer freed. There is no record of any bearer who wanted to be, by the end.*

Emrys stepped back from the page. The chamber seemed colder now, though the lamps burned on, indifferent.

"Aldric," she said. "He's not just losing his mind. He's the *vessel*. He's been carrying it since his coronation, feeding it scraps without knowing—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "And the hunt. The witch-hunt. Bree, the entity engineered it, didn't it? Flushing out strong gifts, gathering them together, because—"

"Because it's hungry," Bree said grimly, "and a king's crown is a long way from enough anymore. It wants a feast." She was quiet a moment, and then, in a smaller voice: "Emrys. Look at the terms again. Love freely given. Memory freely surrendered. The dearest one there is."

Emrys looked. The words had not changed. But she had changed around them, the way the landscape changes when a storm front comes over the hills—nothing moved yet, and everything was different.

"And every attempt killed the giver," she said. "If there's more in here about the rite—"

The page turned.

Neither of them touched it. The vast vellum leaf swung over on its brass arm with a sound like a ship's sail filling, and settled, and the new page was newer—only a century old, perhaps less, its ink still black, its illumination vivid enough to hurt. It showed a woman in a scholar's robes standing before this very mechanism, and beside her, rendered small at the margin's edge, a second figure: a scribe at a desk, bent over her work, a thread of golden light rising from her fingertips like smoke.

The new page bore a title, and a warning, and beneath the warning, a list. Emrys read the title first:

*On the Unbinding of the Crowned Hunger: Rites, Requirements, and the Four Recorded Attempts.*

Then the warning, underscored twice:

*Let no reader mistake the nature of the cost. The gift does not merely take the memory. It takes the self that memory held up. Four givers have attempted the unbinding. Four givers have died within the hour, empty as cracked bells.*

And beneath that, the list, in a neat column: names, dates, outcomes. Three entries, ancient, their details lost to water damage. And a fourth entry, recent, its ink sharp:

*Attempt the Fourth: Maren Voss of Corvane's Reach, Huntress-Commander, who gave the memory of her sister's voice. Outcome: death, as foretold. Note appended by the archivist: the rite itself succeeded. The Hunger was loosed for nine days before it was rebound by [text lost]. Let it be recorded that the ninth day was called, in the chronicles of that sorrowful season, the Weeping Week, and let no one undertake this rite lightly, nor without heirs to what they leave behind.*

The name landed in Emrys like a dropped stone hitting still water.

"Maren Voss," she repeated. "Bree. The Grand Huntress is named Voss. Ilsa said—Ilsa told us the Grand Huntress was coming to purge the palace herself. What if it isn't a purge? What if Voss knows exactly what's in that crown, because her own blood tried to cut it out once and died paying for it?" Emrys's thoughts were moving faster than her mouth could carry them, skidding, catching fire. "What if she's not coming to destroy the king. What if she's coming to finish her ancestor's rite—and she doesn't care who she empties to do it?"

Bree had gone pale in the lampless light. Slowly, she reached out and touched the painted figure of the scribe in the margin—the small bent woman with the golden thread rising from her fingers.

"Emrys," she said. "Look at the archivist's hand. Look at the note about the fourth attempt. Someone wrote that within living memory, here, in this room." She lifted her eyes. "Someone has been keeping this archive. Recently. And whoever it is—they drew *you*. Before they ever could have known you existed."

The lamps flickered, all together, as if the archive had drawn breath.

And from somewhere deeper in the dark—farther down than the stair they had entered by, farther down than any cellar ought to go—a sound reached them. Soft. Patient. Almost warm.

Footsteps.
