## 12. An Audience Nobody Granted

The letter had come wrapped in oilcloth inside a basket of winter pears, and Seraphine had read it four times before the candle burned down to a stub.

*Your father speaks to empty rooms. He burns letters from friends he has known since boyhood. The hunt is not his—it is what wears him. I know what sits upon his head, and I know how it can be unmade. Grant me an hour and a servant's dress, and I will prove both.*

No signature. Only a small inked sigil at the bottom: a veil drawn back from a crescent moon, the mark of the temple scriptorium in the Veiled Valley, which Seraphine recognized because her childhood nurse had once worn it on a pendant she was never permitted to explain.

That pendant had been listed among Marta's effects when the registry notice came. Seraphine had burned the notice rather than let it sit in her writing desk like a splinter working toward the heart.

Now she stood at the postern gate in the grey hour before dawn, holding a lantern she did not need—she knew this path by feel, every loose stone of it, from the years when sneaking out had been the only rebellion available to a princess with no army and no allies—and waited for strangers who claimed to know what was killing her father.

The gate warden had been paid to be elsewhere. The corridor beyond smelled of wet stone and tallow. And when the two women came up out of the dark—one broad-shouldered and freckled, moving with the easy confidence of someone who had never been afraid of anything; the other slight, hooded, carrying herself with the careful stillness of a deer that had learned what dogs were—Seraphine felt her first flicker of doubt.

"You're the scribe." It came out as an accusation. She hadn't meant it to.

"I'm Emrys Kavanagh." The woman pushed back her hood. She was younger than Seraphine expected—younger even than herself—with ink-stained fingers and eyes that seemed to be listening to something beneath the floor. "And you're Princess Seraphine, though I suspect you'd prefer I didn't say that part loudly."

"Prefer?" Seraphine laughed, and heard how brittle it sounded. "I would prefer, Mistress Kavanagh, that my father were not ill. That three hundred people were not in the hunt registries. That I had not spent two years being formally received by a man who looks through me as though I were furniture that used to belong to my mother. But we are all preferring things tonight, aren't we? So you'll forgive me if I don't extend much courtesy."

The freckled woman—Bree Ashdown, she'd said, hedge-witch, said with a defiant little lift of the chin—snorted. "She's like this with everyone," she told Emrys. "It's not personal."

"It might be slightly personal," Emrys murmured.

"Come inside," Seraphine said, because dawn was coming and standing in doorways discussing treason was how conspiracies died young. "Both of you. And if either of you is a trap, I should tell you that I have very little left worth taking."

* * *

Her apartments occupied the east wing, chosen years ago precisely because they were far from the king's chambers and close to the servants' stairs—the geography of a daughter who wanted access without proximity. She locked the outer door herself, drew the curtains against the brightening sky, and only then turned to face them properly.

"Prove it," she said. "You wrote that you could prove what afflicts the king. Do so."

Emrys didn't flinch. She crossed to the window instead, and held her palm flat toward the curtain, and Seraphine watched the fabric move—not from any draft, but in slow, deliberate ripples, like water responding to a hand passed over a pond.

"The palace hums," Emrys said quietly. "Did you know that? Old stone remembers. There are wards in these walls older than your dynasty, laid by hands nobody records anymore. And threaded through all of it—" her fingers curled closed, and the curtain stilled, "—there is a hunger. It comes from above us. From the throne room. From whatever your father wears on his head during council."

"The crown." Seraphine's voice came out flat. "You're telling me my father is mad because of the crown."

"I'm telling you your father is *at war* because of the crown. There's a difference. Madness surrenders. He hasn't surrendered. Not yet." Emrys turned to face her fully, and there was something in her expression—not pity, worse than pity, *recognition*—that made Seraphine want to step backward. "I've felt what it feels like to have something living inside you that you didn't invite. What he's carrying is larger than anything I can imagine. But the shape of the suffering—I know that shape."

Seraphine sat down. She hadn't decided to; her legs simply ended their argument with her.

"Marta," she said. "My nurse. Her name appeared on the registry eight months ago. Executed within the month. She used to sing songs about the old queens, songs nobody else knew. She wore a temple sigil she wouldn't explain. Was she—"

"Mother Sable smuggled gifted children across the valley border for thirty years," Bree said gently. "If your Marta came from the Veiled Valley, then yes. She was one of ours."

*Ours.* As if Seraphine had been handed membership in something by virtue of grief alone. She looked at her hands—at her father's hands, really, the same long fingers, the same pale knuckles—and thought of the last time he had truly looked at her. Eighteen months ago. He had gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise and whispered, *don't let them crown anyone else*, and then his eyes had gone distant again, and she had told herself it was illness, decline, the slow rot of a tired mind.

She had told herself a great many convenient things.

"The hour you asked for," she said at last. "You'll have it. But not today, and not as a stranger. If you're going to stand in front of my father, you'll do it as my lady's maid, introduced through channels, seen by the household for a week first. His mind may be besieged but his instincts aren't—they were trained into him before you or I were born. A strange face appearing at odd hours will wake something in him that the entity can use."

Emrys nodded slowly. "You've thought about this."

"I've had two years and nothing else to think about." Seraphine rose, went to the wardrobe, and pulled out a plain grey dress, serviceable wool, the kind of thing a moderately paid lady's maid would wear. She thrust it at Emrys along with a linen cap. "Your name will be Wren. You'll be quiet, competent, forgettable. Can you manage forgettable?"

A ghost of a smile touched Emrys's mouth. "I was a temple scribe for eleven years. Forgettable is my native language."

"And you?" Seraphine turned to Bree.

"Oh, I'm nobody's maid." Bree settled into the window seat as though she owned it. "I'll keep the lodgings you find me, learn the city, and be ready to run interference when this goes sideways. Because it will go sideways, princess. Things like this always do."

"Then we're agreed on one expectation, at least."

* * *

The week that followed was the strangest of Seraphine's life, and she had once survived a diplomatic visit from the Ferranese delegation, which involved six days of poisoned small talk and one actual poisoning.

Emrys—Wren—was everything promised. She brushed Seraphine's hair in silence each morning, laid out clothes, carried messages, and moved through the palace corridors with such perfect unremarkability that guards stopped seeing her within two days. But at night, in the safety of the locked apartments, she would sit by the fire and describe what she felt: the pulse of wards in the foundation stones, the way the entity's hunger thickened near the council chamber, the faint wrongness in the air above the throne room like heat shimmer over a road.

"It's growing stronger," she said on the fifth night. "Feeding. Every person the hunt takes, every gift it devours—it's gorging itself. Your father is holding it back on will alone, and will is a finite resource, Seraphine. We don't have seasons. We may not have months."

"Then we go sooner." 

"Tomorrow, then," Emrys said. "He holds private audience on seventh-days. No council, no ministers. Just him and whoever petitions him."

"He hasn't granted a private audience in a year."

"He's going to grant this one." Emrys met her eyes across the firelight. "Because you're going to request it, and he's going to look at his daughter's name on the petition list, and some part of him—the part still fighting—is going to want to know why."

* * *

The audience chamber was smaller than Seraphine remembered, or perhaps it only seemed smaller now that she understood what lived above it. Her father sat beneath the empty coronation canopy—he no longer wore the crown except for formal occasions, she noticed, and wondered if that was Aldric resisting, or the entity conserving itself—and he looked, in the grey morning light, like a portrait of himself left too long in the sun.

"Seraphine." Her name in his mouth sounded like a word in a language he was forgetting. "You petitioned. You haven't petitioned me since—"

"Since Mother's funeral. Yes."

Something moved behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe, or its ghost.

"I've come to ask a boon," she said, following the script she and Emrys had rehearsed until it wore smooth. "My new maid, Wren. She's from the Veiled Valley, and she's copied warding texts her whole life. She believes she can help with your... condition. The headaches. The nights you lose."

Silence stretched. In it, Seraphine watched her father's face do something terrible and beautiful: struggle. His jaw tightened. His hands gripped the armrests. For one suspended moment he was entirely present, entirely her father, and his voice when it came was low and urgent:

"*Don't bring her here. Whatever you're planning, the thing in the crown will feel her, it's been starving, it will—*" And then his gaze slid sideways, and his shoulders dropped, and the man looking at her was a courteous stranger wearing her father's face. "Forgive me. Lost my thread. You were saying—your maid?"

"Wren," Seraphine said, and gestured.

Emrys stepped forward from the shadow of the doorway, and the temperature of the room changed. Seraphine felt it—a pressure, a gathering, like the moment before lightning—and saw her father's nostrils flare, saw his hands clench, saw something ancient and ravenous turn its attention through his eyes toward the young woman in the grey dress.

"Your Majesty," Emrys said calmly, and dropped into a curtsy that was flawless and utterly insubordinate in its composure. "I've come to discuss the crown."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. Four. Five.

When Aldric finally spoke, his voice had layers in it, like a bell struck with something wrapped in cloth—her father's tone underneath, and something else resonating above it, vast and cold and terribly interested.

"*Scribe,*" it said, through her father's mouth. "*We know your kind. We ate your kind while your grandmother was learning letters.*"

"Yes," Emrys said pleasantly. "But I'm not here for you to eat. I'm here to tell you that I've read the archive. I know about the queen who bound you. I know the terms of your binding. And I know"—she took one deliberate step forward, and Seraphine's heart seized—"that you cannot refuse a freely given gift. Those are the old terms, aren't they? The ones you agreed to when you were still capable of wanting anything?"

The thing in the crown went very, very still.

And in that stillness, in the space between one breath and the next, Seraphine saw her father surface—saw Aldric's own eyes widen, saw his lips form a single silent word that she read clearly despite having never been taught it:

*Run.*

Then the doors burst open behind them, and the guards came in, and the audience nobody had granted became, in a single terrible instant, an arrest nobody could stop.
