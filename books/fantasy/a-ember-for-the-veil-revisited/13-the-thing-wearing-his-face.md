## 13. The Thing Wearing His Face

Aldric had learned to count the warning signs the way a sailor learns to read a bruising sky.

The first sign was always the cold. It began at the base of his skull, a thin blade of frost sliding down between his vertebrae, and spread outward until his fingertips felt like someone else's fingers attached to someone else's hands. The second was the silence in the room—the way candle flames stopped guttering, the way the rustle of parchment and the shuffle of boots all seemed to hold their breath, as though the air itself were listening for something. The third sign, the one he dreaded most, was the taste. Copper and old smoke, like biting down on a coin pulled from a fire.

All three came upon him that morning in the council chamber, while Lord Chancellor Hesse droned through the grain tariffs and rain lashed the high windows of the palace.

*Not now,* Aldric thought, gripping the armrests of the throne's lesser chair—the council chair, oak and iron, built for a king who still wished to be a man among men rather than an icon above them. *Not today.*

But the thing in the crown did not negotiate. It had never negotiated. In the early years—God, had it truly been nineteen years?—he had mistaken its presence for counsel. A voice at his ear during long nights of statecraft, offering clarity when his own mind fogged with grief and wine. *Your enemies are gathering,* it would whisper. *Act before they do.* And he had acted, and the kingdom had prospered, and he had told himself the whispers were only his own sharpened instincts wearing a stranger's cadence.

By the time he understood what rode behind his eyes, it was too late to cut it out. He had tried. There was a scar beneath his hairline from the night he'd taken a knife to his own scalp in the dark of his bedchamber, sobbing, half-mad, certain that if he could just reach far enough—

The crown had taken the knife from him. With his own hand. He remembered watching his fingers bend against his will, remembered the thing's amusement rolling through him like oil through water.

"—and so the southern levy stands at four hundred bushels short, Majesty, though I've petitioned the granary masters twice." Hesse looked up from his papers. "Majesty?"

The chamber tilted. The candles burned without flickering. Aldric counted the faces around the long table: Hesse, gray-bearded and patient; Marshal Corven, whose loyalty was to the office of the crown more than the man; Lady Vessing of the Exchequer, who never met his eye; two secretaries taking minutes near the wall. And Seraphine.

She sat at the table's far end, where she had insisted on sitting since her return to the palace—a distance she maintained like a fortress wall, formal and unbreachable. She had come back three weeks ago with no explanation and no apology for six years of absence, and every day since she had watched him with those green eyes of her mother's, sharp as awls, missing nothing. She had seen him speak to empty rooms. He knew she had. A maid had told him—or had the thing told him, planting the knowledge to keep him afraid? The boundaries had rotted through long ago. He could no longer be sure which fears were his own harvest.

"Majesty," Hesse said again, gently.

"I heard you." His voice came out wrong. Flattened. As if spoken through a wall of ice. "Four hundred bushels short. Note it, and note also that the granary masters will answer for it before winter."

He felt it then—the pressure behind his eyes, the slow turning of something vast in the deep water of his mind. It had been restless for days. Restless since the reports began arriving from the fen country: a scribe who healed a dying man with her bare hands, a hedge-witch sheltering her, hunters circling and somehow always arriving a step too late. Vane's reports had grown strange—terse, evasive, threaded with gaps. The thing had noticed the gaps. It fed on strong gifts the way a leech feeds on blood, and somewhere out in the rain-soaked city, it could feel what it wanted. Aldric could feel it feeling. Like standing beside a starving wolf and hearing its stomach turn over.

*She is close,* the presence said, in the wordless way it spoke, meaning pressing directly into his thoughts. *Close enough to taste.*

"Then we are adjourned on the levy," Aldric said aloud, and heard how thick his tongue had become. "We'll resume—"

"No," said the thing, using his mouth.

The word dropped into the chamber like a stone into a well. Every head turned. Seraphine's pen stopped mid-stroke.

"We are not adjourned," Aldric heard himself continue, and the voice was his voice but the music of it was wrong, pitched low and resonant, each syllable placed with a precision no living throat needed. "There is other business. There is a scribe in this city. Unregistered. Ungifted on no registry, and gifted beyond all registries. She is to be found. She is to be brought here."

Hesse blinked. "Majesty, the hunt is already—"

"The hunt is *slow*." The thing leaned forward in Aldric's body, and Aldric felt his own hands flatten against the table, felt the wood creak under a grip stronger than his arms had any right to be. "Corven. Your riders. Where are they stationed?"

"Mostly at the gates, Majesty. The fen district, some—"

"Pull them in. Seal the river quarter. If the scribe breathes in my capital tonight, I will know of it within the hour, or I will know why not." A pause, delicate as a needle. "And the captain. Vane. Her last three reports conflict. Bring her to me as well. Gently, if she permits it."

Lady Vessing had gone pale. Even the secretaries had stopped writing. Aldric screamed inside his own skull, threw himself against the walls of his prison—and the prison was seamless, warm, shaped exactly like himself. That was the cruelest architecture of it. No bars to rattle. Only his own flesh, worn by another.

*You may watch,* the thing said, almost kindly. *You have always been permitted to watch.*

Seraphine rose from her chair.

It was the first time in three weeks she had stood in his presence without being dismissed first, and there was something in the motion—not defiance exactly, but a kind of terrible readiness, as if she had rehearsed this moment in the dark of her rooms. Later, Aldric would understand that she had. Later he would learn about the registry, the nurse's name, the letters burned. But in that moment he only saw his daughter standing in the rain-gray light, her jaw set in the exact line her mother's used to set, and his heart broke cleanly in two inside the cage of his stolen body.

"Father," she said. "That's enough."

The thing turned his head slowly, savoring it. "Princess. You have opinions on the hunt?"

"I have opinions on *you*." Her voice did not shake. "On whatever has been giving orders in your name for the past year. On the executions. On the registries. On the letters you burn, and the conversations you have with no one, and the way you look at people as though you're reading them for flaws." She took one step closer. "I don't know what you are. But you are not my father, and I am done pretending otherwise."

The silence afterward was absolute. Rain wrote its long gray sentences down the windows. Somewhere below, a door slammed in the servants' passage.

And the thing smiled with Aldric's mouth.

"There she is," it said softly. "There is the spine. Your mother had it too. Do you know what became of your mother's spine, little princess? I held it in my hand, in a manner of speaking, the night she—"

"*Enough!*" Seraphine's composure cracked at last, and what showed beneath was not fear but fury, white-hot and six years old. "Do not say her name. Whatever you are, whatever you've done to him—you do not get to wear her memory like a trophy."

"You mistake me." The thing rose, and Aldric's body rose with it, tall and unhurried, and the candles guttered now, finally, as if even flame flinched. "I do not wear memories. I *keep* them. I keep everything this family has surrendered to me. Nineteen years of confessions whispered into pillows. Nineteen years of this king's secrets, which are, by ancient covenant, mine." It moved around the table, and the councilors shrank back, chairs scraping. "And I find, princess, that I am owed a new confession. Yours."

"I owe you nothing."

"You owe me obedience. Your father bound this house to me when the crown touched his brow. Kneel, and order your guards to ride for the scribe, and perhaps I will let you keep the parts of yourself you love best." The smile widened, and Aldric felt the muscles of his face stretch into an expression he had never once made in his life. "Or refuse. I confess I hope you refuse. Refusal ripens things."

"Seraphine," Aldric managed—and it cost him everything, every scrap of will he had hoarded like a miser's coin through nineteen years of captivity—to force three words up through his own throat. They came out strangled, barely human: "*Run. Get out.*"

Her eyes snapped to him. For one heartbeat, father and daughter looked at each other across the ruined space of twenty years, and he saw her understand—not everything, not yet, but enough. Enough to go pale. Enough to plant her feet instead of running.

"No," she said—to the thing, to the room, to him. "I'm not leaving him alone with you."

"Foolish," the thing said, almost tenderly. "Brave and foolish. Your mother was brave and foolish too, at the end."

It reached out Aldric's hand.

Aldric felt the power gather before the movement completed—that deep, tidal pull, the sensation of the crown drinking from some well that had no bottom. The air over his palm darkened, bent, curdled. Around the table, Lady Vessing screamed. Corven lunged forward shouting words that dissolved before they finished. And Seraphine—his daughter, his stubborn, grieving, magnificent daughter—did not run. She grabbed the heavy inkstand off the table and hurled it at his head.

The thing caught it without looking. Crushed it. Ink sprayed black across the floor like a wound opening.

"You resist," it observed. "Good. Resistance has flavor."

The darkened air contracted into a fist. It closed around Seraphine's throat and lifted her off the ground, and Aldric felt her windpipe under his own fingers, felt the flutter of her pulse hammering against his palm, and something in him went past screaming into a place beyond language. *No. NO. Not her. Take me, take the rest of me, burn whatever's left, but not her—*

"She dies," the thing mused, tilting its head as Seraphine clawed at nothing, her face purpling, her heels drumming the air, "unless the scribe comes to me. Consider it an invitation, king. Consider it a *summons*. She can feel me as I feel her. Let her choose how much of this child's death sits on her conscience—"

And then Aldric stopped fighting like a prisoner.

He had spent nineteen years fighting like a prisoner—rattling bars, hurling himself at walls, exhausting himself in rage. But Mother Sable's letter had reached him two days ago, smuggled through channels the thing had not thought to watch, an old woman's careful hand telling him what he already half-knew: *the binding can be broken, Majesty, by a memory freely given, and there is one coming to you who understands the terms.* He had not believed it. Hope was a luxury the thing punished. But belief was not what mattered now. What mattered was the truth underneath the letter, the truth he had known since the first night the crown sang to him:

The thing fed on surrender. All of it. Every kind.

So Aldric did not fight. He *opened*.

He tore open every door in his mind at once—all the sealed chambers, the buried griefs, the locked room where Marguerite's laughter lived, the vault where he kept the memory of carrying a newborn Seraphine through the palace gardens at dawn, her fist wrapped around his finger, the whole world reduced to that small grip. He flung it all wide like a man throwing open the gates of a burning keep, and he poured himself through the breach—not as a battering ram but as a flood, every ounce of love and shame and stubborn human mess he possessed, straight down the channels the thing had opened to feed.

It gagged on him.

For nineteen years it had sipped him through a straw. Now he gave it the ocean. The pressure on Seraphine's throat stuttered. The darkened air shuddered, thinned, and his daughter dropped to the floor, gasping, coughing, alive—alive—and the thing reeled backward through Aldric's body, staggering him against the table, and for the first time in nineteen years Aldric felt his own weight settle back into his own bones like rain soaking into drought-cracked earth.

His hand. His hand was his. He raised it, trembling, and pressed it to his own chest, and felt his own heart slamming there like a fist on a door.

"Aldric—" Seraphine rasped from the floor, ink-black tears where her eyes watered. "Father—"

"Not yet." His voice was ruin, gravel and blood. "Listen—both of you—there isn't time. It's gathering itself. When it comes back—" He laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and awful. "When it comes back, I won't be able to do that again. That was everything. That was the last of me."

"Father, what is happening to you? What is *in* you?"

"The crown." He sagged into the great chair, and the room swam—councilors frozen at the walls, Hesse white as tallow, Corven with his sword half-drawn and no enemy to point it at. "The crown, Seraphine. It has been the crown for nineteen years. The hunt, the registries, the executions—it built them all. It is farming this kingdom for gifts to devour, and it knows the one it wants is close, and it will burn the city down to reach her." He gripped the armrest until the wood groaned. "There is a scribe. Emrys Kavanagh. Whatever you've heard, whatever you believe—she is the only chance I have left, and I have spent my last strength proving it to you."

"Where is she?" Seraphine was on her feet now, swaying, rubbing her throat, and her eyes had gone from fury to purpose in the space of a breath. "Tell me where she is and I'll bring her here myself."

"You can't. The hunt is hunting her. My hunt—" He coughed, and tasted copper. The cold was returning at the base of his skull, patient, inevitable, a tide that had only gone out to gather force. "No. Listen. There's a woman named Bree Ashdown, a healer, and a captain called Vane who has turned her coat—I think, I pray. Find them. Find the scribe. Tell her—" His voice cracked, and he let it. What remained of his dignity could rot. "Tell her the king of Veles begs her. Tell her an old man in a gilded cage is begging her. Tell her I will give anything—"

The frost slid down his spine. The candles stilled.

*Enough,* said the thing, in the deep water where it lived. *You have spent yourself, little king. Now I will spend you.*

Aldric felt his body begin to rise without his consent, joint by joint, like a puppet finding its strings. Through the window, the rain was easing. Through the door, he could hear running footsteps in the corridor—Seraphine's guards, summoned by the screaming, or perhaps already hers, already turned. Through the fading bond of his own final act, he could feel, distantly, impossibly, a warmth moving through the city below. A thread of gold in all that gray. Coming closer.

*She's coming,* he thought, as his hand began to lift against his will, as his mouth began to shape words not his own. *God forgive me. God help her. She's coming.*

The doors of the council chamber burst open.

The thing wearing Aldric's face turned to look at her, and smiled.

"Well," it said, in the king's ruined voice. "The scribe arrives at last. Come in, Emrys Kavanagh. We have been waiting for you since before you were born."
