## 14. Terms of Binding

The archive beneath the old temple had no windows, and Emrys had begun to think of it as a place outside time. Three days they had been coming down—Seraphine with her armfuls of candle stubs, Bree with her satchel of bread and cheese and bitter herbal tea, Emrys herself carrying nothing but the growing weight of what she read.

The queen's private records were kept in a niche behind a false wall of shelving, and the books there were not books in any ordinary sense. They were bound in leather gone soft as cloth, their pages the color of weak tea, and the ink had faded to ghost-gray in places, as though the words themselves were tired of being remembered. Seraphine translated the old court script; Bree read the marginalia, the cramped notes of hedge-healers and unlicensed cunning-folk who had passed these pages hand to hand for centuries like contraband. And Emrys read everything twice, because the first reading never felt real enough to trust.

It was on the third evening, by the light of eleven guttering candles, that she found the rite itself.

She knew it was the rite before she understood a single word of it. The page radiated a kind of gravity. The script was older than the court hand Seraphine had been untangling all week—older, perhaps, than anything Emrys had copied in the valley's scriptorium—and it was written in two colors of ink: black for the spoken words of the ceremony, and red for the terms. The red ink was darker in some places, brownish, as though it had been mixed with something other than gall and wine.

"Here." Her voice came out strange, flattened. "This is it."

Seraphine set down her transcription and came around the table to look over Emrys's shoulder. Bree put down the heel of bread she'd been worrying at and wiped her fingers on her apron, an unconscious habit, as if she were about to handle something that could be stained.

Emrys read aloud, slowly, translating as she went, because saying the words in the old tongue made them worse somehow, and she wanted them plain:

*"To unbind what was bound in hunger, offer what was bound in love. Let one who holds a memory of profound and cherished devotion surrender it freely—not taken, not stolen, not bargained for, but given whole, as a gift is given, expecting nothing in return. The starving god must accept the gift or starve forever. It cannot refuse true love freely offered. This is the only key."*

The candles flickered. Somewhere above them, water dripped through old stone, patient as a clock.

"That doesn't sound so bad," Bree said at last, and her voice was carefully light. "A memory. People lose memories all the time. Fevers take 'em. Falls take 'em. My gran couldn't remember my grandfather's face for the last ten years of her life and she still laughed at his jokes."

"Read further," Emrys said quietly.

She turned the page. The red ink thickened here, dense as dried blood.

*"Let the giver understand: the memory is not lifted from them like a coin from a purse. It is unraveled. And the thread runs through the whole garment. What is surrendered is the moment—but what dies with it is everything the moment touched. The love will be unknown to them after. Not forgotten. Unknown. As if it had never lived in them at all. The heart will carry a wound it cannot find, grieving something it cannot name.*

*"And let the giver understand this also: the binding drinks deep when it is fed. In every recorded attempt, the giver has died. Their heart, emptied of its dearest thing, forgets how to beat. Four times this rite has been attempted in the long centuries. Four givers. Four graves."*

Bree said nothing now. Seraphine's hand had come to rest on Emrys's shoulder without either of them noticing, and her grip had gone tight.

"Four attempts," Seraphine said. "Four deaths."

"The first was the queen herself," Emrys said. She had found the account earlier that day and hadn't yet told the others, and now it spilled out of her like water through a cracked dam. "Maren—no, not our Maren—a different name, Queen Isolde. She bound the entity into her own coronation crown three hundred years ago. It had been devouring the countryside—whole villages emptied, not killed, *emptied*, people walking around with nothing inside. She performed the rite using her memory of—" Emrys stopped. Swallowed. "Her memory of her son. He'd died young, and she gave up every moment she'd ever had of him. Every one. And the binding held, but she lasted nine days afterward. The record says her heart simply... stopped mid-laugh. She was laughing at something her fool said. And then she wasn't."

The silence that followed was the worst kind—the kind that has understanding in it.

"So it's not just dying," Bree said finally, very quietly. "It's dying hollow. You give up the best thing you ever had and then your body decides there's no point going on."

"It doesn't have to be that way," Emrys said. "Look—" She pulled another book toward her, one of the healer's marginalia volumes. "Here. A cunning-woman in the reign of Aldric's great-grandfather wrote that the four deaths weren't the rite's doing. They were grief's doing. The rite takes the memory. What kills you is standing in the space where it used to be, alone, with no one to—" She faltered. "With no one to help you learn how to live around the hole."

"But no one's ever survived to test it," Seraphine said. It wasn't a question.

"No one's ever survived to test it," Emrys confirmed.

* * *

They argued about it the way families argue—which is to say, badly, and with love, and with everyone talking past everyone else while meaning exactly the same thing.

Bree wanted to do it herself. She said her life was half-spent anyway, that she'd already lost her wife Marta to the hunt twelve years ago and the memory of her was the most precious thing she owned and therefore the most worthy offering. "I've had twelve years to wear smooth the edges of missing her," she said, jabbing a flour-dusted finger at the page. "I know how to live around a hole. I'm *practiced*."

"And I'm telling you it's not the same," Emrys shot back. "You wouldn't remember that you'd lost her. You wouldn't even know there was an edge to wear smooth. You'd just be—" She groped for the word. "*Empty.* Walking around with a shape cut out of you and no idea what shape it was."

"Then someone would tell me stories," Bree said stubbornly. "Like I'd tell them for you."

"I'm not letting you die in my place, Bree Ashdown. Don't you dare make me watch that too."

Seraphine sat apart from them through most of it, turning her father's signet ring around and around on her smallest finger. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet enough that they both fell silent to hear it.

"My mother died when I was seven," she said. "My earliest memory of her is her teaching me a song in the palace garden. I can't sing it anymore without crying, so I don't sing it. But I would give it." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and fierce. "If it comes to that. If there's no other way. I want it known that I would give it. I won't argue with either of you about who loves him more or who owes what. I'm telling you so you know it stands."

"Bree and I are arguing because we love each other," Emrys said gently. "That's what arguing is, in a family."

Something moved across Seraphine's face at the word *family*—a flicker, quickly mastered, of a girl who had spent fifteen years eating breakfast alone in a palace of servants.

"We're getting ahead of ourselves," Bree said at last, scrubbing a hand through her curls. "We don't even know if the rite can be done here. We don't know what we'd need. Candles? Circles? Blood?"

"Salt and iron and a freely given memory," Emrys recited from the page. "And the crown present, and both parties willing—the binder and the bound. The king must consent. That's written here too. The entity can't be torn out against his will; it's grown too deep. He has to open the door himself."

"Then we need my father to agree to be saved," Seraphine said grimly, "which means we need my father lucid, which means we need to get to him before the thing in his head eats whatever's left of his agreement-making. Which brings us to the actual problem, which is time."

As if summoned by her words, boots sounded on the archive stairs—heavy, rapid, military boots—and a moment later Captain Ilsa Vane filled the doorway, still wearing her riding leathers, dust on her shoulders, her breath coming fast from the stairs. Her eyes swept the room, catalogued the candles and the scattered books and the three faces turned toward her, and settled on Emrys with an expression Emrys had learned to read as Ilsa's version of panic.

"They're coming," Ilsa said without preamble. "The Grand Huntress. Maren Voss. She left the garrison at Redford two days ago with forty picked hunters and a writ of purgation signed under the king's seal—or the thing wearing his seal. She arrives within the week."

"A writ of purgation," Seraphine repeated, rising slowly. "For whom?"

"For the palace." Ilsa stepped fully into the room and let the door swing shut behind her. "Every soul in it. Voss has read my reports—I made them careful, but she's not careful, she's *thorough*, and thorough people notice when reports contradict each other. She suspects I've gone soft. More than suspects. And she's noticed what I noticed months ago—that the hunt's orders keep pointing inward. Toward the crown itself. Toward anyone with power strong enough to feed what's living in that crown." Her jaw tightened. "She isn't coming to investigate, Highness. She's coming to cleanse. She believes the king is already lost, and she intends to burn the nest out—throne, court, and everyone in it. Including, I'd wager, any scribe recently smuggled into the household."

The candle flames leaned, though there was no draft.

"So we have a week," Bree said.

"You have less." Ilsa unbuckled her sword belt and laid it on the table among the ancient books—an act of surrender so casual it took Emrys a moment to understand its weight. "Because once Voss seals the palace, no one gets in or out, and whatever we mean to do, we'll be doing it inside a hunting ground. I burned my commission tonight. I'm done pretending to be one of them. From this hour I'm yours—yours," she nodded at Emrys, "and the princess's, and the old woman's. So." She looked around the table, at the red-inked page lying open between them, at the four graves' worth of warning written in what might have been blood. "Tell me what it costs. All of it. I want to hear the price named out loud before I help pay it."

Emrys looked down at the rite. Salt and iron and a freely given memory. A thread running through the whole garment. Four givers, four graves—and one cunning-woman's note, scrawled three hundred years ago, insisting the deaths were grief's work, not the rite's. One untested hope, thin as a candle flame in a doorway.

"The price," Emrys said slowly, "is the best memory of love you have. Given away forever. To a god that eats such things. And maybe—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—maybe everything else besides."

Ilsa nodded once, as though Emrys had quoted a supply inventory. But her eyes went, briefly and involuntarily, to the small scar on the back of her own hand—a scar Emrys had seen her thumb tracing in unguarded moments, and whose origin none of them knew.

"Then we'd better choose fast," Ilsa said, "who can afford to pay it."

Above them, faint through layers of stone, the palace bells began to mark the midnight hour—and beneath their bronze voices, so soft that only Emrys, with her waking gift, could hear it, something far below the city began, very quietly, to laugh.
