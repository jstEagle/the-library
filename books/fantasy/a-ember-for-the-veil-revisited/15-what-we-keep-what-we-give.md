## 15. What We Keep, What We Give

The archive beneath the old temple had no windows, and so the night came to them only as a slow accumulation of candle stubs and cold. Bree Ashdown felt it in her knees first—she was fifty-three years old, and stone floors had opinions about women her age—and then in her hands, which had stopped trembling somewhere around midnight and now simply ached with steadiness, the way they did after long surgeries.

She had spent the evening doing what she always did when the world grew too large: she made tea. The archive's little brazier took her dented copper kettle without complaint, and she moved among the shelves of the dead queen's hidden library like a woman in her own kitchen, setting cups before scholars who had forgotten to eat.

Emrys had not touched hers.

The girl sat cross-legged on the floor amid a drift of parchment, her dark hair coming loose from its braid, her eyes red-rimmed and fixed on nothing. She had been fixed on nothing for two hours. Bree knew that look. It was the look of someone standing at the edge of a high place, testing the wind.

Seraphine sat opposite her, still in her maid's disguise though the pretense had been abandoned days ago, the princess's spine straight even on a floor cushion, as if royalty were something carried in the vertebrae. Ilsa Vane stood by the door with her arms crossed—not guarding it, exactly, but positioned between it and everyone else, which for Ilsa amounted to the same posture of care.

And Aldric—the king, though he had asked them twice now to stop calling him that in these rooms—slept in the corner chair, truly slept, his face slack and human and terribly old. The entity had been quiet since Emrys's near-attempt three nights past. Quiet, Bree thought, the way a wolf is quiet when it has found the soft place in the fence.

"Say it again," Seraphine said quietly. "All of it. I want to be sure we've understood."

Emrys's voice came out flat, worn smooth like a pebble handled too long. "The rite requires a memory freely given. Not surrendered under compulsion, not traded, not stolen back by force. Given. A memory of profound love, offered whole, with full knowledge of what is being lost." She turned a page she had already read a dozen times. "And every recorded attempt killed the giver. Four queens' archivists tried it over three centuries. All four died. The binding takes the love and the life together, because—" her throat worked, "—because the entity feeds on love. That's what it is. A starving thing that eats love. You cannot feed it a crumb. It takes the loaf and the hand that holds it."

The candle flames leaned, all of them at once, though there was no draft. Nobody remarked on it. They had all learned not to remark on such things.

Bree set down the kettle.

She had been thinking about this since the second hour, turning it over the way she turned a difficult diagnosis, looking for the angle where the truth became bearable. There wasn't one. So she would do what she had done her whole exiled life: skip past bearable entirely and go straight to necessary.

"Then it can't be Emrys," she said.

Every head turned. Emrys looked up slowly, and Bree watched the girl begin to understand, watched understanding arrive like cold water rising, and pressed on before anyone could stop her.

"It can't be her because she's twenty-two years old and her whole life is ahead of her, and because whatever else is true, the valley needs her, and the king needs her, and—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—and I need her, if we're being honest, which apparently we are tonight. But it also can't be Seraphine, because a kingdom doesn't survive its queen dying before she's crowned. And it can't be Ilsa, because Ilsa is the only person alive who knows how Voss thinks, and come morning we'll need every scrap of that."

"Bree." Emrys's voice cracked on the name.

"And me," Bree said, "I'm fifty-three. My joints ache. My wife has been dead eleven years, and the memory of her is the finest thing I own, and I have already lived most of the life that memory belongs to." She smiled, and found, to her surprise, that the smile was real. "My life is half-spent, love. Let me spend the best part of it well."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the kettle to cool.

It was Emrys who broke it, and she broke it standing up, unfolding from the floor with a speed that scattered parchment like startled birds.

"No."

"Bree—" Seraphine began.

"No." Emrys's gift stirred; Bree felt it the way she felt weather coming, a pressure behind the ears, a taste of iron. The candles flared. "No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to sit there and make tea for everyone and then quietly hand over the only thing you have left of Marta—"

"Maren," Bree said gently. "Her name was Maren."

"—Maren," Emrys corrected, wrecked by the correction, "and expect us to just—accept it? Because you're older? Because you've decided your life counts less?"

"I haven't decided any such thing." Bree kept her voice level, the voice she used for frightened patients. "I've decided my death shouldn't count more than my living. There's a difference."

"There isn't!" Emrys's composure tore clean through. "You think I don't know what you're offering? You think I haven't spent every waking hour since we read that passage imagining giving up my mother—her voice, her hands, the garden, all of it—and do you know why I haven't said it aloud? Because saying it makes it possible. And you just said yours. You just stood there and said it like you were telling us the price of eggs."

"Because it is the price," Bree said. "Of everything. Of Aldric, of the hunt ending, of Seraphine's father coming back to her, of you getting to grow old in a world that isn't burning gifted children. I have weighed it, Emrys. I have been weighing it since the archive opened. And I find—" and here her voice did fail her, briefly, and she let it, because hiding it would have been a kind of lie, "—I find that I can bear losing her again. I have practice. You don't. That's not weakness on your part. It's evidence of mine."

Ilsa spoke from the door, low and rough. "She's right about one thing. Whoever does this dies. We should want the volunteer whose loss costs the living least."

"The least," Emrys repeated, turning on her, and Bree saw the girl's magic rise with her grief, threads of pale light crawling along the shelving like frost blooming in fast-forward. "You'd rank our loves? Put them on a scale? Whose heart weighs less on your hunt-captain's ledger?"

"Yes," Ilsa said simply. "That's exactly what I'd do. It's what I've done my whole career. It's why I burn people's names off lists in secret and hate myself in public. Somebody has to do the arithmetic, scribe, or everybody dies feeling noble."

"Then do the arithmetic properly," snapped a new voice, hoarse with sleep.

Aldric had woken. He rose from the chair unsteadily, one hand braced against the wall, and in the candlelight Bree could see how far the entity had hollowed him—the temples sunken, the eyes ringed like bruises, a man built of scaffolding. But his voice, when it came, was still a king's.

"You are all arguing over who dies," he said. "None of you has asked whether the rite can be survived. Look again at your texts. The four who died—how did they die?"

Bree blinked. "The accounts say the entity... refused to release them. It held on. It drank until—"

"Until the giver had nothing left," Aldric finished. "Until the love ran out mid-gift, and the taking became tearing." He crossed the room slowly, painfully, and laid one shaking finger on the oldest scroll, the queen's own hand. "But read her marginalia. Read what she wrote about the binding she performed—the successful one, the one that cost her a memory and not her life. She writes: *the god cannot refuse a true gift, but neither can it hold what is freely released. The danger is in the holding. Give quickly. Give completely. Do not cling.*"

"He's right," Emrys whispered, pulling the scroll toward her, scanning. "Look—here—she gave the memory and walked away within the hour. The ones who died lingered. They tried to watch, to hold on, to keep a thread of it—"

"They hesitated at the door of their own hearts," Aldric said, "and the thing in my crown ate them for the delay."

"So it can be survived," Seraphine breathed. "If the giver truly lets go."

"If," said Bree.

They looked at each other across the candles, five people and a sleeping god, and Bree felt the shape of the night change around them—from an argument into something worse: a plan.

* * *

They argued until the third candle burned down to a puddle, and then lit another, and argued past that too.

Seraphine argued law and lineage—that a princess could not ask a commoner to die for her father's crown, that the debt was the throne's and must be paid from the throne. Bree told her thrones didn't bleed, people did, and the girl-who-would-be-queen went white and silent and did not concede.

Ilsa argued tactics—that Bree was needed alive, that hedge-craft couldn't be replaced, that the circle Emrys would need for the rite required a trained healer at its center or Emrys's own gift would burn her out from the inside. This was true, and Bree hated her a little for knowing it, and loved her a little for saying it anyway.

Aldric argued guilt, quietly, in the corner—that the crown had been fed by his surrender, decade upon decade of small concessions, and that the debt was his and he would pay it himself if his memories hadn't already been picked over like a carcass. "There is almost nothing left in me that it hasn't touched," he said. "Whatever love remains is compromised. It would take a tainted gift and call it payment." No one could refute him. No one wanted to agree with him either.

And Emrys argued last, longest, and worst of all—worst because she was right and knew it and could not make it land.

"You told me once," she said to Bree, kneeling in front of her chair, gripping her hands hard enough to hurt, "in your kitchen, the first week, when I couldn't sleep for hearing the land sing—you told me about Maren. How she used to sing while she rendered tallow, badly, on purpose, to make you laugh. You told me that story like it was bread you were breaking. You don't get to give away the person who taught you how to tell stories. That's not sacrifice, Bree, that's amputation, and I will not stand in the operating theater and hand you the knife."

Bree looked down at their joined hands—the girl's fingers ink-stained and burning-warm with leashed power, her own spotted and scarred from thirty years of needles and knives—and felt the terrible tenderness of being loved by someone young.

"Oh, my dear," she said. "Do you think I don't know what I'm offering? I have rehearsed losing her every day for eleven years. Every morning I wake and remember she's gone, and every morning I survive it. One more remembering—one final, complete one—is not amputation. It's..." she searched for it, "...it's finishing a letter I've been writing since she died. And you—" her thumb traced the ink stains, "—you have a mother you cannot grieve yet because you haven't begun to lose her. If you give that memory, you won't even know what was taken. You'll wake hollow and never know the shape of the hole. At least I'd know. At least I'd get to choose my emptiness."

"That's not fair."

"No," Bree agreed. "None of this is. Fairness left the room the day the riders entered your valley."

Emrys's grip tightened. "Then hear my counter-offer. Not tonight. Not decided by exhaustion and candlelight. We sleep—we actually sleep—and tomorrow we look for a third path. The queen bound it alone, desperate, with half the knowledge. We have an archive, a king who knows the thing from inside, a captain who knows the hunters, a princess who owns the palace, and a witch who refuses to accept that the books are the whole truth. Give me one day to find a way that costs no one everything."

"And if there is no third path?" Ilsa asked.

"Then we decide together," Emrys said. "Not whoever volunteers fastest. Together. Because that's what a family is, Bree—I've read about them, and I've seen one forming in this cellar, and families don't feed each other to wolves to spare each other the watching."

The word *family* landed in the room like a struck bell.

Bree felt it in her chest, in the place where Maren used to live, and understood suddenly that she had made her offer not only from courage but from fear—fear that if she didn't spend herself first, she might yet lose these new ones too, and her heart had simply preempted the market.

"Tomorrow," she said at last, grudging as a bad patient. "One day. But Emrys—if the day ends where the night began, we put it to a vote, all five of us, and no one overrides the rest. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Emrys, and Seraphine, and after a moment Ilsa, and finally, softly, the king.

* * *

They bedded down among the shelves like refugees, which is what they were. Seraphine draped her cloak over Aldric. Ilsa took the door. Emrys curled near the cold hearth with a scroll still clutched in her hand, asleep in seconds, young enough to manage it.

Bree stayed awake.

She banked the brazier and sat with her cup of cold tea and let herself do the thing she had forbidden herself all night: she remembered. Deliberately. Whole.

Maren in the doorway with rain in her hair, laughing at some joke Bree had already forgotten the words of but never the sound. Maren's hands, strong from the loom, braiding Bree's hair badly on festival mornings. Maren at the end, thin as winter, saying *you'll keep going, you always keep going, it's the most infuriating thing about you.*

Eleven years of mornings, surviving it.

She was still sitting there, cup in hand, when the candles guttered all at once—and did not go out. They burned small and blue and bent toward the sleeping king, all five flames leaning like courtiers bowing, and in the deep dark of the archive Bree heard, very faintly, from the direction of the throne room far above:

Singing.

Badly. On purpose. In a voice that belonged to no one present.

The entity had been listening to their arguments all night—and it had learned, from Bree's own guarded thoughts, exactly which memory was on the table.

Bree set down the cup with a steady hand, woke Ilsa with a touch, and said the words she would regret having ready:

"We don't have until tomorrow anymore."
