## 16. The Grand Huntress Comes

The ravens came first.

Ilsa Vane had learned, over eleven years of hunting, to read the sky the way farmers read clouds. A single bird meant weather. A pair meant a message. But a wheel of them—six, seven, circling the palace's eastern spire with the agitated purposefulness of carrion birds finding something already dead—meant authority. Meant someone important was coming to look at what she had done.

She stood at the window of her quarters in the hunters' wing and watched them settle, one by one, along the frost-rimed guttering, and she felt the particular cold that had nothing to do with the season slide down between her shoulder blades.

Maren Voss.

She had known it would come to this eventually. Reports were like footprints; leave enough of them and even a fool could follow the trail, and Voss was no fool. Ilsa's dispatches from the capital had been careful—careful as a surgeon's knife—but careful in the wrong places. Too much detail about the fen market's confusing alleys. Too little about the scribe who had slipped through her fingers twice. A report that said *nothing* too loudly was its own kind of confession, and somewhere in the last fortnight she had crossed the line from cautious into conspicuous.

Below, in the courtyard, the gates were already opening. Not for Voss herself—not yet—but for her outriders, four riders in the Grand Huntress's slate-grey livery, their horses steaming in the morning cold. Behind them, far down the causeway, dust hung in the air like a held breath. A full company. Maybe two.

Ilsa dressed slowly. She buckled on her sword with hands that did not shake, because she had trained them not to, and she looked at herself in the small cracked mirror above the washstand—a woman of forty-one years, grey coming in at her temples like frost on a fence post, eyes that had watched too many pyres—and she asked herself the question she had been carrying since the valley, since the collapsing beam, since the moment a scribe's hands had glowed like banked coals and Ilsa had turned her face away and let it happen.

*Which side of this do you want to die on?*

She still didn't have an answer. But she was running out of time not to choose.

* * *

Voss arrived at midday, and the palace changed around her the way a household changes when a hard winter walks through the door.

Ilsa met her in the outer courtyard, among the banners and the stamping horses, and made her salute with every appearance of steadiness. Maren Voss dismounted without assistance, which at fifty-eight years old said everything about her. She was not a large woman. She was not an imposing one, either—no towering presence, no theatrical menace. She was small and neat and dry as old paper, with white hair cropped close to her skull and pale eyes that went through people the way an auditor goes through ledgers: patiently, thoroughly, and without any expectation of finding honesty.

"Captain Vane." Her voice was quiet. It always was. Men shouted; Voss never needed to. "You've been busy."

"The hunt continues, Grand Huntress."

"Mm." Voss looked past her, at the palace, at the windows, at the guards on the walls, cataloguing. "Walk with me."

They walked. Through the courtyard, up the steps, into the great hall where courtiers parted before the Grand Huntress like water before a keel. Ilsa matched her pace and waited. With Voss you waited. She would get there when she got there, and rushing her was like rushing a headsman.

"Your reports," Voss said at last, as they passed beneath the gallery where the old kings frowned down in painted oil, "have been interesting reading."

"I hope accurate."

"Oh, accurate. Accurate to the letter." They climbed the stairs toward the council wing. "That's what interests me. Do you know what I look for in a report, Captain?"

"Honesty, Grand Huntress."

"Fear." Voss stopped at the top of the stairs and turned those pale eyes on her. "A hunter who has lost her quarry writes afraid. Excuses. Blame. Weather, terrain, treachery. Your reports have no fear in them at all. They are calm. They are tidy." She smiled, very slightly, without warmth. "Tidy is what a report looks like when the person writing it already knows how the story ends."

Ilsa held her gaze. Eleven years of holding gazes across interrogation tables had taught her that much, at least: the face is a door, and doors can be locked from either side.

"The quarry escaped me twice," she said. "Once in the valley, once at the fen market. I reported both failures plainly."

"You did. And in neither report did you mention—" Voss resumed walking, her boots soft on the stone, "—that your own squad noted your order to search the tannery row *after* the fugitives had already been seen heading east. Or that you interviewed the scribe privately by the fish stalls, alone, for the better part of a quarter hour, and then wrote three lines about it."

The cold slid deeper. So. Someone had talked. Of course someone had talked. Squads were made of people, and people were made of fear, and fear always found its way uphill.

"She was cooperative," Ilsa said. "There was nothing to report."

"There was a great deal to report. You simply chose not to." Voss pushed open the door to the council chamber—the same chamber where, days ago, the king had stood rigid as a corpse while his daughter bled from a wound no one could explain—and gestured Ilsa through ahead of her. "Sit."

Ilsa sat. Voss remained standing, moving along the long table, trailing her fingers over the backs of the chairs like a woman greeting old acquaintances.

"Now," she said, "you will tell me the rest. And Captain—I will know if you lie, not because I am clever, though I am, but because you have been lying for weeks and you are tired of it. Tired men make poor liars. Their stories develop seams."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the shutters and, faintly, from somewhere below, the ordinary noise of a palace that did not yet know it was about to be sealed like a tomb.

And Ilsa Vane, who had spent eleven years perfecting the flat truth and the polished lie, heard herself say:

"What do you know about the king's health?"

Voss's fingers stopped on the back of a chair.

* * *

It was, in retrospect, the only card she had left, and she played it badly—or perhaps not badly, perhaps honestly, which amounted to the same thing against Voss. She told her about the council session. About the king's voice changing, the wrongness of it, the way he had ordered Seraphine arrested and then seemed to wake inside his own body like a man surfacing from deep water. About the blood on the princess's arm and the king's face when he saw it—horror, real horror, horror with nothing kingly in it at all.

She told her about the archive beneath the old temple. About the starving god. About the rite.

She told her most of it, and watched Voss listen, and understood within minutes that she had made a catastrophic miscalculation.

Because Voss didn't recoil. Voss didn't scoff. Voss listened the way a physician listens to symptoms, nodding occasionally, and when Ilsa finished, the Grand Huntress folded her hands behind her back and said:

"Yes. That accords with my own findings."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree.

"Your—"

"I have served the crown for thirty years, Captain. Twenty-two of them under Aldric's father, six under Aldric himself. I knew something was wrong with the king before you did. I have watched him decline for two years. I have read the same histories you apparently found in that archive." Voss began to walk again, slow, thoughtful. "Where we differ is not in the diagnosis. It is in the treatment."

"The treatment—"

"A god bound in a crown. Feeding on gift. Growing stronger with every witch we burn—you understand now why the hunt intensified, why the quotas rose, why I was ordered to sweep whole districts? It was never justice. It was husbandry." Voss's voice remained perfectly level, and that was the worst thing Ilsa had ever heard. "The entity wants gifted ones flushed into the open so it can feed. Very well. Then the answer is not to stop the hunt, Captain. The answer is to give it a feast so magnificent that we can take the whole rotten thing apart while it gorges itself."

Isla felt the words land in her stomach like stones. "What feast?"

"The strongest gift in the kingdom." Voss turned, and for the first time there was something almost like pleasure in her dry face. "The scribe. Emrys Kavanagh. A girl whose power woke violently after a lifetime dormant, who healed a dying man by accident, who slipped through *your* net twice—which tells me she is stronger than your reports admit. We deliver her to the throne room. We let the thing in the crown feed until it is drunk and slow. And then we burn it out of him with fire and iron and whatever relics the Vault can produce. Crude. Costly. But certain."

"And the girl dies."

"The girl dies," Voss agreed, without inflection. "Along with, possibly, the king. Possibly half the court. Sacrifice is the price of certainty, Captain. You of all people know that. How many pyres have you lit?"

"That was different."

"It was exactly the same. You simply hadn't done the arithmetic honestly until now." Voss came around the table and stood over her, small and neat and terrible. "Here is what happens next. By nightfall this palace is sealed. No one enters or leaves without my seal. The princess will be confined to her apartments—for her protection, naturally; she has been consorting with fugitives and I will want to know everything she knows. The scribe and her allies will be found. And you, Captain Vane, will write me a full account of your contacts with the enemy, which I will weigh against your years of service, and which may yet save your neck."

She moved toward the door. Paused.

"You hesitated just now," Voss said, without turning. "When I said the girl dies. A fraction of a second. Don't imagine I missed it. Get your affairs in order, Captain. Whatever you decide to be—decide it tonight."

The door closed behind her. Ilsa sat alone in the council chamber with the painted kings frowning overhead and her heart going like a drum in a procession, and she understood that the choice she had been deferring since the valley had just been taken out of her hands and set on the table in front of her, still bleeding.

* * *

She burned her commission papers at dusk, in the small iron stove in her quarters, one page at a time.

It was not a dramatic act. There was no speech, no gesture worthy of a ballad. She fed the documents in piece by piece—the warrant signed in the old king's hand, the commission renewed under Aldric, the commendations, the authorizations, eleven years of legal permission to do what she had done—and she watched the seals curl and blacken, and she thought about the first pyre she had ever lit, a boy of nineteen in the fen country who had healed his sister's fever and wept the entire time they tied him to the stake, and she thought: *this paper made that lawful. This paper was the law.*

The law was ash. Good.

Then she took a fresh sheet, and a pen, and she forged a quarantine decree.

It was the strangest hour of her life. She had Voss's seal—stolen an hour earlier from the Grand Huntress's field desk while Voss inspected the gatehouse, a theft accomplished with the same hands that had once picked the locks of hedge-cottages looking for hidden charms. She had the format of a quarantine order memorized; she had written dozens. Plague protocols gave her the language: sealed gates, suspended courts, restricted movement, all under pretext of a suspected contamination in the water cisterns. It would not hold forever. It might hold a day. Perhaps two, if the garrison captains were lazy and the winter roads were bad, and Voss's reinforcements were still strung out along the causeway.

A day. Two. Enough hours for a scribe, a hedge-witch, a princess, and a dying king to attempt a rite that had killed everyone who ever tried it.

Ilsa read the finished decree twice, blotted it, and laughed—one short, ugly bark of sound at herself, alone in her quarters, a hunted woman now, an outlaw, a traitor, free.

* * *

She found them in the old nursery suite, of all places—Seraphine's idea, a room no one had entered in twenty years, tucked behind the chapel where the walls were thick and the servants had forgotten the corridor existed. Bree Ashdown had a kettle going on a brazier. Emrys sat wrapped in a blanket by the window, thin and watchful, and Seraphine stood over a table strewn with archive pages, and King Aldric—God help them all—sat in a child's chair by the cold hearth with his head in his hands, whispering to himself in a voice that was sometimes his own.

Four faces turned when Ilsa opened the door. Three of them showed fear. The fourth—Bree's—showed something more like resignation, as if she'd been expecting the knock to come with shackles.

"Ilsa?" Seraphine stepped forward, hand drifting toward the knife at her belt. "What's happened?"

Ilsa set the forged decree on the table. Set her sword belt beside it. Set the ashes of her commission, carried in a small tin box, beside that.

"Voss arrived at midday," she said. "She knows about the entity. She's known for years. She doesn't want it unbound—she wants to feed it. She means to use Emrys as bait, fatten the thing until it's glutted, and then burn it out with the palace around it. The king dies. Emrys dies. Half the court probably dies." She looked around the room, at each of them in turn, and heard her own voice go flat and steady, the voice she'd used to give orders across eleven years of terrible work. "I've bought you time. Quarantine decree, her own seal. The gates are closed in plague protocol—her own outriders are enforcing it, which is a joke I intend to enjoy later. You have perhaps a day. Less, once she checks the cisterns herself, because she will."

Silence. The kettle murmured. Somewhere below, boots rang on stone—Voss's soldiers, sealing the palace, sealing it around all of them.

Emrys rose from her chair. The blanket slid from her shoulders. Up close, in the brazier light, she looked exhausted and frightened and utterly resolved, and when she spoke, her voice was quiet.

"Why?" she asked. "Why burn it? Why risk everything?"

Ilsa considered the question. She thought about the boy in the fen country. About the beam in the valley temple, and the child who should have died, and the way turning her face away had felt like the first honest act of her career. About eleven years of lawful work, and what the law had turned out to be serving.

"Because I lit too many fires," she said, "for the wrong god."

She picked up her sword belt and buckled it on—on the outside of her coat now, openly, where they could all see it—and turned to the door.

"Get some sleep, all of you. Tomorrow we start planning a rite that kills whoever performs it, inside a sealed palace, against the finest hunter the crown ever produced." She glanced back once, at the king hunched in the child's chair, at the girl who meant to save him. "And pray your scribe writes faster than Voss rides."

From the corridor beyond the door, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the great gates booming shut, bar by bar, like a coffin being nailed closed.
