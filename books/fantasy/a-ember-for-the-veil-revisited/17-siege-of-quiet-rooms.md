## 17. Siege of Quiet Rooms

The palace had a sound for every hour of the night, and Seraphine had learned them all as a child hiding from her governess: the groan of the east wing settling at midnight, the scuttle of mice behind the pantry wall at two, the distant changing of the guard with its soft percussion of boots on marble at three. Tonight the palace was silent in a way it had never been silent, and the silence itself was a sound—thick, held-breath quiet, like the whole building was listening.

Then the bells began.

Not the hour bells. The *seal* bells. Three strokes, a pause, three more, ringing down from the watchtower in a pattern Seraphine had heard only once before in her life, during a plague year when she was nine and the gates had closed for four months. Voss's hunters were inside the walls. The palace was closing around them like a fist.

"They're early," Ilsa had said, an hour ago, pressing a knife into Seraphine's hands with the brisk tenderness of a woman arming a soldier. "She was always going to come early. She wants us asleep." Then Ilsa had gone down toward the guard barracks to burn what remained of her career as kindling, buying them hours with a forged quarantine decree, and Seraphine had stood alone in Bree Ashdown's borrowed kitchen holding a weapon she did not know how to use, watching Emrys Kavanagh try very hard not to be afraid.

Emrys was failing at it. Her hands would not stay still. She kept smoothing them down the front of the maid's uniform Seraphine had dressed her in—the plain grey wool, the white apron, the cap that hid the copper of her hair—and then they would drift upward again, fingers twitching at nothing, as if the air itself had become something she needed to touch. Since the archive, since she had read aloud the terms of the unbinding rite in a voice that shook only twice, the warmth in her fingertips had stopped being something she suppressed. It leaked. Seraphine could see it now, in the lamplight—a faint gold shimmer along the scribe's knuckles, like sunlight caught under skin.

"You don't have to do this," Seraphine said. "We could still take the river gate. Get you out of the city."

"And leave your father to that thing?" Emrys looked up, and there it was again—that steadiness that kept surprising Seraphine, surfacing through the fear like stone through shallow water. "You heard what the archive said. Every day it wears him thinner. And when he's gone—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. When Aldric was gone, the entity would need a new crown, a new head, and there was no telling whose.

"He is not an easy man to save," Seraphine said quietly. "He has made himself difficult on purpose, most of his life."

"Good," said Emrys, and almost smiled. "I've never done anything easy."

* * *

The servant passages began behind the linen room, behind a door so deliberately dull that no one had thought to lock it in forty years. Seraphine had found these corridors when she was eleven, running from a tutor's anger, and had spent a whole lost summer mapping them—chalk marks on her palm, names invented for each junction, a kingdom beneath the kingdom where no one could tell her to sit straighter or speak softer or stop asking questions. She had not been down here in fifteen years. Her body remembered anyway. Her feet knew the turn before her mind did; her hand found the second sconce by feel and pressed, and the counterweighted panel sighed open on hinges someone had oiled within the decade.

Someone still used these passages. Someone who was not supposed to exist.

"Lamplight," Seraphine whispered, and Emrys lifted her hand, and flame bloomed above her palm without flint or wick, steady and warm, throwing their shadows huge against the brick. It was the first time Seraphine had seen her call light from nothing, and something in her chest turned over—not fear, though her father's council would have called it that, but a fierce and unexpected gladness. *This* was what the hunts were burning out of the world. This small sun, held in a frightened girl's hand, lighting the way to save a king.

They went down. The passage smelled of tallow and old bread and the ghost of a hundred years of hurried feet. Seraphine counted junctions—*Heron's Corner, the Long Slant, Understairs*—names she had given them in chalk and childish certainty, and at each one she paused and listened while Emrys held the light low and their two sets of breathing filled the dark.

At the third junction they heard boots.

Seraphine pinched out the discussion of light with a gesture—*down, douse*—and Emrys closed her fist, and the dark came back complete. They pressed into an alcove that had once hidden contraband wine, and through the wall came the sound of hunters moving through the gallery above: not the measured tread of palace guards but the quick, eager scuff of men who had been promised something. At least six. Then a voice, dry as paper, giving orders in a tone Seraphine recognized from her childhood nightmares.

Maren Voss herself. Not directing from outside. Walking her own hunt.

"—the royal apartments sealed. The princess's wing especially. If she's abetted this creature, I want to know it before the council does." A pause, and then, in the same flat voice: "The king is ill. You will remember that the king is ill, whatever you see tonight. Say it back to me."

A ragged chorus: *The king is ill.*

Seraphine's nails cut half-moons into her own palms. *Ill.* Is that what they would write in the histories—that her father had died of illness, quietly, conveniently, while a starving god wore his face to its own funeral? The rage came up her throat like bile, and she mastered it, barely, because Emrys's hand found her sleeve in the dark and held on, and the touch said everything words could not have survived saying: *not yet, not here, we are so close.*

The boots faded. They waited until the silence was unbearable and then some, and moved on.

* * *

The passage to the great stair ran beneath the chapel, and it was there they met the first guard.

He was young—younger than Seraphine, surely not past twenty-two—with the raw knuckles and ink-stained cuff of a man promoted too fast from clerk to soldier because the hunt kept eating its own. He stood at the junction where the servants' stair met the undercroft, lamp raised, and when Emrys's light bloomed in answer he flinched so hard the lamp rattled.

"Halt," he said, and then ruined it by adding, "please."

Seraphine stepped forward, letting the hood fall back from her face, and watched recognition move across his features like weather. She knew him. That was the cruelty of it—she knew nearly all of them. She had known this palace's people all her life: the cook's daughters, the stablemaster's sons, the boys who had held her pony's bridle and the girls who had taught her cards in the barracks on winter nights. Her father had once told her, in one of their rare unarmored conversations, that a king's true walls were not stone but people, and that he trusted the palace guard more than any army. He had been right. That was precisely why Voss had sealed the palace—to turn his walls against him.

"Your Highness." The young guard's eyes went from her to Emrys, to the shimmer of light dying between Emrys's fingers, and Seraphine saw the arithmetic happening behind his face: *princess, witch, treason, reward, duty.* His hand moved toward the whistle at his collar.

"I'm taking her to my father," Seraphine said. She kept her voice level, the voice she used in council, the one that sounded like her mother. "King Aldric is a prisoner in his own body. The thing that engineered the hunt—the thing that killed Nurse Wren, whose name is on the registry you were made to swear against—is wearing his crown right now. In twenty minutes I can end it. Or you can blow that whistle, and in twenty years you can explain to your children why the witch-hunts lasted another generation."

The guard's hand stopped. His throat worked. And then he did the bravest ordinary thing Seraphine had ever witnessed: he stepped backward. Out of the way. He turned his face deliberately toward the wall, the way a man turns from a beautiful thing he cannot afford to look at, and said, in a voice that cracked exactly once:

"I heard nothing. There's no one on this stair. No one's been on this stair all night."

Seraphine paused—one heartbeat, two. "What's your name?"

"Don't—" He shook his head hard, still facing the wall. "Don't tell me things I'd have to forget, Your Highness. Just go."

She went. But she carried his namelessness with her down the dark, heavier than any title she owned.

* * *

Twice more the passages tested them, and twice more the palace surprised her.

The second time it was not one guard but four, gathered at the Understairs junction around a shuttered lantern, playing at dice because a sealed palace is a bored palace. Seraphine heard them before she saw them—heard, specifically, one of them say her father's name, and stopped dead with Emrys a step behind her.

"—swear it on my mother, Corvin. Saw him at the west window Tuesday last, talking to nobody. Talking to the *air*. Old Halvane says he's been doing it for months." A rough laugh without much humor in it. "And now Voss seals us in like rats and tells us the king is ill. Ill. My grandmother's ill. Kings don't get ill. Kings get *replaced*, that's how it works—"

"Shut your mouth before someone hears treason in it."

"It's not treason if it's true—"

Seraphine stepped into the lantern-light before courage could fail her, and four dice froze mid-tumble, and four faces came up grey in the little light. She knew every one of them. Corvin, who had fished her out of the ornamental pond when she was eight. The big one, Hesse, whose sister sewed for the household. Two others whose names she knew and whose children would someday know hers, if there were someday and children at all after tonight.

"Princess—" Corvin was up off the crate, bowing, knocking his knee on the dice-board, and the others scrambled after.

"Gentlemen." Her voice did not shake. She would think about that later, about how strange it was that her body chose now, of all nights, to obey her completely. "I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to answer it honestly, because everything depends on it. Your king has spent months fighting something inside his own mind—something that ordered the hunts, that killed innocent people whose only crime was being born different. I am going to free him. Tonight. Will you let me pass?"

Silence. The lantern guttered. Somewhere far above, the seal-bells were still tolling their three-and-three, and Seraphine watched four men weigh their oaths against their eyes, their wages against their consciences, the safe lie against the dangerous truth.

It was Hesse, the big one, the seamstress's brother, who answered. He didn't stand. He didn't declare anything. He simply picked up the lantern, walked to the mouth of the passage that led toward the great stair, and set it down there, lit, positioned so its glow fell exactly where the turning was easiest to miss.

"Dark down that way," he said, to no one in particular. "Always meant to fix that."

Corvin laughed—one short, startled bark—and sat back down and gathered his dice. "Terrible housekeeping," he agreed. "Shameful. Nobody could find their way through here at all."

The other two studied the ceiling with tremendous diligence.

"Thank you," Seraphine said.

"Don't thank us." For a moment Corvin's young-old face was stripped of everything joking. "Just win, Princess."

* * *

The third door was locked, and that was where it nearly ended.

The passage below the chapel narrowed to a crawlspace behind the old confessional—she had forgotten that, forgotten the squeeze and the smell of dust and dried wax—and they emerged in the vestry store, where the path to the great stair's underside was barred by an oak door with a new lock shining in the old wood. Voss's work. Installed within days, perhaps hours. Seraphine put her shoulder to it and felt the futility of her own slight weight against crown ironwork.

"Let me." Emrys knelt, laid both palms flat against the wood, and closed her eyes. The light came slowly this time, not the easy candle-flame of before but something deeper, dragged up from somewhere that cost her. Seraphine watched the veins of her hands go gold, watched the lock's iron blush red and then *soften*, sagging like warm tallow, and the door drifted open with a sound like a long sigh.

Emrys swayed. Seraphine caught her elbow.

"How much does that cost you?"

"Less than it will cost everyone if we're late." Emrys's smile was white at the edges. "I'm fine. I'm—" She stopped. Tilted her head. And Seraphine saw the color drain out of her face all at once, faster than any spell could account for. "Oh," she breathed. "Oh, he's *loud* tonight."

"The king?"

"The thing in him. It knows I'm close. It's—it's like standing outside a house where something's screaming, and the screaming is getting closer to the windows." Her hands had gone cold in Seraphine's grip. "Seraphine. We need to hurry. I don't know how much longer he can hold it."

They ran then. Through the vestry, up the hidden stair behind the organ pipes where the dust of centuries lay undisturbed except for the single line of fresh footprints that Seraphine refused to wonder about, and out through the panel behind the arras in the north ambulatory, into the high stone throat of the great stair itself—and there, at last, the doors of the throne room stood before them, banded in bronze, carved with the history of a dynasty that had never once imagined ending like this.

Between them and the doors: two of Voss's hunters. Real ones, this time. Grey cloaks, drawn steel, faces with no childhood in them anywhere.

Behind them, faintly, through two inches of ancient oak, Seraphine could hear her father's voice. Rising. Cracking. Arguing with someone who was not there—or who was more there than anyone in the world—and beneath it, impossibly layered through the same throat, another voice entirely, smooth and patient and hungry, saying words she could not distinguish but whose tone she understood in her marrow. *Come out. Come out, little scribe. We have been so patient.*

One of the hunters turned. Raised his blade. Opened his mouth to shout the alarm—

—and Emrys stepped forward, and the light came up out of her like dawn breaking all at once, gold flooding the stairwell from floor to vaulting, and the hunter's shout died in his throat as every torch in the corridor flared white and the great doors themselves shuddered in their frames.

"Go," Emrys said, and her voice was not frightened anymore. It rang against the stone with harmonics no human throat should own. "*Go to him.*"

Seraphine went. She put her shoulder to the doors—unlocked, unbarred, as if the thing inside wanted them open, which of course it did—and they swung wide onto the throne room, onto the vast dim hall where a thousand years of kings looked down from their painted heavens, and there at the center, risen halfway from the throne with his head thrown back and his hands clawing at the air as if gripping an invisible throat, was her father.

"Aldric!" she cried, using his name for the first time in eleven years, and his eyes—her father's grey eyes, drowning—found hers across the whole length of that terrible bright hall.

"*Sera,*" he gasped. "Run—" and then the other voice took his mouth, smiling.
