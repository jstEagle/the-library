## 18. The Unbinding

The throne room doors closed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Emrys had expected grandeur. She had expected gold and banners and the weight of a kingdom's history pressing down from vaulted ceilings. What she found instead was a room that seemed to be *dying*—the tapestries along the walls faded to the color of old bruises, the great windows clouded as though smoke had seeped into the glass itself, and the throne at the far end less a seat than a wound, black iron grown over with veins of something that pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

The crown sat upon it.

Not on Aldric's head—on the throne's armrest, coiled like a sleeping serpent, its circlet of dark metal drinking the light from the high windows. Even from thirty paces away, Emrys could feel it noticing her. A pressure against her mind, curious and cold, the way a spider might regard a fly not yet caught in the web.

"It knows we're here," she whispered.

"Of course it knows." Seraphine stood beside her, dressed plainly in a servant's gray, her hands steady even though her voice had gone thin. "It's been watching us since we entered the palace. It let us come." She swallowed. "It wants an audience."

At the door behind them, Ilsa Vane drew her sword—not the ceremonial blade of the hunt but the plain steel she'd carried through the fen market, through the archive, through every mile since she'd burned her commission papers in Maren Voss's brazier. She planted herself between the doors and everything else, a single figure in a room built for hundreds.

"No one comes through this door," Ilsa said. "No one."

"And if it's not people coming through?" Bree asked quietly. She was already kneeling at the center of the floor, chalk in hand, drawing the first arc of the circle they'd memorized from the queen's archive—the rite copied out in Emrys's own careful scribe's hand, each glyph checked three times against the original. "If it's something worse?"

"Then I'll hold the door anyway," Ilsa said. "It's what I'm for."

Emrys knelt across from Bree and took up her own piece of chalk. Her fingers were shaking. She looked down at them and thought, absurdly, of the scriptorium—of ink that would not dry, of the first small warmth she'd ever felt in her fingertips, of Mother Sable saying *your gift is not your enemy, child; it is only louder than you are used to being.*

She breathed out. The shaking slowed. Not stopped—she wasn't sure it would ever fully stop again—but slowed enough to work.

Together, she and Bree drew the circle.

The chalk lines came alive under their hands. Where Bree drew, the marks glowed soft green, hedge-witch green, the color of growing things and kitchen smoke and stubborn kindness. Where Emrys drew, the glow was white-gold, and the stone beneath hummed like a struck bell. When the two halves of the circle met and closed, the sound deepened into something felt in the ribs rather than heard, and the dying light of the throne room bent toward them like grass toward sun.

Aldric cried out.

He stood at the base of the dais, ten steps below his own throne, and he had insisted on standing there—*if I am to be unmade or remade, I will face it on my feet*—but now his knees buckled and Seraphine caught him, hauling his arm across her shoulders, holding her father upright by sheer stubbornness.

"Aldric," she said, close to his ear. "Look at me. Not at it. At me."

His eyes were wrong. Emrys could see it even from the circle—one eye still his own, gray and terrified and human, the other gone silver-bright, reflecting light that wasn't there. The entity was surfacing in him like a drowning man's last struggle inverted: it was the one drowning him, and it fought now because it knew, at last, exactly what they intended.

"You come," Aldric said—and the voice that came out of him was layered, his own voice underneath and something vast and starving speaking over it, through it, wearing it like a glove—"into MY house, little flame. Into the house I built. You think a circle of chalk—"

"The circle isn't chalk," Emrys said.

Her voice surprised her. It came out steady, clear, carrying across the throne room the way her mother's singing had once carried across the temple garden. She hadn't known she could sound like that.

"The circle is a question," she went on, rising to her feet inside it. "And you already know the answer. You've had four hundred years to learn it. A memory freely given. Love, surrendered willingly. That's the term of your binding, and it's the only term, and today someone is going to pay it."

The thing in Aldric laughed.

It was the worst sound Emrys had ever heard—not because it was cruel, though it was, but because it was *tired*. Four centuries of hunger compressed into a single broken note.

"No one has ever paid it gladly," it said. "The queen paid it in agony. Every soul who tried after her burned from the inside out. And you—" the silver eye fixed on Emrys, and she felt its attention like a hand closing around her throat— "you are a scribe. A copyist. You copy love stories written by braver hands. What do you know of giving?"

Emrys's chest tightened. Because it was true, wasn't it? What did she know? She had crossed a border, healed a stranger, survived a market and an archive and a siege of quiet rooms—but all of that had been survival. Survival was not the same as surrender. Survival kept things. Giving meant opening her hands and letting something fall that could never be picked back up.

"Emrys." Bree's voice, low, at her side. The hedge-witch's hand found hers, calloused and warm. "Whatever happens in the next few minutes—whatever you have to decide—I need you to hear me first. Whatever you choose, it will be enough. Do you understand me? *You* will be enough. That thing is lying about your worth the way it lies about everything."

Emrys squeezed her hand. "I understand."

"Then let's begin before I lose my nerve, because I promise you, girl, I am very close to losing my nerve."

They began.

* * *

The rite had three movements, according to the queen's manuscript: the Opening, the Naming, and the Offering. The Opening belonged to the circle-keepers. The Naming belonged to the bound. The Offering belonged—to whoever was brave enough, or foolish enough, or loving enough to make it.

Bree sang the Opening.

Her voice was nothing like a temple choir's—rough, a little off-key, the voice of a woman who sang while stirring pots—but the power in it needed no polish. The circle flared green, then white-gold where Emrys fed her own gift into it, and the two colors braided together, rising off the floor in slow ribbons. The glyphs began to turn around them, a slow wheel of light.

The entity screamed.

It did not scream through Aldric this time. It screamed through the *room*—through the walls, the windows, the iron of the throne itself. Cracks raced up the pillars. The clouded windows shattered outward, and night air rushed in, and with the air came pressure, a howling weight that flattened Emrys's hair against her skull and made the ribbons of circle-light bend like reeds in a gale.

"HOLD IT!" Bree roared over the wind. Sweat streamed down her face; her free hand pressed flat against the stone, pouring everything she had into the circle's foundation. "The circle holds as long as we hold! Don't you dare think about anything except the line!"

Emrys thought about the line. She poured herself into it—every lesson Bree had taught her in the kitchen, every hour of control won and lost and won again, every scrap of the strange loud magic that had gotten her hunted across half a kingdom. The circle blazed. The wind broke against it like water against a seawall.

At the door, something hit the wood hard enough to crack it.

Ilsa didn't turn. "How many?" she called back, sword raised.

"Three shapes!" Seraphine shouted from the dais, where she'd dragged her father down behind the throne's bulk for shelter. "They came up through the floor—they're made of—of *smoke and teeth*, Ilsa, I don't—"

"Smoke and teeth. Fine." Ilsa rolled her shoulders. "I've fought worse. I've fought Voss before breakfast."

The door burst inward.

What came through were not hunters. They were what the hunt had been *feeding* all these months—all the fear and grief and stolen power the entity's servants had harvested, given shape. They moved wrong, joints bending in directions joints shouldn't go, faces like smudged charcoal with white eyes burning in the middle. Three of them filled the doorway, and behind them, far below in the palace, Emrys heard shouting, running feet, the whole building waking to war.

The first shape lunged.

Ilsa met it mid-leap. Her blade should have passed through smoke—it didn't; whatever these things were made of, steel bit it, and it shrieked like tearing canvas and recoiled, trailing darkness from a long gash. Ilsa pressed forward, giving ground to neither of them, planting herself in the ruined doorway like a fence post driven into a flood.

"I said," she snarled, parrying a clawed sweep and answering with a cut that opened the second shape from shoulder to hip, "*no one comes through this door.*"

The third shape ignored her entirely. It flowed past her—under her, through the gap between her boots and the floor, liquid-dark—and reformed inside the room, ten feet from the circle, white eyes swiveling toward Emrys.

Seraphine moved.

Later, Emrys would try to remember deciding to do it, and find no decision anywhere—only motion. She stepped out of the circle.

"*NO!*" Bree's scream tore through the wind.

But Emrys was already moving, both hands out, and the gift that had been suppressed her whole childhood, that had woken violently beyond the valley's veil, that had healed a dying stranger without her meaning to—it came out of her now like a dam breaking. White-gold light erupted from her palms and caught the shape full in its smudged face. It shrieked. Light was the opposite of everything it was; light was the thing it had been starved of for four hundred years. It unraveled around her radiance like thread pulled loose, thrashing, diminishing—

—and in that instant of her absence, the circle flickered.

Only for a heartbeat. Only a stutter in the turning wheel of glyphs. But the entity was four centuries old and patient as rot, and it seized that heartbeat with both hands.

Aldric rose.

Not the king—the *thing*, unfurling through him, silver flooding both eyes now, lifting his body off the ground with a strength no human spine should bear. Power poured off him in waves that cracked the flagstones. He turned his head toward Seraphine, who had thrown herself between him and the circle, arms spread, protecting a father who was not currently home.

"Daughter," the entity said, in a voice like a landslide learning to speak. "You will arrest the scribe. You will kneel. You will—"

"No," Seraphine said.

The word was small. It was the smallest word spoken in the throne room that night, and it was the loudest. She stood in the blast-furnace heat of her father's stolen power, in the wind of it, her hair whipping, her eyes streaming, and she said it again:

"No. He's my *father*. And you're just the thing that's been eating him. And I—" her voice cracked, and she let it crack, and kept talking through the crack— "I spent six years being too proud to climb the stairs to his study. Six years. I am not spending one more minute letting *you* have what I was too cowardly to take. Get out of my father."

The entity hesitated.

In that hesitation, Emrys saw it—the truth beneath everything, the thing the archive had hinted at and the queen's manuscript had confirmed. The entity was a starving god, yes, ancient and vast. But it was also, at bottom, simple. It understood hunger. It understood taking. It understood binding and terms and the letter of ancient law.

It did not understand *this*—a woman with no magic at all refusing a god with nothing but.

And in its confusion, for one precious moment, its grip on Aldric slipped.

The king came back into his own eyes like a man surfacing from deep water. He sagged; Seraphine caught him. And Aldric—gray-faced, bleeding from the nose, his voice a ruin—looked past his daughter to the circle, to Bree kneeling alone at its center, to Emrys standing in the wreckage of her own light.

"Now," he rasped. "While it's shaken. Do it *now*. Whatever it costs—do it, and don't you dare look back."

* * *

Emrys returned to the circle. Bree hauled her in by the wrist, and the glyphs leapt back to full brightness the moment her feet crossed the line, the wheel resuming its slow turn as though the interruption had never happened. But Emrys felt the difference. The circle had been wounded. Somewhere in its weave there was a hairline fracture, and the entity knew it, and the entity was gathering itself for one final assault.

"The Naming," Bree gasped. "We do the Naming now, together, and then—" She stopped. Her hands were trembling against Emrys's. Not with fear, Emrys realized. With something worse. With *grief already arriving early*, grief sent ahead of the loss like scouts before an army. "Emrys. Before we go further. There's something I have to tell you about the Offering. Something I should have told you sooner."

The wind screamed. At the door, Ilsa went down under the weight of two shapes and came up swinging, blood on her face, roaring words Emrys couldn't hear. On the dais, Seraphine held her father's hand in both of hers, and Aldric's lips were moving—prayer, or apology, or simply her name, over and over, *Sera, Sera, Sera.*

"Bree," Emrys said. "Tell me later. Tell me when it's done."

"But—"

"We're out of time. Look at the door."

Ilsa was losing. Two shapes pinned her sword arm; the third coiled to strike. And beyond her, down the stairwells and corridors of the palace, Emrys could feel more of them coming, drawn like moths, like sharks, like hunger itself. Minutes. Perhaps less.

Bree looked at the door. Then at Emrys. And whatever she had meant to say, she swallowed it, and nodded, and lifted her chin.

"Together, then," Bree said. "On three."

They spoke the Naming as one voice—Bree's rough alto and Emrys's clear soprano braiding into a single sound, calling the entity by its oldest name, the name the starving god had borne before it was bound, before it was hungry, back when it had been only a small god of a small place whom the world had forgotten until forgetting curdled into rage. The name filled the throne room like water fills a hull.

The entity stopped fighting.

Everything stopped—the wind, the shapes at the door, the cracks crawling through the walls—as if the whole world leaned in to hear its own forgotten beginning spoken aloud. In the sudden silence, the silver eye found Emrys across the ruined room.

"Yes," it said softly. Almost gently. "That was my name. Once." The coils of the crown stirred on the throne's armrest, lifting, turning toward the circle like a flower toward light. "And now, little flame, we arrive at the part you have been avoiding since you walked in. The Offering. Who pays? Who gives? Who opens their hands?" The smile that moved across Aldric's stolen face was terrible in its patience. "I have waited four hundred years. I can wait a moment longer while you choose which of your loves to feed me."

Emrys closed her eyes.

And in the darkness behind her eyelids, unbidden, inevitable, her mother was singing in the temple garden—*the same song, always the same song, the one about the river that remembers the mountain*—and Emrys felt the memory rise in her like a tide, whole and warm and unbearably bright, offering itself.

Beside her, Bree drew a shuddering breath and whispered, "Emrys—wait—there's something you don't—"

But the circle was already listening.
