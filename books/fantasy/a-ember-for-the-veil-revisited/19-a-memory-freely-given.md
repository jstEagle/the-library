## 19. A Memory Freely Given

The throne room had never been meant for this.

Emrys felt that truth in her bones as she knelt at the center of the great cold hall, chalk dust on her fingers and the rite's circle half-drawn around her in lines of silver and salt. The vaulted ceiling rose into darkness where banners of nine generations hung like sleeping bats, and the windows—tall as trees, leaded with the faces of dead kings—let in a gray morning light that made everything look already memorialized. Already grieved for. She tried not to think of it that way, and failed.

*Draw the circle true,* Mother Sable's letter had said, in a hand gone shaky at the edges. *A binding is only as honest as its geometry.*

So Emrys drew it true. Nine points, nine names, each written in the old temple script she had spent ten years copying without ever understanding what the words were *for*. Her fingers did not shake. That surprised her. She had expected terror to arrive like a storm; instead it had settled into her the way the valley's quiet once had, a thing she carried rather than a thing that carried her.

Behind her, Bree Ashdown stood at the northern point of the circle, sleeves rolled, hedge-witch charms braided into her hair with bits of red thread. Ilsa Vane held the doors—actually held them, one shoulder braced against oak banded with iron, a hunting knife drawn and laid flat across her palm like an oath. And at the southern point, past the dais and the throne that nobody was sitting in, Seraphine gripped her father's hand.

King Aldric sat on the steps below his own seat because he could no longer trust himself with it. He looked smaller than his coin-portrait, hollowed, his eyes the color of wet ash. The crown sat on his head still—they could not remove it until the rite began—and it did not gleam. It *absorbed*. Light went into it and did not come out, and sometimes, when Emrys let herself look directly at it, she saw something move beneath its surface. Something turning over in its sleep. Something tasting the air.

It knew she was here. It had known since the moment she crossed the palace threshold, and it had been screaming through Aldric's mouth ever since—not aloud, not yet, but she felt the pressure of it against the inside of her skull like a thumb pressed to a bruise. *Come closer, little scribe. You smell of ink and hunger. Come be read.*

"Emrys." Bree's voice, low and steady, the voice she used over sickbeds. "Last chance, love. Say it plain and we walk out, and we keep running, and we live small and stubborn for forty years. I've done it before. It's not a bad life."

Emrys looked up at her—at the wind-chapped face, the eyes that had found her in a fen market crowd when every hunter in the province had been looking too—and shook her head slowly.

"If we run," Emrys said, "it feeds. On him first, then on every gifted child the hunt drags in to fill its bowl. There's no running far enough. There's only finishing it."

"She's right," Ilsa said from the doors. Her voice was flat as a blade's spine, but her knuckles were white around the knife. "And the Grand Huntress will be through those doors within hours regardless. Quarantine decree or no. So either we do this now, or we die tired."

"Cheerful as ever, Captain," Bree muttered.

"I contain multitudes."

Seraphine laughed—a short, cracked sound, nothing like humor—and then knelt beside her father on the steps. Up close, Emrys could see what the princess's composure cost her: the tremor in her jaw, the way her thumb moved across the back of her father's hand, again and again, like a woman smoothing a wrinkle out of cloth that kept re-forming.

"Papa," Seraphine said. "Look at me. Not at it. At me."

Aldric raised his head. For a heartbeat his eyes were only a tired man's eyes, fifty years deep in a job he had never wanted, and Emrys saw the moment he gathered himself—the last clean coin in his purse, spent all at once.

"Sera," he said hoarsely. "When it's done—if it's done—you burn the hunt registries first. Before anything else. Every name."

"Yes."

"And you tell Vane's Grand Huntress that her commission ended the moment the thing she hunted stopped existing. Let her rage at a dead god's grave."

"I will."

He nodded, once, the way kings nod: an order given, an order received, and then he closed his eyes and said, in a voice that had gone very quiet, "Scribe. Do it."

---

Emrys rose to her feet inside the circle and opened the archive folio against her forearm, though she no longer needed it. She had read the rite so many times in the past three days that the words had worn grooves in her mind, water finding their channel. The queen who made the original binding—Maren, ironically, a name that now hunted other names—had left instructions in a hand that grew more hurried as it went, as if she were writing against something approaching from behind.

*The entity is hunger wearing the shape of a crown. It cannot be killed, for it is not alive. It can only be paid. And its price is fixed and terrible: a memory of love, freely surrendered, by one whose love is true. Not taken. Never taken—it cannot take, that is the flaw at its heart, the crack in its hunger. It must be given. Offer the memory of something loved, loose your grip on it willingly, forever, and the binding severs. But know this, whoever comes after: the giving has killed every soul who attempted it. Love is not a purse one pays from. It is the blood in the body.*

Bree had spent two days trying to find a way around that last line. There wasn't one. There was only Bree standing at the north point now, jaw set, insisting—as she had insisted all night—that it would be her memory given. Her wife, Marta, dead eleven years, laughing in a kitchen full of steam and rosemary. *My life's already half-spent, love. Don't argue arithmetic with a hedge-witch.*

The plan was simple, as plans made at three in the morning always are. Bree would give. Emrys would hold the circle. If the rite turned lethal, the circle would catch what spilled.

Emrys lifted her hands and began.

The words of the old script came out of her in the temple cadence she'd learned at age nine, copying line after line of warding text in a warm room that smelled of ink and beeswax—and oh, the irony of it, that all those copied pages had been preparation, that every stroke of her pen had been a rehearsal, that the valley which suppressed her gift had also, unknowingly, trained the one hand in the world precise enough to draw this circle true.

Power woke under her words.

She felt it rise through the floor first, up through the stone and the bones of the mountain the palace stood on, a low humming like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Threads of silver-green light kindled along the chalk lines, following the geometry she had made, and the morning light through the windows dimmed—not darkened, but *deferred*, stepping back politely as older things took the stage.

And the crown screamed.

Not aloud. Worse than aloud. The sound happened entirely inside Emrys's skull, a tearing, scraping shriek like iron dragged across the floor of heaven, and beneath it a voice that had learned speech from a thousand years of kings:

*NO.*

"Hold," Emrys said, and did not know if she said it to Bree or to herself or to the circle itself.

Across the hall, Aldric convulsed.

It was not a seizure; Emrys had seen seizures, had copied the healing texts describing them. This was different. This was *architecture*. The king's spine arched and his hands locked open like starfish and the shadows in the room bent toward him, all of them at once, bending the wrong way, toward the crown as toward a drain. When he raised his head his eyes had gone the color of tarnished silver, and the mouth that opened was still his mouth, and absolutely was not.

"The scribe," it said, and its voice filled the throne room the way water fills a hull. "Little key. Little unlocked door. Do you know what you are? You are the reason I called the hunt. A gift like yours, untrained, unbound—I have waited ninety years for a vessel that could carry me *out*. Give me your hands, scribe, and I will show you every corner of the world. I will make you a library of everything that has ever been known."

"You're lying," Emrys said quietly. "You don't share. You consume. That's all you are. That's all you've ever been."

The thing wearing Aldric's face smiled, and the smile was the worst thing Emrys had ever seen, because some part of Aldric was still in there, watching his own mouth do it.

"Then I will start with your friends," it said. "The witch first. She tastes of grief. Old grief, kept warm."

It raised one hand—

—and Ilsa Vane moved.

Later, Emrys would understand what the captain had actually done: that she had seen the entity gathering power, had understood it would strike the circle's weakest point, and had chosen to make herself the loudest target in the room. She came off the doors with her knife reversed and hurled it—not at the king, never at the king, she was still a hunt-captain down to her marrow—but past him, into the throne's high back, where it buried itself with a *thunk* that rang through the hall like a challenge.

"Your quarrel is with the crown, not the man," Ilsa called, walking forward with the deliberate slowness of someone baiting a bear. "Or does the great devouring god fear a disgraced captain with one knife?"

*Hold the door,* Emrys had asked her.

Ilsa Vane held nothing now, and held everything.

The entity snarled—a raw animal sound, Aldric's throat wrecking itself around it—and the shadows leapt. Emrys threw everything she had into the circle: the chalk lines blazed, the nine names burned white, and a dome of woven light snapped into being over the five of them just as the darkness broke against it like surf. The impact drove her to her knees. Cold poured through the weave, hungry and patient, probing for flaws in her geometry, in her attention, in her *will*.

"BREE!" Emrys gasped. "Now! While it's pressing on the circle—it's distracted—give it now!"

Bree stepped to the circle's heart.

She had prepared, of course. She always prepared; her cottage had been a museum of labeled jars and annotated margins. She carried Marta's memory wrapped in a sprig of dried rosemary, and she spoke the offering words with tears already running freely down her weathered face, unashamed, the way she did everything.

"Marta Ashdown," Bree said, voice cracking but clear. "My wife. Eleven years gone and I have kept her every day since. I give her back. Freely. Gladly. Take the payment and *choke* on—"

She faltered.

It was small, at first. A hitch in the fourth syllable. Then a widening silence, Bree's lips still moving, forming shapes that had lost their cargo. Emrys watched the horror of it happen in real time: watched Bree reach for eleven years of laughter and steam and rosemary and find her hands closing on smoke. Because that was the trap no archive had warned them of, the cruelty folded into the terms' own logic: *freely given*. To give Marta freely, Bree had to hold her lightly. And to hold her lightly, even for a moment, even on purpose, was to begin losing the grip of a lifetime—and the memory, sensing the loosening, had begun to slip like a fish toward deep water.

"No," Bree whispered. "No, no—Marta—her name, what's her—I know there's a *name*—"

The circle flickered. The entity felt it instantly, the way a shark feels blood, and its pressure against the weave redoubled, and Emrys felt her geometry beginning to fail, line by silver line, nine names guttering like candles in wind.

They had perhaps thirty seconds.

And Emrys Kavanagh, kneeling in the failing circle with blood trickling from her nose and her whole body shaking with the effort of holding light against dark, discovered that she had known this would happen. Not with her mind—with somewhere deeper. Some part of her had run the sums in the night watches while Bree slept and argued and slept again. Some part of her had looked at Bree's half-spent life and her own full one and done the arithmetic the hedge-witch refused to do.

Her mother's garden. The evening light coming gold through the temple colonnade. The particular off-key sweetness of a lullaby sung by a woman who knew she would not live to hear her daughter grow up, and sang anyway, every night, her hand moving slow on Emrys's back. *Sleep, my heart, the veil is kind / it keeps what kings would seek to find...*

Emrys had spent ten years guarding that memory behind walls of discipline, rationing it like water in drought, terrified of wearing it thin—and in that terror had kept it perfect. Whole. Bright as the evening it was made in.

Ready.

"Bree," she said, and her voice came out calm, which surprised her, which seemed almost funny. "Let go. You'll hurt yourself holding a thing that hard."

"I can't—I've almost got it—" But Bree's eyes were wild, emptying, and they found Emrys's and understood everything in them at once. "No. Oh, no you don't, girl, no—"

"It has to be freely given," Emrys said. "That's the crack in it. It can't refuse a gift. Watch."

She turned to the darkness hammering at her circle—to the thing in the crown, the starving god, the hunger that had eaten queens and hunts and a good man's fifty years—and she opened the door in her heart that she had kept locked since she was nine years old.

*Mother. The garden. The song.*

*I give it. Freely. Gladly. Take it and be bound no more.*

She did not lose her grip gradually, the way Bree had. She flung it. She took the most precious thing she owned and gave it away with both hands, the way her mother must have given her—wholly, without reservation, without keeping even a scrap back for herself—and the memory left her like a bird leaving an open cage, trailing gold light through the ruined weave of the circle, straight into the heart of the dark.

For one endless instant, the entity held it.

Emrys saw it happen: saw the hunger close around the offering, saw it taste the evening light and the off-key singing and the hand on a small girl's back—and saw the terms seize it like a hook through the gill. *Freely given love cannot be consumed. It can only be honored.* The old queen had built the paradox into the binding itself, and Emrys finally understood, in the last second, why every giver had died: not because the rite killed them, but because they had tried to give while grieving, tried to pay while clutching, and a divided gift was no gift at all, and the backlash of a refused payment was annihilation.

But Emrys did not cling. Emrys did not grieve. She gave with her whole heart, empty-handed and glad, and so the terms held, and the thing that had worn a hundred kings—

unraveled.

There was no explosion. There was something far stranger: a long, descending sigh, like a great bell falling silent, like a held breath released after centuries. The darkness did not flee the throne room; it *thinly departed*, unwinding out of the shadows and the stones and the crown itself, threads of black smoke separating into filaments, into motes, into nothing, and the last of it funneled upward through the painted faces of the dead kings on the windows and was gone, and the morning light came back in like a family returning to a house after a long absence.

The crown slid from Aldric's brow and rang on the stone steps, and lay still, merely metal, merely old.

The circle's light died gently, name by name.

And Emrys knelt in the center of it all, in the ringing silence, and reached for her mother—

and found a smooth, blank wall where a door used to be.

*Sleep, my heart, the veil is kind—*

Nothing. Not the words. Not the tune. Not the light, not the colonnade, not the hand. She knew the facts the way she knew the year of a king's death from a copied chronicle: *your mother sang to you in the temple garden; you loved her; she loved you.* Facts, filed, weightless. The love itself—the warmth that had been the load-bearing wall of her entire life—was simply gone, and in its place was a hole shaped exactly like a woman's voice, and she fell toward it—

—and was caught.

Bree. On her knees beside her, arms around her, Bree who was still weeping for Marta and now weeping doubly, terribly, *for* her, carrying the grief Emrys could no longer feel, saying her name over and over: "Emrys, love, Emrys, look at me, stay with me, oh, you brave, stupid, magnificent girl—"

Somewhere near the doors, Ilsa slid down the wood to sit on the floor, knife-hand trembling, laughing the ragged laugh of the not-dead.

Somewhere on the steps, King Aldric drew a breath that belonged to no one but himself, opened eyes gone clear and gray and ordinary, and looked at his daughter as if seeing her for the first time in years—which, Emrys thought distantly, he was.

And Seraphine held her father's face in both hands, and over his shoulder her eyes found Emrys's, and they were shining, and her mouth formed the words *you did it, you did it* —and Emrys wanted to feel it. She searched herself for triumph the way her tongue searched for a taste, and found the hole, and fell toward it again.

Bree caught her a second time.

"I know," Bree murmured into her hair, rocking her, tears falling onto Emrys's scalp. "I know, love. I've got you. Whatever it is you can't feel right now—we'll feel it for you. That's what family's for. Do you hear me? That's what we're *for*."

*We*, Emrys thought, from somewhere very far away, at the bottom of a smooth blank wall. *She said we.*

Outside the sealed doors, faint through the oak, came a sound that none of them had earned respite from: horns. Many horns, deep and disciplined, approaching across the palace forecourt.

The Grand Huntress had arrived.
