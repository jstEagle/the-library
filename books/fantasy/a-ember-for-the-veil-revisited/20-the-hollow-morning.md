## 20. The Hollow Morning

Aldric woke on the floor of the throne room, and the first thing he understood was the silence.

Not quiet—silence. For years there had been a voice beneath his thoughts, a low constant murmur like water moving under ice, and he had learned to think around it, to build his days on ground that was never quite solid. He had forgotten what it was to be alone in his own skull. He had not been alone since the day the crown touched his brow at nineteen, when the archbishop set it down and something old and starving looked out through his eyes at the assembled court and thought, with his ears, *yes, this one will do.*

Now there was nothing. Only morning light falling through the high windows in long dusty shafts, and the smell of scorched stone, and somewhere beyond the doors, the sound of weeping—not grief, he realized slowly, but relief, the strange ragged sobbing of people who have survived something they expected not to.

He tried to sit up and failed. His hands would not answer properly; they trembled like an old man's, and when he turned them over he saw the marks of the thing's long occupation—the nails gone dark, the veins of his wrists standing thick and black as if ink had been poured into him and never fully drained. His body felt like a house ransacked by soldiers: everything of value taken, the furniture overturned, the walls defaced. He did not know yet how much of himself was missing. He only knew that when he reached for certain rooms inside his own memory, the doors stood open on emptiness.

He remembered his coronation. He remembered Maren, his queen, dead eleven winters now, and the way she had laughed at his clumsiness with dancing. He remembered Seraphine being born, her first word—*no*, of course it was *no*—the small ferocious weight of her asleep against his chest.

He did not remember the last three council sessions. He did not remember signing the registry decrees, though his signature sat on them in the archives, neat and damning. He did not remember ordering the execution of a woman named Wren Hollis, though he would learn her name within the hour and carry it for the rest of his life.

He lay on the cold flags a while longer, listening to the silence where a god used to live, and understood that this was the shape of mercy: it did not come clean.

* * *

They found him there when the sun was well up—Ilsa Vane first, sword drawn, because she was Ilsa Vane and would draw a blade at her own funeral—then Bree Ashdown, who pushed past her without ceremony, then Seraphine, who hung back in the doorway as if the room were still dangerous.

Perhaps it was. Perhaps he was.

"Father." Seraphine's voice was careful. He heard in it all the carefulness of years, every conversation in which she had tested the waters of him and found them strange. "Can you hear me?"

"I can hear you." His voice came out ruined, sanded down to gravel. "I don't know how much of me is left to do the hearing."

Bree knelt beside him, her hands already moving above his chest without touching, reading whatever hedge-witches read in the air of a body. Her face was grim but not hopeless. "The binding's gone," she said. "Cleanly gone—there's no scar tissue in the way there'd be if you'd fought it off yourself. It was *taken* from you." She glanced up at the others, and Aldric followed her glance to the center of the room, where Emrys Kavanagh lay crumpled amid the burned circle of the rite, Bree's shawl thrown over her, Seraphine's cloak tucked beneath her head.

"She gave something," Bree said quietly. "Something large. She's sleeping now, but—" The healer stopped herself, and Aldric saw the truth arrive in her face before her mouth could catch up to it: the look of a woman realizing that sleep is not the worst of what her patient faces.

"Is she hurt?" Seraphine asked.

"No," Bree said. "That's just it. She isn't hurt at all. She's—" A breath. "We'll know more when she wakes."

Ilsa Vane sheathed her sword at last and stood over Aldric with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she disliked. "Your Majesty. There are hunters in your palace who followed Voss here, and hunters across the realm following your decrees, and none of them know yet that the thing that wrote those decrees is dead. You need to tell them. Today. Before someone decides the king is simply mad, or worse, that he's hiding, and acts without you."

"Vane." He said her name and watched her flinch—he knew what he had signed, what men like her former commander had done in his name, and she knew he knew. "You held the door."

"Someone had to."

"You'll have your pardon. Your commission back, if you want it."

"I don't want it." Flat, immediate. Then, grudgingly: "Thank you for offering. It tells me you're still in there."

* * *

It took three hours and most of his remaining strength to dress, walk the length of the great hall on Seraphine's arm, and stand before the assembled court—hunters and guardsmen and servants and the pale, furious Grand Huntress Maren Voss in chains between two of her own former subordinates—and say what had to be said.

He told them the truth. All of it. That a thing older than the crown had ridden him for thirty years; that it had engineered the hunt to feed; that every decree, every registry, every pyre traced back through his hand to its hunger. He did not ask forgiveness. He watched their faces as he spoke and saw the horror move through the hall like weather, and he thought: *this is what it is to be seen truly.* He had spent his reign being loved by people who were actually loving the thing that wore him, feared by people who were right to fear, and he had known it and been unable to say. Now they looked at him with pity and disgust and confusion, and it was the most honest he had ever been in his life.

Then he dissolved the hunt—all warrants voided, all registries ordered burned in the courtyard that afternoon, all prisoners released under Seraphine's seal. And then, while the hall was still holding its breath, he knelt. It cost him more than the walking had. His knees cracked on the stone like gunshots in the silence.

"The crown passes to my daughter," he said. "Seraphine. Not tomorrow. Now."

"Father—" Her hand was on his shoulder, trying to lift him, and her composure—the beautiful, brittle formality she had worn like armor since her return—cracked straight down the middle. "Stand up. You can't—this isn't how—"

"It's exactly how." He looked up at her, and let her see everything, because he owed her that after years of showing her nothing. "I am a hollowed house, Serry. What's left of me couldn't rule a parish. You've spent your whole life learning to see through kings—that's the education I gave you, God forgive me. Use it." He turned to the hall. "Long live Queen Seraphine."

And the hall, after a heartbeat that lasted a season, answered him.

She was crowned that same evening, hastily, in the same scarred throne room, with Bree Ashdown standing guard at the door and Ilsa Vane commanding the guard entire and no archbishop present because the Church's hierarchy had tangled itself too deep in the hunt to be trusted with the oil. She wore the circlet of state instead of the crown itself. The crown lay wrapped in three layers of blessed linen in a lead coffer, waiting to be destroyed by whatever means the archive texts prescribed, and no one in the room so much as glanced at the coffer, and everyone in the room glanced at it constantly.

Afterward, in the small hours, Aldric found Seraphine alone in the gallery above the hall, watching the registries burn in the courtyard below. Sparks rose past the windows like inverted snow.

"You should hate me," he said.

"I've practiced," she said. "It didn't take." She did not look at him. Below, a clerk fed another ledger into the flames—a whole year of names, going up one page at a time. "There was a woman. Wren Hollis. My nurse. Her name was in the registry. She was executed in the spring."

"I know." His voice failed on the second word and he started again. "I signed it. I don't remember signing it, Serry. I remember nothing of that day except waking with ink on my fingers and a taste like copper, and I have thought about that every day since without understanding it. But the hand was mine. The seal was mine."

"Yes." She was quiet a long time. The firelight moved on her face. "Here is what I've decided, Father, and I want you to hear it as your queen, because I can't yet say it as your daughter. You will live. You will live out your days quietly, in some wing of some palace, and you will not disappear into a comfortable death of drink or remorse, because disappearing is what the thing wanted for you—to use you up and leave nothing. Every morning you wake up and remain a person, you defy it. That is your penance. Not the rope. Not the veil. Just—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, queenly to the last, "—just staying."

"That," Aldric said, "is the crueler sentence."

"I know," said Seraphine, Queen of the realm, and took his hand.

* * *

Emrys woke on the third morning, and Aldric was there when she did, because he could not stay away. Of all of them, he owed her the largest debt and understood the least what it contained. Bree rarely left her side; Seraphine had given orders that the scribe was to want for nothing; Ilsa stood watch outside her door as if hunters might still come. But only Aldric came to sit in the chair by the window and look at the girl who had unmade his prison and not know how to feel anything about it except the vast dumb pressure of gratitude, which sat in his chest like a stone he could not swallow and could not spit out.

She woke slowly. Her eyes opened, fixed on the ceiling, and moved across it as though reading something. Then they found him, and there was a moment—polite, distant—in which she clearly tried to place him and could not quite manage it, not as a king, not as anything.

"You're awake," he said stupidly.

"Yes." Her voice was flat and even, a scribe's voice, reporting facts. "Am I in the palace?"

"You are."

"And the thing is gone. From you."

"Gone." He leaned forward. "Emrys. Do you know what you did?"

"I know what I was told I did." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "They keep telling me. Bree keeps telling me. My mother sang to me in the temple garden and I gave that away, and the thing in your crown had to take it because it cannot refuse a freely given love. I can recite it like a passage. I know all the words." She looked at her own hands on the coverlet, turning them over as if checking they were hers. "But when I reach for her, there's nothing. Not a face. Not even the fact of a face. There's a hole shaped like a person and the edges of it are smooth, and I understand that other people have a mother there, and I understand that I must have loved her, the way I understand that other countries exist. By report."

Aldric had faced down councils and armies and a god. He found he had no words for this.

"I'm sorry," he managed.

"Why?" she asked, genuinely curious, and that question—so simple, so bottomless—was the most frightening thing he had heard in thirty years of possession. Because he knew the answer and she could not even feel the lack of it: *because I know what it is to have things taken from you and smile politely afterward.* The entity had never managed to make him gentle about his losses. This girl had lost more than he ever had, and loss itself seemed to have been taken along with the rest.

"Bree says the grief will come," Emrys went on, in that same level voice, before he could fumble an answer. "She says it's in me somewhere, waiting. She talks to me about her—about my mother. About the singing. She remembers stories I told her on the road, apparently, and she gives them back to me like—" Emrys stopped. Her hands stilled. For a moment something moved behind her eyes, some current under ice, there and gone.

"Like what?" Aldric asked softly.

"Like coins dropped down a well that has no bottom." She frowned slightly, puzzled at her own image. "I keep waiting to hear them land."

Down in the courtyard, someone had begun sweeping the ashes of the registries into a cart, and the sound came up faintly through the window—the small, ordinary industry of a world putting itself back together. Emrys turned her head toward it, listening, and Aldric watched her try and fail to find the feeling that belonged to the sound.

"There's one more thing," she said at last. "Bree won't say it, and Seraphine changes the subject, so I'll ask you, because you don't seem able to lie anymore. The hole. Does it heal? Or do I just learn to build around it, the way people build houses against a cliff?"

And Aldric, who had built around his cliff for thirty years and knew exactly how the houses leaned, and who had also, in the last three days, watched a hedge-witch feed a stranger stories with a spoon and a hunted captain stand an unpaid watch and a new queen hold her father's hand in the dark—Aldric found, to his surprise, that he had an honest answer.

"I don't know," he said. "Ask me again in a year. But I'll tell you what I've seen since you woke: four people in this palace who refuse to let you face that cliff alone. In my experience, that's the only architecture that holds."

Emrys considered this for a long moment with her scribe's eyes, weighing it, entering it into whatever ledger remained to her. Outside, the cart wheels creaked away laden with ash, and the morning light climbed the wall, and far away in the Veiled Valley, in a garden neither of them could see, the first frost of autumn was settling on flowers planted by a woman whose name her own daughter no longer knew.

"All right," Emrys said finally, and it was the smallest word in any language, and the bravest. "In a year, then."

She closed her eyes—not to sleep, Aldric realized, but to listen, reaching once more down into the well for the sound of falling coins. And this time—though it may have been only the light, only his own ruined heart inventing mercies—he thought he saw her lips move, shaping a melody she could not name, a tune rising from somewhere deeper than memory, patient as roots, waiting to be learned again.
