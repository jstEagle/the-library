## 21. Rebuilding From Ash

Bree Ashdown had patched broken bodies before. She had set bones that farmers had shattered falling from haylofts, stitched gashes from reaping hooks, drawn fever out of children with cold compresses and patience and, once or twice, a whisper of the gift that had cost her everything. Broken bodies were honest things. They told you where it hurt. They wept when they needed to weep.

Emrys did not weep.

That was the trouble. Three days after the unbinding—after the entity had unraveled like smoke caught in a rainstorm, after King Aldric had knelt on the flagstones of his own throne room like a man waking from a thirty-year nightmare—Emrys sat in the window seat of the room Bree had given her in the little house on Cordwainer Lane, and she did not weep.

She drank her tea. She ate what was put before her, mechanically, without pleasure but without complaint. She slept, though Bree suspected sleep came to her the way rain came to a slate roof—it touched her and slid off. And when anyone spoke to her, she answered in a voice that was polite and level and utterly empty, the voice of a scribe reading aloud words she had no stake in.

"You're doing too well," Bree told her on the third evening, setting down a bowl of barley stew untouched between them. "That's what worries me."

Emrys looked up. Her eyes were the same eyes Bree had first seen in the roadside inn, wide and startled and full of a terrible new light—but the light had gone out of them now, and what remained was not darkness exactly. It was absence. A room swept clean with nothing brought back in.

"I'm fine," Emrys said.

"You're not. And I'd know. I've been not-fine professionally for fifteen years."

Something moved at the corner of Emrys's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might have lived once. "You said your wife made you laugh like a drainpipe in a storm."

"I did say that." Bree eased herself into the chair opposite. Her knees complained. Everything about her complained these days; grief had settled into her joints years ago and refused to be evicted. "Maren used to say I laughed at things that weren't funny just to fill the silence. She wasn't wrong."

"Maren," Emrys repeated. The name landed on her without weight. "The Grand Huntress."

"The very same, before she traded her herbs for handcuffs." Bree leaned forward. "Lass, do you know what you lost?"

"Yes." Flat. Immediate. "I know *that* I lost something. I know its shape the way you know the shape of furniture in a dark room—you can't see it but you walk around it. I know my mother died when I was nine. I know she sang to me in the temple garden. I know—" Her voice caught, not breaking, just snagging, like cloth on a nail. "I know I loved her. But it's like reading it in a ledger. *Item: one mother, beloved, deceased.* I can't feel the entry."

Bree nodded slowly. She had expected this. She had feared it, too, but expectation made it no easier to sit across from.

"And you think," Bree said carefully, "that because you can't feel it, there's nothing left to feel."

"I think there's nothing left *in me* to feel it with."

"No." Bree reached across the table and took Emrys's hand—the hand that had held a circle steady while a god tore itself apart, the hand that still sometimes glowed faintly at the fingertips when Emrys dreamed, though she didn't know it. "No, lass. What you gave up was the memory. The remembering of it. Not the love itself. Love isn't stored in the head. It's stored deeper—in the marrow, in the way you flinch from certain songs, in the fact that you copy texts with your ink pots arranged just so because someone taught you order is a kindness to whoever reads after you. You didn't lose your mother. You lost your *access* to her."

Emrys's jaw tightened. "And what good is gold behind a wall I can't break?"

"None," Bree admitted. "While you stand outside alone."

* * *

The telling began the fourth night, though Bree didn't call it that. She called it nothing at all. She simply started talking while they shelled peas together at the kitchen table, because her hands had always needed occupation for her mouth to find its courage, and Emrys's hands likewise—scribe's hands, restless without a pen.

"Your mother's name was Lirien," Bree said, snapping a pea pod. "Mother Sable wrote to me after you'd gone through the veil—did you know that? Old woman, long fingers, sharper than a barrel of needles. She sent letters ahead of you like breadcrumbs. I have them all still, in a tin above the larder. In hers she told me about Lirien Kavanagh, who came to the valley as a young woman with a voice like a struck bell and a temper like a wet cat."

"A wet cat," Emrys repeated. Her hands had stopped moving.

"Her words, not mine. Said Lirien could argue scripture with the High Cantor and win, and did, twice, and both times he secretly enjoyed it because nobody had stood up to him since his seminary days. She worked the temple gardens. That's where she met you—or rather, where you met her, being carried in on a deacon's shoulder at eight months old, screaming loud enough to crack a bell."

"I don't remember any of this."

"I know," Bree said gently. "But your body does. Watch." She hummed—three notes only, an ascending phrase, half-remembered herself, secondhand from Mother Sable's letter, which had included the notation in a careful clerk's hand. *She sang this to Emrys every night. The Garden Song, the valley folk call it.*

Emrys went still. Not frozen—still the way water goes still before it remembers it is a spring. Her lips parted. And though she said nothing, though she claimed nothing, her breath changed rhythm to match the tune, drawing where the melody drew, releasing where it released.

"There," Bree said softly. "There she is."

"That's not her. That's just—a song."

"That's a song a mother sang to her daughter ten thousand times until the daughter's lungs learned it better than her mind ever did." Brie set down the peas. "Memory is a story we tell, lass. Stories can be stolen. But the telling itself—the cadence, the breath—that lives somewhere the entity couldn't reach. You are still her daughter down to the bottom of you. We're just going to go down and find her, bit by bit."

Emrys looked at her then, truly looked, and Bree saw the first crack in the emptiness—not a breaking but a thaw, ice shifting on a winter river. "And if I find nothing?"

"Then I'll tell you stories until you dream some new ones, and those will count too." Bree shrugged. "I raised no children of my own. Maren and I tried everything but the ordinary ways. So you'll forgive me if I take the chance to practice on you."

* * *

It became ritual. Every evening after supper, Bree opened the tin above the larder and read a letter, or told a story, or simply described: the temple garden with its blue-flowered rosemary hedges; Lirien's habit of talking to bees as though they were errant nephews; the winter the valley froze its waterfall solid and Lirien led the children out to slide down it despite Mother Sable's thunderous disapproval—and how Mother Sable, arriving to scold, had instead sat down hard on the ice and slid the whole length of the falls shrieking like a girl.

Emrys listened the way a starving person eats—slowly at first, suspicious of the food, then helplessly, hungrily, unable to stop.

Some nights she felt nothing and said so, flat and ashamed, and Bree would say, "Good. Now you know the shape of the hole. You can't fill a hole you won't look at." Other nights something small surfaced—a smell of crushed rosemary, a flash of brown hair against green leaves—and Emrys would grip the table edge as though the room were tilting.

On the ninth night, Seraphine came.

The princess arrived unannounced and unroyal, in a plain gray cloak, carrying a basket of oranges and a look on her face that Bree recognized as the expression of a woman who has rehearsed a speech six times and intends to deliver none of it. She was queen now in all but ceremony—Aldric's abdication was being formalized by councils and heralds, and Seraphine had spent the weeks since the unbinding dismantling the hunt registry name by name, a labor she described as "pulling nails out of a crucifixion."

"I can't stay long," she said, sitting awkwardly at the kitchen table, which had never hosted royalty and handled it badly, wobbling. "I just—I wanted to bring these. Oranges. From the palace conservatory. My father used to—" She stopped. Started again. "I heard you liked stories. About the temple. I thought perhaps I owed you some. About what happened after. What your sacrifice bought."

Emrys said, "You don't owe me anything."

"I owe you a father," Seraphine said quietly. "A whole one. I'd written him off as lost—I'd written *us* off. And then a scribe with no army and no title walked into a throne room and unmade a god with a memory." She pushed the basket across the table. "So let me pay in the only coin I have. Stories. And time."

She came the next night too. And the next. She learned where the cups lived. She learned that Bree's stew required bread as ballast and that Ilsa Vane took her tea bitter enough to strip varnish.

Because on the eleventh night, Ilsa came as well.

* * *

Ilsa Vane arrived at Cordwainer Lane looking like a woman who had dressed for a tribunal and found a birthday. She stood in the doorway in her best coat—her only coat, Bree suspected, the hunt having confiscated or disgraced the rest—with her commission papers conspicuously absent and her hands conspicuously uncertain what to do without them.

"I burned them," she said, by way of greeting. "What was left of them. Voss is in custody; Aldric's testimony will hang her or worse. The hunt's dissolved. Officially. There's paperwork." Her mouth twisted. "There's always paperwork."

"Would you like tea?" Bree asked.

"I would like," Ilsa said, "to make a proposition. And I'd like to make it standing, because if I sit down I'll lose my nerve, and Ilsa Vane does not lose her nerve, she misplaces it strategically."

"Propose, then," Bree said, folding her arms, though she already knew—had known since the fen market, since the moment a hunt-captain had turned her squad aside with a lie and chosen, for the first time in her iron life, the harder right over the easier wrong.

Ilsa cleared her throat. She addressed a point approximately three feet to the left of Emrys's head.

"I am a woman without employment, without reputation, and without any talent except finding people who don't wish to be found, which I am given to understand is now illegal or soon will be. I have money saved that I cannot spend in polite company. I have skills useful mostly to criminals and midwives. And I have—" her jaw worked— "debts. To this house. To each person in it. Debts I cannot repay because what was taken from you, scribe, cannot be returned, and what was risked by you, witch, and you, Highness, cannot be balanced."

"So you propose," Seraphine said slowly, "to—"

"I propose a household." The words came out of Ilsa like splinters, one at a time, rough and necessary. "Formal. Sworn. Under whatever law Her Majesty cares to invent for it—which I'm told she's rather good at inventing, lately. A hearth-share. A common purse. I cook, gods help us all, because I've eaten Bree's cooking and someone must intervene. I keep the books. I stand the door at night so the rest of you can sleep. I—" and here her voice finally failed her, and she finished hoarsely, "I would like, very much, to come home to somewhere. I never have."

The kitchen was silent. Rain had begun against the window, soft and businesslike.

Emrys stood. She crossed to Ilsa—the hunt-captain who had looked away at the crucial moment, who had delayed the search a day, who had misdirected her squad at the fen market, who had burned her papers in a palace under siege—and she took the woman's hands in her own glowing, gifted, grieving ones.

"You looked away," Emrys said. "When everyone else watched. You looked away, and it saved my life, and then you kept looking away, further and further, until you were looking straight at us." She squeezed. "Yes. Come home."

Seraphine was already pulling off her signet ring. "There's an old form," she said, "from before the hunts. Hearth-law. Older than crowns. You swear on salt and firelight—no priests, no parchment, nothing the crown can dissolve." Her eyes were bright and fierce. "I'd be honored to swear it with you. All of you. If Bree agrees to be head of hearth, since it's her roof."

Bree found, to her enormous irritation, that she was crying. "Fifteen years I've eaten alone at this table," she managed. "Of course it's my roof. It's all of yours now too. Gods, one of you fetch the salt before I become completely undignified."

They swore it that same night, because grief had taught all four of them not to wait for perfect conditions. Salt in an iron dish. Firelight from the hearth Bree had cooked a thousand solitary meals over. Hands joined in a rough circle—witch, scribe, soldier, sovereign—and Seraphine spoke the old words slowly, translating as she went: *By bread and by burden, by sorrow shared and joy halved, I bind my luck to yours.*

When it was done, Ilsa sat down abruptly, as if her legs had finally filed their resignation. "Well," she said. "That's that, then."

"That's that," Bree agreed, wiping her eyes with her apron.

* * *

The weeping, when it finally came, came on a Tuesday.

Bree would remember the ordinariness of it forever. No anniversary, no letter, no trigger anyone could name. Just a grey afternoon, rain on the glass, Ilsa mending a net by the fire, Seraphine sprawled on the floor copying out the new hearth-laws in triplicate because she distrusted clerks, and Emrys at the window seat with the tin of letters open on her lap, reading—for the first time—one of Mother Sable's pages in her own voice, aloud, because the night before she had dreamed in her mother's cadence and woken with the melody caught in her teeth.

"*Lirien says you'll be tall,*" Emrys read. "*She says you'll have her stubbornness and your father's eyebrows, and she says she's sorry about the eyebrows. She says—*" 

Emrys stopped.

Bree looked up from her mortar. Ilsa's needle froze mid-stitch. Seraphine's pen lifted from the page.

Emrys was staring at the letter, and her shoulders had begun to shake, small tremors at first, then great ones, and the sound that came out of her was raw and rusty and ancient, the sound of a nine-year-old girl locked inside a grown woman for three hollow weeks, finally given the door.

"Oh—oh, lass—" Bree was across the room before she'd decided to move, gathering Emrys in, and Emrys clung to her like a drowning woman, sobbing words that broke apart even as they left her:

"I can't remember her—I can't—I loved her so much and I can't remember *her*—"

"No," Bree said into her hair, rocking her, her own tears falling freely now. "You can't. And it isn't fair, and I won't pretend it is. But listen to me, Emrys Kavanagh, listen: you are weeping for her. Do you understand? The grief is *there*. Which means the love is there. Nobody weeps like this for an empty ledger-entry. She's in you. She never left. We'll walk down to her together, as many times as it takes, for as many years as it takes, and every story we tell you, you'll keep this time—new memories, ours and yours, built around the hole like a tree grows around a wound until you can't tell which is which."

Ilsa had come to kneel beside them, one broad hand resting on Emrys's shoulder, saying nothing, because Ilsa's silences had always been her most fluent language. And Seraphine—queen-to-be, who had been raised to believe tears were a currency spent only in private—simply sat down on the floor with the rest of them, in the firelight, in the rain-sound, and took Emrys's hand and held it, and stayed.

They stayed that way a long while, four women on the floor of a small warm kitchen, while the storm worked itself out against the windows and, slowly, more gently than it began, so did Emrys.

Later—much later, when the fire had been fed and the kettle put on and Emrys lay sleeping on the settle with the letter still loose in her hand—Bree stood at the hearth watching her household breathe, and thought about gardens. About seeds in dark soil, dormant, patient, needing only tending.

On the table, among the orange peels and the spilled salt and the triplicate laws, lay a letter that had arrived that morning from the Veiled Valley. Bree had not yet shown it to Emrys. Mother Sable's hand, unmistakable, on the outside—and beneath the elder's name, a second line in different ink, newer, that Bree had read four times already with her heart climbing:

*Come in spring. Bring her. The garden is blooming—and there is something here she should see.*

Bree tucked the letter into the tin, closed the lid, and let herself believe.
