## 22. New Ink, Old Garden

The road into the Veiled Valley had not changed, and that was the strangest thing of all.

Emrys walked it on foot, as she had a thousand times before, though now the road felt shorter than memory insisted—memory that was not hers anymore, or not entirely. She had left Bree and Ilsa and Seraphine at the crossroads inn two days south, with kisses and jokes and Ilsa's gruff insistence that she take the good cloak, and for two days she had climbed through foothills that hummed faintly under her feet, the way all land did now, the way it had since the morning the entity unraveled and the world's magic stopped flinching from itself.

The valley's border was invisible. It always had been. But Emrys felt it now in a way she never could have as a girl: a softening of the hum, like stepping out of wind into a walled garden. Inside the Veil, the threads went quiet. The valley kept its old bargain—no magic within—and for the first time in her life, Emrys understood what that silence cost, and what it had protected, and why Mother Sable had spent fifty years tending both sides of that ledger without telling anyone.

The wardstone at the gate still stood where it always had, squat and mossy, unremarkable to anyone who didn't know. Emrys laid her palm against it.

Nothing happened. Nothing needed to. The hunt was dissolved; the registries burned by Seraphine's own decree in her first week as regent; the relic that had pierced the wards locked now in the archive beneath the temple, labeled in Emrys's own careful hand: *Let this be studied, and let it never again be used.*

She walked up through the orchards in late-afternoon light. Someone had pruned them well. Apple blossom drifted across the path like slow snow, and Emrys caught it on her sleeves and did not brush it away.

The temple came into view the way it always had—grey stone gone gold at this hour, bell tower sharp against the sky—and Emrys stopped at the edge of the courtyard and simply stood there, letting the shape of it settle over her. This was the place that had made her. This was the place she had fled from in terror with a stolen travel pass and a heart full of wasps. This was the place where she had been a scribe who hid warmth in her fingertips and thought that hiding was the same as living.

The scriptorium windows were open. Through one of them she could see the long tables, the sloped desks, the ink pots catching light. And at the far table—the one by the window, the one Emrys had worked at for eleven years—a young woman sat copying, tongue pressed to the corner of her mouth, and around her hovering hand, unmistakable in the open air, curled a ribbon of pale gold light.

Emrys smiled until her face ached.

They weren't hiding anymore. Not here. The Veil still held—it would hold for generations yet, Mother Sable said, barring crowns and relics—but what happened inside a person, that was nobody's registry now.

"Are you going to stand there blooming at my apple trees all evening," said a voice behind her, "or are you going to come inside before the tea goes cold?"

Mother Sable had shrunk. That was the first thing. Her shoulders had narrowed, her hands had gone translucent at the knuckles, and she used a stick now—not quite a cane, not quite admitting to be one—to cross the cloister. But her eyes were exactly the same: black, dry, quick, missing nothing, forgiving less than they appeared to.

"You knew I was coming," Emrys said.

"I know everything. It's the burden of being old in a small place." Sable looked her up and down with theatrical thoroughness. "You're thinner. Your hair's a disgrace. You've got ink on your wrist in three colors, which means you're writing something, which means you've finally stopped moping."

"I wasn't moping."

"You were moping for four months. Bree wrote me letters. Very detailed letters." Sable turned toward the refectory door. "Come. Tea. Then you'll tell me about the book, because you have the look of someone with a book. Everyone does, when they finally start one. It's insufferable."

* * *

The refectory smelled the same—beeswax and barley bread and lamp oil—and Emrys sat in her old seat at the long table and found that her body remembered the exact height of the bench, the way the grain of the wood had worn smooth under her forearms. She told Sable about the house in the capital, the narrow tall house on Cordwainer Row with the crooked chimney that Bree had fallen in love with at first sight and Ilsa had fallen in love with second, after inspecting every beam for structural treachery. She told her about the room they'd given her, the big one at the back with the good light, because "you're the one making something," Seraphine had said, in the tone of a princess signing a decree, "and we will not have you squinting in an attic like some martyr."

"And the book?" Sable asked.

Emrys turned her teacup slowly on the table. "It's about the queen," she said. "Maren the First. The one who bound the starving god." She had found the queen in the archive beneath this very temple, in ledgers and letters and one impossible journal, and had carried her out of that buried dark and into the light of a finished manuscript. "Everyone remembers her as the Witch-Queen. The burnings started under her name, twisted about. I want—" She stopped, restarted. "I want people to know what she actually paid. What it cost. What it means, that binding can be undone by love freely given. Nobody knows that story. They only know she won."

"And do you?" Sable asked quietly. "Know what it means?"

Emrys looked at her hands. There was ink on the wrist, three colors—umber, verdigris, and the deep violet she mixed herself now—and below the ink, faint white scars where the rite had taken hold of her, where a god had unraveled through her like thread pulled from a seam.

"No," she said honestly. "Not all of it. But I'm writing toward it."

Sable nodded, satisfied, and said nothing more, which was her way of saying everything.

* * *

In the morning, Emrys went to the garden.

She hadn't planned it. She woke before the bell, in her narrow childhood cell in the novices' wing where Sable had put her without asking—which was correct, because it was hers—and lay listening to the valley's particular silence, and then she was walking, barefoot in the dew, down through the cloister to the walled kitchen garden on the temple's south side.

Her mother's garden.

She knew that it had been, even if she couldn't remember it being. That was the shape of the hole in her: not absence exactly, but the outline of an absence, like the negative space around a pressed flower. Mother Sable had told her the story, months ago, in a letter written in her cramped merciless hand: *Your mother, Lira, kept the south garden for nine years. She sang to it. She sang to you in it, evenings, when the light came slantwise through the wall. You were three when the fever took her. You used to say the plants grew because they liked her songs better than the rain.*

Emrys read the letter so many times she could recite it. She could not remember a single note of the song.

But the garden—she stood at the gate now and looked at what nine years of other hands had made of it—was still recognizably a garden built by someone who loved it. Beds laid out in curves rather than rows. A stone bench in the corner where the sun lingered longest. An old plum tree against the south wall, gnarled and heavy with unripe fruit, planted too close to the stones by someone who hadn't expected to be there forty years to watch it crowd.

Emrys opened the gate and walked in and knelt in the dirt, and put her hands into the warm earth, and wept.

She hadn't been able to, at first. That was the terrible arithmetic of the sacrifice: grief requires a beloved, and her beloved had been taken whole. In the hollow morning after the unbinding she had looked at Bree across a breakfast table and said, in a voice that frightened them all, "I know I lost something. I just can't feel what." For weeks she had moved through the world like a page with the ink washed off—clean, blank, functional. Bree had refused to accept it. Bree, who had lost her wife and rebuilt herself around the loss like a vine around a dead trellis, had sat with Emrys night after night and told her stories: about Lira Kavanagh, who smuggled gifted children through the Veil with her husband before he was taken and she came home to the valley; who grew feverfew because it looked like scattered stars; who died singing, the nurse said, or maybe the nurse invented it kindly, and it didn't matter, because it was true anyway.

*Grief shared becomes bearable.* Bree had said that once, hands around a teapot, and Emrys had hated her a little, and then, slowly, over months, discovered it was simply the truth.

So now she knelt in her mother's garden and cried, and it hurt, and the hurt was proof of something rather than nothing, and she let it run its course like weather.

When she looked up, eyes streaming, a boy of perhaps eight stood at the gate staring at her—one of the temple foundlings, freckled, alarmed.

"Are you sick?" he demanded.

"No," Emrys managed. "I'm remembering somebody."

"That doesn't make you cry."

"It does," Emrys said, wiping her face with her wrist, adding a fourth color of smudge to the ink, "if you do it properly."

He considered this with great seriousness, then held out a slightly bruised plum, stolen from the tree behind him. Emrys took it. It seemed like the correct liturgy.

* * *

She stayed five days.

On the second day, Sable put her to work in the scriptorium—not at her old desk, which belonged to the golden-handed girl now, but at a spare table near the door—and Emrys copied warding texts with her own gift humming quiet under the Veil, and found the work had changed flavor entirely. Once it had been hiding. Now it was choosing. She wrote out the ward-sigils in her best formal hand, and beside each one, in smaller cursive, she added notes for the new edition Sable was preparing: *this sigil may be safely learned by the ungifted; this one should be taught with the calming exercise first; this passage reflects the fear of its era, not its necessity.*

A book about the old texts, for a new age. Everyone had the look of someone with a book, apparently.

On the third day she taught the foundlings letters in the morning and herb-lore in the afternoon, at Bree's request relayed through Sable—*ask her to check the feverfew beds, mine have gone woody and she has opinions*—and discovered eight children following her around the garden like ducklings, demanding stories about the capital, about the palace, about whether Princess Seraphine was really seven feet tall (she was not; she was merely magnificent).

On the fourth day she sang.

She didn't know the song. That was the point, or part of it. She stood among the vegetable beds at evening with the light coming slantwise through the wall exactly as the letter described, and she made up a song, low and tuneless and half-laughing, about the plum tree and the freckled boy and the tea that was always cold, and she sang it to the garden because her mother had sung to a garden once, and gardens remember. The beans that season came in heavy. Bree would have had theories.

On the fifth day, Mother Sable found her packing at dawn and sat down heavily on the novices' cell's single chair.

"You're leaving."

"Yes."

"The house on Cordwainer Row."

"Three people are waiting for me there. One of them burns things when she cooks and pretends it's intentional. Another is drafting agricultural reform legislation at the kitchen table and growling at it. The third keeps sharpening knives that are already sharp." Emrys folded her spare shift. "And I have a manuscript due to a very impatient printer, and a garden of my own going in behind the house, and I promised the boy I'd write."

Sable was silent for a while. Beyond the window the bell rang for prime, and neither of them moved.

"When you first came back," Sable said at last, "I watched you walk through the courtyard, and I thought: she looks like a stranger here. And then I watched you in the garden, and I thought: no. She looks like someone who belongs in two places. That's rarer than belonging in one. Most people never manage even the one."

"I learned it from a household," Emrys said. "We're four people from four ruined beginnings sharing one crooked chimney. Belonging turns out to be something you build, not something you find."

"Did you read that somewhere?"

"I'm writing it somewhere. There's a difference."

Sable laughed—dry as ever, but warm underneath—and rose with the help of her not-quite-cane, and embraced Emrys briefly and fiercely, smelling of lamp oil and barley bread, and then pushed her back to arm's length and inspected her one final time.

"Send me the book when it's printed. The first copy, mind, not some polite later printing."

"You'll get page proofs, and you'll mark them all up, and I'll ignore half your corrections."

"Naturally." Sable paused at the door. "Your mother would be proud of you, Emrys. I know you can't hear that the way you'd want to. But it's true, and true things should be said aloud whether or not they land where aimed."

Emrys stood in her narrow cell, in her old valley, holding the words like water in cupped hands.

"Thank you," she said. "For keeping the garden. For keeping the letter. For keeping everything."

"Somebody had to," said Mother Sable, "until you could."

* * *

She crossed the border at dawn, as she had fled across it once, but this time she stopped at the wardstone and rested her hand on the moss and said goodbye properly, and this time, passing out of the Veil, the world's hum rose around her not like a flood but like an orchestra tuning—all those threads of power waking to greet her, the land recognizing her, and she walked into it unhurried, unafraid, wearing her mother's old traveling cloak that Sable had pressed on her at the gate, *it's been in a chest for years, it wants walking.*

The journey south took four days and she enjoyed nearly all of them. At the fen market she bought plums from a stall near the spot where Ilsa Vane had once cornered her with hunters and lies, and ate them as she walked, and left the pits in her pocket to plant. At a roadside inn she healed a carter's sprained shoulder openly, for a hot meal and a song, and nobody reached for iron, and nobody flinched, and the carter's wife played a fiddle badly and everyone danced anyway.

On the last evening she crested the hill above the capital and saw the city spread out gold and grey, the palace on its rock with the new banner flying—Seraphine's sun-and-quill, chosen over her father's crown-and-thorn—and smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, including, she knew without looking, one crooked chimney on Cordwainer Row.

The house smelled of burnt onions and lamp oil and, beneath it, the green scent of the seedlings in the cold frame that Bree was babysitting with excessive tenderness. Ilsa answered the door with a knife in hand out of pure habit, saw Emrys, and put the knife down with the closest thing to a smile her face permitted, which was the loosening of a frown by two degrees—an embrace from Captain Vane.

"You're brown," Ilsa said. "Valley agrees with you."

"Valley and I have come to an understanding." Emrys stepped in, set down her pack, breathed the burnt-onion air. "Where is everyone?"

"Kitchen. Seraphine's brought work home again. Some petition about hedge-witch licensing." A pause, perfectly deadpan. "She's named you as an expert witness. There may be shouting. There's usually shouting."

"Wonderful," said Emrys, and meant it, and went toward the noise and the light.

That night, after the shouting (there was shouting; it ended in laughter; the petition passed committee by month's end), after the meal and the dishes and Seraphine's account of the latest council session delivered in devastating imitation of three separate ministers, Emrys climbed to her room at the back of the house with the good light, and sat at her desk, and unwrapped the parcel she had carried the whole way from the valley: cuttings from her mother's garden. Feverfew, thyme, a whip of the old plum tree, wrapped in damp cloth and hope.

She carried them down in the dark and planted them by lantern-light in the little plot behind the house, where the soil was already turned and waiting, where six apple pips from the temple orchard had already sprouted their first pale leaves. She pressed the feverfew in with her palms. She sang to it—her own made-up song, wordless, half-laughing—and around her wrists, softly, the threads of the world leaned close to listen, and the little plants stood up straighter in the dark, and that was not magic exactly, or not only magic, and it did not matter which, because it was true anyway.

Then she went up to her desk, trimmed the lamp, and opened the manuscript: *An Ember for the Veil*, in her own hand, three hundred pages and counting. On the title page beneath the title she had already written the dedication, the only part of the book she had never once revised:

*For my mother, whose name I keep, whose garden I grow, whose song I sing though I cannot recall the tune. Love outlives remembrance.*

She dipped her pen. Outside, the city hummed; inside, the household settled into its creaks and breathings; in the garden, the feverfew took root.

Emrys Kavanagh began to write.
