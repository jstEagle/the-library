## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bees woke before I did, the way they always did, and by the time I came down the hill with my veil pushed up around my ears they had already decided the day was worth working. I envied them that. A bee has never once lain awake wondering whether it was meant for something larger than its hive.

"Good morning, tyrants," I told them.

They ignored me, which is the correct way to treat anyone who opens your house and steals from you. I set the smoker down on the flat stone beside the third hive — the one Maren called the Widow, because she'd killed two queens in a row and sulked about it ever since — and worked the lid loose with slow hands. Slow hands matter with bees. Fast hands tell them you're afraid, and afraid is a thing they can smell the way I can smell woodsmoke or rain coming off the ridge.

I am not afraid of bees. I want that on the record, somewhere, in case anyone ever writes this down. Whatever else is true of me, whatever the world outside the treeline believes, I was never once stung out of malice. The Widow got me twice last summer, but both times were my fault, and both times I apologized aloud like an idiot, and both times she seemed to accept it.

That's the whole of my kingdom: four hives, a crooked cottage I rent from a shepherd who thinks my name is Tam, a garden that grows what gardens grow, and a valley that forgets.

I should explain the valley.

* * *

The Veil sits in a fold of mountains that no map bothers with, a green thumbprint pressed between two ridgelines, maybe six miles end to end if you walked it honestly. There are perhaps forty people here. They farm, they keep sheep, they argue about the mill pond, they bury their dead under rowan trees because that's how it's always been done. To look at us you'd see nothing but poverty and peace.

But walk to the northern treeline — the one where the pines go dark and the air changes taste, like water left standing too long — and something happens at the edge of it. Or rather, something stops happening.

Magic dies there.

Not fades. Not thins. Dies, the way a candle dies when you pinch it: instant, total, a little offended about it. Any spell carried across that line simply ceases. A witch can stand at the border with her hands full of light and watch it gutter out of her fingers like breath off a mirror. Charms unravel. Wards collapse into ordinary rope and rhyme. Enchanted things cross over as merely things — a sword becomes iron, a talking ring becomes jewelry, a curse becomes a bad mood.

And memories do the same.

This is the part outsiders never believe until they feel it. Anyone who enters the Veil carrying magic begins to forget themselves. Not all at once — it comes on gently, like falling asleep in front of a fire. First the small things go: the name of your horse, the reason you came, the face of whoever sent you. Then the larger ones. Wizards have wandered in here chasing rumors and ended up living out their days as contented carpenters, unable to say why they'd chosen carpentry, only that it felt right. The valley keeps you. It keeps you so well that after a year you couldn't leave if you wanted to, because wanting requires knowing what you're missing.

I've lived here three years, and I remember everything.

That should be impossible. I know it should be impossible, because Maren told me so herself, back when she still spoke to me in more than single sentences. The Veil takes the memory of anyone touched by magic, and I am — I was — the most touched person in any room I've ever stood in. But I wake every morning as Odile Coldharbour, apprentice runaway, and I remember the road south, and the night I left, and the thing I felt rising in my chest like a second heartbeat when I ran.

Maren did that. She built me a hole in the forgetting and tucked me inside it, the way you'd shelter a flame with cupped hands. I don't know what it cost her. I've never asked. We don't speak, except through the bees, which sounds like a riddle and isn't: her hives and mine share a drone line, and once a season she sends word by marking a queen in a color only another keeper would notice. Blue means stay hidden. Red means trouble passed us by. Last spring she sent white, which I'd never seen before, and which I chose to interpret as nothing at all.

Her cottage sits at the far end of the valley, past the mill, close enough to the treeline that her garden grows strange. I take a longer route to town specifically to avoid passing it. This is cowardice, probably. I've made peace with being a coward; it's kept me alive.

* * *

Here is what a day looks like when you are hiding from the entire architecture of the world:

You rise before the sun because hives don't care about your nightmares. You check the brood frames for mites. You scrape propolis off your knuckles and fail, as always, to get it all off, so that you spend the rest of the day smelling faintly of resin and feeling vaguely sealed shut. You carry wax down to Toma the chandler, who pays you in candles and gossip and never asks why a grown man with soft hands and no family tends bees in a valley nobody remembers to visit. You eat bread. You mend the fence the goats broke, again, with the same wire, again. You watch the light come down the eastern ridge and think, every single time, without meaning to: *somewhere out there, past those mountains, there is a drowned kingdom, and everyone who could tell me why I flinch at my own reflection is dead or lying.*

Then the sun sets, and you bar your door even though nothing in the Veil needs barring, and you sleep, mostly.

It's a good life. I want to be clear about that. It is genuinely, truly a good life, and there are nights — most nights — when the smallness of it feels like the warmest coat I own. Nothing here knows what I am. Nothing here can reach me. The valley holds me the way the hive holds the queen: fed, protected, and utterly without options.

The itch starts around midday, usually. Today it started early.

* * *

It started with the stranger's horse.

I was at the Widow's hive when I heard hooves on the north road — not the soft double-beat of a valley pony but the long stride of a big animal, a warhorse or something near it. Nobody rides warhorses here. Nobody rides anything bigger than a mule, because the passes won't allow it and the valley doesn't need it.

I put my hand flat against the hive box and felt the hum change pitch. Bees go quiet when something wrong walks past. Every keeper knows it. They don't sting first; they hold their breath.

Through the alders I saw him: a rider in a grey traveling cloak, leading his horse on foot, moving slowly along the road toward the treeline like a man reading a letter he didn't like. He stopped at the edge of the pines. He stood there a long moment, one hand on his horse's neck, looking into the valley the way a man looks into a well he suspects holds something he lost.

Then he turned around and rode away.

That's all. That's the whole event. A man looked at our valley, frowned, and left, and by tomorrow he wouldn't remember doing it — the Veil would take the errand and the road and probably the horse's name right out of his head, and he'd find himself three days south wondering why his boots smelled of pine.

Except.

Except that he hadn't crossed the line. He'd stood at it, and I'd watched his face while the forgetting took its grip, and what I saw wasn't confusion. It was resistance. He'd planted his feet like a man fighting a current, jaw set, eyes narrowed, and for one long moment — I counted it, five heartbeats, maybe six — he held himself against the Veil the way you hold a door against wind.

Travelers don't do that. Travelers dissolve. I've seen it happen: the wool merchant who came through two summers ago chasing a debt, laughing at some joke of his own one minute and blinking at us like a newborn the next. The forgetting isn't painful and it isn't optional. Nobody fights it, because by the time you'd know to fight it, you've already forgotten there's anything to fight.

He knew. He stood at my valley's door and he knew exactly what was trying to take his errand from him, and he leaned into it anyway, and then — deliberately, unhurried, like a man finishing a chore — he let it win. He turned his horse and rode south, and I stood among my hives with smoke curling around my wrists and understood that I had just watched someone test a lock.

* * *

I finished the inspection. I want credit for that. My hands shook enough that the Widow's girls noticed and buzzed at me in that low offended register, and I told them, "Not today," and they let it go, because unlike me they have no imagination.

But all afternoon the thought worked at me like a burr in a sock: *someone is checking the lock.* And locks get checked for two reasons only — to make sure they hold, or to learn how to open them.

By evening I'd talked myself into three explanations, each stupider than the last. A surveyor. A lost pilgrim. A smuggler scouting routes. The Veil has been forgotten by the world for a hundred years; that's its whole purpose, its whole genius. Whoever built it — and I have my suspicions, and my suspicions wear Maren's face — built it to be boring. Valleys that kill spells and eat memories don't attract armies. They attract nothing. That's the point.

So why did the rider lean into the forgetting like a man leaning into a gale?

I sat on my step with the last light going amber behind the western ridge and let myself do the thing I never let myself do, which was think about the outside. About Ashvael, the kingdom beneath the sea, the one whose name I can't hear spoken without my teeth aching. About Vane Morrel, whose name I know only because Maren said it once, spitting, like a curse she'd bitten in half. About the free cities, and the councils, and the wars that happen out there in the loud world while I count bees.

Three years of quiet. Three years of the valley holding me like a cupped hand.

And today somebody stood at the rim of the cup and looked in.

* * *

I told myself I'd sleep on it. I told myself a lot of things, standing there in the blue dusk with the hives murmuring behind me like a congregation. Then I did the thing I'd sworn I wouldn't do, which was walk the long way around the mill pond, past the shepherd's fallow field, right up to the low stone wall at the edge of Maren's garden, where the cabbages grow slightly wrong and the rowan tree leans like it's eavesdropping.

Her window was lit. Of course it was. Maren sleeps the way cats do, in installments, and her lamp burns at all hours because she says darkness is "a professional courtesy to things that prefer it."

I stood in the cold with my hands in my pockets and rehearsed what I'd say. *Someone rode the north road today. He fought the Veil. What does that mean? What did you build? What am I?* Forty questions, and underneath all of them, the one I've carried since the night I ran: *are we safe?*

She came to the window before I knocked. She does that too — senses a listener the way the bees sense weather. For a moment her silhouette just stood there, small and bent and unmistakable, and I thought she might open the casement and say my name, my real name, the one I haven't heard spoken aloud in three years.

She pinched out the lamp instead.

I stood in the dark a moment longer, feeling like a fool, feeling worse than a fool — feeling *seen*, though there was nothing to see me but cabbages. Then I turned for home, and that's when I noticed the smell.

Woodsmoke, yes, from her chimney. Pine, from the treeline. But under it, faint and wrong, like a coin held too long in a closed fist: ash and salt. The smell of a drowned place. The smell I sometimes catch in dreams and wake from with my heart going like a drum.

It was coming from the north. From the treeline. From beyond it, thin as thread, riding a wind that shouldn't have been able to carry anything across a border where even memory dies.

Something out there had found a way to whisper through the Veil.

And the whisper, as far as I could tell, was my name.
