## 2. A Book That Should Not Know Me

The bees woke before I did, which was usual. I liked to think of it as their way of keeping me honest—no sleeping in for a man whose livelihood hung in wax and wing. The morning was cold enough that my breath fogged over the hive lids, and I stood there with my hands in my armpits watching the first scouts stumble out into the grey light like tiny drunkards reporting for duty.

I'd been in the Veil three years now. Long enough that the valley had stopped feeling like hiding and started feeling like living, which was either the best thing that had ever happened to me or the most dangerous lie I'd ever told myself. The valley didn't care which. It took you in, smoothed your edges, and let you forget—gently, thoroughly, the way water smooths a stone—that there had ever been anything sharp about you at all.

That was the whole trick of the place. Magic couldn't cross the treeline. Whatever anyone had been outside—a hedge-witch's apprentice, a runaway, whatever name I'd carried down out of the mountains—the valley dissolved it. Not cruelly. Kindly, almost. Like a mother wiping jam off a child's face.

I sold honey. That was what Odile Coldharbour did here. Not much of a name. Not much of a life. And every morning when I lifted the hive lids and the smoke curled up sweet and I heard the hum go quiet under my hands, I thought: this is enough. This is enough. This is enough.

The repetition was the point. A prayer against wanting more.

But some mornings the prayer didn't take, and this was one of them. I don't know why. Maybe the light came through the pines at an angle that reminded me of somewhere else. Maybe it was the dream I couldn't hold on to—something about a door, a door standing open, and me standing in front of it, and my hands shaking so hard I couldn't reach for the latch no matter how badly I wanted to. I woke with my fists clenched so tight my nails had left crescents in my palms.

So when I packed the honey jars into the basket—six of them, wrapped in straw, heavy as sin—I told myself it was just a trade day. Paper day. Every few weeks I walked the valley road to Aldous Vane's house and traded honey for paper, because paper cost coin and coin was something a beekeeper in a hidden valley didn't have much use for. Aldous kept the archive. He was the closest thing the Veil had to a scholar, a priest, and a government, all rolled into one stooped, ink-stained body.

I told myself it was just paper day.

My hands knew better. They'd been restless since dawn.

* * *

Aldous Vane's house sat where the valley floor began its long tilt toward the river, a squat stone building with a slate roof furred green with moss and a chimney that smoked crooked, like it had given up trying to be straight. He lived surrounded by his books. Not shelves of them—*walls* of them, stacked from floor to ceiling in every room, in the kitchen, in the hall, in the privy, God help him, until the house itself seemed less a dwelling than a container, a jar with a man sealed inside it.

He answered the door slowly. He always answered the door slowly, as if opening it were a decision requiring deliberation, as if each visitor might be the last visitor he ever chose to admit. His eyes were pale behind spectacles thick as bottle-bottoms, and they went straight past my face to the basket.

"Six jars," he said. "Good."

"Dark honey this time. Late season. The heather's been strong."

"Strong heather makes strong honey." He said it like a verdict. Then he held out his hand—not for the jars, but flat, palm up, waiting—and I understood he wanted me to come in, and I understood also that I did not want to come in, and I understood, thirdly and finally, that I would come in anyway, because refusing Aldous Vane required a kind of social courage I had never managed to cultivate in three years of careful, deliberate smallness.

His hallway smelled of dust and tallow and old glue. Books leaned against the walls in columns that swayed slightly when you passed, like reeds. I set the basket down inside the door and followed him back toward the study, walking carefully, because a toppled column of Aldous's books could bury a man.

The study was worse than the hall. Better, if you were the kind of person who loved that sort of thing—which Aldous was, entirely, and which I was not, mostly. Desks buried under folios. A globe with half its countries worn away by thumbs. An iron-bound chest in the corner that I had never once seen him open in all my trade days, though I'd looked at it sidelong often enough, the way you look at a dog that might bite.

"Paper," he said, and pulled a bundle from a drawer without looking, the way another man might hand you bread. "Same as last time. Rags, pressed thin. It takes ink well enough if you're patient with it."

"I'm patient," I said.

"You are." He said it approvingly, and then, because he was Aldous and could never leave well alone: "Perhaps too patient. Patience in a young man is either discipline or cowardice, and only time tells which."

I took the paper. I should have taken the paper and left. That was the script—honey for paper, a little talk about weather and hives, a nod, a walk home before the light went. Three years of the same script. But today the script snagged somewhere, caught on something, and I found myself still standing in the study when I should have been halfway home, and Aldous was saying, "You'll want to see this," and I hadn't asked to see anything.

He hadn't asked me either. That was the strange part. He'd simply decided, mid-conversation, mid-handing-over-of-paper, that today I would see it. As if the script itself had changed while neither of us was looking.

"This," he said again, and turned from his desk carrying a book.

Not just any book. I knew books. I'd grown up around Maren's grimoires, her bundles of dried herbs tied with red thread, her jars of things I'd been forbidden to ask about. I knew what a dangerous book looked like: leather gone stiff with handling, corners rubbed round, spine cracked from being opened too many times in too many anxious hours.

This book was none of those things. Its leather was supple. Its corners were sharp. Its spine was unbroken.

It looked *new*. And Aldous said it was a hundred years old.

"A ledger," he said. "The valley's ledger. Prophecies, records, the doings of notable days. Begun by the first keeper, continued by every keeper since. My father kept it. His mother before him. I keep it now." He laid it on the desk between us, flat, and did not open it, and stood there with his hands resting on the cover as if it were a sleeping animal he was keeping calm.

"Why show me?" I said. "You've never shown me. In three years you've never once shown me the ledger."

"No," he agreed. "I haven't."

"So why now?"

He didn't answer directly. Aldous rarely did. Instead he opened the book—to the end, to the newest pages, the ones with the most recent entries—and turned it to face me, and stepped back from it, physically stepped back, as if putting distance between himself and whatever happened next.

And there it was.

The page was dense with entries going back weeks, months. Weather. Births. A roof fire at the miller's. The finding of a dead fox, noted with scholarly dispassion. Ordinary things, the ordinary sediment of an ordinary valley, recorded in Aldous's careful hand—or his father's, or his grandmother's, the entries marching backward through time in different inks, different slants, different centuries.

Except the newest entry. The entry at the bottom of the page. The entry dated *today*.

It wasn't in Aldous's hand. I knew his hand; I'd watched him write receipts for honey across three years, a cramped, upright, meticulous script. This was different. Older-looking. Fainter, somehow, as if it had been written a long time ago and yet sat at the bottom of the newest page, dated today, dated tonight, actually—the entry specified evening, after dark, the hour of the lamp.

And it bore my name.

*Odile Coldharbour.*

I read it twice. Three times. My lips moved the second time, though I hadn't meant them to. The letters didn't change. The name didn't change. The date didn't change.

There was more than the name. There was a line beneath it, a line of prophecy-styled verse, the kind of thing I'd seen fragments of in Maren's books and never understood and never wanted to understand:

*"When the ember wakes beneath the veilless sky,*
*the drowned door opens, or the keeper dies."*

I want to tell you I felt nothing. That I read it the way you read a stranger's letter opened by mistake, with a mild shrug and a passing curiosity. That is what I should have felt, in a valley where magic died at the treeline, in a valley built on forgetting, holding a book that was supposed to be about weather and births and dead foxes.

What I actually felt was vertigo.

Not fear exactly. Fear has edges. This had none. It was the sensation of the floor becoming a wall, of the valley tilting, of three years of careful smallness rotating slowly around a single written line of ink until "Odile Coldharbour, beekeeper" and "Odile Coldharbour, ember" were the same word written twice, and I could not unsee either one.

"The ink," Aldous said quietly, and I noticed he was speaking very quietly, the way you speak near a sleeping animal, "is older than the page."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," he said, "that whoever wrote this entry wrote it a hundred years ago. And the book waited. The book held the entry back until the date arrived. Ink drying backward, we call it, in the trade. The words appear when their time comes, not before." He adjusted his spectacles. Behind the thick glass his eyes had gone very still. "The book knows things. It has always known things. Usually the things it knows are small. A hard winter. A good harvest. Tonight it appears to know you."

"But I'm nobody," I said, and heard my own voice crack on the word *nobody*, which frightened me more than the ledger did, because I'd spent three years building nobody, brick by brick, day by day, and here was a century-old book demolishing it in eleven written characters. "I keep bees. I'm a beekeeper. That's all. That's everything. There's nothing else."

"There is a line of verse," Aldous said, "that disagrees."

"The verse could be about anyone. 'Ember.' 'Keeper.' Those are—those are common words. Symbols. Half the prophecies in Maren's—" I stopped. I shouldn't have said Maren's name. Saying it aloud in this valley was like striking a match indoors. But Aldous's head had tilted anyway, birdlike, interested.

"Maren," he repeated. "Your former mistress. The hedge-witch you apprenticed to, before."

"Before," I said, and left the sentence unfinished, because finishing it meant explaining why I'd run, and explaining why I'd run meant talking about magic, and talking about magic in the Veil was like talking about drowning to a man who couldn't swim.

"The verse doesn't say *anyone*," Aldous said. "It says your name. Names are not symbols, boy. Names are anchors. A symbol can float free and attach itself to any soul that fits. A name is specific. A name is a hook set in one particular fish." He closed the ledger gently, both hands, the way you close a coffin lid. "I showed you because you deserved to see it before I seal it. And because I confess a certain curiosity. What exactly were you, before you were a beekeeper?"

"A beekeeper," I said. "Before that, nothing. Before that, an apprentice who ran away."

"From Maren."

"From her magic. From what she wanted me to be. From—" My voice did the cracking thing again. I hated it. I picked up the paper bundle and held it against my chest like a shield made of rags. "Nothing. I'm nothing. Whatever the book thinks it knows, it's wrong. Books are wrong all the time. You said yourself it's usually small things. Weather. Harvests."

"Yes," Aldous said. "Usually."

"*Usually.*"

"Boy." He came around the desk, slow, his joints ticking like cooling stone. He stopped an arm's length away and looked at me over the spectacles, and for the first time in three years of trade days I saw that Aldous Vane was not merely old and dusty and harmless. There was something underneath the dust. Something that had been waiting, maybe, the way the ledger had waited. "In a hundred years, this book has recorded the deaths of two people by name before they died. Both times, the death came as written. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That it's never wrong."

"That it is *never wrong*," he said, "and that tonight, after dark, in the hour of the lamp, something concerning you is supposed to happen. Something to do with embers and doors and dying. I don't know what. The book doesn't explain itself. It never has. It writes, and we live forward into what it has written, and afterward we understand, and by then it's too late to matter." He paused. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the entry is wrong for the first time in a century. Unless someone changes what happens tonight." His pale eyes rested on me, weightless and enormous. "You could stay here. In my study. After dark. Sit in the hour of the lamp with me and let the book watch you do nothing. Perhaps nothing will be enough. Perhaps the ink will dry backward into blankness. It has never happened. But it has never been tested."

I thought about it for perhaps four seconds. Four seconds of genuine, honest consideration, sitting there in a room made of paper, holding paper, being offered the chance to spend the night doing nothing in particular in the safest valley in the world while a book watched to see if nothing could beat a prophecy.

Then I thought about Maren. About what she'd say if she learned I'd spent the night in Aldous Vane's study sitting still while a book watched me. About the way she used to say that the worst place to stand during a storm is under the tallest tree, and the second-worst place is indoors staring out the window at it, because fear grows in enclosed spaces the way mold grows in damp walls, silently, structurally, until the whole house is rotten.

And I thought about the door in the dream. Standing open. Me in front of it, shaking.

"No," I said.

"No?"

"I'll go home. Light my lamp. Do what I always do." I heard myself say it and marveled at how calm it sounded, how reasonable, how much like a man with a plan rather than a man fleeing a sentence written a hundred years before his birth. "If the book wants to write about me, it can write about a man feeding his stove and banking his bees for winter. Let it prophesy *that*."

Aldous studied me for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and there was something in the nod that I didn't understand until later—something that looked, in retrospect, horribly like pity.

"As you like," he said. "Take your paper. Take your honey money, such as it is." He pressed a few coins into my hand, warm from his pocket. At the door he stopped me with one raised finger, and said the thing I would spend the rest of the night failing not to think about:

"The book has been wrong zero times in a hundred years, boy. But it has also, in a hundred years, never once written about someone who stayed. Everyone it has ever named was already leaving. Think on that. The prophecy may not be a chain. It may be a door." He smiled faintly, terribly. "And doors, as any keeper of archives will tell you, swing both ways."

I walked home fast. Faster than the light. The valley was beautiful in the way it was always beautiful, gold light going long across the fields, smoke rising straight from chimneys, the mountains standing guard around us like patient, half-asleep giants. For three years I had looked at that view and felt held. Safe. Enclosed. Kept.

Today, for the first time, I looked at the mountains and saw walls.

I got home as the sun touched the ridgeline. I fed the stove. I lit the lamp—the lamp, the actual lamp, the one from the entry, the hour of the lamp—and set it on the table where I always set it, and told myself firmly that I was a man doing ordinary things at an ordinary hour, and that books were paper, and paper was trees, and trees had no opinions.

Outside, full dark came down. The hour of the lamp arrived and sat with me. I banked the stove. I checked the bees' quilts. I drank tea I didn't taste. The entry said *after dark*, and after dark came, and went, and nothing happened, and I sat in the lamplight feeling smug and relieved and alive in a way I hadn't felt since I'd first read my own name in a dead man's ink.

Nothing happened for nearly the whole hour.

And then, in the last minutes of it, when I had almost begun to believe, I heard hooves on the valley road. Slow hooves. Unhurried. The hooves of a horse being ridden by someone who knew exactly where he was going and exactly whom he was going to, in a valley where no one was supposed to be able to find anything, least of all me.

I pinched out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening.

They were coming up my path.
