## 4. The Rider on the Ridgeline

The bees told me first.

Not in words—bees don't have words—but in the way the whole hive went tight and hot against my palms when I lifted the lid that morning, a hum like a held breath. I'd kept Maren's hives for three years by then, and I'd learned their moods the way you learn a person's: the lazy gold drone of a good nectar day, the sharp ragged buzz of a wasp raid, the low boiling note of a swarm about to leave. This was none of those. This was the sound of something on the wind that shouldn't be.

I set the lid back down and stood in the rowan's shade with my gloves hanging from one hand, listening to my own valley.

The Veil was quiet the way it always was. That was its whole trick, really—not silence exactly, but *forgetting*. A traveler crossed the treeline at the northern pass and by the time his boots touched the first meadow grass he'd lost the reason he'd come. Not all at once. It went out of him gently, like warmth leaving a cup. I'd watched it happen a dozen times from the ridge above the cottage: peddlers, pilgrims, one very confused tax collector who ended up fishing in the mill pond and couldn't say why. They'd stay a day, maybe two, pleasant and purposeless as clouds, and then wander back out the way they'd come, remembering nothing but a nice walk.

Maren had built it that way. Or rather—she'd told me once, in one of her moods when she talked too much and then hated herself for it—the Veil had been here before her, and she'd only learned to feed it. "A valley is just a bowl," she'd said, stirring something that smelled like iron and violets. "You have to teach the edges to spill."

So when the bees went tight, I did what any sensible runaway does with a bad feeling. I ignored it. I scraped comb, strained honey through muslin into the chipped crocks, and told myself the weather was turning. There was thunder over the western fells, or something like thunder, a pressure behind the ears that had no storm attached to it.

It was past noon when I saw the rider.

* * *

From the high pasture you can see the whole northern border of the Veil: the grey scar of the old road climbing out of the free marches, the treeline where the pines go dark and dense as poured ink, and above them the ridgeline, bare and windswept, where the world stops pretending to be gentle.

He came along that ridgeline at an easy trot, a single figure in a long coat the colour of wet slate, and even at that distance I knew the cut of it. Everyone in the free cities knew it now. Vane Morrel's livery—no crest, because Morrel hadn't decided what his crest was yet, just the coat, just the boots, just the way his soldiers stood like they owned the ground under other people's feet.

My first thought was the ordinary one: another traveler. He'll hit the treeline and forget.

My second thought arrived slower, colder, and would not leave.

The road didn't go along the ridgeline. The road dropped to the pass, because the pass was the way in. Nobody rode the ridge unless they wanted to see everything first—and nobody *could* ride the ridge, not with a horse, not without knowing the ground, unless someone had told them the ground.

I crouched in the bracken and watched him come down off the rocks toward the trees, and I waited for the forgetting to take him. It was almost funny, how much I wanted it to work. Three years of safety balanced on the shoulders of one stranger in a slate coat. Go on, I thought at him. Lose your errand. Forget your master's name. Come out the far side of those pines wondering why you're hungry.

He reached the treeline and stopped.

For a long moment he just sat there, horse stamping, and I could feel him looking at the forest the way a man looks at a door he's been warned about. Then he laughed—I heard it, thin and carried on the wind, a short bark of contempt—and rode straight in.

The pines swallowed him.

I stayed in the bracken until my knees ached. An hour, maybe more. The forgetting had never taken longer than the time it took to walk from the edge to the stream. I counted streams. I counted my own heartbeats, which was stupid, so I stopped.

When he came out the southern side of the wood, he was still riding in a straight line.

* * *

Here is what you have to understand about what that meant, and why my hands went cold around the honey knife that night, though at the time I only felt a numb sort of disbelief, like watching water run uphill.

The Veil wasn't a wall. Walls can be climbed. It was more like a tide of un-being that rolled inward from every edge, and anything made of intention—letters, maps, spells, purposes—dissolved in it. A soldier under orders forgot his orders. A hunting dog forgot the scent. Even the wild magic that lived everywhere else in the world died at our borders; that was the point of the place, that was why I'd run to it specifically, why Maren had chosen it for me. Nothing that could find me could survive the crossing.

And this man had ridden through it like a man riding through fog.

Which meant one of two things. Either the Veil was failing—three years of Maren's feeding undone, the bowl cracked—or something on the outside had grown strong enough to hold its shape against the tide. Something with a purpose so heavy it could wade through forgetting and keep its errand intact.

Both possibilities pointed at the same word, and the word was *me*.

I want to be honest about what I did next, because this is the part of the story people never believe, and I've stopped apologizing for it. I went home and I made dinner.

There was bread to bake, there were crocks to seal with wax, there was a hive that needed feeding before frost. Fear is a strange animal; it doesn't always roar. Sometimes it sits down at your table and eats with you, and you let it, because the alternative is admitting that the life you built—the small, deliberate, bee-smelling life—is already gone. So I baked. I ate half a loaf standing up. I told myself he was a scout mapping passes for Morrel's army, that armies don't care about one hedge-witch's valley, that whatever he wanted it wasn't a boy with flour on his sleeves.

Then, full dark, I saw his fire on the southern slope, small and steady, and I understood that he wasn't passing through. He was waiting for something. Or looking.

I went to find Maren.

* * *

Her cottage sat in the fold of the eastern hills, an hour's walk if you took the sheep track and didn't mind the dark. I hadn't been inside since the night I left her—three years ago, seventeen years old, slamming her door hard enough to crack the latch, shouting things I still heard sometimes when I couldn't sleep. *You cut it out of me. You had no right.* And her face, which I'd expected to be angry and wasn't, which was worse: tired, and sorry, and absolutely unmoved.

But she was my mistress still, in the old sense, and the rider was her problem before he was mine. That's what I told myself on the walk. The truth is simpler and less flattering: when the floor opens under you, you go to the person who dug the hole.

Her windows were lit. Of course they were lit. Maren slept four hours a night and spent the rest doing things with herbs that would have gotten her drowned in any city in the marches.

She opened the door before I knocked.

"I know," she said.

"You don't know what—"

"A rider in slate." She stepped back to let me in. "I felt him cross at midday. Sit down, Odile. Put the kettle on. You look like a boy who hasn't eaten, which means you've eaten and it didn't take."

The cottage smelled the same, and that undid me more than anything else could have. Dried yarrow, tallow, the sour-sweet reek of her fermenting jars in the corner. Six years of my childhood hung in that smell, and three years of exile hadn't scrubbed it off me. I put the kettle on because my hands needed a job.

"He didn't forget," I said. "He rode the ridgeline, he went through the pines, and he came out the other side still knowing what he came for. Your Veil didn't touch him."

"It isn't my Veil." She lowered herself into her chair by the hearth, and I noticed—for the first time, because I hadn't looked at her properly in three years—how old she'd got. Her hands, always strong as root-stock, had gone knuckled and thin. "I feed it. I've fed it since before you were born. But a tide is only ever as strong as what pushes against it, child. Most things push lightly. Orders, greed, curiosity—they dissolve like sugar."

"And him?"

"Someone has written his errand in something heavier than ink." She looked into the fire. "Blood, most likely. Or oath-work. The usurper's inquisitors swear themselves body and name to their quarry. It's old Ashvaellan practice, actually—we invented it, we should be proud." Her mouth twisted. "They bind the hunt into their bones so no glamour, no border, no mercy can shake it loose. The forgetting slides off a man like that the way rain slides off oilcloth."

The kettle began to sing. Neither of us moved toward it.

"Inquisitors," I said. My voice came out flat, which was a mercy. "Morrel's inquisitors are in the Veil."

"One of them. A scout of sorts. The rest—" She stopped herself, and I watched her decide, deliberately, how much to tell me, the way she'd done my whole apprenticeship. Except this time she caught herself doing it, and something in her face gave way. "The rest are coming, Odile. Weeks behind him, perhaps days. He's the needle; they're the thread."

"Why?" I turned to face her. "Why me? Why now? I'm nobody. I'm a beekeeper. I've done nothing but keep bees and stay small for three years—"

"Because of the book."

The words landed in the room like a dropped stone.

"What book?"

"Aldous Vane keeps a ledger. Prophecy-record, the family's called it, keeper to keeper, a hundred years and more. It writes itself, after a fashion—entries appear that no hand set down. Mostly it's rubbish. Weather omens, births, the odd flood. But three nights ago Aldous found a new entry." She met my eyes, and hers were dry and terrible. "Tonight's date. And your name beside it."

The room tilted. I gripped the edge of her table, and the wood was real under my palm, and that was the only real thing.

"That's impossible," I said. "That book is older than I am. Books don't—things don't write backward—"

"The ledger doesn't record the future, child. It records *weight*. When enough intention gathers around a moment—a death, a crowning, a door opening—it stains the page ahead of time. Your name has gathered weight. Enough of it that the ink showed through a century of blank paper." She rose, stiffly, and took the kettle off the heat herself. "Somebody out there believes something enormous about you. Believes it hard enough to bend a prophecy book. And belief like that travels, Odile. It travels faster than armies."

I thought of the rider on the ridgeline, laughing at the treeline. Laughing, because the forgetting had broken against him like water against a pier.

"They think I'm the ember," I said. I'd heard the word only twice, both times in Maren's muttered half-sleep, both times followed by her waking and changing the subject with the skill of a woman who'd had practice. "From the old stories. The thing that opens Ashvael."

Maren went very still.

"Who told you that word?"

"You did. In your sleep. Twice." I stood up out of my chair, and three years of exile fell off me all at once, and underneath it was the angry seventeen-year-old with his hand still raised to slam her door. "No. No more of this. You sealed something in me—I've known that since the night I left, don't look surprised, I've had three years to work it out. I can't do magic. Me. In a world where every hedge-brat in the marches can light a candle with a sneeze, I am a hole in the shape of a person, and you did that, and you never told me why, and now there's a man on the south slope whose errand is heavier than your entire valley, and you will tell me. Right now. All of it."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, an owl asked its question and got no answer.

Maren set the kettle down with a click, and when she turned around she had the face I remembered from the worst day of my life—tired, and sorry, and absolutely unmoved, except that now I could see the fourth thing underneath, the one I'd missed at seventeen because grief makes a poor lens.

She was frightened.

"All of it," she repeated softly. "Oh, child. You don't want all of it. But you're right—you need some of it, and you need it tonight, while that man is still sitting by his fire deciding whether you're worth the report." She pulled a key from around her neck—one I'd never seen, black iron, worn smooth—and nodded toward the floor beneath the hearthstone. "Help me move the stone. What I buried, I buried deep, and I buried it with the last of my strength, and digging it up is going to hurt us both."

I should have run, right then. Every instinct I'd sharpened for three years said *run*, said *the bees, the small life, the forgetting*. But the rider's fire burned on the southern slope, patient as a held breath, and somewhere behind him a thread of inquisitors was already paying itself out across the map toward a valley that had forgotten how to defend itself.

I knelt down and took hold of the iron ring.

The hearthstone came up easier than it should have, as if it had been waiting.

* * *

What lay beneath it, and what Maren told me while the small hours drained away—that belongs to the next part of this account, and I set it down separately because I have never managed to make it sit quietly beside anything else.

But I will say this much, because it explains everything that came after.

Before dawn, I stood at Maren's eastern window and looked down at the rider's camp. His fire had burned to embers. He stood beside it, facing north, facing the heart of the Veil, facing—though he couldn't possibly have known it—directly at the cottage where I stood. Even at that distance I felt it, the way you feel a stare through the back of a door.

He raised one hand. Not a wave. A salute. Slow, and formal, and certain.

He knew exactly where I was. He had known since the ridgeline.

And the dread that had sat in my stomach all day finally finished its work, because dread is only fear without a direction, and mine had just found one. It hardened as I watched him, grain by grain, into something with edges. Not courage—I want to be clear about that; I have never mistaken what I felt that morning for courage. It was closer to arithmetic. *They are coming for me. Running buys weeks. Hiding bought three years and is now spent.*

Which meant the only question left was the one Maren had spent six years making sure I couldn't ask:

What am I, that a usurper's whole machine would cross a valley that unmakes men, to reach me?

Behind me, the black iron key lay on the open hearthstone, and the thing Maren had buried was beginning, at last, to answer.
