## 5. What My Mistress Buried

I did not sleep.

I sat on the floor of my own small house with the beeswax candle burning down between my knees and watched the dark under the door, waiting for it to grow teeth. Every traveler who crossed into the Veil forgot himself at the treeline—that was the valley's whole gift, its whole reason for being. A man could walk in carrying a name like a knife and walk out with empty hands and a mild smile, wondering why he'd come.

The rider on the ridgeline had not forgotten himself. He had stood there in Morrel's grey-and-crimson livery, patient as a nail, and then he had turned his horse along the border as if following a scent.

Magic was reaching into the Veil. And magic only ever reached for one thing here.

Me.

By the time the candle drowned in its own wax, I had made my decision three times and unmade it twice. The fourth time held. I put on my coat, took my knife because a knife is honest even when nothing else is, and walked up the hill to Maren's cottage in the blue hour before dawn, when even the birds hadn't decided what to believe yet.

Her chimney was already smoking. Of course it was. Maren had never once in eleven years been caught by surprise, and I had never once managed to sneak up on her, and I hated how much comfort I still took from that.

The gate creaked. The garden smelled of comfrey and woodsmoke and the particular green rot of her compost heap, which she claimed was the most magical thing she owned. The door opened before I knocked.

"You look terrible," Maren said. She was a small woman built like a knot in timber, grey-haired, brown-faced, with hands that looked soft and were not. "Come in before the light sees you."

"I'm not coming in."

She sighed the sigh of a woman who has raised an apprentice and knew exactly what that tone meant. "Then we'll do it standing in my yard like strangers. Fine."

"Why didn't he forget?" I said. "The rider. Everyone forgets. That's the whole—the entire point of this place. Why didn't *he*?"

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Then she stepped back from her doorway and left it open, an invitation and a surrender both, and went inside without me.

I stood in the yard while the sky went from slate to dirty pearl. Somewhere down the valley, Aldous Vane would be waking to his ledgers, and his daughter would be waking beside whatever she'd found in them last night, and somewhere beyond the ridgeline a man in usurper's colors was doing arithmetic with my life. I thought about walking away. North, maybe, over the high pass where the Veil thinned and a man could lose himself honestly instead of by enchantment.

I went inside.

* * *

The cottage smelled exactly as it always had—dried yarrow, tallow, the iron tang of her cauldron even cold—and that smell hit me like a hand on the chest, because it was the smell of the years I'd run from and apparently never left. Eleven years in this room. Boiling herbs. Learning which mushrooms lied. Being told, over and over, in her flat unlovely voice: *You have no talent, boy. It's a mercy. Be glad of it.*

I had believed her. Gods help me, I had *thanked* her.

Maren sat at her table with both hands wrapped around a clay mug she wasn't drinking from. There was a second mug poured for me. She'd known I'd come. She'd probably known since the moment the rider crossed the treeline, because Maren had eyes in the back of her head and, I now suspected, several other places besides.

"Sit down, Odile."

"Don't." My voice came out cracked. "Don't say my name like that. Like you're allowed."

"Who else is allowed?"

"The ledger is allowed!" I heard myself shouting and couldn't stop. "A hundred-year-old book in Vane's archive is allowed! It has tonight's date in it, Maren. Tonight's date and *my name*, written before I was born, and there's a man on your ridgeline wearing Morrel's colors who walked through forgetting like it was a puddle, and you're sitting there offering me tea!"

"Drink the tea," she said. "You'll want your hands busy for what comes next."

I picked up the mug mostly so I wouldn't throw it. The tea was chamomile and honey—my honey, traded to her last month, come back around like everything in this valley came around. I drank it anyway.

"Tell me about the Veil," she said.

"What?"

"Tell me what you know about it. All of it. Everything I've told you across eleven years. Say it out loud."

I set the mug down harder than necessary. "It's a valley where spells die at the treeline. Hedge-folk settled it generations ago because they wanted to be left alone. Travelers forget themselves crossing in. Nothing magical can find anything here. You told me that when I was twelve and crying because I thought I'd done something wrong, and I believed you, and I built my whole life on it."

"And what am I?"

"You're a hedge-witch. Herbs and charms and weather-sense. Small craft. You told me—" and here my voice did something humiliating, wobbled, "—you told me small craft was all either of us had."

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "I told you a great many things."

She got up, went to the hearth, and knelt. I watched her move two stones at the firebox's edge—stones I had seen ten thousand times, stones I had scrubbed soot off as a chore—and behind them was a cavity, and in the cavity was a bundle of oilcloth tied with cord gone black with age.

She brought it to the table and untied it with fingers that were not quite steady, and I noticed that, and noticing it frightened me more than the rider had. Maren's hands did not shake. Maren's hands had set broken bones and pulled teeth and once, memorably, held a hawk's talons away from my eye without flinching.

Inside the oilcloth lay three things.

A ring of dull silver, plain as a wedding band. A scrap of cloth, deep blue, embroidered with a crest I did not know—a tower beneath a wave, stitched in thread that seemed to move when I wasn't looking directly at it. And a page of parchment, old, brittle, covered in writing that hurt to read—not because it was faded but because my eyes kept sliding off it, the way they slide off a word you almost know.

"That's mine," I said. I don't know why I said it. I don't know how I knew. But the certainty landed in me like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling and never hitting bottom. "That page. It's about me."

"It *is* you," Maren said. "Or it was. Read it."

"I can't—the letters won't—"

"They will. They've been waiting eleven years for you to be ready. Apparently ready came early." She pushed the parchment across the table. "Read it, Odile. And then you can hate me properly, with all the facts in hand."

I read it.

It was a birth record. Not the kind a village keeps—the kind a kingdom keeps. The script was formal, courtly, and the ink had gone brown with age, and every line of it slid through my understanding like fish through fingers until suddenly, horribly, it didn't.

*Odile, son of Maerin and Prince Alaric of the House of Tides, third of that name, born in the ninth year of the Drowning. Blood of Ashvael. Heir of the Gate.*

I read it again. The words stayed. They had stopped being slippery because some part of me had stopped fighting them.

"That's not possible," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "Ashvael drowned a century ago. The whole kingdom. It's a story. It's a story mothers tell children so they don't swim past the markers."

"Ashvael drowned ninety-eight years ago," Maren said. "And stories are how kingdoms survive their own funerals. Your father's line survived it too. Barely. In exile. In hiding. Generation after generation, whittled down by loyalists who wanted to crown a child-king and start a war, and by Morrel's people, who wanted the opposite." She tapped the blue cloth with one blunt finger. "Your mother sewed that. She was sewing it the night they came for us."

"*Us*," I repeated.

"Me and her. I was her door-keeper. Her guard. Her—" Maren stopped, and for the first time in the eleven years I had known her, she looked old. Not aged. Old, the way a wall is old, holding something up long past any reasonable expectation. "I loved your mother, boy. That's the part I never told you because it makes everything else worse. I loved her, and she handed you to me out of a burning safehouse with Morrel's hounds two streets away, and she said *keep him small, keep him hidden, keep him forgotten*, and I have spent eleven years keeping that promise at the expense of every other promise I ever made. Including the ones I made to you."

My mouth was dry. The tea sat in me like lead. "What promises?"

"To tell you the truth when you were grown." She laughed, short and bitter. "I kept putting it off. Grown became fourteen, sixteen, eighteen. There was never a day that felt safe enough. That's the coward's arithmetic, and I did it every morning for a decade."

"Maren." I made myself say her name slowly, the way you'd handle a blade. "What did you do to me?"

And she told me.

Not all at once. She told it plainly, in her flat voice, the voice she used for setting bones, and somehow that was the cruelest thing—to hear the shape of my own stolen life described like a procedure.

There was a working. An old one, older than Ashvael, older than the Veil itself—or rather, the working *was* the Veil, or the Veil was what remained of it. A sealing. A naming folded backward into silence. She had taken everything I was—every spark of the blood in me, every whisper of the talent that ran in the royal line like water runs downhill—and she had knotted it shut and buried the knot in the land itself. The Veil's forgetting wasn't a property of the valley. It was *me*. My sealed name, radiating silence like a drowned bell radiates stillness, spreading outward until no spell could hold its shape within miles of it. I hadn't hidden in the Veil.

*I was* the Veil.

"The traveler forgets himself at the treeline because your buried name tells him to," Maren said. "The rider remembered because Morrel's inquisitors have finally grown strong enough—or desperate enough—to push against the seal. Which means the seal is weakening. Which means it was always going to weaken, because nothing holds forever, and I am sixty-three years old and tired down to the marrow."

"You cut it out of me." I was on my feet. I didn't remember standing. "You cut it out of me and I never—I thought there was something wrong with me. I thought I was *empty*. Do you know what that's like? Growing up feeling like a room with the furniture taken out? And you *knew*. You sat there and watched me grieve over a self you amputated, and you told me it was a mercy—"

"It was a mercy," she said, and rose too, and she was half a head shorter than me and did not step back. "You want to know what the alternative was? Your mother's brothers were found in a well in Corren. Your cousin was crowned by loyalists at nine years old and dead at nine and a half, and the war her crowning started burned three towns. Every child of your line who carried their power openly died of it, Odile. Every one. I chose emptiness over a grave. I'd choose it again. I'll choose it again tomorrow if you ask me to."

"I'm not asking."

"No," she said softly. "You're not. And that's the other thing I have to tell you, and you'll hate it worse."

The fire popped. Outside, the first real light was coming up the valley, catching the frost on her bean rows, making the whole world briefly beautiful and indifferent.

"The seal isn't just hiding you anymore. It's failing. When it goes—if it goes badly—it won't fade. It'll rupture. Everything I buried in you will come up at once, uncontrolled, and you'll burn out everything within a league, starting with yourself. Unless it's released properly. By choice. With a rite I can teach you and a price I can't pay for you."

"What price?"

"That's not today's lesson." She gathered the ring, the cloth, the parchment back into the oilcloth with careful hands, and pressed the bundle into mine. My mother's crest stared up at me from the blue embroidery. A tower beneath a wave. "Take these. Take the road south out of the valley—you'll understand why soon enough, or someone will make you understand. Don't come back here. Whatever you hear, whatever happens, don't come back."

"You're coming with me."

"I'm not."

"Maren—"

"*Boy.*" Her voice cracked like a whip, and then, terribly, it gentled. "I sealed your name into this valley. Do you understand what that means? The Veil ends when the seal ends. The moment you walk out of it free, every spell in a hundred miles remembers how to work—including every scrying glass and blood-hound and truth-compulsion in Morrel's arsenal. Someone has to stay inside the circle and keep feeding it while you get clear. Someone whose name is already woven into the working." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I had ever seen, because it was warm. "I built this cage, Odile. Let me be the last thing it holds."

I wanted to argue. I want to tell you I fought her, that I refused, that I said something grand. What I actually did was stand in my mistress's kitchen holding my mother's crest and feel eleven years rearrange themselves around a new shape—betrayal and love in the same fist, squeezing, indistinguishable, each one making the other hurt worse. She had lied to me every day of my life. She had saved my life every day of my life. Both things were true. Neither canceled the other. I understood, dimly, that this might be what being grown actually meant: carrying true things that refuse to reconcile.

"When did you last eat?" she asked, because she was Maren, and the world could end and she'd still ask that.

"I'm not hungry."

"Liar." She turned to her pantry, and her back was very straight and very narrow, and I thought: *she's been standing between me and the world my whole life and I never once saw her do it.*

She was wrapping bread and hard cheese in a cloth when the light changed.

Not dawn. Dawn had already happened. This was a different light—an orange flicker, wrong for the hour, dancing on the far wall through the window. I went to the glass and looked down the valley toward the cluster of buildings at its heart, toward the archive with its thousand shelves and its hundred-year ledger with my name drying in it.

Smoke was rising from Aldous Vane's roof. A lot of smoke. Too much smoke for a chimney, too much for a cookfire, black at the base and climbing fast into the clean morning air.

Behind me, Maren went very quiet. When I turned, she was looking at the smoke too, and her face had gone the color of the ash it came from.

"The book," she said. "They've found the book. Odile—they aren't searching for you anymore."

She gripped my arm, hard, her soft-looking fingers digging in like roots.

"They've started burning everything that knows your name."
