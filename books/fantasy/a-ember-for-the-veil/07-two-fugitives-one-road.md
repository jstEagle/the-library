## 7. Two Fugitives, One Road

I ran before dawn, because running at night felt like something a guilty man does.

That's a lie I told myself on the ridge above Maren's cottage, breath steaming, the honey-house keys still in my fist where I'd gripped them so hard they'd printed their teeth into my palm. The truth is I ran at night because I was afraid, and fear doesn't care what hour it keeps. But there was a kind of dignity in waiting for the grey light. A man who flees by daylight is at least admitting he's seen what chases him.

The Veil let me go the way it always did—reluctantly, like a dog that knows its master is lying about coming back. At the treeline the air thickened. That was the border of the silence, the place where spells died and travelers forgot why they'd come. I had crossed it a hundred times to sell honey in the lowland markets, and every time I stepped back through, the valley took a little of the road off me. Wiped it clean. By the time I reached the cottage I could never quite remember the face of the last person I'd haggled with.

This time I didn't step back through. This time I walked down out of the hills with the valley's quiet still clinging to my shoulders like smoke, and I thought: *if magic can't reach me here, then everything that happens next will happen out there, where it can.*

Behind me, somewhere up in those hills, Aldous Vane's ledger sat in its archive with tonight's date drying in it and my name beside it. *Odile.* Not Odile Coldharbour—Maren had burned that name out of me years ago, or so she claimed—but just *Odile*, plain as a stone in a shoe.

A book a century old should not know me. A book a century old knowing me meant one of two things: either the world was smaller than I believed, or I was larger than I'd been told. I didn't want either answer.

So I ran. Down through the birch stands where the honey-gatherers worked in summer, past the shrine with its moss-eaten god whose face no one in the valley could describe twice the same way, along the drover's road that wound toward the low country and the river and, eventually, if a man kept walking, the free cities.

I had a plan. It was not a good plan, but it had the two qualities I required of plans: it was simple, and it involved putting distance between myself and everything that knew my name. Walk south. Stay off the main roads. Trade honey for bread—I'd brought three combs wrapped in cloth, because a man fleeing prophecy still has to eat—and keep moving until I found some town where nobody read ledgers.

I made perhaps nine miles before she caught me.

* * *

She came out of the alder brake on the river side of the road, and my first stupid thought was that the valley had followed me after all—that the quiet itself had grown legs, because Sera Vane moved without sound even on ground that crackled under my own boots.

"Odile."

Not a question. She stood with the morning behind her, and she was holding a book.

I want to say I considered my options. I want to say I weighed the drover's road against the riverbank, calculated whether I could outrun a girl who'd spent her life walking archive floors. What I actually did was stand there with honey dripping through my pack where a comb had cracked, and say:

"You're supposed to be sealing books."

"I sealed it." She came closer, and I saw her properly for the first time since the night in her father's archive—the night I'd read my own name upside-down across a desk and felt the floor tilt. Her eyes were red-rimmed. There was ash on her sleeve. "I sealed it, and then I carried it out of the building while it burned."

The words went into me cold, like well water. "Burned."

"The archive. Last night." She said it flatly, the way you say things you've said once already to yourself and cannot afford to say again with feeling. "Riders in grey livery. They came up the valley road and the wards didn't stop them—they *couldn't* stop them, do you understand? Magic isn't dying at our treeline anymore. Something is holding the door open from outside." Her hand tightened on the book until the leather creaked. "My father gave me this and told me to run south. He's still there, Odile. He's standing in his archive telling an inquisitor that the ledger in question is a copyist's error."

"And you left him."

"And he *ordered* me to leave him." For a moment her voice cracked and I saw the girl underneath the archivist, the one who used to steal honeycomb from my trade-bundles when we were fourteen and pretend she hadn't. Then the archivist came back, jaw set. "He'll be alive tomorrow. Inquisitors don't kill archivists; they interrogate them. And my father has been lying to powerful men since before I was born. He's very good at it."

"You're not," I said. "Lying. You're terrible at it. You're doing it right now about being fine."

Something flickered across her face—anger, maybe, or gratitude, or both wearing the same coat. "Give me the pack."

"What?"

"The pack. You've got maybe four days of food in there and you've already crushed a comb. You walk like a beekeeper, which is to say slowly and apologizing to the ground. If you're going to survive the march to Corren, someone has to manage the route, the fords, and the fact that every ferryman between here and the free cities has been shown your face."

"I don't have a face," I said. "I have a description that changes. That's the whole point of the Veil—"

"The Veil is *burning*, Odile." She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "The rider your mistress saw on the ridgeline—he crossed the border and remembered himself. Do you know what that means? It means someone found the seam Maren sewed you into and started picking at it. Every mile you run alone, that seam unravels further. You need—" She stopped herself. Breathed. Started over with the words arranged more carefully, like a scholar arranging a citation. "The council of the free cities has sworn protections. Sanctuary law. If I deliver you to Corren, no power in the lowlands can touch you without declaring war on six cities at once."

"And what do you get?"

The question landed harder than I intended. She flinched—actually flinched, a small motion of the shoulders, quickly mastered.

"My father's life depends on the ledger reaching the council," she said. "That's what he told me. The ember must reach the free cities or be extinguished. Those were his words. So yes, Odile, I get something. I get to be useful instead of useless. I get to do the one thing I was trained for." She held the book out between us, open, and the wind turned the page for her, and there it was again: tonight's date, a week gone now, and my name in ink older than her grandfather. "And I get to deliver you. That's my oath. Sworn when I was sixteen, before I knew you existed outside of a marginal note. So don't look at me like I'm doing you a kindness. I'm doing my duty. You happen to be inside it."

* * *

We argued for most of the morning.

It began with the direction of travel—she wanted the river road because it was fast, I wanted the high sheep tracks because they were empty—and escalated through the questions that mattered, though we dressed them as logistics. Where was I going? South, obviously. Why? Because south was away. Away from what? From everything that wanted me. And what did I think waited in the south besides more of everything, bigger and better armed?

"You can't outrun a prophecy by walking," she said. We'd stopped at a stream to water ourselves, and she crouched with her fingers in the current, not drinking, just grounding herself the way her father did before difficult catalog work. "Believe me. I've read four hundred years of attempts. Kings tried it. Saints tried it. One man sailed past the edge of every map and the ledger recorded his arrival back at his own front door."

"Then what's the point of any of it? If it's all written—"

"It's not written." She looked up at me, and the light through the alders made moving shadows on her face. "That's the first thing you learn and the last thing you understand about prophecy-work. The ledger records. It doesn't command. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of my profession. A record is a mirror, Odile. You can smash a mirror. You can't smash what it shows you."

"Your mirror has my death in it somewhere. I saw the way you looked at the later pages."

Her hands went still in the water.

"I'm a fast reader," I said. "Upside-down, even."

"Then you also saw that the entry is undated." She rose, wiped her hands on her skirt, and met my eyes with the flat honesty I'd just accused her of being incapable of faking. "There's a chapter heading. There's no date beneath it. In a hundred years of entries, that has happened exactly twice. My father thinks it means the ink hasn't decided yet." A pause. "I think it means you haven't."

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow under a fallen beech, close enough to the road to hear an occasional cart, far enough that firelight wouldn't show. She built the fire small and efficient, a traveler's fire, and I realized she'd done this before—archivists apparently learned more than letters.

We ate hard bread and the one surviving honeycomb, and split it with a fairness that surprised us both, and then sat in the firelight not talking, which was its own kind of argument, and a more honest one.

Finally she said, "You should know what I am to you. Formally. So there's nothing between us that's unwritten."

"That sounds ominous."

"It's accurate." She drew the ledger onto her knees but didn't open it. "By oath and office, I am a courier of persons. The council pays my order to find people the prophecies touch and bring them in—willingly if possible. My warrant names you. If you refuse to come, I am bound to use whatever persuasion is necessary short of harm. If you flee, I follow. If someone tries to take you before I deliver you, I am bound to defend you with my life, because you are cargo, and cargo that reaches its destination matters more than the courier." She recited it evenly, like a tariff schedule, and only her white knuckles betrayed her. "That's what I am. I wanted you to hear it from me and not discover it on a bad day."

"And what are you when you're not cargo-law?" I asked. "When you're just Sera?"

The fire popped. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and quit.

"When I'm just Sera," she said slowly, "I'm the girl who used to steal your honeycomb. Who taught you to read the old script because her father wouldn't waste lessons on a hedge-witch's boy. Who sat with you on the archive steps the summer we were fifteen and listened to you explain bees for two hours because the way you talked about them was the only interesting thing in my whole week." She stared into the fire. "She's still here. She's just carrying a warrant now. Both things are true at once, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

I turned my honeycomb piece over in my fingers. Wax, and sweetness, and the memory of ten thousand wings.

"If I run again," I said, "you'll follow."

"Yes."

"If someone offers you enough to hand me over—"

"No." Fast, this time. Too fast to be duty. "Ask me anything else. Ask me the hard version. But not that one."

"Why not that one?"

"Because I've already answered it," she said, "and you heard me answer it, and if you make me say it aloud on the first night, I'll have to live with having said it, and so will you, and neither of us needs that weight yet. Ask me again in Corren. If I'm still worth asking, I'll answer."

I looked at her across the small fire—this girl with ash on her sleeve and a burning archive behind her and a book on her knees that knew my name better than I did—and I understood that I had stopped planning my escape somewhere around midday, and hadn't noticed.

"All right," I said. "Partners. With conditions. I don't enter any city through a gate. I don't sign anything. And when we reach Corren, I get to hear every word spoken about me, including the ones in rooms I'm not invited into. Deal?"

"Deal." She almost smiled. It transformed her face and then retreated, cautious. "One condition of mine. When I say run, you run. No heroics, no questions. The ledger says you matter. I intend to keep you alive long enough to find out why."

"Spoken like a true cargo-handler."

"Spoken like someone who read your death chapter, Odile." She banked the fire with two sticks, precise as punctuation. "Sleep. I'll take first watch. And Odile—" I paused, half-turned. "The rider on the ridgeline. He wasn't looking for a runaway apprentice. He was looking for an ember. Whatever Maren told you you are—whatever you've decided you are—it's not what they think you are. Which means it might not be what *you* think you are either."

I lay back against the beech root and pulled my cloak around me, and above the black line of the trees the stars came out, indifferent and enormous, the same stars that hung over Ashvael's drowned towers, wherever those were, whoever I was to them.

Somewhere south, a city debated my fate in a language of votes and tariffs. Somewhere north, an old man lied beautifully to men in grey. And here, in a hollow under a fallen tree, a keeper and her cargo shared a fire and pretended that was all they were.

I was nearly asleep when I heard it—a sound from the road, faint but wrong. Hooves, slowing. Stopping.

Sera's hand closed on my wrist in the dark, and her whisper was barely breath:

"*Run.*"
