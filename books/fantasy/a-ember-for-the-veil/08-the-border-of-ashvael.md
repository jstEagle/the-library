## 8. The Border of Ashvael

The road stopped pretending on the third morning.

Up until the pass it had been a road—ruts and stones and the occasional milestone with its face worn smooth by weather and indifference. Past the pass it became a suggestion. Grass grew up the middle of it in a spine of green, and the milestones gave way to standing stones that had no markings at all, or markings so old the wind had eaten them down to whispers.

Sera walked ahead of me with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heart. She'd barely spoken since Grey Ford, since the ferry where she'd lied for the first time in her life and then spent a full day being quietly horrified about how easy it had been.

"You're staring at my back again," she said, not turning around.

"You have a distinctive back."

"Odile."

"I'm checking for riders." That much was true. Since the ferry I'd been checking for riders the way other men checked the sky for rain—constantly, uselessly, out of habit more than hope.

"There are no riders. There's nothing out here but grass and ghosts." She said *ghosts* lightly, the way archivists say things they've read about a hundred times and never seen.

I didn't answer, because the grass was starting to worry me more than any rider could.

It grew wrong here. Not wild—we'd come through plenty of wild country, and wild grass has a kind of honest chaos to it, every blade fighting its neighbor for light. This grass grew in patterns. Swirls. Long spiraling whorls that you only saw when you stood still long enough for your eyes to give up arguing about them, and then you couldn't unsee them, and they looked less like wind-rows than like handwriting.

*Someone wrote this field,* I thought, and immediately wished I hadn't, because the thought fit too well, and thoughts that fit too well in the contested marches had a way of becoming true.

"The ruins start today," Sera said. "According to the marginalia." She patted the ledger through the oilcloth without breaking stride. "Aldous's notes say the old pilgrim road runs along the coast shelf. At low tide, Ashvael surfaces. Towers. A harbor gate. He called it—" she paused, consulting memory the way she consulted everything, precisely and completely—"*the kingdom showing its bones.*"

"And at high tide?"

"At high tide it goes back to sleep."

"And us?"

"We should be very quiet," Sera said, "and very fast, and we should not touch anything."

We walked on. Behind us the Veil lay folded into the mountains like a letter no one would ever open again. Ahead of us the land sloped down and grayed out, green thinning to salt-scrub and black rock, until we crested a low rise and the sea appeared all at once, enormous and indifferent, filling half the sky.

And there, between us and the water, lying across the coastal shelf like something a giant had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up: Ashvael.

* * *

The tide was out. Of course it was out. I was beginning to suspect the tide did whatever the ledger wanted.

The drowned city rose from the mudflats in stages. First the stumps of a breakwater, black stone furred with weed, marching out into the shallows like the teeth of a comb. Then rooftops—dozens of them, hundreds, slate and copper gone green, breaking the surface of the exposed flats at angles that suggested the whole city had knelt down mid-stride and stayed kneeling. Then, far out where the water went from gray to iron, the towers. They stood full-height even drowned, and the sea had carved them into something between architecture and reef. Gulls nested in what might once have been bell chambers. From this distance the birds' cries came across the flats thin and high, like voices pitched specifically to unsettle travelers.

"It's bigger than Corren," Sera said. There was something raw in her voice I'd never heard there before. Sera's voice ran the usual gamut from precise to extremely precise; this was neither. "Corren is the largest free city. This was one of nine. Aldous wrote that Ashvael alone held two hundred thousand souls before the drowning."

"What happened to them?"

She was quiet for a moment. The gulls filled the silence, badly.

"The ledger doesn't say. It says the sea took the city in a single night and that the royal line survived, scattered, and that the door remained." She touched the ledger again. "Everything else is commentary."

"Commentary written by whom?"

"That," Sera said, "is the question that has kept three generations of archivists employed."

We picked our way down onto the flats. The mud was firm under the top crust, dark and glittering with mica, printed everywhere with bird tracks and the deeper channels of eels. The smell was kelp and salt and something older underneath—the smell, I decided, of a place that had been holding its breath.

Here's what I haven't told anyone, because telling it makes it real: the closer we got to those ruins, the more I felt like a tuning fork someone else was striking.

It started as a hum behind my sternum. I told myself it was nerves, or hunger, or the aftermath of too many nights sleeping cold. But nerves don't synchronize. By the time we reached the first rooftops—walking along their ridgelines because the streets between were deep channels of silt—the hum had become a pulse, slow and tidal, and I noticed with a detached horror that it matched the waves. In. Out. In. Out. Something in my chest breathing with the sea.

My mistress Maren had sealed me when I was six years old. I remembered almost nothing of it—a burning taste in my mouth, her hands over my eyes, her voice saying words I wasn't meant to understand, and afterward a silence inside me so complete I'd grown up believing it was just what people were like. She'd told me once, in the last conversation we'd had before I ran, that what she'd put in me wasn't dead. It was asleep. *There's a difference,* she'd said, *and the difference will matter someday.* I'd hated her for the ambiguity of that sentence for four years.

Standing on the roof of a drowned house, feeling my heartbeat keep time with an ocean, I finally understood what she'd been trying to tell me.

"Odile." Sera had stopped ahead of me. "Stop walking."

"I'm fine."

"You're glowing."

That stopped me. I looked down at my own hands. Nothing. I looked at the mud. Nothing. "Sera, I'm really not—"

"Not visibly." She turned, and in the flat gray light her face had gone the color of paper. "In the ledger. Marginalia, page ninety-one. *When the heir walks the bones, the bones will know him.* I thought it was metaphor. Archivists think everything is metaphor right up until it isn't." She pointed past me, down the ridge of the roof, toward the street below.

The silt was moving.

Not sliding—not shifting the way wet ground shifts. Moving *toward* me. Thin filaments of it, lifting off the surface like smoke made of mud, drifting upward through air that had no business supporting them, and gathering. Below us, along the length of the drowned street, the same thing was happening in a dozen places. The mud rose in threads and ribbons, hung trembling in the gray air, and oriented itself—every thread, every ribbon—like compass needles finding north.

Finding *me.*

"Maren's seal," I said. My voice came out strange, doubled, as if someone were saying the words a half-second after I did. "She sealed it. It can't—it's *sealed*—"

"Sealed isn't gone," Sera said. She'd pulled the ledger out and was flipping pages one-handed, fast, oilcloth flapping. "A seal is a door too, Odile. You taught me that yourself, the night we left Grey Ford—you said the Veil was a door, not a wall. Everything Maren built was doors." Her finger stopped on the page. She read. I watched her read, watched her lips move, watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"Sera."

"This isn't the magic waking up." She closed the ledger slowly. "This is the magic answering. There's a distinction in the text. *What sleeps may wake; what answers has always been awake.*" She looked at me, and beneath the fear in her face there was something else, something worse: wonder. "Your mistress didn't cage a beast, Odile. She taught it to hold its breath. And we're standing in the one place in the world where it remembers how to exhale."

The nearest threads of silt touched the rooftop edge and dissolved, absorbed, and where they dissolved the black stone *warmed*. I felt it through my boots. Felt it the way you feel a word spoken directly into your ear in a crowded room—meant only for you, unmistakable, impossible to pretend otherwise.

The hum behind my sternum resolved into something almost like language. Not words. Older than words. A pressure, a leaning-in, the way a sleeping animal leans toward a hand it knows.

*You're late,* it seemed to say. *You're so late. We kept everything. We kept all of it.*

"No," I said aloud, and my voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "No, I'm not—I'm not him. I'm not anybody. I'm a beekeeper. I keep bees. I'm nobody's heir—"

"Odile." Sera's hand found my wrist. Her grip was strong and shaking at the same time. "Who are you talking to?"

"The mud," I said. And then, because lying to Sera had gotten difficult somewhere on the road and I hadn't noticed exactly when: "The city. It knows me. It's been waiting for me, and it's *happy*, and I don't know why that's the worst part but it is, it's the worst part, Sera, because Maren taught me that magic takes. Magic is appetite. That's the first thing she ever taught me. And this—" I gestured at the rising threads, the warming stone, the whole kneeling city, "—this isn't taking. This is *giving*, and I don't know what it wants in return."

The gulls went silent. All of them, all at once, the way a room goes silent when the wrong person walks in.

Far out among the towers, where the water met the iron-colored horizon, something surfaced. Just briefly. A shape like a spire, but moving, deliberate, rising and falling with none of the chaos of a wave—and around it the sea flattened in a perfect ring, a mile wide maybe, glass-smooth in a world of chop.

Then it sank, and the ring smoothed over, and the gulls started up again as if nothing had happened, and somehow that was worse.

Sera's hand was still on my wrist. Neither of us moved for a long moment.

"Aldous's notes," she said finally, very quietly, "mention a warden. Out past the harbor gate. Something the royal line left to watch the door." She swallowed. "He wrote that it had not been seen in living memory. He wrote that in the margin, and then he crossed it out, and then he wrote underneath, in different ink: *seen it twice now. both times after visitors left. it watches the shore.*"

"So it's watching the shore."

"It's watching the shore."

"And we're standing on the shore."

Sera looked at me, and despite everything—the fear, the mud hanging in the air, the thing in my chest breathing with the sea—the corner of her mouth twitched. "You're catching on to archiving remarkably fast."

We went inland. Away from the water, up off the flats, into the hills where the ruins thinned and the grass resumed some semblance of honest disorder. I looked back exactly once, at the crest of the ridge.

The drowned city lay in the evening light like a body at rest. And along the whole broken curve of the harbor gate, from one end to the other, small lights had begun to burn—green-white, deep underwater, spaced with terrible regularity, like windows lighting one by one in a house preparing for company.

The pulse behind my sternum leaned toward them. I made myself turn away.

"We camp in the hills," I said. "Tonight. Tomorrow we find whatever door the Magpie's map shows and we're gone before the tide comes in."

Sera nodded. But as she spread the bedroll, I caught her looking at my hands—at the faint, unmistakable shimmer that clung to them now, that hadn't been there that morning, that I suspected would never fully wash off again.

And in the night, in the space between one wave and the next, I dreamed in a language I'd never learned, and woke with salt water on my lips though we were a mile from the sea—

and found, pressed into the mud beside my head, a single footprint. Bare-footed. Child-sized. Filled to the brim with seawater that had no reason to be there, sixty feet above the tideline, and no reason to be warm.
