## 9. The Toll of Names

The river came out of the hills the way secrets do—quietly, then all at once.

Sera heard it before she saw it, a low gray murmur beyond the last stand of alders, and her whole body tightened the way it always did when the world offered her something she couldn't read. Books she could read. ledgers she could read. Rivers were ink that moved, and every crossing was a sentence you had to finish once you'd started it.

"Is that the Vessa?" Odile asked from behind her. He was carrying the honey jars again, because he'd taken them from her twice already without asking, and every time he did it Sera felt something complicated move in her chest that she had no time for.

"The Vessa," she said. "Which means we're past the marches. Which means someone will be keeping accounts."

"Someone is *always* keeping accounts," Odile said. "It's the one universal truth. Bees keep accounts. Rivers keep accounts." He shifted the jars and gave her a crooked half-smile. "Archivists especially."

"Don't flatter your species."

"I'm flattering yours."

She didn't answer, because answering would have meant smiling, and smiling would have meant something she'd sworn not to mean. She pushed through the alders instead and came out on the bank, and there it was: the ferry crossing at Greywater Bend, a wooden platform worn silver by forty years of boots, a rope strung across the current thick as a burial cord, and a flat barge moored midstream with a lantern already lit against the dusk.

And beside the rope, in a chair dragged down from somewhere, sat the toll-keeper.

He was old in the way river men are old—all weather and wire, hands like knotted roots—and he was watching them before they'd even cleared the trees. Not idly. Sera knew the difference between a man watching traffic and a man watching for something. Her father had taught her that too, along with cataloguing systems and the proper way to break an oath so God noticed it was broken for a reason.

Her father was ash now, or close to it. The archive was ash entirely. And the ledger with Odile's name written backward into a century-dead page was wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, riding under her coat like a second pulse.

*Keep walking*, she told herself. *Cross like anyone crosses.*

"Toll's two bits a head," the old man said as they came onto the platform. He didn't get up. His eyes went over them slowly—her boots, Odile's hood, the shape of the jars—and stopped on Odile a half-second longer than it should have. "Four bits. And the ferryman asks names."

Sera had known this was coming since the treeline. Every toll on the Vessa asked names; it was an old custom, older than the free cities, older than the usurper, a superstition wearing the clothes of law. *The river keeps honest accounts.* Travelers gave their names to the water so the water wouldn't take anything else. It was supposed to be harmless. It was supposed to be a folk rhyme.

But nothing about names was harmless anymore. Names had weight now. Names appeared in books that should not know them. Names were currency, and she was carrying a king's ransom strapped against her ribs.

"It's just us," she said. "Two travelers, southbound."

"Ferryman still asks." The old man rose from his chair, joints cracking like green wood, and Sera saw what she'd been afraid she'd see: a ledger of his own, small and swollen with damp, hanging from his belt by a leather thong. A pencil stub. The particular patience of a man who writes things down. "River custom. You pay the iron, you pay the ink. No names, no crossing—not tonight, not with the roads full of riders asking after runaways."

*Asking after runaways.* So the news had come this far already. Of course it had. Inquisitors traveled fast when they had coin, and faster when they had faith.

Odile, beside her, had gone very still—the specific stillness of prey animals and boys who grew up sealed away from their own power. She could feel him deciding whether to bolt, could see the calculation happening behind his eyes: the river, the dark, the cold water, the jars. She stepped forward before he could decide anything stupid.

"Fine," she said. "Names first, then."

The old man opened his ledger. The pencil waited.

And here is what Sera Vane knew, standing on that silvered platform with dusk coming down blue over the water: she had never lied. Not once. Twenty-three years of sworn archivist's discipline, of her father's catechism drilled into her before she could write her own name—*the record is sacred, the record is true, an archivist who lies poisons every page she touches*. She had testified before councils. She had catalogued forged charters and identified false marginalia by the smell of the ink. She had built her entire self on the load-bearing wall of *I do not lie*.

She thought of Odile laughing in her father's archive three winters ago, laughing at some terrible joke about bees, his whole face open, unaware that a book in the next room had already written him down.

She thought of inquisitors' torches.

"Corren Hale," she said, "and brother Wick, pilgrims south to the salt shrines."

* * *

The lie came out of her smooth and easy, and that frightened her more than any rider on any ridgeline.

Not a stumble. Not a stammer. She'd expected—she didn't know what she'd expected. Lightning. Some crack in the architecture of herself. Instead the false names simply left her mouth the way true ones would have, clean as a well-cut key, and the world did not stop turning, and the river did not rise up offended, and the old man wrote *Hale, Corren* and *Hale, Wick* in his damp little book with a hand that shook only from age.

She had chosen *Hale* without thinking, which was its own kind of horror—inquisitor Hale's name, plucked from rumor and set on Odile like a coat. Or maybe she had thought about it exactly enough: if anyone checked, let them chase a name belonging to the very hounds hunting them. There was a cruelty in it she hadn't known she carried.

"And the purpose of travel?" The old man looked up. His eyes were pale as river ice. "Pilgrims register at the shrines, these days. New rule. Usurper's rule."

"Pilgrims who can't afford the registration fee," Sera said, "walk around the registration. We're poor, grandfather, not stupid."

Something flickered in his face—amusement, maybe, or recognition. Old men who kept tolls on forgotten rivers had survived usurpers before. He grunted, closed the ledger, and held out his hand for the coins.

Then Odile ruined everything by being kind.

"Here—" He set down the honey jars, dug in his pocket, and came out with a small cloth-wrapped comb, the sample he'd been saving to trade at the next market. He pressed it into the toll-keeper's palm. "For the ink. Honey keeps the pages from molding. My sister's fussy about her books."

*Sister.* The word landed in Sera like a struck bell. He said it easily, naturally, a fiction offered to shield hers, and he smiled at the old man while he did it, and Sera stood there and understood with terrible clarity that she was going to love this person for the rest of her life, and that this realization arrived at the worst possible moment, on the worst possible riverbank, in the middle of the first lie she'd ever told.

The toll-keeper turned the honeycomb over in his root-knuckled fingers. He looked at it. Then he looked at Odile—longer this time, much longer, long enough that Sera's hand drifted toward the knife at her hip without her permission.

"You're not from the marches," he said.

"We're from Corren," Sera said. Second lie, smoother than the first. The load-bearing wall came down another course. She felt it go and stood anyway.

"Mm," said the old man. "Corren. That's what I thought." He tucked the honeycomb inside his coat, picked up his ledger, and jerked his chin at the barge. "Rope's on the starboard side. Pull steady, don't fight the current, and whatever you hear in the water tonight—don't answer it. River keeps honest accounts, but it also keeps echoes, and echoes lie." He settled back into his chair. "Safe crossing, pilgrims."

* * *

They pulled the ferry across in failing light, and the river talked to them the whole way.

It wasn't words. It was almost-words—the sound of the current against the hull rearranging itself into shapes that wanted meaning, the way fire wants to be a face. Sera hauled the rope and did not answer, and beside her Odile hauled the rope and did not answer, but she watched his reflection in the black water watch the sound, lean toward it, hungry for it the way he must have been hungry all his hidden life for anything that knew his name.

"The Veil forgets travelers," he said quietly, halfway across. Not a question. An observation, in the flat voice he used when something hurt.

"That's the story."

"What does the river do?"

"Forgets too, supposedly." Sera pulled, and pulled. "Give your name to the water and the water holds it so nothing else can. It's a folk custom, Odile. It's a toll-keeper's excuse to charge four bits."

"Except he didn't charge us four bits," Odile said. "He charged us two, and took honey, and looked at me like my grandmother owed him money. And he said *that's what I thought*, which means he thought something, which means—" He stopped pulling. The barge swung lazily in the current. Downstream, the almost-words swelled. "Sera. What if the river doesn't forget? What if it *keeps*? What if every name given honestly to every ferryman on the Vessa is written down somewhere worse than a damp ledger?"

The cold came off the water and into her bones. Because it was a foolish question, superstitious, unworthy of a trained archivist—and because her father had died protecting a book that proved the foolish questions were the real ones. Ink drying backward. Prophecies filed a century early. The world had spent the last week teaching her that the archive was bigger and stranger than any catalogue, and here was Odile, standing on dark water, asking the exact right wrong question.

"Then we gave it false names," she said, "and it has nothing to keep."

"Do rivers accept counterfeit coin?"

"*Pull*, Odile."

They pulled. The far bank came up slow and silvered, and as they stepped off onto the mud, the almost-words behind them resolved, briefly and horribly, into something that sounded like a single syllable of a single name, spoken by a hundred drowned voices at once—a syllable that rhymed, unmistakably, with the beginning of *Odile*.

Neither of them spoke. They walked up off the bank into the pines until the river was only a gray noise behind them, and then Sera made camp because making camp was a thing her hands knew how to do when her mind had gone traitor.

* * *

The fire was small, because small fires were survivable fires. Odile sat across it turning one of the empty honey jars in his hands, and Sera sat pretending to study the ledger's marginalia, and neither of them said anything for a long time, which was itself becoming a language between them—one she was fluent in and terrified of.

"You lied for me," Odile said finally.

"I lied for the mission."

"You've never lied before. I've seen you correct strangers' grammar with your *eyes*. Don't tell me it was for the mission."

Sera kept her face aimed at the page. *The door opens not by blood but by surrender*, someone dead had scrawled in the margin a hundred years ago, and she read it four times without reading it once. "My father had a saying," she said. "An archivist who lies poisons every page she touches."

"And you touched a page for me."

"I poisoned the whole book." She closed the ledger. Across the fire, Odile was looking at her with an expression she refused to examine, so she examined the flames instead, which were easier. "Two lies. Three, counting Corren. I'm apparently prolific."

"Sera."

"*Wick*," she corrected. "Your name is Wick. We're pilgrims. If we're going to commit perjury we might as well maintain continuity."

His mouth twitched. Then, seriously: "Thank you."

"It doesn't require thanks. It required nothing. It was tactics."

"Sera."

"What?" It came out sharper than she meant, and the sharpness cracked something, and suddenly she was talking because if she stopped she would feel it. "You want to know what happened back there? Nothing happened. That's what happened. I said false words and the sky didn't fall and my hands didn't burn and the river didn't reject me, and do you understand what that means? It means twenty-three years of *the record is sacred* was a choice, not a nature. It means I was never incapable of lying. I was just never given anything worth lying for." The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the river murmured its almost-names. "Until now. Is that what you wanted to hear?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice was gentle in the way that hurt most. "No. I wanted to hear you say you don't regret it."

"I don't regret it."

"Even though it costs you something every time."

"*Especially* then." She fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "My father used to say the archive burns twice. Once with flame. Once with compromise. He thought the second was worse." She looked up at Odile across the small fire, at the boy whose name she'd counterfeited, whose death date waited three chapters ahead in the book against her heart. "He was wrong. Let it cost what it costs."

For a while, neither of them spoke. The night deepened. The river kept its accounts.

Then Odile lay back against his pack, put his hands behind his head, and said, in the tone of a man trying very hard to be casual: "So. Sister."

"Don't."

"I'm just noting the family structure. Very sudden. No warning."

"I panicked."

"You said it like you'd practiced."

"I did *not*—" She stopped. Heard herself. Threw a pinecone at his head, which he caught, grinning, and she lay down facing away from him where he couldn't see her face do whatever it was doing, and the last thing she heard before sleep was his voice, soft, unguarded, almost happy:

"Goodnight, sister Wick."

She woke before dawn to the sound of hoofbeats on the north road.

Not one horse. Six or more, moving in formation, coming down toward the crossing they'd left behind—and beneath the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable, the toll-keeper's voice raised high and hard, arguing, refusing, and then a sound like wet paper tearing that Sera's mind declined, for several seconds, to identify.

By the time she was on her feet, Odile was already up, already listening, his face gone white as the frost on the pine needles.

"He wouldn't give them the book," Odile said. "The little ledger. He wouldn't give them our names."

Down through the trees, on the gray ribbon of the river, a light kindled—small, then larger, orange against the dawn. The toll-house, burning. And rising above the hoofbeats and the fire-noise came a new sound: a man's voice, amplified by magic or office or sheer cold authority, calling orders across the water.

"*Search the far bank. They crossed within the day. Find the pilgrims.*"

Sera wrapped the ledger tighter against her heart, took Odile's wrist, and pulled him south into the pines at a run—two liars, two pilgrims, one name ahead of them and six horses behind.

Behind them, the river kept what it had been given.
