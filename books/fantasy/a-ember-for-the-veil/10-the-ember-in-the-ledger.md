## 10. The Ember in the Ledger

We read by firelight in the ruined chapel, three miles off the ferry road, because Sera said no one watches ruins and I said no one watches anything in the rain. Both of us were wrong, probably. We'd been wrong all week.

The ledger sat between us on a fallen slab of altar stone like something that had been caught and didn't intend to stay caught.

I had been avoiding it. I want to be honest about that, because everything that came after started with the avoiding. For six days I had carried the book in my pack, wrapped in oilcloth, and every time my hand brushed the strap I felt it the way you feel a tooth you've decided not to bite down on. A book that knew my name before I was born. A book that had written *Odile* in ink older than Odile. Maren had spent eleven years teaching me that magic dies at the treeline, that smallness is a kind of armor, and here was an object that made smallness look like the joke it probably always was.

Sera, meanwhile, had been treating the ledger like a patient.

She'd washed her hands in the rain first. She turned pages with a bone folder she kept in her sleeve, never touching the vellum skin to skin. When she found a marginal note she read it twice, once aloud and once with her lips moving. She had grown up inside this book the way I'd grown up inside Maren's garden, and she knew its moods.

"You're going to have to look at it eventually," she said.

"I'm looking at it."

"You're looking at the fire."

"The fire's honest about what it wants."

She didn't laugh. Six days ago she would have—the memory of her laugh was one of the things I was carrying along with the honey jars and the guilt—but since the archive burned she'd gone quiet in a way that frightened me more than the rider on the ridgeline had. Grief had gotten into her the way water gets into a wall, invisible until something shifts.

"Chapter nine," she said instead, and turned back to the beginning of the prophecy proper.

I'll give the ledger this: it did not make itself easy. The script changed hands four times across the century, different keepers adding commentary in the margins like men arguing across a long table through time. The core text was the oldest, in a hand so cramped and angular it looked like the letters were trying to escape the page.

Sera read it the way Aldous must have taught her, translating as she went. "*And in the hundredth year of the drowning,*" she said, "*the ember shall wake in the north country, in a vessel unmarked, beneath a seal of love wrongly cast.*"

"A seal of love," I said.

"Maren."

"I know who." The fire cracked a knot. "Wrongly cast is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"She hid you. That doesn't sound wrong to me."

"She hid me badly enough that a book knew about it." I heard the bitterness come out of my mouth and didn't entirely try to catch it. "Keep going."

"*The ember is not flame nor jewel nor blood-in-vial,*" Sera read, "*but a living key cut to the lock of Ashvael. In his hand the drowned door opens; against his hand it stays shut. Kings have drowned for less. Kings will drown again.*"

The wind moved through the chapel's empty windows, and I noticed—I made myself notice—that neither of us had said *he* yet. We'd been circling it for days. *The ember. The ember.* As if it were a thing that could be put in a box or thrown in a river.

"In his hand," I said. "Open. Against his hand, shut."

"Yes."

"So it's not that I carry it. It's not that I'm the basket the ember rides around in." My voice sounded far away, like someone else's from outside a window. "It's my hand. It's me opening it or not opening it. The prophecy isn't about a person who has a thing. It's about a person who *is* a thing."

Sera set the bone folder down. "Odile—"

"And Morrel wants a door." Everything was connecting with the ugly click of a lock turning. "He doesn't want me dead. Dead embers don't open doors. He wants me *willing*. He wants me standing in front of Ashvael saying yes. That's why the inquisitors hunt instead of just putting an arrow in me at the first ford." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong too. "I've spent six days running from killers. Turns out they're kidnappers. That should feel better. Why doesn't that feel better?"

"Because being wanted alive is worse," Sera said quietly. "Dead, you're finished. Alive, you're *useful*, and people do anything to useful things."

She knew. Of course she knew. Her whole life was archives, and archives are where civilizations store what they've done to useful things.

"Read the next part," I said.

"No."

"Sera."

"It's marginalia. Keeper's notes. Not even prophecy, just—commentary. It's not authoritative."

"Sera. Read it."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide whether lying to me would work. We both knew it wouldn't. She'd lied exactly once in her life, at the ferry toll, and it had cost her something visible; she wasn't going to spend another lie on mercy. That was one of the things about her. Her honesty was a blade she couldn't sheathe.

She turned forward through the pages, past tonight's date and my name, to an entry three chapters ahead of where we sat. The paper there was darker, foxed at the edges, and the ink—when she angled it to the firelight—was brown-black, old, the same aged umber as the entries from ninety years ago.

Written in a book whose newest entry had appeared in ink that predated its own page. Written for a date that hadn't happened yet.

"*The ember wakes, the ember runs,*" she read, and her voice had gone flat, archivist-flat, the tone she used when the words were too heavy to carry any other way. "*The ember stands before the door of his fathers and refuses it. The door takes offense. The ember burns out at Grey Ford, under a grey sky, unmourned but one.*"

The fire popped. Rain worked at the chapel roof, patient as anything.

"Grey Ford," I said.

"There's a crossing called that. On the Sere. South of the marches. It's—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It's three chapters from here, Odile. Maybe eight days' walk. Less if we're followed, and we're followed."

*Unmourned but one.*

I didn't ask which one. I could see from her face which one. I looked at the fire instead, because the fire was easier, and thought about how two weeks ago my largest ambition had been getting the autumn hive through the frost without losing the queen.

Eight days.

Here is what I want to record accurately, because it matters: my first feeling was not fear. It was relief. A horrible, shameful, washing relief, the kind you feel when a fever breaks—*oh, it ends. There is a last page. I can stop running in eight days either way.* And underneath the relief, terror, and underneath the terror, and this is the part I almost missed because it was so quiet: anger. Not at the book. At the sentence. At the grammar of it. *Refuses it, and the door takes offense.* As if doors have feelings. As if kingdoms keep grudges. As if the whole machinery of prophecy were nothing but a lever with my name on the long end.

Maren used to say that hedge-craft is mostly learning which rules are real and which ones are just repeated because repeating them keeps children indoors. Eleven years of *don't cross the treeline, the spells will find you, the valley forgets strangers, stay small, stay small, stay small.* And I had believed her, because believing her kept me alive, and then I'd believed the ledger for the same reason, because it was heavy and old and everyone was willing to kill for it, and things people kill for must be true.

But Sera had told me, the second night, what she'd found in the archive before it burned: the entry with my name had appeared in ink that predates the page. Ink drying backward. A book writing history into its own past. Which meant the book was not a record. It was an argument. Someone—or something—was making claims about the future in the most persuasive format ever invented: the past tense.

"I'm not dying at Grey Ford," I said.

Sera's head came up.

"I mean it. Listen to me. This book wrote my name in hundred-year-old ink and got the date right, fine, I'll grant the date, tonight proved that. But it says I *refuse* the door. Has anyone asked whether I get a say in the refusing? Because refusing is a choice, and a choice can change. The whole prophecy hangs on me doing one specific thing—one refusal—at one specific place—and then burning out. Pull one thread—" 

"You don't know it's a thread," Sera said. "You don't know it's not load-bearing."

"Neither do you. Neither does the book." I stood up, because sitting still with my own death date three chapters away had become unbearable, and paced the length of the altar slab. "Your father kept this book for forty years. What did he call it?"

"A ledger." Her brow furrowed. "An account."

"An account. Ledgers balance. They're not fate, they're arithmetic—somebody did sums and wrote down what the sums said. And sums can be wrong, or the numbers feeding them can change." I turned back to her. "What did your father believe? Did he believe it was fixed?"

She was quiet a moment, and I watched grief move through her face like weather. "He believed it was *accurate so far.*" Her voice roughened. "He used to say prophecy is a mirror, not a chain—that it shows you what you'll do if you keep being what you are. Change what you are, and—" She stopped herself, jaw tight, as if finishing the sentence would be a betrayal of the dead.

"And?" I said softly.

"And he died for a book he thought might be wrong," she said. "So forgive me if I don't find that comforting."

The rain thickened. I crouched back down across from her, close enough to see the ledger's page breathing firelight, my death written in a brown-black hand, neat as a beekeeper's records. *Unmourned but one.* Whoever wrote that had known something about me. Had known I'd be easy to write off. One mourner. They hadn't guessed the mourner would read it over the ember's shoulder.

"Then we take after your father," I said. "Accurate so far. We'll be the error."

"That's not—" She exhaled. "You can't out-argue a book, Odile."

"You can't out-run one either, and I've been trying for six days." I reached out and, breaking every rule of her training, put my bare finger on the margin below the Grey Ford entry. The vellum was cold. "So we stop running from it and start reading it. Every keeper's note, every margin, every crossed-out word. If this thing is arithmetic, I want to see the working. Somewhere in a hundred years of commentary there's a keeper who asked the same question I'm asking."

"Which is?"

"What happens if the ember says yes."

Sera stared at me. Then, slowly, she pulled the bone folder from her sleeve again, and something in her shoulders shifted—a settling, a soldier picking up a weapon she'd hoped never to use. Duty, rerouted. Not delivering me to the council. Delivering me to the argument.

"He'd open Ashvael," she said. "That's the whole terror of it. The ember's whole purpose. Say yes, and the drowned kingdom's door swings wide—and whatever drowned it comes back out with the tide."

"Then the answer's no," I said. "But it has to be *my* no. Not the book's. Not Maren's seal. Not a council binder's cage. Mine. The prophecy only works if I refuse the way it wrote me refusing—small, scared, sealed. So we break the seal first. We find whatever Maren buried in me and dig it up, and then, standing at Grey Ford whole, I refuse again, differently, and watch the sums fail."

For a long moment she said nothing. Rain, fire, the ledger's patient weight.

"You understand what you're describing," she said finally. "Unsealing you means your magic comes back. All of it. Whatever Maren locked away to hide you—it comes back, and it comes back *loud*, and every inquisitor on the continent will feel it light up."

"I know."

"And the Veil will let you go. The valley forgets, but it won't forget you twice."

"I know that too."

"And if you're wrong about the mirror—if your father was wrong, if the chain holds—" Her voice didn't shake, but only because she was holding it. "Then the book wins, and Grey Ford happens, and I'll have helped deliver you to the exact page you die on."

*Unmourned but one.* I reached across the altar stone and took her hand off the ledger, off my death date, and held it. Her fingers were cold from the rain and did not pull away.

"One mourner's already more than the book budgeted," I said. "Come on. Help me find the flaw in the accounting. You're the archivist. I'm just the mistake in the column."

That earned me half a laugh—broken, wet, real—and she wiped her eyes with the heel of her free hand and opened the ledger back to its beginning, and we read the way besieged people eat, fast and without ceremony, trading the book between us while the candle drowned.

It was near dawn, Sera asleep sitting up with her head against my shoulder, that I found it. A marginal note in the oldest hand, squeezed sideways into the gutter of the page, so faint I'd read past it four times:

*The ember is not the fuel. The ember is what remains when the fuel has burned. Ask what burned.*

I sat very still, so as not to wake her, and did not sleep again.

Because three chapters hence, at Grey Ford, under a grey sky, the ledger promised I would stand before the door of my fathers and refuse it—and I finally understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own handwriting, that the book had never once claimed I would refuse *willingly.*

Something was going to make me say no.

And eight days south, at a crossing called Grey Ford, it was already waiting.
