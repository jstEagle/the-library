## 11. Inquisitor Hale

The inquisitor's tent smelled of tallow and wet wool, and Hale hated both, because they reminded him of the winter his family had crossed the Saltmarsh on foot.

He sat at a camp table with a map of the free cities spread before him and did not look at it. He was looking instead at a small object wrapped in oilcloth: a shard of glazed tile, blue as deep water, chipped at one corner. He had carried it for twenty-two years. It had once been part of a wall in Ashvael's high city, in a house with his name carved over the door. He unwrapped it every night and wrapped it again every morning, and he could not have said why, except that some things you keep so that the world cannot pretend they never were.

Outside, Morrel's army made its small noises—hammering, horses, men laughing badly. Forty thousand soldiers camped on the marches above the drowned kingdom, close enough that on quiet nights the tide-sounds came up through the ground like breathing.

A knock. His adjutant, Brannoc, put her head in without waiting for leave, which was her one insolence and the reason he kept her.

"The archive's burning," she said.

Hale set the tile down carefully, as if it might wake. "Whose order?"

"Yours."

"I know whose order." He heard his own voice go flat and didn't correct it. "Who lit it?"

"Vane Morrel's men. The local ones. They got there ahead of your writ." Brannoc came in and stood at the table's edge, arms folded. She was Ashvaellan too, though she'd been a child when the kingdom drowned and remembered it the way you remember a story someone told you wrong. "They took the keeper alive first. Then they burned everything behind him."

"And the ledger?"

"Gone, or ash. The keeper's daughter is missing. So is a boy."

"A boy," Hale repeated.

Brannoc shrugged. "Beekeeper's apprentice, they're saying. Ran from a hedge-witch years back. The locals are frightened of him now, in that way where they can't say why."

Hale looked at the map again, properly this time. The Veil lay to the west, a smudge of green the cartographers always left half-blank, as if the valley embarrassed them. Somewhere in it, a book a hundred years old had written down yesterday's date and a stranger's name. Somewhere near it, an old man was being carried toward Morrel's court in chains, and a girl and a boy were running along roads that narrowed every hour.

"Describe him," Hale said.

"Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Brown hair. Nothing else worth saying. He kept bees."

"He kept bees," Hale said, "and a century-old ledger learned his name last night." He tapped the map, west of the Veil, where the mountains came down to the marshes and the ruins surfaced at low tide like the backs of drowned cattle. "That is not nothing, Brannoc. That is the whole of everything."

* * *

People assumed Morrel's inquisitors were monsters, and Hale let them assume it because fear moved armies faster than orders did. But an inquisitor's true trade was not terror. It was reading. You read burn patterns and ledgers and faces; you read what people built their lies out of, because no one lied from nothing—they lied from wishes, and wishes had shapes.

Hale had been reading the prophecy of the ember for eleven years, since the night Vane Morrel had put a cup of wine in his hand and said, *You're the only man in my court who still prays to Ashvael, so you're the only man I can trust with its future.* flattery, Hale had thought then, and been grateful for it. He'd drunk the wine. He'd read the books. And the more he read, the more certain he became, because the prophecy did not speak in riddles so much as in promises: *an ember shall go into the dark and come back kindled; the door of the deep shall open to its own; and the drowned shall rise.*

He believed it the way other men believed in harvests.

His wife, Ilsette, before the fever took her, had used to argue with him about it. She'd been born in exile, in a rented room in Corren with salt coming through the plaster, and she said a kingdom was not a place you went back to but a thing people did together, and everyone who knew how to do it was scattered or dead. He'd said, *Then we gather them.* She'd laughed—not cruelly—and said, *And if they won't be gathered?*

She'd died asking it. He still couldn't answer her, which was why the question lived in him like a splinter working toward the heart.

He answered it with work instead. Eleven years of work. He had mapped every ruin that surfaced in the marches, interviewed every fisherman who claimed to have seen lights under the water, collected every variant text of the prophecy from six countries' archives—some bought, some borrowed, several simply taken. In all those years he had found exactly one thing that mattered: the ember would be a person, and the person would come out of hiding when a book told him to.

Now a book had told a beekeeping boy to exist.

* * *

In the morning he rode down to the shore of the drowned kingdom, which he did sometimes alone, though it was against sense and slightly against orders.

The tide was out. The ruins stood black and dripping in the grey light—a tower here, the ridge of a roof there, streets running away into the water like seams of a garment. Gulls worked the mudflats. Farther out, where the water went from brown to green to a blue that had no business being that color this far north, the tops of the great spires showed, furred with weed, patient as gravestones.

Hale dismounted and stood at the tideline. Twenty-two years ago he had stood on a hill above this same shore—he'd been nine—with his mother's hand hard on his shoulder, watching the kingdom go down. Not sinking, exactly. The water had come up out of the ground, out of wells and cellars and the mouths of statues, as if the land itself had opened its throat and swallowed. The royal line had died in a single night, the histories said, all of them, every heir, and the wild magic that filled the kingdom had nowhere left to pour itself, so it poured into the sea instead and pulled the city after it.

Except the histories were written by the survivors, and the survivors had needed the royal line to be dead. Dead heirs meant no one to fight over. Dead heirs meant the exiles could grieve and move on.

But prophecies were also written by survivors, and this one said otherwise.

"You're down here again," Brannoc said, behind him. She had followed him, as she followed him everywhere, at a distance that managed to be both respectful and judgmental.

"My homeland is under the water, Brannoc. Where else would I stand?"

"On higher ground." She came up beside him and looked out at the spires. She never saluted them, never bowed her head, never did any of the small devotions the older exiles performed at the tideline. "Morrel wants you at midday. The council's debating whether to move on Corren or wait for the girl and the boy to surface."

"Corren will shelter them," Hale said. "Whatever else the free cities are, they're sentimental."

"Then Morrel will want Corren. And he'll want your terms for it."

Hale turned up his collar against the wind coming off the water. It smelled of salt and rot and, beneath both, faintly, of something floral—the weed-beds far below the surface, blooming in the dark without any sun. When he was a child, those gardens had been the pride of the low city. His grandmother had helped tend them. She'd said flowers that grew without light were proof that Ashvael was blessed, that even its shadows flowered.

Now the shadows were forty fathoms deep and still flowering, and his grandmother's bones were somewhere among them, and a boy who kept bees had woken up inside a book.

"Here are my terms," Hale said. "The city opens its gates. No sack, no fires, no reprisals against the council. The archivist's daughter comes to me unharmed, with the ledger, and anyone who touches her answers to me. And the boy—" He stopped. Looked at the water. "The boy comes to me alive, whole, and afraid of exactly the right things."

"That's not terms, that's a wish list."

"Then Morrel can wish with me or without me." He mounted. From up here the drowned city looked almost peaceful, the way graves do. "One more thing. Find out who lit the archive. If they burned anything before my writ arrived—if they burned pages, Brannoc, texts, anything readable—I want their names, and I want them flogged where the exiles can watch. We do not burn books about Ashvael. We have lost enough of it."

* * *

Midday brought him into the usurper's pavilion, all crimson and gilt, where Vane Morrel received petitioners under a canopy sewn with the sigils of three conquered provinces. Morrel himself sat forward in his chair the way he always did, elbows on knees, a big plain-faced man who liked people to think power sat on him lightly. It sat on him like a hand on a dog's scruff, and Hale had known it for eleven years and never said so.

"The keeper talks," Morrel said, by way of greeting. "An old man, but talkative. Says the ledger named a boy in a hidden valley. Says his own daughter took the book and ran."

"He'd know," Hale said. "It's his book."

"He also says—" Morrel smiled, and the smile was the worst thing about him, because it was genuinely warm; he enjoyed good news however it arrived— "that the ember isn't what we thought. That the prophecy doesn't promise us a weapon. It promises us a key."

"A key opens doors either way," Hale said.

"Don't be careful with me, Hale. It's early." Morrel waved at a servant for wine. "The council at Corren is split. Half of them want to sell the boy to me for a treaty. The other half want to bind him and bury him in a cellar until he stops being dangerous. Either way, he ends up caged, and caged things are cheap. But you—you want him brought to me gently, like a relic. Tell me again why I indulge you?"

Because I am the only person in your empire who wants what you claim to want, Hale did not say. Because you need a patriot to do a thief's work, and I have been doing it for eleven years without looking down.

"Because I bring him to you working," Hale said aloud. "Frightened things break. Broken keys open nothing. Let me manage the taking, and you'll have your ember standing up and willing."

"Willing." Morrel tasted the word and found it amusing. "Fine. Take your exiles, take your writ, take whatever you need. Bring me the boy and the book." He drank, and over the rim of the cup his eyes were patient and cold and entirely awake. "And Hale—if the key turns out to fit a door you'd rather I didn't open, remember who taught you that ambition is a debt."

"I've never been taught anything by you that I agreed with," Hale said, and bowed, and walked out of the pavilion with his spine straight, and only when he reached his own tent did his hands begin to shake.

He unwrapped the tile. Blue as deep water. Chipped where a soldier's boot—or a panic, or a wave—had caught it, twenty-two years and four hundred miles from the wall it came from.

He thought of Ilsette asking her question. *And if they won't be gathered?* He thought of the boy keeping bees in a valley that forgot itself, and the girl running with a book in her arms, and the old man in chains downstairs, and he understood, with the clarity that came to him only at the tideline, that he was going to find them first—not Morrel's dogs, not the local burners, him—and that what he did when he found them would decide whether he had spent his life restoring a country or robbing a grave.

He wrapped the tile. He called for Brannoc, and for his horse, and for maps of the road to Corren.

By dusk the whole camp knew the inquisitor was moving, and by midnight, so did the two fugitives on the western road—though they did not yet know it—because a rider in usurper's livery had stopped at a ferry crossing, laid a silver coin shaped like a fish on the toll-keeper's board, and asked one question:

*A boy who keeps bees, and a girl carrying a book. Which way?*
