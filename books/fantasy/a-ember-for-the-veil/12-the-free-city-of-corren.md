## 12. The Free City of Corren

We saw Corren two days before we reached it, which was the first sign that it was not like other places.

Most cities announce themselves with smoke. This one announced itself with silence—a pale line on the horizon that resolved slowly into walls, and above the walls a hundred towers flying banners of every free city from here to the Salt Coast, each one refusing to match its neighbor. Sera counted them aloud as we walked, an archivist's habit I was learning to find steadying.

"Corren, Maravel, Ostenhedge, Brask," she said. "The old four. Then the younger ones—Saltmere, Dunlow, Kess." She paused at a banner of deep green stitched with a white gate. "Ashvael's seat is empty."

"Has been for a hundred years."

"That doesn't stop them flying the space for it." She said it lightly, but her hand drifted to the ledger in her pack—the way it had a dozen times a day since we left the Veil, checking it was still there, still open to my name.

I understood the impulse. I checked myself the same way. Reaching up under my collar for the scar at the base of my throat where Maren's sealing sigil sat like a swallowed stone, confirming it hadn't cracked, that nothing had leaked out of me onto the road behind us. The marshes between the Veil and Corren were dotted with shrines to forgotten river-saints, and every one of them made my teeth ache when we passed, which Sera told me meant my magic was listening even if I wasn't.

"It shouldn't do that," she'd said the first time. "Not inside the seal."

"It's not doing anything," I lied. "It's just—it knows things are near. Like a dog smelling rain."

She didn't believe me. She was getting better at telling when I lied.

Now, under the shadow of the outer wall, a queue of carts and pilgrims stretched half a mile, and I felt the familiar clench in my chest—the fear of gates, of lists, of men with stamps and questions. But Sera simply took my elbow, turned her shoulders so her archivist's sash showed, and walked us past the entire line to a side postern where a bored guard straightened up fast.

"Sera Vane. Archive of the Veil, sworn keeper." She produced a wax tablet with a seal I hadn't known she carried. "My witness and ward. We carry material relevant to the council's session."

The guard looked at me. I looked at the ground, doing what I did best: being small. Being nobody. A hedge-witch's apprentice, travel-stained, dull-eyed, unremarkable.

It worked better than it should have. The guard waved us through, and only when we were inside the roar of the market streets did I realize what bothered me about it.

"He didn't ask your witness's name," I said quietly. "Your ward's name."

Sera's step hitched, just slightly. "Corren guards check seals, not faces. The council trusts paper over people. It always has."

"That's not trust," I said. "That's laziness wearing trust's coat."

She laughed despite herself, and the sound loosened something in me, and for maybe fifty steps I let myself believe we'd actually arrived somewhere safe.

* * *

The safe part lasted until evening.

Sera had contacts in Corren—an archivist's network of cousins and former classmates and one landlady who owed her mother a favor—and through them we got a room above a chandler's shop in the Tallow Quarter, cheap because it smelled permanently of rendered fat and came with a landlady who asked no questions as long as you paid in advance and didn't burn tallow near her curtains. Sera went out at dusk to deliver letters and feel the political temperature. I stayed in with the ledger, because apparently I was now a person who read prophecy by candlelight in a room that smelled like candles' raw materials.

I had gotten as far as the third chapter—where the language shifted from the formal old Ashvaellan script into something more personal, more urgent—when Sera came back up the stairs too fast.

"They're debating it tomorrow," she said. "First session. It's already on the agenda."

"Debating what?"

"You." She dropped onto the stool across from me, pulling off her boots with the violence of someone who needed to hurt something and had only her feet available. "Item seven: 'The question of the ember named in the Vane ledger, and Corren's response thereto.' I heard three delegates arguing about it through a door. Odile—one of them used the word 'bargaining chip.'"

I closed the ledger on my finger to keep my place, which struck me, mid-motion, as absurd. Careful with the book, even as the book discussed selling me.

"Trade me for peace," I said.

"Not officially. Officially there will be very serious men using very careful words like 'diplomatic resolution' and 'moratorium on provocation.' But yes. Morrel has been offering the cities a non-aggression pact for two years, and everyone knows the price he actually wants. If they can hand him the ember without technically handing him anything—" She stopped. Looked at me properly. "I'm sorry. I thought the council would be a shield. That's why I brought you here instead of east."

"You brought me here because the ledger says the ember reaches the free cities," I said. "You've told me this four times. You don't have to keep apologizing around it."

"I know. I just—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I spent my whole life believing the archive was the safest place in the world. Paper doesn't lie, paper doesn't betray, paper just remembers. And now the paper is being used to decide whether to sell you, and I don't know what to do with that."

"Then we leave tonight."

"And go where? Every road east runs through territory Morrel patrols. Every city south is smaller than Corren and less able to refuse him." She lowered her hands. Her eyes were red-rimmed but steady, and I hated how much I loved that steadiness. "There's nowhere left that doesn't have to make a decision about you, Odile. So we make sure the decision gets made by people who've at least read the whole prophecy."

"The council hasn't read the whole prophecy."

"No," she said. "But I have. And I'm going to be in that chamber tomorrow, testifying, because a sworn keeper of the Vane archive has standing to address the session, and nobody thought to exclude me because nobody imagined the keeper's daughter would be sitting next to the thing itself."

I looked at her for a long moment. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"My mother used to say those are the same thing viewed from different distances." She stood, and stretched, and some of the fear burned off her the way it does when a person finally picks a course. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow you need to look harmless."

"That's the one thing I'm genuinely qualified for."

* * *

The council chamber of Corren had been a temple once—you could tell from the bones of it, the high ribs of stone overhead and the empty alcoves where gods used to stand before the free cities decided gods were bad for trade. Now the alcoves held flags, and the altar dais held a semicircular table where nineteen delegates sat in order of their cities' founding, and below the dais rows of benches for petitioners and clerks and anyone else important enough to watch power happen.

Sera found us seats at the back. I wore her spare clerk's robe, grey and shapeless, hood up. Underneath it my sealed magic sat quiet as a held breath, and I kept my eyes down and listened to item after item roll past—tariff disputes, a grain shortage in Brask, a border arbitration between two towns I'd never heard of. Ordinary problems. Problems belonging to a world where prophecies weren't keeping appointments with my life.

Then a delegate rose, and a clerk called the session to order on item seven, and the temperature of the whole room changed.

His name, the placard said, was Delegate Corm of Brask: a heavy man with a merchant's rings and a farmer's weathered face, and he opened the way politicians everywhere open—by expressing grave concern.

"Delegates, we face a matter touching the security of every free city." He didn't shout; he didn't need to. The chamber was built to carry ordinary voices. "Vane Morrel's armies sit forty days from these walls. His envoys offer us peace—genuine peace, binding and witnessed—in exchange for a single individual. One boy. Not a citizen of any of our cities. Not a soldier. Not a contributor to any of our treasuries or granaries. A runaway apprentice who stumbled out of a hidden valley carrying a title he never earned and a danger none of us can measure."

He let that sit. Around me, heads nodded. Not eagerly—not cruelly, most of them—but with the weary reasonableness of people doing arithmetic.

"One individual," Corm repeated, "against tens of thousands of lives. I did not become a delegate to gamble my grandchildren on a riddle written by dead men."

An answer came immediately from the far end of the arc—a thin woman in Saltmere grey. "Delegate Corm would sell a person like a shipment of dye."

"Delegate Ilse mistakes me. I would trade nothing. I would recognize reality. Morrel takes what he wants eventually. The question is only whether we pay in one life or ten thousand."

"And when he comes back?" Ilse's voice was dry as old vellum. "Today the ember. Tomorrow the ledger. Next year, anyone whose grandmother ever swam in Ashvaellan waters. Tyrants don't close accounts, Delegate. They keep the ledger open."

"The ledger," said a third voice—old, papery, and instantly familiar in a way that made my neck prickle, "is precisely the problem."

I turned. Three rows ahead, an elderly man in the deep green of the empty Ashvaellan seat had risen. The green banner delegate. The seat that had flown empty for a hundred years.

"The Vane prophecy does not merely describe the ember," he said. "It promises restoration. The reopening of Ashvael. Delegates, our ancestors drowned a kingdom full of wild magic to save the world from it, and this book says a boy can undo that seal. Whether he wishes to or not. Whether he even understands what he carries." He spread his hands, and I saw they trembled—not with age, I realized. With feeling. "I have read the marginalia. The heir of Ashvael's royal line walks the earth again. Gentlemen, ladies—I ask you to consider what Morrel would do with a key to the drowned kingdom. And then ask what we would do, if we could trust the hand that held it."

A murmur ran the benches. Someone behind me whispered, *the Ashvael seat*, and someone else answered, *they still send a delegate, every session, for a hundred years—ceremony, nothing more.*

Ceremony, watching me from three rows away.

"The proposal before the body," the moderator said, rapping for order, "is that the council extend protective custody to the individual identified in the Vane ledger, such custody to include binding of his latent abilities under supervision of this council's appointed—"

Binding. The word went through me like cold water through cloth. Maren's word. The thing in my throat stirred against its seal, and I had to put a hand flat on the bench to remind it, and myself, that we were in a room full of stone and witnesses and I could not afford to be interesting.

"There is a complication," Corm said smoothly, "with protective custody. Namely, that we cannot protect. Morrel's army is forty days out. Our combined levies number perhaps eight thousand. Custody is not shelter, delegates. Custody is a promise we lack the soldiers to keep."

"Then we keep our promises anyway," Ilse snapped.

"With what?"

"With honesty! Put the choice to him. The individual exists, presumably nearby, given the quality of intelligence Morrel's agents have shown. Ask him. He may prefer to run. He may prefer—" she hesitated, and I watched her choose the word carefully— "*to serve.*"

Serve. Run. Be bound, be traded, be spent. I sat in my borrowed robe and listened to nineteen strangers allocate my future among those verbs, and the strangest thing happened: hope curdled. Because I had walked forty leagues toward this room believing that walls meant safety, that councils meant justice, that somewhere the world contained an institution too large and too decent to be afraid. And now I could see clearly that the council was just men and women, tired and outnumbered, doing arithmetic with my name in the sum—and that the kindest of them, the ones nodding at Ilse, were nodding because serving the free cities sounded better than serving Morrel, not because anyone in this chamber had considered for one moment that I might belong to myself.

Nobody had said *he*. Only *the individual*, *the ember*, *the asset*. Even the woman defending me couldn't quite say *him*.

Except—

"—and further," the green delegate was saying, "this body should understand that the keeper of the Vane archive is present in Corren. Sworn to deliver the ember to this council. When she surfaces, the question resolves itself: custody becomes duty, duty becomes law."

Cold moved up my spine. Sera's hand found mine on the bench between us, gripping hard.

They knew. Not that I was here—not yet—but that she existed, that she was coming, that the book would arrive with its own escort of obligations. Somewhere in this city, clerks were already drafting the writs. All we had done by reaching Corren was arrive at the place designed to catch us.

The session adjourned without a vote—deferred, the moderator said, pending testimony from the archive's representative. Pending Sera. Pending me, though no one knew it.

Out in the corridor, amid the shuffle of robes and the drone of clerks, Sera pulled me into an alcove between a statue of some forgotten magistrate and a notice board thick with tariffs. Her face had gone the color of the chalk on the agenda board.

"They're going to bind you," she said. "Legally. With paperwork. They'll make it sound like sanctuary."

"I heard."

"Odile." Her grip tightened. "I came here to give you to the council because the council was supposed to be the good ending. That was the whole architecture of my life—the archive, the oath, deliver the ember to the free cities and everything balances. And now I'm standing in their hallway and I don't—" Her voice caught. Steadied. That terrible steadiness again. "I don't think I believe in the architecture anymore."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Down the corridor, delegates emerged in knots, already negotiating, already trading item seven for grain quotas and border favors. Corm was laughing at something. Ilse was writing furiously. The green delegate stood alone by the doors, looking at nothing, trembling faintly, a patriot of a country under the sea.

"Sera," I said quietly. "Who else knows you're in the city?"

Her silence was an answer.

"Then we have until she testifies," I said. "However long your network buys us. After that, the choice stops being theirs or ours and starts being a writ."

She looked up at me, and something shifted in her face—not the archivist, not the keeper's daughter, but the girl who had once, in another life, memorized a laugh she wasn't supposed to keep.

"Where do we go?" she asked.

I thought of the third chapter of the ledger, the urgent hand, the words I hadn't finished reading. I thought of a smuggler's name Sera's landlady had let slip over dinner, half-joking: *if you ever need a door nobody official knows about, you want the Magpie.*

"There's someone in this city," I said, "who sells ways out."

And behind us, unnoticed, the green delegate of Ashvael turned from the council doors and began, unhurried, to follow.
