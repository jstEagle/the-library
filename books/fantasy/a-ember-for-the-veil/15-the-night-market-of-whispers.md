## 15. The Night Market of Whispers

The smuggler's market did not exist during the day.

Odile learned this the way he'd learned everything since fleeing Corren's walls: slowly, uncomfortably, and from Sera, who delivered the information in the flat tone she reserved for facts she disliked admitting she knew.

"Below the tannery row," she said, hunched over the ledger in the abandoned smokehouse where they'd spent the daylight hours pretending to sleep. "There's a stair that goes down past the curing pits. The tanners think it floods. It doesn't. It goes to the old river gate—the one Corren's founders sealed when the city stopped wanting to be a port." She glanced up. "My father traded with them. The archivists always did. You can't record what the world forgets unless somebody's willing to sell it to you."

"And these people sell forgetting," Odile said.

"They sell *out* of it." Sera closed the ledger with the careful hands she used for it, as if it might bruise. "Memories. Names. Debts. The Magpie deals in all three, and he's the only one who's ever claimed to know a way through Ashvael that doesn't end in drowning." She looked at him then, really looked, and Odile felt the weight of what she wasn't saying settle over both of them like the smokehouse's old soot. *You have a death date three chapters from now. We have eleven days. This is the only door.*

"All right," he said.

"You don't have to sound brave about it."

"I'm not being brave. I'm being out of options. There's a difference, and I'd appreciate it if you noticed it."

The ghost of a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Noted."

* * *

The stair behind the tannery row was exactly as unpleasant as advertised. It descended in a spiral of slick stone, past the reek of curing pits and the drip of water that had been dripping, Odile suspected, since before Corren had a name. His candle—the only light he'd allow, because Sera's lantern announced them to anyone watching—guttered twice and held.

At the bottom was a door made of river-boat planking, scarred and tarred, with no handle. Only a knocker shaped like a fish, mouth open, waiting to be fed.

"You're supposed to put a coin in its mouth," Sera whispered.

"What happens if you don't?"

"Nobody knows. Nobody who didn't has come back to say."

Odile dug out the last of the honey-money—a bent copper with a face so worn it might have been anyone's—and set it on the fish's tongue. The mouth closed. Somewhere beyond the door, a bell rang once, though he couldn't see how.

The door opened on darkness and the smell of the river, and a voice said, "Two of you. One marked. Come in before the tide changes its mind."

* * *

The night market of whispers was held in the bones of Corren's first harbor—a cavern of vaulted stone where the river had been turned aside a century ago and now ran black and patient along one wall, lit by lanterns that burned without flame, green-gold and cold. Stalls lined the old quays: not stalls of goods but of *absence*. One merchant sold silence, decanted into stoppered jars, each labeled in a hand Odile couldn't read. Another sold the last words of the dying, written on slips of vellum that whispered when the river breathed. A woman with silver-threaded braids offered to buy your nightmares at a fair rate and sell them back to you at interest.

Nobody looked at anybody. That was the rule, and it was enforced not by guards but by the simple, universal understanding that everyone here was someone the surface world was better off not seeing.

"Keep your hood up," Sera murmured. "Keep walking. The Magpie's at the far end, past the memory-brokers. And Odile—"

"I know. Don't tell anyone my name."

"Don't tell anyone *any* name. Names have weight down here. Spend them carefully."

They walked. Odile kept his eyes down and his ears open, which was how he caught the fragments that made his skin crawl: *—the ember, they say it's a boy—* and *—Morrel's offering a barony for whoever finds him—* and, from a stall they passed too close to, a memory-broker holding up a vial of smoke and saying to a customer, "You're sure? It's a good memory. First snow. You'll never have it again."

The customer, a hollow-eyed dockhand, nodded. "I'm sure. I want to stop waking up cold."

Odile walked faster.

The Magpie's stall was the last one, built against the sealed harbor gates themselves—two doors of black iron taller than a church, crusted with salt that shouldn't have been there, forty miles from any sea. The smuggler sat behind a table of river glass and old brass, and he was not what Odile expected, though Odile couldn't have said what he'd expected. A cutthroat, maybe. A conjurer.

The Magpie was a small, neat, gray-haired man with a clerk's hands and a bookkeeper's eyes, wearing a coat with too many pockets, and he looked at Odile the way Aldous had looked at his ledger: as a page he'd already read.

"The keeper," he said. Not a question.

"Don't call me that."

"You'd prefer 'boy'? 'Fugitive'? 'His Highness'?" The Magpie's smile was thin and dry as paper. "I've been paid in all three words this month, and they all mean the same thing. Sit."

There were two stools. Sera took the one nearest the door, out of habit or duty, and Odile sat, and the Magpie folded his clerk's hands and waited, which Odile slowly understood was a technique. The silence stretched. The river breathed against the quay. Finally Odile cracked.

"You know a door out of Ashvael."

"I know *of* one." The Magpie produced, from one of his too-many pockets, a scrap of oilcloth and unwrapped it with the tenderness of a man handling a relic. Inside was a coin—but not a coin. A disc of black glass, cloudy at the center, with a hole through it as if it had once hung on a chain. "There were three keys to the drowned kingdom. One went down with the royal house. One is in the keeping of the Veil's witch—don't look surprised, boy, I've sold her candles for forty years. And one came to market." He tapped the glass disc. "This one."

Odile's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the river air. *A key of black glass.* Sera's father had pressed something into her hands in a burning archive, and she hadn't shown it to him, and he hadn't asked, and now a smuggler forty miles away was describing it like inventory.

He didn't look at Sera. It was the hardest thing he'd done all week.

"And you'd sell it," he said. "To me."

"I'd *trade* it." The Magpie's eyes sharpened. "I don't sell to the marked. Selling implies you could pay in coin, and coin is the least interesting thing about you." He leaned forward. "Here is what I know, keeper, so you understand what you're bargaining for. The drowned gate of Ashvael doesn't open for force. It doesn't open for blood—plenty of Morrel's fools have bled on it and gotten nothing but wet. It opens for an heir who *willingly gives up the inheritance*. Renunciation, freely spoken, at the threshold. The kingdom itself decides whether to believe you." He sat back. "That's the door out. That's what your death date is, if you're wondering. The ledger's not predicting your funeral. It's predicting your coronation in reverse."

The words landed in Odile's chest like stones dropped down a well. He heard them hit bottom, one after another, and the bottom was a long way down.

"You've known this," he said slowly. "You've known this and you've sat on it."

"I've known it and I've *priced* it. There's a difference, and I'd appreciate it if you noticed it." The Magpie's dry echo of Odile's own words should not have stung as much as it did. "Information is like honey, boy. It spoils if you give it away, and it ferments into something dangerous if you hold it too long. Tonight it's ripe. Tonight you're here. Tomorrow Morrel's inquisitors will be in this market asking prettier questions than yours, and my price will have changed."

"What's your price?"

The Magpie looked at him for a long moment. Then, gently, almost kindly, he said:

"A memory. Of your choosing."

* * *

The rules, as the Magpie laid them out, were simple in the way of all terrible things.

He would draw the memory out through the black glass key—the key was the instrument, that was why it came to market, that was why it was *his*—and once drawn, the memory was gone. Not hidden. Not sleeping. Gone, the way a burned page is gone. Odile would keep the facts of his life the way you keep a story you've been told about yourself: he would know that something had happened, know its shape, know its date, but the living center of it, the warmth, the weight, the *truth* of it, would be in the glass, and the glass would be in the Magpie's pocket, and the Magpie did not give refunds.

"You may choose anything," the Magpie said. "Most people choose their worst day. Grief is heavy, and they want to travel light." He turned the key over in his fingers, and the lantern-light moved in its cloudy heart like something alive turning over in its sleep. "Some choose their best. Those are the sadder cases, in my experience. You can survive your worst day twice. No one survives their best day twice."

"And if I choose nothing?" Sera asked, from her stool by the door. Her voice was very controlled. "If he walks away?"

"Then the key goes to the next buyer, and the next buyer will not be a boy with a death date and a conscience. It will be a man with an army." The Magpie shrugged, small and neat. "I am not a good man, archivist's daughter. I am a fair one, and only within the terms of the trade. The tide's turning. Choose."

* * *

Odile chose to stand up and walk to the edge of the quay, because he could not think sitting down.

The black river ran past him, patient as a ledger. Above, through the vaulted stone, he could just make out the underside of Corren—the city that had debated him like a shipment of grain, that had offered him sanctuary shaped exactly like a cage, that was even now, perhaps, drafting the terms of his binding. Behind him, the Magpie waited with a key that could open a drowned kingdom and a price that could empty a man. Beside the door, Sera waited too, and that was the problem. That was the whole problem, laid out as clean as a line of fresh ink.

Because Odile knew exactly which memory he would choose, and he had known it since the Magpie said the word, and it shamed him.

Not Maren's cottage. Not the day his magic first woke and burned the well-water to steam and his mistress had looked at him with fear she tried to hide. Not even the night he'd run, the treeline, the moment the Veil swallowed his name.

He would choose Sera.

He would choose the archive, the first night, the candlelight, her laughing at something he'd said—actually laughing, the sworn archivist, the prophecy-keeper's daughter, the girl sent to deliver him to a council of binders, laughing like the oath had slipped off her for one whole second. He would choose that, and he would hand it to a smuggler in a coat with too many pockets, and he would walk out of this market knowing she was his ally and feeling nothing of why that mattered. He would travel light. He would survive. And some night on the road, when she looked at him a certain way, he would feel the hole where that laugh used to be, and he would not even know what he was missing, because that was the cruelty of the trade: you couldn't grieve what you couldn't remember.

*That's what the price really is,* he thought, watching the river. *Not the memory. The not-knowing. He's selling you a smaller life, and calling it a door.*

But the ledger had his death in it. Three chapters hence, whatever that meant—days, weeks, the turn of a page. And the Magpie's door was real. Renunciation, freely spoken. A way out that wasn't a grave.

*If I forget her, I can't be hurt by the date. I can't be hurt by her either. I can walk to Ashvael's gate like a man walking to a well, and give up the throne, and feel nothing, and live.*

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it. Not that the temptation was strong, but that it was *quiet*. It didn't roar. It just sat there, reasonable as arithmetic: trade the warmest thing you have for the only exit, and stop being a person things can be taken from.

"Odile."

Sera's voice, behind him. He hadn't heard her cross the quay.

"You're doing the thing," she said, "where you decide something alone and then tell me afterward like it's weather. I've read enough prophecy to know how that ends."

He almost laughed. That was the trouble. That was the exact trouble.

"The price is a memory," he said, not turning around. "Of my choosing."

The silence behind him went very still.

"And you're standing at the river," she said carefully, "because you're deciding which one."

"I'm deciding whether the trade is worth what's left after."

"Odile." She came around to face him, and the cold green lantern-light put her half in gold and half in shadow, and he thought: *this is what I would be trading. This exact light. This exact stubbornness.* "Whatever you're about to hand him—don't. Whatever it is. The ledger says the keeper chooses. Not the smuggler. Not the council. Not the book. *You.* And I've spent my whole life reading about men who chose the easy door, and every single one of them—" she stopped, and he watched her decide to say it, watched her choose the true thing over the safe one, "—every one of them ended up a footnote in something my father was trying to warn people about. Don't be a footnote."

"And if the easy door is the only one?"

"Then we find a hard one." She said it simply, the way she said everything that mattered. "I'm an archivist. I've read ten thousand years of hard doors. One of them opens."

For a moment neither of them moved. The river breathed. Somewhere in the market behind them, the nightmare-broker laughed at something.

Then Odile turned and walked back to the stall, and Sera fell in beside him, and the Magpie watched them come with his clerk's hands folded and his bookkeeper's eyes bright with interest.

"Well?" the Magpie said. "What will you give me?"

Odile set his palms flat on the river-glass table. "Nothing tonight."

"Nothing is not a trade."

"Then here's a trade." His heart was hammering, but his voice came out steady, and he was distantly surprised to find that it sounded like a man who had already decided something. "You keep your key. You keep your door. But you tell me *where* the gate is—exactly, the approach, the water, the way in—and you tell me what the rite requires, word for word. And in exchange, when this is over, when the ledger's last page is written, I come back and pay you. Whatever you ask. A memory, a name, a debt. I'll owe you, and I'm the last heir of Ashvael, and my debts are going to be worth something very soon."

The Magpie was quiet for a long moment.

"That," he said finally, "is the worst bargain I've been offered all year, and the only one I'm tempted by." He turned the black glass key over once more, and in its cloudy heart the light turned too, like an eye considering. "You understand I could simply take it. The key draws, boy. It doesn't ask."

"You could," Odile agreed. "But then you'd have the memory of a dead man, and dead men's memories spoil fastest of all. You said so yourself. Information ferments." He held the smuggler's gaze. "I'm worth more alive and owing."

The Magpie smiled then, a real one, small and crooked, the first honest thing in the whole market.

"Your witch taught you to bargain, or the bees did?"

"The bees."

"Of course they did." He swept the key back into its oilcloth, back into his impossible pocket, and from another pocket produced a stub of charcoal and a square of tanned hide, and began, with a clerk's neat precision, to draw. "The gate is here—under the Widow's Teeth, where the river splits around the drowned bell tower. You approach at slack water, and only slack water, or the current will take your boat and the gate will take the current's leavings. And the rite—" his charcoal paused, "—the rite requires a witness. Someone who loves the heir enough to hold the door open from the outside while he speaks. That part's not in your ledger, I'd wager. It wasn't in mine either. I learned it from the last man who tried, and he learned it too late, and the door took him anyway."

Odile felt Sera go still beside him.

"Who was the last man who tried?" she asked.

The Magpie finished his map, folded it, and held it out—but he didn't let go when Odile took it. For a moment they were connected by a square of hide, smuggler and heir, across a table of river glass.

"He was a door-keeper's apprentice," the Magpie said. "From a valley that forgets. And the reason I know his face is that his witch still buys candles from me, and every year on this date she buys one extra, and she never says for whom." He released the map. "Go. Slack water is in four days. The Widow's Teeth are nine days' ride. And keeper—"

Odile paused at the edge of the lantern-light.

"The inquisitors reached the surface gate an hour ago. They're asking after a boy and an archivist's daughter." The Magpie's eyes glinted, green and cold. "I'd take the tanners' stair up and the north wall down, and I'd run faster than you've ever run in your life. The market forgets, but Corren's gates don't. By morning, there will be a man named Hale standing at every one of them—and Hale, of all Morrel's dogs, is the one who *prays*."

* * *

They climbed in silence, the tanners' stair unspooling above them, Sera's hand cold in his where the stair was narrow enough to need it. It was only at the top, in the reek of the curing pits, with the first gray hint of dawn showing between the rooftops, that she spoke.

"His face," she said. "The last man who tried the gate. You knew it. I saw you know it."

Odile stood very still.

"It was my face," he said. "Older. Tired. But mine, Sera. The Magpie sold my mother a candle every year for a man who looked exactly like her son—and the ledger says I die at the gate in three chapters—and I'm starting to think the book isn't predicting anything at all." He looked east, toward the Widow's Teeth, toward the water, and his voice came out flat and strange. "I think it's remembering."

Below them, faint through the stones, a bell began to ring—the harbor gate's bell, the fish-mouthed door, ringing once, twice, a third time.

The sound of a coin being placed on a tongue.

Someone else was going down to the market. And from the direction of Corren's north wall, thin on the wind, came the first horns of the morning watch—and under them, almost too far to hear, a voice calling through a speaking-trumpet, ordering the gates closed, the city searched, and a name read out that made Sera's hand tighten on his until it hurt.

His name.

The real one. The one only two living people were supposed to know.
