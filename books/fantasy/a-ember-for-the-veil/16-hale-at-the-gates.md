## 16. Hale at the Gates

The banners went up at dawn, and Hale made sure they were his own.

Not Morrel's black sun with its devouring rays—those flew only over the usurper's seat, and Hale had spent three years making certain they never flew over anything he loved. These were grey and white, the old quartered field of Ashvael's marches, a design so out of fashion that most of his soldiers thought it was simply his personal standard. Let them think that. It was easier than explaining that he had sewn the first one himself, at fourteen, from his mother's good tablecloth, the week before the floods took everything.

"Signal flags on the east ridge," said Vess, his second, squinting through the glass. "Corren's answering. They've lit the council brazier."

"Of course they have." Hale lowered his own glass. The city sat in its bowl of farmland like a fat purse on a merchant's table: white walls, red roofs, the council spire rising out of the middle with its eternal smoke. Corren had never been taken by storm. Corren had never needed to be. Every army that had ever come to its gates had been bought, fed, or waited out, and the city wore that history like a smirk.

He intended to be the first to change it, or the first to be paid enough not to try. Both outcomes suited him. Only one suited Morrel, and Hale had learned to keep the difference between those two facts folded up small, where no one could see it.

"How many in the fields?" he asked.

"Farmers coming in. We're letting them." Vess paused. "You said we were letting them."

"I said what I said." Hale handed back the glass. "A city that watches its harvest walk through its gates unmolested remembers it. A city that watches us burn wheat remembers that too, but it remembers it angry, and angry cities hold walls longer. Open the roads until noon. After noon, we close them."

"And after noon?"

"After noon," Hale said, "I go and have a polite conversation."

* * *

The parley ground was a trampled circle halfway between the army's pickets and Corren's western gate, marked out with stakes and white cord because even threats, in the free cities, required paperwork. Hale rode out with six men and a white banner and the particular exhaustion of a man about to say words he had already said in his head four hundred times.

The gate opened. A delegation came down the switchback road: five councilors in their chain-of-office colors, a clerk with a portable writing desk carried between two porters, and a squad of city guards who looked like they had been given their helmets that morning. At the center walked a woman Hale knew by reputation if not by face—Delegate Ossa, silver-haired, famous for talking three sieges into retirement.

She stopped ten paces off and looked at him for a long moment.

"Inquisitor Hale," she said. "They told me you'd be wearing the black sun."

"They told you correctly. I declined." He dismounted, which surprised her; he could see it flicker across her face. Parley was traditionally conducted between men on horseback so that neither could claim the humility of standing. He stood anyway. His knees ached, and besides, he wanted her to look down slightly at nothing at all.

"You fly the marcher quartering," she said. "That's either sentiment or strategy."

"It's memory."

"Memory is strategy that got old." She smiled without warmth. "State your terms, Inquisitor. My clerk is ready, my lunch is getting cold, and I'm told your general has a schedule."

Hale let the silence sit. This was the part he was good at—the part Morrel valued, though Morrel would never say so. Not the killing. The arithmetic of fear, done aloud, in a level voice, in front of witnesses.

"The Ember of Ashvael is in your city," he said. "A person. Young, unbound, carrying the old blood. You know this, because your archivists have known since the ledger entry surfaced, and because my riders have watched two fugitives come up the salt road for nine days. I am authorized to offer the following. Surrender the ember to my custody, alive and unharmed, and the host of Vane Morrel passes Corren by. No siege. No requisition. No winter garrison eating your cellars empty. Your walls stay whole, your harvest stays yours, and the council keeps its seats and its heads."

Ossa's expression did not change. "And if we decline?"

"Then I invest the city tonight. I take the river works tomorrow. I burn the granaries at Ford Hollow the day after, and every refugee from the surrounding valleys comes inside your walls to eat food you do not have. By month's end you will be negotiating with me over soup." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way a physician describes an amputation. "I would prefer not to. I have seen a city starve. It is not a thing I recommend."

"You've seen a city starve," Ossa repeated softly. "Ashvael."

The name landed in the space between them like a coin dropped in a quiet room.

"Yes," Hale said.

"My grandfather traded there. Dried fish upriver, iron down. He said the Ashvaellans sang before every meal, even the bad ones. Especially the bad ones." Her eyes went to the quartered banner above his men. "Is that what this is, Inquisitor? A song before a bad meal?"

"This is terms," Hale said. "The singing is finished. What remains is whether Corren buys its peace with one person who came here asking nothing, or pays for it in a currency you cannot mint fast enough. You have until the morrow's dusk. I'll expect your answer at this ground."

He turned to mount, and Ossa spoke again, quieter, pitched for him alone.

"There's a story in the archives," she said. "That when Ashvael drowned, the door-keepers sealed it from the inside. That the kingdom chose the water. If that's true—if the old blood chose drowning once already—what makes you certain it wants to be dug up?"

Hale kept his hand on the saddle and did not answer for a moment. Somewhere behind the city walls, faint on the wind, a bell was ringing the noon hour, and under it, thinner, some child's voice practicing a scale.

"Because everything that drowns," he said finally, "kicks on the way down."

* * *

His tent smelled of tallow and wet canvas and, underneath, the rosemary oil he rubbed into his hands at night because his mother had used it and because some habits are load-bearing. He sat at the camp desk with the campaign maps weighted by a stone that had once been a doorstop in a house outside Ashvael City, and he did not look at the stone, and he looked at it constantly.

Vess came in with the day's tallies and found him staring at the eastern wall of the tent as though it might open onto somewhere else.

"Report," Hale said, before she could ask.

"Roads closed at noon as ordered. Last carts in were a wool factor and a widow with eight goats, who argued with Corporal Bren for twenty minutes and won. Grain stores at Ford Hollow—" Vess hesitated. "You gave the order to hold the burning."

"I gave the order to hold the burning."

"You did. I'm recording that you did." She set the tallies down. "Morrel's liaison asked why. I told him you wanted the grain intact for provisioning after surrender. He wrote it down. He also wrote down that you let the farmers in, and that you fly the wrong banner, and that you stood on foot at parley like a man courting votes instead of issuing threats."

Hale turned a page of the map he wasn't reading. "Does the liaison have a name?"

"Tassin. He's twenty-three and ambitious and he writes everything down."

"Then let him write this too." Hale looked up. "Tell Tassin that Inquisitor Hale has burned eleven towns for the Regent's peace and does not require instruction in the craft from a boy whose first campaign is still wet behind its ears. Tell him the ember comes out of Corren within two days, one way or another, and that when it does, his report can say whatever flatters him."

Vess didn't move. Twenty years she'd served him, back to the marsh campaigns, back to the ugly winter at Callow Ford, and she had earned the right to stand in his tent and look at him with that flat, patient stare until he said the true thing under the spoken thing.

"You want them to refuse," she said.

"I want the terms delivered. That's the assignment."

"You want them refused." She tapped the map, the quartered banner stitched into its corner in his own hand, years ago, in different ink. "You've drawn the investment lines loose on the west side. Any competent defender would see the gap. You've held the granaries. You let the harvest walk in. Either you've gone soft, sir, or you're building yourself a door out of this siege, and I'd like to know which, because if it's soft, I need to start planning around you, and if it's a door, I need to know where it leads."

Hale sat with that. Outside, the army murmured—ten thousand men cooking supper, sharpening, gambling, telling each other the city would fold in a day because cities always folded. Ten thousand men who believed, because he made sure the reports read that way, that this was a straightforward errand: retrieve the relic, reopen the kingdom, go home rich.

Only Vess knew there was no home to go back to. Only Vess had ever heard him say the word *Ashvael* without the official phrasing around it, the way a man says the name of a dead wife.

"It leads to the drowned gate," he said at last. "It always leads to the drowned gate. Everything I've done for nine years leads there. Morrel thinks he wants a weapon. The council thinks they want a hostage. The prophecy-mongers think they want a savior." He pressed his thumb against the stone doorstop, cool and river-smooth. "I want a door open. One time. Enough to stand in the street where my mother's house stood and smell bread baking, even if the street is underwater and the bread is a hundred years gone. That's all. And then whatever the kingdom decides—drowned, restored, burned to glass—I'll accept it, because it will have answered, and no one living has ever heard it answer."

Vess was quiet a long moment. "And the boy? The ember?"

"The boy is the key, not the prize. Keys get used. That's what keys are for." He heard his own voice and disliked it. "If it can be done without breaking him, I'll prefer that. If it can't—"

"If it can't," Vess said, "you'll tell yourself the kingdom is worth more than one runaway hedge-witch's apprentice, and you'll be right, and it'll sit in you like a swallowed stone for the rest of your life. I've watched you carry stones a long time, sir. You've room for maybe one more."

"Get out of my tent, Vess."

"Aye, sir." At the flap she paused. "For what it's worth. The widow with the goats said the city's frightened. Said the council's been arguing since the parley ended. Said there's a faction that wants to bind the boy themselves rather than give him to either army." She let that land. "Binding takes a chamber, and chambers take votes, and votes take till morning at the earliest. Thought you'd want it in your ledger."

She left. Hale sat alone with the maps and the stone and the rosemary ghost of his mother's kitchen, and he took out the letter he kept in the false bottom of the campaign chest—a letter written in a dead language to a dead city, beginning *To the Door-Keepers of Ashvael, whoever yet lives*—and he added a line beneath the others, in ink he mixed himself from marcher oak-gall because imported ink felt like lying:

*I am coming. I am bringing an army I do not trust to a gate I do not understand. Judge me by what I do at the threshold, not by what I burned to reach it.*

He blotted it, put it away, and slept badly, as he had slept badly for nine years, dreaming of a street with bread in it.

* * *

Dusk of the morrow came grey and windless. The parley ground again, the same stakes, the same white cord, but this time the delegation from Corren was smaller and the clerk had left the writing desk behind, which told Hale everything before anyone spoke. You brought the desk to negotiate. You left it to deliver a verdict.

Delegate Ossa stood in the same place, ten paces off, and this time she did not smile at all.

"Corren declines," she said.

Something moved through Hale—not surprise, he had built the gap in his western lines precisely so that this refusal would cost less than it should—but something older, almost relief, the feeling of a fever breaking. *Good,* said a voice in him that sounded young and fourteen and sewing a tablecloth into a flag. *Good. Now we go and see.*

"You understand the consequence," he said aloud, flat, official, the physician's voice again. "Investment begins tonight. The granaries at Ford Hollow—"

"—are untouched, yes, we noticed, and the council noticed, and Delegate Imre noticed especially, and it will be raised in session, Inquisitor, rest assured, everything is raised in session." Ossa clasped her hands. "But hear the shape of our refusal, because it is not the refusal you think. We will not surrender the ember to Morrel's custody. Nor will we surrender him to yours. But the council has voted—narrowly, bitterly, with two delegates walking out—to bind him. To cage the fire in lawful ash, here, under our own seal, so that no throne, drowned or dry, may use him. He will live. He will live small. You may report to your Regent that the ember is extinguished in every way that matters."

Hale stood very still. Behind Ossa, the city bell began to ring—not the hour, the alarm pattern, slow and heavy—and torches were moving along the wall-tops, and somewhere in the warrens of Corren, in a chamber he could picture without ever having seen it, clerks and binders were preparing to do to a runaway boy exactly what a hedge-witch had once done to hide him, except this time there would be no valley to forget itself around him, no mistress to bury his name, no door out.

Bound fire was dead fire. He had watched bound mages in Morrel's service—things that ate, slept, obeyed, and had nothing behind the eyes but walled-up light. The kingdom would never answer a knock from a caged fist.

Unless.

"Delegate," Hale said, and his voice came out lower than he intended, almost gentle. "When does the binding proceed?"

"At dawn. The chamber sits at first light."

"Then we have a night," Hale said, "you and I. And I find, Delegate Ossa, that I am suddenly very interested in your city's sewers."

Ossa stared at him. Above her shoulder, the council spire smoked against the last of the light, and Hale turned his horse toward his own lines, already composing the message he would send ahead through channels Morrel did not watch—an old smuggler's cutout, a Magpie who owed him—and thinking, with a homesickness so old it had worn smooth as riverstone, *Hold on, boy. Whatever you are, hold on until morning.*
