## 18. Fugitives Again

The binding chamber's alarm was still ringing in my bones when the Magpie pulled me down through a hatch that smelled like a century of Corren's dinners.

"Feet first," she hissed. "Always feet first. A man who goes headfirst into a dark hole has already decided to die."

I went feet first. The drop was shorter than I feared and filthier than I'd imagined—my boots hit standing water ankle-deep, warm in a way I refused to think about. Above me, Sera dropped without hesitation, landing light as a page turning, and then the Magpie came down last, hauling the hatch shut behind her and throwing a bolt across it.

Above us, muffled by stone and timber, the shouting continued. The council had voted. The binders had come for me with their silver collars and their polite voices. And Sera had stood up in front of forty delegates and called her oath a lie to its face.

I hadn't had time to process any of it. There hadn't been time for anything but running.

"This way." The Magpie moved through the dark like she owned it, which, in a sense, she did. She was smaller than I remembered from the night market—a wiry woman of indeterminate age, wrapped in gray layers, her face half-hidden behind a scarf that smelled of lamp oil. "Keep your left hand on the wall. If you lose the wall, you're lost. If you touch anything with your right hand, you'll wish you hadn't."

"What's on the right?"

"Sewers," she said.

* * *

We walked. Or rather, we waded, sloshed, climbed, and occasionally crawled, through a labyrinth beneath Corren that no map officially acknowledged. The Magpie navigated by touches and murmurs—two taps here, a whispered word there—and twice she stopped so abruptly that I walked into her back, and both times she held up a hand and we waited in the dripping dark until whatever she'd heard passed.

Sera kept close behind me. I could feel her presence the way you feel a fire at your back—not warmth exactly, but an awareness. She hadn't spoken since the chamber. Treason, I suspected, required a certain silence while it set, like mortar.

Finally I couldn't stand it. "You didn't have to do that."

"Yes, I did."

"You could have let them bind me. You'd have kept your oath. Your archive. Your father's name. Everything."

Her voice came out of the dark, steady and quiet. "Odile. They were going to put a collar on you that does what Maren did to you for fourteen years, except this time you'd have agreed to it. I've seen what that kind of cage makes of a person. I wasn't going to watch them build one around you and call it sanctuary."

I wanted to say something worthy of that. What came out was: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. We're under a city that wants you bound, ahead of an army that wants you dead or delivered, and above us is a man who wants to use you as a key." A pause. "Also, you're standing in sewage."

"I'm aware."

"Good. Just checking."

Despite everything—the alarms still faintly ringing overhead, the collar-shaped future we'd escaped—I laughed. It came out ragged and surprised, and in the dark I heard Sera laugh too, brief and real, and something in my chest unclenched for the first time since Aldous Vane's ledger had shown me my own name in century-old ink.

The Magpie glanced back at us. I couldn't see her eyes behind the scarf, but I felt the look anyway, dry as paper. "Save it," she said. "Romance keeps poorly underground."

* * *

An hour in—or what I guessed was an hour; time moved strangely when the only clock was the ache in your legs—we stopped in a wider channel where three tunnels met, and the Magpie produced a stub of candle from somewhere inside her layers and lit it with a flint she handled like a surgeon's tool.

In the small light, I got my first real look at where we were. The walls here were older than the rest—older stone, fitted without mortar, and carved. Faint lines ran up the blocks in patterns I almost recognized: waves, and beneath the waves, spires. Towers drawn underwater.

Sera saw them too. Her breath caught audibly.

"Ashvaellan," she said. "This is Ashvaellan work."

"Corren stands on old foundations," the Magpie said, holding the candle closer. "Most cities do. People build on top of what drowned rather than dig it out. Cheaper." She traced one of the carved spires with a finger, and for a moment her voice lost its smuggler's flatness. "There are those who say the sewers of Corren follow the streets of Ashvael. That if you walked them far enough, you'd end up at the old gate."

"The drowned gate," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "The door out."

The Magpie looked at me over the candle flame. "That's the story you bought from me in the night market, keeper. Yes. There's a way. But ways under cities are like promises—they cost more than they appear to."

"You already took your price," I said. "A memory. I paid it."

"You paid a deposit." She smiled, and it wasn't entirely unkind. "The rest comes due when you actually use the door. But that's tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem is getting you out of Corren before the binders think to look down."

She pinched out the candle, and we moved again.

* * *

The sound reached us before anything else: boots. Many boots, on stone above us, moving with the purposeful rhythm of soldiers searching.

The Magpie pressed us into a side alcove—a maintenance niche barely wide enough for the three of us—and killed the little shuttered lantern she'd been using. In the blackness, I heard Sera's breathing slow deliberately beside me, the way Aldous must have taught her: an archivist's discipline, control applied even to breath.

The boots grew louder. Then, impossibly, they grew *closer*—not above us anymore but in our own tunnel, coming toward us, torchlight bleeding around the alcove's edge.

I felt my magic stir.

It had been doing that more and more since the marches—a heat under my sternum, a pressure behind my eyes, like water building against a dam that Maren had built and I had spent fourteen years pretending wasn't there. In the binding chamber it had surged hard enough to crack the floor tiles before Sera's hand on my arm brought me back. Now it rose again, answering fear the way a fist answers a threat, and I clamped down on it with everything I had.

If it broke loose here, in these tunnels, we would drown in the flood. So would half of Corren.

*Don't*, I told it. *Not now. Not here.*

The torchlight swung toward our alcove.

And stopped.

"Here?" said a voice I knew. Young, precise, carrying the particular weariness of someone performing authority he wasn't sure he believed in. Imre. The delegate who had offered me sanctuary shaped like a cage.

"Nothing in this section, Delegate," said another voice—older, bored, a city watchman following a rich man's orders.

"I'll check this alcove myself. Protocol." Boots approached. Torchlight filled the niche, blinding after the dark, and I stood blinking in it with Sera's shoulder against mine and the Magpie a shadow at our backs, and Imre Vass stood in the tunnel looking at us.

He was maybe twenty-five, with the fine clothes of the council and the hollow eyes of someone who hadn't slept since the vote. He looked at me for a long moment. I looked back. Behind him, the watchman waited, picking at his teeth.

"There's nothing here," Imre said finally, not raising his voice. "Old nesting spot for rats. Move on."

"Delegate, protocol says—"

"Protocol also says the council's business is the council's business, and this is mine. Move. On."

The watchman moved. His boots faded down the tunnel, and then it was just the three of us and the torch in Imre's hand and the drip of water counting seconds.

"Why?" I asked. It came out rougher than I intended. "You offered me the collar yourself. You argued for it. You said binding was mercy."

Imre's jaw tightened. He glanced back the way the watchman had gone, then stepped into the alcove's shadow, lowering the torch so his face fell into darkness. "I argued for it because I believed the council would keep its word. Shelter you, protect you, negotiate with Morrel from strength." His laugh was short and bitter. "Then I watched them spend forty minutes debating how much peace was worth, and I realized none of them were talking about you at all. You were never a person in that room. You were a figure in an equation. And I thought—" he stopped, started again. "My grandmother came from the river country. When Morrel took it, she used to say the worst thing about thieves isn't what they steal. It's what they make you become while you're guarding what's left. I watched good people in that chamber become accountants of other people's freedom tonight. I don't want to be an accountant."

"So you lied to the watch," Sera said quietly. "For us."

"For the version of myself I'd like to still be tomorrow." He dug in his coat and pressed something into my hand: a small waxed-paper packet. Inside, by torchlight, I saw dried meat, two apples, and a folded scrap of a map. "The east sally gate. The watch rotation changes at the fourth bell; there's a gap of a hundred count. The Magpie will know it." He straightened. "I never saw you. If anyone asks, I searched this alcove thoroughly and found rats."

"Imre." I didn't know how to hold what I was feeling. Gratitude was too small a word and too large a debt. "When this is over—"

"It won't be over," he said simply. "Not for years, maybe. Men like Morrel don't stop, and councils like ours don't learn fast enough. But go. And Odile—" he hesitated, and for a moment he looked very young, "—whatever the ember is, whatever they say you can open—some doors are locked for reasons. Make sure you know whose reasons you're serving before you turn the key."

He turned and walked away, torchlight shrinking down the tunnel, and then we were alone in the dark with an apple each and a hundred-count gap in a watch rotation, given to us by an enemy with a conscience.

I found, absurdly, that my eyes were hot.

"Eat," the Magpie said, striking the lantern alight again. "Apples keep worse than secrets."

* * *

The east sally gate was a rust-bitten door in a wall of older stone, and the Magpie opened it with a length of wire and what sounded like a personal insult. Beyond it lay a drainage culvert, and beyond that, the night—open sky, actual stars, air that tasted of rain and river instead of rot.

The fourth bell rang as we slipped out. Somewhere behind us, a watchman yawned through his hundred-count gap.

We moved along the culvert until the city walls fell away behind us, and then up a sheep track through wet grass, and then we were on the open downs with Corren's lights glittering below like a spilled purse, and I made myself stop and breathe.

Sera stood beside me, hair plastered to her forehead, sleeves filthy to the elbow, and she was watching the city with an expression I couldn't read.

"They'll expel her," she said. "Me. Strip my archivist's rank, strike my name from the rolls. Maybe worse, once Morrel's ambassadors start asking questions." She said it evenly, the way you report weather. "Everything I trained for. My father's whole life's work, and I'm the daughter who burned it."

"Do you regret it?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Below us, a bell began tolling the alarm we'd been outrunning, thin and late.

"When I was nine," she said, "my father caught me copying a prophecy out of the sealed ledger. Just copying—it was an exercise, he checked my transcription against the original every week. And I asked him why we bothered preserving prophecies nobody was allowed to act on. And he said—" her voice caught, steadied— "he said, *because someday the book will be right about something terrible, and the only question that matters is whether the person reading it loves the world more than the book.*" She turned to look at me. "I spent ten years thinking that meant loving the archive. Tonight I found out it doesn't."

The words settled into me like rain into dry ground. I wanted to answer them with something equal, and I had nothing equal—all I had was the truth, plain and unarchived.

"I'm glad it was you," I said. "In the chamber. Of everyone in the world who could have broken their oath for me, I'm glad it was you."

Something moved across her face—there and gone, banked like a fire at night. "Don't be glad yet," she said. "We haven't survived the week."

* * *

The Magpie led us north through the downs all night, avoiding roads, following tracks only she could see. Toward dawn we sheltered in a ruined shepherd's hut, and she built a tiny fire of driftwood and finally unwound her scarf, and I saw her face properly for the first time: sharp-boned, weathered, with a scar running through one eyebrow and eyes the gray of deep water.

"Now," she said. "While you eat. Business."

I braced.

"The door you want—the drowned gate of Ashvael—I can take you to it. But understand what you're asking. The gate isn't a door like doors. It's a wound in the world that was stitched shut on purpose, by people who had very good reasons, and everything your ledger says about opening it, the ghosts on the other side will have opinions about too." She fed a stick to the fire. "And there's a second thing. The price we agreed. One memory, my choosing, paid when the door opens. I told you it was a deposit. Here's the truth of it: memories of a keeper aren't like other memories. Yours are load-bearing. Take the wrong one out and the whole house comes down."

"Then choose carefully," I said.

"Oh, I intend to." She looked at me across the flames, and for a moment the smuggler fell away and something older looked out—something that had been watching doors a very long time. "But here's what I'll give you free, keeper, because I liked your face in the night market and I like it less now that it's famous. Before you pay me anything, read the marginalia in your ledger. All of it. Your archivist's father wrote more than dates in that book, and I'd wager my boat he wrote more than either of you has read."

Sera's head came up sharply. She'd been silent, turning the ledger over in her lap, and now she opened it with hands that weren't quite steady, thumbing past the entry with my name, past the death date three chapters hence, into the margins—tiny script, cramped, urgent, filling every white space Aldous Vane had ever owned.

"He did," she breathed. "Odile. He did. Look—"

She turned the book toward the firelight, and I leaned in, and the first line of her father's hidden hand read:

*To whoever finds this—if the ember is reading, the door was never meant to be opened from this side.*

Outside, far to the south, the bells of Corren were still ringing.
