## 22. After the Flood

I woke to the smell of river mud and woodsmoke, and for a moment I did not know where I was or why my hands were shaking.

Then it came back: Grey Ford, the prisoner exchange, the general in Morrel's black arriving with too many soldiers and too few intentions. Odile standing between them with his palms open like a man begging. And then the river rising—not rising, *standing up*—a wall of brown water taller than a church, folding over the ford and everything on it, while Odile screamed a word I didn't know in a language that made my teeth ache.

The general's column was gone. Not routed. Gone. Swallowed. The river had taken forty men and a banner and left nothing but a churn of foam and one boot wedged in a willow root.

Sera, I told myself. You are Sera Vane, archivist of Corren, daughter of Aldous Vane, keeper of the ledger that started all of this. You are lying in a hay barn above the ford because you ran until your lungs tore and then someone—Odile, it had been Odile—caught you before you fell.

You are alive. He is alive. That is all the accounting that matters this morning.

I got up. My knees were bad about it. Outside, the village was already awake, and something in the sound of it—the quality of the voices—told me before I saw anything that the world had changed shape overnight.

* * *

There were perhaps two hundred people in the hamlet below the ford, the kind of place that doesn't appear on maps except as an ink fleck: a mill, a shrine to some river god whose name even the priest had forgotten, fields stitched along the waterline. They should have been mourning. The flood had drowned their mill race, silted their fields, torn a bridge loose from its moorings.

They were not mourning.

They were kneeling.

I came out of the barn into grey light and found them arranged in the lane in rough rows—farmers with their hoes planted in the mud like halberds, miller's wives with flour still ghosting their sleeves, children pushed forward by their mothers' hands. All of them facing the well at the lane's center, where Odile stood with a blanket around his shoulders and an expression I recognized from the day I first showed him the ledger: the look of a man reading a page he cannot believe is real.

An old woman knelt nearest him. She held up a loaf wrapped in linen, arms trembling with the effort of holding it high.

"Lord of Ashvael," she said. "Heir of the Gate. Bread for our king."

"I'm not—" Odile's voice cracked. He tried again. "Please get up. Please."

"He raised the river," someone said behind me, not unkindly, just stating it the way you'd state the weather. "The river stood when he said stand. It took the usurper's men and it spared us."

"It spared us," another voice took up, and then they were all saying it, a low tide of murmur rolling through the rows of kneeling bodies, *it spared us, it spared us,* until the words stopped being words and became something older than language—a chant, a name being shaped without yet having syllables.

I found Odile's eyes over the old woman's bowed head.

*Help me*, his look said.

And I, who had spent my whole life believing that truth was a thing you owed every person who asked for it, looked away first.

* * *

Later, in the miller's back room, with the door shut against the murmuring village, we finally fought about it.

"They knelt," Odile said. He was pacing, which he did when he was frightened, though he'd never admit it. The blanket was gone; he'd refused it after the first hour. "Two hundred people, Sera. On their knees. In the mud."

"Yes."

"That's what Morrel wants people doing. That's what the whole war is *about*. And they did it to me." He turned on me, and there was real anger under the fear, the good kind, the kind with somewhere to go. "Did you see the old woman? She called me lord. She called me heir. She thinks I'm—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "She thinks I'm something I've spent twenty years making sure I wasn't."

"She thinks you saved her life."

"The river would have spared them anyway! It wasn't aiming at them. I didn't aim at *anything*—" His hands were shaking again. I wanted to hold them still. I did not. "I have never once in my life aimed, Sera. That's the whole point of Maren's seal. That's why I ran. Because the last time this power was loose in the world it drowned a kingdom, and everyone in that valley knows it, and now two hundred strangers want to put a crown on the exact same mistake."

"Is that what it was?" I asked. "A mistake?"

He flinched as if I'd struck him.

I hadn't meant it cruelly. But I am my father's daughter, and my father taught me that a question honestly asked is never cruelty, however much it costs. So I asked it again, softer.

"You leveled the ford. You killed forty men—maybe more, we don't know—and you saved a village and me and yourself. Was that a mistake, Odile Coldharbour?"

"That's not—" He pressed both fists against his eyes. "When you say it like that it sounds like arithmetic. Like the deaths cancel against the lives and come out even. It doesn't work like that. None of it works like that."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't. But you don't get to call it only a disaster either. That's not honest. That's just fear wearing honesty's clothes."

For a long moment he stood very still, fists against his eyes, breathing. Through the wall I could hear the village moving around outside, footsteps pausing at our door, moving on. Being watched. Being *waited on.*

"When I was small," he said at last, dropping his hands, "Maren told me a story about Ashvael's last king. My—about the last king. How when the wild magic rose, he went down to the gate and he could have shut it and saved himself, but instead he opened it wider, because he believed he could command the flood." His laugh was terrible. "He believed he was the exception. Every heir believes they're the exception. And now there are people kneeling in the mud telling me I'm the exception, and the worst part, Sera—the part I can't say out there—is that some small voice in me likes it."

The confession hung in the dim room. I watched him hate himself for it.

"Good," I said.

His head snapped up. "*Good?*"

"If you liked it completely—if there were no small voice arguing—that's when you'd be dangerous. A man who feels no pull toward a crown can't be trusted near one, because he's never tested whether he'd grab it. You feel the pull and you're horrified by it. That's not corruption, Odile. That's a conscience working exactly as designed."

He stared at me. Outside, the murmur of the village swelled briefly and faded—someone singing, maybe, or praying. I thought of my father, dead in a burning archive, handing me a book and a key of black glass and thirty seconds of instructions. *The ember must reach the free cities.* I had sworn oaths about this boy. Half of them I'd already broken. The rest were waiting their turn.

"What do I do?" he asked. Simply, like a child. "They won't stop kneeling. I can't stay here. I can't lead them anywhere. What do I *do*?"

I thought about the ledger. About how prophecy works—not as a chain, though I hadn't understood that yet, not fully—but as a mirror, showing each reader the face they bring to it. Two hundred villagers had read a wall of standing water and seen a king. I had read the same event and seen a frightened boy who needed to keep walking.

"You tell them the truth," I said. "As much of it as they can carry. Then we leave before dawn tomorrow, and by the time they realize we're gone, the story will already be growing past us. Stories always do. Let it grow. It'll protect you better than any army."

"And if the story grows into something that gets people killed following it?"

"Then that's the cost of being the ember," I said, "and you don't get to set it down. Believe me. I've checked."

* * *

At dusk I went out among them, because someone had to, and because I wanted to understand what we'd become to these people. An archivist's habit: when a text starts behaving strangely, read it closely.

The old woman with the bread was named Grett. Her son had been taken by Morrel's press-gangs two winters ago and she spoke of the flood the way pilgrims speak of miracles. "My mother told me stories of Ashvael," she said, pressing the same loaf into my hands—I'd refused it twice already; the third time seemed rude. "Said the true line would come back when the world needed it most. Said the heir would come out of hiding like a coal out of ash." She patted my arm with a hand like tree bark. "You're his lady, then?"

"I'm his—no. No, I'm his friend. His companion. We're traveling to the free cities."

"To give him up to those council vultures?" Her eyes sharpened suddenly, and I saw that under the reverence was something harder. These were border people. They knew what councils did. "Or to crown him?"

"Neither," I said, and heard how weak it sounded.

Grett studied me for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she apparently decided to be merciful. "Whatever you are," she said, "keep him walking. Kings die in palaces. This one needs to stay moving."

It was, oddly, the sanest counsel anyone had given me in weeks.

Other households were less gentle. A young man with a soldier's bearing—deserted from some garrison or other, by the way he stood—asked me precise questions through the fence: How many could Odile raise? Could he raise the rivers at Corren? At Morrel's capital? When would he march? Each question had an army folded inside it, and I answered none of them, and walked away with my skin crawling, understanding that word of yesterday was already traveling faster than we could. By tomorrow night it would reach Morrel's survivors. By the day after, it would be in every tavern from here to the coast, growing fat and strange: *the heir of the drowned kingdom rose the river and ate a legion.*

We had to leave tonight. Not dawn. Tonight.

* * *

But when I went back to tell him, Odile was already outside.

I found him at the edge of the lamplight, at the boundary where the lane gave up and became field, and the kneeling villagers had followed him even there—a crescent of them, keeping their distance, not demanding anything, just *watching*, the way people watch a fire. And Odile stood in the center of their attention with his head bowed, and I saw him do something I have never forgotten since.

He knelt back.

One knee, in the cold mud, in front of a farmer's boy no older than eight, who had been staring at him all evening with the naked hunger children have for the impossible. The boy's mother gasped and moved to pull him away, and Odile shook his head once—*let him*—and stayed down there, eye level, mud soaking through his trouser leg, until the boy worked up the nerve to touch his sleeve.

"You're not a lord," the boy said. Loudly. Children are the only honest witnesses.

"No," Odile said. "I'm not."

"But the river listened to you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And Odile, who could have given the boy a legend, who could have given him a prophecy, who could have given him anything at all because the whole village was straining to hear his answer—Odile gave him the truth.

"Because I was born able to ask," he said. "That's all it is. Asking. Anyone might have been born able to ask. It doesn't make me better than your father. It makes me more careful than your father has to be. Do you understand?"

The boy considered this with great seriousness. "No."

"Good," Odile said, and smiled, and it was the first real smile I'd seen on him since the ford. "Hold onto that. The day it makes sense to you is the day someone's sold you something."

The crowd made a sound—not disappointment, not quite. Something between relief and loss, like a congregation watching its icon step down off the altar and turn out to be a man. Some of them drifted home after that. Not all. Never all. But fewer knelt the next morning, and the ones who did, did it quicker, more casually, the way you nod to a neighbor—which was, I think, exactly what he'd been trading his legend away to buy.

* * *

We left at moonrise, taking nothing but the loaf Grett wouldn't let me refuse, the ledger, and the key of black glass on its cord around my neck. A handful of the faithful walked us to the field's edge despite Odile asking them twice not to. The deserted young soldier was among them. He pressed a short knife into Odile's hand—good steel, honest weight—and said only, "If you ever do march, send for the ford country first."

Odile took the knife because refusing it would have been its own kind of lie. I saw him weigh it in his palm the whole way across the first field, like a question with an edge.

We walked until the village lights sank behind the hills, and then a little further, because neither of us said stop. Finally, at a crossroads marked with a leaning waystone, he halted.

"Maren," he said.

I blinked. "What?"

"I need to go back. To the Veil. To Maren." He was looking at the waystone as if it were a signpost pointing somewhere no map admitted. "Everything that happened today—it started because she sealed me and never told me what I was sealing *into*. If I'm going to be whatever the villages and the prophecies and Morrel all need me to be, I want to know why the one person who loved me chose to bury it. I want to know what she buried *with* it." He turned to me, and the moonlight made his face look younger and older at once. "Come with me. Or don't. But that's where I'm going."

I touched the key of black glass through my shirt. My father's last order had been to take the ember east, to the council, to safety. The road east lay straight ahead, flat and sensible and lit by stars.

The road west toward the Veil led straight back into everything we'd escaped.

I had already broken half my oaths. I was beginning to suspect that the other half had been written by people who never imagined the world as it actually was.

"West, then," I said.

Odile nodded once, shouldered his pack, and turned down the darkening road—and neither of us saw the rider who had been shadowing us since dusk, far back on the ridgeline, turn his horse west too.
