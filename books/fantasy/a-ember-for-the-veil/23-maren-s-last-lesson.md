## 23. Maren's Last Lesson

I carried her up the valley in the grey hour before dawn, and she weighed less than the honey crates I hauled every summer morning.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was that the bees followed us.

They came out of the hives in a low brown ribbon, thousands of them, drifting at shoulder height like something being poured. They didn't sting. They didn't land. They just kept pace while I stumbled through the frost-stiff grass with my old mistress folded against my chest, her breath coming in small wet clicks, and when I looked back at the cottage I'd rebuilt for her—rebuilt twice, once after the storm and once after she burned it herself in one of her forgetting fits—the door stood open on darkness.

The Veil had never once let me feel magic. Ten years in this valley, and the treeline had eaten every spark that crossed it, including the ones inside me. But now the whole bowl of the valley hummed like a struck bell, and I understood, with the dumb animal clarity of grief, that the humming was coming from her.

Maren was leaking.

"Put me down by the water," she said. Her voice had gone to paper. "Not the well. The river. I want to hear it move."

"The river's outside the—" I stopped. Outside what, exactly? I'd lived here a decade and never asked myself where the Veil ended, only that it did, only that spells died at the treeline like moths against a lamp. "Maren, I need to get you inside. There's blood."

"There's always blood, love. It's how you know the plumbing's still honest." A cough took her, long and tearing, and when it finished she lay very still against my arms and said, without any of her usual armor on it: "I'm dying, Odile. I'd rather do it by running water than under your terrible roof."

I put her down by the river.

* * *

She made me build a fire first, which was absurd, because she'd taught me fire-craft when I was eleven and knew six ways to warm a person without flint. She watched me strike sparks like an examiner grading a slow student, and when the flame caught she sighed as if I'd passed something more important than fire-building.

"Sera?"

"With the horses. She'll be back by midmorning." Sera had ridden east two days ago to buy salt and news, and I'd woken alone in the dark with Maren's name in my mouth and the sense—unshakeable even then—that I needed to go to her. "I can send for her."

"Don't you dare." Maren's hand shot out and gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both. Then it drained away between one heartbeat and the next, and her fingers were just an old woman's fingers, knotted, cold. "What I have to say is for you. And it won't keep, so stop fussing with my blanket and listen."

The bees settled into the reeds around us, a low brown drift, wings shivering. The river ran fast and silver-grey. Somewhere behind us the valley went on being the valley, green and small and safe, and I felt—for the first time in ten years—like a man standing on a stage just before the scenery falls.

"You sealed me," I said. "You sealed my name and my magic into the Veil. I know."

"I know you know. The drowned archive told you, didn't it? Little fish always find the sunken libraries." Her mouth curved, then flattened. "But knowing isn't understanding, boy, and you've had neither the time nor the temperament for understanding lately. So. Last lesson. Pay attention, because I'm only saying it once, and I've been rehearsing it for sixty years."

She turned her face to the river.

"I wasn't hiding in the Veil, Odile. I was *in* it. Held in it. The way you hold a coal in ash so it doesn't burn the house down."

"Maren—"

"Ashvael had three door-keepers when the kingdom drowned," she said, over me, over the river. "One for each gate. North, south, and the deep gate under the water. When the royal line sealed the wild magic below the tide, they needed people to hold the seals—living anchors, because magic doesn't trust stone or oath, but it trusts flesh. My mother held the south door. Her mother before her. I was consecrated at birth, marked at seven, and at nineteen I stood in a corridor that no longer exists and swore my life to a kingdom that had already begun to rot from its own power."

The bees stirred. I didn't.

"When Ashvael went under," she said, "the doors should have died with it. Most of them did. But a seal anchored in living flesh doesn't drown, boy. It *travels.* I woke up on a beach a hundred leagues from home with the deep gate still burning a hole in my chest, and every loyalist, every usurper, every ambitious fool on two continents able to smell it if I ever stood still too long. So I ran. I ran for thirty years. And then I found a valley where magic couldn't live—and I understood that it couldn't live there because something was already eating it."

She looked at me then. Her eyes were clear in a way I'd never seen them, the fog of her forgetting-fits burned away entirely, and what looked out of them was not my fussy, secretive, honey-thieving old mistress. It was someone older. Someone who had watched a kingdom die and then spent a lifetime standing guard over the grave.

"The Veil isn't a place," she said. "It's a wound. I cut it myself, sixty years ago, out of my own seal—a hole in the world that swallows magic, big enough to hide in, and I hid my half of the deep gate inside it. Me and my burden. And then twenty years later a woman with a baby came over the ridgeline running from Ashvael's loyalists, and I recognized the child's blood before I recognized his mother's face."

My mouth had gone dry. "You're saying the Veil is yours."

"I'm saying the Veil is my prison sentence, served willingly, and that you grew up inside it like a bee in a sealed jar." She coughed again, shorter this time, and waved off my hands. "I bound your magic not to cage you, whatever you've decided to believe. I bound it because your magic and mine are the same inheritance, and mine was loud enough that even muffled, it drew hunters twice in the years before you came. Yours would have drawn armies. So I buried it deep, and I buried your name with it, and I built you a small life with bees and bread and no history at all, and I told myself that smallness was mercy."

"And now?"

"Now I'm dying, and dead women make terrible jailers." She smiled, and it cracked something in me, because it was her smile—the one she wore when the honey came in good, when she cheated me at cards, when she said *good morning, apprentice* like it was the only appointment that mattered in the world. "The seal's unraveling. Can't you feel it? The whole valley's been ringing since sundown. That's my anchor going out, thread by thread. Which means the Veil will come down soon after—not today, maybe not this month, but soon. And when it goes, everything I buried in it comes up for air. Your magic. Your name. And the location of the last living seal on the deep gate of Ashvael, which will be sitting in your chest like a lit window in a dark country."

The river kept running. It seemed obscene that it should.

"So don't tell me I betrayed you, Odile," she said, softer. "I did. I stole your life and called it safety, and I'd do it again, and that's the truth of old women—we are not sorry, we are only sorry it wasn't enough. But hear the rest of it. Hear the part that matters."

She reached up. I bent down. Her hand found the collar of my shirt and pulled, gently, until her lips were near my ear, and the bees went absolutely silent, ten thousand wings stilled at once, the whole valley holding its breath.

"I release you," she said.

The words weren't loud. They didn't need to be. Something underneath them moved—a vast thing turning over in sleep, a keel shifting against a rope—and heat bloomed through my chest, down both arms, into my palms, and for the first time since I was nine years old I felt my own magic the way you feel your own heartbeat: not as an intruder, but as *mine.*

I gasped. The fire flared waist-high and blue-white and settled. Frost retreated three feet in every direction, the grass steaming faintly. The river ran backward for the length of a heartbeat, then thought better of it.

"Oh," I said. Very stupidly. "Oh, it's—"

"Loud," Maren said, sagging back. "Yes. It always was. Why do you think I liked the bees? Only things in the world quieter than a sealed door-keeper." Her eyes had begun to lose their focus, but she fought to keep them on me. "Listen. Last part. The last part."

"I'm listening."

"The gate needs a renunciation. An heir, speaking aloud, giving up the throne—with a witness who loves him enough to hold the door. You know this. The archive told you this. But the archive couldn't tell you why, because the archive wasn't there." Her grip tightened. "It's not ceremony, boy. It's surgery. The throne of Ashvael and the wild magic are the same thing—one vessel, passed hand to hand down the royal line, and every heir who took it poured a little more of themselves into the flood. That's what drowned us. Not a curse. Generosity. Kings who loved their kingdom so much they became its container until the container burst."

Her breath rattled. I took her hand. She let me.

"So when you renounce it—if you renounce it—you have to mean it all the way through. Not the crown. Not the power. Not the grief. All of it given away, freely, in front of a witness who loves you, because the door opens outward and love is the only hinge it trusts." A ghost of a laugh. "Your Sera will do. I watched her argue with you about salt prices. Nobody argues about salt prices unless they intend to stay."

"She doesn't—" I started, uselessly, and Maren squeezed my hand hard enough to hurt.

"Boy. I am sixty seconds from finding out what's on the other side of the deep gate, which is more than anyone in four generations has known. Do not waste them telling me what my own eyes can see." She pulled me closer. "Now. The burden. Take it properly."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's the qualification." Her voice was going somewhere far away and she hauled it back by force. "Every door-keeper before me took the seal because they were born to it and never asked. You get to take it because you understand it. That's the whole difference between inheritance and curse—the asking. So I'm asking. One keeper to another. Will you carry it to the gate?"

The valley hummed around us. The bees rose, all together, a slow spiral above the river, gold-brown in the first true light. My chest burned with a fire I had spent ten years learning to pretend wasn't there, and I thought about the ledger with his name in it, and the drowned corridor, and Sera's face when she broke her oath for him in front of the entire council, and I thought about Maren, who had hidden me not out of duty but out of the same fierce, undeserved love she was now handing me like a lantern.

"Yes," I said. "I'll carry it."

"Good," she whispered. "Good. Then the last thing, and then you can fuss over me all you like."

She closed her eyes.

"The Veil dies with me. When it goes, the world comes flooding in—roads, maps, memory, everything this valley swallowed for sixty years. Including everyone who ever looked for us." Her hand went slack in mine, but she wasn't done; I could see her fighting for each word like a swimmer fighting current. "There was a rider on the ridgeline last autumn. You saw him. You thought he forgot himself, the way travelers do."

"He did forget. He rode back down."

"No, love. He rode back down *knowing.* The Veil was already thinning—it's been thinning since the night your name appeared in the ledger. He carried my location out of this valley in his teeth, and Morrel has had all winter to chew on it." Her eyes opened one last time, and they were clear, and they were afraid, and it was the fear that frightened me, because I had never once seen Maren afraid. "The army isn't coming to Corren first. Corren was the feint. They're coming *here.* For the gate. For you. And they'll be—"

She never said how long they'd be.

The breath went out of her between one word and the next, quietly, the way she did everything—without fuss, without ceremony, as if dying were simply another chore on the list, right after feeding the bees. The fire sank to embers. The bees descended, all together, and settled over her like a second blanket, and the valley's hum changed key, thinned, and began—note by note—to fall silent.

I sat by the river holding my dead mistress's hand while the sun came up, and I felt the Veil begin to die around me. It went slowly at first, then all at once: a pressure lifting, a silence breaking, and beyond the treeline, for the first time in sixty years, I heard the world. Cart wheels on a distant road. A dog barking. Horses—many horses, iron-shod, moving fast, coming down out of the eastern pass with the sound of a landslide learning to march.

Sera was out there somewhere, riding home with salt and news, straight through their picket lines.

I laid Maren's hands on her breast, banked the fire over her per her rules—she'd have haunted me otherwise—and stood up with a fire in my chest that no seal would ever cage again, and started to run.
