## 25. The Magpie's Price Called In

The Magpie came at dusk, the way she did everything—unannounced, sideways, and already inside.

I had left the shutters barred and a hair across the doorframe, an old habit from Maren's cottage, and when I turned around from the hearth she was sitting in my chair with her boots on the table, turning a silver coin over her knuckles like it owed her money.

"Hair was on the frame," she said. "Cute. You want to know how I got in, or would it ruin your week?"

"I'd rather not know."

"Smart boy." She flipped the coin, caught it without looking. "You've been hiding in a smuggler's loft for four days, Odile Coldharbour—or whatever we're calling you this season—and you haven't spent a copper, made a friend, or looked once at the harbor. Do you know what that tells me?"

"That I'm careful."

"That you're waiting." She leaned forward, and firelight caught the rings on her fingers—seven of them, none matching, each one probably stolen off a corpse with better taste than its owner. "And things that wait are things that owe."

She was right. That was the worst of it. Four days since Corren's binding chamber, four days since Sera broke her oath in front of the whole council and I broke faith with every plan I'd ever made, and the Magpie had gotten us out through Corren's underbelly—through sewers that smelled like the inside of the city's regrets—for a price to be named later. *Later* had arrived riding the dusk like everything else.

"Sit down," she said. "Debts are better discussed sitting. Standing debts turn into brawls."

I sat on the edge of the bed because there was nowhere else. The loft was one room above a chandler's shop, all tar-smell and rope-coil, with a window that looked out on nothing but other windows. I had chosen it for exactly that. I was learning that about myself: I chose rooms the way drowning men choose water.

"The price," I said. "Name it."

"Oh, I named it weeks ago. Weren't you listening?" She smiled with about half her mouth. "A memory of your choosing. That's my trade, boy. It's why they call me what they call me. I take the things people can't afford to keep and sell them to people who can't afford to have them. Perfectly legal in three cities, merely fatal in two."

"And which is this one?"

"This one," she said, "is the third kind. The kind where nobody's written the law yet." She produced something from her coat—a sliver of glass, black as the key Aldous had given Sera, but longer, curved, polished until it drank the firelight whole. "Memory takes memory. I give you this route out of a city that wants you bound in iron, and you give me one thing you carry in your head. Your pick. Most people take their worst day. Guilt's heavy, and they travel lighter after."

"What do you do with them?" I had asked her this before, in the sewer, with Sera's hand cold in mine and the dark rushing past. She hadn't answered then either.

"Wouldn't you like to wear my rings," she said. "Here's tonight's menu, since you've been moping too long to think straight. Option one: the Veil. Every hour of it. The valley forgets you, you forget the valley, and no ledger can find you where your own mind hasn't been. Cleanest escape in the trade."

My stomach turned over. Twenty years of beeswax and woodsmoke, Maren's voice calling me in from the hives, the particular silence of a place built to hold nothing. "No."

"Option two: the prophecy. Give me the ledger's contents—all those lovely dates with your name in them—and the book goes blank as far as you're concerned. Morrel's inquisitors could hold it open in front of your face and read you a shopping list. Hale himself could recite your death date and you'd hear poetry. Very popular with fugitives, that one."

"You could take it?" I said. "The knowledge of it? Out of my head entirely?"

"Child, I could take the word *the* out of your head if you paid me enough." Her eyes were quick and flat and old. "So. The valley, or the prophecy. Or—" she turned the glass shard slowly, letting it catch light it shouldn't have been able to catch, "—you name something else and I'll tell you if it's enough."

I thought about Sera. That was inevitable; I thought about Sera the way other people breathed, constantly and without noticing until someone pointed it out. She was downstairs right now buying us bread with a lie about her sister lying sick in Karst Hold, and she had broken a sworn oath of thirty years' architecture to do it, and if the Magpie took her out of my head—the laugh, the argument on the road, the way she said *Odile* like she was testing whether the name would hold weight—

"No," I said aloud, before the thought could finish forming. As if saying it gave it teeth.

The Magpie raised one eyebrow. "I hadn't offered her yet."

"You were going to."

"Of course I was going to. I'm a professional." She set the glass shard down on the table between us, and it sat there like a knife at a peace talks. "But look at you, protecting her from me. Here's what you don't understand about my trade, ember-keeper. Everything you're hoarding in that head—every memory you won't pay with—is collateral I can't collect. And you are currently the single most valuable fugitive on the continent, sitting in my loft, with my route in your feet and my debt in your mouth. If Morrel's men pull your fingernails out tomorrow, all that treasure burns for nothing."

"So this is charity."

"This is inventory management." She stood, finally taking her boots off my table, and crossed to the window. Dusk had gone blue outside. Somewhere below, the chandler's boy was singing something tuneless about the sea. "But sure. Charity. My grandmother drowned in Ashvael when the tide came in and stayed, you know. Whole family on the salt side of the wall. When I heard there was a door that opened on the drowned kingdom, I didn't think *gold*. I thought maybe somebody down there kept ledgers too. Maybe somebody remembers my grandmother's name." She shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "Point being, even magpies roost somewhere."

For a moment neither of us spoke, and the singing went on below, wrong in all the right places.

"You want a memory of Ashvael," I said slowly.

"I want *paid*," she said. "Ashvael would be a bonus."

I should explain what I had been doing for four days, besides waiting.

Every night I took out the letter I was writing and didn't send—to Sera, though she slept one floor below me—and added a line and crossed out two, and every night the same problem sat in the middle of the page like a stone in a shoe: I could not remember my mother's face.

I remembered that she existed. I remembered facts, the way you remember facts about a country you've never visited. Her name was Ilsabet. She smelled of river water and some flower whose name Maren refused to tell me. She sang, badly, on purpose, because she thought it was funny. She carried me through a treeline that killed spells, when I was small enough to carry, and handed me to a hedge-witch who sealed my name into the stones of a valley, and she walked back out of the Veil alone to be found by Ashvael's loyalists within the year.

That was the inheritance. Not the throne—the throne was a rumor, a gold leaf glowing in a map room. This was the inheritance: I was alive because a woman I could not picture had done the bravest thing anyone ever did for me, and I could not even pay her the interest of remembering her face.

Maren said once—I was twelve, I'd asked, which was against the rules—that grief was a room you furnished yourself. I had furnished mine with an empty chair.

Four days of writing letters to a woman about a mother I couldn't see. And now a thief sat in my loft offering to unburden me of anything I chose.

"Show me how it works," I said.

The Magpie turned from the window. For the first time since she'd arrived, something moved behind her eyes that wasn't arithmetic.

"Careful," she said softly. "That's a merchant's question. Merchants get merchant answers."

"How does it work."

She picked up the glass shard and held it flat, like a mirror, and beckoned me close. In its surface I saw—not my reflection. A corridor. Doors, dozens of them, standing open onto lit rooms, and in each room something glimmering moved, like fish under ice.

"Every memory has a door," she said. "This finds the door. You put your hand here, you think hard about what you're selling, and you push. It hurts about as much as pulling a tooth, and afterward there's just—a gap. Smooth. You'll know something's gone the way you know a tooth's gone, by the tongue finding the hole. People hate that at first. Then they love it. Then they come back and sell me more." She lowered the shard. "So. The valley? The prophecy? Name it, and let's both sleep rich."

And I thought about what mattered.

Not what hurt most. Not what was heaviest. The Magpie had assumed I'd think like her customers—think like a man carrying too much—and for four days I had been exactly that man. But sitting there with the black glass between us, I understood something with the plainness of a bell struck once:

If I sold the Veil, I lost the only home I'd ever had. If I sold the prophecy, I lost the only map of what was coming. But those weren't the real prices. The real price was this: everything I knew about who I was had been built in that valley, and everything I might become was written in that book, and if I emptied myself of either to run faster, I would arrive wherever I was going as nobody, which was the one thing Morrel's binders couldn't do to me worse than I'd have done it to myself.

Sera had taught me that, actually. On the road, in chapter after chapter of arguing. *They can bind your magic,* she'd said once, furiously, somewhere past the marches, *but only you can volunteer for the smaller cage.*

"The valley stays," I said. "The prophecy stays."

The Magpie sighed like a woman watching coins roll into a drain. "Then we're at an impasse, and I start considering whether Morrel pays better than—"

"I'll pay with something else."

"There is no something else. There's the inventory in your head or there's—"

"My mother's face."

The sentence came out steadier than anything I'd said in days, which was strange, because it wasn't quite true yet—it was a payment proposed on goods I couldn't produce. I pressed on anyway, hands sweating, voice level.

"You want Ashvael. You said it yourself—you want to know whether somebody down there remembers your grandmother. Well. My mother was born Ilsabet Vael, last trueborn daughter of the royal line before me, and she lived in Ashvael for nineteen years before it drowned. She walked its streets. She knew its names." I swallowed. "I don't have her face. I never did. But if your glass can find doors, then it can find the door to what I *do* have—and what I have is this: the shape of a promise I made when I was too young to know I was making it. I promised myself I'd remember her. I've carried that promise twenty years and never once been able to keep it, because there's nothing under it. Take the promise. Take the guilt of failing it. Take the empty chair, Magpie—that's the heaviest thing I own, and I'll tell you truly, it's worth more than any hour of the Veil, because the Veil I can rebuild and the chair I cannot."

Silence. Below us the boy had stopped singing.

The Magpie looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her do sums behind her eyes—real ones, not the performance. Then she laughed, short and unwilling, like a cough.

"You're offering to sell me your *grief about not grieving.*"

"I'm offering you the only thing I have that touches Ashvael honestly. Everything else I have of it is gold leaf and rumors, and you can steal those anywhere."

"It's worthless," she said. "It's the memory of nothing. No buyer on the continent would give me a bent nail for it."

"Yes," I said. "So you'll keep it. Because it's the one thing in your collection nobody else will ever have—a piece of the last heir of Ashvael that isn't his face or his blood or his throne. Just his hollow. Just the exact shape of what losing a kingdom costs a boy who never saw it." I leaned forward. "You said even magpies roost somewhere. Roost here. Nobody else will ever know what this cost me, and you will, forever, and that's rarer than any soldier's worst day."

Another silence, longer. Outside, full dark now, and the harbor bells ringing the second watch.

"You're a strange one," the Magpie said at last, quietly. "Hale says the same thing about you, by the way. He mentioned it in a report I read before I burned it." She let that land—let *Hale* land, let *report* land—and watched me go still. "Later. One debt at a time." She turned the glass shard over in her fingers, and her thumb traced its curve like a rosary. "The promise-memory. It's thin stock, ember-boy. Thin stock, high risk, no resale. Normally I'd laugh you out of my loft."

"But."

"But." She set the shard down between us, handle toward me—which I realized only then was the first honest gesture she'd made, because the thing had no handle, and she was giving me the part that didn't exist. "My grandmother's name was Odile."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly. "What?"

"Common name in the salt districts, before the tide. Half the women in the low quarter were Odiles; that's why I never use mine." She was not looking at me. She was looking at the shard. "When I read the ledger's margin notes in your lady friend's handwriting—the night we ran—I thought it was a joke the world was playing. The drowned kingdom sends up its king with a washerwoman's name. Fine. Fine. Sell me the promise. We'll call it fair, and I'll call it a favor, and neither of us will say the word *grandmother* again, because I've survived forty years by not owing anybody anything, and I am not starting with a dead queen and a beekeeper's boy."

She pushed the shard across the table.

I put my hand on it. It was warm, warmer than glass should be, and faintly it hummed, or my blood did, or the boundary between them was thinner than I'd believed. I thought about the promise. I thought about the empty chair, and the unfurnished room, and twenty years of reaching for a face and closing my hand on river water. I thought: *this is what matters, that it mattered.*

"Push," the Magpie said.

I pushed.

It did hurt, about as much as a tooth. Less than I deserved. And then there was a smooth place in me where something used to sit, and I ran my mind over it the way she'd said I would, tongue against the gap, and I started to laugh—weakly, stupidly, tears running down my face at nothing—because the hole itself felt lighter. Cleaner. The chair was gone from the room. Not filled. Gone. And the room, for the first time in my life, had space in it for something new.

"Well," said the Magpie, sliding the shard away into her coat, and her voice had a rasp in it I hadn't heard before. "Pleasure doing business. Route's good for six days; the chandler owes me and his boats are slow but they float. After that, you're on your own, and so am I." She paused at the ladder down, one ringed hand on the rail. "Ember-boy. When you get wherever you're going—and you will get there, I've seen enough ledgers to bet on it—there's a woman downstairs who lied to her council for you. Buy her something. Memories are cheap. Bread's expensive."

Then she was gone, down into the lamplight and tar-smell, taking my hollow with her.

I sat a long time in the dark. Footsteps on the stair eventually—Sera's, I knew them by rhythm now, quick and decisive—and the door opened on her arms full of bread and her face full of questions, because she could read me too well and I was still crying.

"Odile? What happened? Did she—what did you give her?"

"Nothing I needed," I said, and found it was true, and found also that truth could be a kind of grief, and that both could live in one chest at the same time without crowding. I wiped my face. "Sera. The Magpie said something before she left. About Hale. She's seen his reports—recent ones. Sera, I think he's feeding someone intelligence on Morrel's camp."

Sera set the bread down slowly, and in the dark her eyes were very bright.

"Then we need to talk," she said, "about where we're going next—because I know exactly who he's feeding."
