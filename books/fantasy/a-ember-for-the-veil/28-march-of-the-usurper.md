## 28. March of the Usurper

The road to Corren was black with soldiers by the third morning.

Dain Hale stood on the ridge above the river crossing and watched them come—columns of Morrel's host unspooling along the floodplain like ink poured into water, ten thousand men and the supply trains that fed them, the baggage carts groaning under siege gear that had no honest purpose this far north. Corren had walls older than the usurper's grandfather. You did not bring rams to a negotiation.

You brought them when you had already decided what the answer would be.

"General." His aide, Vessik, a young man with a clerk's posture and a soldier's eyes, reined up beside him. "Dispatch from the vanguard. They've reached the Ash ford. The bridge is burned."

"Of course it is." Corren's council was slow and quarrelsome and vain, but they were not stupid. "How long to rebuild?"

"Two days with the engineers. A day if we strip timber from the villages."

"Then we take two days," Hale said. "I want the men rested before the walls. Tired soldiers throw torches carelessly."

Vessik blinked. "Torches, sir?"

Hale looked at him for a long moment. Vessik was twenty-four, competent, and had joined the host because Morrel paid in land instead of glory, which made him either a cynic or an optimist depending on the year. He had not yet been told what the march was actually for. Hale wondered, not for the first time, how many of the ten thousand below knew.

"The council will offer terms," Hale said. "Surrender of the fugitives in exchange for the army passing. That is the arrangement."

"Yes, General. And if they surrender them?"

Hale turned his horse down toward the camp. "Then we pass."

He did not say the rest, because the rest was written in a sealed order he carried against his ribs, and because saying it aloud would make Vessik complicit, and there was no reason to spend another man's soul before it was necessary.

The order said: *Take delivery of the ember. Then burn Corren.*

*Leave nothing that could shelter the free cities' council. Leave nothing at all.*

* * *

Morrel's court traveled with the army the way a tumor travels in a body—at the center, growing.

The usurper had taken over a wool merchant's house in the last town before the marches, and by the time Hale arrived it had been transformed: guards at every door, maps nailed over the wallpaper, clerks scratching in every corner like mice in a granary. Morrel himself sat at the head of the long table in what had been someone's dining room, eating from someone's plates, wearing the grey-and-gold that his tailors called the colors of the restored realm.

"Hale." Morrel did not look up from the map. "Tell me about Corren's council."

"They'll bargain. They always bargain. Imre of the salt delegation has been sending feelers since winter."

"And the ember?"

"Gone to ground. My riders found the shepherd's bothy empty. Cold hearth, two bedrolls, boot prints going south toward the high passes." He paused. "They were warned somehow. Or they're simply better than we are."

"They're children," Morrel said. "A runaway apprentice and an archivist who forgot her oaths. Children run well, but they run without a destination. Find where they're going and you find them waiting there." He finally looked up, and his eyes were bright and patient and entirely without warmth. "You believe they'll go to the drowned gate."

"I believe the girl's father spent forty years studying the prophecy. I believe whatever he knew, she carries now."

"Good." Morrel rolled up the map with a snap. "Because here is what I want understood, Hale, so that there are no misunderstandings later. The gate opens or everyone involved in its failing dies. That includes the council of Corren, whose continued existence I have tolerated only because they were useful for finding the boy. Once the ember is in hand—" he smiled, small and satisfied, "—their usefulness concludes."

Hale kept his face still. He had been keeping his face still for eleven years, since Ashvael drowned and he learned that grief, publicly displayed, was read as weakness.

"You mean to honor the terms," he said. It was not quite a question.

"I mean to win," Morrel said pleasantly. "Terms are what winning looks like afterward. Write whatever history you like." He waved a hand, dismissing him. "See to the ford. We move on Corren within the week."

Outside, the evening was cold and clean and smelled of woodsmoke from other people's homes. Hale stood in the merchant's yard a while, breathing it in, and thought about a city of thirty thousand people, most of whom had never heard of Odile Coldharbour, and what it meant that their survival now depended on whether one exhausted general chose to obey an order he had already decided to break.

It occurred to him that he had been choosing sides for longer than he'd admitted. He had simply been very careful about noticing.

* * *

The first intelligence he sent was almost accidental.

There was a woman in his supply corps named Brannoc—a quartermaster's clerk, sharp-eyed, formerly of the river towns, with cousins in Corren she wrote to through channels that were technically smuggling and practically tradition. Hale had known about her letters for a year and let them continue, because a quartermaster who feared discovery was a loyal quartermaster, and because the letters were harmless: prices, shortages, gossip.

On the second night after Morrel's order, Hale walked through the supply encampment in the dark, past the wagon lines, and stopped at Brannoc's tent.

"General." She came to her feet, startled, a ledger clutched to her chest like a shield.

"At ease. I need a letter carried tonight. Not your usual channel—your usual channel is being watched as of tomorrow. There's a tanner outside the east wall of Corren, name of Osk, who owes me money. He can get paper inside."

Brannoc's face did something complicated. "What kind of letter?"

"One you don't read." He held it out. "And one you never mention. If anyone asks why you left camp, you were drunk and lost, and I fined you a month's pay. I'll dock it myself so the record's true."

She took the letter without reading it, which told him everything he needed to know about her judgment, and said only, "The tanner. Osk. East wall."

"Thank you, Clerk."

That night, in his own tent, Hale sat with his campaign maps and marked, in pencil, in a cipher only he maintained, everything the letter contained: the route of the vanguard. The timing of the ford's repair. The sealed order's existence—not its contents, not yet; a man could deny a rumor but not a confession—and the fact that terms offered to Corren would be terms betrayed.

He addressed it to no one. He described the drop point: a hollow oak on the pilgrim path three miles south of the city, a place a fugitive pair moving toward the mountains might plausibly pass. He did not know if they had ears in Corren. He did not know if they were even alive. He knew only that if he was going to commit treason, he would do it properly, which meant doing it in the dark, in pencil, addressed to nobody, so that for one more day he could pretend he hadn't.

He sealed the packet in oilskin and buried it at the oak himself, riding out alone with an escort dismissed to a fictitious scouting errand, and pressed the earth flat with his palm like a man laying flowers.

* * *

The ford was rebuilt in a day and a half. The engineers stripped timber from two villages, and the villagers stood in the cold watching their barns come apart plank by plank, and no one gave them anything for it, and the soldiers didn't meet their eyes. Hale rode the line and made himself look. That was the discipline he had set for himself years ago: look at everything. If he was going to serve a thing, he would look at it whole, so that when the day came to stop serving it, he would know exactly what he was stopping.

Vessik caught up with him at the far bank. "Scouts report movement in the high country. Small party, southbound, fast. Could be smugglers."

"Or our fugitives."

"Could be, sir. Orders?"

Hale looked south, toward the passes, toward the drowned marches where a kingdom slept under green water and a boy who wanted nothing was walking toward the one door in the world that wanted him. Ten thousand men behind him. One sealed order against his ribs.

"Let them run," he said. "We're not hunting rabbits anymore. We're driving deer—and I want them driven exactly where they're already going. Note the sighting, log it as uncertain, and tell the outriders to hold at the treeline. No engagement without my word."

Vessik hesitated. "The Lord Protector wants the ember found, General."

"The Lord Protector wants the ember delivered," Hale said, "and it will be. Delivered frightened makes it wild. Delivered calm makes it useful. We do this my way or we do it badly." He put enough iron in it that the young man saluted and dropped back, and Hale rode on hating himself a little for the fluency of the lie, and more for the fact that it wasn't entirely a lie—he did intend to deliver the boy somewhere. Just not to Morrel.

At the next ridge he stopped, dismounted, and pretended to study the ground while Vessik ranged ahead. In the lee of the saddle he took out a second letter, shorter than the first, and wrote it fast in a fair hand:

*To the archivist's daughter, if this reaches her:*

*Your father's archive burned on my watch and I have regretted it since, though regret is cheap. Here is what isn't. Morrel's order is to burn Corren after the exchange regardless of terms. The vanguard crosses at the Ash ford in two days; the main body follows within the week. His engineers carry fire-pots sufficient for a city, which should tell you everything about the negotiation you were promised. The council must evacuate the lower quarters or refuse the parley outright—either is survivable; trusting him is not.*

*I was born in Ashvael. I watched it drown and I served the man who profited by it, and I told myself service was the same as loyalty. It is not. I am done telling myself things.*

*The boy should know: the gate does not open from the outside. Whatever the prophecy says, whatever Morrel believes, the door answers to blood and will, not to armies. An army at the gate is just an audience.*

*Burn this.*

*—D.H.*

He sealed it, buried it beside the first, and rode back down to his army, his face arranged into its eleven-year stillness, his heart hammering like a traitor's, which was precisely what it was.

* * *

Three days later, at the edge of the burned marches, a rider found the hollow oak.

Not the rider Hale expected. The Magpie's people worked the passes, and the smuggler's network was the only post that ran through contested country without asking questions—but Hale had gambled on the fugitives having bought passage once already, back in Corren, and gambling was all this was. He crouched at the oak in the pre-dawn grey, dug up both packets, and found the first gone. Taken. Someone had come, someone had read, someone had chosen to leave the second in place—which meant the message was accepted, which meant it was working, which meant that somewhere ahead of his army, two exhausted young people now knew that the parley was a pyre and the negotiator was an arsonist.

Or it meant a trap. Morrel had spies everywhere, and some of them were patient, and some of them were cleverer than Brannoc.

Hale stood in the cold with the second packet in his hands—the one addressed to nobody, the confession in pencil—and considered, one final time, putting it back in the earth and riding away and being, for another week, merely the man who followed orders.

Instead he broke the seal, added a line in the margin of his own letter, and reburied it:

*If you doubt me, ask yourself who ordered the archive spared its eastern wing. Ask who pulled his riders off the high passes. I cannot prove anything to you. Proof is for courts. This is a war, and wars run on trust extended across enemy lines by people with no better option. Extend it or don't. But decide quickly. We reach the Ash ford tomorrow.*

He mounted and rode back toward the fires of his own camp, ten thousand strong, and felt lighter than he had in eleven years, which terrified him more than the gallows did.

* * *

Morrel summoned him at dusk.

The usurper stood at the window of the commandeered house, looking north toward the distant smudge of Corren's walls, and did not turn when Hale entered. "My engineers ask about fire-pots," Morrel said. "They want to know how much to allocate for the assault. I told them to prepare for a full burning. They asked me—politely—whether that was consistent with accepting the city's surrender." Now he turned, and his smile was the pleasant one, the patient one. "What did you tell them, Hale?"

"That allocation is their business and strategy is mine."

"Mm." Morrel crossed the room slowly, hands clasped behind his back, a man taking a stroll through property he intended to acquire. "You've been odd lately. Careful. Quiet in councils where you used to argue. My spies say you dismissed an escort twice this month. My spies say a quartermaster's clerk left camp drunk one night and was fined for it—an entry in your own hand, which is unlike you; you delegate paperwork like a man allergic to it." He stopped an arm's length away. "So. Before I decide whether these are the habits of a tired old soldier or the habits of a traitor, you may explain them to me."

Every instinct Hale owned screamed to deny, deflect, laugh it off. Eleven years of stillness offered him the script. Instead he heard himself say, calmly, "Corren surrenders the fugitives, and we burn thirty thousand people anyway. That is the order. Yes?"

Morrel's eyes narrowed. "The order serves the realm."

"Ashvael was the realm," Hale said quietly. "Ashvael drowned. Everything since is just a man collecting rent on a grave." He let the silence sit. "I'll see the ember delivered, my lord. On that you have my word. What happens to Corren after—I'll decide when I'm standing at its walls, with my hand on the torch, looking at the people inside. As any commander should."

For a long moment Morrel studied him, and Hale understood that the usurper was weighing something, and that whatever he decided, Hale had just stepped off a ledge and could not climb back.

"Sentiment," Morrel said at last, softly. "It rots officers. I've had to prune it before." He turned back to the window. "Go to Corren. Take the parley. Get the boy. And Hale—if the city burns and your conscience burns with it, remember you were given the chance to object, and objected in exactly the way a traitor would."

Hale saluted, turned, and walked out into the dark.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. Inside, where the fear lived, there was also something else, unfamiliar and clean, like the first cold breath after a fever breaks: he had shown Morrel his cards—or enough of them—and the game was now simple. Two moves remained. Deliver the fugitives somewhere safe, and keep ten thousand men from lighting a match.

He went to find Brannoc, and to write the letter that would burn his bridges all the way down.

*The vanguard moves at dawn,* it began. *Trust no parley. And when you reach the gate, boy—whatever you intend to sacrifice there, make sure it costs Morrel more than it costs you.*
