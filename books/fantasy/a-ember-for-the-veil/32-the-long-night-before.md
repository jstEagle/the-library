## 32. The Long Night Before

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Odile finally picked up the pen.

He'd been sitting with it for an hour, the way you sit with a knife you mean to use on yourself. The paper lay blank in front of him, and the blankness was a kind of mercy, and he hated it.

*Dear Sera.*

No. Too much like a letter between people who would see each other again.

*Sera.*

Better. Colder. A letter written by a man who had already left.

He wrote: *If you're reading this, then I've done a stupid thing, and you're allowed to be angry about it for exactly as long as it takes you to burn this page.*

His hand shook. He set the pen down and pressed his palm flat against the table until the shaking stopped. Outside, the wind came down off the high passes and worried at the shutters of the shepherd's bothy where they'd spent the winter — except Sera wasn't here anymore. Sera was in Morrel's camp, three valleys east, and every hour he sat here writing was an hour they might be doing anything to her.

*That's how they get you,* Maren used to say. *Not with spells. With arithmetic.*

So he made himself do the arithmetic. One apprentice hedge-witch, half-trained, whose magic erupted once by accident and leveled a river ford. Against an army of ten thousand and a usurper who wanted Ashvael's door opened more than he wanted his next breath. There was no version of that sum where fighting won. There was only the version where he walked into the camp at Grey Ford at dawn, said *I'm the ember, take me*, and bought her life with his surrender.

Hale would honor the trade. He believed — Odile was almost sure he believed — that returning the ember to Ashvael was a holy act, a restoration, the one thing that could make right everything Morrel had broken. A man like that kept his bargains. It was the only currency he had left.

And if Hale didn't keep it — well. Then Odile would be in the camp anyway, close enough to try something stupid, and stupid was the one thing he'd always been good at.

He took up the pen again.

*I want you to know it isn't a sacrifice. Everyone will call it that — the council, the ballad-makers, whoever survives to write this down. They'll say the heir of Ashvael gave himself for love of the free cities, and it'll sound clean, like something out of Aldous's ledger. It wasn't clean. I did it because I couldn't stand the world without you in it, and because I've finally understood that the throne was never mine to refuse or to take. It belongs to a drowned kingdom full of ghosts who chose the water over their own kings. Let them have their choice. Let me have mine.*

*You held the door for me at the ford. You lied at the ferry. You broke your oath in front of the whole council with your voice steady as a bell. Everything brave I have ever done, I learned by watching you do braver things first.*

*Don't come after me. That's not a request, it's the price. If you come after me, the trade means nothing, and they'll kill us both and open the gate anyway over our bodies. Stay away. Be angry. Live a long time. Learn to hate me a little; it helps.*

*The bees on the south slope of the Veil will swarm in late spring. Someone should tell them.*

*Yours — not in any way the law recognizes, but yours —*

*Odile*

He read it twice. His eyes blurred on the last lines and he let them. Then he folded the page into quarters, sealed it with a drip of candle wax and the flat of his thumb, and wrote *Sera* on the outside in letters small enough that only she would bother to read them.

Where to leave it? Not with Imre — Imre was back in Corren, arguing in council chambers, doing the slow honorable work of trying to stop the cities from selling him out. Not with the Magpie, who sold memories and would sell a sealed letter's contents within the hour for the right price.

He settled on the hollow under the hearthstone, where Maren had once hidden her grimoire from inquisitors, in another life, in another valley. He pried the stone up, laid the letter flat beneath it, and pressed the stone home with both hands, the way you press earth over a seed you aren't sure will sprout.

Then he sat back down and looked at what remained of the night.

It was very long. He had counted on that. He had planned for the long night the way he planned everything now — in hours and distances and the weight of things carried. Boots by the door. The ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. No fire lit, because a man walking to his death shouldn't waste warmth on comfort.

But the night was longer than he'd planned for, and somewhere past the middle of it, sitting alone in the dark with his own heartbeat for company, Odile discovered the thing he should have known all along: this was the loneliest hour of his life, and there was no spell for it, and no one coming.

That was when someone knocked.

Three raps, spaced evenly, deliberate. Not the Magpie's knock — hers was a quick patter, four beats, like rain starting. Not a soldier's knock either; soldiers kicked.

Odile rose without a sound and took up the iron poker from beside the dead hearth. He stood to the hinge side of the door, the way Maren had taught him when he was twelve and still afraid of wolves. "Who's there?"

"Open the door, Coldharbour." The voice was low, roughened, familiar in the way a scar is familiar. "Or don't, and I'll freeze on your step, and you can explain to Sera why you let her only ally die of cold."

Hale.

Odile's grip tightened on the poker. Every sensible part of him screamed to stay silent, to let the man freeze, to wait for dawn and walk to Grey Ford as planned with no complications. But Hale had said *Sera*, and hope is a stupid animal that answers to its name.

He lifted the bar and opened the door a hand's width.

Inquisitor Hale stood on the step with snow to his knees and frost in his grey beard, wearing no livery, no colors, nothing but a plain traveler's cloak going white at the shoulders. He looked older than he had at Grey Ford. He looked like a man who had been walking a long time and had stopped pretending otherwise. Under one arm he carried a leather map-case, and his hands — Odile noticed this with a strange, detached clarity — were shaking, though whether from cold or something else he couldn't say.

"You're supposed to be in Morrel's camp," Odile said.

"I was." Hale's breath smoked. "May I come in, or must we conduct treason on your doorstep?"

Odile stepped back. It was, he thought, either the wisest or the most foolish thing he'd ever done, and the ledger would probably know which.

* * *

Hale sat by the unlit hearth and refused the blanket Odile offered, and refused the tea, and finally accepted the tea after all, holding the cup in both hands without drinking, as if the warmth itself were enough. Odile kept the poker leaning against the wall within reach. Neither of them pretended otherwise.

"The trade stands," Hale said first. "Dawn, Grey Ford, you for the girl. I gave my word and I'll keep it. What I'm about to tell you doesn't change that. It changes everything else."

"Then tell me."

Hale set the cup down. From the map-case he drew a roll of vellum and spread it on the table, weighting the corners with the salt dish and the candle stub. Odile leaned in. By the weak light he saw the camp at Grey Ford rendered in a fine military hand — picket lines, sentry posts, the officers' tents, the horse lines, the frozen river, and at the center, marked with a small careful circle, a tent labeled in Hale's own writing: *the prisoner*.

"I've fed the council intelligence all winter," Hale said. "Corren knows Morrel's supply lines better than Morrel does. None of it matters now, because Morrel marches in six days regardless, and the orders I received last week—" He stopped. His jaw worked. When he continued, his voice was flatter, stripped down to load-bearing timber. "The orders are to burn Corren. After the exchange. After you're delivered. Whether the free cities kneel or not. Especially if they kneel."

Odile felt cold move through him that had nothing to do with the open door. "He wants the gate. Once he has me—"

"He doesn't want the gate." Hale laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "Boy, I've served that man for nine years. I thought he wanted what I wanted — Ashvael raised from the water, the old blood restored, our homeland given back. I told myself every atrocity was a road to that. And then last month I stood in his war tent and heard him give the true order." He turned the map slightly, so the drowned kingdom's coastline faced Odile. "He doesn't want Ashvael restored. He wants Ashvael *emptied*. The magic under that water — the wild plague-magic your ancestors drowned their own kingdom to contain — he wants it for himself. A gate that opens onto a living kingdom makes him a liberator. A gate that opens onto raw power makes him a god."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"So the trade," Odile said slowly, "delivers me to a man who doesn't want to restore anything. He wants to crack the gate open and hold it open while he drinks."

"Yes."

"And Sera?"

"Alive. Unharmed, as far as I could see. She's worth more to him breathing — she's the archivist, the prophecy-keeper's daughter, the one person besides you who can read the rite. He needs her until the gate opens." Hale's eyes came up, and for the first time Odile saw what was underneath the inquisitor: not a patriot, not a hunter, just an old man standing in the wreckage of the story he'd told himself for nine years. "After the gate opens, boy, no one is worth anything to him. Not her. Not me. Not you."

Odile looked at the map. At the little circle around the prisoner's tent. At the river, frozen solid, running past the camp like a road made of glass.

"You came here to confess," he said. "Why? What do you want from me?"

"I want what I've wanted since I was a nobleman's son in a city that's now forty feet underwater." Hale's voice cracked and he let it. "I want Ashvael to stay drowned. I've finally understood that's the only mercy left for it. My homeland is a grave, Coldharbour, and graves should be left in peace." He drew a second paper from the case — smaller, folded, soft at the creases from handling. "These are the watch rotations. The gap in the east picket where the ice is thickest. The hour the general changes guard, which is the only hour the prisoner's tent stands unwatched. I can't free her myself. They know my face too well, and if I'm caught near her tent before the exchange, Morrel moves her somewhere I'll never find, and everything ends."

"So you need me."

"I need someone mad enough to go in tonight instead of dawn." Hale met his eyes. "I know what you planned. I watched you at Grey Ford; I've watched men prepare to die my whole career, and you had the look. Walk in at dawn and you're his. Walk in tonight, take her out through the east ice, and maybe — *maybe* — nobody has to be traded at all."

Odile stood very still. The plan he'd built all night — the clean arithmetic, the letter under the hearthstone, the terrible simplicity of *I'm the ember, take me* — sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. And Hale was offering him a different shape entirely: not surrender, but theft. Steal her back. Risk everything on ice and darkness and a traitor's maps.

"If we're caught," Odile said, "she dies tonight instead of in six days."

"If you don't go, everyone dies in six days, and Corren burns first." Hale began rolling the map, precise, unhurried, a man packing up his whole life. "I won't pretend it's a good choice. I stopped believing in good choices around the time I burned my first archive. But it's the only one left where anyone wakes up in spring."

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it Odile thought he heard the far-off groan of the river ice at Grey Ford, three valleys east, shifting its weight in the dark like something turning over in its sleep.

He looked at the hearthstone, where the letter lay folded and sealed. *Don't come after me.* Well. He wasn't coming after himself. He was going ahead.

"Hale," he said. "When we burn Morrel's camp tonight — and I'm saying *when*, because I've decided — what happens to you?"

The old inquisitor pulled his cloak tight and almost smiled, and it was the saddest thing Odile had seen all winter.

"Tonight," Hale said, "I finally find out which side of the fire I've been standing on."

He unrolled the map again, and together, in the last dark hour before dawn, two men who should have been enemies bent over the plans of an army and began, quietly, to plot its ruin.
