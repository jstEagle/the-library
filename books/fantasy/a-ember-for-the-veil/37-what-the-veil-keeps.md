## 37. What the Veil Keeps

We came back to the valley in the last week of thaw, when the snow on the high passes went rotten and the rivers ran the color of old milk. Sera rode ahead of me the whole way down, and every mile she grew quieter, the way people do when they're approaching something they loved and expect to find changed.

She wasn't wrong to expect it. The Veil was dying. I could feel it the way you feel a tooth you've been ignoring finally letting go—loose, aching, inevitable. For eleven years that valley had been a held breath. Maren had sealed it, stitched it shut with her own name and her own years, a pocket of the world where magic couldn't follow and couldn't be born. She'd built it to hide a boy. The boy was done hiding. The breath was ready to be released.

I felt it let go on a Tuesday.

We were still two days out, camped under a leaning pine with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth between us, when the air changed. There's no other way to say it. The air changed. It went from the flat, close stillness of the Veil—the stillness I'd lived inside so long I'd stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat—to something with texture in it. Something with currents.

Sera sat up in her bedroll. "What was that?"

"That," I said, "was the valley forgetting to be a valley."

Because that was the truth of it, and I'd had time on the road to understand what Maren had never told me plainly: the Veil wasn't a wall. It was an amnesia. Travelers crossed the treeline and forgot why they'd come. Letters posted out of it arrived with the sender's name smudged. Maps, when anyone bothered to draw them, left a blank space with a cartographer's shrug for a label. The valley didn't keep people out. It kept *memory* out, and it kept us—me, Maren, everyone who lived under its eaves—out of the world's memory too.

Now the amnesia was lifting, and the world was going to remember us all at once.

* * *

We came down into the valley proper on a morning of hard blue light, and I'll be honest: I braced for ruin. I'd seen what happened when magic met the places that had been kept from it. I'd seen Grey Ford, still scarred a season later, the river running wrong where my power had torn the water from its bed. I half expected to crest the ridge and find the valley burned, or drowned, or simply *gone*—unraveled like a knot cut loose.

Instead the bees were flying.

Maren's hives stood where they'd always stood, in their crooked row behind the cottage, and the bees were out in the thaw-warm air doing the ordinary arithmetic of spring, and I sat on my horse in the middle of the track and laughed until Sera looked at me like I'd gone soft in the head.

"They're just bees," she said.

"They were always just bees." I wiped my eyes. "That's the whole point. Everything here was always just itself. That's what she bought for me. Not safety. *Ordinariness.* And I threw it away the first chance I got, and now—" I gestured at the valley, at the smoke rising thin and domestic from three chimneys downriver, at the world with its memory coming back like blood into a numb limb. "Now we find out what it costs."

The cottage was as Maren had left it, which is to say: as I had left it. She'd died in the spring of the war, in the house of a midwife downriver, with her hands folded and the seal on my magic broken clean through, and she'd left the cottage to me in a will written in her own cipher, which Sera cracked in an evening because Sera could crack anything written by anyone who wasn't trying to be unreadable. The will said: *Keep the bees. Forgive yourself. The rest is furniture.*

So we kept the bees.

* * *

The world remembered us slowly, at first, and then all at once.

A tinker came through in the second week—a real tinker, with a cart and a mule and no confusion in his eyes about why he'd come—and bought honey and stayed the night and left with his memory intact for the first time in a century of his trade's crossings. He was the first stranger in eleven years to look at me and see me, and I spent the whole of his visit half-hiding behind the smokehouse before I caught myself and made myself stand in the yard and be seen.

He didn't recognize me. That was the strange gift of it. The boy who'd fled the valley, the heir of Ashvael, the ember, the renouncer—I was all of those things in the world's new memory of the war, and this tinker had heard the songs already, songs with my name in the third verse, and he looked straight at me over his mug and said, "You've got good bees. Rare to find hives this calm this far north."

"That's the valley," I said.

"Was," he said, agreeably. "Valley's just a valley now, from what they say down in Corren. Whatever was odd about it's gone out of it."

Gone out of it. I thought about that all night. The Veil hadn't been destroyed. It had been *spent*—every stitch of it, every year of Maren's life poured into the seal, all of it burned through in one long expenditure that ended the night I spoke the renunciation at the gate and the kingdom chose the tide. The valley wasn't warded anymore. It was just a place. High ground, good water, cold winters, and bees that liked the heather on the south slope. The magic that had made it a refuge was gone out of it, and what remained was what had always been underneath: a valley.

I went out at dawn the next morning and stood at the treeline where spells used to die, and I did the thing I'd been afraid to do since we came home. I held out my hand and called a small light into my palm.

It came. Warm, gold, obedient, mine. The same magic that had leveled a ford, that had opened a drowned gate, that had been named in a prophecy a century before my birth. It sat in my hand like a tame bird and did exactly nothing but shine, because I asked it for nothing but shining.

The valley didn't reject it. The valley didn't even notice. The treeline stood where it had always stood, and my light burned at its edge like a candle in a doorway, and nothing happened at all, and I stood there until my arm ached and then I let the light go out, and I walked back to the cottage, and Sera had the porridge on, and that was the whole of it. That was the miracle. Nothing happened.

* * *

The key of black glass I kept in my shirt, against my skin, from the night the archive burned until the morning I buried it.

Aldous had pressed it into Sera's hands in the smoke and the screaming—*the ember must reach the free cities or be extinguished*—and for a year it had been the most important object in the world. It opened the ledger's binding. The ledger that had held my name before I was born, that had recorded my death date and then, in a shepherd's bothy in the snow, rewritten itself in front of my eyes to show me that prophecy was a mirror and not a chain. The ledger that Sera had read to me by firelight, cover to cover, after the kingdom drowned, looking for anything we'd missed. There wasn't much. A few entries about the free cities. A final page, blank, waiting.

The key had no other purpose. It opened one book. But I carried it anyway, the way you carry a dead man's knife—not for use, but because putting it down felt like a second, smaller death.

I buried it in the spring, on a morning of rain, in the row behind the hives where Maren had taught me my first herb-lore and my last lesson. I didn't dig deep. A hand's depth, no more. I wrapped it in the same oilcloth that had carried the ledger through a war, and I laid it in the earth, and I knelt there a while with the rain coming down through my collar.

Sera came out and stood beside me without a word. She'd gotten good at that, over the winter—knowing when my silences wanted company and when they wanted to be left alone. This one, she judged correctly, wanted company.

"People will ask where it is," she said eventually. "Someday. Scholars, maybe. The council's archivists. They'll come asking about the key."

"They'll come asking about a lot of things." I tamped the earth down with my palms. "They can ask the valley."

"The valley doesn't keep secrets anymore."

"No." I looked at the hives, at the bees working the rain-wet heather, at the cottage with its smoke rising straight in the still air. "It doesn't. But it keeps *other* things now. It keeps what a valley keeps. It keeps the seasons and the graves and the people willing to stay. That's enough keeping for one place."

I set a flat stone over the spot. Not a marker anyone else would read—a stone like a hundred others in that row. But I'd know it. And someday, if some archivist with kind eyes and Sera's patience came asking, maybe I'd point them to it. Maybe I wouldn't. A key that opens one book, and the book's last page is blank, and the blank page is the point—the key could rest. We all could.

* * *

Here is what I understood, kneeling in the rain with the earth under my hands: I had spent my whole life thinking home was a seal.

First it was the Veil itself—Maren's amnesia, the treeline where spells died, the smallness I'd mistaken for safety. Then, when that broke, I thought home might be a throne. Ashvael had offered itself to me at the gate, all its ghosts and all its grief, and I had said no, and the kingdom had said *good*, and the tide had taken us both at our word. Then, for a while on the road, I'd thought home was the absence of pursuit—the next valley, the next border, the place where the horses couldn't hear the army behind us.

It was none of those. It had never been any of those.

Home was Sera reading in the doorway with her boots off, her lips moving over the ledger's marginalia even now, out of habit, even though the ledger was done with us. Home was the argument we had every few days about whether the south hive needed splitting, an argument we'd now had eleven times with no resolution and no desire for one. Home was the way she said my name—not *Odile* the way the songs said it, with the weight of Ashvael behind it, but the way she'd said it the first night on the road, furious and frightened, when I was just a runaway she'd sworn to deliver and had chosen not to.

I had magic in me now, unsealed, intact, a river with no banks. I had no throne, no kingdom, no prophecy, no name in any ledger but the ones a parish priest keeps. I was, by every measure that had ever been used on me, *nothing*—no ember, no heir, no keeper, no instrument.

I was a beekeeper in a valley that had forgotten to be magic, and I was loved, and I have never in my life been less nothing than that.

* * *

We planted the row behind the hives with comfrey and valerian that summer, over the stone, over the key, over the whole buried year. Sera took to the bees with the terrifying competence she took to everything—she'd read three books on apiculture by midsummer, all of them wrong in the particulars of our valley, all of them argued with in the margins in her small fierce hand. The bees, I told her, could not read her annotations. She said the bees should aspire to it.

In the evenings we sat on the step and watched the light go down the far ridge, and sometimes travelers passed on the road below—the road was a real road now, remembered, marked on maps, carrying real traffic—and sometimes the travelers waved, and we waved back, two people on a step in a valley that was only a valley.

One evening, late in the summer, Sera put down her book and said, "The last page is still blank."

"I know."

"You've looked?"

"Every morning." I had. The ledger lived on the mantel now, unkeyed, its binding open, and every morning with the porridge I turned to the final page and found it blank as new snow. "It's not waiting for anything. That's the thing I finally understand. It's not a prophecy with the ending torn off. It's just—paper. The book's finished with us."

"And you're sure that doesn't frighten you?"

I thought about it honestly, the way she always made me think about things honestly. "It did. For a long time, the blank page was the scariest thing in it. A prophecy at least tells you what you're for." I turned my hand over, palm up, and let a little light gather there, small as a moth, warm as a hand. The bees droned. The ridge went gold and then violet. The light sat in my palm and asked for nothing and was asked for nothing. "Now I think it's the kindest thing anyone's ever given me. A page with nothing on it. Room to be *for* whatever I choose."

Sera leaned her head on my shoulder. "And what do you choose?"

Below us, the road ran on toward Corren and the free cities and the whole remembered world. Behind us, the hives hummed their one long note, and the stone in the herb row kept the key, and the valley—just a valley, only a valley, finally and forever only a valley—held its people the way valleys do: without magic, without memory, without anything but ground and water and the stubbornness of those who stay.

I looked at my palm, at the small banked light, at the woman against my shoulder, at the blank page waiting on the mantel with nothing to say.

"Today?" I said. "Today I choose the bees."
