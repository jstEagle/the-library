## 39. The Bees in Spring

The valley had changed its mind about being hidden.

That was the only way I could describe it. For as long as I had known it, the Veil had felt like a held breath—a place where spells unraveled at the treeline and travelers forgot why they'd come before they reached the first farmstead. Now the road in was just a road. Carts rumbled along it carrying slate and timber and, twice in our first week back, gawkers who wanted to see where the heir of Ashvael kept his bees.

I did not correct them. It was easier than explaining that I kept bees because bees did not care who my grandmother had been.

"Third load this morning," Sera said, watching the second cart grind past toward what remained of Maren's cottage. "Word travels."

"Word has always traveled. The valley just used to eat it."

She laughed—the low, surprised laugh I had learned to work for—and shouldered her end of the beam we'd hauled up from Corren. We walked the last mile in the kind of companionable silence that takes a war to earn.

The cottage was worse than I remembered. When the Veil dissolved, it seemed the valley had decided to stop pretending all at once, and Maren's house had been holding itself together out of habit and hedge-craft for thirty years. Now one wall leaned like a drunk, the roof was a suggestion, and the herb garden had gone gloriously feral, as if the plants knew no one was left to scold them.

Sera dropped her end of the beam in the mud and put her hands on her hips.

"You lived here?"

"I hid here. There's a difference."

"There is," she said. She turned in a slow circle, taking in the crooked wall, the collapsed byre, the hives at the treeline—still humming, still tended by no one through a whole winter and somehow alive, because bees are stubborn in ways even magic isn't. "The difference is that hiding looks like this, and living is going to look better."

* * *

We started with the roof, because spring does not wait and neither does rain.

Sera turned out to be good with her hands in a way that shamed me. She was an archivist's daughter; I had expected her to be all ink-stains and careful pages. But she could set a rafter true by eye, tie a knot that would hold a drowning man, and she swung a mallet with an economy that suggested she'd spent more of her childhood hauling than reading. When I asked, she only said, "My father believed you couldn't keep records if you couldn't keep a building standing over them."

Aldous Vane. Her father, dead in the smoke of his own archive, who had pressed a ledger and a key of black glass into her hands and told her the ember must reach the free cities or be extinguished. I caught her sometimes, in the first weeks, looking east toward the passes where Corren sat, her expression doing arithmetic she never shared. Grief is like that. It doesn't finish. You just get better at carrying it while your hands are full.

My hands were very full. There was mortar to mix and a well to clear and a lean-to to raise against the coming winter, and every evening I fell asleep so fast and so deep that I didn't dream, which was its own kind of mercy. The nights I did dream were getting gentler. Less often now the gate, the tide, Morrel's host going under the water with their banners still flying. More often just the valley, green and ordinary, and someone laughing in another room.

On the twelfth day, the first of Maren's old neighbors came up the path—an old woman named Bette with a basket of seed potatoes and no pretense whatsoever.

"Heard the witch's boy came home," she said. "Brought your girl some potatoes. And I'll want comfrey off you come summer, if you're keeping the garden on."

"If I'm—" I stared at her. "Bette, I don't know the first thing about most of these herbs."

"Then you'll learn, won't you." She set the basket down inside the doorframe, which wasn't technically a door yet, and looked around the ruin without flinching. "Maren Coldharbour pulled my son back from a fever the priests gave up on. Never charged me a copper. Valley doesn't forget things anymore, they say. Fine. Then let it remember this." She fixed me with a look like a nail driven flush. "You'll do."

She left before I could answer, which I suspect was deliberate. Sera emerged from behind the leaning wall, where she'd been pretending to inspect the foundation stones, wearing the expression of someone trying not to smile at a funeral.

"You'll do," she repeated.

"Shut up."

* * *

The bees were the thing I feared most, and it took me three weeks to admit it.

I had avoided them all those years ago, after I ran from Maren—avoided the hives, the hum, everything that smelled of her craft. Magic had died at the treeline of the Veil, but the bees lived right at its edge, close enough to the border that I had always half-believed they carried something of the outside in their bodies, something that might recognize me, might call out across the sealed air of the valley to every listening ear in Ashvael: *here, here, the ember is here.*

It had been a child's fear. I was finding, now, how many of my fears were children's fears wearing adult clothes.

I went up to the hives on a bright afternoon in late April, when the orchard behind the ruined cottage had gone white with blossom. The hives stood where they had always stood—five boxes of weathered larch, lichenous, patient. No one had fed them. No one had opened them since Maren died, and yet when I laid my palm against the nearest lid, the sound from within was not the desperate roar of a starving colony but a low, even contentment, like a house where someone is sleeping well.

"They shouldn't have survived," Sera said behind me. I hadn't heard her climb the path; I still sometimes didn't, though she claimed she was deliberately loud. "Tam told me wild colonies overwinter fine, but these are kept stock. Someone should have lost them all."

"Someone did lose them all," I said. "Maren died."

"Then who kept them?"

I didn't have an answer. But when I lifted the lid—smoke first, properly, because Maren had taught me that much before I ever ran—the bees rose around my hands in a golden haze and did not sting. They settled on my sleeves, my knuckles, the backs of my wrists, and their hum climbed briefly into a register I felt in my teeth, and then eased.

They knew me. That was the whole of it. Not as the ember, not as the heir, not as the name in a century-old ledger. They knew my hands from a hundred small kindnesses before I ran, and apparently bee-forgiveness runs longer than mine did.

I stood there until my eyes stung worse than any sting would have, and Sera stood beside me and said nothing at all, which is the finest thing one person can do for another.

We split the hives that summer, five becoming eight. It felt like the right arithmetic. Like something growing instead of something hidden.

* * *

The ledger sat on the mantel for months before either of us touched it.

It had earned its rest, I suppose. That book had carried us across the contested marches, through the drowned archive, to the gate itself—it had recorded my death date in ink older than the page it sat on, and then, in a shepherd's bothy in the snow, it had rewritten itself while I watched, the entry dissolving like sugar in warm water and reforming as something conditional, something unwritten. *The prophecy is a mirror, not a chain.* I had understood that at twenty, bleeding and terrified at a winter window. Understanding it at leisure, in peacetime, was harder. Mirrors show you what you're doing with your face right now. Chains at least tell you where you stand.

One evening in June, with the new roof tight against a soft rain and the fire lit against the damp, Sera brought it down and set it on the table between our supper bowls.

"It's time I did the archivist's duty," she said. "Properly. Not as a keeper of a prisoner's record. As a keeper of a record."

"What's the difference?"

"A keeper guards what the book says. An archivist asks what the book is *for*." She opened it.

The early pages were the ones I knew from nightmares: dates in a hand like frost, entries naming kings and floods, the founding of the Veil, my own arrival recorded decades before my birth. Middle pages full of the marches and the free cities, the council, the binding chamber. Then the last written entry, made the night at the gate, when the kingdom chose to remain drowned and took Morrel's army down with it:

*The door closes by choice. The line ends by choice. What was buried stays buried, and what was carried is laid down.*

And beneath it, blankness—but a strange blankness, a living one. Every archivist knows paper yellows. This page stayed white. When Sera tilted it to the firelight, the blankness shimmered faintly, the way water does when something vast moves beneath it.

"The last page isn't empty," Sera said softly. "It's *unwritten*. There's a difference too. Empty means nothing will come. Unwritten means nothing has come yet." She ran two fingers across it, careful as touching a moth's wing. "My father would have given his remaining teeth to study this. Ink that waits."

"For what?"

"For whoever writes next, I suppose." She looked up at me across the table, firelight in her hair, and smiled the particular smile she saved for observations she considered obvious. "It won't be prophecy anymore. Prophecy needed the door closed and the kingdom drowned and a boy sealed in a valley. All of that's finished. Whatever gets written on this page now will just be... a record. Of things people actually did."

"That's terrifying," I said. "Prophecy at least gives you someone to blame."

"Welcome to freedom. It's terrible. I highly recommend it."

She closed the ledger and left it on the shelf above the fire, spine out, between a manual of beekeeping and Maren's grimoire—which I had finally opened that spring, and found less spellcraft than I expected and more gardening advice, as if the woman who had sealed a prince's name into a valley had spent her stolen centuries mostly worrying about blight.

The key of black glass we did not keep on the shelf. We buried it under the biggest hive, in the dark among the roots, where the hum would keep it company. Some doors should be remembered and none should be opened, and a key buried among ten thousand sisters seemed as safe a place as any in the world.

* * *

Summer came on. The cottage grew walls.

Not quickly—nothing about us was quick, we had both decided, having sprinted across half a continent—but steadily. Sera traded letters with the council in Corren; Imre wrote twice a season now, dry and funny and increasingly frank about how badly the council had handled nearly everything, and how the free cities were arguing about whether Ashvael's waters should be marked as holy ground or fishing rights. Sera wrote back four pages for every one of his, arguing for holy ground with the fury of a woman who had held a door open while ghosts sang beneath her feet. I added postscripts. They got shorter as the years would go on, I suspected. *Leave it drowned. Plant something over it. That's all.*

Hale's name came up less often, but it never quite went away. The chief inquisitor who had burned an archive and besieged a city and died face-down in the mud of a camp he had betrayed, whispering that Ashvael should stay drowned—I thought of him most often at dusk, for no reason I could name except that he had been a man drowning in homesickness, and I knew something about wanting a home so badly it made you dangerous. We had buried him near the ford, under a stone Sera had cut herself. No livery. Just his name, and the word *kept*.

In July, the valley's other returned families began appearing at our door with excuses. A broken plow. A sick goat. A child with a cough. None of it magic—there was precious little craft in me beyond what Maren had beaten into my hands, herbs and hives and the small honest magics of tending—but word had gone round the free cities that the heir of Ashvael could open doors and level fords, and rural people are practical. If a man can do miracles, the reasoning goes, perhaps he can also manage a plow.

I managed the plow. The goat recovered on its own, insulted. The child's cough yielded to honey and thyme, and her mother wept on my doorstep as if I'd raised the drowned kingdom itself, and I stood there awkwardly holding a jar while Sera tried not to laugh, and afterward I sat on the doorstep a long time.

"That's going to keep happening," Sera said, sitting beside me. The evening was gold at the edges. "People bringing you their ordinary disasters. Wanting you to be more than you are."

"Or less," I said. "Wanting me to be a king, or a saint, or a hedge-witch. Everyone keeps handing me a crown of one size or another." I turned the jar of honey in my hands, the light moving through it. "You never do. Why not?"

She was quiet long enough that I almost apologized for asking.

"Because I met you before any of it," she said finally. "Before I knew what the ledger said. I met a boy trading honey for paper, who laughed at my father's terrible puns and blushed when I caught him looking at the shelves. Everything else—the ember, the heir, the keeper—that was all costume. Underneath it you were always just..." She shrugged, and her shoulder knocked warmly against mine. "Just someone who talks to bees. I've never once needed you to be more than that."

The kiss, when it came, was not the first—we had stopped not-naming it somewhere in the mountains, in the pause before the storm—but it was the first one given in a place that was ours, on a step of a house we had built, with no army anywhere within a hundred miles and no ledger anywhere recording it. Some moments deserve to be unrecorded. That's what freedom is for.

* * *

Autumn found the roof holding, eight hives heavy with honey, and the herb garden tamed into rows that would have satisfied Maren and appalled her in equal measure—for I planted them in the shapes she'd taught me but refused, on principle, to add the warding sigils at the corners. The valley no longer needed sealing. Nothing here needed hiding.

I thought about her constantly now, and it no longer tasted like betrayal. She had caged me out of love and lied to me out of fear and died passing me a burden heavier than any crown, and she had also, once, sat up with me all night when I was nine and feverish, telling me stories about a kingdom beneath the sea where nobody had to be anything. She had been the last door-keeper. I was the last of the line. And the door was shut, and the burden was buried under a beehive, and I liked to think that wherever she was, she was furious about the sigils and glad about everything else.

Sera found me at the hives on the first cold morning, breath steaming, and handed me a letter that had come up from the valley with the week's supplies.

"Imre?" I asked.

"Imre. The council has finally voted on Ashvael's waters." She let me break the seal and read it, and watched my face while I did.

Holy ground. Unanimous, which Imre reported must have involved blackmail, bribery, or a miracle. Marked on no chart. Mourned officially, once a year, in all nine cities.

"Well," I said. "That's that, then."

"That's that."

I folded the letter and stood a moment with my hand resting on the warm wood of the hive, feeling the faint vibration of ten thousand bodies keeping each other alive through the winter to come. Behind me the cottage smoked gently from its new chimney. In the garden the comfrey and the valerian slept under mulch. On the mantel inside, a book waited with a page that was not empty, only unwritten, for whatever we would actually do.

"Sera," I said. "There's a girl down in the village—Bette's granddaughter's child. Tam mentioned her at the harvest fair. Six years old." I hesitated, hearing myself say it, feeling the old fear rise one last time and, for the first time in my life, fail to find anywhere to attach itself. "Last week she froze a puddle solid in midsummer. Just looked at it, and it froze. Her mother's frightened. Says she doesn't know anyone who can tell her whether to be afraid."

Sera was already smiling.

"And what do we tell her?" she asked.

I looked at the hives, at the valley, at the road that led everywhere now, and found the answer had been waiting in me a long while—banked, not buried.

"We tell her to come up in spring," I said. "We'll start with the bees."
