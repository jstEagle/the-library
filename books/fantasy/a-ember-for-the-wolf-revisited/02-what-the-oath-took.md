## 2. What the Oath Took

The Guild of Impossible Things held its tribunals in a room with no door.

Not *no exit* — there was always a way out, the guild was punctilious about that, almost superstitious — but no door you could point to. You walked down the corridor on the third floor where the wallpaper showed a repeating pattern of keys, and somewhere between the fourth key and the fifth the corridor became the tribunal chamber, and afterward you came back out between the same two keys and could never quite say how. Rafael had kept the ledgers for eleven years and he had been summoned three times, and each time the transition happened in the same blind spot in his attention, like a blink he couldn't remember blinking.

Tonight the room was full.

Nine masters sat in a crescent behind a table of black oak that had, according to the guild's own inventory, no origin and no price. Candlelight moved over their faces without warming it. At the crescent's center sat Master Corvane, Keeper of Debts, a narrow man built like a folded letter, and it was Corvane who had taken the child from Rafael's arms an hour ago — gently, courteously, the way you'd take scissors from someone about to run.

"The accounts," Corvane said now, "from last night. Recite them."

Rafael stood in the middle of the floor with his hands loose at his sides, because a ledger-keeper's hands were his testimony. "Fourteen entries closed. A widow's shadow returned to her at cost. The debt of the drowned bell in Harrowmere marked uncollectable, third assessment. Two favors called in, one owed to us, one we now owe—"

"Skip to the end of the shift."

Rafael's mouth went dry. He knew what she wanted skipped to, and he knew, in the way you know a staircase is missing a step, that something at the bottom of it was broken.

"At the ninth hour," he said, "a child arrived at the south door. Mud to the knees. No name offered, none asked. She spoke—" and here his voice did the thing it had done last night, reached for the shape of the language and found the shape still there, perfect and alien, sitting in his mouth like a coin from another country. He said a sentence of it. The candles all leaned east at once.

No one in the crescent moved, but the room itself seemed to flinch, the way a sleeping animal flinches.

"You transcribed it," Corvane said.

"I began to. The ledgers began writing themselves before I finished the first line. In the same script." Rafael hesitated. "In *her* hand, sir. Not mine. The ledgers answered her."

"And then?"

And then. That was the hole. Rafael stood in the tribunal room with nine masters watching him and felt around the edges of the last hour of his shift the way you feel around the edge of a tooth, and found the same smooth absence he'd been finding all morning. He remembered the child's face, mud-streaked, gray-eyed, utterly calm. He remembered the ledgers shivering open. He remembered reaching for the transcription pen.

He did not remember what he wrote. He did not remember the child leaving. He did not remember walking home, or waking, or the walk back through the morning fog with the guild seal heavy in his pocket. There was a stretch of perhaps four hours with no more texture than the inside of his own eyelids.

"Then?" Corvane prompted again, softly, and the softness was worse than shouting.

"I don't know," Rafael said. "Sir. I don't remember."

* * *

They gave him an hour in a side room with bread and watered wine, which was either courtesy or bait, and then they gave him the archive.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its records three floors down and one floor sideways, in a hall where the shelves were arranged by guilt rather than subject. Rafael had catalogued most of it himself and could navigate it blind. Tonight two lantern-bearers walked him down the aisle marked DEBTS, ANCESTRAL, and Master Corvane walked beside him with a single folio under one arm, and the lanterns made the shelf-tops glitter with dust that fell upward instead of down, which Rafael had long ago stopped remarking on.

"Read," Corvane said, and set the folio on the reading stand.

It was old. Older than the guild's founding charter; the binding was sewn with wire that had gone black. The pages were ledger paper, though, the same rag-blend the guild still used, and the hand across the opening page was a clerk's hand, neat and tired.

Rafael read.

*Entry the First. Whereas the court beneath the mountain holds in collateral the soul of the free city of Vael, being payment rendered in the year of the Long Frost, let it be recorded that the debt stands: one soul entire, sold whole and not in part, redeemable only by the seller's blood or the buyer's release.*

"Vael," Rafael said. "There's no Vael."

"There was," Corvane said. "Read on."

He read on. Entry the Second recorded the terms of escrow. Entry the Third recorded interest — souls accrued interest, apparently, compounding in dreams — and Entry the Fourth was crossed out so thoroughly that the ink had soaked through six pages, and beneath the crossing-out, in a fresher hand, a single line:

*Amended per oath of the undersigned clerks. Let the signatories forget their part in this, for a debt remembered is a debt resented, and a debt resented is a debt contested.*

Below that, signatures. Seven of them.

Rafael looked at the names and felt nothing, which was the trouble, because he recognized the handwriting of the third signature and it was his own.

Not similar. His. The particular way the F failed to close, the small vanity of the downward slash on the R. He had signed thousands of entries in eleven years and he knew his own hand the way he knew his own gait. But the entry was dated — the ink told him this without his asking, the way old ink will, a pressure and a patience in it — the entry was dated sixty-three years ago.

"I'm thirty-four," he said. His voice sounded fine. That seemed like a betrayal by his own body.

"Yes," Corvane said.

"That's my hand. And it's not."

"It's your hand," Corvane agreed. "Hands are like souls. They get passed along."

Rafael stepped back from the stand. The lantern-bearers did not move; the dust continued falling politely upward. He thought: *they edited me.* He thought it calmly, catalogued it, filed it in the tidy architecture where he kept everything, and then the architecture buckled, because if they had edited this, what else? Eleven years of ledgers, but also — his mother's face, was that real? The sea town he'd grown up in, could he find it on a map? He tried, standing right there among the shelves, to draw the map of his own childhood in his head, and found the coastline smudged. Rivers that trailed off into blank vellum. A whole province of himself labeled, in effect, *here be nothing.*

"How much is missing," he asked, "in total?"

"We don't measure what we've taken," Corvane said. "That would be keeping a record of it, and the guild keeps records only of debts, not of mercies."

"That's not a mercy."

"No," Corvane said, after a moment. "It isn't."

* * *

They kept the child in the guest cell on the second floor, which was furnished like a good inn and locked like a bad one, and when Rafael pushed the door open past midnight — nobody stopped him, which he noted, and filed under *either permission or trap* — she was sitting cross-legged on the bed with the guild's copy of the world atlas open on her knees, reading it upside down.

"You came back," she said, in ordinary Trade Tongue. Her voice was small and even, a child's voice with a strange stillness underneath, like a pond over a deep place.

"I came back," Rafael said. "Do you have a name?"

She considered him. Gray eyes, mud still dried in the seams of her ears, maybe seven years old, maybe older in some way that maps didn't cover.

"Wren," she said. Then, in the First Tongue, she said something else — four syllables, gentle ones — and every hair on Rafael's arms stood up, because the words meant something. He understood them without wanting to. That was the nature of the language, apparently: it wasn't learned, it was *recognized*, the way you recognize your own name shouted across a square.

She had said: *You're the one who carried me.*

"Wren," he said carefully, "when? When did I carry you?"

She tilted her head, and for a moment she looked less like a child than like an accountant reviewing a discrepancy.

"You don't remember," she said. Not a question.

"No."

"That's all right." She closed the atlas and slid off the bed. "I remember enough for both of us. For now." She came and stood very close, looking up at him, and her next words were in the First Tongue again, and they went through him like a nail finding a stud:

*The mountain remembers your smell.*

Rafael knelt down slowly, so they were eye to eye. Outside, somewhere over the rooftops, thunder that was not weather rolled along the hills — a deep, rhythmic sound, too regular, like drums, like boots. Three days' march away, maybe less. Word had come up the river road at dusk: Veyra's army had crossed the ford at Ashbridge, burning as it came, and the free cities were counting their walls.

"Wren," he said. "What's under the mountain?"

"My soul," she said, matter-of-fact, the way a child says *my tooth.* "And part of yours. We should go get them before the bad queen does."

She took his hand. Small, cold, certain.

And Rafael Fairweather, who had spent eleven years recording other people's impossible debts in careful columns, felt the blank space in his own memory lean against him like something alive — and heard himself say, in a voice steadier than he had any right to feel:

"Then we'll need a map."

The child smiled for the first time. It did not reach her eyes, which were busy doing arithmetic.

"We'll need more than that," she said. "We'll need a knight."
