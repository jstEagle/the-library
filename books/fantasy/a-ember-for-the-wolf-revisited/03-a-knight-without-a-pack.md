## 3. A Knight Without a Pack

The wolf-knights of Callenmoor had three rules carved into the lintel of their chapterhouse, and Sera Voss had broken the only one that mattered: *carry no order above your pack.* Twelve years later she still woke some mornings reaching for fur that wasn't there, for the warm weight of her brothers and sisters sleeping around her in the winter hall. She'd traded all of it — the pack, the oaths, the grey cloak with its silver clasp — for one word spoken at the wrong moment to the wrong general.

*No.*

Exile had taught her things the chapterhouse never could. It taught her that a sword earned more coin in the free cities than a conscience did. It taught her which taverns watered the ale and which innkeepers counted knives. And it taught her that when a guild sent a runner to find you, you listened first and negotiated after, because guilds paid in gold and asked fewer questions than armies.

So when the runner found her in Duskhollow — a nervous boy with ink-stained cuffs and a letter sealed in grey wax — she read it twice, pocketed the coin advance, and told him she'd be at the Guild of Impossible Things by sundown.

She didn't tell him she already hated the place.

* * *

The guildhall was everything she expected. Narrow, tall, crammed with paper. Maps hung from every wall like flayed skins of countries that had never done anything to deserve it. The smell was ink and lamp oil and something older underneath, a smell like a locked room.

The cartographer who met her was called Fairweather, and he looked exactly like a man called Fairweather should look: tired, precise, apologetic about existing. His eyes had the flat shine of someone who spent his nights indoors with ledgers, and his hands were stained with ink to the wrist, as if he'd been caught in the act of writing and never washed.

"Sera Voss," he said, reading from a card rather than looking at her. "Formerly of the Callenmoor chapterhouse. Discharged for—"

"You'll want the short version," Sera said. "I refused an order. They took my cloak. I kept the sword."

His gaze came up then, and she saw something flicker behind the clerk's composure — not fear, exactly. Recognition of a kind. As if her answer had knocked on a door inside him that he didn't remember owning.

"Good," he said quietly.

That surprised her. Most employers wanted the shame left out of the introduction.

"The contract is escort work," Fairweather went on, sliding a paper across the table. "East. Through the Thornmere passes and down into the lowlands beyond. Two weeks' travel, perhaps three if the weather turns. Payment on completion, half again if we arrive ahead of Veyra's army."

"Veyra's army is a week behind you at best," Sera said. "Everyone in the east is running. What are you carrying that's worth outrunning it?"

Fairweather glanced toward the stairwell. A child stood on the third step.

Sera had seen plenty of children on roads gone bad — hollow-eyed, quiet, carrying everything they owned in their arms. This one was different. This one watched her the way she used to watch the treeline before an ambush: not afraid, but counting. Mud had dried in layers on the child's boots, days of travel deep, and there was nothing else. No pack. No blanket. No doll, no knife, no coin. Just mud and a stare that weighed her like a butcher weighs meat.

"This is Wren," Fairweather said. "Wren is what we're carrying."

"Wren is a child."

"Wren," said Wren, in a voice like dry leaves, "is going east whether you come or not."

The words were plain trade-tongue, but something under them wasn't. For half a heartbeat Sera heard them twice — once as speech and once as something older, felt in the teeth, like thunder heard through stone. Her hand moved toward her sword hilt without asking permission. She forced it back down.

"I don't cartographer work," she said. "Last guild map I followed led my whole patrol into a marsh that your people swore wasn't there. Lost two wolves to the drowning-mud. Your kind draws lines on paper and calls it truth."

"And yet you're here," Fairweather said.

"Because gold spends the same whether I like the buyer or not." She looked at the child again. Wren stared back, unblinking. "Half up front. Full rate for the child — children double the trouble."

"Done," said Fairweather, too fast, and Sera understood that whatever this was, money was the least of it. She signed the contract with a name she hadn't used in twelve years and tried not to feel the hook going in.

* * *

They left before dawn, three figures on the eastern road while Duskhollow slept off its fears: the clerk with his map-case strapped across his back like a shield, the child walking between them with that same counting stare, and Sera out front where she always walked, because rearguard was for people who trusted what followed them.

By midmorning they passed the first burned farmstead.

It was recent — a day old, maybe less. The thatch still smoldered in places, sullen and orange against the grey morning. Whoever had done it hadn't looted much. They'd taken the grain, killed the dogs, and left the rest as a message. Sera circled the yard once, reading the ground the way other people read letters. Hoofprints, unshod, light riders moving fast. Scout work. Not soldiers holding ground — soldiers looking for something, burning as they searched.

"They're ahead of us," she said, coming back to the road. "Not behind. Your week of grace doesn't exist. Veyra's scouts are already in the passes."

Fairweather went very still. "Ahead of us. Looking for what?"

"What do you think?" Sera jerked her chin at Wren, who stood regarding the burned farmhouse with an expression no child should own — not horror, but recognition. Like reading an old letter. "Whatever's written in that small skull, the usurper wants it badly enough to send riders through hostile country before her army's even formed. We're not escorts anymore, clerk. We're the quarry."

"We should move off the road, then."

"We should move faster." But even as she said it, Sera was looking at the child again. Wren crouched at the edge of the yard, by a fence post blackened nearly through, and touched it once — gently, the way you'd touch a grave-marker. Then Wren stood, brushed off small muddy hands, and came back to the road without a word.

That night they camped in a hollow off the pass road, cold food, no fire. Sera took first watch and sat where she could see both her charges sleeping. Fairweather slept like a man braced for interruption, curled around his map-case. Wren slept like a stone, arms crossed over the chest, and once, in the dark hours, began speaking — clear, fluent syllables in a language Sera had never heard and somehow recognized anyway, the way a dog recognizes a whistle pitched above human ears. It lasted ten seconds. Then Wren rolled over and slept on.

Sera sat with her sword across her knees and her heart going hard against her ribs.

She'd sworn off packs. That was the whole point of exile — one loyalty had cost her everything, so she'd built a life with none. Employers were employers. Contracts were contracts. You walked the road, you took the coin, you didn't let anyone close enough to be taken from you.

But she'd also been a wolf once, and a wolf knows what it's watching. She was watching two creatures asleep in the dark — one hunted, one haunted — and every year of training she'd ever had was telling her the same thing: *these are yours now. Keep them alive.*

*Damn it,* she thought, with feeling.

* * *

Two days later they came down out of the high passes into Thornmere vale, and the vale was dying.

Smoke rose from three points along the valley floor, thin columns standing straight in the windless air. The road below was crowded with the dispossessed — carts, cattle, families walking with the blank forward stares of people who had stopped deciding things. A woman carrying a baby flagged them down and asked if the free cities had fallen yet. When Fairweather said he didn't know, she nodded as if that were the worst possible answer and shuffled on.

At a waystation whose keeper hadn't fled, they learned the shape of it. Riders in black-and-copper, flying Veyra's sunburst, moving valley to valley ahead of the main host. Burning granaries and records-houses. Asking, always, after strange children. Children who spoke wrong. Children born after a debt.

The keeper's voice dropped on those last words, and his eyes slid to Wren sitting in the corner eating bread, and Sera saw the exact moment he decided not to notice the child existed.

Outside again, in the cold afternoon light, Fairweather stopped in the middle of the road.

"He knew," the cartographer said. "He knew what she is."

"Everyone's going to know what she is, if we keep walking her down the king's road." Sera checked the sky — two hours of light, maybe less. "We cut north at the fork ahead. Shepherd tracks through the high ground. Harder walking, but no scouts."

"That's not on the map."

"The map," Sera said, "is a drawing made by people who weren't being hunted. Trust me instead. I've survived twelve years of not trusting anything, and I'm still here."

Fairweather looked at her then — really looked, the clerk's flatness cracking — and for a moment she thought he was going to argue. Instead he said, "Why? You said it yourself. Gold spends the same either way."

It was a fair question, and she'd had two days to avoid answering it, so she answered it.

"Because I watched riders burn a farmstead where the family got out clean, and I thought, well, that's just scouts making a point. And then I watched that child touch a burnt fence post like it was a headstone, and I thought: I know why I broke my oath. I broke it because someone told my pack to forget a thing that deserved remembering, and I said no." She shouldered her pack. "Nobody's forgetting this kid. Not on my watch. That's not loyalty, clerk. Don't get sentimental. It's just the only order I've ever given myself that I haven't regretted."

She turned up the track before he could reply, because replies were dangerous and dusk was closer than it looked.

Behind her, hooves on the road. Sera turned, hand on hilt — but it was only a lone rider coming up the valley at speed, black-and-copper scarf pulled over the face, and he wasn't slowing. He was looking at a paper in his hand as he rode, and then he raised his head and looked directly at the three of them, and Sera saw him decide.

He wheeled the horse south, toward the smoke, riding hard to carry what he'd found.

"That," Sera said quietly, "is our hour gone. Move."

And far to the east, past the passes they meant to cross, something answered — a sound rolling down out of the high country like a struck bell heard through the bones of the mountain, and Wren, small and mud-crusted at her side, went rigid and said, in the First Tongue or something near it, a single word that Sera felt in her teeth:

*Soon.*
