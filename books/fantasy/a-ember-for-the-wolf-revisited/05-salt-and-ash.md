## 5. Salt and Ash

The drowned city of Vell had no business still standing, but it had never been much interested in what people expected of it. Half its towers leaned out of the harbor like drunkards arguing with the sea, and the other half had lost that argument generations ago. Sera Voss picked her way along a rooftop ridge — once a street, before the water came — and decided it would do.

Rain was coming off the east in gray sheets, and behind them, somewhere past the hills, Veyra's vanguard was doing whatever vanguards did to villages that couldn't run fast enough. They'd seen the smoke at midday. Wren hadn't cried, which worried Sera more than crying would have. The child had just watched the horizon burn with the flat attention of someone taking notes.

"There," Sera said, pointing to a bell tower whose top floor stood clear of the floodline, its roof mostly intact. "We sleep high. Water keeps things honest."

"What's that supposed to mean?" Rafael asked. The cartographer was soaked through, his ledger satchel clutched to his chest like a second heart. He'd carried the thing through a river crossing, an ambush, and a night in a thorn brake without once letting go of it. Sera had known men who loved their swords less.

"It means drowned things don't lie about being dead."

He looked at her like he was trying to decide whether that was wisdom or nonsense. She'd seen the expression before. Most soft-handed guild folk wore it around her for the first three days.

* * *

They made camp on the tower's top landing, under a ceiling furred with salt bloom. Sera built a small fire from driftwood — it burned blue-green and hissed like something alive, but it gave heat, and heat mattered more than aesthetics. Wren curled up against Rafael's side within minutes, asleep the way children sleep when they've learned not to waste time on it.

Sera took first watch because she always did, and because she didn't trust the cartographer's eyes yet. Guild-trained eyes saw routes and resources. Hers saw exits.

For a while there was only the rain and the sea working at the city below, patient as a debt collector.

"You knew one of them," Rafael said quietly. He didn't open his eyes. "In the village we passed. The burning one. You looked at the smoke and you said a name."

"I didn't say anything."

"You mouthed it." He opened his eyes now, firelight catching the strange pale color of them. "I watch faces. It's part of making maps — you learn where people look, what they flinch away from. You flinched."

Sera turned a piece of driftwood in her fingers until the grain bit her palm. She could refuse to answer. She'd refused plenty of people plenty of things. But the rain made the world small, and small worlds made truth cheap.

"Her name was Odessa Brann," Sera said. "She ran a ferry crossing at Millbrook Ford. Fed my whole pack one winter when our provisioning fell through. Roast goat, every solstice, whether we could pay or not." She nodded toward the smoke-stained dark beyond the broken window. "Veyra's outriders burned the ford two days ago. I'd bet my sword arm Odessa didn't run. She never ran from anything. Stubborn as a mule with a title."

Rafael sat up carefully, so as not to wake the child. "You were close."

"My pack was stationed near Millbrook for six years. Close doesn't cover it. We were—" She stopped. Started again. "Do you know what a wolf-knight's oath actually binds? Not the sword hand. Everyone thinks it's the sword. It isn't. It's the memory."

His brow creased. "I don't understand."

"The Order of the Gray Pack swears to hold the free cities' griefs for them. Every atrocity the cities commit or suffer, the pack remembers it so the cities can heal clean. That's the sacred duty. That's what they tell you at induction, anyway, all incense and trumpets." Her laugh came out wrong, scraped raw by salt air. "What they don't tell you is that the remembering goes both ways. Sometimes the cities want to forget something they *did*. And sometimes the Order agrees to help."

The fire popped. Below them, water lapped at stone with terrible patience.

"Three years ago," Sera said, "my pack was ordered to forget a massacre."

Rafael went very still.

"Not to avenge it. Not to investigate it. To *forget* it. A company of the Gray Pack rode into the Hollow Vale to put down a tax revolt — forty households, farmers, weavers, a village that made its living off dye-flowers. Something went wrong. The commander panicked, or the orders got garbled, or someone wanted the vale empty for reasons that never reached my rank. By morning the vale was ash and everyone who lived there was dead, including two of my own wolves who'd been sent ahead as envoys." Her voice stayed level. It had taken years of practice to keep it level. "And then the Order's archivists came with their rites and their censers, and they knelt us down in a circle, and they told us to release the memory. To give it to the deep archive so the realm could move forward unburdened."

"And you refused."

"Twelve of us refused." She stared into the blue-green flames. "Eleven changed their minds when the rite started. It's not painless, refusing. The forgetting pulls. It feels like relief, if anything. Like setting down a weight you've carried so long you forgot it was heavy. Eleven of my brothers and sisters set it down and smiled and thanked the archivists." She flexed her scarred hand. "I bit through my own lip and held on. Held the names of forty households in my teeth while the rite tried to pry them loose. When it was over, they struck me from the rolls. Stripped my colors. Called me oath-broken in front of the assembled Order." A pause. "The funny thing is, I'm the only one left who remembers the Hollow Vale at all. The massacre has no witnesses now except me. Which makes me either the realm's last honest grief or a madwoman shouting at fog. Some days I can't tell which."

Silence, except for the rain. Then Rafael spoke, so softly she almost missed it beneath the weather.

"My memories feel borrowed."

She looked at him.

"That's not — I know how it sounds." He laughed, and his laugh was worse than hers, thinner, frightened. "But you asked why a ledger-keeper volunteers to walk into Veyra's path, and I lied to the guild masters when they assigned this, and I'll lie to you too unless you'd rather I didn't. The truth is I can't account for large portions of my own life. There are years I can describe in perfect detail and feel nothing about. My mother's funeral — I remember the flowers, the order of service, what everyone wore. I remember it like a map. Like terrain surveyed by someone else. And there are other things, gaps shaped like absence, edges where something was cut out and the cut was stitched closed so neatly you'd never notice unless you went looking." His hands had found the ledger satchel again, knuckles white. "I went looking. After the child arrived. After the ledgers started writing themselves. I pulled my own service records, and Sera — there are entries in my file in a cipher I've never been taught, referencing oaths I have no memory of swearing. One of them mentions 'the mountain debt.' My handwriting. My seal. No memory of it at all."

Wren stirred against him and settled again.

"You think the guild edited you," Sera said. "Like the archivists tried to edit us."

"I think the guild does to its clerks what your Order does to its knights. Quietly. For good reasons, I'm sure. Good reasons are always the ones used." He met her eyes, and there was something raw underneath the clerk's composure, something young and terrified. "You held onto forty names when everything in you was screaming to let go. How?"

"How do you think?" She fed another stick of driftwood to the fire. Blue flame crawled up the grain. "I decided some things matter more than comfort. That's the whole trick. That's the entire secret of it. You decide, once, hard, and then you keep deciding every single day, usually alone, usually tired."

"And if you find out the thing you forgot is something terrible? Something you did?"

Sera studied him. The rain, the ruined city, the sleeping child — all of it seemed to hang on his next breath.

"Then you carry it," she said. "Carrying is the job. Anyone can drop a weight. Wolves don't drop."

* * *

Wren woke near dawn, silent as ever, and walked to the broken window while the other two pretended to sleep. The child looked east, where the fires had burned through the night, and spoke four words in the First Tongue. The syllables moved through the drowned city like a current through water, and in the flooded streets below, something ancient shifted its weight and answered.

Sera came awake with her hand on her sword hilt, heart slamming. "What did it just say?"

Rafael was already sitting up, face bloodless. "I don't know. But the ledgers—" He opened the satchel with shaking hands. Inside, across every page, fresh ink was drying. Lines and lines of it, written in no hand he owned: coordinates, depth-soundings, a route descending beneath the earth. And at the bottom of the final page, in letters that glowed faintly like banked coals, a single instruction in plain Common:

**THE MOUNTAIN OPENS FOR THE INDEX. COME BEFORE THE USURPER.**

Sera read it twice. Outside, far to the east, the fires of Veyra's vanguard had gone out — not extinguished, she realized with a chill, but *relocated*, a line of new smoke rising directly along the road to the mountain.

"They're marching for the same place we are," she said. "And they're faster."

Rafael closed the satchel. "Then we stop being careful."

Sera looked at the child standing in the gray light, at the drowned city holding its breath around them, and understood with absolute clarity that the careful part of her life had ended three years ago in a circle of kneeling wolves. Everything since had just been the long walk to here.

"East, then," she said. "Down, after that. And gods help anyone who gets between us and the door."
