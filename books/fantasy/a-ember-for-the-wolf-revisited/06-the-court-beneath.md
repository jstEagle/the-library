## 6. The Court Beneath

The mountain did not have a door. It had a debt.

That was what the map said — Sera's copy of it, the one she'd taken from the dead courier at the ford, its ink still faintly warm. Where a door should be drawn there was instead a single line of ledger-script, and when Wren touched it with one finger the stone before us opened like a throat.

We went in because there was nowhere else to go. Veyra's vanguard was two days behind us and closing, and the mountain was the only direction left that wasn't toward her armies or back toward everything I'd broken.

The passage descended in long spirals, and the walls were not stone. They were paper. Compressed, ancient, layered like sediment — pages upon pages pressed into strata, and here and there a line of text surfaced through the rockface like a vein of ore. I put my hand against it as we walked. The words moved under my palm. They were accounts. Debts, mostly. Some of them were very old, and some of them, I realized with a cold that started in my teeth and worked downward, were written in my own hand.

"Rafael." Sera's voice, low. She'd seen me stop. "Keep walking."

"I wrote this." The entry was dated forty-one years ago. A sale. A soul, portioned. A buyer listed only as *the court*. My signature sat beneath it, neat and clerkish, the signature of a much younger man. "Sera, I wrote this."

"You don't remember writing it."

"No."

"Then maybe you didn't." But her hand rested on her sword-hilt the whole time she said it, and she watched me the way you watch a man standing on ice.

Wren walked between us without speaking. The child hadn't spoken a word of common tongue since we'd entered the mountain. Everything now came out in the First Tongue, soft and syllabic, and the walls answered. As we descended, the veins of text brightened where Wren passed, the way iron filings rise to a lodestone. The mountain knew the child. Worse — the mountain was *expecting* the child.

After a time I stopped counting the turns. The air grew warm and smelled of tallow and old vellum, and then the passage simply ended, opening without warning into a hall so vast my cartographer's eye failed it entirely. I have drawn cities from memory. I once mapped the drowned quarter of Saltmere from a rowboat in a storm. I could not map this room. Its proportions refused to hold still. Every time I looked up, the ceiling was farther away.

It was lit by candles that burned without consuming anything — flames shaped like small, patient hands. And it was full of people.

Not courtiers. Not guards. *Collateral.* That was the word that arrived in my mind uninvited, the way certain guild terms do. They stood in alcoves along the walls, hundreds of them, motionless, each one dimmed somehow, translucent at the edges, like figures seen through frosted glass. Some were dressed in the fashions of kingdoms that no longer existed. One wore the grey sash of the Guild of Impossible Things.

"Sera," I said quietly. "Don't look at them too long."

"Why?"

"Because I think looking is how they keep you."

* * *

The broker received us on a dais made of stacked ledgers — actual ledgers, thousands of them, fused into a throne by weight alone. It — he? — looked like a man who had been described to someone over a very long distance and reconstructed from the description. A face, roughly. Hands, roughly. Eyes that were not eyes but entries, small moving lines of script where the whites should have been.

"The cartographer," it said. Its voice was the sound of a page turning. "And the wolf-knight. And—" Here it paused, and every candle in the hall leaned toward Wren like grass in wind. "The index. Come home at last."

"She's not an index," Sera said. "She's a child."

"She is a *record*," the broker said, with the mild patience of a clerk correcting a junior. "A living index of a soul sold to this court four generations ago. The soul itself resides in my vault. The index is the key that opens it. You have carried the key across half a world to me. I commend your diligence. Few couriers arrive intact."

"We're not couriers," I said. "We're thieves."

"Yes." The broker's mouth curved, and the curve was wrong, too many degrees of it. "I know. That is why you are interesting. Thieves rarely come so far down. Usually they stop at the door and sell something instead. Everyone sells something, eventually. Time. Memory. A name. A night. You would be astonished what men pay for a single forgotten evening." Its script-eyes flicked to me. "You especially would not be astonished. You've sold before."

The floor did not move, but I felt it tilt.

"I don't remember—"

"No," the broker agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

* * *

They gave us a cell, because even courts beneath mountains have procedures, and procedures are how the damned comfort themselves. Ours was a room walled in ledger-pages, with a candle that burned without eating itself and a table set with bread and water that tasted faintly of ink.

"They're going to offer us a trade," Sera said. She sat with her back to the wall, sword across her knees, though I'd already understood the sword was useless down here. You cannot cut an account. "Whatever they want, they'll dress it up as a bargain. That's how these places work. Nobody robs you if they can make you sign."

"Wren is not for sale."

"Rafael." She said my name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Look at the child."

I looked. Wren sat cross-legged on the pallet, tracing the ledger-script in the wall with one finger, and the script rose to meet the touch like wheat to sun. The child was humming something in the First Tongue, and the walls hummed it back, and for a moment the whole cell glowed with borrowed light.

"That soul in their vault," Sera said. "If it goes back where it belongs — into her line, into *her* — what happens to this court? To him?" She nodded upward, toward the broker's distant dais. "He's not going to let us walk out with his collateral. He'll buy one of us first. He always buys one."

"He can't buy me. I'm guild. My memories are oath-sealed."

Sera looked at me for a long moment, and there was something in her face I'd learned to dread on the road: pity.

"Rafael. What do you think the oaths took?"

* * *

The broker came for me alone, at an hour that might have been midnight or might have been no hour at all. It dismissed Sera with a gesture and Wren with a look, and led me up — not out, never out, but up — through galleries of the dimmed and waiting, until we stood before an alcove near the top of the hall.

"This one arrived yesterday," the broker said. "Well. Yesterday in the way time is kept here. Do you see?"

In the alcove stood a young man, translucent at the edges, dressed in the grey sash of the Guild of Impossible Things. He was perhaps twenty-five years old. He had ink stains on his fingers — the particular constellation of stains a ledger-keeper gets, across the second knuckle of the right hand, the middle finger braced below.

I looked down at my own right hand. The same constellation, faded but unmistakable, sat on my skin like a scar.

"His name is Rafael Fairweather," the broker said gently. "Junior clerk, third desk, Guild of Impossible Things. Forty-one years ago he signed a supplementary instrument regarding the mountain debt. His fee was substantial. His fee was himself — the years of his life in which the transaction occurred, surrendered to this court as surety. We have held them ever since. He has been waiting for you to come collect them."

The young man turned his head. Slowly, the way things move in dreams. He looked at me, and I looked at him, and there was nothing uncanny about it at all, which was the most horrifying part. He had my jaw. My habit of holding one shoulder slightly higher than the other. He looked at me the way you look at a stranger on a road who reminds you unbearably of someone.

Of yourself.

"Give me the child," the broker said, "and I will give you back the years. All of them. Every hour the oaths ate, every entry the guild struck from you, every morning you woke knowing something had been taken and couldn't name what. You will walk out of this mountain whole, Rafael Fairweather. Complete. You will remember why you came here, and what you did, and whose name is on the sale beside yours. Is that not worth more than a child you met eleven days ago? A child who is, after all, only a copy? The original sits in my vault. The girl is a footnote."

The candle between us burned without flickering. Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of pressed paper and stone, I thought I heard Veyra's war-drums, or wanted to.

Here is what I have learned about temptation, and I learned it in that hall: it never offers you power. Power is for lesser devils. Real temptation offers you *yourself* — the self you suspect is buried somewhere, the missing rooms of your own house. It does not ask you to become someone else. It asks only to give you back what was stolen, and all it costs you is someone else's everything.

"I don't need to remember," I said.

"Everyone says that."

"I don't need to remember," I said again, and my voice shook, and I hated it, and I said it anyway, "because whatever I did forty-one years ago, I did it to *her family*. And if I take those years back, I take them back for myself. For my wholeness. My completion." I looked at the young man in the alcove — at the boy I had been, waiting all this time in the dark to be reclaimed. "He can stay. Someone has to sit with what he did."

For the first time, the broker's script-eyes stopped moving.

"You refuse."

"I refuse."

"A pity." The broker folded its too-many-jointed hands. "Because the offer was never mine to withdraw — and it was never only yours to accept. There is a second party to the instrument of forty-one years ago. A co-signatory. And she has just arrived at my gate with an army at her back, and she intends to collect."

Far above us, through the body of the mountain, the drums stopped.

And in the silence that followed, Wren began to scream.
