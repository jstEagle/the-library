## 8. The Price of Names

The broker's court smelled of ink and cold stone, and Sera Voss had been in enough war councils to know when someone was about to ask for something unbearable.

They sat at the base of the mountain's root, in a hall that should not have fit inside the rock — vaulted ceilings lost in darkness, and shelves rising from the floor in concentric rings like the inside of a throat. Every shelf was crammed with books. Not books, she corrected herself. Ledgers. And in the ledgers, if you looked too long, things moved.

The broker stood at the center of it all: a tall figure stitched together out of clerical grey, face smooth as an unmarked page. When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere, the way a rumor does.

"You wish to reach the vault," it said. "The vault holds what was sold. Nothing beneath this mountain moves without payment. You have coin?"

Rafael spread his hands. "We have guild scrip—"

"We have nothing," Sera said flatly. "He means he has nothing."

The broker's blank face turned toward her. She felt the attention of it like a blade laid flat against her skin.

"Then we must discuss alternatives," the broker said. "Coin is for merchants. For souls, we deal in heavier currency." A pause, theatrical as any magistrate's. "A name. Freely given."

Sera had heard oaths break. She had heard men scream their own names into the dark until they forgot why. But this — this was different. The ledgers around them rustled, pages turning without wind, and every one of them seemed to lean inward, listening.

"What does that mean, exactly?" Rafael asked. His voice was careful, the voice of a man who kept accounts and knew that terms mattered.

"It means," said the broker, "that one of you surrenders the name they were given at birth. Not an alias. Not a title. The true word your mother spoke over your cradle. In exchange, I open the way to the vault, ward you past the sentinels, and hold the door behind you."

"And the person who gives it?" Rafael's knuckles were white on his satchel strap.

"Is written," the broker said simply. "Recorded. Held. They persist — but as entry rather than creature. It is not death. It is filing."

Wren, small and mud-streaked and speaking in that terrible old tongue even now, tugged at Rafael's sleeve and said something low. Sera didn't need the translation the child had taught them in fragments on the road. She knew a refusal when she heard one. Wren stepped forward, chin up, offering herself the way children offer everything — because no one had yet taught them the price.

"No," Sera said.

She surprised herself with how steady it came out.

"The child doesn't pay," she said. "Neither does the cartographer. He needs his memory to find whatever it is you keep in that vault; I've seen his kind navigate by intuition alone, and his is edited to ribbons. That leaves me."

"Sera—" Rafael started.

"Quiet. Grown soldiers are talking." She turned back to the broker. "My name for the passage. All three of us through, the door held, no tricks. Swear it in whatever language you file people in."

The broker regarded her for a long moment. Then it did something she hadn't seen it do before — it inclined its head. Respect, or the shape of respect worn by a thing that had never needed it before.

"A knight without a pack," it said. "You broke your oath once. Do you know what your name weighs, Seraphine Voss?"

Her whole body went cold.

She hadn't heard that name in eleven years. Her pack had used it. Her mother had used it, once, through a door she'd never gone back to. She had buried *Seraphine* somewhere on the road east of Harrowmere, next to her shield-brothers, and become Sera because the short version hurt less to say.

"How do you know that name?" she asked quietly.

"I keep everything sold, sworn, or surrendered," said the broker. "Your oath of knighthood is filed here. Your refusal is filed here. Your pack's massacre at Blackfen Ford — the one you were ordered to forget — is filed here, all forty-one entries of it. I could read you their last words. Would you like that?"

"No," Sera said. Her eyes were burning and she would not blink. "But I'll tell you what I'll add to your collection."

* * *

Rafael argued. Of course he argued — he was a ledger-man, and somewhere under all those edited memories was a man who believed debts could be renegotiated. Sera let him run through it while she unbuckled her sword belt, piece by piece, the way she'd done ten thousand mornings.

"You don't even know what happens to you," he said. "Filing. What does *filing* mean? You could be trapped in one of these books forever, read like a—"

"Sera."

"—what?"

"My name will be Sera. Just Sera. Let them have the rest." She set the sword down on the broker's reading table, gently, the way you lay down something that has kept you alive. "Rafael. Listen to me. I broke my oath because my pack was ordered to forget forty dead villagers, and I refused. Everyone told me refusing was pride. Madness. Eleven years I've wondered if they were right." She looked up at the endless shelves. "This isn't forgetting. This is the opposite. I'm going into the record. Permanently. On my own terms, for something I chose." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, unused. "That's not surrender, cartographer. That's the only defiance I've got left."

Wren crossed the space between them and wrapped both arms around Sera's waist, and said something in the First Tongue, soft, against her armor.

"I know," Sera said, though she didn't. "I know. Go get yourself back."

The broker produced a book. Of course it did. It was bound in grey leather, blank, and when it opened, the pages breathed.

"The terms," the broker intoned. "Passage for three to the vault door. Warding past the sentinels. The door held for one turning of the sandglass beyond your entry. In exchange: the name Seraphine Voss, daughter of Maren, knighted at Harrowmere, exiled at Blackfen Ford. Given freely?"

"Freely," Sera said.

And then — because she was who she was, because her whole ruined life had been built on saying the true thing at the worst possible moment — she added:

"And write this part down too. I give it for them, not for you. And I'd do it again."

The broker paused. Somewhere in the dark of the shelves, a thousand ledgers turned a thousand pages at once.

"So noted," it said, and there was something almost warm in the flat voice. Almost.

* * *

It didn't hurt. That was the worst of it.

She had expected pain, or cold, or the tearing feeling of the oath-breaking, which had felt like being unstitched. Instead there was just a pen — where the broker had gotten a pen, she never saw — drawing a long stroke across a page, and with each inch of the stroke, a piece of her went quiet.

*Seraphine* went first. She felt it lift out of her like a splinter drawn clean, and with it went her mother's voice at the door, the smell of the training yard at Harrowmere, the weight of the title *knight*. Not gone — she could feel them, distantly, the way you feel a room you've left. Filed. Held. Somewhere behind her, Rafael made a sound like a man being wounded on her behalf.

*Sera* stayed. Small, stubborn, self-made. Apparently the brokers of the deep courts had no jurisdiction over names you gave yourself. Some spiteful, flickering corner of her found that hilarious.

Her body was going translucent at the edges. She could see the shelves through her own hand. So she spent her last moments of solidity doing the only useful thing left: she gripped Rafael's shoulder hard enough to feel it, and put her other hand on Wren's head.

"East corridor, follow the salt line, don't stop for the voices — they'll sound like your pack, cartographer, they'll sound like everyone you ever lost, keep walking." She was talking fast now, edges fraying into light. "And when you get out — if you get out — go north. There's a valley past the ashline where nobody's ever drawn a border. I always meant to see it. Map it for me. Put my name in the corner of the map, even if it's not mine anymore. Put *Sera* in the corner."

"I'll put it in the legend," Rafael said, and his voice cracked straight down the middle. "Prominent placement. Guild-standard."

"Worst cartographer I ever met," Sera said.

"Only cartographer you ever met."

"That's why it's the worst."

And then the stroke of the pen ended, and so did she — mostly. What remained was a shifting, weightless awareness, and then motion, and then the strangest sensation of her long strange life: she felt herself being *read*. Opened. Set among forty-one entries from Blackfen Ford and a knight's oath and a mother at a door, shelved in the dark beside everything else that had ever been surrendered in this place.

It wasn't rest. But it wasn't oblivion either. She hung in the grey leather of the book, aware, waiting, and thought: *so this is what it's like to be a true thing someone finally wrote down.*

Through the paper, faintly, she felt the vault door open. Felt two heartbeats pass her — one steady, one small and blazing — going down into the dark toward the thing that had been sold.

And felt, far above, through stone and shelf and story, an army arriving at the mountain's crown. Veyra's host, tens of thousands of boots, marching to collect.

The sandglass had begun to turn.

Hold the door, she willed at the broker, at the whole rotten beautiful court. *You swore it. Hold the door.*

Somewhere below, in the vault where souls were kept like winter stores, Rafael Fairweather was about to come face to face with his own signature — and Sera, filed and nameless and freer than she had been in eleven years, would not be there to pull him out of it.
