## 9. Ember

The vault door was made of ledger-leather, bound in iron, and it knew Rafael's hand.

He understood that the moment he pressed his palm to it — not the way a lock knows a key, mechanically, indifferently, but the way an old dog knows the foot that kicked it. The leather shuddered. Somewhere behind it, pages turned on their own, thousands of them, a sound like wind through a dead forest.

"It recognizes you," Wren said. The child's voice was thin down here, worn by the mountain's weight. "Why does it recognize you?"

"I don't know." The lie came easily because he had been telling versions of it for thirty years. *I don't remember. The oath took it. It isn't my fault.*

Sera stood with her back to the corridor, sword drawn, watching the dark they had climbed out of. She hadn't spoken since the broker's court. Since she had — no. He would not think about that yet. Thinking about it would unmake him, and the vault required him whole.

"Open it," she said. "Whatever it costs, open it."

The leather parted like a mouth.

* * *

Inside was the smell of every library he had ever loved and every one he'd been afraid of: dust and vellum and something under it, sweetish, like coins held too long in a warm hand. The vault was not large. That was the horror of it. All of this — the sold souls, the collateral, the debts of six generations — and it fit in a room smaller than the guild's map room, because souls compressed beautifully when you wrote them down properly. He had known that once. He had been *taught* that once.

The master ledger sat on a lectern of black stone, open to nothing, its pages blank and waiting. Around it, in cases of glass and bone, were the lesser records. And around those, faint as breath on winter glass, moved lights. Fragments. The unsold remainder of things.

Wren walked toward the ledger the way children walk toward frozen ponds.

"Wren." His voice cracked. "Wait."

"You wait," the child said, not turning. "I've waited my whole life. My mother waited her whole life. Her mother before her." Wren's small hand hovered over the ledger's cover. "It's in there, isn't it? The part of me they took. I've felt the hole in me since before I had words for holes."

Rafael reached the lectern and put his own hand flat on the master ledger, and the vault exhaled, and the memory came home.

* * *

He was twenty-two again, ink-stained, ambitious, flattered to be noticed.

The guild masters had called him into the sealed room at midnight and told him he had been chosen for a great work — chosen, they emphasized, for his beautiful hand. His script was famous among the apprentices. Fairweather could write anything, they said. Even the First Tongue, if someone spelled it out for him slowly.

A man from beneath the mountain sat across the table. He did not eat the food they offered him. He slid a single page across the tablecloth, and on it was written a soul — or the first third of one — in characters that seemed to move when Rafael wasn't looking directly at them.

"Copy this," the man said. "Precisely. Do not read it aloud. Do not read it silently with your lips moving. Copy only."

"Why?" young Rafael asked, because he was twenty-two and stupid with honesty.

"Because it must be written twice," the man said. "Once to keep and once to sell. That is how all valuable things work."

And Rafael, whose mother owed the guild money for his father's burial, whose apprenticeship could be ended with a word, picked up his pen.

He remembered now the exact weight of the page. He remembered that halfway through, the letters began fighting him, curling away from his nib, and he remembered pressing harder. He remembered the sound — he had spent decades pretending he hadn't — a thin sound, almost outside hearing, like a wire tightened past its note. The soul protesting its transcription.

He finished at dawn. They paid him three silver marks and an oath, and the oath ate the night out of his head, and he went back to his cot and slept dreamlessly and woke up a man who had never done anything wrong.

Until tonight. Until a mud-splattered child knocked on the guild door speaking the First Tongue, and the ledgers began writing themselves in answer, and some buried splinter of him had known — had *always* known — whose hand had made this possible.

*Rafael.* Wren was tugging his sleeve. "Your face is wrong. What did you see?"

* * *

Here is what he said, kneeling on the vault floor so their eyes were level, because some truths deserve the indignity of height:

"When you were born — before you were born — your grandmother's grandmother sold a soul to the court below the mountain. Not hers. A soul she carried. It has passed down your bloodline ever since, a debt attached to heirs, and every generation the court collected a little more of you to hold as surety against the original loan. That is the hole you feel. That is why Veyra wants you — because whoever holds you holds a power even she doesn't understand."

Wren nodded slowly. "And you?"

"My guild keeps the ledgers for that debt. Every entry needs two hands. Someone copied the original." He heard his own voice go flat, clerk-flat, the voice he used for numbers too large to feel. "It was me, Wren. Twenty-two years old, and I wrote your soul out of the world because I was afraid and flattered and paid. Three marks. You have been missing a piece of yourself for thirty years because of three silver marks and my handwriting."

The silence after that was very long. Sera, at the door, had gone still in the way of a wolf deciding whether a thing is prey or kin.

Then Wren did something he had not prepared for, could not have prepared for, would never have imagined in thirty years of self-accusation. The child put both small hands on either side of his face.

"You came," Wren said. "You could have stayed at the guild balancing your little impossible accounts forever. You didn't. You walked into the mountain." A pause, fierce and precise. "Fix it, then. That's what ledgers are for. Corrections."

*Corrections.* Yes. Yes, that was the word. Not absolution — he did not deserve the word absolution and would refuse it if offered. Corrections. A debt entered wrongly could be struck through. A line drawn clean. His whole career, his whole careful gray life, had been preparation for exactly one act, and here it was, waiting on a lectern of black stone.

Above them, faintly, the mountain shuddered. Dust sifted from the ceiling seams.

"Veyra's reached the gates," Sera said. Her voice was steady but her knuckles were white on the sword hilt, and he understood suddenly that she was grieving and refusing to do it yet, saving it like coin. "However you're going to do this, cartographer. Do it now."

* * *

Rafael opened the master ledger.

The pages were blank because the ledger was not written in; it was written *from*. It was the source, and everything else — the guild's ledgers, the broker's records, the oaths that had eaten his memories — were copies, and copies obey. He found the entry by touch rather than sight, the way you find your own name in the dark. There. Page nine hundred and forty-one, in a hand he knew better than his own, because it *was* his own, younger and more cowardly:

*Soul, fragmentary, lineage of Ash (see collateral schedule VII). Held against debt. Transcribed by R.F., verified, sealed.*

Three letters. His initials weighed less than a sparrow's bone and had cost a child a third of a soul.

"Do you hear it?" Wren whispered. And Rafael did — beneath the ledger's paper silence, a sound like a wire tuned almost to its note, the same thin cry he had heard at twenty-two, waiting thirty years for someone to come loosen it.

He set his fingers at the top of the entry.

Every instinct the guild had bred into him screamed. You do not tear a page. You do not unmake an entry. The ledgers are the world's memory; without them nothing is owned, nothing is owed, nothing means. He thought of the masters' faces. He thought of his gray rooms, his precise accounts, his safe and edited life.

He thought of Sera standing guard at a door she would not walk back through.

He tore the page out.

The sound was enormous — not loud, but *deep*, a note struck at the bottom of the mountain, and every glass case in the vault fogged white, and the fragments of light stopped drifting and began to *move*, streaming toward the torn page like sparks up a chimney in reverse. The entry unraveled in his hands, his own beautiful handwriting dissolving letter by letter, *transcribed* becoming *unwritten*, *sealed* becoming *free*, and then the page itself went soft as ash and blew apart, and what was left — what came through the space where it had been — was warmth.

It passed into Wren like sunrise passing into a window.

There was no thunder. There was just a child straightening, filling, the hollow places in Wren's face quietly closing the way water closes over a thrown stone, and when Wren opened their eyes they were the color of banked coals and there was a wholeness in them that made Rafael's chest hurt to look at.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, I remember all of me now." And then, turning to him, gentler than he deserved: "I remember *you*, too. The young one with the pen. You cried while you copied it. Did you know that? It kept you from being wholly theirs. That's why the oath worked so well — there was so much guilt to bury."

The mountain groaned again, closer. Somewhere far above, iron met iron, and shouting began — the particular layered shouting of an army finding a city's gates already broken.

"They're in," Sera said. She turned from the doorway at last, and Rafael saw what she had hidden: the ledger-lines already faintly moving across her skin, silver script climbing her throat like ivy, her name being called in, letter by letter, to pay their passage. She smiled at him, wolfish and unafraid. "Get the kid out, cartographer. Take the long stairs. And Rafael —"

Whatever she meant to say was lost, because the vault door burst inward in a bloom of firelight and smoke, and through it came the sound of Veyra's army, and Sera Voss stepped forward to meet them with her name burning on her lips like a drawn blade.

Rafael grabbed Wren's hand and ran.
