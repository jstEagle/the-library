## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The charms sold best after dark, and so did lies.

Odile Winterbourne had learned that particular arithmetic in her first year behind the counter of the Winterbourne Charm Shop—her mother's shop, though her mother was four years gone and the lease said only *Odile* now—and she had never once had cause to revise it. By day, Vael's Lantern District belonged to fishmongers and cloth merchants and the gray-faced clerks of the Crown's tithe office, people who walked past her window without seeing it. But at night, when the market stalls bloomed down the middle of Candlewright Street like a second, gaudier city, the ones who needed her came out: the girl whose sweetheart sailed with the river fleet, the midwife who wanted a charm against childbed fever and could not quite meet Odile's eye while she asked for it, the old soldier with a tremor in his hands who bought the same luck-knot every month and never once asked whether it worked.

She was wrapping his third luck-knot of the season—the same knot, the same fraying red cord, the same hands that shook too badly to tie it themselves—when she felt the first thread of her wards go slack.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing about small magic; it almost never announced itself. It felt like a draft under a door. Like a note played slightly flat in a song you'd heard ten thousand times, so faint that only someone who had tuned the instrument herself would ever notice.

Odile noticed.

Her hands kept moving—fold the paper, tuck the fold, wax the seal—because her hands had been trained to keep moving through worse than this. But somewhere beneath the counter, beneath the floorboards, beneath the cellar where she stored her dregs and failures in corked jars, the invisible lattice she had spent eleven years weaving around her little shop went quiet the way a struck bell goes quiet: not all at once, but in a long diminishing hum that ended in ordinary silence.

Three layers of concealment-wards, keyed to her blood, renewed every new moon for twelve years. And now nothing. She reached for them the way you reach for a tooth that has come loose and found instead the raw place where it used to be.

"Two coppers," she said to the old soldier, and her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. Level as a carpenter's rule.

He paid, nodded the nod of a man who had survived three campaigns by never asking questions, and shuffled out into the market noise. The bell above her door gave its small tin cheer. Odile stood in the sudden quiet of her shop and pressed her palm flat against the underside of the counter, against the sigil she'd burned into the wood herself at nineteen years old, and felt exactly nothing answer her.

*Invisibility stops working,* she thought, with a strange, weightless clarity, *and everything else keeps going.*

Outside, the night market roared on. Somewhere a fiddler had started up. Somewhere the chestnut seller was arguing about the price of chestnuts, which he did every evening from six bells until close, on principle. The lamps along Candlewright Street burned their usual amber, and the smell of frying onions and tallow smoke drifted through her open window, and none of it—none of it—knew what she knew.

She rolled down the shutter anyway. Bolted it. Stood with her back against the door and listened to her own heart doing something unprofessional.

Then she lit her lantern, flipped the sign to CLOSED, and went upstairs to do what any sensible charmwright would do when the walls of her hiding place fell down around her: she made tea, sat at her workbench, and tried to rebuild the wards before whatever had knocked them down came looking.

---

The tea was cheap leaf, oversteeped, drunk from a cup with a chip in the rim because good cups were for customers. She worked with her sleeves pinned back, chalk dust on her fingers, drawing the first circle of the concealment lattice onto a slate she kept for practice. It should have flowed. After eleven years it flowed the way walking flows, the way lying flows; her hand knew the shapes the way her tongue knew the small polite falsehoods of market life—*yes, that one's fresh, no, I can't take it lower, my mother's recipe, very old family, very traditional.*

Halfway through the second ring, the slate went cold under her fingers.

Not metaphorically cold. Cold like river-stone in March, cold enough that her breath should have fogged, and the chalk dragged as if she were writing through honey, and the line of the circle wavered and broke, and the whole pattern guttered out like a candle in a doorway.

Odile sat back. Looked at her hands. They were steady. That was somehow the worst part—that her body hadn't gotten the message yet, that it still believed in the ordinary machinery of the world: charcoal, chalk, tea, tomorrow.

She had been six years old the last time the magic in Vael had behaved strangely, and even then it had only been strange the way weather is strange—a green sky, a double shadow, her mother going white and pulling her indoors and saying nothing, ever, about anything. Her mother had been very good at saying nothing. It was practically the guild motto.

*The Worshipful Company of Charmwrights,* dead these twelve years, its hall sealed by Crown writ, its members scattered or silenced or simply faded into other trades, its name spoken—if spoken at all—with the fond condescension people reserve for things that no longer matter. Quackery, the tithe clerks called it now. Superstition. Illegal under the Statutes of Clean Fire, a law so old most people assumed it was a joke, until the year the Wardens started arresting hedge-healers and it stopped being funny.

Odile had stayed small. That was the whole art of it, the true craft her mother had passed down—not the wardwork, not the charm-knots, but the staying small. Sell trinkets to people who wanted to believe. Keep the real work under the floorboards. Renew your wards on the new moon, keep your name off every list, be a woman of no consequence in a city that rewarded consequence with a cell.

And for twelve years, smallness had worked. It had worked the way a key works, smoothly and silently, and tonight, without warning, the lock had turned to sand.

*Something found me.*

She didn't let herself think it fully. Thinking it fully meant deciding what to do about it, and what to do about it was a question with no answer except running, and running meant leaving the shop, and the shop was—

The shop was her whole life. She saw that clearly for the first time, sitting in the lamplight with a dead slate in front of her, and the clarity of it frightened her more than the broken wards did. Four rooms above a counter. A ledger full of small transactions. A calendar marked with new moons. Twelve years, and she could count on one hand the nights she'd spent beyond the sound of the market bell.

She had built herself a life the size of a charm-box, and she had told herself it was safety.

The fiddler outside had moved on to a slower tune. Chestnut smoke. Lamplight. Ordinary Vael, humming its ordinary song.

And then the stars went out.

---

It happened while she was watching the window, which is the only reason she trusted what she saw—because she was already watching, already braced, already half-expecting the night to misbehave.

Above the roofline of Candlewright Street, above the crooked chimneys and the washing-lines and the bell tower of Saint Aldhelm's-in-the-Marsh, the sky was doing what autumn skies in Vael always did: burning low orange at the horizon, going to deep blue, pricking out its few brave stars between the chimney pots. There were never many stars over the city. Smoke and lamplight saw to that. But there were always some—three or four hard bright ones above the north roofs, the kind sailors steered by, the kind her mother had called by names Odile had never learned.

They went out the way lamps go out when a draft moves down a street. Not one, then another. All together. One instant: four stars, steady, indifferent. Next instant: nothing. A sky like the inside of a closed box, black and total, from horizon to horizon, and below it thirty thousand people who had never looked up in their lives and therefore did not see it happen.

Odile saw it happen.

She was on her feet without remembering standing. Her teacup was on the floor—it must have fallen; there was tea spreading across the boards, dark as ink, and she couldn't remember hearing it break—and she was at the window with both hands on the sill, leaning out into the market noise and onion-smoke, staring up into a sky with no stars in it at all.

Not clouded. Not smoked-over. *Gone.* The way a word goes when you've repeated it too many times. The way a face goes in a dream.

Down the street, the fiddler played on. The chestnut seller argued on. A woman laughed outside the Broken Compass, high and bright and utterly unafraid. Nobody else had looked up. Nobody else had been watching the sky the way a person watches a thing they have spent twelve years being afraid of.

But the beggar who slept in the chapel porch across the street—he had seen. He was standing in front of Saint Aldhelm's with his neck craned back and his mouth open, and even at this distance Odile could see him making the gesture, the old one, thumb pressed to sternum, the warding sign that respectable Vaelers had laughed out of fashion a generation ago.

And further down, near the mouth of the street where the market thinned toward the river, a figure in Warden gray had stopped walking. Was standing very still. Was looking up.

Was turning, unhurried, methodical, a man consulting something at his belt—a pendant, a lens, a small glass thing that caught the amber streetlight and threw back a color that was not amber at all but a deep banked orange, ember-colored—and beginning, with the terrible patience of officialdom, to walk up Candlewright Street. Past stall after stall. Past the fiddler. Toward her door.

Odile drew in the shutters. She blew the lamp. She stood in the dark of her own bedroom, heart slamming, and thought with the cold tidy part of her mind that had carried her through every bad night since she was nineteen:

*They can't know it's me. Nobody knows it's me. I am nobody. That is the entire point of me.*

But the wards were down, and the wards had been the point of her, and down the street, boots on cobbles, unhurried, coming closer, and the last thought she had before she began, in the dark, moving quietly, gathering the things a person gathers when invisibility stops working—coin, knife, her mother's ledger, the jar of dregs she had sworn she'd never need—

The last thought was this: *Twelve years of small magic, and it ends on an ordinary Tuesday, over burnt tea, with the stars gone out like snuffed candles.*

The knock, when it came, was very polite.

* * *

Three slow raps, spaced like a man counting.

Odile did not move. Below her, through the floorboards, she heard the market absorb the sound and keep singing—Vael was very good at not hearing things—and then a voice, level and official and not unkind, which was somehow worse:

"Shopkeeper. Crown Warden. A moment of your time."

She looked down at her hands in the dark. Steady again. Good. Hands first; the rest of her could follow.

She took the stairs down slowly, lit the lamp behind the counter as if she'd just remembered closing-up chores, and opened the door on its chain the way any cautious tradeswoman would, letting the light fall out onto the cobbles and the man standing in it.

Gray coat, silver braid, boots clean despite the market muck. Younger than his voice, older than his eyes—those had gone careful and early, the eyes of a man who read people for a living. On his breast, the Crown's warrant-badge: a tower stamped in dull lead.

And at his throat, on a chain, half-hidden by his collar, a pendant of cloudy glass the size of a plum stone, and inside the glass something glowed. Faint. Patient. The exact banked-orange of a fire that has been told to wait.

Ember-glass. She didn't know how she knew the word. She only knew that her mouth had gone dry and some locked room at the bottom of her had rattled its handle once, hard, and gone still.

"Warden," she said, and put a shopkeeper's apologetic tiredness into it. "I'm closed. If it's about the stall tax, I paid at the—"

"It's not about tax." He produced a notebook, but didn't open it. "Halvard. Second Circle Wardens. We're making inquiries about unlicensed charmwork in this district." His gaze moved past her, unhurried, cataloguing her shelves: the luck-knots, the sailor-charms, the rows of pretty glass bottles of colored water that any honest woman would know were only colored water. "You sell charms, Mistress...?"

"Winterbourne. Odile Winterbourne." She let the chain stay on the door one heartbeat longer than politeness strictly allowed, then lifted it, because a guilty woman would have kept it on. "Charms, yes. Love-luck and safe passage and cures for warts. If the Crown wants its cut on the superstition trade, Warden, you're late—the tithe men took their share this morning."

One corner of his mouth acknowledged the joke without warming to it. "May I ask where you were at eight bells?"

"Here. Closing up." True. "Counting my till, same as every night." Also true. She had learned from her mother that the best lie is a house built mostly of truth, with one wall left off. "Why? What happened at eight bells?"

"You tell me."

"The stars went out." She said it flatly, wearily, the way one says *it rained.* "Half the market saw it. The other half will claim they did by morning. Is that illegal now too?"

For a moment—one breath, two—Halvard looked at her with those careful early eyes, and she understood that he was reading her the way she read a customer deciding whether to buy, weighing want against price, and that if he decided wrong about her, the price would be paid in a cell under the Second Circle.

Then the pendant at his throat pulsed. Just once. A slow brightening, like a coal breathed on.

He glanced down at it. She watched him glance down at it. He saw her watching.

"Keep your shutters barred, Mistress Winterbourne," he said, closing the notebook he had never opened. "Strange nights bring strange company." He stepped back into the street, and paused, and added, without turning: "You didn't ask what charmwork we were inquiring about. Most people ask."

"Most people are curious," Odile said. "I'm tired."

He walked away down Candlewright Street, gray coat swallowing the lamplight, and the ember-glass at his throat glowed against his back like a small red eye that did not need to blink.

She chained the door. She leaned against it. Above the shop, through the little skylight over her stair, she could see the sky where the stars had been, black and blank as a page with everything written on it scraped away.

And far off—past the walls, past the marshes, out of the north, where no stars shone anymore to see it—something howled. Long, ragged, human-shaped grief in a wolf's throat, and underneath it, fainter, stranger, a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard: a low resonant hum, like a struck bell, like a word half-remembered, like a shard of something calling a name.

Her name.

Odile Winterbourne stood in the dark of her shop with her hand pressed flat against the door, listening to the silence where her wards used to be, and understood, with perfect and terrible clarity, that the life of no consequence was over.

Whatever was coming for her was already inside the walls.
