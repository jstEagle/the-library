## 2. Twelve Years of Small Magic

The morning after the stars went out, Odile Winterbourne did what she always did: she opened the shop.

She swept the step. She set the bell jar of luck-charms in the window where the early light could catch the copper wire coiled inside each one. She ground dried rowan bark with a mortar that had belonged to three guildmistresses before her, and she told herself, as the pestle turned and turned, that the sky was simply overcast. That Vael sat under a low gray ceiling of cloud, as it often did in autumn, and that the absence overhead was weather and nothing more.

But she had sold a fog-charm to a fishwife only last week, and Odile knew cloud when she saw it. This was not cloud. This was a held breath. The whole sky pressed down flat and starless even at noon, and the light it gave was thin and sourceless, coming from everywhere and nowhere, like light remembered rather than made.

Customers came anyway. They always came. Fear was good for business, though she hated herself a little for knowing it.

"Something for sleep," said a cooper's widow, wringing her hat in her hands. "Since the—since they went away. I lie awake counting nothing."

Odile reached past her to the third shelf, where the gentle things lived, and her hand paused on a sachet of valerian and chamomile bound in blue thread. Ordinary herbs. Ordinary knots. She could have sold it as it was, and the woman would have slept, because most of sleeping is permission.

Instead Odile took it into the back room, and there, in the little warded alcove she had built behind the flour sacks, she breathed on the sachet and whispered into the weave the smallest charm she knew. Not a working, really. A suggestion. A thread of warmth laid along the fabric's grain, so faint that any Warden's glass would read it as body heat, as living breath, as nothing.

Small magic. The guild's last lesson, and its first.

She wrapped the sachet in paper and charged the widow four coppers, and when the woman left clutching it like a psalm, Odile stood a long moment in the doorway watching her go, one hand pressed flat against the doorframe, and felt again that ache—that reaching—she had felt the night before, when the stars went out all at once, like candles snuffed by a passing hand, and instead of screaming, the city had simply gone on, and something inside Odile had risen toward the dark sky like a dog straining at a leash, wanting—I want—

Wanting what? She could never finish the sentence.

* * *

Twelve years ago, the Charmwrights' Guild had occupied a hall of pale stone on Candlemaker's Row, with arched windows and a library of nine thousand volumes and forty-one registered members whose names were carved in the lintel by the door.

When Odile joined, at seventeen, there were six members and a leaking roof.

She had come to Vael from the fens with her mother's second-best dress and a letter of introduction written by a hedge-priest who owed her family money. The letter said she had *the knack*. In the fen country this meant she could coax cream from milk that wanted to turn, calm a cow's kicking with a hummed note, find lost thimbles. Small things. Fen-things. Her mother had called it *the family way*, and spoken of it the way one speaks of flat feet or a weak chin—inherited, incurable, best not mentioned in company.

In Vael, the knack was illegal. Had been for sixty years, since the Statutes of Unification. Magic practiced outside Crown charter was called *wildwork*, and wildwork earned you the post, or the mines, or the sea-wall gangs, depending on your health and the magistrate's mood. Charms were still sold openly—the law pretended not to see them, because half the palace bought love-sachets and headache-knots itself—but the making of them had to be licensed, and licenses had not been issued in a generation except to the Guild of Court Artificers, which was to charms what a tombstone was to a door.

Odile learned all this in her first week, standing in the leaky guildhall while Guildmistress Sereth Corvane counted the six of them aloud, twice, as if hoping the number might improve with attention.

"You are the last intake," Sereth said. She was a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of dishwater, and she looked at Odile the way a farmer looks at the last seed potato in a blighted year. "You should understand what that means. It does not mean you are the future. It means we are going to teach you how to disappear."

"I came to learn charmwork," Odile said. "Properly. The old craft."

Sereth laughed—not unkindly, but with a weariness that had calcified into structure, like coral. "Child, the old craft is why there are six of us. Sit down. Lesson one."

Lesson one was this: *Never be seen doing anything.*

Not *never be caught*. Caught implied a chase, evidence, a story worth telling. Sereth meant something stranger and more total: the art of the Charmwrights in their last age was invisibility. You worked so small that no working ever existed to be found. You charmed the wool, never the sweater; the yeast, never the loaf; the hour of the night-watchman's nap, never the watchman. Your magic lived in margins, in preconditions, in the hundredth part of a hundredth part, and if anyone asked how your luck-charms worked, you smiled and said *copper wire and good intentions, mostly*, and you believed it a little yourself, because the safest hiding place for a craft is inside its own disappearance.

"Why bother?" Odile had asked, in her second month, when Sereth taught her to split a single warmth-charm across nine candle stubs so that no one stub burned brighter than tallow had a right to. "If we're this good at nothing, why do anything?"

Sereth had gone very still, in the way Odile would later learn meant an old wound being carefully stepped around. Then she said: "Because somewhere north of here there is a border, and along that border there are wards older than the Crown, and someone has to keep a lamp lit near them. And because—" and here her voice changed, softened, became almost young— "because the work matters, Odile. Even small. Especially small. A thing kept alive at any size is alive. You'll understand someday, or you won't, and either way you'll sweep the step tomorrow morning."

Odile had not understood. She understood the technique, the tradecraft, the folding of fire into fractions—but underneath the lessons she felt a hunger with no object, a homesickness for a home she'd never had. Sometimes, grinding rowan bark, her hands would move through a sequence too complex for the charm at issue, some elaborate knotting she'd never been taught, and Sereth would watch from across the workroom with that stillness again, and say nothing, and make a mark in a ledger.

Once—only once—Odile had asked about the ledger. About the tallies Sereth kept against her name, week after week, in columns headed with symbols Odile didn't recognize.

"Inventory," Sereth said.

"Of what?"

"Debts," Sereth said, and closed the ledger, and asked her to mind the shop.

* * *

Presently, the bell over the shop door rang, and Odile came out from the back wiping her hands, already arranging her face into shopkeeper's mildness—and stopped.

A boy stood in the doorway, maybe ten, mud to his knees, holding out a folded paper. He wouldn't come past the threshold. His eyes went around the shop the way a bird's eyes go around a room, quick, corner-to-corner, looking for the open window.

"You're Odile Winterbourne," he said. Not a question. "My gran says you fixed her hip."

"Your gran says I gave her a liniment that helped, and that's a different thing." Odile took the paper. It was cheap stock, the kind used for handbills, and folded twice. "Who sent you?"

"A man. Paid me a copper. Said wait till daylight." The boy shrugged, feral and indifferent. "Stars came back for a minute last night. My da saw. Down by the river. One star, then gone again." He said it casually, the way children report the impossible, and Odile's hands went cold around the paper.

"One star," she repeated.

"Then it went out again." He glanced at the shelves, calculating, and she gave him a copper and a luck-knot for his trouble, and he ran.

She unfolded the paper inside. On it, in a cramped hand she almost recognized, was a single line:

*The guildhall stands. Go tonight. Bring nothing you love.*

And beneath the words, drawn small and precise, a symbol she had last seen in the columns of Sereth Corvane's ledger.

* * *

She had been nineteen when Sereth died. Eleven years ago now. The other five members had scattered or passed in the ordinary ways—two fevers, one marriage into respectability, one arrest that nobody spoke of, one simply gone north one day with his tools and no goodbye. By the end it was Odile and Sereth in the hall on Candlemaker's Row, keeping the lamps lit, sweeping the step, selling small magic in smaller pieces.

Sereth died in autumn, sitting up in her chair with the ledger open on her lap, and her last coherent act—witnessed by the physician's apprentice who'd come too late—was to close it and hold it shut with both hands, and say, clearly, *burn this.*

The apprentice, being honest and poor, had brought it to Odile instead, expecting perhaps a reward.

Odile had stood in the deathroom holding the ledger—her debts, her inventory—and known with a certainty that arrived from somewhere below thought that if she opened it, her life would stop being the life she was living. She felt it physically, that pull, the same leash-tug she'd felt beneath the vanished stars: something enormous standing just behind her shoulder, patient, waiting to be turned toward.

She had burned it. Every page, in the guildhall's great cold hearth, alone, while rain came through the roof. She remembered feeding the pages in one at a time, watching symbols curl and blacken, and feeling—with grief she couldn't account for and so converted, neatly, into relief—like a woman paying off a debt she didn't remember taking, and calling the payment freedom.

Three days later the Crown's surveyors condemned the hall. Structural failure, they said. She'd signed the transfer papers in a clerk's office smelling of dust and sealing wax, taken her share of the guild's remaining stock, and opened her little shop on Marrow Lane, and begun the life she had lived ever since: sweeps, sales, small magic, sleep.

Eleven years of it. And every year, the leash pulled a little shorter, and the wanting-with-no-object got louder, until she had learned to sleep through it the way you learn to sleep through a churchbell.

*Burn this,* Sereth had said. But the paper in her hands now was not ash. Someone had saved something. Or copied something. Or Sereth herself had arranged, in her last deliberate days, for seeds to be planted in future soil.

*Bring nothing you love.*

Odile looked around the shop—at the shelves she had built, the jars, the copper wire, the little alcove behind the flour sacks that was the closest thing to a home her craft had ever owned—and understood the instruction perfectly, and hated it.

Night came early under the dead sky. She lit no lamp. She locked the shop and walked through streets gone strange with starlessness, past the taverns where people drank with their collars up and the wardens' patrol lanterns burning a shade too bright, compensating. Candlemaker's Row stood dark and half-deserted these years, and the guildhall—she had not let herself think of it in its true name for a decade—was supposed to be rubble.

It was not rubble.

It rose against the lightless sky exactly as memory had kept it: pale stone, arched windows, whole. Only the lintel above the door was wrong. The carved names of the forty-one members had been filled with something dark, and as Odile climbed the steps she saw that the carving had changed—forty names chiseled over, effaced, struck through with mason's strokes. One remained untouched, at the end, freshly recut, the grooves still sharp-edged and pale:

**ODILE WINTERBOURNE — FINAL SIGNATORY — THE EMBER COVENANT**

Her own handwriting's rhythm in the letters, though she had never held a chisel in her life.

Inside, the hall smelled of dust and, beneath the dust, of something older and warmer—woodsmoke, pine, wet fur, a scent that made her throat close without reason. On the master's desk, in the workroom where Sereth had taught her to fold fire into fractions, lay a ledger. Bound in gray leather. Closed. On its cover, embossed in tarnished silver, the same symbol from the boy's paper.

Odile stood over it a long time. Outside, somewhere below the dead stars, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then the whole city's dogs, all at once, all in the same direction—north—and she thought absurdly, with her hand already on the ledger's cover: *something is coming through.*

She opened the book.

Thirteen lines. Thirteen embers, listed like an inventory of the sun. And at the bottom of the final page, dated twelve years ago to the season, in ink that had not faded a shade:

*I, Odile Winterbourne, being the last, do sign.*
