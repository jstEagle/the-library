## 3. The Warden at the Door

The knock came at half past nine, which was wrong in three ways.

First, no one honest knocked on a charm-shop door after dark in Vael; they came during market hours, haggling over luck-knots and fever-charms like everyone else. Second, it was three measured raps, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who had never once had to ask to be let in anywhere. Third—and this was the one that made Odile's hands go still over the counter, mid-wrap of a dried lavender bundle—the shop's little bell above the door had not rung.

She had charmed that bell herself, six years ago, to sing when anyone crossed her threshold with ill intent. It had a voice like a struck wineglass, high and sweet and impossible to miss.

It said nothing now. Which meant either the bell had finally died of neglect, or the man outside carried enough Crown iron to smother a charm before it could draw breath.

Odile set down the lavender. She wiped her palms on her apron out of habit, then took the apron off entirely—a shopkeeper expecting a customer kept an apron on; a woman answering her door at night wanted to look interrupted, inconvenienced, ordinary. She banked the lamp low so the light fell warm and amber instead of bright and revealing, and she opened the door on its chain, just far enough to show one eye and the edge of her frown.

"Yes?"

"Citizens' inquiry." The voice was deep, unhurried, and pitched to carry no farther than her doorway. "Warden Halvard, Third Circle. May I come in, mistress...?"

"Winterbourne." She let the pause stretch a beat too long, the way a tired woman would. "It's nearly ten o'clock, Warden."

"It is." He did not shift his weight, did not glance away, did not do any of the small things guilty men do. He simply stood there under the guttering streetlamp, rain beading on the shoulders of a gray cloak cut in the severe style of the Crown's Wardens, and waited with the patience of a man being paid by the hour to wait.

She unhooked the chain because refusing would have been worse than admitting him. The hinges complained; she'd have to oil them, if there was an afterward to any of this.

He stepped inside and stopped, as she'd known he would, in the middle of her shop—not touching anything, but reading everything. Charmwrights' shops were cluttered places by design: bundles of herbs drying from the rafters, jars of river-stones and grave-dirt and salt from four different seas, wax seals cooling in rows, a workbench scarred by twenty years of knives and candle-drips. To a casual eye it was chaos. To Halvard's eye, she suspected, it was a ledger, and he was already adding columns.

"Small shop," he said.

"Small trade."

"Mm." His gaze moved along the shelves. "Luck-knots. Fever-wards. Hearth-blessings." He named each item without picking it up, and each name landed precisely where it should, which told her he knew exactly what he was looking at. "All licensed work. All guild-stamped."

"The Charmwrights' Guild has stamped my goods for eleven years," Odile said. "You can check the rolls."

"I have checked the rolls." He turned then, and she got her first real look at his face in the lamplight, and wished she hadn't, because it was not a cruel face, and that made everything harder. He was perhaps fifty, weathered brown, with a jaw like a dock piling and eyes the flat gray of a winter harbor. There were lines around those eyes that might have come from squinting into wind—or from long practice at looking at people while deciding what they were worth. "Mistress Winterbourne. You joined the Guild eleven years ago. Before that?"

"Before that I was nobody."

"Everyone is somebody somewhere."

"Not me, Warden. I'm from up-country. A farm near Marrow Ford, if you know it. Nothing there but mud and cousins. I came to Vael because mud doesn't sell and charms do." She let a little bitterness into it, the true flavor of a life spent staying small. The best lies, her first guildmistress had taught her, are built on load-bearing truths. *Give them something real to stand on, and they'll trust the whole floor.*

Halvard nodded slowly, as though filing it. Then, mildly, terribly: "There hasn't been a farm near Marrow Ford in thirty years. The ford silted. The village moved east."

"Then I'm from wherever it moved," Odile said, and smiled, tired and wry, the smile of a woman who had answered this question from tax collectors and census-takers and one memorable drunk with a grudge. "Warden, do you make a habit of auditing every charm-seller in the Lantern Quarter? Because if so, you'll be busy until spring, and I'll want compensation for the interruption."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite amusement. Recognition, maybe—the look of a man who had expected a rabbit and found something with slightly sharper teeth.

"I make a habit," he said, "of following signatures."

* * *

He did not sit when she offered him a stool. He moved through the shop instead, slow, hands clasped behind his back like a man touring a museum of things he already owned, and Odile drifted with him, keeping the counter between them where she could, her heart doing a quiet, disciplined drumbeat against her ribs.

*Signatures.* Every charm left one. That was the first law of the craft and the first danger of it: magic was work, and work left marks, the way a saw leaves sawdust, the way a pen leaves ink. Licensed charms carried the Guild's stamp woven into the working itself, a signature within a signature, and the Crown's Wardens could read those stamps the way priests read scripture. Unlicensed magic—*wildwork*, the law called it, with the contempt baked into the word—left a different mark. Rougher. Warmer. A smell of woodsmoke where there should be candlewax.

And last night, when the stars had gone out all at once like snuffed candles, when her wards had screamed down her arms and died, Odile had done something she had not done in twelve years.

She had rebuilt them. Quickly. Desperately. Without a stamp, without a guild-form, without thinking, because the night had felt like a held breath and something old in her body had known what to do before her mind caught up. Wildwork, hot and fast, laid down in the dark of her own back room while the sky above Vael stood black and starless and wrong.

She had told herself no one would feel it. One woman's wards, in one narrow shop, among ten thousand charms in a city full of licensed magic. A drop in the river.

But Wardens followed signatures. He had said it the way other men said *good morning*.

"Strange night last night," Halvard remarked. He had stopped before the window, looking out at the rain, at the street where the lamps burned and the stars did not. "I don't suppose you noticed anything."

"Everyone noticed something," Odile said. "The whole city's talking about it. Priests are calling it a judgment. Fishwives are calling it an omen. My neighbor swears it's the King's astronomers playing tricks with mirrors." She shrugged. "Me, I think the sky owes us all a few bad nights. We've had a good run."

"You're not frightened."

"Should I be?"

"Most people are." He turned from the window. In the low lamplight his face was half shadow, and the shadow made him harder to read, which she suspected was the point of the lamp being low—except the lamp was hers, wasn't it? She had banked it herself. For her own reasons. "Frightened people talk. They tell you what they saw, what they felt, what their grandmother said about the last time the stars went dark. You're telling me jokes."

"My grandmother died before I could walk, Warden. Jokes are what's left."

He looked at her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere down the street a dog began to howl, thin and rising, and stopped abruptly as if someone had hushed it.

"Three nights ago," Halvard said, "a fishmonger on Sable Row sold a charm that wasn't guild-stamped. Small thing. A cramp-ward for his wife's hands. He carved it himself from his grandfather's notes. Do you know what happened to him?"

Odile kept her face pleasant and empty. "No."

"A fine. Confiscation of tools. Six months on the work-gangs repairing the sea-wall." Halvard's voice didn't change at all; it stayed level as a frozen pond, which somehow made it worse. "For a cramp-ward. For love, really—he made it because his wife couldn't hold a knife anymore. The law doesn't care why. Wildwork is wildwork. The last time unlicensed magic spread through this city, we lost the border provinces. Everyone remembers that, even if they pretend they don't." He paused. "So when a signature turns up on my instruments that reads like nothing I've seen in twenty years of service—old work, deep work, the kind of work that hasn't been done since the Guilds thinned out—I take an interest. Professionally speaking."

"That sounds very exciting," Odile said. "I hope you find whoever it was."

"So do I." And here he did something small and deliberate: he reached up and drew out, from beneath the gray collar of his uniform, a cord. On the cord hung a pendant.

Odile's breath went shallow and careful, the way it does when you step onto ice and hear it speak.

The pendant was a teardrop of glass, no bigger than her thumbnail, and it was not empty. Deep in its heart, something glowed—a grain of light, amber-red, pulsing with a rhythm too slow to be a heartbeat and too steady to be a flame. Ember-glass. She knew it the way you know a face from a dream: not from any living memory, but from somewhere older, some room in her mind she had never been given the key to. Ember-glass was supposed to be legend. Guild-fable. The stuff the old Charmwrights had whispered about before the Guild thinned to a handful of graybeards and a ledger no one read. Captured fire from the border pacts, the stories said. Sealed light from a bargain older than the Crown.

It should not exist. It certainly should not hang around the neck of a Crown Warden, tucked under his collar like a saint's medal.

"Why do you wear that?" she asked, and heard too much curiosity in her own voice, and hated herself for it.

Halvard glanced down at it as if he'd forgotten it was there. "Evidence," he said. "From a case. Long ago." He tucked it away again, and the gesture was quick—quicker than a man moves with something meaningless. "It reminds me that magic always costs more than the seller admits. Whatever you buy in a shop like this, mistress, someone paid for it. Remember that."

"I sell luck-knots," Odile said. "The cost is two coppers."

"For the knot." He smiled then, briefly, and it did not reach the harbor-gray eyes. "Yes."

* * *

He asked his questions for another quarter hour, and they were good questions, patient questions, the questions of a man laying stones across a stream one at a time to see which ones held. Where had she trained? *(Under Guildmistress Corvane, dead these six years, conveniently.)* Had she ever worked outside her license? *(Once, as an apprentice, a birthing-ward for a neighbor—it was in the Guild's dispensation records, go look.)* Did she know anyone who dealt in unlicensed charms? *(Everyone knows someone, Warden; I know a boy who sells "dragon scales" that are painted fish-skins, if that counts.)*

She lied well. She had spent twelve years learning to lie well, though she had never thought of it that way—she had thought of it as learning to be small, to be dull, to be a woman whose whole soul fit inside a shop the size of a closet. But it was lying, all of it, the flat voice and the tired smile and the invented farm near a drowned ford, and she was better at it than she had ever wanted to be.

And through all of it, some animal part of her mind kept circling back to the pendant. To the grain of sealed fire beating in its glass heart like a second, slower pulse in the room.

Because here was the thing she had not said, the thing she would not say even inside her own skull except sideways, in the dark: when Halvard had drawn it out, Odile's wards—the new ones, the wild ones, the ones she'd laid down bleeding magic at midnight—had *sung*. Not aloud. Underneath. A single low note of recognition, like a dog hearing its name in a stranger's mouth. Her magic knew that pendant. Her magic *leaned toward it*, the way a sunflower leans at noon.

Which meant the pendant was not merely evidence. Evidence didn't call to wards across a room. Whatever slept in that teardrop of glass was kin to whatever she had knotted into her walls last night—and kin, moreover, to something deeper, something in her bones, some inheritance she had no memory of receiving.

The stars had gone out. A Warden wore captured fire around his neck and called it evidence. And Odile Winterbourne, last apprentice of a dying guild, keeper of luck-knots and fever-wards, stood in her lamp-lit shop telling beautiful lies while her own blood hummed traitorously toward the enemy's throat.

*What are you?* she thought at the hidden pendant, at the man wearing it. *And what am I, that I want to answer?*

* * *

At last Halvard stopped asking questions, and the silence he left was a kind of question too.

"Well." He settled his cloak on his shoulders. "Thank you for your time, Mistress Winterbourne. You've been helpful."

"I've been awake past my bedtime," Odile said, moving to open the door. "Is that the same thing?"

"In my experience, helpfulness and exhaustion look identical." He paused on the threshold, one hand on the frame, and the rain came in cold on the draft. "One more thing. Idle curiosity. Your wards—the licensed ones on your shop. When did you last renew them?"

The question dropped through her stomach like a stone through still water.

"Last month," she said. "Guild schedule. Why?"

"No reason." He looked past her, into the shop, at the walls she had re-woven in secret at midnight with magic old enough to make his instruments sing. His expression gave away nothing at all, and that nothing was louder than an accusation. "Only that your shop feels..." He searched for the word, and chose it with care. "*Well-kept.* More than the license requires. Good night, Mistress Winterbourne. Lock your door. These are strange nights for small shops."

He stepped out into the rain. The street swallowed him, gray cloak gone gray-black, boots unhurried on the cobbles. He did not look back, which frightened her more than if he had.

Odile shut the door. Shot the bolt. Stood with her forehead against the wet-cold wood and breathed until her hands stopped shaking.

Then she lit a candle, carried it to the back room, and pulled aside the rug to look at the ward she'd laid there—her midnight work, her sin, her signature. It gleamed faintly under the candlelight, threads of warm gold knotted into patterns she did not remember learning, in a style older than the Guild's stamps, older maybe than the Guild itself. Her hands had known it. Her hands had known it the way they knew how to bleed.

*Old work,* Halvard had said. *Deep work. The kind that hasn't been done since the Guilds thinned.*

He was hunting a signature. He had walked into her shop and stood three feet from it all evening, and she did not know whether he had found it or not, and his face had been a locked door, and around his neck, under Crown gray, a piece of the old world's fire had pulsed and pulsed and called to her like kin.

Outside, far off toward the river, a dog began to howl again—that same thin, rising note—and this time it did not stop. Other dogs took it up, street by street, a ragged chorus spreading through the dark of Vael, and Odile stood alone in her shop with a candle in her hand, listening, feeling the wards on her walls shiver like struck strings.

Something was coming over the water. Something the dogs smelled and the stars had fled.

And the ember-glass at the Warden's throat had sung to her blood as if it had been waiting twelve years to say her name.
