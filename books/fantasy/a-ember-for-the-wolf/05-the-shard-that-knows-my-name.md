## 5. The Shard That Knows My Name

The wolf-kin bled on my floor for the better part of an hour before I found my hands again.

That's not quite true. My hands had been moving the whole time—pressing cloth to the gash along his ribs, knotting tourniquets, doing the small competent work of a woman who had patched cut palms and scalded wrists for twelve years without ever once being asked what she was really good for. It was the rest of me that had gone somewhere else entirely, standing back in a corner of my own skull, watching, while some older and colder part of me took over the triage.

He was enormous even half-collapsed against my counter, his fur going gray at the muzzle, his breathing a wet, labored rasp that I did not like at all. The wound was deep—not a blade wound, or not only a blade wound. The edges of it were seared smooth, cauterized by something that had burned as it cut, and around the burn the skin had gone a strange pale silver, like frost blooming under the surface. Warden-steel, I thought, though I'd never seen what Warden-steel did to anything but charms and the people who made them.

"Stop looking at it," he growled, when he caught me staring. His voice was rough gravel soaked in river water. "You'll sew your fingers into it."

"I've been mending things since before you were whelped, whatever you are."

"Wolf-kin." He said it the way a man says his own name at a trial. "And you're the charmwright."

"I'm a shopkeeper."

"You're a signatory." His eyes—they were the color of wet slate, gray shot through with something darker, something almost like grief—fixed on me with an intensity that made my skin crawl. "You just don't know it yet."

I should have thrown him out. I want to be honest about that, all these years later; I want it on the record that some rational part of me stood at the door of my own shop with its hand on the latch and understood, clearly, that harboring a wounded wolf-kin three days after the stars went out was the fastest road in Vael to a Warden's cell, or worse, a Warden's question room. The rational part of me understood perfectly.

But there is a thing that happens in the body that has nothing to do with reason. When he spoke the word *signatory*, something behind my sternum turned over—slowly, heavily, like a key too large for its lock—and every hair on my arms rose, and my mouth said, "Sit still. You'll tear the stitching," before my mind had decided anything at all.

So he sat still. And I stitched. And the shard of stone he carried—the one that had hummed, that had *sang*, when I came near it—sat wrapped in oilcloth on my counter between us, radiating a warmth I could feel from four feet away, like a hearthstone left banked overnight.

I told myself I would not touch it.

* * *

Here is what I knew about covenant-stone, which is to say here is what I knew about nothing: the Charmwrights' Guild had kept, in its better days, a single fragment of it locked in an iron box beneath the guildhall floor, and no apprentice in living memory had ever been permitted so much as to look at it. Old Master Corvel used to tell us it was the heart of the old pacts, the treaties struck between men and the wild clans before Vael had kings, back when the border was a promise instead of a wall. He told us it sang if the right person stood near it. He told us this the way you tell children about ghosts—to frighten them into obedience, and because he half-believed it himself.

Then the guild died its slow death, and the iron box went into the sealed archive, and the archive went into the Crown's custody during the Reforms, and everyone agreed it was better that way. Safer. Small magic for small people. I had built a whole life on the wisdom of staying small.

The shard on my counter was maybe the size of a man's fist, black shot through with veins of ember-orange that pulsed—actually pulsed, slowly, in time with something. I didn't let myself think about what it might be in time with until much later, when I noticed that my own pulse matched it exactly.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

He didn't answer. His eyes had closed. For one lurching moment I thought I'd killed him after all, that I'd spent an hour saving a dead man, and I reached out—stupidly, instinctively, my hand already moving before thought caught up—

And my palm came down flat on the bare shard.

* * *

I have tried, in the years since, to describe what happened next, and I have never once succeeded. Language was built by people who had never held a piece of their own stolen life in their hand. But I will try anyway, because trying is all I have.

It burned. That part is simple. It burned like a coal, like a brand, like the forge-tongs my father used to pull from the fire when I was six and too curious for my own good—I felt that childhood burn again inside the new one, pain stacked on pain, memory folded inside memory, which should have been my first warning of what the thing actually was. I heard my own voice scream. It sounded very far away, and very young.

And then the burning opened, the way a door opens, and I fell through it.

I was standing on a wall.

Not any wall. *The* wall—the border wall, the great gray spine of it running east to west beyond the marshes, taller than any building in Vael, older than any king. I knew it instantly and totally, the way you know your own face in a mirror, and that knowledge itself was wrong, was impossible, because I had never in my life traveled farther north than the Ash Market. But I knew the exact grain of the stones under my palms. I knew how the wind came off the wild lands smelling of pine and snow and something animal and alive. I knew that if I leaned out past the battlement I would see the forest going on forever, dark and breathing, and that somewhere in it were people who—

Beside me stood a man.

I cannot tell you his face. This is the cruelest part, and I have made my peace with never forgiving it: the memory was mine, whole and vivid, every stone and every wind-smell rendered in perfect fidelity—and where his face should have been there was only a blur, a smear of warm color, like a portrait left out in the rain. I knew he was tall. I knew he wore a travel cloak the color of autumn bracken, and that his hands were scarred across the knuckles, and that one of those hands was holding mine.

His thumb moved against my wrist. Once. A small, absent motion, the kind of touch you give someone you have touched ten thousand times, someone whose pulse you know better than your own.

We were watching the sun come up over the wild lands. We were laughing about something—I could feel the laugh in my chest, feel it shaking loose in both of us, though the sound itself was gone. And I was saying something, or he was; one of us was speaking words that mattered, words that had weight enough to be remembered, and I strained toward the sound of it the way a drowning woman strains toward light through water—

"*—swear it on the ember,*" the blur said, in a voice like low thunder rolling far away. "*Both of us. Whatever comes.*"

And I felt my own mouth open to answer. Felt the vow rising in me like water rising in a well. Felt, underneath it, something else—a cold thread of dread, a knowledge that this vow would cost more than either of us understood—

The image tore.

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees on my own shop floor, my palm smoking, the smell of scorched flesh thick in my nose, and Renn's hands—huge, careful, impossibly gentle for hands with claws—holding my shoulders to keep me from cracking my head on the flagstones.

"Breathe," he was saying. "Charmwright. Breathe."

My palm was blistered in the exact shape of the shard, a perfect negative pressed into my skin, the lines of it already weeping. And across the burn, in fine raised welts like scars that had always been waiting under the surface, ran a line of characters in no alphabet I knew—except that I did know it. Somewhere below knowing. Somewhere in the place the shard had just torn open.

"What did you do to me?" My voice came out shredded.

"Nothing." Something moved behind the slate-gray of his eyes—guilt, I would learn to name it later, guilt wearing the mask of patience. "The stone did what it does. It knows its own."

"It *burned* me—"

"It called you," he said. "There's a difference. There's all the difference in the world." He crouched in front of me, and up close I could see how exhausted he was, how thin the wall of his composure had worn, blood loss and ward-sickness and whatever journey had brought him bleeding through a collapsed border. "What did you see?"

"Nothing. A wall. A—" I stopped. My tongue would not shape it. Saying *a man whose face I can't remember* aloud felt like handing someone a knife handle-first and asking them to hold it steady. "Nothing that matters."

"That's a lie."

"That's my business. I sell charms. Lying is adjacent to the trade."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across his face that was not anger and not suspicion but something achingly closer to tenderness, and it frightened me worse than the burning had. Then it was gone, packed down behind the granite of him, and he said only: "More will come through the broken wards. Wardens first, then worse. If they find that shard in your shop, they will burn this building to its foundations with you inside it, and they will call it law."

"So I should throw it in the river."

"You won't." He said it with terrible certainty, and the worst part, the part I have never confessed to anyone until now, is that I knew he was right. My palm throbbed in the shard's rhythm. My pulse matched its pulse. Some door in me had been opened and it did not close again just because I wished it shut.

"Who are you?" I whispered. "Really. What are you to—that stone. To me."

He held my gaze for a long moment. Outside, the false night pressed against the windows, starless and total, and somewhere down the lane a watchman's bell rang the hour with nobody to see the moon it rang for.

"My name is Renn," he said finally. "And I need somewhere to hide. Somewhere below ground. Do you have a cellar?"

"Why would I possibly—"

Footsteps. Heavy, booted, unhurried, stopping directly outside my shopfront. Through the fogged glass, a lantern-flare swept the street, and with it came a sound I had learned to fear in the guildhall's dying days and had prayed never to hear at my own door: the low, resonant hum of warden-glass sweeping for unlicensed magic.

Renn's ears flattened. My hand, throbbing, curled closed around the shard before I consciously decided to pick it up—and it did not burn me this time. It warmed, like a hand clasping back.

"Cellar," I breathed. "Now. Move."
