## 6. A Wolf in the Cellar

The cellar beneath the Charmwright's Rest had not been swept since before I was born.

I knew this because I was the one who'd been meaning to sweep it for six years, and because the cobwebs above the wine racks hung thick enough to strain soup through. It smelled of dust and old tallow and the ghost of my grandmother's rosemary charms, and it was, as of an hour past midnight, occupied by a bleeding wolf-kin who kept insulting my floorboards.

"You live like this?" Renn lay on a pallet of folded sacking I'd dragged from behind the cask rack, one arm pressed to his ribs, gray eyes tracking the ceiling with the wariness of a man who expected it to fall on him. "This is where you keep your dead?"

"It's where I keep my wine."

"Ah." He glanced at the empty racks. "You drank your way out of a covenant, did you?"

I set the basin down harder than necessary. Water slopped over the rim and pooled between us, catching the light of the single lamp I'd brought down—the good lamp, shuttered to a knife-slit of flame, because the last thing either of us needed was a glow spilling up through the floorboards into a street that would soon be crawling with Wardens.

"Drink," I said, and pushed the cup at him. Willowbark and feverfew, bitter enough to make his ears flatten. He took it anyway, because he was sensible about some things.

He drank. He grimaced. "You're trying to poison me."

"If I were trying to poison you, you'd never know. That's rather the point of poison." I knelt beside him and reached for the ruined edge of his shirt. "Hold still."

"I am holding still."

"You're holding still the way a cat holds still when it's deciding whether to bite."

That earned me something that wasn't quite a smile—more the memory of one, worn smooth by disuse. "You've owned cats?"

"No." I peeled the fabric back from the wound and made myself look. "I've owned customers. Same temperament."

* * *

The wound was worse than I'd let myself believe when he'd collapsed through my back door an hour ago, all teeth and blood and a voice like gravel dragged over iron, saying *hide me* as if it were a request and not a demand. Three gashes raked across his ribs, deep enough that the lowest one showed something I preferred not to name. Claw-marks. Wolf-made, or something shaped enough like a wolf that the distinction hardly mattered.

"What did this?" I asked, threading the needle by lamplight.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if it's going to come looking for you. Things that leave wounds like that tend to have opinions about unfinished meals."

His jaw worked. For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer, and then, quietly: "A ward-hound. One of the Crown's. It caught me at the breach before the wards finished falling."

"A ward-hound crossed the border?" I pulled the first stitch tight and watched him refuse to flinch, which was its own kind of flinch. "Those things are leashed to the Warden-captains. They don't leave Vael's side of the line."

"They don't *used* to." His voice had gone flat, the way voices do when they're carrying more weight than they want to show. "The wards didn't fall, charmwright. They were opened. From your side of the wall."

The needle stopped in my hand.

I thought of the night market two nights past—the moment every star in the sky had gone out at once, all of them, like candles pinched by a single enormous hand. I'd told myself it was a warding failure, an accident, old magic dying of neglect the way everything old died. The Crown's story, repeated in every market stall and tavern since: *the wilds broke the pact; the wilds turned off the stars.*

"You're lying," I said.

"I'm many things, but I'm not that." He sat up despite my glare, wincing, and held my gaze with those pale eyes that seemed lit from somewhere behind. "Your kings wanted the border open. Open borders mean raids, and raids mean the people beg the Crown for protection, and protection means power. Your stars went out so your own city would be afraid of us."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I was there when the order was written." Something moved across his face—grief, or its close cousin—and then it was gone, shuttered away like the lamp. "Sew. You're making it worse by stalling."

I sewed.

* * *

Seven stitches in, he passed out.

Not dramatically—he simply went quiet mid-insult, mid-sentence, something about how my suturing technique resembled a child's attempt at net-mending, and then his head tipped back against the stone wall and his breathing went long and slow. Just like that. As if trust were a door he'd been leaning against all night and finally let himself fall through.

I sat back on my heels and looked at him properly for the first time.

Wolf-kin were stories in Vael. Nursery bogeys. *Mind the treeline, little one, or the wolves will carry you off.* Mothers said it without thinking, the way they said *don't wander near the river,* and children grew up believing the northern wilds were full of monsters with human hands. I had grown up believing it. Every charm I sold at the night market—every anti-wolf sigil, every *safe-passage* knot, every little brass bell meant to warn off kin-ears—had been built on the same foundation: *they are not like us, and what is not like us is dangerous.*

And here was one asleep against my cellar wall with my thread in his skin, and he looked—

Tired. That was the word that kept arriving and refusing to leave. He looked tired the way old soldiers look tired, the way my grandmother had looked tired in her last year, carrying something heavy long after her arms had stopped being asked to carry it. There was silver shot through the black of his hair. A scar split his left eyebrow. His hands, even unconscious, were curled slightly inward, as if ready to catch something falling.

I should have turned him out. I knew that. Every rational part of me—the part that had survived twelve years in Vael by being small, by being forgettable, by selling harmless charms and asking no questions—screamed that harboring a wolf-kin while the Crown hunted whatever had broken the border was suicide wearing the mask of kindness.

But he'd said *your kings opened the wards,* and the terrible thing, the thing I couldn't stitch shut, was that some buried part of me hadn't been surprised.

* * *

He woke at dawn—or what I judged was dawn, down where no light reached—to find me sorting dried herbs at the workbench, mostly because I hadn't slept and needed my hands busy.

"You stayed," he said. His voice was rough with sleep. He sounded almost accusatory about it.

"Don't flatter yourself. If you died down here, I'd have to explain a corpse to whoever comes asking, and I'm very bad at explaining corpses."

"That's a specific skill to be bad at."

"I contain multitudes." I brought him water and bread and hard cheese, and watched him eat with the controlled desperation of someone who'd gone hungry often enough to know better than to bolt it. "Questions first. Then you can sleep again."

"I answered your questions."

"You answered *one.* The rest you deflected with dramatic pauses, which I'll admit were very well done." I sat on an overturned crate across from him, arms crossed. "Who are you? Truly. Not a name—a *what.* Why come to me? Out of every door in Vael, why does a wounded wolf-kin bleed through the back of a failing charm shop?"

For a long moment he only looked at me. The lamplight did something strange to his eyes, turning the gray nearly silver, and I had the uncomfortable sense of being measured against something—a memory, maybe, though whose, I couldn't say.

"You don't remember me," he said at last. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite an accusation either. More like a man confirming a wound he already knew the shape of.

"Should I?"

"No." He looked down at his bandaged ribs, and his thumb moved over them absently, tracing the edge of the linen. "No, you shouldn't. Forget I said it."

"Riveting conversation. Truly, the bards will weep."

Something huffed out of him that might have been a laugh in a former life. "You always—" He stopped. Closed his mouth. Started again, carefully, like a man walking on river ice. "You ask too many questions for someone who sells luck-charms to drunk fishermen."

"And you dodge too well for someone who claims to need help. So we're both frauds. Wonderful. We'll get along famously." I leaned forward. "Here's what I know, wolf. Last night a Warden came to my shop asking questions about illegal magic. This morning I'm hiding a creature the Crown has spent three generations teaching us to fear. Either I'm the biggest fool in Vael, or you know something about those vanished stars that makes the risk worth it. Convince me it's the second one, because the first option keeps me up at night."

He was quiet for so long I thought he'd refuse. Then he reached into the ragged remains of his coat—slowly, telegraphing the movement, which I noted and chose not to comment on—and drew out a wrapped bundle of oilcloth, no bigger than a loaf of bread.

He unwrapped it on his palm.

The shard inside was black stone shot through with veins of dull red, like a coal that had forgotten how to burn. It was ugly. It was unremarkable. And the moment my eyes settled on it, the hair on my arms stood up and the scarred old ache in my right palm—the one I'd had since childhood, the one every healer in Vael had shrugged at—woke up and *pulled.*

Not pain. Pull. Like a fishhook set behind my navel, drawing me toward that ugly little rock with a certainty that had nothing to do with my head.

"Do you feel that?" I whispered.

Renn watched my face with an intensity that bordered on painful. "Feel what?"

"This." I extended my hand toward the shard, stopped myself an inch away. The pull strengthened. My palm throbbed in time with it, an old scar remembering a newer name. "It's humming. Don't tell me you can't hear it—it's practically shouting."

"I can't hear anything." But his voice had changed, gone careful and wondering, and he turned the shard slowly in his fingers, watching me watch it. "It's covenant-stone. From the Ember Covenant—the pact between Vael and the wolf-kin, the one your history books say never existed. The border wards were built on stones like this. Thirteen of them. Embers, we called them." He paused. "When the wards fell, the embers scattered. I've spent twelve years finding them."

"Why?"

"Because they're the only proof of what your kings destroyed. And because—" he hesitated, and I watched him decide, deliberately, to give me less than the whole truth, "—because if the embers aren't rejoined before the thirteenth moon turns, the realms drift apart forever. No crossing. No contact. Ever. Everything on the other side of that wall might as well stop existing."

The cellar felt suddenly smaller. Colder. I thought of the starless sky, of Halvard's ember-glass pendant glinting at his throat, of the way the ache in my palm had followed me my whole life like a debt I couldn't locate.

"And this shard," I said slowly, "does what, exactly?"

"It remembers." He held it out to me, open-palmed, an offering or a test—I genuinely couldn't tell which. "Covenant-stone holds what was sworn on it. Touch it, and it shows you. Most people get nothing. Stone doesn't open for strangers."

"And for me?"

"I don't know," he said. "That's why I came to you."

I stared at the shard. The pull in my palm had become a pulse, steady as a second heartbeat, and underneath the fear—underneath the very reasonable, very survival-oriented fear—was something else. Something that felt disgracefully like *homesickness* for a place I'd never been.

If I touched it, I understood without knowing how, nothing would be the same afterward. Some doors only swing one way.

"Charmwright," Renn said quietly. "Whatever it shows you—it isn't a trick. Whatever else I am, I'm not lying to you about this."

"That's the problem," I heard myself say. "I believe you."

I reached out and touched the stone.

* * *

Fire.

One image, seared white behind my eyes and gone in a heartbeat: a high stone wall under a sky full of stars—all the stars, every one of them burning—and beside me a figure in a gray cloak, close enough that our shoulders touched, close enough that I could feel him breathing. His face was blurred, smeared like wet ink, but I knew—I knew with a certainty older than my own name—that I loved him. That we were standing witness to something enormous. That I had just sworn something I could never take back.

And then it was gone, and I was kneeling on a cold cellar floor with blood running from my nose onto my clenched fist, and Renn was gripping my shoulders, saying my name—my *name*, Odile, which I had never told him—in a voice cracked clean through with something that sounded terrifyingly like grief.

"Odile. Breathe. Look at me. Breathe."

I breathed. The cellar reassembled itself around me: the wine racks, the cobwebs, the shuttered lamp, the wolf-kin with my stitches in his ribs and my name in his mouth.

"How do you know my name?" My voice came out thin.

Something flickered across his face—too fast to read, gone before it landed. "You talk in your sleep," he said. "You were out for a few minutes. You said it."

I hadn't been asleep. We both knew I hadn't been asleep. But I was shaking too hard to fight about it, and he was watching me with an expression I couldn't afford to examine, and outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been dreading all night:

Boots. Many boots. Moving up my street in the gray pre-dawn, unhurried, methodical, the cadence of men conducting a search they intended to finish.

Renn's head lifted. His nostrils flared. "Wardens," he breathed. "Six of them. Maybe seven."

The pull of the shard still sang in my palm. The image of the blurred face still burned behind my eyes. And upstairs, someone knocked on my shop door—three slow raps, polite as a headsman.

"Back room," I whispered, hauling him upright by his good arm. "There's a false panel behind the cask rack—my grandmother built it during the purges. Move."

He moved. And as I banked the lamp and climbed the stairs toward the knocking, one thought rang in me louder than the boots, louder than the shard, louder than fear itself:

*I stood on that wall. I swore that oath. And someone made sure I would never remember who was standing next to me.*
