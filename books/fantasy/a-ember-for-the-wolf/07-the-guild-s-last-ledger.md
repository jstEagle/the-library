## 7. The Guild's Last Ledger

The guildhall of the Charmwrights stood at the end of Tallow Row like a tooth left standing in a broken jaw.

Odile had walked past it a thousand times in twelve years and never once looked up. That was the trick of it—her own trick, she realized now, though she didn't yet have words for what that meant. The building wore its ruin openly: roof half-collapsed into the great workroom, windows boarded with planks gone gray as driftwood, the guild's sign hanging by one chain and turning slowly, patiently, in whatever wind found its way down the row. *Charmwrights' Guild. Licensed by Crown Charter.* The gilt had flaked off the letters until only the word *Guild* remained legible, and even that was more suggestion than fact.

She had told Renn to stay in the cellar, and he had laughed at her—the low, gravel-pitched laugh of someone who found her orders quaint—and said, *Wolves don't take direction from small magicians.* Then he had stayed anyway, because his leg was bound in her good linen and because the shard of covenant-stone in his fist had not stopped humming since dawn, a sound like a struck bell heard through water, and it hummed louder the closer they came to this street.

"It wants you," he'd said at the cellar door, and there had been something in his face she couldn't read—something older than suspicion, older than the wariness between them. "I've carried it through three wards and a river and it has never once made that sound."

"Then it can want all it likes," she'd said. "I'm going because I don't have a choice. Not because a rock asked nicely."

He had watched her climb the stairs with those gray eyes, and just before she reached the top, he'd said something so quiet she almost missed it: "You always did hate being asked nicely."

She'd stood on the bottom step with her hand on the rail, heart doing something strange and slow, and decided not to ask him what he meant by *always*.

* * *

Tallow Row smelled the way it always had—tallow and rain and the sweet rot of the chandler's waste-barrel—but today the smell seemed to come with weight, pressing against her like a hand flat against her sternum. The night market would open in six hours. She should be home grinding moonwort and pricing luck-knots. Instead she was walking toward a building she had spent twelve years not looking at, following a pull in her own chest that felt exactly like the shard's humming sounded.

That frightened her more than Halvard had.

Wardens could be lied to. Stars could come back or not. But when your own body pulled toward a thing like iron toward lodestone, when your feet knew a road your mind had never walked—you were no longer the one holding the reins.

The guildhall doors were chained, but the chain had rusted through at one link, and when Odile touched it, it crumbled into orange dust like something that had been waiting years for permission to let go. The doors themselves swung inward without sound.

Inside, dust hung in shafts of gray light where the roof had opened to the sky. Rain had gotten in over the years; moss furred the edges of the great workbench where forty charmwrights had once worked shoulder to shoulder, and a puddle lay still as glass beneath the hole in the ceiling, reflecting the daylight in a perfect rectangle. Her boots left prints in the dust—prints that went only forward, never back, and she noticed this and hated that she noticed.

"You've been here before," she said aloud, to hear a voice, even her own.

But her body knew the room. Knew that the third floorboard past the threshold gave a half-inch underfoot (it did). Knew that the stairwell to the archive was behind the false panel shaped like an honor-board (she found it without searching). Knew that the air in the archive below was cold and dry and smelled of cedar and old ash, and that if she stretched out her hand in the dark—

Her fingers found the lamp bracket on the wall. First try. In perfect darkness.

Odile stood very still while her heart slammed and her breath came shallow and fast. Somewhere above, wind moved through the broken roof, and the sign on Tallow Row creaked its slow creak, and none of it touched the silence she was standing in.

"There is a reasonable explanation," she whispered. "There is always a reasonable explanation."

She lit the lamp with a match because lighting it with magic felt suddenly, obscenely wrong, and carried it down into the dark.

* * *

The archive was smaller than the guildhall above—two rooms, maybe three, though part of the far wall had collapsed and buried whatever lay beyond under a fan of brick and plaster. Shelves lined the surviving walls, most of them empty. Not emptied in haste: emptied carefully, spines aligned, dust undisturbed on the shelf-edges where books had been lifted straight out and away. Someone had cleaned this place out properly, methodically, years ago. A confiscation, probably. When the guild fell, the Crown would have come for its records the way it came for everything—Halvard's ember-glass pendant swam up in her memory, glowing faintly, and she pushed the thought down.

One shelf alone had been left untouched. It stood in the innermost corner, behind the fallen masonry, accessible only by edging along the wall sideways with the lamp held high—and it was not untouched so much as *unreachable*, unless someone came looking the way she was looking now, guided by something other than sense.

Three objects sat on that shelf.

A candle stub, burned down to a nub, its wax gone amber with age.

A ring of keys, green with verdigris.

And a ledger, bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth, with no title on its spine and no lock on its covers.

Odile picked up the ledger last. Her hands were shaking. She told herself it was the cold.

She sat on the floor with her back against the shelf, because her legs had opinions about standing, and opened the book across her knees.

The first pages were mundane enough to make her laugh—a single, unsteady note. Inventory records. Wax, silver wire, powdered garnet, forty pounds of tallow from the Chandlers' Cooperative, receipt attached. Charmwrights keeping books. The world's dullest conspiracy.

Then, a third of the way in, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Older. And the entries changed with it.

*Item: the Covenant of Ember, being thirteen parts, forged between the City of Vael and the Free Peoples of the North.*

*Let it be recorded: the pact is not paper. Paper burns. The pact is thirteen embers, each holding one true memory of the making, and while the embers burn unbroken, the border holds and both peoples see each other plain.*

*Thirteen embers. Thirteen memories. Let them be scattered, that no one hand may hold them all.*

Odile turned the pages faster. There were diagrams—circles divided into thirteen wedges, each wedge marked with a symbol she almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a face across a market crowd. There were names, columns of them, some struck through with a line and the word *fallen* beside them in a later hand. Signatories. Keepers. People who had died carrying their pieces of a promise.

And then, near the end, a fresh page. The ink darker. The hand—*

She stopped breathing.

She knew that hand. Of course she knew it. She saw it every day, in the price-tags she chalked for the night market, in the little labels she inked for her charm-jars, in the signature she pressed into receipts without thinking: *O. Winterbourne.*

Her own handwriting looked back at her from a page dated twelve years ago.

*Being the last living signatory of the Ember Covenant, I do accept the charge entire. Twelve embers scattered beyond my reach. The thirteenth remains with me, and with me it will remain, for I am done with giving things away.*

*I do further bind myself thus: let my memory of the making be sealed, that no Warden's truth-spell, no torturer's art, no enemy's cunning may draw from me what I carry. What I do not know, I cannot betray.*

*The price is known to me. I pay it freely.*

*Signed,*

*Odile Winterbourne, final signatory of the Ember Covenant*

Below the signature, in fresher ink—fresher by hours, not years, the letters still sharp-edged as if cut yesterday—someone had added a single line:

*The thirteenth ember remembers where the others sleep. Follow the burn.*

The ledger slipped from her fingers.

It didn't fall so much as settle, pages whispering, coming to rest on the dusty floor face-up, and Odile sat with her arms wrapped around her knees and stared at the wall opposite and tried to remember how to be a person.

Final signatory. Twelve years ago. She had done sums in her head a hundred times since the stars went out—counting backward, trying to find the shape of her life—and every sum landed on the same hollow year, the year she could never account for. She had told herself it was grief. She had told herself it was illness. She had built a whole quiet life on top of that missing year the way you build a house over a sinkhole, telling yourself the floors are level.

She was twenty-three years old and the last page of her own history was written in her hand, and she could not remember writing it.

"I paid it freely," she said out loud. Her voice came out wrong, flattened, echoing slightly in the cold room. "What does that mean? What did I pay?"

The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

*Follow the burn,* the fresh ink said. Someone had been here recently. Someone knew she would come. The thought should have terrified her, and it did—but underneath the terror was something worse and stranger: relief. Because if someone had written that line for her, then someone knew. Someone held the other end of the story she had torn herself out of, and Odile discovered, sitting in the ashes of the Charmwrights' Guild, that she wanted it. Whatever it cost. Whatever it was.

She wanted to know who she had been.

Her palm ached. She looked down and saw the old scar across it—the one she'd had as long as she could remember, a thin white seam like a healed burn, the one the fortune-teller at the Ash Market had once frowned at and refused to read. Except now, in the lamplight, the scar wasn't white anymore. It had gone pink. Warm. Faintly luminous, like a coal breathing under skin.

The shard in Renn's fist, three streets away, was humming. She could feel it now—not hear it, feel it, a vibration climbing her bones—and it was pulling, gently, insistently, northward. Toward the marshes. Toward something drowned.

"Follow the burn," she repeated.

She took the ledger. She took the key ring, on the chance that somewhere in Vael there was still a lock these keys loved. She left the candle stub where it lay—then, after a moment, picked that up too, because twelve years of instinct said never leave wax behind, wax is memory made solid, and if there was one thing Odile Winterbourne was learning about herself, it was that she had spent a lifetime handling memories without knowing what they weighed.

At the foot of the archive stairs she paused, the lamp raised, and looked back at the buried far wall. Under the fallen bricks, just visible, a strip of painted plaster: the edge of some old mural, a fragment of blue that might have been sky, and within the blue, a single gold point that might have been a star.

Twelve years, she thought, walking up into the gray light of the ruined hall. Twelve years of staying small. Of cheap charms and careful lies and never looking up at a certain building. Of a scar that never ached and a year that never happened and a life with a wall in it, smooth and painted-over, that she had called contentment because she had no other name for the absence.

She had signed herself empty. And some part of her—the part that kept the shop, that priced the luck-knots, that lied beautifully to Wardens—had crawled out of that emptiness and built a person, and that person was standing in a dead guildhall holding her own handwriting and shaking.

The sign creaked overhead. Somewhere across the city, the noon bells rang, and the sound fell into the streets like stones into water.

Odile climbed the steps to Tallow Row and shut the door behind her, and the chain crumbled again at her touch, obedient, as if the building itself had decided she was allowed to leave.

She was halfway down the row before she saw them: three men in Warden gray, walking the street's far side with the unhurried gait of people following a scent. The tall one in front turned his head as he walked, reading doorways, reading chimneys, reading the air itself the way she'd seen Halvard read a charm-table. And hanging against his gray coat, swinging gently with each step, was a pendant of ember-glass—and inside it, unmistakable even at this distance, a fleck of gold light pulsed. Once. Twice.

In rhythm with the scar on her palm.

Odile stepped back into the doorway of the guildhall, heart hammering, ledger pressed against her ribs, and understood two things at once with terrible clarity: the Crown had not come here today by chance.

And whatever she had sealed away twelve years ago, she was not the only one who wanted it back.
