## 8. Halvard Closes the Net

The charm-signature came in with the morning reports, and Halvard knew before he finished reading it that the night had gone badly for someone.

He sat in the Wardenhouse on Candlemaker's Row, where the light through the leaded windows fell in gray bars across his desk, and read the notation twice. *Residual warding activity, unlicensed, high concentration. District: Lanternmere. Street: Sallow Lane.* The clerk who'd transcribed it had a careful hand, but no transcription could soften what the numbers meant. Someone on Sallow Lane had been holding back the dark with magic strong enough to register at the survey station three streets over — and then, at the moment every star in the sky went out, they had stopped.

Or been stopped.

Halvard set the report down and looked at his hands. They were steady hands, broad, scarred at the knuckles from twenty years of warden work — door-kicks and manhunts and the particular violence of arresting people who were only ever trying to survive. He'd broken fingers with these hands. He'd held weeping mothers back from wagons. He was good at his job, which was the problem, because being good at it meant he could not pretend, even to himself, that he didn't understand exactly what the numbers on this page would cost whoever had made them.

"Ser Halvard." A junior warden stood in the doorway, rain still dripping off his oilskin. Pell, his name was — nineteen, earnest, still flinched at thunder. "The survey captain wants confirmation. Are we moving on Lanternmere?"

"We're moving when I say we're moving." Halvard rose, took his coat from its hook, and settled it across his shoulders. The Warden's badge sat cold against his chest, a circle of iron with the Crown's sun stamped in the center. *We see all things,* the motto went. It was supposed to be comforting. Lately it only felt like a threat aimed inward.

"Get me the district files," he said. "Every licensed practitioner on Sallow Lane. And Pell — quietly. No wagon bells."

The boy blinked. "No bells, ser?"

"No bells."

* * *

Sallow Lane in the rain was a street trying very hard to be unremarkable, and mostly succeeding. Laundry sagged between upper windows. A fishmonger argued with a gull. Children splashed in the gutters with the specific joy of children who did not yet know their street had appeared on a list.

Halvard walked it slowly, without escort, the way he preferred. Wagons and squads got you doors slammed and evidence burned. Walking alone got you the small things: the way a shopkeeper's eyes flicked toward a certain doorway; the fresh scratch of new wards chalked over old ones; the smell, faint under the rain and fish, of ember-dust and tallow — the smell of charmwork done recently and done well.

The licensed practitioners file gave him four names. A hedge-physicist whose registration had lapsed two winters ago. A retired weatherwright who hadn't worked a spell in a decade if her dues ledger was honest. A candle-chandler licensed for blessing-work only, candles that kept moths away and milk from souring. And at the far end of the lane, in a narrow shop with a painted sign reading **WINTERBOURNE CHARMS — Wards, Blessings, Small Mercies**, an Odile Winterbourne, journeyman rank, Charmwrights' Guild, license current.

*Charmwrights' Guild.* Halvard felt the words settle into him like a stone dropped into deep water. The guild had been dying when he was a boy and was dead now, or near enough — dissolved by decree eleven years past, its hall sealed, its members scattered or sworn off. Which meant either the guild's records were wrong, or Odile Winterbourne was carrying a dead guild's license like a lantern carried in daylight: technically lit, meaning nothing, meant to make you look away.

He stopped in front of her shop.

It was a good shop. That was his first impression and he distrusted it immediately, because good shops belonged to people who wanted to be seen, and the signature on his report belonged to someone who had spent years making sure no one looked at all. The window display was modest and charming: luck-knots in red thread, a jar of river-stones polished smooth, little tin charms shaped like keys and combs and house-spirits, each tagged with a price low enough to be kindness rather than commerce. In the corner, half-hidden behind a stack of protective braids, sat a single shelf of serious work — threshold-wards, hearth-seals, the kind of craft that took real training. Priced higher. Marked plainly. Not hidden, exactly.

But not advertised, either.

The door chimed when he opened it, a small honest bell, and she looked up from behind the counter.

She was younger than he expected — late twenties, dark-haired, ink-stained fingers, a smudge of silver dust along one wrist where charmwork left its residue. Her eyes did something interesting when they landed on his badge. They didn't widen. They didn't drop. They went *still*, the way water goes still over something deep, and Halvard had spent twenty years learning to read that stillness, and twenty years wishing he couldn't.

"Morning," she said. "Looking for anything particular, Warden?"

"Just looking." He let his gaze travel the shelves with the unhurried attention of a man pricing goods. "You get much trade?"

"Enough. Rain brings them in. Everyone wants a luck-knot when the sky's behaving strangely." She said it lightly, but he caught the edge beneath it — the whole city was talking about the vanished stars, and here was a charm-seller pretending it was small talk. "Can I help you find something? Wardens usually want one of two things. Protection, or information. We only stock the first."

Something in him almost smiled. Almost. "And if I wanted information?"

"Then you'd want the kind I don't sell." She wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried. "I'm a licensed practitioner, Warden. Journeyman rank, Charmwrights' Guild. Everything in this shop is registered, taxed, and boring. If you're here about the other night—" she shrugged, "—I closed early. Stars going out is bad for business and worse for nerves. I went home and I stayed there."

"I don't recall saying I was here about the other night."

"You didn't." Her eyes met his, level and gray-green and entirely too calm. "But you've got the survey report folded in your coat pocket, left side, and you keep touching it through the wool like it might bite. So."

Halvard stood very still. Twenty years of instinct told him three things at once: she was lying, she knew he knew she was lying, and she was better at this than ninety percent of the criminals he'd ever hunted — better because she wasn't performing innocence, she was simply refusing to perform guilt, which was far harder to catch.

"Your license," he said, taking out the report anyway, since concealment was clearly wasted. "The Charmwrights' Guild was dissolved by Crown decree. Eleven years ago. Your papers shouldn't be valid."

"They're valid." Something flickered behind the calm — not fear. Grief, maybe, or its cousin. "The dissolution dissolved the guild, Warden, not its members. My rank was earned. My work is mine. You can check the registry."

"I intend to." He unfolded the report and laid it on the counter between them, the numbers facing up. "Do you know what this is?"

She looked at it. She was good — very good — but for half a heartbeat her breath changed, a small hitch, a sailor feeling the deck tilt. "Numbers?"

"A warding signature. Unlicensed. High concentration. This street." He watched her face the way a fisherman watches still water. "Whoever cast it is either very talented or very frightened. Possibly both. The pattern broke at moonrise the night the stars went out — cleanly, deliberately, like a hand letting go. Do you know anything about that, Mistress Winterbourne?"

"No." She said it simply. And then, because she was either braver or more foolish than most, she added: "Should I?"

"That depends." He leaned forward slightly, lowering his voice though the shop was empty. "There are things moving in this city that you don't want to be adjacent to. The Crown has doubled the survey rotations. Every unregistered working in Vael is being mapped, and mapped again, and cross-referenced. When the net closes — and it will close — the people caught in it won't be interrogated by men who walk alone in the rain and buy luck-knots. They'll be taken to places with no windows. Do you understand me?"

For a moment — just a moment — the shopkeeper vanished and he saw the person underneath: tired, watchful, carrying something heavy behind the ribs. Then the shutter came down, gentle as a lid.

"I understand that you're threatening me in my own shop," she said, "and that you haven't actually bought anything, which my landlord would consider poor form." She slid a luck-knot across the counter. "On the house. For the road. Even Wardens need the luck."

He took it. He didn't know why he took it. His fingers closed around the little knot of red thread and it was warm — warm the way living things are warm, faintly, wrongly — and he put it in his pocket next to the report and hated himself a little for both.

At the door he paused. "One more question. Off the record."

"Wardens don't do off the record."

"This one does." He turned. "The night the stars went out. Did you feel anything? Before it happened. Most practitioners did — a pulling, a tearing, like a stitch coming loose. Half the hedge-witches in the city woke up screaming. If you felt nothing, that tells me one thing. If you felt everything—"

He let it hang.

Her face did not move. But her hands, resting on the counter, curled slowly inward, closing around nothing, the way people close their hands around old wounds.

"Good day, Warden," she said.

* * *

The Wardenhouse at dusk smelled of ink and wet wool and the onion stew nobody admitted to liking. Halvard sat at his desk with the day's findings spread before him and did not write any of them down.

That was the trouble. That was always the trouble, and it was getting worse.

Here was what he had: a charm-shop with a dead guild's license and a proprietor too skilled to be merely a journeyman. A warding signature of extraordinary quality, broken deliberately at the exact hour the heavens failed. A woman who read his pockets, refused to flinch, and gave him a luck-knot that was still, hours later, faintly warm in his pocket like a sleeping bird.

And here was what he also had, and could not put in any report: twelve years of this. Twelve years of tracing signatures to cellars and attics and sickrooms, and finding not conspirators but grandmothers keeping frost off a dying grandson's lungs; not traitors but fathers lighting rooms they couldn't afford candles for; not enemies of the Crown but people who had simply refused, in small illegal ways, to let the dark take everything. Twelve years of writing *subject detained, contraband destroyed, fine assessed* over the top of human misery until the words wore grooves in him like water wearing stone.

The decree said magic unregistered was magic dangerous. The decree said the guilds had grown proud and careless and had brought the border-wars on themselves. Halvard had believed it once, the way you believe the floor will hold you — without thinking, because thinking about it was unthinkable. But the floor had started creaking lately. The stars had gone out, all of them, in a single night, and the Crown's response had been to double patrols and arrest a candle-maker on Tanner's Row whose crime was selling charms against nightmares. The stars went out and the Crown reached for chains, and somewhere in Halvard's chest a voice that sounded uncomfortably like his dead mother asked: *and what does that tell you about who's afraid of whom?*

"Pell." The junior warden looked up from his desk across the room. "The Winterbourne woman. Pull her full history. Guild enrollment date, sponsor, examination records. And check whether the guild archive has anything on a last signatory — some final roster, a covenant roll, whatever survived the sealing."

"The guild stuff's all impounded, ser. Palace archives. Sealed by order of—"

"I know whose order." Halvard rubbed his eyes. "Check what's *not* sealed, then. Enrollment lists were public once. Someone remembers."

Pell hesitated. "Are we detaining her, ser? Captain Voss will ask. The signature's strong enough that he'll want a raid. Wagons, irons, the works. He's already talking about it."

Halvard looked at the luck-knot lying on his desk. Red thread, woven tight, the pattern one his grandmother used to tie around children's wrists during fever-season — a pattern that had been legal once, and was now contraband, and was now sitting in the pocket of a Crown Warden like a small warm accusation.

"Not yet," he said.

"Ser?"

"Not yet, Pell. I want to know what I'm fishing before I drag the lake. If we raid that shop on a signature and a feeling, and she's clean, Voss looks a fool and so do we. If she's not clean—" he paused, hearing his own hesitation, hating it, "—she won't run. Women like that never run until they have a reason. Let's not give her a reason before we know whether we need one."

Pell nodded and went back to his files, and Halvard sat alone with the lamplight and the rain against the glass, and told himself the lie that good soldiers tell: *this is caution. This is procedure. This is diligence.*

It was not diligence. He knew what it was. It was the sound of a man walking to the edge of his own life and looking down.

Because here was the thing he had not written anywhere, the thing that had followed him home from Sallow Lane and sat beside him through the stew and the reports and the long gray evening: when he had laid the survey report on her counter and asked if she knew anything about the night the stars went out, Odile Winterbourne had lied to him. He was certain of it. Certain the way he was certain of weather.

But she had lied *to protect something*. And in twenty years of hunting, Halvard had learned to sort lies into two piles — the lies told by people hiding guilt, and the lies told by people hiding from wolves — and the second pile had been growing larger every year, while the first pile shrank to almost nothing, and no one in the Wardenhouse above the rank of sergeant seemed willing to say that out loud.

The stars went out. The wards on the border — the great old wards, the ones in songs — had failed the same night; the dispatches from the north said the wilds were *open*, said wolf-sign had been seen inside the pale for the first time in a generation. And the Crown's answer was to hunt charm-sellers in the rain.

Unless, said the small voice, the Crown wasn't hunting the sellers at all. Unless the sellers were just the ones easy to find, and the thing the Crown was truly afraid of had slipped through the fallen wards wearing a shape no wagon-bell and no iron could hold.

Halvard picked up the luck-knot. Turned it in his fingers. Warm. Still warm, after all these hours, which should have been impossible, which meant it wasn't a trinket at all but a message, a *keep walking, warden, walk away* stitched in red thread by clever hands —

— or a tether. A marker. Charmwrights marked things; it was the first thing they learned. And he had carried it willingly into the Wardenhouse, past the threshold-wards, into the heart of the machine.

He laughed once, short and without humor, and set it down.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere across the city, on a narrow lane full of laundry and gulls, a light was burning in a charm-shop window after hours, and Halvard found himself standing at the dark glass of the Wardenhouse, watching the direction of Sallow Lane as if the rain might part and show him something.

The report needed filing. Captain Voss needed an answer. The net needed closing; the net always closed; that was what nets were for.

Instead, Halvard took out a fresh sheet of paper, and in his careful warden's hand wrote a single line at the top:

*Odile Winterbourne — open inquiry.*

Not a warrant. Not an order. An *inquiry* — the slowest, quietest instrument the law allowed him, the difference between a fist and a following.

He signed it, blotted it, and was reaching for the case file to begin in earnest when Pell came back down the stairs two at a time, rain-flecked and pale, holding a scrap of paper like it had bitten him.

"Ser. The registry clerk sent this over. The Winterbourne woman's enrollment record — it came up flagged. Cross-referenced." He swallowed. "Against the sealed guild materials. Whoever filed the flag used a palace cipher. Ser, her name isn't just in the guild rolls. It's in the restricted index. The one they keep for—" he lowered his voice, though the room was empty, "—the Ember Covenant files. Ser, she's listed as a signatory. The last one. Dated twelve years ago."

Halvard stood very still. Outside, the rain came down on Vael like a curtain drawn between the city and the sky where the stars ought to be.

"Get your coat, Pell," he heard himself say. "We're going back to Sallow Lane tonight."

Because if the palace had flagged that name — if the Crown had known for twelve years who she was and left her sitting in a little shop selling luck to poor folk — then she wasn't a suspect at all.

She was bait. Or she was a bomb. And either way, the net was already closing around her, and Halvard intended to be standing between it and her when it fell — though God help him, he could no longer have said which side of the rope he'd be holding when it did.
