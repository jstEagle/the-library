## 9. Flight Through the Ash Market

**Odile**

The Ash Market woke before dawn, as it always did, in a haze of charcoal smoke and cheap tallow. Odile moved through it with her basket on her hip and her hood drawn low, buying things she did not need because not needing them was the whole point. A twist of dried nettle. A stub of beeswax. A paper of salt that she would carry home and never open.

Three days since the guildhall ledger had cracked her open like an egg. Three days since she had read her own name in a dead woman's handwriting and felt the floor of her life tilt beneath her. She had told Renn they should leave Vael at once, north, before Halvard's net drew tight — and then she had spent three days doing nothing of the kind, because leaving meant admitting that the city she'd built her small life inside was already lost to her.

"You're stalling," Renn had said this morning, leaning against her doorframe with his arms crossed, gray eyes flat as river stones.

"I'm being careful."

"You're being a merchant. Weighing what you'll lose." He'd said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse.

Now she threaded between stalls where vendors shouted down the price of ash-glazed pots and secondhand boots, and she tried to feel like a woman running errands instead of a woman running. It almost worked. The market was good at anonymity; half its trade was in things people didn't want traced to them. Charms of dubious legality changed hands here under the noses of the Wardens, wrapped in butcher's paper like cuts of meat.

She was haggling over the nettle — purely for the look of it, for the ordinary theater of it — when the bells began.

Not the market bell. Not the hour bell. The *ward* bell, the one mounted on the customs house that hadn't rung in living memory, and when it started every voice in the Ash Market stopped at once, hundreds of conversations dying mid-word, so that the silence rang louder than the bronze.

Then came the whistles. High, three-note, repeating. Warden-signals, from the north end and the south end and — her stomach dropped — the west, where the only alley out of the square ran past her own street.

They were closing the box around her.

"Move," said a voice at her shoulder, low and rough, and Renn's hand closed on her basket handle, steering her sideways with the casual firmness of a man guiding a drunk friend home. He'd appeared out of nowhere, as he always did, wearing the gray hooded cloak she'd finally bullied him into. "Don't run. Running is a confession."

"What are they—"

"Charm-signatures. Yours." His voice stayed level, but she could hear the effort holding it there. "The wards went down nine days ago and everything you've cast since has been written on the city like ink on wet paper. They've been reading your street all morning."

The first stall overturned somewhere behind them. Then a scream, short and furious, cut off by the crack of a binding-cord snapping taut. Odile didn't look back. Looking back was how people got caught; some instinct older than her memory knew that.

"They're not just after me," she breathed. "Listen — they're clearing the whole market."

"Cover," Renn agreed grimly. "A raid this size needs a reason, and 'one charmwright' isn't one. Someone told them something bigger was here." His hand tightened on hers through the basket handle. "Someone told them about the shard."

The covenant-stone shard sat wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her cellar, humming its low note day and night. She'd felt it through the floorboards while she slept, like a heart under the house.

The crowd broke. That was the terrible thing, how fast it broke — three hundred people who had been arguing over pennies a moment ago now flowing toward the exits like water finding cracks, and in the gaps between them the Wardens advanced in their slate-gray coats, visored, unhurried, spreading out in pairs with the practiced geometry of men who had done this a hundred times. One of them raised a rod of pale wood and spoke a word, and the air above the market *shimmered*, a dome of faint light settling over the square like a lid.

"Containment ward," Renn said. "No one leaves until they've looked at everyone."

Odile's hands had gone cold. "There are children here."

"There are always children here. That's how they make it normal." He pulled her into the shadow between a pottery stall and the wall, and for a moment they stood pressed together in the smell of clay dust while Wardens moved through the thinning crowd, checking palms, checking throats for telltale sigil-scars, turning over baskets with the butt of a rod.

A woman near the well refused to open her satchel. Odile watched it happen as if from very far away: the Warden's patient repetition, the woman's rising voice, the second Warden stepping in, the cord, the scream, the woman dragged toward the customs house with her satchel spilling dried flowers across the cobbles. Hedge-charms. Probably luck-knots for her daughter's wedding. The crowd watched and then, deliberately, stopped watching.

"That could be me," Odile whispered.

"It will be, if we stand here." Renn's eyes swept the square, calculating, and she saw him arrive at the same answer she did: the containment dome covered the market, but the market had been built against the old city wall, and the wall had *doors*. Cellar doors. Coal chutes. The buried veins of a city that had been digging under itself for four hundred years.

"The tanner's arch," she said. "Behind the smokehouse. There's a drain — my father used to say it ran clean through to the river district before they bricked the low half."

"Before they bricked it," Renn repeated. "You know a way in?"

"I know a way it *was*."

He almost smiled. "Good enough. Stay behind me. And Odile—" he caught her chin, turned her face to his, and his eyes were suddenly very serious, very close, "—when I say get down, get down and don't watch. Whatever happens. Promise me."

"Whatever happens," she echoed. "Renn, what's going to happen?"

The whistle sounded again, closer. Two Wardens rounding the well, working stall by stall toward the smokehouse row.

"Down," Renn said.

And the world tore open.

* * *

**Renn**

He had promised himself he wouldn't. Twelve years of discipline, twelve years of walking on two legs through cities that would nail his pelt to a gatehouse, and he had promised himself that no charmwright, no matter what she'd been to him, was worth burning the last advantage he had.

But the Warden's rod was coming up, and the binding-word was forming on the man's lips, and Odile Winterbourne was standing in the open with her cold hands full of nettles, looking at the rod the way people look at snakes — frozen, disbelieving, a half-second too slow — and Renn discovered that twelve years of discipline weighed less than nothing against that.

He shrugged off the cloak and let the change take him.

It was never painless. It was a peeling, a pouring, bones sliding and re-racking themselves like a cartwheel collapsing and rebuilding mid-roll, and he rode it down onto all fours with a sound that wasn't quite a roar and wasn't quite a scream. When it finished he stood taller than a man at the shoulder, gray-black fur bristling along a spine built for the northern wilds, teeth long enough that the Warden's next word died unspoken.

The square *screamed*. Not one voice — all of them, the whole market shrieking as one animal, and Renn felt their terror hit him like weather. He hated this part. He hated being the thing they fled from. He planted himself between Odile and the two Wardens and let his lips peel back from his teeth, and he put every year of exile into the growl that rolled out of him.

To their credit, the Wardens didn't run. The older one — scarred jaw, steady stance — shifted his grip and spoke the binding-word after all, and the cord of pale light whipped out and caught Renn across the foreleg, searing through fur to muscle. He snarled and snapped the line with one wrench of his neck; it burst like rotten rope, sparks scattering across the cobbles. But the motion cost him balance, and the younger Warden was already circling wide, rod leveled at—

At Odile. Because she hadn't gotten down. Because she was standing there staring at him — at the wolf, at the impossible wolf that had been drinking tea in her cellar for three days — with an expression Renn couldn't read and had no time to read.

"*Get down,*" he tried to say, and of course it came out as a bark, and of course she flinched, and the young Warden's rod swung toward her with the binding-word half-formed—

Renn hit him.

Not to kill. He was proud, afterward, in the way a drowning man is proud of grabbing driftwood, that he managed not to kill. He took the man at the waist and drove him into the pottery stall, and the world became shards and shouting and the acrid bite of containment-ward magic crawling along his flank where someone had tagged him with a cutting-sigil. Blood ran hot into his left eye. Somewhere above the chaos the dome overhead flickered — his passage through it had scored it, or the Wardens were losing concentration, he couldn't tell.

A hand seized his ruff. For one white-hot instant he nearly bit down on reflex — and then the scent reached him, soap and beeswax and fear, and he checked himself with his jaws an inch from Odile's wrist.

"*Run,*" she was saying, hauling at him with strength she shouldn't have had, her voice cracking. "This way — the smokehouse — come *on*, you great stupid—"

He came. Lame on the bound foreleg, blood in his eye, he came, because she was pulling and because twelve years of searching had ended at a night-market stall with a woman who bought nettles she didn't need, and he would have followed her into the sun itself.

Behind them, the older Warden blew three sharp notes on his whistle — the pursuit signal, the one that would bring every slate-gray coat in the district — and then, strangely, hesitated. Renn caught it in one backward glance: the man standing amid the wreckage with his rod lowered, looking at the wolf, at the fleeing pair, with something on his scarred face that was not hatred.

Doubt. It looked like doubt.

Then the smokehouse swallowed them.

* * *

**Odile**

The tanner's arch was exactly where her father's stories had left it: a brick mouth behind the curing racks, half-hidden by forty years of ash heaps, breathing out cold air that smelled of river-stone and old dark. The door had rotted to lace. Odile put her shoulder through it and the whole frame gave at once, spilling her down three steps into blackness, and Renn flowed past her — still a wolf, still bleeding, enormous in the narrow passage — and caught her fall against his shoulder before she struck the stone.

For a heartbeat neither of them moved. His fur was hot and damp under her hands. His heart hammered against her palm like a fist on a door.

"You're a wolf," she said stupidly. Above them, muffled by earth and brick, the market screamed on. "You've been sleeping in my cellar and you're a *wolf*."

He made a sound in his chest — she would learn later it was the wolf-kin equivalent of a bitter laugh — and then light bloomed, soft and amber, as he shook his head and the change took him backward: fur receding, spine lengthening, the wolf folding up into the man, naked and shaking and gray-faced with blood loss, kneeling on the tunnel floor with his hand pressed to the gash along his ribs.

"Wolf-kin," he corrected, through his teeth. "We prefer the distinction. Wolves don't pay taxes."

"You're hurt."

"I'm *alive*, which given the circumstances is the more remarkable condition." He tried to stand, failed, and accepted her shoulder without comment, which frightened her more than any wound. Renn did not accept help. She'd learned that much in three days.

The tunnel was low and dripping and ran straight as a spear-shaft into the dark. Behind them, faint now, the whistles kept sounding, and once — horribly — she heard the containment dome shatter with a sound like a struck glass, and the market's noise spilled out across the district, and she thought of the flower-woman's satchel, and the luck-knots scattered on the cobbles, and the children pressed against their mothers' legs.

"They'll burn the Ash Market for this," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "They'll call it a den of illegal craft and they'll shut every stall, and everyone who ever sold me nettle will be questioned, and it will be my fault."

"Yes," Renn said simply. No comfort in it, no varnish. Just the truth laid out like a blade. "That's what exposure costs. That's why I stayed small for twelve years. Why *you* stayed small." He paused, and she felt him choose his next words carefully. "The difference is, they were always going to find you, Odile. The shard hums. The embers wake when you come near. You could have hidden another twelve years and it would have ended the same — except the moons would be gone, and the border sealed, and everything the covenant promised would be ash. At least now it's ash *with witnesses*."

She laughed — a short, broken sound that surprised them both. "Is that supposed to be comforting?"

"No. It's supposed to be true. Comfort comes later, if we live."

They walked. The tunnel bent twice, passed a junction choked with collapsed brick, and began to climb, and the air sweetened from river-mud toward rain and grass. Odile held most of Renn's weight and thought about the moment in the square — the wolf rising out of the man like a wave out of the sea, the crowd screaming, and herself, standing in the open, unable to move, unable to look away. Not afraid of him. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that sat in her chest like a coal. Every other soul in that market had seen a monster. She had seen something she *recognized*, the way you recognize a room from a dream, and the recognition had rooted her to the ground while Wardens closed in.

"Why didn't I run?" she asked quietly.

Renn was silent for three steps. Four. Five. "Because some part of you remembers what I am," he said at last, "even if the rest of you doesn't."

"And what are you?"

Ahead, gray light showed through a grate — the world outside, the river district, the road north. He stopped beneath it and looked up, and the light fell across his face, and for a moment he looked so much like grief given a human shape that her breath caught.

"Yours," he said. "I was yours. Come on — the road north starts here, and we've maybe an hour before they think to search the drains."

He boosted her to the grate. She pushed it aside and climbed up into the grey morning, into the rain-smell and the wide world, and turned to pull him after her — and froze.

On the far side of the canal, on the customs-house steps, stood a single Warden. Slate coat, visor pushed up, and beneath it a face she knew: Halvard, the questioner, the man with the ember-glass pendant. He was looking directly at her. He had been for some time, she realized — long enough. And he made no move toward his whistle.

Instead, slowly, deliberately, Halvard touched two fingers to the pendant at his throat, glanced north along the road, and walked away.

Renn pulled himself up beside her, followed her gaze, and went very still.

"He knows," Odile whispered.

"He knows," Renn agreed. "Which means either we've found an ally in the Crown's own coat—" he took her arm, turned her north, and set off along the canal at a limping run, "—or we've just been given enough rope to hang us both. Either way, the city's done. The marshes are a day ahead, and the drowned chapel beyond them. Can you walk?"

Odile looked back once at Vael — at the smoke of the Ash Market rising crooked and wrong against the morning, at the walls that had held her small safe life — and found that she felt no fear anymore. Only a grim, clarifying anger, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the shard's hum answering from the bundle at Renn's back, like a compass needle swinging home.

"Yes," she said. "Walk faster."
