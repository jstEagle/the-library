## 10. The Road North

The gates of Vael closed behind us like a mouth.

I felt it more than heard it—the deep iron groan rolling through the ground, through the soles of my boots, up into my teeth. Somewhere behind that wall, Halvard was standing in the smoking ruin of the Ash Market, and somewhere in his careful Warden's ledger, my name had just been written in a heavier hand.

"Keep walking," Renn said at my shoulder.

"I am walking."

"You are walking like someone deciding whether to run." He kept his hood up, his stride loose and unhurried, a man strolling out of the city on ordinary errands. Only the tension in his jaw betrayed him. "Wardens watch for runners. Walk like you're bored."

"I've never been bored in my life."

"That explains a great deal about the last two days."

We passed the last of the way-shrines—little alcoves where travelers once left copper coins for safe passage—and I made myself not look back. The road bent north along the old canal, following a ribbon of gray water toward the marsh country, and Vael's walls fell away behind us by degrees: first the towers, then the rooftops, then only the smoke, a smudge on the morning like a bruise.

My hands would not stop shaking. I kept them buried in my pockets, curled around the shard of covenant-stone wrapped in cloth, which pulsed faintly against my palm like a second heartbeat. It had been pulsing since the market. Since Renn had torn off his own skin in front of forty strangers to save mine.

*Point of no return*, some dry voice in my head observed. *You passed it around the time the wolf started talking.*

* * *

We walked until the sun stood high and the city was a memory, and then we walked until it wasn't even that. The canal road gave way to a drover's track, the track to a footpath, the footpath to nothing much at all—wet grass and tussocks and the first black fingers of marsh reeds poking up from the earth like the world testing whether we'd notice.

Renn finally called a halt in the lee of a fallen boundary stone, one of the old markers from before the Crown standardized everything worth standardizing. It was carved with interlocking knots, wolf-teeth and human hands, worn nearly smooth. He crouched beside it and ran his thumb across the carving with an expression I couldn't read.

"You knew this stone," I said.

"Everyone knew these stones. They lined the whole border once." He sat down heavily, favoring the wound in his side that my needlework had pulled shut but not closed. "Your kings knocked most of them down when they built the wall. Said they confused travelers."

"Did they?"

"No." He looked up at me, and there was something almost gentle under all that wariness. "They told the truth. That's why they were dangerous."

I unwrapped the shard. In daylight it didn't look like much—a splinter of gray rock the length of my finger, shot through with veins of dull red. But when I held it bare against the open sky, those veins brightened, just slightly, like coals remembering fire. And it hummed. Not sound, exactly. A feeling in the bones of my hand, a tuning-fork note struck somewhere below hearing.

"It's been doing that since the market," I said. "Getting stronger as we go north. What is it?"

"A piece of the covenant-stone. The wall itself, or what the wall used to be, before your guild—" He stopped himself. Jaw tight. "Before the breaking. There were thirteen embers set into it, one for each moon of the pact-year. When the covenant broke, the embers scattered. The shard is a piece of the same making. It wants to go home."

"And home is north."

"Home is everywhere the embers fell." He nodded at the horizon, where the marshes spread out flat and silver-green to the edge of sight. "But the nearest one—the first one, if you want to think of it that way—is close. Two days' walk, maybe three with your legs."

"My legs are fine."

"Your legs are fine," he agreed gravely, "in the way that a man says *fine* when you ask him about a burning house."

I almost laughed. That startled me—that anything in me could still laugh—but the laugh came out cracked and strange, and Renn pretended not to notice, which was its own small kindness.

"The first ember," I said. "Where?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Then he said, "In a chapel. Drowned, now. Out past the Sedgefen, where the old pilgrim road went under when they cut the new canals. The Charmwrights hid it there twelve years ago, when the breaking started. Someone swam it out into the dark and let the water take it, because no Warden would think to look beneath a church."

"How do you know that? You're not a Charmwright."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "Everyone knew where the embers went," he said. "It was the whole point of scattering them. Many hands, many hiding places, so no one theft could end it all." He rose, wincing, and shouldered the bundle of supplies we'd bought with the last of my honest coin. "Come on. We want the Sedgefen causeway before dark, or we'll be sharing our bed with adders."

I got up too, and then I didn't move. Because something had snagged in me, small and sharp, the way a fishhook snags in a sleeve.

*Renn had known her name before she gave it.* Back in the cellar, bleeding on my floor, he'd said *Odile Winterbourne* like a man reading it off a page he'd memorized. And now he spoke of the embers' hiding places with the ease of a man reciting his own address.

"Who told you about the chapel?" I asked.

He paused, half-turned away, the light catching the gray of his eyes. "A dead woman," he said. "Come on."

* * *

The Sedgefen took the rest of the day and all of my certainty.

I had imagined marshes as flat. Nobody had warned me they were *alive*—breathing, shifting, full of voices. Reeds taller than a man whispered in a wind I couldn't feel at ground level. Water lay everywhere and nowhere, black mirrors between the tussocks that reflected a sky going slowly the color of pewter. Birds cried out over the flats with voices like grieving women. Twice something large moved beneath the surface of a pool and did not come up.

Renn led without hesitation, reading the ground the way I read charm-script—weight shifts, color changes in the reeds, the angle of the water birds. "Step where I step," he said, and I did, and hated how quickly I obeyed, and obeyed anyway.

Somewhere in the third hour of wading, the fear caught up with me.

It had been waiting, I think. All through the flight from the market—all through the running and the shouting and Renn's terrible transformation, fur and fang and a body unfolding like a nightmare made flesh—I had been running on something that wasn't courage, only momentum. Now the marsh slowed us to a walk, and momentum ran out, and what was left underneath was cold and clear and waiting.

*I am a charm-seller,* I thought, thigh-deep in freezing water, holding a humming rock. *I sell love-lures to farm boys and headache-charms to grandmothers. Three days ago my largest ambition was a new sign for the shop. Now there are Wardens who know my face, a wolf who knows my name, and a voice in my skull telling me the stars went out because of something I signed.*

Because of something I signed. Twelve years ago. When I would have been—

Nineteen. Nineteen years old, signing away stars.

"I can hear you thinking from here," Renn said. He'd stopped ahead on a hummock of higher ground, and the low sun put him half in silhouette, broad-shouldered and still, watching me flounder. "You've gone quiet in a loud way."

"Is that a wolf-kin thing? Hearing people think?"

"It's a Renn thing. You're very easy to read." He waited while I hauled myself up beside him. Mud sucked at my boots; I gave up on dignity entirely and sat down hard. "Say it, then. Whatever it is. It's been circling since the causeway."

I meant to say *nothing*. It was on my tongue, ready, the same well-made lie I'd given Halvard at my own door. But out here there was no door to stand behind, no counter between us, no shop. Just reeds and rust-water and a man who had bled on my floor and asked for nothing.

"Why do you keep looking at me like that?" I said instead.

"Like what?"

"Like you're checking whether I'm still there. Every hour or so. Like you're counting me."

For a moment he said nothing at all. A marsh bird called somewhere, long and lonely. Then: "People have died on this road," he said. "People I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "It's a long road, and I've walked it alone before. Counting is a habit."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He stood, offered me his hand, and pulled me up out of the mud with a strength that reminded me, unhelpfully, of the market—of what lived under his skin. "Ask me again when we're past the fen. Some answers need solid ground under them."

"And some questions need it," I said. "That's why I'm not asking the real ones yet."

His mouth curved—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile lived. "Then we understand each other."

* * *

We made camp that night on a shelf of dry ground at the fen's edge, where the reeds thinned and the first low hills of the north country showed themselves as darker shapes against the dark. Renn built a small fire—smaller than he wanted, he said, because smoke carried—and I ate hard bread and dried fish and watched the light move on his face.

The shard lay between us on the bedroll, and its pulse had changed. Stronger now. Steadier. It beat in time, I realized, with nothing in my body—which meant it beat in time with something else. Something ahead of us, in the dark hills, keeping its own slow heart under the water.

"Tell me about the chapel," I said.

"There's not much to tell. Old pilgrim church, dedicated to some saint the Crown struck from the calendar two hundred years ago. When the canals came, the engineers diverted the fen-waters and the valley flooded. The tower top still shows in dry summers, they say. Locals call it the Drowned Lady and won't go near it." He fed a twig to the flames. "The ember is in the crypt below the nave. Under thirty feet of black water."

"Thirty feet." I turned that over. "And we're going to swim for it."

"We're going to figure out the swimming part tomorrow. Tonight we're going to sleep." He glanced at me sidelong. "Unless you'd rather sit up cataloguing everything that frightens you. I find that helps some people. Mostly it doesn't."

"What frightens me," I said, "isn't the water."

He waited. He was good at waiting. It occurred to me, watching him, that patience like that was earned somewhere, paid for in years, and I wondered—not for the first time—what those years had cost him, and why every road in his story seemed to bend around me.

"It's that I'm not frightened enough," I said. "Halvard will have my description by now. My shop is ash, my name is in a Warden's book, and I'm sitting by a fire eating fish with a wolf-kin exile, hunting pieces of a pact I don't remember signing. By every measure I should be terrified out of my mind. And I'm not. I'm—" I searched for the word and found it and it scared me more than any of the rest. "*Relieved.*"

The fire popped. Renn looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face made him nod slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd suspected for a long time.

"Twelve years is a long time to live small," he said quietly.

"You keep saying things like that. As if you knew me before."

"Do I?"

"Don't do that." The anger came up fast and clean, and I let it, because it was better than the other thing underneath. "Don't answer a question with a question like we're trading charms. You knew my name before I gave it. You know the guild's hiding places. You look at me like I'm a letter you've been carrying for years, waiting for the chance to deliver it. So either tell me what you know, or stop pretending you don't."

He didn't flinch. He set another twig on the fire, deliberate, giving himself the moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and even.

"If I tell you everything tonight, you'll do one of two things. You'll believe me, and it will break something in you that needs to stay whole until the moons turn. Or you won't believe me, and you'll leave, and you'll die alone in the fen with a rock in your pocket." His gray eyes came up and held mine, and there was no evasion in them at all—only a kind of weary, bottomless care that frightened me worse than any lie could have. "So I'll tell you this much, and no more. Everything you've lost, you lost on purpose. And when you remember why, you'll need someone standing next to you who was there. I intend to be that person. That's all I'll say tonight."

The silence after was very wide. The fire settled. Somewhere out in the black water, a bird cried once and went quiet.

"On purpose," I repeated.

"On purpose."

I looked down at the shard, pulsing its patient pulse against the cloth, pointing north like a compass needle made of grief. And I understood, suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water closing over my head, that I had been afraid of the wrong things. Not the Wardens. Not the water. Not even the wolf.

The thing I should have feared was the truth, waiting for me under thirty feet of fen-black water in a drowned church, wearing my own handwriting.

"Then we'd better get there fast," I said.

Renn studied me across the fire, and slowly, for the first time since I'd known him, he smiled—a real one, brief and tired and warm, gone almost before it arrived.

"Yes," he said. "We'd better."

* * *

We broke camp before dawn, in that hour when the marsh breathed mist and the world was all grays and silences. The shard's pulse had strengthened overnight; I could feel it through the cloth now, insistent, urgent, a fist knocking on a door.

Renn paused at the edge of the fen and looked north, where the hills rose dark against a sky just beginning to bruise with light. "Dry summer," he murmured. "If we're lucky, the water's dropped. If we're lucky, you might see the tower from the ridge."

"And if we're not lucky?"

"Then we swim in the dark, and you meet your past thirty feet down." He glanced back at me, and the mist moved between us and thinned. "One more thing, Odile. Before we go in—whatever the ember shows you, whatever comes back—it isn't all of you. Remember that. Memory is a room, not a house."

I didn't answer. I was looking past him, up the long slope of the ridge, where the rising light had begun to pick out the details of the north country—and there, far off across a drowned valley, thin as a needle against the brightening sky, stood the broken crown of a stone tower.

The shard burned against my palm like a held breath.

It knew the way home.
