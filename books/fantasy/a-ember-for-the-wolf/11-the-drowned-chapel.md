## 11. The Drowned Chapel

The marshes began where the road gave up pretending.

One moment there was a track of packed clay running north between hedgerows, and the next the hedges were gone and the ground had gone soft and dark beneath them, sucking at their boots with every step. Mist lay over the reeds like a sheet pulled up to the chin. Odile walked behind Renn because walking in front felt too much like trusting him, and because he moved through the wet country as if he'd been born to it—which, she supposed, he might have been. His bare feet left prints that filled with black water and closed over, erasing him. Hers stayed. She tried not to think about what that meant.

"You're limping," she said, mostly to break the silence.

"I'm favoring my left side," Renn said. "It's different."

"It's not."

"The difference matters." He didn't look back. The wound across his ribs—the one she'd stitched by lamplight in her cellar three nights ago—had been healing with a speed that unsettled her, wolf-quick, but the fever that came before it was gone still clung to him. She could see it in the set of his shoulders, the careful way he turned. "If I limp, something is wrong. If I favor, something is merely broken."

"That's the most wolf-kin thing you've ever said."

"Everything I say is the most wolf-kin thing I've ever said. You have very little to compare it to."

She almost smiled. That was the trouble with him, she was learning: he made forgetting feel less like an absence and more like a choice she kept making, hour after hour, to stay guarded when some traitorous part of her wanted to laugh at his dry, bitter humor and ask him questions—where he'd been, what the wilds were like, why he looked at her sometimes as though she were a word on the tip of his tongue.

The shard rode against her sternum in a pouch of oiled leather. It had hummed all morning, low and patient, the way a kettle hums before it boils. Since they'd crossed out of Vael's shadow, since the walls had shrunk behind them to a gray smudge and then to nothing at all, the humming had grown. Not louder, exactly. Closer. As though the sound weren't coming from the shard but toward it, drawn along some thread only the stone could feel.

"How far?" she asked.

Renn stopped. He lifted his muzzle slightly—not a gesture, exactly, more a habit of attention—and tasted the air. "The chapel drowned eighty years ago when the river changed its mind," he said. "The water never fully agreed to let it go. We'll know it by the bell."

"Bell?"

"Drowned bells ring strange. Ask any fisherman." And he waded onward without explaining, which was becoming its own kind of conversation between them: him refusing, her enduring.

* * *

They reached the water at midafternoon, though under the mist it might have been any hour at all. A broad mere spread out ahead of them, flat as hammered pewter, and from its center rose the skeleton of a tower—a square stump of old pale stone, window-holes black, crowned with rotted beams where a roof had once argued with the sky. Beside it, just breaking the surface, hung a bell in a leaning frame of oak gone silver with age. The rope was gone. The clapper swung anyway, slow and silent, stirring rings across the water that reached the shore long after they should have died away.

Odile stood hip-deep in the cold shallows and felt the shard go quiet.

That was worse than the humming. All day it had pulled her forward like a fish on a line, and now, with the chapel in sight, it simply stopped—settled against her skin warm as a sleeping bird, waiting. As if it had brought her here and no longer needed to insist.

"There's a path," Renn said. He pointed with his chin. Beneath the water, faintly, a line of paving stones ran out toward the tower, furred with weed, some sunk deep, some barely covered. "The monks built it for floods. They expected to be drowned eventually. Very practical people, the border-folk. They built everything twice."

"Did you come from a place like this?"

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "I came from the wilds. Everything else is story." And he stepped onto the first stone.

The crossing took the better part of an hour. The water was waist-deep at the deepest stretch and cold enough to make her gasp, and twice the stones vanished entirely and Renn caught her arm without comment and steered her true, his grip firm and brief and strangely gentle for hands that could, she knew, open her throat like a letter. She hated how much she noticed that gentleness. She noticed it the way you notice a door left ajar in a house you thought empty.

At the tower's base, a doorway gaped above the waterline. Inside, the air changed—colder, older, tasting of stone and silt and something faintly sweet, like incense burned so long ago the smoke itself had become part of the walls. Renn went first, low and wary, and she followed with one hand on the wet stone and the other cupped around the little witchlight she conjured, a bead of green-gold glow no brighter than a firefly. Small magic. Safe magic. The kind that had kept her invisible for twelve years.

The nave stretched out before them, flooded to a depth of perhaps four feet, and above it the vaulted ceiling climbed into darkness where swallows—or things shaped like swallows—nested and muttered. Silt had buried the floor. Pillars rose out of the black water in ranks, and where the light touched them she saw carvings: figures of men and wolves standing shoulder to shoulder, hands clasped, carved again and again down the length of the nave, worn nearly smooth by water and time until the clasped hands were just shallow hollows, like the memory of holding.

"Oh," Odile said softly. She hadn't meant to say anything at all.

"This was a covenant-house," Renn said quietly. "Before the Crown called them heresy. There were dozens along the border once. This is likely the last one standing." He paused. "Standing. More or less."

The shard pulsed against her chest, once, like a heart deciding.

She waded in.

* * *

The water grew deeper past the third pillar—chest-deep, then neck-deep, and she had to swim the last stretch, witchlight held high, while Renn glided alongside her half-submerged and silent as a log. The far end of the nave rose into a shallow apse where an altar still stood above the floodline, a single slab of pale stone on two carved supports. The carvings here had survived better: a woman and a wolf, facing each other, and between them a flame that someone had defaced with a chisel long ago, gouging out the fire and leaving only the shape of the burning behind.

On the altar lay nothing. Of course nothing. Eighty years of water and thieves.

But the shard was screaming now—no, not screaming, *singing*, a high thin note she felt in her teeth and behind her eyes, and when she dragged herself dripping onto the altar's edge and set her palm flat against the cold slab, the note resolved into a word.

Her name.

Not *Odile*. The other one. The one the ledger had used, the one written in her own handwriting twelve years ago in a guildhall that smelled of ash: *signatory*. The name the ember-glass recognized. It poured up through the stone and into her bones, and the witchlight guttered, and the world folded shut around her like a book.

* * *

Wind.

Wind off the high country, hard and clean, snapping banners somewhere below her and carrying the smell of pine and woodsmoke and, faintly, of wolf. Stone under her feet—wide stone, parapet-smooth, the kind of stone you only get on a wall built to be walked. She was nineteen. She knew it the way you know things in dreams, without being told: nineteen and terrified and lit from within like a lamp, because tonight was the night, the night the pact would be signed, the night the war ended or didn't, and beside her stood a man whose shoulder touched hers.

She couldn't see his face.

She tried. She turned her head and the memory itself flinched away from her, the way water flinches from oil, and all she got was an impression: tall, gray-eyed—she was almost certain about the eyes, gray like the mere outside, gray like weather—and a scar, maybe, or a mark, at the edge of his jaw. His hand found hers on the parapet. His fingers were warm. His thumb moved over her knuckles once, twice, a small unconscious rhythm, and her body knew the rhythm. Her body leaned into him like a plant toward sun.

*"Say it back to me,"* he said, and his voice was—she clawed for the voice, and the memory gave her only the shape of it, low and roughened, a voice used to open spaces. *"All of it. If you're going to swear your life away, swear it whole."*

*"I'll swear it when you do."*

He laughed. Oh, she made him laugh; she could feel her own mouth curving, feel the joke land between them like a coin tossed into a well. *"Coward,"* he said fondly.

*"Strategist. There's a difference. Ask anyone."*

Then the wind shifted and below them the fires of two camps showed—one human, one not—laid out along the base of the wall like offerings on an altar, and an old voice called up the stair, and it was time, and the man beside her went still in a way that made her chest ache even now, even inside the memory, because she could feel what he wasn't saying. He was afraid. Not of the oath. Of losing her to it.

*"Whatever happens in there,"* he said quietly, *"whatever they take, whatever it costs—you look for me after. Promise me. However long it takes. You look for me."*

*"I promise."*

*"Say it whole."*

*"I will look for you. However long it takes. I—"*

And the memory tore.

Not faded—tore, like cloth ripped along a seam someone had cut in advance, deliberate and clean, and through the rip she glimpsed one last thing: her own hand pressing down on parchment, a candle guttering, thirteen points of light arranged in a circle on the table before her, and blood—her blood, welling bright on her thumb where she'd cut it—and a voice, her own voice, younger, saying words she could not hear, and the lights going out one by one like stars—

Stars going out.

* * *

She came back to herself face-down on the altar, coughing marsh water, with Renn's hands gripping her shoulders and her name—her plain market-day name—being said in that low roughened voice, over and over, and for one vertiginous heartbeat the two voices lay on top of each other so perfectly that she grabbed his wrist and said, "Say that again."

"Odile." Careful. Guarded. Wrong.

"No—before. What did you call me?"

His eyes were gray. She had known that since the cellar; she had catalogued it and filed it away and told herself it meant nothing, that gray eyes were common as rain. But now she was looking at the edge of his jaw, at the thin pale mark that crossed it like a signature, and the mere was gray beyond him, and the wind off the water smelled of pine, and her hand was still on his wrist where a thumb had once moved over her knuckles twice, a small unconscious rhythm her body knew.

"Odile," he said again, and this time his voice shook, and she watched him choose—she *saw* him choose—to close the door. "You fell. You were under too long."

"I remembered." Her own voice sounded far away. "There was a wall. I was nineteen. There was a man, and we swore an oath together, and I promised—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "I promised him I would look for him. However long it took. And Renn—" she laughed, and it came out cracked and awful, "Renn, I can't remember his face. I remember his *hand*. I remember that he laughed at my jokes and was afraid of losing me and had a scar on his jaw, and I cannot remember his face, and I don't understand why that doesn't frighten me more than it does, and I don't understand why looking at you right now feels like standing in a house where all the furniture has been carried out but the shadows are still on the walls."

The mist moved over the broken roof. Somewhere above them the silent bell turned on its beam, and the clapper stirred, and rang—not sound, exactly, but pressure, one deep pulse that passed through the water and the stone and her ribs, and the shard against her chest answered it, singing, and Renn flinched as though struck.

"You know," she whispered. "Don't you. You know who he was."

For a long moment the only sound was water dripping from the vaulted dark, keeping time like a patient clock. Then Renn took her hand off his wrist—gently, gently, the way you take a coal from a child—and said, "The ember is here. Under the altar. Can you stand?"

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have today."

"Then give me tomorrow's."

But he was already moving, wading to the altar's base, feeling along the underside of the slab with clever fingers, and she understood suddenly that he wasn't avoiding her question—he was drowning it, the way the river had drowned this chapel, thoroughly and forever, and that the kindest thing he thought he could do for her was lie. She had lied for a living for twelve years, selling charms that worked small wonders and telling customers they were nothing at all. She knew what a merciful lie sounded like from the inside.

She stood on shaking legs and joined him at the altar, and together they found the seam—a hairline crack in the pale stone, fitted so finely no thief had ever seen it. When she pressed her bleeding palm to it, the stone sighed apart like a book opening, and there in the hollow, cradled on a bed of lead and linen gone to rot, lay the first ember.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. A bead of glass, or something like glass, no bigger than a robin's egg, and it held a light that was not orange or gold but the color of the moment a candle catches—between dark and flame, undecided. When she lifted it, warmth ran up her arm, and the torn edge of the memory smoothed by one stitch: *gray eyes*, certain now, certain the way grief is certain.

Behind her, Renn made a small sound. She didn't turn. If she turned, she would ask again. If she asked again, he would refuse again. And some part of her—some part older than the forgetting—understood that he was carrying something heavy enough to bend him, and that prying it from his arms here, in a drowned church at the end of the world, might break them both.

So instead she said, "Twelve more," and closed her fist around the ember's small stubborn light.

"Twelve more," he echoed, and there was relief in it, and shame, and underneath both, so faint she might have invented it, something that sounded terribly like hope.

They waded back down the nave together, past the worn carvings of clasped hands, and behind them in the dark the drowned bell turned slowly on its beam, ringing its soundless peal for a covenant everyone had forgotten—including, she was beginning to understand, the woman who had sworn it.
