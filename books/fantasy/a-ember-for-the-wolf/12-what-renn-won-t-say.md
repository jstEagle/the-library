## 12. What Renn Won't Say

The marshes did not end so much as dissolve.

Renn had crossed the Sedgefen twice in his life—once as a cub riding his mother's back through the reed-tunnels, once as a young exile running south with nothing but a stolen name—and both times the fen had felt less like a place than like a decision the world had half-made and abandoned. Water lay everywhere and nowhere. The ground accepted each step with a soft, treacherous courtesy, and then, three steps later, swallowed it to the knee. Reeds grew taller than a man and whispered constantly, though there was no wind worth the name.

Odile walked ahead of him on the raised causeway stones, her hood up against the drizzle, her staff probing each stone before she trusted it. She had not spoken in an hour. Not since the chapel.

He watched the set of her shoulders and read them the way he'd once read weather over the northern plains: something had broken loose in her down there, under the black water, and she was still deciding what shape to be around the wound of it.

*She saw the wall,* he thought. *She stood on it again, if only for a heartbeat.*

And she still could not see his face.

He should have been grateful. He had spent twelve years telling himself that this was mercy—the mercy of silence, the kindness of letting her walk free of a memory that had cost her everything. But watching her now, small and stubborn and soaked to the waist in fen water, hauling herself over stones that wanted her dead, gratitude felt like a word other men used.

"You're staring," she said, without turning.

"I'm watching your footing."

"My footing has been fine since noon."

"Your footing went into a hole at noon."

She stopped. Turned. Her face beneath the hood was pale, rain-flushed, and furious in the particular way that meant she was frightened underneath and hated him for noticing. "The chapel," she said. "You knew what was down there."

"The ledger said the first ember was given to the water. I told you that."

"You told me it was *in* the water. You didn't tell me what taking it would—" She stopped herself. Pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, as if holding a door shut from the inside. "I was nineteen years old, Renn. I was standing on a wall. There was someone beside me and I could hear his voice and I couldn't—I couldn't see him. It's like looking at a face through frosted glass. Like my own mind is keeping something from me." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated that too; he could see her hating it. "Do you know why?"

The causeway stones hummed faintly beneath their feet. Or perhaps that was only the shard in his pack, answering the ember now warm against her chest. He kept his face still. He had gotten very good at stillness; exile taught it the way winter teaches trees.

"No," he lied.

* * *

They made camp that night on a shingle bar where the fen widened into open water and the reeds drew back as if in deference to something. Renn built the fire low and screened, the way he'd built a hundred fires crossing country where smoke meant death. Odile sat close enough to feel its warmth and far enough away to make a point about it, drying her boots by the flames and pretending to study a map she already knew by heart.

He cooked. That was the arrangement they had fallen into without ever discussing it: she made the charms and wards that kept them unfindable, and he made the food, because wolf-kin learned early that hunger was the enemy that killed before cold or steel ever got the chance. He spitted river-fish over the coals and watched the fat hiss and thought about all the things a man can say and all the reasons he doesn't.

*Your name is Odile Winterbourne and mine was Renn of the Ashfall line, and when you were nineteen you stood on the Wall of Vael in a blue cloak with frost in your hair, and you laughed at my accent, and I swore an oath beside you with our blood mixed in one bowl, and you called me* belovéd *in the old tongue of the wilds, which no human tongue can say properly, and then—*

And then the guild came. And then the breaking. And then she had knelt before the covenant-stone with tears freezing on her cheeks and asked, begged, demanded, that the price be taken from her—the memories, all of them, every hour of him—because the alternative was to carry the grief of a murdered pact for the rest of a long life, and she had looked at the man whose face she was asking to lose and said, *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I would rather not know you than mourn you forever,* and he had held her hands and said yes. He had said yes. That was the part that lived in him like a splinter worked too deep to draw: not that she had forgotten, but that he had agreed to be forgotten. He had been the one who said *it will hurt less this way.* He had been wrong.

"Renn."

He looked up. She was watching him across the fire, and her eyes had that quality they got sometimes—since the chapel, more often—a searching, almost startled quality, as if she kept catching sight of something familiar in his face and losing it again before she could name it.

"When you look at me like that," she said slowly, "I feel like I'm being remembered by someone."

The fish dripped fat into the coals. Somewhere out in the dark water, a bird cried once and fell silent.

"You remind me of someone," he said. It was even true.

"Who?"

"A woman who was braver than she believed."

Something moved behind her eyes—hurt, or hope, or the two of them wearing each other's clothes. She looked away first, back to her map, and the conversation closed like water over a dropped stone.

That was how it always went, these days. She reached; he withdrew. She asked; he deflected. And every time, the shard in his pack hummed a little louder, as if the covenant itself were impatient with him, as if twelve years of careful silence were an insult to the thing they had both bled to build.

*Let her heal first,* he told himself, the same words he had told himself on the road south, in the ash market, in the cellar beneath her shop while she stitched his wounds and insulted his table manners. *Let her become whole before you hand her the weight of what was lost. If you tell her now—if she learns that the blurred face on the wall was yours, that the voice she heard was yours, that she chose to forget you and you let her—*

If she learned it now, raw from the chapel, with eleven embers still unfound and the moon turning overhead, it would not break her gently. It would break her the way the wards broke: all at once, everywhere, with nothing left standing after.

He fed her the best portion of the fish without appearing to. She noticed anyway. She always noticed.

* * *

On the third day in the fen, the water rose.

It came without warning, the way fen-water did—not a flood but a decision, the whole marsh inhaling at once, and the causeway stones vanished beneath a foot of brown current moving with quiet purpose. They waded. By midday they swam. Odile's pack went high on Renn's shoulders, and the ember against her chest glowed faintly through her wet shirt, a coal-colored star that the murk seemed to bend around rather than swallow.

At dusk they found higher ground: a long spine of ancient masonry rising from the water like the back of a sleeping animal. Renn hauled Odile up onto it and realized, shaking the water from his ears, that it was not natural stone. It was cut. Dressed blocks, mortared, the top of some vast structure drowned centuries ago. A temple, maybe. A wall.

A wall.

His stomach turned over. Of all the drowned things in all the fens of the world, the marsh had chosen to give them this—a fragment of dressed stone with carvings worn nearly smooth, and when Odile brushed the silt from it with her sleeve, the shapes beneath were unmistakable. Interlocking figures. A human hand and a paw, joined at the wrist. The old sigil of the Ember Covenant, older than any kingdom, older than the guild that had betrayed it.

Odile went very still.

"I've seen this before," she whispered.

"It's a common sigil. Charm-sellers copy it for luck—"

"No." She touched the carved wrist where hand met paw, and the ember flared against her chest, and Renn saw her pupils blow wide with returning light. "No, I've *seen* it. On a gate. On a great gate in a great wall, and there were banners, and there was snow coming down, and—" Her breath caught. "There was a man. He was laughing. Why do I remember a man laughing?"

Because it was you, Renn thought. Because you had never seen snow until you came north to the wall, and you caught flakes on your tongue like a child, and when I asked the daughter of a Vaeli charmwright what she found so amusing about my homeland, you said, *the idea that anyone could live here and stay grim.* You laughed until the sentries smiled. You laughed, and I decided in that moment that I would spend my life earning the sound.

"He might have been anyone," Renn said.

Her head came around sharply. For a moment he thought she would argue—he could see the argument assembling itself behind her teeth—but then something in his face stopped her. Some fracture in the stillness he wore. She studied him the way she studied a charm that wouldn't take: patiently, thoroughly, looking for the flaw.

"You grieve," she said at last, quietly. "Did you know that? You carry yourself like a soldier and you joke like a mercenary, but you grieve. I've watched you do it when you think I'm asleep. You sit up and you hold your own hands and you breathe like a man carrying something heavy up a long stair." Her voice softened. "Who did you lose, Renn?"

The water lapped against the drowned wall. The ember pulsed. Out in the darkening fen, the reeds whispered their endless whisper, and for one terrible moment the truth climbed his throat like floodwater, and he opened his mouth—

And saw her, instead, kneeling before the covenant-stone twelve years ago, begging to forget. Saw what knowing would do to her here, now, wet and exhausted and three embers short of salvation, with the Crown's Wardens somewhere behind them and the thirteenth moon turning above. Love, he had learned in exile, was not always speech. Sometimes love was a held tongue. Sometimes love was standing in the doorway of a burning house and choosing which rooms to carry out and which to let burn, and he had chosen, and he would keep choosing, night after night, until the moons turned or he broke.

"No one," he said. "I lost no one. I lost a country. It's not the same."

"It isn't?"

"Countries don't miss you back."

The ghost of a smile touched her mouth—there and gone, like a fish rising—and she let it go. Let him go. She spread her wet cloak on the stone and lay down facing the water, and within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep, the deep sleep of the truly spent.

Renn sat watch, as he always did, and held his own hands, and breathed like a man climbing stairs.

* * *

Near midnight, the shard woke.

It had been humming for days—ever since the chapel, ever since the first ember settled against her skin—but now it sang, a thin urgent note through the leather of his pack, and Renn pulled it out and cupped it in his palms and felt it pulling. North. Always north, but stronger tonight, insistent, a compass needle that had finally found its pole.

And beneath the pull, faint but unmistakable, another sensation. One he had not felt in twelve years and had never expected to feel again.

An answer.

The shard was covenant-stone, a fragment of the great stone at the wall's heart where the original pact had been signed in the mingled blood of both peoples. It remembered. And somewhere far to the north, past the marshes, past the ruined borderlands, something else of the old covenant was remembering back—calling, or being called, the way two tuning-forks wake each other across a room.

Renn closed his fists around the shard until the edges bit.

He knew what it meant. He had known since the stars went out, if he was honest, had known it in the marrow-deep way wolf-kin knew weather: the embers were not scattered treasures to be gathered like coins. They were pieces of a sentence. Thirteen fragments of one promise, and when the last piece slotted home, the sentence would speak itself aloud again—and whatever it said, it would say to everyone. To the wolves in the high passes. To the Crown in its palace. To Odile Winterbourne, who had signed it once in blood and bought her forgetting with the same ink.

When the covenant spoke again, it would say her name. And his beside it. Bound together in the old formula, the marriage-clause, the vow the founders had written when they still believed love between realms was a foundation and not a liability:

*Two names, one oath, till the moons forget the sky.*

She would learn it then, whether he spoke or not. The stone would tell her. The blood would tell her. Everything he was hoarding, everything he was suffering to protect her from, would arrive at once, on the covenant's schedule, in the covenant's voice—with no gentleness in it at all.

Unless he found the words first.

Renn looked at her sleeping form on the drowned wall, hood slick with mist, one hand curled near the ember's glow as if even unconscious she was guarding it. Twelve years he had rehearsed this confession—in border taverns, on frozen roads, in the gray hours before dawn. Twelve years of sentences begun and abandoned. *You knew me. You loved me. You asked to forget me, and I said yes, and I have regretted the yes every single day of a life that has otherwise been nothing but regret.* Every version ended the same way: with her face going through recognition, and horror, and grief, and arriving at the question he could not bear to answer.

*Why didn't you tell me?*

Why indeed. Why had he let her sell charms in a night market for twelve years, small and safe and hollow, while he circled the city like a moth around a lamp? Because she had asked. Because her last clear wish, her final act of will before the erasure took her, had been to live without the weight of him. And he had honored it the only way a coward could—by staying near enough to watch, far enough to be forgotten, loving her in the grammar of silence.

But the moons did not care for grammar. The thirteenth moon was turning, and the shard was singing, and the fen was ending somewhere ahead of them, and beyond the fen the road ran north into the high country where the answers lived.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would find a way to begin. Not the whole of it—never the whole of it, not yet—but a corner of it. A thread she could pull. *I knew you before,* he might say. *Before the wards fell, I knew you.* And see what her face did. See whether hope or terror won.

Behind him, in her sleep, Odile spoke.

It was barely a breath, a word shaped by dream, and the wind almost took it—but the fen was still tonight, unnaturally still, and the syllables came to him whole and clear across the drowned stone:

"—Renn."

Not his name as she knew it. His name as she had once said it—the old-tongue form, the intimate form, the form that meant *my heart's own*, the form no living soul had used in twelve years because no living soul remained who knew it.

She said it softly, the way one says a thing one says often. Then she sighed, and turned in her sleep, and was quiet.

Renn sat motionless until the sky began to gray, the shard singing in his hands, the ember glowing against her breast, and understood at last that the choice he had been making every night for twelve years was no longer his to make. The memories were coming back on their own. Piece by piece, ember by ember, her mind was finding its way home—and it was walking faster than he could run.

Far to the north, past the fen's edge, a light kindled on the horizon that was not dawn: a cold silver radiance climbing above the hills, round and vast and wrong, a moon where no moon had any business being.

The thirteenth moon, showing its face.

Renn stood, shouldered the pack, and went to wake her. Whatever he said next, he would have to start saying it soon.

Because the silence, at last, was running out.
