## 13. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The marsh road gave out a mile past the drowned chapel, and what replaced it was not so much a path as an argument the land was having with itself—black water threading through sedge, hummocks of peat that looked solid and were not, and everywhere the smell of rot sweetened by something almost like flowers. Odile learned to walk the way Renn walked: weight on the outside of the foot, never trusting the ground's first offer.

They had been three days out of the chapel. The first ember sat against her sternum now, sewn into a leather pouch Renn had made from—she had decided not to ask—a strip of oiled hide. It was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and sometimes, when she forgot herself, she could feel it keeping time with her heart.

"You're limping," Renn said, without turning around.

"I'm walking with dignity."

"You're limping with dignity."

"Then we understand each other."

He stopped at the crest of a hummock and stood very still, ears angled forward beneath his hood, nostrils working the wind. She had catalogued his stillnesses by now—the listening one, the scenting one, the one she privately thought of as *deciding whether to tell me something*—and this was the third kind. When he turned back to her, his gray eyes had gone flat and careful.

"There's a light ahead," he said. "A lamp, not a fire. And someone's been burning moon-herb."

"Is that bad?"

"It means an astronomer." He said the word the way other men said *debt collector*. "Or a madman. In the marshes they're often the same."

* * *

The house should not have existed. That was her first thought, standing before it in the last gray light: that the marsh had simply agreed, for a while, to pretend this island was dry, and that the pretense could end at any moment. It was a low stone structure with a domed roof split open like an egg, and through the crack in the dome spilled lamplight and, above it, a slice of sky.

The man who opened the door was old in the way of things left out in weather—browned, cracked, but unmistakably solid. He wore seven coats layered over each other despite the season, and his beard was braided into three tails tied with copper wire. He looked at Renn for a long moment, then at Odile, then at the place where her coat gapped and showed the pouch's edge.

"Well," he said. "You'd better come in before the marsh changes its mind."

Inside, the dome's interior was painted floor to ceiling—or would have been, if there had been a floor. Every surface was covered in charts: inked circles within circles, moons upon moons, some drawn in careful lines and others scratched in with what looked like fingernails. A brass orrery dominated the center of the room, its rings frozen mid-turn, clogged with dust except where a single arm had been polished bright by years of touching.

"I am Emeric Voss," the old man said, "formerly Astronomer Royal to the Crown of Vael, currently keeper of the only correct calendar in the world." He said it without pride, the way a man announces he is the last speaker of a dying language. "And you are the signatory."

Odile's hand went to the pouch before she could stop it. "I don't know what I am."

"No," Emeric agreed. "That's rather the point, isn't it?"

Renn positioned himself between the door and Odile—an old habit, she'd noticed, though she couldn't have said how long he'd been doing it—and asked the practical question. "How do you know what she carries?"

Emeric laughed, a dry rustling sound. "Wolf-kin. Twelve years behind your wall and you still think knowledge travels by roads." He shuffled to a shelf crammed with brass instruments and jars of something dark. "I know because I've spent thirty years watching the sky refuse to behave, and because three nights ago every star in the northern quarter went dark and came back wrong. And because—" he pointed a knotted finger at Odile's chest, "—that ember has been pulling on the tides since the wards fell. I can feel it in my instruments. The whole marsh can feel it. Why do you think the eels have been climbing trees?"

"The eels are climbing trees," Odile repeated flatly.

"Everything is climbing everything, girl. The world knows its appointment is coming even if you don't." He fixed her with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk. "Sit down. This will take some telling, and the moon won't wait on my manners."

* * *

What followed came at Odile in pieces, the way all truths about her life had come since the stars went out—fragments she had to hold up to each other until they made a shape.

Emeric cleared a space among his charts and began laying them down like a card sharp dealing a losing hand.

"Every child in Vael learns there is one moon," he said. "Silver, faithful, monthly. What the Crown does not teach—what the Crown has *forbidden* me to teach—is that there were always two." His finger traced a circle on the oldest chart, a circle drawn in faded red ink. "The second moon is not hidden. It is *held*. Bound at the border wall by the same covenant that binds the realms together. The Ember Covenant did not merely make peace between wolf-kin and men. It anchored the two worlds to each other. One moon for each realm, and the wall between them holding both in place."

Odile thought of the shard in her palm, the blurred face beside her on the wall. "And when the covenant broke—"

"Twelve years ago, yes." Emeric's voice went quiet. "When your guild's pact shattered, the anchor loosened. The thirteenth moon—that's what the old texts call it, the moon that belongs to neither realm—began to drift. Slowly. So slowly no one without my instruments would ever see it." He tapped the orrery's frozen rings. "It takes roughly thirteen years for it to complete one full cycle away from us. Thirteen years of drifting, and then—" he spread his hands, "—the tide goes out and does not come back."

"That's nonsense," Odile said, but her voice sounded thin even to her. "Realms don't just—drift apart. We're standing on one of them."

"We are standing on a boat, girl, moored to another boat, and someone cut the rope." Emeric leaned close, and she smelled copper wire and old ink. "Why do you think the stars went out? Not vanished—*moved*. When the anchor fails, the sky itself begins to slip. Your Crown sealed the border because they think the wilds are invading. They're not invading. They're *drifting closer*, the way two boats drift together when the current takes them, and if they touch before the covenant is reforged—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Odile had seen the border wards collapse; she had felt the night the stars went out like a hand closing around her heart. Two worlds grinding against each other, with everything she loved caught in the seam.

"And the embers?" Renn asked. His voice was carefully level, but Odile heard the strain under it, the sound of a man holding a door shut. "Thirteen embers. Thirteen moons."

Emeric turned to him with something like pity. "You knew more than you let yourself say, exile. Yes. The covenant was forged in thirteen parts—one ember for each turn of the hidden moon's cycle, one anchor-point for each year of grace. Scattered, they cannot hold. Gathered, reforged, signed anew—" he looked at Odile, "—they bind the realms again. But the signing must happen before the cycle completes. Before the thirteenth moon finishes its drift and the window closes forever."

"How long?" Odile asked.

Emeric crossed to the orrery. He took a cloth from his pocket and, with the tenderness of a priest handling relics, wiped the dust from the frozen rings. Then he took the polished arm—the one bright from years of touching—and moved it along its track, counting under his breath, adjusting, counting again.

When he finished, he did not look at her right away. That was how she knew.

"Three turns," he said finally. "Three turns of the silver moon. The thirteenth completes its cycle at the dark of the third moon from tonight. After that, the drift becomes permanent. The realms pull apart, the wilds go dark, and every charmwright's art dies with the connection between them." He straightened, and his old spine cracked audibly. "Including yours."

* * *

She stepped outside afterward because the walls felt too close, because the charts were moons and the moons were deadlines and every surface of that room was counting down at her. The marsh air was cold and smelled of iron. Above the broken dome, the sky had cleared, and the stars were out—but she found she couldn't look at them anymore without checking, without searching for the ones that had gone dark and come back wrong.

Renn found her there. He didn't speak at first. He had gotten good at that—standing beside her in silence like a wall she could lean on without admitting she needed one.

"Three turns," she said. "We have one ember. We need thirteen. And the Crown wants me dead or worse, and half the realm between here and wherever the rest of these embers are hiding." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I sold charms for a living, Renn. Love charms that worked maybe one time in five. Fever charms. Charms to keep moths out of wool. Nobody told me I'd signed up to hold two worlds together with string."

"Nobody told you anything," Renn said quietly. "That was the cruelty of it."

She glanced at him. There it was again—that flicker, that half-sentence swallowed, the look of a man standing at the edge of something he couldn't jump. Since the chapel, since the memory of the wall and the oath and the face she couldn't see, those flickers had been coming faster. He knew something. He knew many somethings. And every time she got close to asking, he retreated behind those gray eyes like a door closing.

"Why do you keep doing that?" she asked.

"Doing what?"

"Starting sentences you don't finish. Looking at me like I'm a letter you're afraid to open." She turned to face him fully. "If you know what I was—if you know who I was—twelve years ago, you owe me more than silence. Three turns, Renn. I don't have time for your discretion."

For a moment she thought he would break. His jaw worked; his hands curled at his sides; the wind moved his hood and showed the sharp line of his ear, too pointed to be human, and he didn't bother hiding it. Whatever he was about to say gathered in him like a wave—

And broke against the door opening behind them.

"Emeric says you'll want provisions," Renn said, his voice gone smooth and closed as river ice. "And he says there's a ford east of here you'll need to cross, and that the pack that holds it doesn't take kindly to strangers. Especially ones carrying embers."

The moment folded itself away. Odile let it go—not because she wanted to, but because the word *pack* had landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, and she was still listening for it to hit bottom.

"A pack," she said. "Wild wolves."

"Wolf-kin who never made peace. Who stayed wild when the rest of us signed." Renn's mouth twisted. "They'll smell the ember on you from a mile off. And they'll smell what I am, and judge me for it."

"Judge you for what? Being exiled?"

"For *leaving*," he said, and there was such weight in the single word that she understood it was not about geography.

From inside the dome, Emeric's voice floated out, thin and cracked and utterly certain: "The ford is two days' walk, and the moon rises full tomorrow night. If you mean to cross, you'd best mean it quickly."

Odile looked north, past the black lace of the sedge, to where the horizon held a darkness deeper than night—the direction of the wall, the direction of everything she had lost and couldn't remember losing. Somewhere out there, twelve more embers waited, and a moon she had never seen was falling away from her, taking the world's future with it.

Two days to the ford. Three turns to the end of everything.

"All right," she said to the darkness, and to the moon she couldn't see, and to whatever part of herself had signed her name twelve years ago and paid for it in forgetting. "Let's find out what else I promised."
