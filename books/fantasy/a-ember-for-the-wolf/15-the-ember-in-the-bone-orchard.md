## 15. The Ember in the Bone Orchard

The dead of the northern wilds did not rest in the ground the way Vael's dead did. Renn had explained this twice on the road from the wolves' ford, once patiently and once with the flat tone of a man repeating himself to a child, and both times Odile had nodded as though she understood. She had not understood. In Vael, graves were holes in the earth closed over with stone, sealed against worms and weather and memory alike. Here, the dead were given *up*—raised into the branches on woven platforms, wrapped in birch bark, so that they might go on watching the forest that had fed them.

Which was why, at the edge of a clearing where the pines grew tall and close as cathedral columns, Odile stopped walking and said, "That's not a tree."

It was a tree. It was also, unmistakably, full of people.

Dozens of pale bundles hung among the black branches, swaying faintly in a wind she couldn't feel below the canopy. Birch-bark wrappings had silvered with age until they caught what little light filtered down, and for one vertiginous moment the whole orchard seemed to bloom—white blossoms on black boughs, each blossom shaped like a sleeping body.

"The bone orchard," Renn said quietly. He had shifted back to his human shape an hour past; he always did before places like this, though he never said why. "The wild packs have kept it since before the wall went up. Every wolf-kin who died under the old covenant is here somewhere."

"And the ember?"

"Buried." He almost smiled at his own phrasing. "Planted. Under the eldest tree, with the first signatories. The pack will have left it alone because the pack knows better than to dig up its grandmothers."

Odile looked at him. "And we're going to dig up their grandmothers."

"We're going to ask," Renn said. "There's a difference. There's always a difference, with the dead. You taught me that." Then his jaw tightened, the way it always did when a sentence slipped out of him wearing more history than he intended. He turned away before she could ask, and began untying the offering bundle from his pack: dried fish, honeycomb wrapped in waxed cloth, a twist of juniper smoke.

She followed him into the trees.

* * *

The orchard smelled of cold resin and old sweetness, the way a hive smells after the bees are gone. Underfoot, the earth was soft and strangely clean—no bracken, no rot—and Odile realized with a small chill that the forest floor here had been tended, swept by generations of careful hands.

Renn moved through the hanging dead without hurry, touching each platform's support rope as he passed, murmuring names too low for her to catch. Some of the bundles were small. One was very small. Odile made herself look anyway, because looking away felt like a kind of theft.

At the center of the clearing stood a pine older than all the others, its trunk broad enough that three people linking hands could not have circled it. Its lowest branches held the most wrappings, and these were different—darker bark, stitched with thread that had once been red. Beneath it, half-hidden by roots that had risen like knuckles from the soil, lay a flat stone carved over with lines of script.

Renn knelt and brushed the leaf-litter from the stone with his sleeve. "This is the register. Every name that signed, and every name the signing cost. The ember sits beneath it." He glanced up at her, and in the dim green light his gray eyes were hard to read. "Once you take it, there's no putting it back. You understand that. The last one showed you a wall and a blurred face. This one—" he hesitated, choosing words the way a man chooses footing on scree, "—the second embers are heavier. They show you costs. Who paid them."

"You could tell me what you know instead," Odile said. It was an old argument between them now, worn smooth with repetition. "You remember everything. You could just *tell* me."

"It doesn't work that way." His voice didn't rise, but something under it did. "A told memory is a story. A returned memory is yours. If I hand you mine, you'll spend the rest of your life wondering if I lied about the shape of it." He looked down at the stone again. "I won't be the one who decides what your life was. That has to come back to you on its own terms or it isn't real."

"That's very noble," Odile said, "and also very convenient."

"I'm aware." And there—he almost laughed, brief and unwilling, and for a moment the clearing felt less like a grave and more like a place two people had once argued happily. Then it passed, the way those moments always passed with him, folding itself away into some room inside him she wasn't permitted to enter.

He set out the offerings at the base of the great pine: fish, honey, smoke. Then he stepped back and gestured her forward. "Your name is in the ledger. It should be your voice that asks."

Odile crouched by the register stone. The script was old covenant-tongue, angular as frost, but as her fingers hovered above the grooves the letters warmed to meaning the way the shard had once burned meaning into her palm. Names. Dozens of names. And near the top, cut deeper than all the others:

*WINTERBOURNE.*

Not once. Three times. Three entries, spaced years apart, each bearing a date, and the third date—

Her breath went thin.

The third date was twelve years ago. The third entry read, beneath her own name, in the same deliberate hand: *given to the orchard. mourned. remembered.*

"Renn," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, the way a floor surprises you by being solid. "Why does this stone say I'm buried here?"

* * *

He came to stand beside her slowly, the way men approach a snare they can't yet see the teeth of. He read the stone. She watched him read it, watched his face do several things in quick succession—recognition, grief, and then a terrible, disciplined blankness, the face of someone closing shutters before a storm arrives.

"Renn."

"There are explanations," he said.

"Then give me one."

"Not before you take it." He turned to face her fully, and his hands rose as if to hold her shoulders and then thought better of it and fell. "Listen to me. Whatever it shows you—and it will show you something, the second ones always do—you have to remember that memories aren't verdicts. A memory is just a moment that happened. It doesn't mean the moment still holds. Things end. People survive things. *You survived things.*"

"You're saying that like you know which thing."

"Odile."

"Don't say my name like that. Don't say my name like you've said it a thousand times in rooms I can't remember." Her hands had begun to shake and she hated them for it. Above them the birch-wrapped dead swayed in their silence, dozens of witnesses, none of them able to tell her anything except that everyone ended up in a tree eventually, and some of them apparently ended up there twice.

Renn exhaled through his nose, slow. "Ask permission first," he said finally. "That part matters. Put your hand on the stone and say why you've come. Then dig. And whatever you see—come back to the sound of my voice. That's not poetry, it's practical advice. The embers pull. You'll want to stay inside them. Don't."

She wanted to argue. She wanted, more than argument, to walk out of this clearing and keep walking south until the trees gave up and the world resumed making sense. Instead she knelt, laid her palm flat on the cold register stone, and said, in a voice pitched low enough that only the roots would hear it:

"My name is Odile Winterbourne. I'm the last signatory. I've come for what's mine."

The stone warmed under her hand like a living thing acknowledging receipt.

They dug together, mostly Renn, because her arms felt far away. Two feet down the spade rang against something that was neither root nor rock: a small coffer of gray wood, unrotted, dry as paper in wet earth. Inside, nested in a bed of juniper sprigs gone brown with age, lay the second ember—a bead of dull orange glass no bigger than a robin's egg, veined with darker threads, like a coal that had decided to stop pretending it was dead.

Odile reached in and took it.

The world folded.

* * *

She stood on a road she knew, and the knowing hit her like cold water, because she had never known it and knew it absolutely: the processional way outside Vael's north gate, the one lined with boundary stones, the one that led toward the wall. Snow falling in fat, unhurried flakes. Bells tolling from the city behind her—the deep mourning bells, nine strokes, the ones rung only for the Crown's own dead.

She was standing beside the road. No one saw her. She understood that immediately, the way one understands things in dreams—not with thought but with certainty. She was a witness placed here. She was the ghost of her own attention.

And coming up the road was a funeral procession.

Black horses. A bier borne on the shoulders of six wardens in dress livery, their halberds reversed, blades trailing in the snow. Behind them walked a column of Charmwrights in guild gray, hoods up, and she recognized the set of shoulders on the nearest one—Maren, old Maren, who had taught her knotwork and died, Odile had been told, the winter after Odile joined the guild. Maren was alive here. Maren was crying here, silently, snowflakes melting on her cheeks.

On the bier lay a body in white.

The procession drew level with her, slow as syrup, and Odile looked down into the open coffin and saw herself.

Younger. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Hair loose, hands folded on her breastbone, a sprig of juniper tucked beneath her fingers. Her face was calm in the way faces in coffins are calm, arranged rather than resting, but there was a small detail wrong with the picture and it took her a long moment to find it: the body was breathing. Barely. The shallowest lift of the breastbone, once every few seconds, invisible unless you were standing where a ghost could stand, staring straight down.

*Alive.* They were burying her alive—or carrying her somewhere to be buried, or carrying her somewhere else entirely, dressed as a corpse, and every mourner in the line either didn't know or had agreed to the lie.

The bells tolled nine again. The procession moved past her, and she turned to follow it because there was nothing else a ghost could do, and as she fell into step she saw who walked at the head of the line, just behind the horses.

A man in a traveling cloak with the hood thrown back, snow gathering in dark hair. Tall. Broad-shouldered. Walking with his head bowed, his hands clenched at his sides, and when he lifted his face to glance back at the bier—checking, checking, the way a nurse checks a fevered child—his features were—

Blurred. Smoothed over, like a thumbprint smudging wet ink. The same blur from the wall. The same faceless presence that stood beside her in every returned memory, guarding its identity the way a vault guards its contents.

But this time she heard his voice. The ember was generous that way, or cruel that way. He spoke to the warden marching beside him, low, urgent, and the words carried to her across the snow with perfect clarity:

"—she's barely holding. The rite takes more out of the signer every time. Tell me honestly, Warden—if the third working fails, what happens to her?"

And the warden, an older woman with a scarred jaw, answered: "What happens to anyone who spends a death they weren't given. Either the covenant accepts the payment in full and she stays dead, or it refuses her and sends her back. There is no middle, wolf-kin. That's the whole horror of it."

*Wolf-kin.* The blurred man was wolf-kin, walking openly in a Vael funeral column, mourning her, asking after her survival with a rawness that scraped the air even through the blur.

The procession turned off the processional way, onto a track she'd never seen, toward a low stone building with a green door. Not a tomb. A hospice? A hiding place? The door opened onto darkness, and the bearers carried her in, and the blurred man followed, and just before the threshold took him the ember-memory did something it hadn't done before: it slowed, and turned, and let her see the last thing her own eyes had seen, from inside the body on the bier.

Looking up past the coffin's rim at falling snow, at the blurred face leaning over her, hearing him whisper—whisper to *her*, to the almost-dead girl in the box—words she felt rather than heard, words the ember refused to give her, sealing them away like letters in a drawer.

Then the vision closed like a hand around a candle flame.

* * *

She came back to the sound of Renn's voice.

"—Odile. Odile. Come back. Come *back*—" His hands were on her shoulders, shaking her gently, and his face was very close and very frightened, and behind the fear sat something else, something older and larger, grief wearing fear as a mask. She surfaced into her body the way a drowning swimmer surfaces, gasping, and found she was sitting in the snow at the foot of the great pine with the ember clutched in her fist so hard its edges had printed themselves into her palm.

"They buried me," she said. Her voice sounded borrowed. "I saw my own funeral, Renn. I was in the coffin and I was *breathing*. There was a warden who said—" she pressed the heel of her free hand against her eye, trying to press the images into order, "—she said either the covenant accepted my death or refused it. Refused it. Sent me back. What does that mean? What *rite*? What third working?"

Renn hadn't let go of her shoulders. Up close, in the green gloom, she watched him weigh truth against mercy and lose, the way he always lost lately, the way he'd been losing since the ford.

"A signatory can pay the covenant's price in advance," he said quietly. "When a working is too large for the power at hand, the signer may spend years of themselves—memories, strength, time. Or—" his throat moved, "—or spend the death they were owed. Die early, in ritual, so the covenant holds the death in reserve and grants the signer its weight in power. Three workings, twelve years ago, needed that much power. So you spent your death. All of it. The covenant took it, and you lived on borrowed hours, and the funeral was—" he faltered. "The funeral was necessary. The Crown believed you dead. It had to believe it, or the workings would have failed and everything after them would have been for nothing."

"Everything after them," Odile repeated. "The erasure. My memories. That was the third working."

He said nothing, which was its own answer, and she was too full of the other revelation to press it.

"But I'm alive." She looked at her own hands as if they might contradict her. "I'm alive twelve years later. How does a spent death come *back*?"

"I don't know." And the terrible thing, the thing that made the hair rise along her arms, was that he meant it—that he genuinely didn't know, that this was a mystery to him too, that the woman he had mourned at a false funeral was sitting in front of him breathing on time that should have run out a decade ago. "I've asked myself that question every day for twelve years," he said. "Every day, Odile. The covenant refused you. Or someone paid a debt on your behalf. Or—" he stopped.

"Or what?"

"Or the reforging needs you specifically, and the covenant has been keeping you the way a lender keeps collateral." He said it gently, but gently couldn't soften it. Somewhere overhead, a birch-wrapped dead shifted on its ropes, and the ropes creaked like a ship's rigging, like a cradle, like something rocking something to sleep.

Odile looked down at the ember in her fist. It glowed faintly now, warmer than before, and in its veined depths she thought she could see movement—not fire, not reflection, but the suggestion of figures, distant, waiting. Thirteen embers, Renn had said. Eleven still scattered. And she had already spent one death buying their recovery, and the covenant had handed it back for reasons no one understood, and somewhere ahead of her waited the final working, the reforging, whose price she did not yet know.

Wonder arrived late, the way dawn arrives in winter—but it arrived. Because underneath the horror of her own open-eyed coffin, underneath the bells and the snow and the blurred man's desperate voice, one fact shone through like a lamp through ice: *someone had loved her enough to walk in a funeral procession for a girl who wasn't dead, and check her breathing every hundred yards, and whisper to her in the dark of the bier.* Whoever he was. Whatever he had been to her.

She got up. Her legs held. She put the second ember into the lead-lined case at her belt, next to the first, and felt the two of them hum against each other like struck tuning forks finding a shared note.

"How far to the drowned chapel?" she asked.

Renn blinked. "Two days. Maybe three, with the marsh thawing."

"Then we shouldn't waste daylight." She picked up her pack. Around them the orchard swayed and creaked, dozens of the dead keeping their long watch, and as she turned to go she could have sworn—one heartbeat, no more—that the smallest wrapping, the very small one high in the eldest tree, swung toward her. As if something in it had noticed her passing. As if it knew her name.

Behind her, Renn stood a moment longer at the register stone, his fingers resting on the third carved entry—*given to the orchard, mourned, remembered*—and crossed out nothing, and changed nothing, and followed.
