## 16. Halvard's Bargain

The summons came at the hour when Vael's lamps burned lowest, and Halvard knew before he opened it what it would say.

He had been a Warden for nineteen years. He knew the weight of paper by its purpose. A patrol rotation was thin and cheap, like bread stretched with sawdust. A reprimand was heavy card stock, stiff as a magistrate's jaw. But a summons written on vellum, sealed in green wax, smelling faintly of the Queen's own myrrh—that was a thing that changed a man's life, and rarely for the better.

He read it standing in his doorway, the night air cold against his neck. *Captain Halvard of the Third Wardens will present himself before the Lord Chamberlain at first bell.* No reason given. Reasons were for men who could still refuse.

Behind him, his clerk—a boy named Petch with ink-stained fingers and a habit of asking questions—cleared his throat. "Bad news, Captain?"

"Ask me tomorrow," Halvard said, and went to find his good coat.

* * *

The Lord Chamberlain's office sat at the top of the Ministry of Borders, in a room designed to make visitors feel the precise altitude of their own insignificance. The windows looked down over the Ash Market, the charmwrights' quarter, the crooked streets where Halvard had spent three weeks chasing a signature of illegal magic that slipped through his net like water through cupped hands.

Corvane Ashgrave did not rise when Halvard entered. The Chamberlain was a narrow man built like a drawn bowstring, all tension and intent, with eyes the color of old pewter. He wore gray because gray was the color of the Crown's patience, and he liked people to know how patient the Crown could be.

"Captain." He gestured at a chair without looking at it. Halvard remained standing. He had learned long ago that sitting made a man shorter, and he could not afford to be shorter today.

"My lord."

"You've been hunting an unlicensed charmworker in the Third Ward for—" Corvane consulted a paper on his desk, though Halvard would have wagered his pension the man needed no consultation— "twenty-six days."

"Twenty-seven."

"And you've caught her?"

"No."

Something flickered across the Chamberlain's face—not anger. Interest. That was worse. "You don't sound ashamed of it."

"I'm not certain shame is warranted yet, my lord."

"Explain."

Halvard chose his words the way he'd choose footing on a frozen river. "The signatures we're tracking aren't amateur work. They're guild-trained, guild-disciplined, and they're being laid in patterns I haven't seen since the Charmwrights' Guild was dissolved. Whoever this woman is—and the market gossip says it's a woman, a charm-seller named Winterbourne—she isn't breaking the law out of greed or malice. She's practicing a craft the Crown outlawed wholesale, and doing it carefully enough that it took me nearly a month to even confirm she exists."

"And your point?"

"My point, my lord, is that careful magic is usually old magic. And old magic in Vael has a way of being connected to things the Crown buried rather than solved." He paused. "I think she may be tied to the Ember Covenant."

The silence that followed had texture. Halvard felt it settle on his shoulders like wet wool.

Corvane set down his pen with exaggerated care. "That name hasn't been spoken aloud in this building in twelve years, Captain. Not since the last signatory died and the wards came down and every star over the northern wilds went dark in a single night." He leaned forward. "Do you know what happened to the last man who investigated the Covenant?"

"I imagine he was promoted."

"He was reassigned to the salt marshes to count eel migrations until he drowned in a bog, quite by accident, quite unsolved." Corvane smiled without warmth. "So you'll understand my caution."

"I understand it perfectly, my lord."

"Good. Then understand this too." The Chamberlain rose and crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back, looking down at the city as if it were a board game mid-play. "The Crown is mobilizing. Within two turns of the moon, the border will be sealed permanently—new wards, Crown-built, Crown-keyed, no gaps, no wild crossings, nothing in or out of the northern wilds ever again. The age of half-measures is ending. And a woman running around the Third Ward selling resurrection-charms from a dead guild, with wolf-kin signatures all over her shop cellar—"

Halvard kept his face still, but something behind his ribs went cold. *Wolf-kin signatures.* They'd found the cellar, then. Or part of it.

"—is a loose thread," Corvane continued, "and loose threads get cut. Bring me Odile Winterbourne, Captain, alive and intact enough to answer questions, and you'll have Captaincy of the First Ward. Command of the border garrison. A house in the Palace Quarter. Everything your nineteen years have earned and everything they haven't."

It was a handsome offer. It was, in fact, the offer Halvard had been working toward since he was a farm boy with a sword too big for him and a head full of law. He heard himself say, "And if I bring her dead?"

"Then you'll have a commendation and a smaller house." Corvane turned from the window. "Alive is worth more. The Queen wants to know who else remembers the Covenant. Dead women keep poor secrets."

* * *

Halvard walked back across the city instead of taking the Warden's carriage, which was not like him. He told himself it was to clear his head. He told himself a great many things on that walk, past the shuttered guildhalls and the boarded charm-shops and the ward-lamps burning their dull, sanctioned orange—the color of magic the Crown approved of, which was to say, magic that had forgotten how to be anything more than decoration.

He stopped outside Odile Winterbourne's shop.

It had been raided two nights ago. He had led the raid himself, or rather, he had stood in the street while his sergeants did the breaking, because some part of him had already begun to distrust his own hands. They'd found the trapdoor. They'd found the cellar. They'd found blood on the cot down there—human blood, and something else, something the analysts' reagents couldn't name, turning their vials the color of storm-light.

They had not found the woman. They had not found the wolf.

His sergeant, Breck, had wanted to post watchers on every gate. Halvard had authorized two, at the south gates only, knowing full well there were six ways out of Vael that didn't involve gates at all. He had told himself it was efficiency. He had told himself a lot of things lately, and fewer and fewer of them survived daylight.

The shop's door hung splintered on one hinge. Through the gap he could see the interior: shelves overturned, glass ground into the floorboards, the smell of burnt sage and something older underneath, something like snow and pine and animal musk. On the wall above the ruined counter, someone had scrawled in charcoal—Breck's men, marking the premises condemned—but beneath the official mark, in a different hand, smaller and steadier, someone had written a single word:

*Remember.*

Halvard stood in the doorway a long time. A lamplighter passed and gave him the nod men give Wardens, that mixture of respect and wariness, and Halvard nodded back and did not move.

Nineteen years ago, fresh out of the academy, he had been assigned to the border archives as a junior records officer. Everyone hated the archives; it was where ambition went to die. But Halvard had loved it, quietly, the way some men love graveyards—for the stories under the stones. He had catalogued boxes of confiscated guild materials, and once, only once, he had opened a crate marked with a sigil he didn't recognize: a flame inside a circle of interlocking teeth. Inside had been ledgers, correspondence, and a treaty draft so beautiful in its construction that he had read it twice just to feel the architecture of it. Two peoples. One wall between them, and one promise stitched through the wall like thread through cloth. Shared watchfires. Shared winters. Shared names.

His commanding officer had found him reading it and gone white as tallow. The crate was sealed. Halvard was transferred within the month. He had assumed, for nineteen years, that the transfer was routine.

Now, standing in a wrecked charm-shop with the word *remember* staring at him like an accusation, he allowed himself to consider the possibility that it had not been routine at all. That the Crown had not merely outlawed the Covenant. That it had *buried* it—buried it alive, the way you bury something still breathing—and had been standing on the grave for twelve years, whistling, telling everyone the ground was empty.

And now the stars had gone out. Now the wards had fallen. Now a woman with guild-trained hands and a wolf in her cellar was gathering embers across the realm while the Crown scrambled to seal the border before anyone could ask why the sky over the wilds had gone blind.

*Loose threads get cut,* Corvane had said.

Or, Halvard thought, a thread gets pulled, and the whole garment comes apart, and you finally see what it was woven from.

* * *

He requested a private audience with the Lord Chamberlain the following morning, and got one, because ambitious captains requesting audiences were exactly the kind of thing Corvane Ashgrave collected.

"Well?" Corvane said, before Halvard had fully cleared the doorway. "Where is she?"

"That's what I've come to discuss, my lord. Not her location. My terms."

Corvane's eyebrows rose a fraction—an imperial measure of surprise, perhaps a sixteenth of an inch. "Terms. You were offered the First Ward yesterday."

"You offered me the First Ward for a capture I haven't made. I'm offering you something better for something better."

"Better than the First Ward." The Chamberlain's voice was dry as August. "This should be entertaining."

Halvard planted his feet. He had rehearsed this speech four times in the night, and each version had collapsed under scrutiny, and so he abandoned the rehearsal and simply said the true thing, which was the only kind of speech that had ever worked on dangerous men.

"I want access to the sealed guild archives. All of them. The Charmwrights' records, the Covenant correspondence, the confiscation crates from twelve years ago. Unsealed, uncensored, and with my name on the access ledger so there's no ambiguity about who read what."

The silence lasted long enough that Halvard heard the clock in the corner tick eleven times.

"You're refusing the promotion," Corvane said at last, slowly, like a man testing ice, "to ask permission to do paperwork."

"I'm refusing the promotion because I can't catch this woman until I understand what she's carrying. And I can't understand what she's carrying until I've read what the Crown took from the people who made it." He held the Chamberlain's gaze. "You want her found, my lord. So do I. But I've chased her for twenty-eight days on instinct and rumor, and I've learned exactly one useful thing: she isn't running *from* something. She's running *toward* something. Thirteen somethings, if the market talk is true. Embers. Fragments of the old Covenant. And every Warden in Vael could march on her tomorrow and it wouldn't matter, because you can't arrest a woman for walking toward a thing unless you know what the thing does when she reaches it."

"And you believe the archives will tell you."

"I believe the archives will tell me why the stars went out. Which seems, forgive me, my lord, like a question someone in this building ought to be urgently asking."

Corvane moved to the window again. Down below, the Ash Market was waking—stalls unfolding, awnings cranking open, the small commerce of small lives proceeding under lamps that burned the Crown's approved orange. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its performance quality. What remained was quieter and considerably more frightening.

"Do you know what I was doing twelve years ago, Captain? The night the wards fell?" He didn't wait for an answer. "I was a junior clerk in this very ministry. I watched the dispatches come in—one every few minutes, then one every few seconds, then a wall of them, towers reporting, garrisons reporting, the observatory reporting that the northern sky had simply *ended*, as if someone had snuffed a candle the size of heaven. And do you know what the Minister of Borders said to me, at midnight, with the reports piled to my chin?"

Halvard said nothing.

"He said: 'Burn the guild files. All of them. Tonight.'" Corvane turned. "Some were burned. Not all. Someone in the chain of custody was either careless or sentimental, and a portion of the archive survives in the deep vaults beneath this building. I have never read it. Do you know why?"

"Because reading it ends careers."

"Because reading it *changes* careers," Corvane corrected. "There's a difference. Men who go into those vaults curious come out something else. I've seen it happen twice. Once to a records officer named Halvard, twenty years ago, who opened a crate he shouldn't have and got shipped to the marshes for his trouble—yes, Captain, I know about that; I signed the transfer order myself, following instructions I didn't question, and I have wondered about it ever since. And once to a junior clerk who watched a minister order a bonfire at midnight and decided, right then, that whatever was in those files mattered enough to save some of them." His pewter eyes fixed on Halvard. "So understand what you're asking for. You're not asking for paperwork. You're asking to become the third man."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below, the city went on buying and selling, unaware that the sky had a hole in it.

"Yes," Halvard said. "That's exactly what I'm asking."

"And if you read those files and learn the Crown is guilty of what I suspect it's guilty of—what are you going to do about it? Because I'll tell you what the options are, Captain. There are two. You can put it back in the box and go catch your charmwoman and take your ward and your house and live comfortably ever after on top of the grave. Or you can pull the thread." Corvane came around the desk, close enough that Halvard could smell the myrrh on him. "And threads, once pulled, don't go back. Ask yourself whether you're a man who can live with a pulled thread. Most aren't."

Halvard thought of the word on the shop wall. *Remember.* He thought of a treaty draft so beautiful he'd read it twice. He thought of nineteen years of telling himself the transfer had been routine.

"I've been living on top of that grave for two decades, my lord," he said. "I find I'd rather know what's in it."

For a long moment Corvane studied him, and Halvard had the strange sensation of being weighed by a scale far larger than the room.

"The vaults are accessed by writ," the Chamberlain said finally. "Writs require two signatures. Mine, and the Minister's. The Minister signs anything I put in front of him after his third glass of wine, which occurs daily at approximately the fourth bell." He returned to his desk, drew a sheet of paper, and began to write. "You'll have your writ by evening. Read what you read, Captain, and tell no one—including me—until you must. If what's down there confirms your suspicions about the Winterbourne woman, then your hunt changes shape. You won't be bringing her to me."

"What will I be doing?"

Corvane finished writing and pressed his seal into green wax. He held the writ out, and for the first time since Halvard had known him, the Lord Chamberlain's face held something that was almost—almost—hope.

"You'll be deciding whose side the law is actually on," he said. "And Captain—if you find her first, before the border seals, before the Queen's new wardens close the net—you might ask her what the thirteenth moon means. Because I've read the dispatches from the observatory, and there's a moon up there that shouldn't exist, and it is very nearly done counting."

Halvard took the writ. It was lighter than he expected, and heavier than anything he'd ever carried.

* * *

The archive entrance lay behind the Ministry's record rooms, down a stair that smelled of stone and time, past a door with three locks and a sigil burned into the iron: a flame inside a circle of interlocking teeth.

Halvard stood before it with the writ in his hand and the lamplight trembling, and he thought of the farm boy with the too-big sword, and the junior clerk in the graveyard of ambitions, and the captain who had hunted a charmwoman for twenty-eight days and understood, only now, that he had never been hunting her at all.

He had been hunting the truth of a fire someone else had tried to smother.

He unlocked the first lock. Then the second. Then the third.

The door swung inward onto darkness, and the darkness breathed out the scent of old paper and older secrets, and somewhere far to the north, beneath a sky with a hole in it, a woman was pulling threads of her own.

Halvard stepped through, and pulled his.
