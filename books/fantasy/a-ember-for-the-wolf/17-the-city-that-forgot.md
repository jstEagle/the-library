## 17. The City That Forgot

The road bent where no road should have bent.

Odile noticed it first as a wrongness in her feet—a hitch in the rhythm of walking, as if the ground itself had flinched. For three days they had followed the old drover's track north through hill country gone gold with autumn, and for three days the track had run straight and sure beneath their boots. Now it swerved, wide and unnecessary, around a shallow valley that looked no different from a hundred others: bracken, birch, a stream threading silver through the middle of it.

"Renn." She stopped. "Look at the road."

He came back to stand beside her, gray eyes narrowing. He'd been ranging ahead in his wolf shape since dawn, and he still moved like the wolf even on two legs—weight forward, head slightly tilted, always listening to some frequency she couldn't hear.

"It goes around," he said.

"Roads don't go around nothing." She pointed at the valley floor, where the grass grew long and even, unbroken by wall or ruin or well. "Roads go through things. That's what roads are for. Someone paid good money to make this one curve."

"Or someone made it forget there was anything worth going straight for."

She looked at him. He was watching the valley with an expression she'd learned to read carefully over the weeks of their flight—not fear exactly, but the particular stillness of a creature that has smelled something dead upwind.

"You know something," she said.

"I know a smell." His nostrils flared. "Ash that isn't ash. Old burning, worked into the earth. And under it—" he hesitated, and she saw him decide whether to say the next thing, "—under it, charmwork. Guild work. Very old, very deep, and cut off clean, like a bell silenced mid-ring."

Odile's hand went without thinking to the pouch at her belt, where two embers lay wrapped in wool and warded in charms of her own making. They had been warm all morning. She had told herself it was the sun.

"The map," she said.

They spread Halvard's stolen survey chart across a flat stone—the one she'd lifted from his saddlebag in the chaos after the Ash Market, the one whose margins were annotated in a clerk's cramped hand. She'd studied it a dozen times. Now she studied it again, properly, tracing the route they'd walked from the marshes northward, and felt the cold arrive in her stomach before her mind caught up.

There was no valley here. There was no bend in the road. The drover's track ran ruler-straight across the chart, through the heart of the depression, and where the stream should have been there was only blank vellum—and not blank the way paper is blank when nothing has been written on it. Blank the way a page is blank when something has been scraped away. When she tilted the stone to catch the light, she could see it: a ghost of ink, a shadow where letters had been lifted off the surface, and beneath them, faint as breath on glass, the outline of a name.

*Hollowmere.*

"I've never heard of Hollowmere," she said.

"No," Renn said quietly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They went down into the valley because Odile could not make herself walk past it.

She told herself it was caution—they needed water, and the stream was there, and it was foolish to camp on open hills when shelter offered itself. But that was a lie she let herself tell, the way she had once let herself believe she was only a charm-seller, only a small woman with small magic, only someone who had never stood on any wall and sworn anything to anyone. The truth was simpler and worse. Something in her chest pulled toward that valley like a hook set behind the sternum, the same pull she'd felt outside the drowned chapel, outside the bone orchard, outside every place the covenant had left its fingerprints. The embers knew the way home. And whatever had happened here, it belonged to the same family of wounds.

The village announced itself gradually, the way grief does. First a fence post, rotted soft, standing alone in the bracken. Then a lane—cobbles under the grass, rounded and mossy, but unmistakably laid by human hands. Then an orchard, trees gone wild and crooked, still bearing small hard apples that no one had picked in years, if the windfalls moldering black beneath them meant anything.

Then the houses.

They were stone, low-roofed, built in the old style she'd seen only in the guildhall's oldest engravings—before Vael grew tall and proud and started building in imported marble. Doors stood open or hung off hinges. A loom sat in one doorway with thread still strung through the heddles, bleached colorless by seasons of weather. In another house, a table was set for a meal: bowls, spoons, a clay jug tipped on its side. Dust lay over everything in a smooth, undisturbed gray, thick as snowfall, and nothing—not a cup, not a chair, not a single spoon—had been touched since it fell.

"Renn." Her voice came out strange. "Where is everyone?"

He stood in the middle of the lane, head high, reading the air. When he answered, his voice had the rough edge it got when the wolf was close to the surface.

"Gone. Not dead—there's no death-smell, no old blood, nothing under the dust. Gone *clean*. This wasn't a raid or a plague or wolves at the gates." He turned slowly, taking in the empty doorways. "This was taken out. Root and branch. Every living soul, every memory of them, every mark they left on the world's ledger."

"People don't just vanish."

"People don't," he agreed. "But covenants do. And places built on covenant-ground—" he crouched, pressed his palm flat against the cobbles, and Odile watched the fur ripple up his forearm as something in the stones answered him, or failed to answer him, "—this was covenant-ground once. I can feel the channels under the town. Ley-veins, your guild would have called them. They're not broken. Broken would be better. They've been *sealed*. Capped with Crown-work, poured over the top like lead over a well."

Odile knelt beside him and did what she had spent twelve years pretending she didn't know how to do. She opened her senses to the place.

It was like pressing her ear to a door and hearing screaming on the other side—very far away, very quiet, but unmistakable. Under the seal, under the silence, the village's magic was still alive. Stunted, starving, pressed down into a space too small for it, but alive. Charmwork doesn't die easy; she'd built her whole small life on that fact, selling little wards and luck-knots that outlasted their buyers. Whatever the Crown had done here, it hadn't killed the town's magic. It had buried it breathing.

And she recognized the seal-work. That was the worst of it. The signature woven through the cap-stone was formal, bureaucratic, precise—the same hand, metaphorically speaking, that had drafted the Warden's charter, the same grammar of authority that ran through every tax stamp and border writ in Vael. She had seen its cousin on Halvard's ember-glass pendant. She had felt its like pressing down on the Charmwrights' Guild for decades, licensing them into irrelevance, regulating them into forgetting what they'd once been.

The guildhall ledger rose in her memory: thirteen embers, her own name dated twelve years past, and above the list, in faded ink, the covenant's founding clause. *Let the two realms see each other clearly.*

Someone had been working in the opposite direction for a very long time.

"They didn't erase this village because it broke a law," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's—flat, cold, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "They erased it because it *remembered*. Because there was magic in the ground and people who lived alongside it, and that's a problem when your whole kingdom is built on the claim that magic-touched places are lawless and dangerous and need the Crown's protection." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Protection. Gods. We're not being protected. We're being *edited*."

Renn looked at her sideways. "You're shaking."

"I'm angry."

"I know. I can smell it." He said it without mockery, which somehow made it worse—or better. "Good. Anger with a direction is worth more than courage."

* * *

They found the shrine at dusk.

It stood behind the last house, half-swallowed by a briar thicket: a small round building, older than everything else, its walls marked with carvings worn nearly smooth. Odile pushed through the briars, arms bleeding, because she already knew what she would find inside and needed to be proven right about something, even something terrible.

Inside there was no altar to any god of Vael. There was a hearth, cold for decades, and above it a carved relief of two figures facing each other across a flame—one human, one wolf-headed, their hands meeting over the fire. Around them ran a ring of script in the old covenant-hand, the same script she'd found in the guild ledger, the same script that now lived in her own skin in ways she was still discovering.

At the base of the hearth, someone had chiseled out a section of the carving. The gouges were deliberate, methodical, official. But chisels miss things. In the corner of the relief, protected by a ridge of stone, one small detail survived untouched: a third figure, tiny, standing between the two larger ones—a woman with her arms raised, holding the flame aloft.

A signatory. A bridge. Someone who had stood in the fire's path so both sides could see each other.

Odile sank down onto the cold hearthstone, and the anger that had been carrying her all day finally found its floor and cracked through it.

"Twelve years," she said. "I spent twelve years learning to be small. Learning that magic was something you hid, that the guild was something you apologized for, that the best you could hope for was to be overlooked." She pressed her palm against the carved woman, against the little flame. "And it wasn't failure. It wasn't decline. It was a *policy*. Somebody decided that the world was easier to govern if the old pacts were forgotten, and they've been erasing it ever since—villages, guilds, embers, people. Hollowmere isn't the first. Look at the seal-work—it's practiced. It's *routine*. How many towns like this are there, Renn? How many roads bending around holes in the world?"

"I don't know." He filled the shrine's doorway, blocking the last red light, and for once he didn't look like an exile or a guide or a man keeping secrets. He looked like what he was: the last free citizen of a realm the Crown insisted didn't exist. "But I know the wilds-side of it. Every year the maps my people keep show less of yours. Villages that traded with us, sang to us across the wall—gone quiet, then gone. We thought it was your kings' natural rot. We thought humans simply forgot, the way humans do." His jaw tightened. "Nobody forgets like this. This takes *work*."

"And the wardens think they're keeping order." She thought of Halvard—his careful questions, his ember-glass pendant, the doubt she'd glimpsed in him even while he hunted them. "Most of them probably do. You don't need monsters when you can hire honest men and tell them lies with stamps on them."

The embers pulsed warm against her hip. Two recovered, eleven scattered, and somewhere north of them the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its turn. She had been thinking of the quest as restoration—putting back what was broken, rebuilding the old pact stone by stone. Sitting in a dead village's shrine, with the seal of the Crown humming overhead like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing, she understood for the first time that it wasn't restoration at all.

It was testimony. Every ember they took back was a witness un-silenced. And the Crown would come for witnesses.

"We should burn this place down," she said suddenly.

Renn's ears pricked. "Say again?"

"The seal. It feeds on silence—if nobody comes, nobody grieves, nobody remembers, it holds forever. But a fire draws eyes. A fire makes smoke visible for miles. Let the Crown explain why a village that isn't on any map is burning." She was on her feet before she'd finished deciding, pulling kindling from the briars, dragging the rotten fence posts. "Let some warden ride out to investigate and find cobbles and a loom and a table set for supper, and have to write a report explaining how a place that officially does not exist has officially been on fire. Make them *say it out loud*, Renn. Make them put it in ink. Ink can be read."

He watched her work for a moment, and then—he smiled. Not the wry, guarded almost-smile she usually got. A real one, sharp-toothed, the wolf looking out.

"There she is," he said softly. "The woman from the wall."

She didn't ask what he meant. She was tired of asking what he meant. But the words landed somewhere deep and warm, and she carried them with her as they worked.

* * *

The fire took the dry-brush first, then the briars, then caught along the orchard windfalls until the whole valley floor glowed like a second sunset. They lit it in three places, upwind, so it would sweep toward the sealed ley-channels and not the stream. Odile stood at the valley's rim with the heat on her face and watched the flames crawl over Hollowmere, and she made it a vow in the old covenant-hand, tracing the words in her mind over the roar:

*I see you. Whoever you were. I will make them see you too.*

Renn stood beside her, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the fire wasn't already saying louder.

It was Renn who broke the silence—but not with words. His head snapped north, nostrils flaring, and his whole body changed, the ease draining out of him.

"What?" Odile's hand went to her knife.

"Riders. Several. Coming fast along the drover's road." He turned to her, and the firelight made his gray eyes look molten. "Not wardens. Wardens ride in formation and stink of iron and bureaucracy. These smell of leather and oil and *purpose*." A pause. "And one of them smells of ember-glass."

Her stomach dropped. "Halvard."

"Or someone wearing the same perfume." He was already shifting, bones sliding, the wolf pouring up out of him like water finding its level. He tossed his head toward the dark line of the hills. *Run now, talk later,* the gesture said.

Odile took one last look at the burning valley—at Hollowmere, erased from every map, finally impossible to ignore—and ran.

Behind them, the fire climbed higher. Ahead, in the black hills, hoofbeats were closing.
