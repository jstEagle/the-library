## 18. Two Travelers, One Blanket

### Odile

The fire was small on purpose.

Odile had built it the way she'd been taught in her first year with the Charmwrights—low, tight, ringed with ash so the light wouldn't carry past the first stand of pines. A fire you could see from a road was a confession. She had spent twelve years learning how not to be seen, and even now, three days north of Vael with a Warden's net closing behind them and a wolf-kin sleeping ten feet away, her hands did the old work without being asked.

She fed the flames one twig at a time and watched Renn's breathing.

He slept sitting up, back against a boulder, arms crossed over the bandage she'd wrapped around his ribs that morning. Even asleep he looked like a man guarding a door. The firelight moved across his face and found things she hadn't let herself study before: the scar splitting his left eyebrow, the gray of his eyes gone silver when they were closed, the way his jaw worked once, twice, as if he were chewing on something even in dreams.

*You knew me,* she thought. *Before all this. Before the shard burned my hand. You looked at me in that cellar like a man reading a letter he'd written himself.*

She looked away. Fed the fire another twig.

The marshes were behind them now, and the drowned chapel with its cold gift of memory—her own voice, nineteen years old, swearing an oath beside someone whose face the ember refused to give back to her. Three days of walking had not dulled it. If anything the memory had grown teeth in the dark, surfacing every time she closed her eyes: wet stone under her boots, wind off the border wall, and a hand in hers, callused and warm, belonging to no one she could name.

"Your fire's dying."

Renn's voice, low and rough with sleep. He hadn't opened his eyes.

"It's meant to be dying."

"It's meant to be *small*. There's a difference." He cracked one eye, then both, and sat forward with a wince he tried to hide and failed. "And you're feeding it one stick at a time like a woman paying a debt she doesn't agree with."

"That is exactly what I'm doing."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might take root. He dragged his bedroll closer to the fire, moving stiffly, and Odile made herself keep her eyes on the flames instead of the way he favored his left side. The wound from the ford was healing wrong. She'd said so that morning. He'd said wolves healed fast. She'd said wolves weren't supposed to argue with hedge-charms either, and he'd said nothing, which between the two of them amounted to a treaty.

"The pack will have sent word ahead by now," he said. "That we passed the ford. That I paid the passage-rite." His voice flattened on the last words, and Odile remembered the sight of him kneeling in the shallows, baring his throat to a wild alpha while the pack circled and jeered, and how he had never once looked at her while he did it—as if letting her see would have cost more than the humiliation itself.

"They had no right to make you do that," she said.

"They had every right. I left the wilds. I broke the old law." He shrugged, and the shrug pulled at his ribs; she saw him trap the flinch behind his teeth. "Passage is passage. I'd pay it again."

"You shouldn't have to."

"No." He looked into the fire. "I shouldn't."

The silence that followed wasn't empty. It was the kind of silence that had weight and shape, that pressed against the ribs like a hand. Odile turned the twig in her fingers until it blackened.

"Ask me," she said suddenly.

His eyes came up. "Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not asking since the chapel. You think I haven't noticed? You watch me like—" She stopped, started again. "Every time I go quiet, every time I touch the ember through my shirt, you look at me like you're waiting for something to break. So ask. What did I remember? Who was standing next to me on that wall?"

The fire popped. Somewhere down the slope an owl called, once, and got no answer.

"I don't know who it was," Renn said.

The lie was smooth. It was also, Odile thought, the worst lie she'd ever heard, because Renn lied about everything else badly—about how much his ribs hurt, about whether he'd eaten, about whether the rite at the ford still stung—and here, on this one thing, his voice went flat and clean as new ice.

"You're lying to me."

"Yes," he said simply, and looked back at the fire, and she realized with a strange lurch that she had expected him to deny it, and that his honesty was somehow worse.

"Why?"

"Because some things should come from the embers or not at all." He said it quietly, without heat, the way a man recites a law he doesn't enjoy. "If I tell you what you were to—if I tell you what I know, and I'm wrong, or I'm right too soon, then the memory isn't yours anymore. It's mine, wearing your face. You'd spend the rest of this road wondering if you felt anything yourself or if I put it in you." His jaw tightened. "I won't do that to you. Not again."

*Not again.* The words slipped out and hung there, and Odile watched him hear them, watched the whole wolf go still the way prey goes still, hoping the hawk hasn't marked it.

"Not again," she repeated. "Renn. When was the *first* time?"

"There's a stream fifty yards east," he said, standing too fast, swaying once before he caught himself on the boulder. "We should refill the skins before dark tomorrow. I'll take first watch."

"You can barely stand."

"I've stood worse."

"That's not an answer to anything you ever say. Has anyone told you that? You talk like a locked door with a man behind it."

For a moment—one long breath—he almost laughed. She saw it fight its way up and get beaten down somewhere below his collarbone. "A door keeps things safe," he said. "Or keeps them in. Depends which side you're on." And he walked off toward the dark edge of the camp with a gait that pretended to be casual and wasn't, leaving Odile alone with the fire and the ache of a conversation that kept ending just before it began.

She hugged her knees and watched the flames and let herself want, for exactly the length of one burning twig, a life she couldn't name: a kitchen, maybe, or a room above a wall, someone humming off-key, morning light. The wanting came and went like weather. It always had. Twelve years of small magic, twelve years of staying invisible, and the strangest part of all this—the embers, the wolves, the Warden's pendant glinting in Halvard's collar—was that the wanting had stopped feeling like a symptom and started feeling like a clue.

*Someone took a life from me,* she thought. *And left the shape of it behind, the way a chair holds the impression of the person who just stood up.*

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Renn sat down with his back to a pine and did not sleep.

### Renn

He counted her breaths because counting was better than thinking.

Forty-one breaths to the hundred, steady, slowing. She'd rolled into her bedroll an hour ago after pretending for two hours that she was mending a strap on her satchel that needed no mending. Now she slept the way she seemed to do everything—efficiently, guardedly, one hand curled near the ember-pouch even in sleep, fingers twitching sometimes as if the stone spoke to her in dreams.

It probably did. His had spoken to him for twelve years, and he'd learned to stop listening, mostly, the way you learn to stop listening to a river.

*Not again.* He winced at himself in the dark. Twelve years of discipline, of keeping the story behind his teeth, and one tired woman asking honest questions had knocked the boards loose in a single sentence. He could still feel the shape of the truth pressing at the back of his throat, hot as a coal: *You stood on the wall at nineteen with frost in your hair and you swore an oath to my people and to me, and I gave you a courting-gift carved from antler, and when the covenant broke you chose to forget me rather than carry the grief, and I have spent twelve years walking a border that no longer exists looking for a woman who didn't know she was lost.*

He could say it. Four sentences. Less than a minute of breath.

And then what?

He'd played it through a thousand times on the road, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. She would believe him—she was too honest not to, once the shock passed. And then every mile after that would be haunted by it. Every time the embers returned a memory, she'd be measuring it against his version, checking his work, unable to feel her own past without his shadow standing in it. Grief borrowed is still grief, but it comes with interest. He had watched it happen to others of his kind who'd had their histories told to them by survivors. They walked crooked ever after.

No. The embers would tell her. The embers were hers. He would walk beside her and keep his mouth shut and his heart in a box, and if the thirteenth moon rose before the last ember was found, then—

Then he would tell her everything with whatever time remained, and damn the consequences. He wasn't a saint. He was a wolf who had loved a charmwright across a border wall, and there was only so much silence a body could hold.

The fire had burned low. He rose, quietly, and added wood—not one guilty twig at a time the way she did, but enough to make a proper flame, because the night had turned cold and she shivered in her sleep, a small motion of the shoulders she'd have denied.

Her blanket was thin. City-made, guild-issue, the kind of thing that kept out a draft and nothing else. His own bedroll was wolf-woven wool, dense with heat, made for northern nights. He looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at her.

There was a solution to this that involved one blanket. He was aware of it the way a man is aware of a cliff edge—precisely, and with a certain fatal curiosity. He was also aware that she'd wake, and startle, and armor herself in barbs before her eyes fully opened, and that whatever fragile thing had kindled between them over these weeks—trust, reluctant and unasked-for—would scorch.

So he did the coward's arithmetic. He crouched, lifted the edge of her blanket, and laid his bedroll over her feet and legs like a second layer, tucking it along her side with a care that felt absurdly ceremonial, and told himself the heat transfer was purely practical.

She stirred. Her eyes opened halfway, unfocused, finding him in the dark.

"Renn?" Sleep slurred the word soft.

"Go back to sleep. You were cold."

"You gave me your—" She frowned, surfacing a little further, and he braced for the barb, the deflection, the *I don't need looking after.*

What she said instead, in a voice barely above the fire's murmur, was: "Did you used to do this? Before?"

His hands went still on the wool.

"I don't know why I said that," she murmured. "It just—it feels like something that happened. Someone fixing a blanket. I can almost—" Her brow creased, chasing something down a corridor that had been walled shut. "There was a window. Cold glass. Someone laughing at me for—"

"Don't chase it," he said, too fast, too rough. He gentled his voice by force. "If you chase it, it runs. Let the embers do their work."

She looked at him for a long moment, and in the half-light her eyes were dark and searching, and he understood that she was doing to him exactly what he'd done to her for three days: reading the locked door, listening for the man behind it.

"One day," she said quietly, "you're going to run out of doors."

"Probably."

"And when you do, I'll be standing there. And I'll want to know why you made me wait."

"I know," Renn said. It came out of him before he could stop it, plain and bare as a bone on snow, and he saw her register it—saw her breath catch and her fingers curl slightly in the wool—and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing left to say that wouldn't burn the whole careful structure down.

She closed her eyes. Within twenty breaths her breathing slowed again, though her hand stayed curled near the ember-pouch, and once, just once, her fingers uncurled outward, toward where he sat, as if reaching for something in a dream.

He sat back against the pine and did not move and did not sleep and counted stars through the pine branches, the few stubborn ones that had come back since the wards fell, and told himself the ache in his chest was just the wound.

* * *

### Odile

Dawn came gray and smelling of frost.

Odile woke under two layers of bedding and lay still for a moment, cataloguing: warmth in her feet, stiffness in her back, the fire burned to white ash, and Renn already up, packing the camp with the economy of a man who'd done it ten thousand times. He didn't look at her. She didn't mention the blanket. Some gifts were easier to accept silently, and some silences were easier to keep than to break, and she was beginning to suspect the two of them had built an entire architecture out of things unsaid—a house with no doors, furnished entirely with almosts.

They ate hard bread and dried apples. They doused the fire. They walked.

By midmorning the pines thinned and the land opened into moorland, brown and gold, cut by an old road that wasn't on any map Odile owned. Renn moved better than yesterday but still favored his left side, and when they stopped at noon beside a leaning waystone, she made him sit and unwrapped his bandage without asking, because asking gave him room to refuse.

The wound was angry. Not festering—not yet—but the edges were slow, too slow, and when she pressed her palm above it and let the smallest thread of her craft flow, the charm-sense came back strange: a faint wrongness in the flesh, like a note played just under pitch.

"This needs more than thread-and-spit," she said. "There's something in it. From the ford—the water, or the rite. It's resisting."

"Wolf wounds close slow when the heart's divided," Renn said, which was either folk wisdom or deflection, and with him it was usually both.

"That's not a thing. That's not medicine."

"It's true anyway."

She tied the bandage tighter than strictly necessary and enjoyed his wince more than was strictly kind. But as she knotted it, her thumb brushed the skin below his ribs, and the shard-memory surfaced unbidden—wet stone, wind, a hand in hers—and for one vertiginous second her fingers seemed to know the geography of his body, the exact spot where an old scar should be, and she looked up sharply and found the scar was *there*, a pale seam along his side, and her stomach dropped through the moorland into somewhere very cold.

"What?" Renn said, watching her face.

"Nothing." She tied off the bandage. Stood. Kept her voice level through sheer professional habit. "We should make the ridge before dark. You said the chapel road meets the shepherd's track past it."

He studied her a moment longer than comfortable, then nodded and rose, and they walked, and the moor opened ahead of them gold and endless, and Odile turned the discovery over in her mind like a coin she wasn't ready to spend: *I knew his body before I knew his name. Whatever we were, we were not strangers.*

Near dusk, from the top of the ridge, they saw the valley below—and Odile stopped so abruptly Renn nearly walked into her back.

Down in the fold of the hills, perhaps two miles off, a fire burned. Not a traveler's fire—a camp. Many fires, ranked and regular, and even at this distance she could pick out the geometry of pavilions, the picket lines of horses, and moving among the tents, tiny with distance, figures in uniforms that caught the last light.

Wardens. A full company, camped across the road south of them.

Renn went very still beside her, nostrils flaring, reading the wind. "They're not hunting us," he said slowly. "Too settled. Too many supply wagons. That's a garrison forming up." His voice changed, hardened. "They're sealing the border roads. Every pass south of here."

Odile's hand found the ember-pouch at her chest without her deciding to touch it. Two embers recovered, eleven to go, and the Crown building a wall of men between her and the next one.

"We go around," she said.

"We go around," Renn agreed. "Through the high ground. There's an old drover's path—"

He stopped.

Odile followed his gaze back down into the valley, to the camp's western edge, where a single rider had detached from the picket line and was climbing the far slope alone—unhurried, deliberate, angled not toward the road but toward the ridgeline. Toward them. And even at two miles, even in failing light, there was something in the rider's bearing that Odile recognized the way she'd recognized the scar on Renn's ribs: a knowledge arriving ahead of its reasons.

The rider paused at the crest of the far hill. For a moment, impossibly, he seemed to look directly at them.

Then he raised one hand—not a salute, not a signal. Something older than either. And turned his horse away.

"Odile," Renn said quietly, "we need to move. *Now.*"

Below them, in the gathering dark, the first horn of the Warden camp sounded—once, low, like a question asked in a language she almost remembered.
