## 19. The Ember-Taker

The rain came in off the marshes an hour before dusk, and Renn smelled the wrongness beneath it.

He had learned, over twelve years of exile, to read a country the way other men read letters. The road south of the bone orchard was ordinary enough—wheel ruts gone soft with water, hedgerows dripping, a crow complaining somewhere in the gray. But under all of it ran a thread of scent that did not belong: tallow and hot iron and something older, something like burnt sugar left too long on a stove. Charm-work. Old charm-work, laid down in layers, the way a man lays down coats of varnish to hide the grain of bad wood.

Someone had been on this road for days. Someone who wanted to be found by exactly one person, and hidden from everyone else.

Renn shifted Odile's pack higher on his shoulder and said nothing. She walked ahead of him with her hood up, her hands buried in her sleeves, and every few strides her right hand—the one the shards had burned—flexed open and closed, as if it were holding something invisible. She had done it since the chapel. He suspected she didn't know she was doing it. He suspected, too, that if he told her what the gesture meant—*you are counting embers you no longer remember carrying*—she would look at him with those winter-gray eyes and ask him how he knew, and he would have to lie again, and the lie was getting heavier every day, like a stone carried in the mouth.

Two embers now rode against his chest, sewn into a leather wrapping beneath his shirt. The first sang low and constant, like a hearth heard through a wall. The second, taken from the grave of a woman whose funeral Odile had apparently attended while dead, hummed at a higher pitch, restless, almost eager. Together they made a chord. And chords, in Renn's experience, could be heard from very far away by very particular ears.

"You're doing it again," Odile said, without turning around.

"Doing what."

"Listening to something I can't hear." She glanced back at him, rain beading on her lashes. "Your ears go sideways. Like a dog hearing a whistle."

"Wolf," he said. "And yes. There's someone ahead of us."

Her whole body changed—not fear exactly, but the coiling readiness he remembered from a wall at nineteen, when she'd stood between him and a Warden's crossbow and dared the man to loose. Twelve years erased, and the spine still there. It undid him a little, watching it.

"Bandits?"

"Bandits smell like sweat and old blood. This smells like—" he searched for the word and hated it when it came, "—like a guildhall. Tallow, ink, charm-smoke. Someone who's spent their life around craft-magic."

Odile stopped walking. The crow went silent, which was worse than any sound.

"The guild is ash," she said. "You told me. Burned out twelve years ago, the night the wards fell."

"The guild *halls* are ash. Guilds are made of people." Renn looked down the road, at the place where the hedgerows pinched together over a stone bridge, and let himself say the thing he'd been circling since morning. "And one of them has been following our scent for three days."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen boundary marker—a granite post carved with interlocking knots, half-swallowed by moss, one of the old covenant-stones that had once ringed the borderlands like beads on a string. Odile built the fire small, the way she always did, cupping it with her hands and whispering to it until it burned low and blue and gave off almost no light. Small magic. Staying small. He had watched her do it a hundred times now and it still caught at his throat, because he knew—she did not—that she had once been the loudest thing in two kingdoms, a woman whose oaths had rung off standing stones, and she had chosen to shrink herself down to whispers and night-market charms just to survive what she'd survived.

She sat across the fire from him, working a whetstone along her knife, and after a while she said, quietly: "Tell me about the guildmaster."

Renn kept his face still. "What makes you think I know him?"

"Because your jaw did the thing it does when you're deciding whether to lie to me." She didn't look up from the blade. "It does that a lot lately. I've started keeping count."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "His name was Aldous Vey. Master of Signatures, they called him—the man who kept the covenant's true text, who taught every signatory how to write their name into the wards without burning themselves hollow. He was old when I was young. Kind, I thought. He used to bring honey-cakes to the wolf-kin children at the border markets. They climbed on him like a favorite uncle."

"And?"

"And then the Crown came to him with an offer. Not threats—offers. A pension. A title. A wing of the palace archive with his name over the door." Renn's voice came out flatter than he intended. "The night the wards fell, Vey opened the guild's inner vault from the inside. He gave the Wardens the ember-glass, the ledgers, the names of every signatory. Eleven people died holding the door so the youngest ones could run. And Vey walked out through the smoke carrying the master ledger under his arm like a hymnal."

Odile had stopped sharpening. In the firelight her face was very pale and very still. "The ledger I found. In the guildhall."

"A copy. Vey kept the original. Which means he knows everything we know. Every ember. Every resting place." Renn met her eyes across the small blue fire. "Including the fact that there are only four left worth taking, and only one of them lies anywhere near this road."

The rain ticked against the fallen stone. Somewhere behind them, far off toward the orchard, a nightbird started up and then thought better of it.

"He'll come for ours," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"He's been coming for ours since the drowned chapel. The question is whether he comes alone."

* * *

He came at moonrise, and he did not come alone, and he did not come the way Renn expected.

Renn woke because the fire had died—not guttered, not faded, but *died*, snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, and the cold that rushed into its place was the specific cold of worked magic, of air being spent like coin. He was on his feet before his eyes opened, and by the time they did, there were lanterns lit in a ring around their camp, six of them, held by six figures in oilskin cloaks with their hoods back.

In the center stood an old man, dry under a canopy of woven reeds that two of the figures held above him like courtiers bearing a canopy of state. He was smaller than Renn remembered, stooped, with white hair cut close as a penitent's and ink stains still ghosting the knuckles of his right hand. His eyes were wet and warm and terrible, the eyes of a man who has wept so often at his own sins that the weeping has become a kind of comfort.

"Renn of the Northmarch," Aldous Vey said, and his voice was exactly as kind as Renn remembered, which was the cruelest thing about it. "You've gotten old in the wrong places. Sit down, boy. You look half-starved."

Behind Renn, he heard Odile rise, slow and quiet, and felt the heat of her hand find his elbow—found it, he realized, precisely where a knife would sit if she were holding one, which she was, having somehow drawn it without a sound. Twelve years erased and the spine still there.

"Six of them," she murmured. "Two have crossbows."

"I see them."

"Do you?" Her voice was steady, but he could smell the fear coming off her, sharp as struck flint, and underneath the fear something else, something that made his chest ache: recognition. Some part of her, some part the embers hadn't reached yet, knew this man. "Renn. Why do I feel like I should know him?"

"Because you signed your name in front of him when you were nineteen," Renn said, and had the satisfaction of seeing Vey flinch. "He held the book. You asked him if it would hurt. He told you no."

"It was true at the time," Vey said mildly. "Everything hurts less before it happens. That's practically the definition of youth." He stepped forward, out from under his canopy, letting the rain touch his bare head like a man demonstrating his humility. "I haven't come to fight you. Look at me. I'm seventy-one years old; my fighting days ended before either of you were born. I've come to make a trade."

"There's nothing you have that we want," Renn said.

"Oh, I don't know about that." Vey smiled, and it was the smile from the honey-cake days, and Renn felt sick. "I have the master ledger, girl. The true one, with annotations in my own hand—every ember's location, every warding laid over it, every *price*. Do you know what the third ember costs to take? No? Of course you don't. You're running on a copy with half a page torn out." He turned that warm gaze on Odile, and Renn saw her grip shift on the knife. "Come with me willingly. Give me the two you carry. In exchange, I'll tell you what you really are, what you really did, and why the Crown has been hunting signatories for twelve years instead of simply killing them. I'll tell you the truth, Odile Winterbourne. All of it. Freely given."

"That's not a trade," Odile said. "That's a ransom with extra steps."

"It's the only honest offer anyone has made you in twelve years." For just a moment, the warmth dropped out of Vey's voice, and underneath it was something exhausted and ancient and horribly sincere. "Child, I read the covenant's terms before you were born. I taught its last signatory how to hold a quill. I know better than anyone alive what reforging it will cost—and I am telling you, as the man who betrayed it, that some prices should not be paid blind. Let me buy the embers. Let me carry the burden. You can go back to your shop and your small charms and live out your life unburdened. Isn't that what you chose the first time? To forget? To be *small*?"

The words landed like arrows. Renn heard Odile's breath catch, heard the tiny sound of her certainty cracking, and he moved without deciding to—stepped between her and the old man, shoulders squared, and let the change come halfway up his throat so that when he spoke, his voice carried the growl under it.

"She chose nothing blind," he said. "And neither will she choose again. Walk away, Vey."

Something moved in the treeline.

Renn caught it a half-second too late—not the crossbowmen, who were exactly where they'd been, but a seventh figure, downwind, patient, who had been there since before the lanterns were ever lit. The scent hit him a heartbeat before the shape did: tallow and iron and burnt sugar, concentrated, *close*—

"—left!" he shouted, and shoved Odile aside as the net came down.

It was warded mesh, guild-work, the kind used for taking wolf-kin alive, and it took him across the shoulders and drove him into the mud with a weight like a falling roof. Silver thread laced through the links bit through his shirt and burned like brands. He heard Odile scream his name, heard steel ring against steel as she met the first of Vey's hired blades, and he thrashed against the net and felt it drink his strength the way sand drinks water, every movement costing twice what it should—

Through the mesh, inches from his face, he watched Aldous Vey walk calmly past the fight, kneel beside Odile's abandoned pack, and draw out the leather wrapping with the tenderness of a man lifting an infant from a cradle.

"No," Renn rasped. "Vey. *Vey.* Those aren't yours to—"

"They were never mine," the old man agreed, tucking the wrapping inside his coat, where it immediately began to glow faintly through the wool, like coals breathing. "That was rather the point of me." He looked down at Renn, and for one unguarded moment his face was naked with something that looked almost like grief. "I'm sorry about the silver, boy. Truly. But you'd have fought me to the death for them, and she needs you alive more than I need your forgiveness."

Then he straightened, raised one hand, and every lantern in the ring went out at once.

Darkness. Rain. The taste of blood where Renn had bitten his tongue. He heard footsteps retreating—six sets, unhurried, disciplined—and one set that limped slightly, favoring the left leg, an old man's walk, growing fainter down the road toward the river.

He heard Odile's blade skitter away across stones. Heard her drop to her knees beside him, her hands finding the net's edge, hissing as the silver burned her fingers too.

"Hold still," she was saying, and her voice was shaking now, all the steadiness gone, "hold *still*, I can get these knots, I can—"

"Odile." He forced the word through his teeth. "Listen to me. The ledger copy. The torn page. Whatever he offered to tell you—he wasn't lying about the price. The third ember. Check the page. *Check the page tonight.*"

Her hands stilled on the mesh. Above them the clouds were tearing apart, and through the gap the thirteenth moon showed its face, thin and white and a little further along its cycle than it had been the night before.

"Why?" she whispered. "What does it cost?"

But Renn was looking past her, down the dark road, at the faint coal-glow fading inside a traitor's coat—two embers, singing their chord, being carried away by the only man alive who knew all thirteen verses of the song.

"Because," he said, "he's going to the glacier. And he'll get there before us."
