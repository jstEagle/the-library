## 20. The Guildmaster's Confession

The rope around Odile's wrists was charm-woven. She knew it by the way it drank her magic—the faint, familiar numbness crawling up her arms like cold water, the same feeling as a ward failing, as stars going out. Someone had made this rope with care. Someone who knew exactly what she was.

She sat in a chair in the center of a room that smelled of tallow and old paper, and she did not struggle, because struggling against a binding like that was like struggling against her own name.

The man across from her was older than she'd expected. She had imagined the Ember-Taker as something feral—a beast in a man's shape, all hunger and no patience. Instead he was thin and gray, with ink-stained fingers and the stooped shoulders of someone who had spent forty years bent over a desk. His coat was fine once. Now it was patched at the elbows with cloth that didn't match, mended by hand, badly.

He looked less like a traitor than a schoolmaster. That frightened her more than fangs would have.

"You're not drinking your tea," he said.

Odile looked down. There was, absurdly, a cup of tea on the table beside her chair, steaming, with honey already stirred in. "I'm not thirsty."

"It's not poisoned. If I wanted you dead, you'd be dead. The rope would have seen to it before you woke." He turned his own cup slowly on its saucer, one full rotation, then another. A habit. A man who calmed himself with small rituals. "You're Odile Winterbourne. Charmwright, third circle, guild number—" he recited a string of digits without looking at anything, "—registered under the amended rolls of the year the guild fell. You sold luck-knots in the night market for twelve years and never once drew the Wardens' eye, which is more than any of us managed. You were very good at being small."

"Who are you?"

"Master Aldous Veyne." He said it the way other men said their death sentences. "Last Guildmaster of the Charmwrights. The one they blame."

Renn's voice was in her head even though Renn was miles away, bound and gagged in the root cellar where Veyne's men had left him—or so she desperately hoped. *Don't listen to him. Whatever he says, don't drink anything, don't sign anything, don't believe him.*

But there was no one to say it aloud, and the room was quiet except for the wind worrying the shutters of what she now understood was the ruined guildhall itself—the place she had found the ledger, the place her name had been waiting for her in faded ink. Veyne had brought her back to the scene of the crime. Her crime, apparently. Or his. The walls were hung with shadow where shelves used to be, and through a crack in the boarded window she could see the thirteenth moon's pale ghost, waxing toward full.

"You took an ember," she said. "From us. From the bone orchard."

"I took back what was always mine to carry." He set his cup down. "And I have been waiting eleven years for someone to come and take it from me. Do you know how long eleven years is, when you're waiting to be punished?"

*Don't believe him.* But her heart was doing something treacherous, some slow lean toward the shape of his grief.

"Why did you take it?" she asked. "Why betray the covenant? Why sell it to the Crown?"

Veyne laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as pages turning. "Sold it. Yes. That's the story, isn't it? The story I let them tell, because it was simpler than the truth and because I deserved a simpler sin than the one I committed." He rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the boarded window. Through the gap, moonlight laid a pale stripe across his face. "Ask me properly, Odile Winterbourne. Ask me the question that's been sitting in your chest since the shard burned your palm. Ask me why you can't remember."

Her mouth went dry. "I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" He turned. His eyes were gray and kind and utterly merciless. "Thirteen embers. Twelve scattered, one carried. Each one gives back a piece of what was taken from you. You've held two—you've stood on a wall beside a man whose face won't come, and you've walked behind your own coffin. And still you haven't asked yourself the only question that matters." He came closer, and up close she could see that his hands trembled, finely, constantly, like a plucked string. "Who took your memories, child? Magic doesn't just fall out of the sky. Memory-taking is a craft. It requires a craftsman. It requires consent."

"No," Odile said.

"It requires," Veyne went on, gently, horribly, "*consent*. The old law. The deep law, older than the Crown, older than the guild. No one can be unmade without their own hand on the knife. So ask yourself, Odile. Who signed the permission?"

"I was nineteen," she whispered. "I was—I couldn't have—"

"You were twenty-three," he said. "Twelve years ago, on the night the wards fell. You walked into this hall with blood on your sleeves and ash in your hair, and you knelt where you're kneeling now—no. Not kneeling. Standing. You never knelt, even then. That was the thing about you." Something moved behind his eyes, some old tenderness he immediately strangled. "You stood in front of the whole surviving guild, what was left of us, nine people in a burning building, and you told us to take it. All of it. Him. The wall. The oath. The wolves. Everything."

The room tilted. Odile gripped the arms of the chair, and the charm-rope hummed against her skin as if pleased to have something to hold.

"You're lying," she said. "Why would I—why would anyone—"

"Because the covenant was breaking," Veyne said, "and it was breaking because of you."

* * *

He poured more tea. Neither of them drank it. Outside, the wind rose, and somewhere below the floorboards—below, she realized with a lurch, the very cellar where Renn lay—something shifted and settled, patient as stone.

"The Ember Covenant wasn't a treaty between kings," Veyne said. "Kings don't make covenants. Kings make contracts, and contracts have loopholes. A covenant is different. A covenant is a promise written into the blood of everyone who signs it, and it binds their heirs, and their heirs' heirs, until the last signatory dies or the terms are fulfilled. Thirteen embers to anchor it. Thirteen signatories. Nine humans, four wolf-kin. Your guild forged it two hundred years ago, when Vael was small enough to be honest."

"And the Crown?"

"The Crown grew. Crowns do. And a growing thing needs to eat." He resumed his seat, folding his trembling hands. "Three generations ago, the founding kings discovered the covenant's terms in the guild archives—and stole them. Word for word. The borders, the trade rights, the passage laws, the mutual defense clauses—all of it lifted wholesale and rewritten as royal decree, with the wolf-kin struck out entirely. Rendered beasts. Vermin. Legal nothing. The city has been living inside a stolen promise ever since, wearing our covenant as its skin while calling the people we wrote it with monsters."

Odile thought of the village erased from maps. Of Halvard's ember-glass pendant. Of the ledger with her own name dated twelve years ago. Pieces she had been carrying separately slid toward each other, grinding.

"So the guild decided to take it back," she said slowly. "Reforge the original. Make the Crown's claim void."

"We tried. For years, quietly. But a covenant needs all thirteen embers and a living signatory willing to pay the price, and the price—" Veyne's hands stilled. "The price is memory. That's the old bargain. To bind two realms together, the signer must give up something the realms themselves cannot give: the ability to see each other clearly. Every signer pays. Our founders paid in pieces—faces, names, whole decades. It's why the guild dwindled, why we forgot our own purpose generation by generation. We ate ourselves hollow keeping the pact alive."

"And me. What did I pay?"

"Nothing," Veyne said. "Not yet. You were the final signatory—the youngest, the strongest, the only one born after the theft, which meant the covenant could pass to you clean, unspent. Thirteen embers, thirteen lives, and yours was the keystone. When the reforging came, you would pay everything at once. Every memory of the other realm. Every memory of the wall, the wolves, the pact—" he paused, and his voice dropped, "—and of the man you loved, who was the pact, as far as you were concerned. Renn of the Northreach. Your betrothed across the border. The reason you ever learned wolf-tongue. The reason you ever believed any of this was possible."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped stone hitting the bottom of a well. She had known it—of course she had known it, since the cellar, since the first time he said it—but hearing it in this room, from this man, in this context, made it a different word entirely.

"So I knew," she said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking through a wall. "Before. I knew him."

"You were going to marry him at the turning of the year. On the wall itself, both realms watching. It was going to be the first public signing of the new age—the covenant renewed with a wedding, hope made visible." Veyne smiled, and it broke halfway through. "And then the Crown found out."

* * *

The fire, he said, had started in the archive wing. Warden-fyre, the kind that eats paper and leaves stone. In one night the guild lost its records, its charter, six of its thirteen embers, and seven of its members. The survivors fled to the hall. And Odile—

"Odile stood where you're standing," Veyne said, "and told us the Crown had given her a choice. They knew who she was. They knew what she was. Kill her, and the covenant died with her—clean, legal, permanent. The wolves would be locked out forever, the border sealed, the theft complete. Or—" his throat worked, "—or she could renounce. Publicly. Renounce the guild, the pact, the wolf-kin, all of it, and live. They wanted her broken where the wolves could see it. They wanted the north to watch its champion kneel."

"She refused," Odile said. It wasn't a question. Some part of her, some buried spine of herself, already knew the answer.

"She refused," Veyne agreed. "Of course she refused. So the Wardens moved to take her, and the guild fought, and the hall burned around us, and in the middle of it all she understood something none of the rest of us had understood in two hundred years." He leaned forward. "If she died, the covenant died. If she renounced, the covenant died. Either way, the wolves lost everything. Unless—"

"Unless the covenant was already broken," Odile breathed. "Unless it broke *before* the Crown could break it. On my terms instead of theirs."

Veyne closed his eyes. "She came to us with the idea before the roof fell in. Reforge the covenant—not as restoration but as renewal. Gather the embers, stand at the wall, write the new pact in her own blood. And pay the price willingly, in advance, so that no Warden's blade or renunciation could touch what she'd already given away. You cannot threaten a woman with taking what she has already surrendered. The Crown's choice evaporated. Their leverage was ash." He opened his eyes. "It was brilliant. It was the bravest thing I have ever witnessed. And it required her to forget the man she loved *now*, tonight, before the Wardens reached her—because if she remembered him, she would never be able to walk away from the wall afterward. She would stay. She would fight. She would die. And the realms with her."

"So she asked."

"She begged," Veyne said. "On her knees, finally, in front of all nine of us. *Take it. Take him. Take all of it, and hide the pieces where I'll have to earn them back, so that when I get there—if I get there—I'll have chosen it again with every step.*" His voice cracked on the last words, and he pressed his lips together until it passed. "We were guildmasters. Craftsmen. We did what she asked. It took all nine of us, working together, and it nearly killed three of us, and when it was done she looked at us with those clear gray eyes and didn't know our names, and she walked out into the smoke and opened a charm shop, and she stayed small, and she stayed alive, and the Crown's hunters lost the scent because there was nothing left in her to hunt."

The silence afterward was enormous. Odile became aware of her own breathing, ragged, of tears she had no memory of starting, running warm past her jaw.

"But it didn't work," she said. "The wards fell anyway. The stars went out."

"Because the reforging was never finished." Veyne's hands resumed their trembling. "The covenant needed all thirteen embers and a completed rite. We got eight gathered before the Crown's purge scattered the rest—we hid them, buried them, drowned them, froze them, and we scattered the knowledge of where. Then the guild was hunted to nothing. I ran. I took the ninth ember and I ran, and I let them call me the betrayer, because a betrayer is a simple thing to hunt, and while they hunted me, they weren't hunting the trail of embers." He laughed again, that dry terrible sound. "Eleven years, Odile. Eleven years carrying the thing, waiting for the last signatory to remember enough to come and take it. And then three weeks ago the wards failed, the stars went out, and I felt the twelfth ember wake. And I knew. I knew either she had come back to finish it—or the Crown had found the trail first, and everything we burned for was about to be for nothing."

"So you ambushed us."

"I tested you." He nodded toward her bound wrists. "The shard that burned your palm. The chapel. The orchard. You've been following a trail I salted with questions, and you've answered every one of them the way she would have. Stubbornly. Sideways. Never once kneeling." He stood, and from inside his coat he drew something wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table between them.

The ninth ember sat in its nest of black velvet like a coal that had forgotten how to go out. Light moved beneath its surface, slow as breath. Odile felt it in her teeth, in the scar on her palm, in the hollow space behind her ribs where a man's face should have been.

"One ember remains unaccounted for beyond this one," Veyne said. "The tenth. And the thirteenth—" his eyes flicked to the window, to the pale ghost of the hidden moon, "—the thirteenth was never hidden at all. It rises on the night of the final turn, at the wall, when the rite is done. It makes itself." He pushed the velvet nest across the table toward her. "Take it. Finish it. Pay the price she chose."

Odile stared at the ember and did not move. Because there was a splinter in her now, working deeper with every heartbeat, and she had to say it aloud before she could pull it free.

"If I chose it," she said, "why does it feel like this? Why does it feel like something was *done to me*?"

Veyne's kindness, when it came, was the cruelest thing in the room.

"Because knowing you consented doesn't make the loss smaller, child. It makes it yours." He rose, stiffly, and began untying the charm-rope from her wrists, his trembling fingers clumsy at the knots. "She knew that too. She knew exactly what it would feel like, standing here twelve years later, learning what she'd paid. She paid for this moment too. This exact moment. The grief of finding out." The rope fell away, and feeling flooded back into her hands like blood into sleeping limbs, painful and alive. "That's what she bought with all of it. Not safety. *Agency.* The right to choose it twice."

Below the floorboards, something heavy shifted. Once. Twice. Then a sound that was not stone settling at all—a low, rising note, half growl and half grief, vibrating up through the guildhall's bones, and Veyne's head snapped toward the door.

"They're awake early," he said. "Your wolf. He's been listening."
