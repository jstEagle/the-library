## 21. I Chose to Forget

She walked until the guildmaster's cellar was a memory behind her, and then she walked further, because memory had become a thing she did not trust.

The road south of the abandoned mill was no road at all—only a cart-track gone soft with rain, hemmed in by blackthorn hedges that clawed at her cloak as if the land itself objected to her passing. Odile let them. Let them tear threads from the hem, let the mud suck at her boots, let the cold find every gap in her clothing. Pain was simple. Pain stayed where it was put. It was the rest of her that would not hold still.

*I asked for it.*

The words kept their own rhythm with her footsteps. *I asked for it. I asked for it.* Four beats to a breath, like the counting-song charm-sellers sang in the Ash Market when trade went bad.

The sky above was still starless. It had been eleven days now since the stars went out, and she had almost grown used to the blankness of it—the sky like a sheet of slate wiped clean by a careless hand. Tonight there was only the thirteenth moon, swollen and wrong-colored, hanging low over the western hills like a coin dropped in milk. Its light did not silver things. It made them gray, all of them: the hedges, the mud, her own hands when she held them up.

Gray hands. A gray woman walking a gray road. She wondered if this was what the Crown's unmaking looked like from the inside—not fire, not wards collapsing in thunder, just this slow leaching, this quiet fading, until a place or a person simply stopped being worth noticing.

She stopped at the crest of a hill because her body demanded it, not because she chose to. Below, a farmhouse sat dark among its fields, chimneys cold, doors standing open to the night. Another emptied place. The Crown had been sweeping the countryside clean of magic-touch for years—she'd known that since the village erased from every map—and yet seeing it laid out below her, ordinary and abandoned, made something in her chest crack along an old seam.

*You did this too,* said a voice in her that wore her own tone, her own dryness, her own habit of turning wit against herself. *Not the Crown. You. You unmade yourself, and you didn't even have the decency to notice.*

"No," Odile said aloud, to the empty road. Her voice came out cracked. "That isn't—I didn't *know*."

But that was precisely the shape of it, wasn't it? That was the horror with teeth. She hadn't known because she had made herself not know. She had stood—wherever she had stood, in whatever room or ritual-circle twelve years ago—and she had handed over the man she loved the way you hand a charmwright your grief at a funeral: deliberately, knowingly, paying the price with her eyes open.

And then she had asked to forget the asking.

She slid down against the hedge until she was sitting in the mud, and she laughed—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it—and then the laugh broke apart into something else entirely, and she pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth and wept without grace or dignity, the way she had not wept since she was a child, since before she learned that tears were a currency best kept for spending alone.

* * *

The Ember of Knowing sat heavy in the pouch at her belt.

She had taken it from the guildmaster's desk while he lay bleeding on his own floor—Corvan Ashgrave, last guildmaster of the Charmwrights, traitor to his own covenant, dying by inches from the wound Renn's claws had given him. He had watched her lift it with eyes gone wet and strange.

"You'll want to be careful with that one," he'd rasped. "It doesn't show you what happened. It shows you why."

She should have left it in the pouch. She had known, even then, kneeling in that mean little study with the ink smell and the blood smell braided together, that some doors are better left shut. She had spent twelve years on the other side of one, after all.

But her fingers had been already working the clasp.

The ember had not burned this time. That was the courtesy of it—it had simply opened, the way a well opens when you drop a stone, and she had fallen inward through her own history:

*A room lit by thirteen candles. Her own hands, steady, holding a knife over her own palm. Renn's voice—she knew it instantly, knew it the way you know a song you've forgotten you loved—saying her name, saying* don't, *saying* there has to be another way, Odile, there is always another way. *And her own voice answering him, calm in a way she had never once managed in her present life:* There isn't. The covenant is breaking because I cannot carry both. Love for you and duty to Vael—they pull in opposite directions, and the wards are strung between them like a rope in a tug-of-war, and I am letting go of one end or watching it tear me apart. So I choose. I choose the work. I choose the city. Take it from me—all of it, him, everything—and give me back my hands.

*And the ember had answered. And she had watched herself, from outside herself, watch the memories leave.*

That was the worst of it. Not the loss. The *watching*. In the memory she could see her own face as the erasure took hold—the small confusion, then the smooth settling, like a bed being made over a sleeping body. She had looked *relieved*.

Odile pulled the pouch open now, in the moon-gray dark, and stared at the ember without touching it. It glowed faintly, a coal-color, patient. Nine embers remained to find, if the ledger spoke true—or rather, four found, nine scattered, and the thirteenth somewhere no ledger named. Each one a piece of her. Each one a door she would have to walk through.

"I hate you," she told it quietly.

It glowed on, indifferent as the moon.

* * *

Somewhere on the road behind her, Renn was following.

She knew it the way you know weather coming—some animal sense under the skin. He would track her, of course. He had spent twelve years searching for a woman who did not remember him, and now he had finally found her, finally spoken to her, fought beside her, bled beside her, and she had run from him into the dark like a startled deer, and he was going to follow anyway, because that was apparently what love looked like when it had been starved for a decade and refused to die.

He had called her name as she fled the mill. Just once. Just *Odile*, in that low rough voice, and she had heard in it twelve years of calling it into empty country, and she could not bear it, so she had run faster.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape, sitting in the mud with the moon going down: every kindness he had shown her, every barb traded in her cellar, every long silence on the north road—he had carried all of it knowing. Knowing she had stood in a candlelit room and chosen the wards over him. Knowing she had then paid to forget that she had chosen, so that she would not even have to live with it. He remembered the wall where they swore their oath. He remembered the betrothal across realms. He remembered her laughter, presumably; her hands; whatever private language two people build over years. And she remembered none of it, and worse than none of it—she remembered the *reason*. She had done it to survive, yes. But survival was a choice too. She had looked at the sum of what they were and decided the world was worth more.

And maybe it had been. Maybe it *was*. Maybe the wards had held these twelve years because of her sacrifice, and every child in Vael who slept unafraid owed their sleep to the hole where her heart used to be.

Or maybe—said the small cold voice at the bottom of her, the rock-bottom voice, the one that lived down where despair pooled like winter water—maybe the covenant had broken anyway. Maybe the stars had gone out regardless. Maybe she had thrown away the only true thing she ever owned, and bought nothing with it at all.

That was the thought that finally flattened her. Not guilt. Arithmetic. Twelve years of self, spent, and the ledger showed nothing gained.

She lay back in the mud and looked up at the blank sky and let the cold come into her the way it wanted to.

* * *

Time passed. She did not measure it. The moon moved; the cold deepened; her tears dried to salt on her cheeks, and still she lay there, a charmwright of the dying guild, last signatory of the Ember Covenant, lying face-up in a farmer's ditch like something the tide brought in and declined to take back.

It was the ember that ended it. Not the one in the pouch—a different warmth. The shard of covenant-stone, the first fragment Renn had carried across the fallen border, the one that had hummed at her passing and burned her palm with a picture of a wall and a blurred face. She had carried it since. She had half-forgotten it.

Now it warmed against her hip like a cat settling close, and she felt—distinctly, unmistakably—*pity*. Pity flowing out of a stone.

"Don't," she muttered. "I know what you are. You're a piece of the pact. You don't get to have feelings."

The warmth persisted, gentle, maddening, and beneath the pity something else: a pull. Southward and slightly east. Toward the marshes, and the drowned chapel, and the first ember she had claimed. Toward the others waiting beyond it, scattered like sparks from a fire kicked apart.

The covenant wanted her to go on.

"Well," she said to the stone, to the sky, to the whole rotten arrangement of worlds, "the covenant can want things. It's good at taking them. Wanting seems fair."

But she sat up. That was the humiliating truth about rock-bottom, she thought—it was a floor, and floors were for standing on. She wiped her face with the least muddy part of her sleeve, and she made herself think, because thinking was the one craft she had never surrendered.

Twelve years ago she had decided the world outweighed her heart. Fine. Cruel, but fine. What had she actually bought with it? Time. Wards that held until they didn't. And now the wards were down, the stars were out, the clock was running—the astronomer exile had said it plainly: when the thirteenth moon completed its cycle, the realms would drift beyond each other forever, and everything living on either side of the border would forget the other existed, permanently, down to the stones. Wolves and men sealed away from each other into separate twilights.

Unless the covenant was reforged. Unless the embers were gathered. Unless she—the last signatory, the woman who had already paid once—paid again.

She understood now what the final price would be. She had felt it in the guildmaster's confession, in the old texts' phrasing, in the way every ember she touched asked a little more of her: the pact ran on sacrifice, and hers had always been the same coin. Memory. Self. Whatever she rebuilt it with, she would rebuild it out of her own flesh of recollection. Perhaps the years with Renn again. Perhaps everything.

"So that's the bargain," she said. "I buy the world twice, and I don't get to keep the receipt either time."

The ember in the pouch pulsed once, softly. Agreement, or apology. With covenant-stone it was hard to tell the difference, and she suspected the difference did not exist.

She got to her feet. Her legs were stiff and her boots were ruined and her dignity was somewhere back in the guildmaster's study, dead on the floor next to Corvan Ashgrave. Good riddance to all three.

And then—because she was Odile Winterbourne, because she had survived twelve years of small magic and one very large forgetting, because despair had never once in her life managed to fully extinguish the pilot light of her terrible humor—she heard herself say, to the gray fields and the gray moon:

"The next man who makes me choose between him and the world had better be prepared to share."

The silence offered no comment. Somewhere off in the dark, a fox screamed, high and eerie, and she chose to hear it as applause.

* * *

She built no fire. Fires drew Wardens, and Halvard was out there somewhere—Halvard with his ember-glass pendant and his conscience, which was more dangerous by far than a Warden without one. Instead she wedged herself into the hollow of the great blackthorn at the hilltop, wrapped her arms around her knees, and let the shard's warmth stand in for heat.

Sleep came in shallow tides. Between the tides, fragments surfaced, and she let them, because fighting them hurt worse:

Renn's hands bandaging her forearm in her cellar, absurdly deft for claws that size. His voice, dry as her own: *"Your bedside manner is a crime, charmwoman."* Hers, back: *"So report me."*

The wall. Always the wall now, in the ember-dreams. Wind off the northern wilds, banners cracking, and a presence beside her whose face would not resolve no matter how she turned toward it—but whose shoulder fit against hers exactly, as shoulders learn to do over years, and whose breath kept time with hers without either of them trying.

Her mother's voice, from before any of it: *"Small magic keeps you safe, Odile. Never be the loudest thing in the room."* She had built her whole life inside that sentence. She had hidden so well she hid from herself.

And underneath everything, steady as groundwater: *I asked for it. I chose. Nobody took it from me.*

She woke once, near dawn, certain she had heard a sound on the road below—the soft pad of weight on wet ground, the click of a pebble. She did not reach for her knife. She lay still and listened to the gray hour, and she thought about what she would say to him when he came, because he would come; there was no version of this story in which he did not come.

*I'm sorry* was true but insufficient. *I didn't know* was true but a lie. *I chose the world over you and I'd probably choose it again and I cannot make myself sorry for that, only for the manner of it, only for the cowardice of also choosing to forget that I chose* —that was the truth, the whole ugly architecture of it, and she could not imagine fitting it into her mouth.

Maybe she wouldn't have to say anything. Maybe he would look at her with those amber eyes that held twelve years she didn't own anymore, and maybe he would understand that the woman he'd searched for had died in a candlelit room, and what he'd found instead was her ghost wearing her habits.

Maybe that was fine. Ghosts could walk roads. Ghosts could gather embers. Ghosts could save two worlds and pay the toll again and never once have to look at what they'd lost, because they'd already sold the looking.

The thirteenth moon was sinking now, fat and pale over the hills, a little thinner than the night before, a little closer to the end of its cycle. She counted without wanting to. Two turns gone, by the astronomer's reckoning—no, nearly three. Three turns remaining, and nine embers scattered between her and everything.

Odile Winterbourne climbed out of the blackthorn hollow, stood up straight in the pre-dawn gray, and rolled her shoulders the way she did before opening the shop on market days. Small ritual. Small magic. It would do to start.

"All right," she said softly—to the stone, to the road, to the man surely following somewhere behind her in the dark, to the girl she had been on the wall. "All right. Onward, then."

And far to the north, at the edge of hearing, or perhaps only at the edge of imagining, something howled—long, low, and unmistakably searching—and Odile turned toward the sound with her heart doing a thing hearts should not know how to do, and began, deliberately, to walk south to meet it.
