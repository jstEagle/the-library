## 22. Renn, Unmasked

The cave smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and Odile had not spoken to him in two days.

Renn knew the shape of her silences by now. There was the silence of exhaustion, which came soft and even, like snowfall. There was the silence of anger, which had edges. And there was this—the silence she had carried since the guildmaster's cellar, since the words *she asked for it* had landed in her chest like a thrown knife. This silence was a wall. He recognized it because he had helped her build the first one, twelve years ago, on a border wall under a sky full of stars that had not yet gone out.

She sat near the fire with her back to him, the stolen ember cradled in a scrap of wool in her lap. It glowed faintly through the fabric, a heartbeat under cloth. Nine embers recovered. Three to find. One turn of the moon left, maybe less. And she would not look at him.

He had told himself he was protecting her. That was the story he had carried across the wilds and the marshes and the bone orchard, through fords and raids and a glacier that had nearly taken him. *She cannot bear it yet. The memories will break her if they arrive out of order. Let the embers do their work. Let her come to it whole.*

It had been a noble story. It had also been a lie he told himself so he would not have to watch her face when she learned it.

"Odile." His voice came out rough. He had not spoken since dawn either.

She didn't turn. "If you're going to say the guildmaster lied, don't. I've had two days to decide whether he lied, and I've decided he didn't. You flinch every time I mention the wall. You flinched when he said my name. You've been flinching since the ash market, and I was too busy running to see it."

"I don't flinch."

"You're a terrible liar for a wolf." She turned then, and the firelight caught her face—the hollows under her eyes, the burn-scar on her palm where the first shard had marked her. "You're many things, Renn. Patient, infuriating, stubborn as winter. But you are not a good liar. So tell me what I asked for. Tell me what I paid."

The fire cracked a knot of pine. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines above the cave, and for a moment it sounded like the sea, and for a moment he was nineteen again, standing on the wall with the whole night spread out below him like a promise.

He had rehearsed this a hundred times. On the road north. In the drowned chapel while she wept for a man she couldn't name. At the ford, while the wild pack circled and he bared his throat to wolves who despised him. He had rehearsed it in a hundred registers—gentle, blunt, formal, desperate—and every rehearsal had collapsed the moment he imagined her eyes.

There was no rehearsal now. There was only the fire, and her face, and the ember pulsing in her lap like a second heart.

"You were nineteen," he said. "It was midsummer, and the stars were still whole, and you were standing on the border wall at Vael's north gate selling charms to travelers who couldn't afford them, because that's what you did—gave things away and called it selling so no one's pride would be wounded." He heard his own voice steady itself, the way it did in wolf-form, in a hunt, when everything in him went quiet and aimed. "I was supposed to be scouting the wall for my pack. Instead I bought a charm against bad dreams I didn't have, and paid double, and you looked up at me and said, *You're the first customer all day who didn't try to haggle down a blessing.*"

Odile had gone very still. The kind of stillness that prey animals have, and that Odile had never once had in all the months he'd known her. She was always moving—hands, eyes, thoughts. Now she was stone.

"Your name was Odile Winterbourne," he said. "Journeyman of the Charmwrights' Guild, third daughter of a cooper, terrible at haggling, worse at staying small. And my name was Renn of the Greyfall pack, and I had no business falling in love with a human woman, and I did it anyway, in about four days, which my mother said was a record even for our kind."

"Stop." Her voice was barely air.

"I can't," he said. "I've been stopping for twelve years. It's my turn."

* * *

He told her all of it, because once the dam broke there was no holding anything back, and because he owed her every drop.

He told her how the border had still been porous then—how wolf-kin and humans had traded at the wall's shadow, cautiously, illegally, in the years before the Crown's edicts hardened. How the Charmwrights' Guild had been dying in Vael, squeezed by Warden inspections and royal charters, and how a handful of guildmasters had done something desperate and magnificent: they had gone north and negotiated with the packs. A covenant. Shared wards. Shared magic. A wall that belonged to both peoples instead of either.

"You were the guild's youngest journeyman," he said, "and the only one the wolves trusted on sight, because you never once tried to cheat us, and because you laughed at things that weren't funny, which the elders found disarming. They made you a signatory. Thirteen embers to seal it, thirteen names, and yours was the last—inked twelve years ago this autumn, in the guildhall, with your own blood."

"The ledger," she whispered. "I saw my name. I thought—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I thought it was a mistake. A clerical error. I thought, *that can't be me, I've never been anywhere, I've never done anything.*"

"You'd done everything," he said. "You just weren't allowed to remember it."

He told her about the betrothal. That was the hardest part to say aloud, harder even than the breaking, because it was the part that was purely theirs, unshadowed by politics or betrayal. How the covenant's terms had required a binding between the realms—not a marriage of state, the elders had been clear about that, but a *betrothal of signatories*, a personal pledge woven into the magic, two people who chose each other so the pact would have a heartbeat. How he had asked her properly, in the human fashion, down on one knee on the wall at midwinter with his breath steaming and his hands shaking, because he'd heard humans did it that way and he wanted every part of it to be in her language.

"And you said," he said, and his voice did break here, and he let it, because she deserved to hear it break, "you said, *Yes, obviously, you enormous idiot, I've been waiting for you to ask since midsummer.*"

Odile made a sound he had never heard from her—not a sob, not a word. Something between. She had both hands over her mouth now, and the ember in her lap had begun to glow brighter, reacting to her, or to him, or to the truth filling the cave like water filling a hull.

"The guildmaster," she said through her fingers. "Corvan. What he told me—"

"Corvan sold us." The old anger rose in Renn's chest, familiar as a scar. "He'd been taking Crown gold for years, feeding the Wardens the guild's secrets. When the covenant was ready to be sealed, he went to the palace and told them everything—the embers, the terms, the betrothal. The Crown couldn't allow it. A pact that made the border belong to everyone made it belong to no king. So they moved to break it before it could take root. Wardens seized the guildhall. The packs mustered at the wall. And in the middle of it, someone—"

"You," she said. It wasn't a question. Her hands came down from her mouth slowly. "The guildmaster said the memories weren't taken from me. He said I *asked*. He said I went to someone and begged."

Renn looked into the fire. He had known this moment would come, and he had imagined it would be the relief—that after this, everything would be lighter. No one had told him it would feel like opening a wound to clean it.

"The covenant was dying," he said. "The embers were scattered, the guild was broken, the packs were being hunted. And the magic of it—the part that bound the two of you, the betrothal-ward—was turning feral. A broken covenant doesn't just end. It curdles. It would have burned through you. Through both of us. It would have taken your mind first, because you were the last signatory, because the whole weight of it came down on your name." He made himself look at her. "There was a rite. Old magic, older than the guild. A signatory could set down the memory of the covenant—all of it, everything tied to it—and the magic would release her. It would go dormant instead of destructive. But it had to be given freely. No one could take it from you."

"And I gave it."

"You insisted." He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and short. "That's the thing you don't understand yet, Odile, because you don't remember who you were. You didn't weep and bargain and hesitate. You walked into that rite like you were walking into a guild meeting. You told the old charmwright—Maren, she held the rite—you told her, *If the price of surviving this is forgetting him, then I'll pay it, and I'll pay it gladly, because a world where he's alive and I'm broken is worse than a world where I don't know what I lost.* And then you looked at me—"

He stopped. The fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, and in that light her face was all shadow and shine, and she was watching him the way people watch a door they're afraid will open.

"You looked at me," he said again, quieter, "and you said, *Don't follow me. Don't spend twelve years standing outside a charm shop like a fool. Find someone who remembers you.* And then Maren spoke the words, and you forgot me between one breath and the next, and you looked at me—Odile, you looked *through* me, like I was a stranger at a market stall. And I said yes, ma'am, to the charm against bad dreams, and paid double, and walked out, and I have been walking back toward you ever since."

* * *

The silence that followed was not her wall. He could tell the difference now. It was the silence of a woman standing in the ruins of her own life, counting what remained.

"Twelve years," she said at last. Her voice was scraped raw. "You said you searched. The guildmaster said it too—he sneered it, like it was pathetic. Twelve years doing what?"

"Learning to be human-shaped." He shifted, and his bad shoulder—the one the glacier had wrenched—throbbed its complaint, and he ignored it. "Your Crown wards don't just keep wolves out. They unmake us a little, if we stay too long inside them. Every year in Vael's shadow cost me something. My nose went dull. My hearing flattened. Some winters I couldn't find the wolf in me at all, and I thought—I thought maybe I'd spent it. Spent all of him, just to walk your streets and check your shop."

"You came to the shop." Not a question. She was rebuilding it, piece by piece; he could see her doing it, the way she rebuilt a broken ward, testing each stone.

"Every market night for the first three years. I'd buy something small. A luck-knot. A candle-charm. You never recognized me. Why would you? I was just a customer with a northern accent and too much coin." He smiled, and it hurt. "You told me once—year four, I think—you told me I had kind eyes, and that I seemed like a man waiting for something, and did I need a charm for finding lost things? And I said no. I said I'd already found it. I just couldn't pick it up."

Odile's breath caught. He watched her hand drift to the counter of her memory, to the shelf where she kept that moment without knowing what it was, and touch it, and find it changed.

"The night the stars went out," she said slowly. "The wards failed. And you crossed into the city, wounded, carrying the shard. Was that the first time you'd—"

"The wards falling was the only door I'd ever get. I'd been waiting at the border for two years, watching, waiting for a gap. When the stars went out, I ran. I ran through the night and half the next day with Warden arrows in me, because the shard was singing and I knew—I *knew*—it was singing for you." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, coals painting him red. "I didn't know if you'd help me. I didn't know if the woman who forgot me would even stop to bind a stranger's wounds. I told myself it didn't matter. That the embers mattered. That the covenant mattered. That I could stand next to you for one more season and ask for nothing."

"And instead I hid you in my cellar and insulted your bandaging technique."

"And I fell in love with you a second time," he said, "which I didn't think was possible, and which turns out to be worse, because the first time I got to keep it."

* * *

She stood up. He watched her walk to the mouth of the cave, where the night lay blue and starless beyond the pines, and stand there with her arms wrapped around herself, and he did not follow her because some part of him still remembered, across twelve years and one erasure, exactly how much space she needed.

"The man on the wall," she said to the darkness. "Every ember. Every memory it's given back. There's always someone beside me. Always. I can feel the shape of him—tall, and he stands on my left, and when I swear the oath he takes my hand and his palm is rougher than mine. I've been grieving him for weeks, Renn. I've been grieving a blur." She turned. The starless dark made her eyes very bright. "It was you. It was always you. I've been carrying you around inside me like a splinter, and I didn't even have the mercy of knowing your name."

"Odile—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and it was trembling, and she let him see it tremble, which he knew—for her—was a kind of gift. "Don't comfort me yet. Let me be angry for one minute. Because you *knew*. You have known since the cellar. You let me weep in a drowned chapel over a face I couldn't see while you stood right there with the face. You watched me piece myself together out of scraps and you never—"

"Because you asked me not to." He was on his feet now, and the wolf was close under his skin, close enough that his voice had gone low and rough. "Not this you. The you on the wall, twelve years ago. *Don't follow me. Don't make me remember before I'm ready.* I have broken a great many promises in my life, Odile Winterbourne. I broke my pack's law to love you. I broke my exile's terms to cross the border. I broke a blood-oath to the Greyfall elders to keep the shard out of their hands. But I did not break that one. I have kept it like a coal in my chest for twelve years, and I am telling you now only because you asked, and if I have wounded you by keeping it, then I will carry that wound the way I carry the rest—gladly, and without complaint, because the alternative was disobeying the last thing the woman I loved ever said to me."

The cave was very quiet. The ember in its wool wrapping pulsed once, twice, like something listening.

"You kept a coal in your chest," she repeated. Her voice had gone strange. "That's what the charm was. The first one. The one I sold you on the wall, the day we met. It was a charm against—" She stopped, and her hand went to her mouth again, and this time what came out was a laugh, broken and wondering and wet. "It was a charm for keeping an ember burning. I'd forgotten the words to my own charm and you remembered them. *A coal in the chest, a light in the dark, kept against the cold, kept against the heart.*"

"Kept against the heart," he agreed.

She crossed the cave in four steps and hit him like a wave hits a sea-wall—not gently, not softly, fists against his chest and her forehead down against his collarbone, and he wrapped his arms around her and held on, held on the way he had wanted to hold on since a cellar floor and a needle and thread, since a market stall and a lie about lost things, since a midwinter wall and a shaking knee.

"You idiot," she said into his shirt. "You enormous, patient, terrible idiot. Twelve years."

"Twelve years," he said into her hair. "Worth it. Every year. Every ward I walked through. Every winter I couldn't find my own shape. I'd pay them all again, and I'd pay them twice, for five minutes of this."

She pulled back far enough to look at him, and her face was a ruin and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and she said, "The embers. The last three. When the covenant is reforged—" and then she stopped, because they both knew what the twelfth ember had shown her, what the rite demanded, what the price would be, and the knowledge moved across her face like a cloud across a field.

"I know," he said softly. "I've known since before you did."

"And you're still here."

"I made you a promise at a campfire," he said. "I'll remember for both of us. Every wall. Every oath. Every midwinter. Every time you laughed at something that wasn't funny. You'll forget, and I will keep it all, and when the realms can see each other clearly at last—because of what you give—I'll start telling you the story from the beginning, and I'll tell it every day if I have to, until something in you remembers the shape of it. Until your heart learns your own name back."

She was crying now, openly, without shame, and she pressed her palm—the scarred one, the marked one—flat against his chest, over the heart, over the coal that had never gone out.

"Then tell me one thing now," she whispered. "Before we move again, before the road, before the last three embers and whatever they cost. Tell me the thing you've wanted to say for twelve years and haven't said."

He framed her face in his hands, rough palms against her jaw, and gave her the truth whole.

"I love you, Odile. I loved you before the covenant and I loved you after the breaking and I loved the stranger who sold me a charm against bad dreams, and I will love the woman who forgets me tomorrow just as much, because it was never the remembering that made it true. You could empty me out of every year we had and I would still choose you, at nineteen, at ninety, at first sight, every sight. That's the whole of it. That's everything I've been carrying."

She rose up and kissed him, and outside the cave the starless dark pressed close, and somewhere far to the north the hidden thirteenth moon turned its last pages, and the ember in the wool wrapping flared bright enough to throw their shadows huge against the stone—two shadows, one wall, one story waiting to be told again.

When they finally broke apart, breathless, her forehead against his, she said the words that turned his blood to ice and purpose in the same instant.

"The shard," she said. "Renn—it's singing. Listen. It's been singing since you said her name."

He listened. And beneath the wind and the coals and the sound of his own hammering heart, he heard it: the covenant-stone in their pack, humming a note he had heard only once before, twelve years ago, in a guildhall full of candlelight and blood.

It was singing the way stones sing when another ember wakes.

And it was singing from the north—from Greyfall, from the heart of the pack that had exiled him—from the last place on this earth Renn had ever sworn he would never go back to.
