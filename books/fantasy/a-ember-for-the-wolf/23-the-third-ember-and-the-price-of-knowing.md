## 23. The Third Ember and the Price of Knowing

The guildmaster's camp sat in a bowl of hills where the road bent toward the river, three fires burning behind a hedge of sharpened stakes. Odile crouched beside Renn in the bracken above it, close enough to smell woodsmoke and roasting meat, and hated how ordinary it looked. Men laughing around a fire. A kettle on a hook. As if the man who had sold thirteen realms' worth of covenant were only another tired traveler warming his hands.

"There." Renn's voice was a breath against her ear. He pointed with his chin to the largest tent, where a lantern glowed amber through the canvas. Inside, something answered the shard in her satchel—a faint pulse she felt against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "He keeps it close. He sleeps with it under his pillow, the arrogant fool."

"He knows we're coming," Odile said.

"Of course he knows. He wants us to come." Renn's gray eyes caught the firelight. "Aldous Vey has never once in his life met anyone head-on. He'll have traps laid, guards posted, some clever cruelty arranged for whoever walks through his door."

"Then we go around."

"No." Renn's jaw worked. "We go through, tonight, before the moon sets. Every hour we wait is an hour off your thirteenth turn, and he'll be moving again by dawn."

She studied him in the dark—the lean line of him, the old scars she'd mapped without meaning to, the way his ears turned toward every sound in the camp below. Since the wolves at the ford, since the blanket they'd shared and not shared, she had been learning him the way one learns a language: slowly, then all at once. She knew now what he would not say. He was the man on the wall. He was the face the embers blurred, the name her own mind had scoured away. And he had spent twelve years carrying that alone so she could carry everything else.

"I don't like this plan," she said.

"You haven't heard the plan."

"That's why I don't like it."

His mouth curved—there, and gone. Then he pulled the wolf-kin bone knife from his belt, reversed it, and offered it to her hilt-first. "If I fall," he said quietly, "you take the ember and you run. You don't argue with a dying man, Odile Winterbourne. It's rude."

"Don't." The word came out harder than she meant. "Don't do that. Don't make speeches like it's settled."

"It's never settled. That's the point of them." He closed her fingers around the knife's haft, his hands warm over hers, and for a moment neither of them moved. Below, the fires crackled. Somewhere far north, a moon neither of them could see ground its slow way toward fullness.

"Tell me something true instead," she whispered.

Renn looked at her for a long time. "You used to hum when you worked. On the wall, mending the ward-stones. Badly. Off-key, always the same three notes." His voice roughened. "I'd walk the whole length of the rampart just to hear it wrong."

Then he kissed her brow, once, like a seal pressed on wax, and dropped over the crest of the hill into the dark.

* * *

It went wrong almost immediately.

Odile came in from the south as planned, through the gap where the stakes stood thin, following the ember's pulse while chaos bloomed at the northern edge of the camp—shouts, a bell, the deep baying cough of a wolf letting men know it had arrived. Renn had promised to draw them, and draw them he did; she could track his progress across the camp by the way it screamed.

The guildmaster's tent was unguarded. That should have warned her.

Inside, Aldous Vey sat at a folding table with a decanter of wine and two cups, as if expecting guests for supper. He was smaller than she remembered from her brief captivity—gray-bearded, soft-handed, wearing a guildmaster's rings on fingers too fat for them. The ember lay on the table before him, a sliver of light like a coal that refused to die.

"Mistress Winterbourne." He poured the second cup. "Sit. We should talk terms. You of all people understand terms."

"I don't drink with traitors."

"You signed the same pact I did." His smile was patient, terrible. "You simply weren't told you were signing. Sit down, girl, or the six men in the walls of this tent will make you."

And they stepped out of the canvas shadows indeed—six of them, cudgels and hooked knives, ringed behind her before she'd even finished turning.

Odile's mind went cold and quick, the way it had learned to in twelve years of staying small. *Negotiate. Wait. Renn.*

"The ember," Vey said, tapping it. "Do you know what it shows me? Not memories. Money. Leverage. Each fragment of the old covenant is a key to the border wards, and the Crown will pay a duchy for one key. I've already sold the location of four. This one—" he lifted it, and it seared a hiss out of him even through a glove, "—this one I keep."

"You're burning yourself holding it."

"A small price. Everything worth anything is." He leaned forward. "Here are my terms: you give me the shard you carry and your word that you'll stop this mad quest, and I let you walk. You've seen what the Crown does to magic-touched villages, Odile. What makes you think thirteen embers in the hands of a charm-seller changes anything?"

"What makes you think it doesn't?"

And then the back of the tent tore open like paper.

He came through it as wolf first—all the stories true and worse: shoulders like a doorway, fur the color of wet slate, eyes catching the lantern-flame gold. Two of the six men went down before their minds finished deciding to be afraid. The third swung a cudgel and connected with a sound Odile would hear in her sleep for years.

But the wolf did not fall.

The wolf took the blow on the shoulder, turned inside the arc of it, and put the man into the table. Wine and blood spread together across Vey's maps.

"Stop him!" Vey shrieked, backing toward the tent wall with the ember clutched to his chest—and Odile moved, because if Vey escaped with it, all of this was ash. She got a hand on his coat. He twisted; the ember between them flared white-hot with proximity, and pain shot up her arm as if she'd gripped a forge iron, but she held on, screaming, holding, until—

One of the hired men hadn't panicked.

She saw him only peripherally: calm face, professional hands, a long skinning knife coming around in a short efficient arc meant for the wolf's throat as Renn rose from the fifth man's ruin.

"*No!*"

She didn't remember crossing the space. She remembered the bone knife in her hand—his knife, given hilt-first—and she remembered throwing it badly, wide, useless. She remembered Renn turning, not fast enough, taking the blade not in his throat but along the great artery of his shoulder and chest where the cudgel had already broken him open.

The world narrowed to a single red line drawn across her whole life.

What happened after happened in pieces. Vey wriggling free, the ember vanishing into his coat, the tent collapsing in flame where a kicked lantern found the wine. The calm man with the skinning knife fleeing into the night because six against one had become one against a wounded wolf and a woman with murder in her hands. Renn shifting back to his own shape mid-stride, human and half-naked and folding to the earth like something whose strings had been cut.

She got her arms under him. He weighed nothing. He weighed everything.

"Odile." Blood at the corner of his mouth, and still he tried to smile. "Told you. Rude to argue."

"Shut up. Shut up, hold on—" Pressing both hands to the wound, feeling the heat of him leaving through her fingers. Around them the camp burned and no one came; whatever Renn had done to the north end of it, they wanted no more of wolves tonight. "Hold on. Hold on, you stubborn, insufferable—"

"Odile." His hand found her wrist. His grip was still strong. That was the cruelest part—that there was still so much strength left to fail. "Listen. The ember. Vey rides west. There's a ford—"

"I don't care about the ember!"

"You have to care about the ember." Each word cost him; she watched each one cost him. "That's the whole—point of you. Of me. Of—" A wet cough. "The healer. Hollow Vale. Half a day east. Don't stop. Don't—"

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not leaving me. I'm—coming with you. Obviously." And his eyes rolled back, and the grip on her wrist went slack, and Odile Winterbourne knelt in the ashes of a traitor's tent with the man she had once chosen to forget bleeding out between her hands, and understood with perfect, blinding clarity that she could not lose him twice.

* * *

She built the travois from tent poles and the guildmaster's own fine canvases, because the world had a sense of humor and she had stopped consulting it. She loaded him with supplies she stole without shame: blankets, a flask of spirits, a haunch of someone's supper. The ember was gone. The shard in her satchel pulsed once as she dragged Renn's body into the trees, and she snarled at it like it was laughing.

"Half a day east," she told him, though he couldn't hear. "You don't get to die before I yell at you properly. That's my term. I'm a signatory; I know how to write terms."

He didn't answer. His breathing had gone shallow and wrong, a whistle at the bottom of each pull.

All night she hauled him through the hills, over streams that soaked her to the thigh, down deer trails that no cart had ever cut. Twice she fell and kept her body between his and the rocks out of pure animal instinct. Once she stopped and pressed her ear to his chest and counted heartbeats the way she'd counted stars the night they vanished—desperately, one at a time, refusing to reach zero.

And somewhere in the dead hours before dawn, kneeling in wet fern with his blood dried black on both her arms, the guilt finally caught her and pinned her the way it had been circling all night.

*I threw the knife wide.* That was the thought, over and over, the wheel her mind rode down into darkness. *He took a blow meant for him and I threw his own knife wide and he stepped into a blade because I screamed his name like a fool who couldn't watch him fight his own battle.*

She had spent twelve years learning to stay small. Small magic, small life, small risks. And the one moment that demanded largeness—demanded a thrown knife, a struck bargain, a hand that didn't shake—she had failed it, and Renn was paying the toll.

"You knew," she said to his unconscious face, hauling the travois over a root. "On the hill, before. You knew it might go this way and you kissed my forehead and gave me your knife anyway." Her voice cracked. "Why do you keep doing that? Why do you keep paying for me?"

The forest didn't answer. Neither did he.

Dawn found her staggering out of the treeline into a valley full of mist, and at the bottom of it, exactly as promised, a crooked cottage with smoke rising from a chimney and charms of outlawed make turning gently in the garden wind like silver fish.

Hollow Vale.

She gave a hoarse cry that was half sob and dragged him down the slope, and the cottage door opened before she reached it, and a small gray woman looked out—looked at Renn, at the wound, at Odile's face—and simply stepped aside and said, in the voice of someone who had done this many times before:

"Bring him in. Put him by the fire. And child—" her eyes flicked to the blood, "—wash your hands before you touch anything of mine. Grief leaves a residue."

* * *

The healer's name was Maren, and her house smelled of yarrow and hot iron, and she worked on Renn for four hours without once asking how he came to be carved open in the wilds. Odile sat in the corner and watched, and the watching was its own wound.

She had seen magic all her life—small magic, charm-magic, sleight-of-hand magic. She had never seen power like this: Maren's hands moving over the torn shoulder humming those low working-songs, threads of light drawn out of herbs and stones and the healer's own steady heart. No bargains. No pacts. No price taken from the helpless. Just skill, and patience, and the outrageous kindness of a stranger.

"Why?" Odile asked at last, when the bleeding had stopped and the terrible gray of Renn's face had warmed by one shade. "This magic is outlawed. The Wardens would burn this house. Why risk it for a wolf-kin you've never met?"

Maren didn't look up from her bandaging. "Because forty years ago a wolf-kin carried my daughter nine miles through snow with a fever, to a Charmwright who broke her guild's rules to save her." She tied off a knot. "The world calls it debt, child. I call it Tuesday. Now boil water."

So Odile boiled water, and changed dressings, and learned to grind willow bark, and did not once let herself hope out loud. When dusk came, Maren pushed her gently toward the chair beside the bed.

"Sleep. He'll live or he won't, and neither state cares whether you're standing."

"I can't." Odile looked at Renn's face, slack and young-looking in sleep, the bandages wrapped thick across his chest and shoulder. "When the ember was stolen, I froze. In the tent—I had one throw, one clear throw, and I missed, and he—" Her hands shook in her lap. "Everything that happens to him happens because of me. The exile. The hunt. Twelve years searching for a woman who paid to forget him. And I just stand there, and he bleeds, and I—"

"Stop." Maren's voice wasn't unkind, but it was iron. "Do you know what I see, when I look at the pair of you? I see a man who made his choices long before he met you again, and would make every one of them twice. And I see a woman who carried him through hill and water all night without stopping, which is more than most loves manage in a lifetime." She gathered her instruments. "Guilt that eats you serves no one, child. Guilt that moves you—now, that's a tool. Choose which kind you're sharpening."

She left Odile alone with the fire and the shallow rise and fall of Renn's breathing.

For a long time Odile sat and listened to it, counting again—one beat at a time, refusing zero. Then, because sitting still was beyond her, she checked his satchel for fresh dressings and found instead, folded at the bottom, worn soft with age: a strip of banner-cloth, wolf-gray, embroidered along one edge in a style no human hand made. And stitched into its corner, tiny, in thread that had once been gold:

*A.W.*

Aurora Winterbourne. Her mother's mark. The token given to signatories' kin, twelve years and a lifetime ago—kept at the bottom of a bag, carried through exile and wilderness and war, all this way.

She put it back exactly where she'd found it and pressed her sleeve hard against her mouth until she could breathe again.

Near midnight, his fever spiked.

Maren fought it with cold compresses and bitter draughts, but near dawn Renn's eyes opened at last—unfocused, burning, somewhere very far from this room—and his hand came up off the blanket, reaching, grasping at air.

"Odile," he said. Not her. Someone else, someone from before; the name was a rope thrown twelve years into dark water. "Odile—the wall—they're breaking the wards, I have to—the wall—"

"I'm here." She caught his hand and held it against her cheek. "I'm here. The wall holds. Sleep."

"Can't—leave her—can't leave her alone up there—"

"She's not alone." Odile's voice broke clean in half, and she let it. "She's right here. She's holding your hand. Look."

And something in her voice, or her touch, or the twelve years between them, reached him. His eyes found hers. Focus came back into them, and with focus, anguish—for one raw instant she watched him understand exactly where he was, and who was holding him, and how much she must now know.

"Odile." Her name, this time hers, wrecked.

"I know," she said. "I know who you are. I know what you did. And Renn—" she bowed her head over his hand, tears falling freely onto his knuckles, hating herself, loving him, drowning in the arithmetic of what his love had already cost and might yet cost, "—if you ever scare me like this again, I will bring you back and kill you myself."

A ghost of his old smile. "Rude," he breathed, "to threaten a dying man," and Maren, appearing with a fresh draught, said flatly, "He's not dying, and you're both fools, and the fever breaks by morning. Out of my sickroom, both of you."

But Odile didn't sleep, even after he sank into true rest. She sat by the fire with the shard in her palm, cool for once, quiet, and stared north through the little window toward the border she could no longer see.

Nine embers remained. Ten moons had gone. Somewhere west, a fat man in guildmaster's rings rode with stolen light sewn into his coat, and somewhere farther than that, the hidden moon ground on toward the turn that would part the realms forever.

And she understood, sitting in the gentle wreckage of Hollow Vale, what the twelfth ember would eventually tell her—what the covenant's shape had been trying to tell her all along. That every restoration had a price. That pacts were written in something, and something was always taken.

"Not this time," she whispered to the fire. "Whatever it takes next—I pay it. Me. No one else."

Behind her, in the small room, Renn slept on, breathing, alive, unaware that the woman he'd bled for had just begun making promises the future intended to collect.

In the garden, wind turned the outlawed charms like silver fish, and far to the north, unseen, the thirteenth moon rose one night fuller than the last.
