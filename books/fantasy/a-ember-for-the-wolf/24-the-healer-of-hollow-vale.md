## 24. The Healer of Hollow Vale

Renn's blood had stopped arguing with me somewhere past the second mile.

That was how I had come to think of it—his blood arguing, his body fighting the wound the way he fought everything, stubbornly and without complaint. But by the time the road dipped into a valley so green it looked like something remembered rather than real, the argument was over, and losing. He had one arm slung across my shoulders—I still didn't understand how someone that size could be carried at all, let alone by me—and his weight had gone from heavy to terrible, the weight of a thing no longer resisting.

"Stay awake," I said. I had been saying it for an hour. It had stopped working two miles ago.

"Mm." His breath fogged against my ear. "You're... very bossy for a charm-seller."

"You're very heavy for someone who's supposed to be healing."

"Wolves heal fast." A wet sound in his chest when he laughed, which frightened me more than anything the guildmaster's knife had done. "Just need... a minute."

"You've had your minute. You've had forty of them." I adjusted my grip on his belt, my palm slick where it shouldn't have been. "Renn. Look at me."

He did, and his eyes were wrong—not gray anymore but pale, washed-out, like frost forming over water. The ember we'd clawed back from the guildmaster hung against my chest under my coat, warm as a swallowed coal, and every few steps I felt it pulse faintly, as if it were worried too. Embers didn't worry. They only remembered. But I had stopped trusting my understanding of what they could do.

"There," Renn said. His muzzle of a voice had gone thin. "Smoke."

I saw it a moment later—a single thread of chimney smoke rising from the fold of the valley, from a cluster of low buildings that seemed less built than grown, turf roofs and wattle walls half-swallowed by garden. Hollow Vale, a weathered signpost had said at the top of the ridge, though the sign had been defaced with the Crown's black strike-mark, the same brand they put on outlawed things. On villages like this one, if the village was lucky enough to survive being branded at all.

"It's marked," I said. "Struck off. The Crown doesn't—"

"The Crown doesn't heal wolf-kin either." His eyes were closing again. "Choose."

I chose. There was never really a choice; there hadn't been since the moment I'd watched him fall, since the moment three embers ago I'd understood that the man whose face I couldn't remember was the man whose death I couldn't survive remembering. I turned down into the valley, and Renn's weight settled onto me like an answer.

* * *

The woman who opened the door of the first house was old in the way trees are old—all rings and weather, still standing. She took one look at me, one look at what I was carrying, and stepped aside without a word.

Inside smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and something sharper underneath, metallic and green, like sap from a cut stem. She cleared her table with a sweep of her arm—dried herbs, bundles of twine, clay pots scattering—and pointed.

"Lay him there. What happened?"

"A knife. Below the ribs, angled up." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "It's been hours. The bleeding—" I peeled back the bandage I'd made of my own shirt, and even I, who had seen wounds only in other people's stories, understood what I was looking at. "The bleeding has mostly stopped, but he's cold, and his breathing is wrong, and his eyes went—"

"Pale." She was already at his side, her small hands moving over him without quite touching, the way I'd seen a dowser pass a rod over dry ground. "Yes. That's not the knife, girl. That's what the knife woke up."

"I don't understand."

"No," she said, not unkindly. "You wouldn't yet." Then she looked up at me, and her eyes were quick and brown and utterly without fear. "I can't promise you he lives. But I'll spend what I have trying. Can you live with that bargain? Because I don't make the kind your guild used to make. No trades. No prices. I just do the work and hope."

"We're not—" I started, and stopped. *We're not the guild* was true in every way that mattered and false in the way that had gotten people hanged. "I'm the last signatory of the Ember Covenant," I said instead, because lying to this woman while she fought for his life felt like spitting in a well. "And he's mine. Whatever that means. Save him."

Something moved across her face—recognition, or its ghost. "So it's you," she said softly. "The valley's been humming for two days. Old Mother Alder swears the bees came back early." She rolled up her sleeves. "Well then. If you're going to stand there, stand there usefully. Boil water. Tear those sheets. And whatever you see me do, you don't flinch and you don't pray to the Crown's saints. They've got no jurisdiction here."

* * *

What I saw her do changed something in me that I am still, months later, finding the seams of.

She did not draw a circle. She did not speak words of binding or pay out power in measured, agonizing tolls, the way the covenant's old texts described—*an ember for an oath, an oath in blood, and blood remembers*. She simply knelt beside Renn and laid both hands flat on the ruined flesh of his side, closed her eyes, and *listened*.

That was the only word for it. Her breathing slowed until it matched his. Her heartbeat, I swear this, I saw it in the pulse at her throat—even with his, falling into rhythm the way two clocks in the same room will slowly swing together. And where her hands rested, the torn tissue did not knit itself with violence, with the searing grab of power I'd felt from every ember. It eased. Like a knot loosening under patient fingers. Like something being persuaded rather than compelled.

"What is that?" I whispered. I was holding boiled linen and I had forgotten to tear it.

"Hedge-work." She didn't open her eyes. "Older than your covenant. Older than the wall, probably. You don't take power from anything—you just lend the body your attention until it remembers what whole feels like." Her brow furrowed with effort now, sweat beading at her hairline despite the cool room. "The Crown calls it outlawed because it can't be taxed, tallied, or turned into a weapon. First law they ever passed after the founding, did you know that? Before they touched the border at all. They outlawed the healing first."

"They did?" I thought of the archive beneath the palace, of the ledgers proving the founding kings had stolen the covenant's terms wholesale, and felt the pieces click against each other like stones in a pouch. "Because a king can't sell a miracle that grows free in ditches."

"Now you're learning." She smiled without warmth, and then all the smile left her, because beneath her hands Renn shuddered—a deep, full-body convulsion, and the pale frost-look surged back into his face, and the wound, which had been easing, began to weep dark blood again.

"He's fighting me," she said, and now there was strain in her voice. "Wolf-kin bodies are strange to this work—they're already halfway wild, already listening to themselves. And he's..." She pressed harder. "He's holding on to the hurt. Like he thinks he deserves it."

"Why would he think that?"

"Ask him, if he wakes." She shut her eyes tighter. "Girl—the tallow candle on the shelf. Green mark on the base. Bring it to me and *don't light it*, just hold it near his head, and whatever it shows you, keep tearing linen anyway."

I brought the candle. Unlit, as she'd said. And the moment it came within a handspan of Renn's brow, the air above the wick shimmered—not flame but something like heat-haze given intention, and in the haze I saw—

I saw the border wall. I saw snow. I saw a young woman with my own crooked nose laughing at something a young man was saying, her breath white in the air between them, and I saw the young man reach out and tuck a strand of hair behind her ear with fingers that had calluses exactly where Renn's had calluses, and his face was—

Blurred. Still blurred. Twelve years and eight embers, and the candle-haze blurred him the same as the memory-shards always did, as if some part of the price I'd paid was that his face was held in escrow, kept back even from these stolen glimpses, to be paid out only at the end.

But I heard him. The haze carried sound, faint as a voice through a wall.

"—always, Odile. Even if you forget me. Especially then."

The candle-haze collapsed. I was standing in a healer's house in a struck-off valley with an armful of torn linen and no memory of having fallen to my knees.

"Ah," the old woman said quietly, still working. "So it's that kind of forgetting."

"You saw—"

"I saw nothing. I'm busy." But her voice gentled. "Hold the candle steady, girl. He needs to hear whoever he needs to hear."

I held it. I held it while she worked through the afternoon and into the dusk, while she lent Renn her heartbeat and her patience and, I began to suspect, years of her own life—she aged in front of me, subtly, the way a fire ages when you feed it green wood. I held the candle and the haze flickered and sometimes showed me nothing and sometimes showed me snow on a wall and once showed me a table set for two in a room I almost recognized, two cups of tea, one gone cold.

He needed to hear someone. I couldn't tell if it was me, or the woman I'd been, or the man I couldn't see. So I did the only thing I could think of. I talked to him. Low, under my breath, the way you talk to a frightened animal or a sleeping child—about the raid, about how we got the ember back, about Halvard's soldiers and the marsh road and the taste of river water, about anything, everything, filling the room with my voice so that whatever part of him was deciding whether to stay would know there was someone here who had crossed a country carrying him.

"Stay awake," I told him, which wasn't fair, because he couldn't, but it was the only prayer I knew.

Near midnight, his fever broke.

* * *

The healer—her name was Maren, she told me much later, after—slept like the dead for fourteen hours, and I sat between her bed and Renn's and did not sleep at all, because every time I closed my eyes I heard *even if you forget me, especially then*, and I had learned what happened to me when I was alone with sentences like that.

In the morning Renn's color had come back, the pale frost gone from his eyes, and Maren made us porridge with honey and would not hear a word of payment.

"Don't be stupid," she said, waving off the coin I pushed across the table. "You don't pay for hedge-work. That's the entire point of it. The day I charge for mending what's broken, I'm a guild, and I've seen what your guild's bargains cost." She nodded at the ember's shape beneath my coat, where its warmth had become familiar as a second heartbeat. "Blood prices. Memory tolls. Everything paid forward and nobody told the interest." She snorted. "The wolves at least bargain honestly. Your kings wrote the dishonesty into law."

"The covenant demands a sacrifice," I said quietly. "From whoever reforges it. A memory. I know which memory." I looked at Renn, asleep again but breathing right, his chest rising and falling in the honest rhythm of a man merely tired. "I've known for two moons. I haven't told him."

Maren stirred her tea. She had the particular stillness of women who have watched a great many people die and intend to be neither rushed nor dramatic about any of it.

"May I tell you something impertinent, signatory?"

"You may."

"That magic of yours—the ember-magic, the covenant—it takes things *from* you. Always. Payment, payment, payment. And everyone acts as though that's just what power costs, as though the taking is the nature of it." She sipped. "But my grandmother taught me otherwise, and her grandmother taught her, all the way back past the founding. Power isn't a ledger. Power is a *garden*. You tend it, it feeds you. You strip it bare, it turns to dust and blows away. The covenant takes because the men who wrote it could imagine nothing but taking." She set down her cup. "When you go to that wall, signatory—and you will go, the valley's been dreaming about it for days—you might ask yourself whether the price is truly demanded. Or whether you've simply never seen anyone refuse to pay it."

I stared at her. "The twelfth ember showed me the terms. I read them. In the original script."

"And who translated the original script for you?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Every ember's memory had arrived pre-interpreted, wrapped in understanding, the old language unfolding into meaning inside my skull as though the meaning lived in the ember itself.

"Embers remember," Maren said, gently now. "But remember *through* whoever holds them. That's not corruption, necessarily. Just... soil. Different soil, different crop." She rose and began bundling bread and dried meat and a jar of the sharp-smelling green salve into a cloth. "Perhaps the covenant genuinely requires your memories of him. Or perhaps twelve centuries of kings and guildmasters reading the terms through their own hungry hearts wrote the requirement in. I couldn't say. Neither could you." She pressed the bundle into my hands. "But I'll tell you what I can say. I mended a wound today without taking a penny from anyone, and the world didn't end. Started there, if you ask me. Start there and work backward."

* * *

We stayed two days. On the second evening, Renn woke fully, ate an amount of food that alarmed me until Maren laughed and produced a second helping, and then lay propped on his elbow watching me mend his shirt with the particular intensity of a man composing a sentence he isn't sure he's allowed to say.

"Maren says you fought her," I said, not looking up from my needle. "While she healed you. That you held on to the hurt."

His ears flattened slightly—the wolf-kin tell I'd learned to read before I ever understood why I could. "She talks too much."

"She said you thought you deserved it."

A long silence. Outside, the valley breathed—wind in the gardens, a dog barking once, far off, contented. Eight embers' worth of memory sat in me like stones in a riverbed, and around them, the water of everything I still didn't know.

"When the covenant broke," Renn said finally, "twelve people died. People whose names I know. Whose funerals I stood at." His thumb moved absently over the new pink seam along his ribs. "I lived. I got to carry the embers' locations in my head and search for you for twelve years, and they got graves. So yes. Some nights I think the knife had the right idea." He glanced up, quick and wry and breaking my heart. "Don't look at me like that. I submitted to a rite of shame at the ford for less."

"I'm not looking at you like anything."

"You're looking at me like Maren."

"She's growing on me."

"She grows on everyone. It's the hedge-work." He lay back, wincing only a little, and for a while we listened to the valley together. Then, quietly, in the tone of a man approaching a wall he's circled many times: "Odile. When you found the ninth ember. What did it show you?"

I set down the shirt. This was the question I'd been circling too, and we both knew it, and I had promised myself in the dark hours that if he asked, I would answer honestly, because whatever else the covenant had made of me, I refused to become a person who loved through silence the way he had loved through silence for twelve years.

"It showed me the guildmaster," I said. "Signing the betrayal. And it showed me—" My hands weren't entirely steady, so I folded them. "It showed me asking for the erasure. Choosing it. And Renn, I remembered why. Not everything. But enough. I remembered that the breaking of the covenant was going to burn through me—that because I was the last signatory, everything the pact connected would come apart *through* me, and it would have taken my mind with it unless something was taken out first. And the something was you. The memory of you was the keystone. Pull it, and the rest of me holds." I met his eyes. "I chose to forget you to stay alive, and I have spent eight embers learning to hate myself for it, and Maren today said something that I haven't been able to put down."

"What did she say?"

"That maybe the price isn't real. That maybe it's just never been refused." I heard my own voice go rough. "The reforging is in six days. At the wall. And I know what the terms demand, and I was ready to pay it—I've been ready since chapter and verse—and now there's an old woman in a struck-off valley who mended you for free, and I can't stop thinking: what if the thirteenth term isn't a toll at all? What if it's a test? What if the covenant was written by people who could only imagine taking, and the last signatory is supposed to be the one who finally imagines something else?"

Renn was very still. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way a man's voice is careful when hope is dangerous.

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I pay, and you remember for both of us, like you promised."

"And if you're right—if refusing works—" He sat up fully now, ignoring the pull of the wound, and his gray eyes had a light in them I hadn't seen since before the glacier. "Odile. If you're right, then everything changes. Not just for us. For the wolves, for the vale, for every place the Crown struck off the maps. A covenant that doesn't feed on its signers—" He stopped. Laughed, unsteadily. "You'd be writing a new kind of magic. With your blood, at the wall, in six days."

"Yes," I said. "Which is why it's convenient that we leave tomorrow."

His laugh died. "Why tomorrow?"

"Because of this." I drew the ember from under my coat and set it on the blanket between us, and for the first time since the bone orchard, I let myself look plainly at what had been troubling me. It pulsed. Not steadily—not the slow heartbeat-warmth I'd learned—but irregular, urgent, like a fist knocking. "It started last night. All nine of them, I think, resonating. Something's calling them home." I met his eyes. "And this morning, Maren's granddaughter came up from the south track and said there were soldiers camped at the ridge. Crown blue. A full company." I watched the color of that land on him. "They haven't come down yet. Maybe they're lost. Maybe they're hunting. But either way, Renn—we're out of valleys."

Outside, far off on the southern ridge, a horn sounded. Once. Low. Patient.

Waiting.
