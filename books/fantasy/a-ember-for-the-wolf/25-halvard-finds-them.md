## 25. Halvard Finds Them

The pass had a name once. Halvard had looked it up the night before, in a survey ledger so old its pages shed brown flakes like insect wings: *Cael's Rest, alt. 1,900 feet, disused since the Second Partition.* Now it was just a throat of gray stone between two shoulders of mountain, and at the bottom of that throat, on a road barely wider than a cart track, two travelers were making camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower.

He had been watching them for an hour.

They were careless. That was what struck him first—not their fire, which was small enough, screened behind stones, but the way they moved around each other. The Winterbourne woman crouched at the flames with a tin kettle while the wolf-kin stood at her back, facing outward, and every few minutes they exchanged words too quiet for even Warden-trained ears at this distance. It was not the choreography of fugitives. Fugitives flinched. These two had arranged themselves like people who expected the world to leave them alone.

Halvard knew better. He had spent twenty-two years learning exactly how little the world left anyone alone, and how much of that leaving was his own doing.

He shifted his weight off his bad knee and considered, again, the packet in his breast pocket. Three days ago it had been a sealed writ of commission—*Halvard, First Warden of the Crown's Wardens, empowered to take alive or dead one Odile Winterbourne, charmwright, and one Renn of no recorded house, wolf-kin, both wanted under statute eleven of the Border Act.* He had read the statutes so many times he could recite them the way other men recited prayers. Statute eleven was clean work. Statute eleven said magic without license was theft from the Crown, and theft was answered with rope or iron depending on the mood of the magistrate.

Then he had gone into the archives beneath the palace, and statute eleven had begun to look like something else entirely.

* * *

He'd told himself it would be a quick errand. A day among the sealed shelves, pulling the guild's founding charters to confirm what he already suspected—that the Charmwrights' Guild had operated illegally for decades, that Odile Winterbourne was simply the last loose thread of a criminal enterprise, nothing more. He had wanted the paper trail tidy. Halvard liked tidy. It was why he was good at his work.

The archivist, a stooped man named Pell with ink-stained cuffs and the eyes of someone who had spent forty years pretending not to read what he filed, had brought him the boxes without comment. *Guild records, pre-Partition. Restricted by order of King Aldric II.* And Halvard had opened the first box expecting lists of fines and confiscated stock, and found instead a covenant.

Not a charter. Not a guild constitution. A *covenant*, written in two hands—one human, one something broader-palmed and claw-tipped—and signed in blood by thirteen signatories, human and wolf-kin both. He had read it twice standing up, then sat down on a crate and read it a third time slowly, because his hands were not steady.

It was a treaty. A real one. Between Vael and the northern packs, older than the Border Act by two hundred years, older than the dynasty itself. And appended to it, in the dry clerical hand of some long-dead royal scribe, was a memorandum:

*By order of His Majesty: the terms herein are to be assumed as Crown property. The border wards shall be redrawn upon this model, the tolls reassigned, the signatories dispersed. Let it be entered that the wolves broke faith first.*

There was no evidence the wolves broke faith first. Halvard had spent four days searching for it—any scrap, any incident report, any raid record—and what he found instead was a pattern. Village after village, erased from maps. Guildhalls burned with the ledgers inside. Signatories dead in accidents, or fled, or—his pen had stopped over this entry—*memory-bound by mutual consent of the remaining parties, nature of binding undisclosed.* Twelve years ago. The date matched the woman's guild registration exactly.

She hadn't hidden from them. She had been *unmade*, and the Crown's own paperwork called it housekeeping.

Pell had come back on the fourth evening with a lamp and found Halvard still sitting among the boxes, and the old man had looked at him with something that was almost pity. "You'll want to be careful what you do with that," Pell said. "Men have been promoted for less. Men have disappeared for more."

"I'm not looking for promotion," Halvard had said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and hated himself for it, because three weeks ago he would have meant it differently.

*Now* he crouched above Cael's Rest with a writ in his pocket and watched the last signatory of a stolen treaty pour tea for a wolf, and understood that the choice in front of him was not duty against doubt anymore. Doubt had lost that argument days ago. What remained was uglier: whether he was the kind of man who could stand in front of the truth and still do his job.

His soldiers were two miles back at the ford, following orders to sweep the valley at dawn. He had perhaps six hours before they arrived here regardless of what he did. Six hours to decide whose side the law was on.

He went down the scree with the noiseless step Wardens learned in their first winter, and was ten feet from the fire before the wolf-kin's head came up.

Renn did not growl. That was somehow worse. He simply turned, all at once, the way a door turns, and put himself between Halvard and the woman, and his eyes in the firelight were the flat amber of something deciding whether you were weather or meat.

"First Warden Halvard," Halvard said, hands open and away from his sword. "Alone. Unarmed, if it matters."

"It doesn't matter," Renn said. "You've got a knife at your belt, a crossbow bolt's worth of reach, and six friends downstream. But say your piece."

"Winterbourne." Halvard raised his voice slightly. "I know you can hear me. I'd rather talk than fight, and I'd very much rather my soldiers don't find you, which gives us until dawn to be honest with each other."

Silence. Then, from behind the wolf's shoulder, dry as old paper: "Wardens aren't honest. They're *procedural*." Odile Winterbourne stepped into the firelight, and Halvard saw what twelve years of small living had made of her—a thin woman with charm-scarred fingers and eyes that had done arithmetic on him before he finished speaking. "But you came down alone, which is either a trap or a confession. Sit down, Warden. Tea's nearly ready. If we're going to betray each other, we should do it warm."

* * *

She poured three cups. Halvard noticed she gave the wolf his first.

"You went into the archives," she said. It wasn't a question.

"How do you know that?"

"Because you're sitting here instead of blowing a whistle." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "A Warden with a writ doesn't parley. A Warden with a *conscience* does. Consciences get installed by reading things the Crown didn't want read. So—which shelf?"

"The guild records. Pre-Partition." He hesitated. "And the memorandum. *Let it be entered that the wolves broke faith first.* There's no incident report. There's no raid log. There's a hundred years of quiet erasure and one sentence of justification with nothing underneath it."

Odile looked at Renn. Something passed between them that Halvard couldn't read—old argument, or old agreement.

"And?" Renn said. "You found a lie in an old book. Congratulations. The Crown is built on lies; that's what crowns are *for*. Why are you telling *us*?"

"Because there's a difference between knowing a thing and having proof of it." Halvard set down his cup. "I have spent three days trying to build a case out of memos and margins, and I can't. The originals are gone—or locked deeper than Pell can reach. What survives are copies of copies and scribes' notes, and a magistrate will call that forgery before I finish the sentence. Unless—" he looked at Odile directly now, and made himself say it— "unless the last living signatory of the original covenant stands up in front of the right people and says what was taken from her."

The fire popped. Somewhere down-valley a night bird called, once, and quit.

"You want me to testify," Odile said slowly. "To the Crown. Against the Crown."

"To whatever court will still hear it. To the guild remnant, if any survive. To *anyone* with standing." Halvard spread his hands. "I can't prove the theft with paper. But a signatory's testimony, witnessed under oath, carries weight the archives don't. Blood-signatures bind. Even the Crown's lawyers admit that—it's the whole reason they unmade the signatories instead of arresting them. Arrests create records. Forgetting creates nothing."

"That's why they took my memories." She said it quietly, mostly to herself, and Halvard watched her arrive at the thought in real time—the way a person watches ice shift on a river. "Not punishment. *Housekeeping.* You can't prosecute a witness if she can't remember being one."

"Yes," Halvard said. "And I think you're recovering them. The embers." At her sharp glance he added, "I've read the pursuit reports. Shard-hum readings across four provinces, all trending north along your route. I'm not a fool, Winterbourne. I just used to be a loyal one."

Renn had not moved through any of this. Now he spoke, low. "Say the rest of it, Warden. The part you came down the hill to say."

Halvard breathed out. This was the cliff edge. He had rehearsed it on the climb down and it still felt like stepping into air.

"My soldiers sweep this valley at dawn. I can delay them—I'll send them west along the river, tell them I've picked up false trail. That buys you two days, maybe three." He met the wolf's flat gaze and held it. "In exchange, when you have what you need—all thirteen embers, the full covenant, whatever proof exists—you bring it to me first. Before you run, before you burn anything, before you vanish over the border. You give me the chance to put it in front of people who matter, with my name beside yours. That's my offer. My career, probably my neck, thrown in free of charge."

"And if we refuse?" Odile asked.

"Then at dawn I whistle, and we do this the procedural way, and I spend the rest of my life knowing exactly what I helped bury." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something—he let her see it cost him. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to use me. There's a difference, and it favors you."

Silence again. The wolf rose, slow, and walked a half-circle around Halvard, close enough that Halvard could smell pine and blood and something wilder underneath, and stopped at his shoulder.

"Why?" Renn said. "One word of truth. Not strategy. Not conscience-theater. Why does a man burn twenty years of loyalty in one week?"

Halvard looked into the fire. He thought about lying, decided these two would smell it, and answered plainly.

"Because when I was nine years old, the Crown unmade a village two valleys east of where I was born. I remember the year the maps changed. I remember my mother saying *there was never a town called Marrowdale*, and I remember believing her, because believing her was easier." He looked up. "Last month I found Marrowdale in a guild ledger. Population four hundred. Gone so completely that the only place it still exists is a book the Crown tried to burn. I've been enforcing that erasure my whole life, Winterbourne. Every ward I've set, every charm I've confiscated—I was the broom." His voice went rough. "I'd like to stop sweeping."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Odile laughed—short, exhausted, real.

"A broom with a conscience." She glanced at Renn, and whatever silent negotiation happened between them concluded. "All right, Warden. Here are our terms, and they're not negotiable. One: you delay your soldiers, and when you can't delay anymore, you lose our trail honestly—no staged captures, no traps dressed as mercy. Two: we keep the embers. When the time comes, you'll see everything—but we decide when. Three." Her eyes hardened. "If you ever point steel at him again, the bargain dies, and so does your case, because I'll scatter what I've recovered to every hedge-witch and outlaw between here and the sea and let the truth drown in rumor. Clear?"

"Clear."

"Then drink your tea, First Warden. You look terrible."

* * *

They talked until the moon set. Halvard told them where the patrols would be, which passes were watched, which magistrates might still be reachable and which were bought to the bone. In return, carefully, in pieces, Odile gave him fragments—the drowned chapel, the bone orchard, the guildmaster's ambush—enough to show him the shape of the thing without handing him the map. Renn said almost nothing, but Halvard caught him watching the way a man watches weather that might yet turn, and did not blame him. Trust was a currency Halvard had debased for two decades; he didn't expect to be paid in it overnight. He only needed to be paid in *time*.

Near dawn, he climbed back up the scree. At the top he paused and looked back. The fire had been killed; the campsite might never have existed. Only Odile stood visible for a moment against the graying sky, and she raised one hand—not friendship, not quite. Acknowledgment. The salute one professional gives another across a line they've agreed, provisionally, to share.

Halvard turned west toward the river and his waiting soldiers, and began composing the lie that would buy two days.

Behind him, far to the north, under a sky still missing its stars, something glinted on the road ahead of the two travelers—something small, and warm, and humming, like a coal that refused to die.
