## 26. The Archive Beneath the Palace

They came back into Vael the way water comes back into a cellar—through cracks no one had bothered to guard because no one remembered they existed.

Odile had forgotten them too, or so she believed until Renn stopped at a rusted grate in the canal wall below the Tanner's Quarter and said, "This is the one you showed me," and her hands moved before her mind could object, fingers finding the hidden catch in the iron's scrollwork as naturally as finding her own pulse.

"I showed you?"

"Twelve years ago." He didn't look at her. He'd been careful about looking at her since the night on the border wall, since the confessions that sat between them now like an ember banked under ash—hot, patient, too dangerous to stir. "You said the Charmwrights kept a second door into the palace, from the old days, when guilds still checked crowns instead of serving them."

The grate swung inward on hinges she must have oiled herself, once, in a life she couldn't recall. The smell of the tunnel beyond hit her: wet stone, old tallow, and beneath it something faintly sweet—the ghost-smell of charmwork, worked into the mortar itself by hands that had trusted someone to come this way again.

*You trusted me to come back,* she thought. *Whoever I was.*

She was someone else now, mostly. Twelve embers' worth of someone else—no. Nine. Nine embers recovered, three still scattered somewhere in the world, and every one of them had returned a piece of Odile Winterbourne to herself until she sometimes felt like a house whose rooms were being lit one by one, each illumination revealing more damage than the dark had shown. She knew the wall now. She knew the oath. She knew the queen with her secret wolf-love and her blood-signed page, and she knew what the twelfth ember would eventually demand of anyone who tried to reforge what had been broken.

But the tunnel knew her better than she knew herself. Her feet found the dry path past the flooded stretch without hesitation. Her hand found the third bracket where the fourth should have been, and pressed, and a section of brick exhaled dust and opened onto a stair descending deeper than the palace foundations had any right to go.

"You've been here," Renn said quietly behind her.

"I've been everywhere, apparently." She struck a charm-light—a small thing, gray-gold, the kind she used to sell for two coppers in the night market—and held it up. The stairs went down into blackness that swallowed her little flame after ten steps. "I just don't remember being here. Which is either very convenient or very alarming."

"Both," Renn said. He shifted the wrapped shard of covenant-stone in his arms—he'd carried it across half the realm, and it hummed now almost constantly, eager, like a dog scenting home. His wounds from the glacier vault had closed but left him moving stiffly, his left arm bound against his chest, and he refused with a flat look every time she suggested he wait above. "Both is usually the answer with you."

* * *

Halvard was waiting at the bottom.

That was the arrangement—they'd sent word through the hedge-healer's network, which turned out to be older and stranger than any of them had guessed—but knowing a Crown Warden was going to be standing in the dark holding a lantern did not prepare Odile for the jolt of seeing him there, tall and gray-cloaked, Warden's badges scraped off his shoulders but the posture of thirty years of authority impossible to scrape away.

"You're late," Halvard said.

"We were followed through the marshes."

"I know. I lost them for you at the weir gate. You're welcome." His lantern light caught his face: hollowed out since Hollow Vale, since the archives request he'd told them about with a warden's flat economy of words. Whatever he'd found in the sealed records had eaten something in him. Or started eating. "Come on. The door at the end answers to guild-blood. I've been standing here for six hours hoping one of you bleeds the right way."

"The right way?"

"Ember-signatory blood." He turned and led them down the last stretch of stairs. "The archive doesn't open for kings. That, it turns out, is the whole joke."

* * *

The door was not wood and not quite stone—a slab of fused charmwork, seamless, covered in script that Odile recognized with a lurch as the same flowing hand from the ledger in the ruined guildhall. The hand that had written her name. When she laid her palm against it, the script warmed, crawled, tasted her, and the door split down the middle with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Beyond lay a room that should not have existed beneath Vael.

It was round, and vast, and old in a way the palace above was not—older than the palace, older than the walls, its floor a mosaic of interlocking sigils that predated every style of charmwork Odile had ever studied. And along the curving walls, in niches cut like reliquaries, stood documents. Scrolls under glass. Ledgers chained shut. Maps of borders that no longer matched any border Odile knew. Thirteen alcoves ringed the chamber, and nine of them stood dark and empty.

"The embers were kept here," Renn breathed. His fur had risen along the back of his neck; in the lamplight the wolf was closer to his surface than usual. "This is a covenant-house. This was *ours*. Neutral ground. The wolves built the thirteenth alcove—" he pointed to the one empty space that differed from the rest, its carvings run through with running-wolf motifs worn nearly smooth, "—this was the signatories' meeting place. Before Vael was Vael. Before there was a crown."

"And then there was a crown," Halvard said. He walked to a lectern at the room's center, where a single document lay open, already waiting—as if the room had known who was coming. "And the crown needed the city to believe the border wards were the king's gift to his people. Read this."

Odile read.

It was the founding charter of Vael. Not the one taught in schoolrooms, with its illuminated kings and its divine right and its language of protection and gift. This was the working draft—the one with corrections, marginalia, crossed-out lines. And in the margins, in a different ink, in the flowing hand, someone had written comparisons. Line by line. Clause by clause.

Every ward-formula. Every term of passage and trade between realm and realm. Every promise of mutual defense, every rite of crossing, every penalty for broken faith—all of it lifted, wholesale, from a document Odile had now seen fragments of nine times over. The Ember Covenant. The founding kings hadn't negotiated with the wolf-kin. They hadn't been granted terms. They had *copied* them—took the covenant's entire architecture and rebuilt it as royal property, cutting out the other signatory entirely, keeping the magic and discarding the kin.

"They stole it," she said. Her voice came out strange and level. "Not just the terms. The *spells*. The wards that fell on the night the stars went out—they were always covenant-work, just... amputated. Cut off from the other half of their power source."

"That's why they failed," Renn said softly. "Twelve years of decay, then collapse. A borrowed heart doesn't beat forever."

"There's more." Halvard's voice had gone very quiet, the way it had when he'd questioned her in her shop a lifetime ago, all those chapters back—except now she could hear what lived under the quiet. It wasn't suspicion. It was grief. He pulled another document from the niche beside the lectern, one of the few unsealed ones, and spread it flat. "Warden orders. Standing orders, renewed by every monarch since the second king. Do you know what the Wardens' actual mandate is? Not lawkeeping. *Unmaking.* Every village erased from the maps—like Corrowmere, the one you passed through. Every magic-touched place gone quiet. There's a schedule. We aren't hunters, Odile. We're erasers. I've spent thirty years holding the pen."

Odile looked at the schedule. Names of places she'd never heard of, each with a date, each date passed except for a handful remaining near the bottom. The ink of the renewals changed over the centuries—different fists, different flourishes, one signature in particular recurring every generation, the current queen's line—but the order itself never varied. Only the handwriting changed.

"It's not a bad king," she said slowly, and heard the thought form as she spoke it. "It's not even a bad dynasty. It's a machine. Each one inherits it and signs their name because signing is easier than asking what they're signing. Halvard—you asked what you were fighting for your whole career, and the answer is nothing. There's no enemy. There's just a copy of a copy of a theft, enforced by people who never read the original."

"Don't," Halvard said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't let me off that easily." He turned to face her, and his eyes in the lantern light were terrible with honesty. "I read the archives three weeks ago. I knew before tonight. And I still requested the promotion. I still wrote the report that put Wardens on your trail in the Ash Market. The system isn't a machine that runs itself, Odile. It's a thousand men like me, each deciding once, each morning, that it's easier not to know. I decided that every day for thirty years. The machine is made of choices. Mine included."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the palace clocks marked the hour—one stroke, two—and Odile thought of the thirteenth moon, waxing toward its final turn, and how much time the machine had left to be argued with.

Renn had drifted to the thirteenth alcove, the wolf-carved one. He stood before it with his ears—if he'd had ears, if he'd been in his true shape—flat, and when he spoke his voice had gravel in it.

"There's something in mine. It's sealed."

"Signatory blood," Halvard said. "Same as the door."

Odile went to him. The seal on the alcove was a small disk of gray glass—ember-glass, like the pendant Halvard had worn the day he came to her shop, and she understood that detail now too, understood why a Warden carried covenant-material: the Wardens had always been issued pieces of the stolen machinery, keys mistaken for ornaments. She cut her thumb on the edge of her own charm-knife and pressed it to the glass, and it melted away like frost.

Inside lay a single sheet of paper, fresh enough that the ink still looked wet after all these years—preserved, alive, waiting. Written in the flowing hand. Addressed simply:

*To the last signatory.*

Odile's hands shook. Renn's hand found her shoulder, steadying, and she read aloud because some things are too heavy to carry silently.

*"If you are reading this, the wards have fallen and the moon is turning, and you have come to the place where the lie was first written down. Know then what we knew: the crown cannot make covenants. Only equals can. The kings copied our terms because they could not bear to ask for them, and the copy has been dying ever since, and everything done to hide its dying—every erased village, every hunted witch, every wolf driven from the borderlands—is the sound of a stolen thing trying not to be discovered.*

*"You will be told the reforging demands your memories. This is true, and it is also the oldest cruelty in the original terms, written by frightened people on a cold wall, and you are permitted—hear me—you are permitted to write the new pact differently. The covenant was stolen once by refusing to ask. It can be remade only by asking. Ask the realms to see each other. Ask, and let the price fall where it falls. But do not let the old terms dictate the new grief.*

*"We could not finish it. Perhaps you can."*

There was no signature. Only a small drawn symbol at the bottom: a wall, a moon, and two figures standing on it, faces deliberately left blank—not blurred, not erased. Left blank. Waiting to be filled in.

Odile stared at those empty faces for a long time. Then she folded the letter with great care and put it inside her coat, over her heart, and turned to find both men watching her.

"The twelfth ember told me the price," she said. "My memories of Renn. All of them. That's the term, that's the bargain, that's what reforging costs." She looked at Renn—at the gray of his eyes, at twelve years of searching written in the lines of his face—and made herself keep talking. "But this says the terms are ours to write. That the memory-price was the old fear speaking. That we can ask differently."

"Then don't pay it," Renn said, hoarse. "Find another way. Write—"

"I don't know yet what the new terms require. That's the point of asking instead of copying." She touched the folded letter through her coat. "But whatever happens at the wall, we do it *asking*. Not the way the kings did. Not taking."

Halvard cleared his throat. When they looked at him, the Warden was holding his lantern up toward the far side of the chamber, where a service stair climbed toward the palace's upper levels—and where, faintly, unmistakably, torchlight was beginning to flicker down through the cracks around a door that had no business opening. Boots. Many boots. Descending.

"That'll be Captain Verel's sweep team," Halvard said grimly, setting the lantern down and rolling his shoulders like a man putting an old career behind him one joint at a time. "I requested access to this archive through official channels. Official channels apparently decided to watch who came to answer my request. Odile—there's a map alcove on the north curve, third from the wolf carving. The old survey maps. They show every place the crown erased, and every place it hasn't gotten to yet."

"Why do I need a map of—"

"Because four of your embers are in the places that haven't been erased *yet*," Halvard said. "Four embers, four villages on the destruction schedule, and Verel's orders are to burn the schedule's next entry at dawn. Take the maps. Run. I'll hold the door."

"Halvard—" Odile began.

"I spent thirty years erasing places," the Warden said, drawing his sword with a rasp that sounded almost like relief. "Let me spend one night keeping one from being emptied. Go."

Above them, muffled and growing nearer, came the measured tread of the crown's boots on stairs built by thieves—and Odile snatched the survey maps from their niche, rolled them against her heart beside the letter, and ran.
