## 27. Embers Four Through Seven

The fourth ember was waiting for us in a shepherd's croft above the Fenwater, sewn into the lining of a dead woman's winter coat.

Her name had been Marta Hale, the widow who lived there now told us, and she had been a charmwright of the old guild—low-ranked, unmarked, the kind of woman history never bothers to bury properly. She had died two springs past and left instructions that the coat go to no one until "the Winterbourne girl comes asking." The widow had nearly burned it twice. She kept it instead out of a stubbornness she couldn't explain, which is how I have come to understand most of the covenant's guardianship works: not through loyalty, but through small magics laid so deep in ordinary things that even forgetting cannot root them out.

I found the ember stitched behind the collar, wrapped in waxed linen, still warm as a sleeping bird.

Renn stood in the doorway while I opened it. He always stood in doorways now—half in, half out, as if he could not decide whether proximity or distance would keep me safer. His arm had healed crooked from the glacier, and he favored it without acknowledging it, the way wolves do.

"Whatever it shows you," he said, "it already happened."

"I know."

"That has never once stopped it from hurting you."

He was right. It never did.

The fourth ember tasted like woodsmoke and iron filings when I pressed it to my palm, and the memory came up through me like water finding an old wellshaft:

—I am twenty-one, and I am standing in the guildhall's back room with my sleeves rolled to the elbow, learning the binding-script that keeps the border wards fed. Master Corvane watches over my shoulder. There is ink on my fingers and on my chin, and someone is laughing at me from the doorway—a low laugh, warm, familiar—and I turn toward it and the face blurs, it always blurs, but this time I catch something new: his hands. Long-fingered, scarred across the knuckles, holding two cups of cider. He sets one beside me without a word because he has learned I don't like being interrupted while the script is wet.

"Drink," the blur says. "You've been at it six hours."

"I'll drink when the wards hold."

"The wards held yesterday. They'll hold tomorrow. You're not saving Vael tonight, Odile. You're just making sure it stays."

And I drink, and the cider is spiced wrong—he always gets the spices wrong—and I love him for it with a completeness that makes my present self grip the edge of the widow's table until her spoons rattle.

The memory turned, as they all did. Sweet first, then the knife:

—the same room, months later. The script is different now. Harder. Corvane's voice is tight. *Thirteen embers, thirteen anchors, and every anchor needs a living hand.* And I understand, in the way one understands things at twenty-one, instantly and without any grasp of the weight: each signatory will give something. A tithe of self. Corvane gives his left eye's sight. Aldous gives his singing voice. And mine—

Mine is being decided in a whisper between Corvane and the blurred man by the window, and they are arguing about me as if I am not there, and the blurred man's hands are shaking, and I say, "Tell me what it costs or I walk out and you can find another fool," and Corvane says the word, and the memory ends before I hear it—

But my body heard it twelve years ago. My body remembered it now. I came back to the widow's kitchen with salt water on my face and no idea what I was grieving, only that its shape fit some hollow in me exactly, the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Renn crouched in front of me. He didn't touch me. He had learned that too.

"What did it cost?" he asked quietly. Too quietly. As if he already knew.

"I don't know. I never hear the word. It cuts off right before." I wiped my face with my sleeve. "Someone decided it for me. Someone decided before I could—"

"Would it help," Renn said, "to know that he argued against it? That he fought for three days?"

"How do you know that?"

The pause was a heartbeat too long. "Covenant-stone remembers arguments," he said. "It's what it's for."

It was the third lie he'd told me since the glacier. I let it stand. Wholeness was arriving in pieces now, and I was beginning to suspect that some of the pieces were people.

* * *

The fifth ember lay under the millpond at Coldharrow, in a box of black oak that the miller's family had been lowering on ropes every solstice for twelve years, telling their children it was a blessing on the water.

We went down together, because the rope would hold only one of us and Renn refused—flatly, in that tone that ended discussions—to let me go alone. So he dove, and I watched his shadow fold into the green-black water and disappear, and I counted the length of my own heartbeat until I hated myself for counting.

He came up gasping, the box strapped across his back, blood thinning the water around his shoulder where something below had objected. He wouldn't say what. He smiled about it, which frightened me more than the blood.

Inside the box, packed in peat that had done its work faithfully, the fifth ember glowed like a coal remembering fire.

This memory was not a room. It was a season.

—Summer. My twenty-second year. We are traveling the border villages with Corvane's ledger and a wagon of warding-tools, renewing the small pacts: the field-wards, the birthing-charms, the little agreements between human farmers and wolf-kin hunters that have kept the marches fed and peaceful for a hundred years and that no one in Vael knows exist. I am happy. The memory insists on it. I am happy in a way I have not been able to be since, wide-open and unguarded, riding on the wagon's tailboard with my boots off while the blurred man walks beside the wheel and argues with me about whether the moon is following us.

"It follows everyone," he says. "You're not special."

"I'm a signatory of the Ember Covenant. I'm extremely special."

"You're sunburned is what you are."

And there is a village—we call it Hollowbridge, though the map-name doesn't matter anymore because I have seen what remains of map-names—and the wolf-kin there are gray and tall and shy of us, and one of them, an old female with frost in her muzzle, takes my hand in both of hers and says, *You smell like the wall-girl. The one who swore.* And the blurred man goes very still, and I laugh and ask what wall-girl, and the old wolf-kin looks between us and her eyes go soft and knowing, and she says nothing else for the rest of the visit.

That night we camped in a barley field and the blurred man could not sleep and neither could I, and we lay a hand-span apart talking about nothing until the stars wheeled, and just before dawn he said my name—not Odie, not Winterbourne, but my whole name, like an oath—and began to say something after it—

And the memory tore, cleanly, surgically, the way all these memories tore at the moment of greatest tenderness, as if whoever had cut them had known precisely where the seams of me ran.

I surfaced in the miller's loft with Renn's hand hovering an inch from my shoulder, not landing.

"There was a village," I said. "Hollowbridge. Wolf-kin and humans trading eggs for elk-meat. Children with—" My voice cracked. "Children who spoke both tongues. Renn, the Crown says the wilds are mindless. The Crown says the packs took those villages. But they *lived* there. Together."

"I know," Renn said.

"You know. Everyone knows but me. Everyone's been carrying this while I sold luck-charms and called it a life." I stood, too fast; the loft tilted. "How much more is under the water, Renn? How many villages?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he was deciding, I watched him decide it, and it cost him.

"More than you can burn the Crown down with," he said. "And we don't have time to try. Three embers after this one, Odile. Then the moon turns."

* * *

The sixth ember we did not find. It found us.

We were three days north of Coldharrow, cutting through the hill country where the roads gave out, when the shard of covenant-stone Renn carried began to sing—a thin, high note, felt in the teeth rather than heard. We followed it off the path, up a sheep-track, to a cave mouth beneath an overhang of red stone, and inside, arranged on a ledge like offerings on an altar, were bones.

Wolf-kin bones. Old ones, tended ones—each skull faced outward, each long bone laid parallel, and tucked among them bundles of dried flowers renewed so recently that the scent still rose. Someone had kept this place. Someone alive, near, within walking distance.

The ember sat in a hollow of the largest skull, burning steady as a lamp.

"Grave-robbing," Renn said flatly, "is how we got the last one. I'd rather not repeat it."

"We're not robbing." I didn't know how I knew. I walked forward anyway, and the shard in Renn's grip hummed louder, and the ember brightened to meet me, and when my fingers closed on it, the cave fell away.

—I am nineteen. The wall. The day of the swearing—but not the swearing itself; I have seen that already, drowned in the chapel. This is the night before. The wall-top under a sky so thick with stars it seems the dark is the rare thing. I cannot sleep, and so I walk the parapet, and at the northern stair stands a boy about my age, human-cloaked, hood back, and I see the shape of his ears and do not care, because I have spent my whole life on the southern side of stories that said I should.

He does not startle. Neither do I. We simply begin talking, the way weather begins—inevitably, from pressure.

His name is a growl in the throat of his own language, but he offers a border-tongue name for my convenience, and it is *Renn*, and I say it back to him once, and the word fits in my mouth like it grew there.

We talk until the stars turn. About the wards, which he can feel like weather on his skin. About the covenant Corvane means to build, which he believes in and I am only beginning to. About his pack, who sent him to watch the strange human girl who smelled of oath-oaths. About my mother, dead two years, who told me once that the border was a wound and walls are scabs and scabs are not healing, only slowing.

When the sky grays toward morning he says, "Tomorrow you swear your life to my people. Don't you want to know anything about us first?"

"I'm learning," I say. "I started last night."

And he laughs—the low warm laugh, the cider laugh, the wheel-walking laugh—and twelve years fall away from me in a red-lit cave, and I know, I *know*, the way you know your own hands: the blur has a name. The blur has been beside me in every memory, wearing different shadows. The blur is Renn.

The vision held one beat longer—the young Renn turning to go, pausing, saying over his shoulder, "Odile. Whatever they ask you to give tomorrow, give it carefully. Some things don't grow back"—and then it was gone, and I was on my knees on the cave floor with an ember cooling in my fist and the truest thing I had learned in twelve years ringing through me like a struck bell.

I made myself look at him.

He stood very still in the cave mouth, backlit, watching me with an expression I had seen fragments of for weeks and never assembled: the doorways, the silences, the lies that were almost-truths. Grief wearing patience as a coat.

"You," I said. My voice came out ruined. "It's been you. All of them. Every blurred face."

Renn closed his eyes. When he opened them, he said, carefully, gently, in the voice of a man setting down something he had carried a very long way: "Not all of them. But enough."

"Why didn't you *tell* me?"

"Because you asked me not to." The words dropped between us like stones into the millpond. "Twelve years ago. Before you took the tithe. You made everyone who loved you promise that if you ever came hunting your own memory, we'd let you find it yourself. You said—" his voice frayed, caught, held— "you said if someone handed it to you whole, you'd never trust that you'd earned it. That you'd always wonder whose version of you you were living."

The cave was silent except for the ember's faint crackle.

"I hate her," I whispered. "I hate that girl on the wall."

"No, you don't," Renn said. "You're going to thank her. Just not yet."

* * *

The seventh ember was in Vael itself, which meant everything and was impossible, and we did it anyway because Halvard's uneasy shield still held.

He met us at the Salt Gate himself, alone, out of uniform, looking like a man who had aged five years in five months. He walked us through the lower city by servant's stairs and coal alleys, saying nothing, and delivered us at last to a place I had passed ten thousand times without seeing: the Guildhall's original foundation, buried beneath three generations of construction, accessible now only through the cellar of a chandler who owed Halvard a debt no honest ledger recorded.

The old guildhall's foundation chamber was intact. Warded, still, faintly, by scripts I now recognized as my own hand at twenty. And in the center, set into the cornerstone where I had placed it with my own two hands, burned the seventh ember.

Corvane's ledger had listed seven anchors within Vael's walls. Six had been seized and destroyed in the purges, one by one, over a decade. Only this one survived, because no one thought to look beneath the city's own floorboards, and because I had warded it with the one trick the Wardens' detectors could never parse: a script keyed not to secrecy but to *ordinariness*. It read as candle-glow. It read as nothing.

I knelt and touched it, and the last of Vael's memories rose in me, and they were the damning ones, the ones I understood now I had buried deepest.

—I am twenty-three, and it is the night the covenant breaks.

Not the wards falling—that comes later, that comes twelve years later, an echo of an echo. Tonight is the argument. Tonight Corvane stands in this chamber with the other signatories and tells us the Crown has discovered us. Not guessed—discovered. A queen's writ, sealed in red, demanding the covenant's terms be surrendered to the throne, its embers confiscated, its wolf-kin partners declared beasts by law so that the pact may be legally dissolved without perjury.

Aldous wants to run. Maren wants to fight. Corvane, gray-faced, wants to negotiate.

And I—twenty-three, signatory, in love, certain—stand up and say the thing that damns us: "If we surrender the terms, the Crown will use them. There are clauses in that pact that could bind the packs to the throne forever. Slavery with a holy seal. We cannot let them read it."

"Then we destroy the ledger," Maren says.

"And the embers scatter, and the wards starve, and the border opens raw," Corvane says. "There is no clean road, child."

And the blurred man—no. Not blurred anymore. Renn, my Renn, standing at my side in the one memory where he is perfectly clear because I built this memory's hiding-place *around* his face, I protected this one hardest of all—Renn says, quietly, "There's a third road. The old texts. A covenant can be *sundered and stored*. Folded down into its embers. Nothing surrendered, nothing destroyed. Frozen. Until someone can restore it under better skies."

"And the cost?" Corvane asks, though he must know; he has read the same texts.

Renn's jaw tightens. "The storing-signer holds the fold in their own memory. Every term, every clause, every name. They carry it all, unseen, until the reforging."

"Then it can't be Odile," Corvane says. "She's the final signatory. If she carries the fold and the fold fails—"

"It has to be me," I say, because I am twenty-three and certain, and then, softer, because I am also in love and beginning to be afraid: "Doesn't it."

And the memory shows me the rest in fragments, fast as cards thrown down: Renn begging me to choose anyone else. Me drafting the erasure-contract myself, my own legal hand, precise as ever. Renn refusing to sign as witness until I insert the clause—*let her find it herself; let no one hand it to her.* Me choosing what to erase: not the covenant, I needed the craft, but the *cost*. Him. Us. The wall, the summer, the cider, the barley field. Everything that would make me flinch when the moment came to carry a folded covenant alone through twelve unwitnessed years.

And the last fragment: my own voice, steady, signing: *Let the tithe be my love, freely given, so that duty may walk light.*

Then white. Then a charm shop. Then twelve years of small magic.

I came back to myself on the cold foundation stone with both hands pressed flat and Renn's arms already around me, and this time I let them be there.

"I chose it," I said into his shoulder. "God help me, Renn, I drew up the papers myself. I erased you on purpose and then I hired you to guide me and never once—"

"You kept the covenant alive," he said roughly. "Twelve years. Alone, hunted, small, and you kept it breathing. Every ember we've found knew your name, Odile. The *stone* sings for you. Do you understand what that means? It means the fold held. It means you won."

I pulled back far enough to look at his face—unblurred now, unbearably clear, the face from the wall, the face from the barley field, older by twelve hard years and still the same.

"Four remain," I said. "Four embers, and one moon-turn left, and now I know exactly what reforging asks of me. The fold passes to whoever signs anew. I carry it all again—or it dies with me."

"I know," Renn said.

"Then say it. Say what you've been not-saying since the cave."

He cupped the back of my head, forehead to my forehead, and gave me the only truth left that didn't hurt to hear: "Then we go get the eighth one first. There's a story attached to it you need to hear before you decide anything. About a queen. And who she loved."

Above us, muffled through three floors of Vael, the palace bells began to ring—and the rhythm was wrong, urgent, the pattern Wardens used for a breach.

Halvard's shield had broken.
