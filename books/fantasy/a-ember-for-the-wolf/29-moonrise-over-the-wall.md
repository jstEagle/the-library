## 29. Moonrise Over the Wall

**Odile**

The wall was smaller than she remembered.

That was the wrong thing to think, of course, because she hadn't remembered it at all—not truly. She had been given it back in pieces: a chapel's drowned bell, a funeral procession through snow, a blurred face on a parapet with the wind pulling at both their cloaks. The memories came to her like light through cracked glass, in slivers, and none of them had prepared her for the simple fact of the stone—gray, mortared with lichen, running from the marsh flats to the mountains in a line so straight it looked drawn with a ruler by someone who had never seen anything crooked in their life.

"It's smaller than I thought," she said.

"Everything is," Renn said. "When you're nineteen, walls are mountains. When you're thirty-one, mountains are walls."

They had come up the old stair on the Vael side, the one the Wardens no longer guarded because there was nothing left to guard—the wards were down, the border was a rumor, and the Crown's soldiers were massing two days south for whatever permanence they intended to impose. Above them the sky did that thing it had begun doing again since the third ember: flickering with half-born stars, points of light stuttering into existence like lamps being lit one by one in a vast dark house.

Renn walked the parapet like a man walking the length of his own spine. His wounds from the glacier had closed but not forgotten; she could see the stiffness in his left leg, the way he favored it without acknowledging it, and she had learned not to offer her arm because he would only refuse it in that gentle, infuriating way of his.

"You've been here before," she said. It wasn't a question. So much of what she said to him now wasn't a question. "Here. On this wall."

He stopped. The wind coming off the northern wilds carried the smell of pine and frost and something older, something like the inside of winter itself.

"Yes," he said.

"When?"

* * *

**Renn**

She knew. He could see it in the set of her shoulders as she faced the north country—she knew, or she had guessed, and she was done letting him choose his silences.

Twelve years of searching had taught him every possible way to approach this moment except the direct one. He had rehearsed speeches in a hundred cold campsites. He had composed confessions in his head while crossing rivers, while bleeding, while lying awake listening to her breathe three feet away with a blanket between them like a treaty line. Every version ended with her looking at him the way she was looking at the horizon now: steady, braced, waiting.

"The first time," he said, "I was seventeen. I'd crossed with a trading party from the Silvermere pack—we still traded then, before your Crown decided trade was treason. There was a girl on the wall. Human. She was arguing with a guard about tariff rates, of all things, and she was winning."

Odile turned. In the stuttering starlight her face was half-lit, half-not, like the realm itself.

"He was cheating the wolf-kin caravans," she said quietly. "Double tolls. Someone had to argue."

"I know. I heard you win." He laughed—a short, unsteady sound. "You didn't know what I was. I kept my hood up. And when the guard finally threw up his hands and stamped our papers, you looked at me—just me, out of all of them—and said, *They'll cheat you anyway, but at least now they'll do it honestly.* And then you smiled like it was a joke, and I went back down the stairs and forgot how to be a wolf for about an hour."

"That was you." Her voice had gone strange. Flat and bright at once, like ice over deep water. "The young trader. My father mentioned him afterward—a northerner who never bargained, just watched me."

"I came back the next market day. And the next. For four years I found reasons to cross that border, and every time, you were on the wall, and every time I told myself I was only—" He stopped. The wind pushed against them. Below, somewhere in the dark of the flats, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"Only what?" Odile asked. But her voice broke on it, and he understood that she already knew, that she had known since the bone orchard, maybe since the shard in her palm, and that everything he said next was going to hurt them both simply by being true.

* * *

**Odile**

*Only falling in love,* some voice in her finished, in a register she hadn't used since she was nineteen.

The memories were almost whole now. Nine embers' worth of return had rebuilt her the way a flood rebuilds a road—not smoothly, but unmistakably, every gap filled with something. She could remember the ledger with her name in it. She could remember the queen's secret signing. She could remember standing exactly here, on this exact stretch of parapet, with snow coming down and a man beside her whose face had been a smear of shadow in the first returned memory and was now—

Was now nearly there. A jaw. Gray eyes catching lantern-light. A hand holding hers over a covenant-stone that hummed like a struck bell.

She could remember everything except the one thing the embers seemed determined to make her earn.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is you've been not-saying since the cellar under my shop. Since the ash market. Since the ford, since the blanket, since the glacier. Say it while we're standing where it happened, because I am so tired, Renn. I'm tired of remembering around you."

He came closer. Not touching. Close enough that she could see the scar at his throat from the guildmaster's wire, the frost-scouring the ice vault had left on his cheekbones, the exhaustion of twelve years worn into him like water wears stone.

"On this wall," he said, "in the winter of the year you turned twenty-two, you asked me to marry you."

The world tilted. She gripped the parapet.

"You didn't ask me," he went on, soft and relentless. "You informed me, the way you informed that tariff guard, with citations. You said—and I have kept these words for twelve years, Odile, I have kept them like embers—you said: *The Covenant binds our realms. It should bind us too. Sign with me and let them all choke on precedent.*"

A sound came out of her that was half laugh, half sob. "That sounds like me."

"It was you. It was the most you thing anyone has ever said in my hearing." His hands were shaking; she saw it, and seeing it undid something in her chest that had been knotted since chapter upon chapter of silence. "And I said yes. And we signed the betrothal into the covenant itself—two names, one oath, so that neither realm could ever dissolve the pact without breaking both of us. Your guildmasters thought it was poetry. My pack elders thought it was politics. We were the only two people alive who knew it was neither."

Below them the flats lay silvered and still. Above, another star fought its way into the sky and held.

"What happened?" she whispered. Though she knew. She had learned it in the guildmaster's cellar, in her own voice confessing across a table: *I asked for it.* "What happened to us?"

"The breaking came. Your guild sold the terms to the Crown, the wards began to fail, and the price of reforging fell on the final signatory—you. And the price was memory. The covenant demanded that whoever held it give up the thing they held dearest." His voice did not waver, and she realized how much it cost him that it didn't. "And you chose to forget me rather than let the whole border fall."

"And you let me."

"I tried not to." Now it cracked, just once, a hairline fracture down twelve years. "I stood in front of you and begged. You looked at me—I will tell you this because you deserve the truth even though it cuts—you looked at me with complete kindness and no recognition whatsoever, and you thanked me for my service to the guild, and you walked away. And I decided that if you could survive forgetting, then I could survive remembering. That has been the arrangement between us ever since. You forgot. I remembered."

* * *

**Odile**

She had imagined this moment, without admitting she was imagining it—in the campfire chapters of their journey, in the quiet dark where tenderness sat between them at arm's length like a guest neither would invite closer. She had imagined relief. She had imagined grief.

She had not imagined anger.

"You should have told me," she said. "In the cellar. At the ford. Anywhere. You let me chase my own life in circles while you sat on the answer like a dragon on gold."

"If I'd told you too soon, it would have broken you before the moons turned." He said it evenly, which made it worse. "The healer told me. Halvard's archives said it plainer. A signatory who learns the full weight of the price too early refuses to pay it. And if you refused, the border dies, the realms drift apart forever, and everyone we gathered at that wall—all those wolves and outlaws and defectors—they lose everything. I chose your pain later over your collapse now. I'd choose it again. Hate me for it if you need to."

"I don't hate you." She said it fast, before the anger could finish forming, because underneath the anger was the other thing, the enormous thing, pressing up through her ribs like spring through snow. "I don't hate you. I'm furious with you and I don't hate you and I think those two things are going to live in me together for a long time. But Renn—"

She stepped toward him. The wind took her cloak and snapped it eastward, toward Vael, and she let it.

"All this time you were remembering for both of us. You've been carrying a whole love affair alone in your head for twelve years. Every anniversary. Every argument. Every—" Her voice failed and she pushed through it. "That's not fair. That was never fair. To either of us."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It wasn't."

"So take it back." She reached out and took his hand—the first time she had initiated touch since the glacier, since he'd bled into her hands on the ice and she had screamed his name with a desperation she hadn't then understood. His fingers were cold and calloused and trembling. "Tell me all of it. Everything the embers haven't given me yet. Every market day on this wall. Every quarrel. What our song was, what you called me when we were alone, what I cooked badly, all of it. I want it back from you. I want to borrow your memory until mine comes home, and then I want us to hold it together."

"Odile." Her name in his mouth sounded different now. Older. Worn smooth like a river stone from saying it to himself for twelve years in empty places.

"There's a condition," she said, and here her eyes stung and she let them. "After the reforging. After I pay the price and forget you again—because I know now, Renn, I know what it costs and I'm going to pay it—you keep telling me. You find me in whatever shop I'm running, in whatever life grows back over the scar, and you start at the beginning. The girl arguing about tariffs. And you don't stop until I know you again. Promise me."

For a long moment he couldn't speak. She watched him try three times, watched a wolf-kin warrior who had submitted to humiliating rites and held mountain passes against armies stand on a broken wall and be defeated entirely by a promise.

"I promise," he said at last.

And then, because the confession had opened a door and they were both too honest tonight to pretend otherwise, he raised her hand—their fingers still laced—and pressed his brow to it, the old gesture of the northern packs, the one that meant *mine to protect, mine to mourn, mine.*

"Not as betrothed," he said against her knuckles. "Not yet. The covenant isn't reforged and I won't bind you twice to the same burning thing. But hear this much, and carry it up the wall with you tomorrow: I loved you at nineteen and I love you now, and the woman between those two loves—the one who forgot, the one who bled nine embers to become herself again—I love her best of all. Because she chose all this. Knowing nothing, she walked the whole road back to me."

The stars were out in earnest now, dozens of them, the sky remembering itself point by point. Odile Winterbourne, last signatory of the Ember Covenant, leaned forward and rested her forehead against his chest, and felt his heart hammering there like something imprisoned and glad of it, and did not kiss him, though the not-kissing was its own kind of vow.

"One more night," she said into his cloak. "Then the wall. Then whatever it costs."

"One more night," he agreed, and wrapped the cloak around them both.

It was later—much later, when the moon had cleared the mountains and laid a road of white light along the parapet—that Odile lifted her head, frowning at the southern dark.

"Renn," she said slowly. "There's a fire in the flats. Due south. It wasn't there an hour ago."

He went still. Then he rose, fluid despite the bad leg, and looked where she pointed, and she watched every soft thing drain out of his face.

One fire. Then a second, answering it. Then, far off, a third—moving. Not toward the wall.

Toward them.

"That's no patrol," Renn said quietly. "Patrols don't travel alone in the dark, and they don't move like wolves." His lip curled back from his teeth. "I know that fire-sign. That's Corvane."
