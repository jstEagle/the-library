## 31. Ten Moons Gone

The ninth ember was still warm when I closed my hand around it, as if it had been holding heat all those years in a dying man's pocket, waiting to be given back.

Master Corvane—the Ember-Taker, the betrayer, the man who had sold thirteen promises for a title and a pension—died an hour after he pressed it into my palm, and he died looking at me with eyes that wanted something I would not give him. Forgiveness, maybe. Or just the sight of my face while I took back what he stole. I gave him neither. I sat beside him because someone should, and I watched the light go out of him like a lamp running dry, and when it was done I stood, brushed the ash from my knees, and walked out of his ruined tower without looking back.

Renn was waiting at the treeline. He had carried me out of that place on his back two nights before, half-dead from the wound Corvane's blade had opened along his ribs, and even now, bandaged and gray-faced, he stood like a wall between me and everything behind us.

"Well?" he asked.

I opened my hand. The ember sat in my palm like a drop of captured sunset, and where it touched my skin the memories came—not flooding this time, not drowning me the way the first ones had, but arriving with the terrible calm of things I had always known and only recently been robbed of.

I saw myself at twenty-three, standing in the guildhall's archive with Corvane, both of us reading the true terms of the covenant by candlelight. I saw him weep. I saw him take the ember from its case while I slept, and I saw—I remembered—chasing him through the snow, screaming his name, knowing I could not stop him and knowing, worse, that some part of me understood why he did it. He had been afraid. That was all. Thirteen embers, thirteen oaths, and the whole architecture of peace between two realms, and it had come down to a frightened old man who wanted the fear to stop.

"He didn't hate us," I said aloud. "That's the worst part. He was just afraid."

"Fear has built more ruins than hatred ever did." Renn took my elbow, steadying me as the last images settled. "What did it show you?"

"The night he stole it. And—" I hesitated, because this part was mine, private, a wound under the wound. "And my mother. She was Charmwrights' Guild too. I'd forgotten. The ember gave her back to me."

He went very still. Twelve years of searching had taught him, I think, that my griefs arrived sideways, unannounced, and the best thing he could do was stand close and let them pass through.

"I'm sorry," he said finally.

"Don't be. It's what they're for." I closed my fist over the ember again, felt its warmth pulse against my palm like a second heartbeat. "Nine down."

"Four left."

"And how long?"

Renn looked up. Above the black teeth of the pines, the sky was clear for the first time in days, and there it hung: the thirteenth moon, the hidden one, the moon no almanac admitted to and every wolf-kin child learned to read by. It was more than half full now, a pale coin stamped with shadow, swelling night by night toward a fullness that meant the end of everything—or the beginning, depending on what we managed before it turned.

"Three turns," he said. "Maybe a little less. The wolves count differently than your astronomers, but not by much."

"Three turns. Four embers." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "One ember per turn, with one to spare. How generous of the universe."

"It isn't generosity we need." He turned north, where the mountains were beginning to show their white shoulders above the trees. "It's speed."

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's cot, its roof half-collapsed, its hearth cold for years. Renn built a fire small enough to hide and I laid out our remaining supplies on a blanket between us, the way a merchant takes inventory, because counting things made the world feel like it still had edges.

Dried meat for six days, if we ate thin. Hard bread for four. A skin of water each, a coil of rope, the hedge-healer's salve from Hollow Vale already half gone from tending Renn's wound. Two embers in the iron box at my belt—the drowned chapel's, the bone orchard's—and seven more bound into the cord across my chest, warm against my sternum, humming faintly whenever I breathed deep. Nine small suns. Four missing teeth in a jaw that needed all thirteen to bite.

"The glacier vault is closest," Renn said, tracing lines in the ash near the fire. "Two of them, sealed together beneath the ice—that's why the guild split them, so no one thief could carry off a pair. Then the salt fen, south past the old wall. And the last one—" His claw-tip paused.

"Say it."

"The last one never left Vael. It's in the palace. Under the throne room, if the old songs are true, in the foundation-stone the first kings built their seat upon." He looked up at me. "They didn't steal an ember, Odile. They built their capital on top of one. Every law, every coronation, every Warden's oath—it's all been standing on our covenant and calling the weight its own."

I thought about that. About Halvard, who had held a mountain pass against his own soldiers so we could live, whose career was now ash somewhere in the Crown's ledgers. About the archive beneath the palace, and the founding charter with its stolen clauses, and the queen who had signed the original pact in blood and been written out of her own history for it.

"Then we go to the ice first," I said. "Then the fen. Then Vael." I met his eyes across the fire. "Because if the Crown seals the border before the thirteenth moon turns—if they get their great working finished—we won't need the palace ember at all. There'll be nothing left to save."

"You've heard something."

"I've seen it." I pulled my pack closer and drew out the scrap of broadsheet I'd taken from a waystation three days back, its ink smeared but legible. A proclamation, stamped with the Crown's sunburst seal. *By order of Her Majesty's Wardens: the northern marches are closed. All crossings to be warded by the Feast of Lanterns. Unlicensed magic within the city bounds punishable by severance.* Severance. A clean little word for cutting a person's gift out of them at the root, leaving them hollow and docile and half-alive.

"Feast of Lanterns," Renn repeated. "That's eleven days after the moon turns."

"Then they're not waiting for the moon. They're racing it." I fed the broadsheet to the fire and watched the sunburst seal curl and blacken. "Halvard told us the mobilization had begun. He didn't tell us how far along it was. They're pulling Wardens from every province. They're chaining wards to the ley-lines themselves—crown-stone and iron, the old theft-magic, the kind that eats whatever it touches. When it's done, the border won't just be closed. It'll be blind. Deaf. Nothing will cross it, Renn. Not wolf, not charm, not prayer. The realms won't drift apart." My voice caught on the truth of it. "They'll be sewn shut."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a fox screamed once and fell silent.

"Then we have three turns," Renn said quietly, "to do what the covenant took a hundred years to build."

"No." I shook my head. "We have less. Because the last ember is under the palace, and getting into Vael with the whole Crown sharpening itself against us—that's not a turn's work. That's a miracle's work. So really we have two turns and a prayer, and I stopped believing in prayers around the time I learned I'd signed away my own memory."

Renn reached across the fire and took my hand. His pads were rough; his grip was careful, always careful, as though I were another ember he was afraid of cracking.

"Then we'll spend them well," he said.

* * *

We crossed into the high country two days later, and that was where we found the road full of people.

Not soldiers. Refugees. Wolves and wolf-kin and the human folk who had lived beside them in the marches for generations—driving carts piled with goats and grandmothers and looms, carrying children who stared at the southern sky as though it might fall. They were fleeing the closing border, streaming north and east toward the wild lands ahead of the Crown's iron, and the sight of them stopped me in the middle of the road like a hand on my chest.

An old ewe-herder, gray-muzzled, walking beside a human woman who might have been her sister for all the years written on both faces. A boy with a charm-strung bracelet—my kind of work, clumsy and earnest, the knots done in the Charmwrights' style—clutched in his fist like a talisman. A Warden's cloak, torn of its badge, being used as a blanket for twins asleep in a cart bed.

"They know," I said. "They can feel it, can't they? The wards going up."

"Everyone can." Renn's ears were flat. "The ley-lines sing when they're chained. The wolves hear it in their teeth. Your charm-folk feel it as a headache that won't break." He nodded at the boy's bracelet. "That's why they run. Not because anyone told them. Because the world is telling them, and for once they're listening."

A woman at the roadside—a wolf-kin with frost in her fur and a babe slung across her chest—watched us pass, and then, as I drew level with her, she did something that undid me more thoroughly than any battle could have. She bowed. Just slightly. Just enough. And behind her, one by one, others did the same, a ripple of small bows down the length of the refugee column, silent, unasked-for, aimed at the girl with the iron box and the burning cord across her chest.

"They know what you carry," Renn murmured.

"I haven't done anything yet."

"You're doing it now. You're walking north instead of hiding south." He lifted his chin toward the column. "Hope doesn't need results, Odile. Sometimes it just needs a direction."

I walked on with my throat tight and my eyes hot, and I did not bow back, because I didn't trust my legs to keep moving if I stopped. But I raised my hand as we passed, once, and the ripple of bows became something else—hands raised back, dozens of them, a wave breaking northward against the current of fear.

* * *

That night we climbed above the treeline, because Renn said the high passes were faster and I said nothing, because I had learned that when Renn spoke of routes he was usually right and always unhappy about it. The wind up there had knives in it. We sheltered in the lee of a cairn older than either realm, its stones furred with lichen, and I built the smallest fire of my life and we ate the last of the bread and I took out the map.

It was a poor thing—inked on waxed cloth, copied from a guild chart I barely remembered making, back in a life I was still reassembling piece by piece. I marked what we had. Nine embers, a checkmark apiece, my handwriting steady until the last mark and then not. I marked the glacier vault, two days' hard climb northeast. The salt fen, five days beyond that, across country the Crown had already begun to garrison. And Vael itself, ringed in fresh ink with the words *under the throne*, and around the whole map, drawn in a shaking line, the border—and the wards crawling along it like a scar being stitched.

"Read it to me," Renn said. "The arithmetic."

"Fine. Three turns. Four embers. Two days to the ice, and the ice wants a day of digging if the vault-mouth hasn't shifted. Five days to the fen through garrisoned country, assuming no one stops us, which no one won't. Then back to Vael—six days, through wards designed to kill anything like you on sight. That's fourteen days of travel alone, and we have twenty-one, and the last ember is buried under the most defended throne in the world." I set down the charcoal. "There's no version of this where everything goes right."

"There never was." He said it without drama, the way he said most true things. "Every step since the stars went out, the odds have been impossible. You went into the drowned chapel anyway. You dug in the bone orchard anyway. You let me carry you out of Corvane's tower with a knife-hole in my side and you kept going." He leaned forward, firelight catching the gray of his eyes. "Impossible is just the price. We've been paying it all along."

I wanted to believe him. I looked at the map instead, at the little ringed words *under the throne*, and I thought about what waited inside me when the last embers came home—because I knew now, knew since the twelfth ember showed me the covenant's heart, that the final price of reforging was not blood or years or strength. It was him. Every memory of Renn, poured into the new pact like wine into a cup, gone the moment the seal was struck. I had known for days and I had not said it aloud and he had not asked, and the silence between us on that subject had grown so large we had built our whole camp inside it.

"Renn." My voice came out smaller than I intended. "When this is over—"

"Don't." Gentle. Immediate. "Not tonight."

"You'll remember. You promised. You'll carry it for both of us, and I'll look at you like a stranger, and you'll have to start the story from the beginning like some—some traveling tale-teller, and I'll listen and feel nothing and—"

"Odile." He moved around the fire, slow, giving me room to refuse, and knelt in front of me, and took both my hands in both of his. "You'll feel something. I've decided that. Whatever the pact takes, whatever the magic does—I've decided you'll feel something, and I intend to spend every day after proving it, until the feeling remembers its own name." His thumb traced the ember-scar on my palm, the mark the first shard had burned into me a lifetime ago. "So don't spend tonight grieving a thing that hasn't happened. Spend it here. With me. That's all I ask."

Above us, past the cairn and the wind, the thirteenth moon cleared the ridge—swollen, silver-shot, two-thirds full and climbing. Its light fell across the map, across the stitched scar of the border, across our joined hands, and for a moment it lit everything with the same impartial, patient glow, as if to say: *I am coming. What will you bring me?*

And that was when we heard it.

Faint, at first—so faint I thought it was the wind finding a crack in the cairn. But Renn's head snapped up, ears forward, and I knew the sound for what it was before my mind finished naming it. Horns. Many horns, far to the south and east, braided together into one long rising note.

And underneath the horns, felt through the stone we sat on rather than heard, a hum. Deep. Rhythmic. The sound of crown-stone and iron drinking from the ley-lines, mile upon mile of it, waking along the border like a vein of lightning deciding where to strike.

"They've started early," Renn breathed.

I looked at the map. At the glacier, two days northeast. At the moon, two-thirds gone toward full. At the hands wrapped around mine, which I was not ready to imagine empty.

"Then we walk tonight," I said, and began rolling up the map.
