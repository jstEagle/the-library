## 32. The Ember Beneath the Ice

The glacier had a name once, Odile supposed, but the maps Renn carried called it only *the White Silence*, and after three days of walking toward it she understood why. Sound died here. Their boots on the packed snow made a muffled crunch that seemed to come from somewhere behind them, and their breath left in white plumes that hung in the air like ghosts reluctant to move on.

"It's there," Renn said, and even his voice came out flattened, as if the ice were drinking it. He stood at the lip of a crevasse field where the glacier's surface fractured into blue chasms deep enough to swallow a cathedral. "The vault is under the third ridge. The old wolf-kin used to call it the Cold Hoard. My grandmother said the ember was sealed in the ice before Vael had walls."

"Your grandmother," Odile said, working feeling back into her fingers inside her gloves, "told you a lot of things."

"She told me stories. I didn't know until this year how many of them were true." He crouched at the edge of the first crevasse and studied the bridging snow with the particular attention of someone who had grown up reading dangerous ground. "We cross single file. I go first. You step exactly where I step."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I spend a very long time being angry at you."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was hard to smile when her ribs still ached from the ford, when the memory of the wild pack's alpha looking down at Renn kneeling in the shallows—*submitting*, baring his throat so that strangers could pass—rose unbidden every time she looked at him. She had seen him truly for the first time that day, and what she'd seen was a man who paid for loyalty in humiliation and never once complained about the exchange rate.

"Renn." She waited until he looked back at her. His gray eyes were pale in this light, nearly the color of the ice. "If the twelfth ember taught me anything, it's that these things take something. Every time. So before we go down there—I want to know what you're willing to lose."

He considered the question honestly, which was one of the things she loved about him, though she was careful not to use that word even in the privacy of her own skull.

"My grandmother said the Cold Hoard doesn't kill thieves," he said. "It tests them. It takes whatever they're holding too tightly." A pause. "For me, that would be an easy question to answer. For you—" His jaw tightened. "You're carrying twelve embers' worth of memory now. If it wants a price from you, I don't know what's left to take."

"There's always something left to take," Odile said quietly. "I'm living proof."

* * *

They crossed the crevasse field in the gray hour before dawn, when the bridges of snow were firmest. Odile kept her eyes fixed on Renn's heels and thought of nothing else—not the ledger, not the wall, not the blurred face beside her at nineteen that she could now see with terrible clarity whenever she closed her eyes. Not the way he had said *I remember for both of us* by the fire two nights ago, and the way she had pretended to be asleep.

The third bridge cracked.

It happened without sound—that was the horror of it. One moment the snow held; the next it simply wasn't there, and Renn dropped through a hole no wider than a grave. His hand shot up and caught the lip of the crevasse, and Odile threw herself flat, her stomach slamming against the ice, and grabbed his wrist.

He weighed nothing and everything. The cold burned through her gloves. Below him the crevasse went down into a darkness that had never known light, and she heard—not saw, heard—the soft wet sound of his grip beginning to fail.

"Don't," he said through his teeth. "Don't pull. You'll go over with me."

"Shut up." She hooked her other arm around a jutting spur of blue ice and hauled. Her shoulder screamed. Somewhere below, wind moved through the glacier's throat with a sound like a choir singing one long note.

"I mean it, Odile. Let go."

"You let go of *that* attitude," she gasped, and got her second hand on his forearm, and dug her boots into the snow, and pulled with everything the last twelve years of small magic and small life had built in her—which turned out to be more than she knew. He came up over the lip like a drowned man breaching, and they lay together on the solid ice, both shaking, his forehead pressed against her collarbone.

"The rope," she managed. "We should have roped ourselves together."

"We should have done a hundred things." He lifted his head. There was blood at his hairline where he'd struck the ice on the way down, already freezing. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. We haven't found the thing that tests us."

* * *

The entrance to the Cold Hoard was a door, which neither of them expected. After the drowned chapel and the bone orchard and the glacier's general indifference to human architecture, a door seemed almost polite—a slab of black stone set into the ice, carved with interlocking circles, warm to the touch despite everything.

"Wolf-kin make doors," Renn said, running his palm along the carvings. "Even for vaults. A threshold means consent. You can't stumble into a place that has a door. You have to choose it."

"Is that a warning or an invitation?"

"Yes."

Beyond the door, a stair descended into the glacier's heart, and the air changed. Warmer—the ember's doing, Odile guessed—but wrong somehow, too still, as if the ice above them were a held breath. They went down with the last of their lamp oil burning in the lantern between them, and the light caught the walls and scattered into a thousand blue sparks, and Odile felt the shard-cord in her chest begin to hum. Twelve embers' worth of memory stirred like sediment in still water.

At the bottom of the stair was a chamber the size of a shrine, and in its center, suspended in a pillar of ancient clear ice, burned the tenth ember.

It was small. They always were. A knot of red-gold light no bigger than a coal from a hearth, and yet the whole chamber leaned toward it—the ice, the dark, Odile's own heartbeat. She stepped forward and the humming became words, or almost words, the covenant-stone trying to speak through her bones: *last signatory, last signatory, come and finish.*

"Wait." Renn caught her sleeve. "Look at the floor."

She looked. The chamber's floor was not stone. It was glass—ember-glass, the same material as Halvard's pendant, laid in a mosaic of interlocking circles that matched the door. And set into the glass at intervals were objects: a rusted sword. A child's shoe. A wooden comb with gray hairs still caught in it. A wedding ring.

"Things people held too tightly," Odile whispered. "The test isn't ahead of us. It's been collecting."

"Then put down your pack," Renn said. "Slowly. Everything you're carrying—if it matters to you, it's a claim ticket."

She knelt and unbuckled the pack with numb fingers. Inside: dried meat for six days, down to four after the crevasse took their spare rope and half the oil. The guild ledger wrapped in oilcloth. Her mother's charm-knife. The thirteenth-moon almanac the astronomer exile had given them. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, the nine embers they had recovered, each one a piece of a woman she was only now becoming.

She laid out the food. The knife. The ledger. Her hands hovered over the embers.

"No," she said aloud. "These aren't mine to hold too tightly. They belong to the covenant. To everyone." She left them in the pack, and the glass beneath did not stir.

Renn emptied his own pack more sparsely: a whetstone worn to a crescent, a bone flute he had never once played in her hearing, a lock of fur tied with cord that he touched once, briefly, before setting it down. She didn't ask. Some things you carried so you wouldn't have to speak them.

Then they approached the pillar together, and the ice began to sing.

* * *

It was not a sound. It was cold in the shape of a sound, rising through the floor, through their boots, into their teeth. The pillar of ice flickered like heat-shimmer in reverse, and Odile felt the chamber take their measure the way a moneylender takes the measure of a debtor—weighing, valuing, deciding what could be spared.

*What do you hold too tightly?*

The question arrived whole inside her skull, genderless and vast.

Her memories. That was the obvious answer, the terrifying one. But she had already given those once, hadn't she? She had stood on a wall and traded the man she loved for the world's survival, and she was prepared to do it again in three moons' time. What else—

*Renn.*

The answer came before she could stop it, and the chamber seized it like a hawk taking a rabbit.

The temperature dropped so fast her breath froze mid-air. Frost raced across the glass floor toward the packs, toward the embers, and Odile shouted and lunged, but the frost passed over everything untouched and went instead for the lantern. The flame guttered. Died. Darkness total, and in the darkness the sound of ice moving, groaning, the whole glacier flexing around them like a fist closing.

"Lantern!" she cried. "Renn, the lantern—"

"I have it." His voice came from her left, steady, and then a spark flared—flint and steel against the charm-knife's edge—and the wick caught, and in the sudden light she saw the ceiling coming down.

Not all of it. A seam had opened directly above the pillar, a crack racing across the vaulted ice, and a wedge the size of a wagon was detaching itself with the slow, inexorable grace of a falling mountain. Directly above Renn.

Time did something strange. Later she would remember it in pieces: herself diving. Her shoulder hitting him at the waist. The two of them sliding across the glass as the wedge came down where he had stood and shattered into a thousand blades of ice, one of which opened her cheek from cheekbone to jaw, and another of which buried itself in the pack—the pack with the embers—and she heard, distinctly, horribly, the sound of glass breaking.

"No—" She crawled to it, her vision swimming with blood. The pack's side was torn open. Three of the recovered embers rolled free across the glass floor, intact, glowing softly. But the fourth—the fourth, the one from the bone orchard, the one that had shown her her own funeral—was cracked down the middle, its light leaking out like yolk from an egg, dimming.

And the chamber was drinking the leak.

She could see it happening: the spilled light running in thin rivulets along the mosaic channels, feeding the interlocking circles, and the circles brightening, and the vast cold presence in the room growing *satisfied*. Fed. The test had asked what she held too tightly, and she had answered *him*, and so it had taken—not Renn, not yet, but the proof of what she'd survived to recover. The payment was due and it had collected.

"Can it be mended?" Renn was beside her, his hands hovering over the cracked ember, afraid to touch it.

"I don't know. I don't—" Her voice broke. Twelve embers' worth of memory, and none of it told her how to mend a broken ember. "The guildmaster would have known. The guild would have—"

The guild was ash. The guildhall was a ruin full of rats and one ledger. She was the last signatory, kneeling in the dark at the bottom of a glacier, bleeding from the face, watching a piece of the covenant die in front of her.

Then Renn picked it up.

"Renn, don't—"

He cupped the broken halves in both palms, and the leaking light poured over his hands, and Odile saw the moment it began to burn him because his face went white and his breath came in through his nose, slow and controlled, the way he breathed when he submitted, when he knelt in rivers, when he paid. He didn't cry out. He held the broken thing against his chest the way you'd hold a wounded animal, and he began, very quietly, to speak in the old tongue—the wolf-kin tongue, the language of the wild side of the border—and Odile did not know the words but she knew the shape of them, because she had heard them once before, twelve years ago, spoken against her hair on a wall at night. A promise. The same promise.

*I remember for both of us.*

The light stopped leaking. It gathered. It sank back into the crack, and the crack narrowed, and the ember sealed itself—not perfectly; a scar of cloudy ice ran through its heart where the break had been—but sealed, whole enough, alive, and Renn slumped forward onto his hands, his palms blistered red and silver, breathing like a man who had surfaced from deep water.

The chamber exhaled. The frost retreated. In the silence, the pillar of ancient ice split cleanly down the middle and released the tenth ember, which drifted down through the air and came to rest in the channel of the glass floor directly in front of Odile, waiting, patient, as if it had merely been checking whether she was willing to pay.

She took it. Of course she took it. She pressed it to her sternum and it sank home with all the others, and the memory came—

—not the wall this time. A kitchen. A small kitchen smelling of bread and woodsmoke, and she was young and laughing at something, and Renn—young Renn, unscarred Renn—was sitting on the table because chairs were for guests, and he was saying, "*When this is over, we'll get a shop. With a green door. You'll sell charms and I'll carry the heavy things and complain about it,*" and she had said, "*You'll love complaining about it,*" and he had said, "*Every day,*" and the memory ended on his laugh—

—and she was back in the dark, on her knees, with blood drying on her cheek and tears freezing in her eyelashes, looking at the man who had just burned his hands to mend her past.

"A green door," she said stupidly.

His head came up. For a moment his composure cracked clean through, the same way the ember had, and she saw everything underneath: twelve years of searching, twelve years of remembering alone, the flute he never played, the lock of fur in his pack.

"You remembered," he said hoarsely.

"A kitchen. Bread. You sitting on the table." She laughed, and it came out wet and broken. "You sat on the table even then."

"Chairs are for guests." He tried to smile and couldn't quite finish it. "You were going to name the shop after your mother."

"*Winterbourne's.*" The name arrived with the taste of bread still in it. "With a green door."

"With a green door."

They stayed like that for a long moment, in the ruined shrine at the bottom of the world, two people holding the same memory from opposite ends like a rope over a crevasse. Then Odile wiped her face, winced at the cut, and started gathering the packs.

"We've lost the rope, half the oil, and four days of food," she said, because someone had to say it, because if she didn't start counting practical things she was going to sit down in this beautiful terrible place and never stand up again. "And we have eight embers. Two more to find, three moons, and a walk back across the White Silence with no rope."

"One problem at a time." Renn got to his feet, swaying slightly, and she took his arm without asking and he let her, which was its own kind of submission, and perhaps the truest kind. "First problem: getting out. The stair may not have survived the fall."

It had, barely—choked with rubble but passable—and they climbed back up into the gray daylight with the glacier groaning behind them like something disappointed but not finished. On the surface, the wind had risen. Snow came sideways now, fine and needling, and the crevasse field they had crossed at dawn was a different country entirely, the bridges gone soft, the landmarks erased.

"We can't cross that in this," Renn shouted over the wind. "We need shelter until it blows out. There were caves in the ridge to the east—I marked them on the way in."

"How far?"

"Half a day. With what we're carrying, maybe more."

So they walked. That was the whole of it, Odile thought as the hours ground past and the light failed and the wind never once paused for breath—they walked, and endured, and endured, and endured. When Renn stumbled she steadied him. When she stopped being able to feel her feet he took the heavier pack without discussion. They did not talk. Talking cost warmth. But twice—once at a rest stop huddled in the lee of an ice block, once in the last violet moments before true dark—she caught him looking at her with an expression she now had the memories to read, and she looked back, and neither of them said anything, and it was the loudest conversation of her life.

They reached the caves as the last light died. A shallow mouth in the rock, dry-floored, out of the wind. Renn got a fire going with the last of the flint and the splintered remains of the broken rope, and they ate half rations of the remaining meat and drank snow melted in the dented tin cup, passing it back and forth, and Odile bound his burned hands with strips of her own shirt because he had no bandages left and refused to say so.

"Two embers left," she said, when the fire had settled. "The eleventh and the twelfth. Do we know where?"

"The eleventh—" He flexed his bandaged fingers carefully. "The astronomer's charts put it south. In the hills above Hollow Vale, maybe. Or beyond them."

"Hollow Vale. The healer's country."

"Yes."

"And the twelfth?"

Renn was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved in his eyes.

"The twelfth is the lie," he said finally. "You told me that yourself, after it showed you the price. It's not in a place, Odile. It's in the pact itself. The last ember is the one we'll forge—or fail to forge—at the wall."

"Then really there's only one left to find."

"Really there's only one left to find."

She nodded slowly and looked into the fire, and the green door shimmered there, and the kitchen, and the laugh. One ember. Three moons. And somewhere behind them, days south, a Warden who had chosen to become a seeker, carrying sealed archives and a conscience that had cost him everything.

The wind screamed past the cave mouth all night, and Odile slept in fits, and near dawn she dreamed of a pass in the southern hills choked with soldiers, and a lone figure standing in the middle of it with his hand raised, saying *not this road, not today* —and woke with her heart hammering, unable to shake the certainty that somewhere, right now, Halvard was buying them time with the last coin he had.

"Renn," she said to the gray morning. "We need to move. South. Today."

He was already rolling the blankets.
