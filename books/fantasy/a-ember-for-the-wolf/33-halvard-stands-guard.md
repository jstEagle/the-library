## 33. Halvard Stands Guard

The pass had no name on any map Halvard trusted.

The Crown called it the Greytooth Approach, one of three routes through the shoulder of the northern mountains where the old border wall crumbled into scree and frost-heaved stone. The soldiers called it the Throat. The locals, before the locals stopped existing, had called it something else—Halvard had found the word scratched into a waystone at the pass's mouth, half worn away: *Vael-enn-dur*. The Breath Between. He did not know what it meant. He suspected he was about to find out what it cost.

He had ridden ahead of his own column through the night, taking the switchbacks two at a time until his horse foundered and he finished on foot, and now he stood alone in the narrowest part of the defile with the wind pulling at his cloak and the first gray light leaking over the eastern ridge like water through a cracked dam. Below him, maybe an hour back along the trail, four hundred soldiers of the Ninth Warden Company were making cold camp. Above him, perhaps six hours ahead if they pushed hard, a charmwright and a wolf-kin were climbing toward the glacier vault with twelve embers' worth of hope in their pockets.

Four hundred to two. And Halvard intended to make it four hundred to none—for as long as he could stand.

*You could still turn around,* said the voice in his head that sounded like his father, dead twenty years and still lecturing. *You could ride down there, report that you lost the trail in the storm, take your reprimand like a man. They might even believe you.*

They would not believe him. He knew that too. Captain Sera Voss ran the Ninth's intelligence desk, and Voss missed nothing—she had already marked the irregularity in his requisitions, the sealed archive access he'd requested under a forged writ, the three days unaccounted for between Hollow Vale and the ford. When this was done, whatever this was, there would be a tribunal. There might be a cell. There was, if the Queen's new edicts held, a gallows with his name already chalked above it.

He turned his collar up against the wind and began, methodically, to prepare the ground for his own ruin.

* * *

He worked the way he'd been taught thirty years ago as a raw recruit on the southern garrisons: not with genius but with patience, reading the land like a ledger and finding every line where it could be made to lie.

The Throat narrowed to forty paces wide at its center, walled by cliffs on the north side and a steep talus slope on the south, all of it littered with boulders the size of cottages where some ancient collapse had come down the mountain and never been cleared. A company could move through it in good order. It could not move through it quickly, and it could not move through it at all if someone made the center impassable.

Halvard walked the length of it twice. Then he found the keystone.

It was a shelf of rock the size of a granary, wedged into the cliff face sixty feet above the trail, held there by nothing but friction and the frozen meltwater that seeped behind it every winter and refroze every night. Any engineer worth his salt would have flagged it as a hazard. Any engineer worth his salt would also have known that you didn't bring it down with four hundred men watching—you brought it down with fire, quietly, hours ahead, and let the mountain do the arithmetic.

He built the fire small, in the crevice behind the shelf, feeding it slow-burning peat cut from the marsh edge two days south, packed tight so it would smolder rather than blaze. The heat would work at the ice seam through the morning. By midday, if the mountain kept its promises, the shelf would come down and close the pass behind him—and behind Odile Winterbourne, who had passed this way in the dark with her wolf-kin walking point, their tracks crisp in last night's snowfall.

He crouched by the little fire longer than he needed to, hands out, watching the peat glow.

*Arson against the Crown's own route of advance.* He almost laughed. His father's voice had gone quiet, which meant even the dead had no argument left. In its place rose another voice, older and stranger—the archivist's voice from beneath the palace, the thin clerk named Pell who had shown him the founding charters with hands that shook. *Read it again, Warden. Read what they took. Read what they signed their names over.*

He had read it. He had read it eleven times since, by candlelight, in safehouses and stable lofts, until he could recite the theft from memory: the covenant terms copied near-verbatim into the Charter of Vael, the wolf-kin clauses struck out, the signature lines reassigned, the original pact declared void by men who had never held the right to declare anything. The Crown had not inherited the border. The Crown had stolen it, then spent three hundred years erasing everyone who remembered the theft.

And now the Crown meant to seal the border permanently before the thirteenth moon turned—not because the realms drifting apart was a threat, but because reconciliation was. Because if the two peoples ever saw each other clearly, every throne in Vael would be standing on stolen ground in broad daylight.

Halvard fed the fire its last handful of peat and stood.

* * *

They came up the trail at the ninth hour, and they came angry.

He heard them before he saw them—the crunch of boots on frozen scree, the metallic complaint of mail, and underneath it the particular silence of officers giving orders they expected to be questioned. He had positioned himself at the pass's mouth, seated on a boulder with his sword across his knees and his Warden's badge pinned where the light would catch it, and when the vanguard rounded the bend he watched recognition move through them like a shiver.

Captain Voss reached him first. She was younger than he was by fifteen years, sharp-faced, competent, and loyal in the way that Halvard had once been loyal—which is to say completely, and without having ever asked herself why.

"Halvard." Her eyes went past him, up the empty trail, then back. "Where's your horse?"

"Foundered below the treeline."

"Alone? You requested no escort."

"I requested no escort," he agreed.

"Then you'd best tell me why First Warden Halvard Ashgrave is sitting in a mountain pass blocking the advance of his own company." She said it evenly, but her hand had drifted toward her hilt, and behind her the vanguard had spread into a loose line without being ordered to—a soldier's instinct, reading the shape of a thing before understanding it.

"The advance is cancelled," Halvard said. "By me."

A murmur ran down the line. Voss didn't turn to quiet it. "On whose authority?"

"Mine." He rose off the boulder, slowly, letting them see how he moved—unhurried, a man with nowhere to be. "I'm invoking Article Nine. Right of interdiction. A senior Warden may hold any ground against any force, including Crown forces, upon declaration of evidence of unlawful command."

"That article hasn't been invoked in a hundred years."

"It's still in the book. I checked. Page four hundred and twelve." He had checked. He had memorized the page number the way other men memorized prayers. "I am declaring evidence of unlawful command, Captain. The order to seal the border is itself illegal—it contradicts the founding charter, which the Crown has falsified. I can show you the documents. Originals. Sealed signatures. I've read them, and once you've read them, you'll either arrest me or you'll understand, and I genuinely don't know which."

"You're mad," someone said from the line. Not Voss. A lieutenant he half recognized, a big farm-country boy named Dree.

"Probably," Halvard said. "But madness isn't a crime. What I'm doing is legal. Look it up while we wait."

"We're not waiting," Voss said. "We have orders to reach the glacier vault before moonset. Two fugitives are—" She stopped. Something crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice. "You've read the archives. That's where the archive access went. Three weeks ago. Your forged writ."

"Yes."

"They'll hang you."

"Likely."

"And you're going to sit here anyway."

Halvard looked at her—at the young, certain face of the man he had been before Pell and the charters and the drowned chapel and the bone orchard, before he had learned that the ground he'd stood his whole life on was a floor laid over a grave. "Captain," he said, "I stood guard over the Crown's lies for thirty years. I'm standing guard over the truth for one morning. It's the better posting."

* * *

They gave him an hour. To their credit—or their hesitation—they gave him a full hour, during which Voss sent runners forward and back, argued doctrine with her lieutenants in low voices, and twice caught herself looking up the empty trail as though she might see the fugitives there, or see something else, something in Halvard's face that answered a question she hadn't asked yet.

He used the hour to talk. Not to argue—he'd learned in tribunals that argument hardened men—but simply to speak, loudly enough for the front ranks to hear, about things they all half-knew and had never said aloud.

About the village of Marrow's Bend, wiped from the maps in his grandfather's time, which older Wardens still spoke of in the tone reserved for ghost stories. About the edict of the ninth year of the old King's reign, authorizing the "administrative dissolution" of magic-touched settlements, a phrase Halvard repeated until it curdled in the cold air. About the charter, and the struck-out clauses, and the queen whose name appeared on the original pact beside a wolf-kin's mark, and how that page had been razored out of the palace copy but survived in the guild archive entire.

"You've seen these documents?" Voss demanded at last, from ten feet away, arms folded.

"I've read them. Copies exist beyond my reach now—in more than one place. Killing me doesn't kill them anymore. That was the point."

"You expect us to commit treason on your say-so."

"I expect you to do what you've always done, Captain. Follow the law. I'm just asking you to read all of it first." He paused. "You were at Hollow Vale."

Her jaw tightened. Everyone had been at Hollow Vale; it was the kind of operation that got mentioned in dispatches and never in conversation. "What of it?"

"The hedge-healer there. Old woman, bad hip, kept a garden of outlawed simples. Did she raise a hand against anyone?"

"No."

"Did anyone die because of her craft, in all the years she practiced?"

"Not that was proven."

"And we burned her stock and put her under bond anyway, and you signed the inventory, and I countersigned it, and we both went home and slept." He let that sit. "I slept fine for years, Captain. I want you to know that. I want you to know it's possible, because it means you won't believe me when I tell you I don't sleep now. Men don't change because of arguments. We change because of ledgers. Go read the ledger."

Behind the shelf of rock, deep in its crevice, the peat fire breathed its slow heat into the ice seam. Halvard felt it working the way you feel a tooth loosening—nothing you could see, everything you could sense.

* * *

At the eleventh hour, Voss made her decision, and it was neither the one he'd hoped for nor the one he'd feared.

She advanced.

Not the full company—she sent a file of twelve, swords drawn, to take him into custody, and she came with them, which told Halvard she wanted to look him in the eye when it happened. He stood from the boulder and drew his sword, and the sound of it coming free was very loud in the narrow throat of the pass.

"Don't," he said. Not to the twelve. To Voss. "Captain. One step further and I invoke the full article, and then every man who crosses this line is party to enforcing an order I have formally declared unlawful—and when the tribunals come, and they will come, because documents survive and I am not the only man alive who's read them—every one of those twelve carries a name on a list. Is that what you want to give them? Their names on a list?"

The twelve stopped. Soldiers were simple creatures in one respect only: they understood liability faster than they understood justice.

"Stand down, Halvard," Voss said quietly. "Please. Whatever you think you've found—"

"I found the truth, and it has a signature on it."

"Then you can present it to the Queen's—"

"To the Queen's justices? The ones who burned the guildhall? The ones who razored a page out of the founding charter with the royal seal's own knife?" He shook his head. "There is no lawful court left to hear it, Captain. That's the whole discovery. That's what took me three weeks to understand. There is no door left except the one those two fugitives are trying to open, and I am going to hold it shut behind them until they're through."

Voss looked at him for a long moment. Wind moved between them, carrying grit and the smell of stone.

"You love her," she said suddenly. "The charmwright. That's what this is."

And Halvard laughed—really laughed, for the first time in weeks, a short harsh bark that startled the front rank. "No. By every right I should despise her. She's a liar and a lawbreaker and she's dragged the realm to the edge of something no one can predict." He leveled his sword—not at Voss, but at the trail behind her, the long climb down toward the world they'd all been born into. "But I've read what the border actually is, Captain. It's not a wall. It's a scar. And scars are how a body remembers it was cut. Seal it forever and we forget we were ever wounded—and a body that forgets its wounds keeps reopening them. Those two aren't breaking the realm open. They're trying to close it properly, for the first time in three hundred years. I'd have thought a soldier would recognize the difference between a wound and a war."

Silence, except the wind.

Then, far up the pass, the mountain spoke.

It began as a groan, deep in the cliff, a sound like a giant clearing his throat, and every head turned upward. The shelf of rock shifted—a hand's width, then another—shedding frost in white veils. The ice seam had given. The peat had done its patient work.

"Move!" Voss roared, and the twelve broke backward, and she grabbed Halvard's arm and hauled him with them, and for one absurd moment he was being rescued by the woman sent to arrest him as sixty tons of granite came down the cliff face in a slow thunderous avalanche and slammed across the trail, raising a cloud of dust and snow that swallowed the pass entire.

When it settled, the Throat was closed. A wall of fresh rubble forty feet high lay where the trail had been—behind them. Ahead of it, up the high switchbacks, the way to the glacier stood open and empty and utterly unguarded.

Voss pulled him around by the collar, dust in her hair, fury and something else fighting in her face. "You brought the mountain down. On your own position. With us on the wrong side of it."

"On the right side of it," Halvard said. "The fugitives passed in the night. That rubble buys them a day. Maybe two. After that, your engineers will clear a path, and you'll follow, and I'll be under arrest, and I'll tell the tribunal everything, and so will you—because you'll have read the ledger by then. Won't you, Captain?"

She stared at him. Behind her, Lieutenant Dree was looking up at the rockfall with an expression Halvard recognized from his own mirror, months ago: the look of a man feeling the floor shift.

"You're under arrest," Voss said at last, mechanically. "Halvard Ashgrave, you're under arrest for obstruction, for destruction of Crown property, for—" She faltered. "For treason."

"Write it down carefully," he said, offering his wrists. "Future generations will read it."

* * *

They bound his hands in front, at least, and let him walk. The column wound back down the trail in grim silence, and Halvard counted the switchbacks as they descended, and at each turn he looked up once at the clean line of the pass, the open road to the ice, and permitted himself exactly one breath of something that was not quite hope.

It was enough. It would have to be enough. The rest belonged to a charmwright with stolen memories and a wolf who remembered everything, climbing toward the last embers with the thirteenth moon bleeding down to its final nights.

At the bottom of the mountain, as they loaded him into a prisoner's cart, Voss climbed up beside him—alone, unasked. She sat with her sword across her knees and did not look at him, and after a mile she said, without preamble:

"Marrow's Bend. The village off the maps. My grandmother was born there."

Halvard turned his head. "I know," he said. "It's in the dissolution rolls. Family name Voss, listed among the relocated. Relocated to a slum, Captain. The rolls use the word 'resettled.'"

The cart jolted on. Voss stared straight ahead at the road south, toward the city, toward the tribunals, and her knuckles were white on the sword hilt, and she said nothing at all.

But she did not report the second copy of the charter hidden in Halvard's boot, and that night, in the cells beneath the Ninth's field headquarters, a runner arrived breathless from the north with a report that made the duty officer go pale: the glacier vault had been opened. The thirteenth ember was taken.

And somewhere above the snowline, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the border wards had begun—very softly—to sing.
