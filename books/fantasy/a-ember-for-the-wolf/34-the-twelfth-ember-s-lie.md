## 34. The Twelfth Ember's Lie

The twelfth ember was hidden in the one place no thief would ever think to look: inside the bell of the chapel at Hollow Vale, cast into the bronze when the chapel was raised, ringing above every wedding and funeral in the valley for two hundred years while carrying its secret in its throat.

It took us a day and most of our remaining rope to lower Renn up into the belfry and then down again with the ember cradled against his chest like an egg of live coals. By the time we made camp that evening in the lee of a fallen watchtower, my hands were still shaking—not from the climb, but from what I already suspected was coming. Eleven embers had taught me the shape of these things. Each one gave back a piece of me, and each piece cost something to hold.

I should have known the twelfth would not be kind.

* * *

We built the fire small, screened by the tower's broken wall. Renn gutted two marsh hens he'd caught at dusk and set them spitting on green sticks, and for a little while there was nothing in the world but the ordinary work of staying alive—fat hissing into flame, the smell of it, the wind combing through the dead bracken beyond our shelter. He moved easily now despite the scar across his ribs, the wound from the glacier vault long closed, though I sometimes caught him pressing a hand to it in his sleep as if checking that it hadn't reopened somewhere deeper than flesh.

"Here." He handed me a bird without looking up. "Eat before you do it. Last time you took an ember on an empty stomach and turned the color of tallow."

"I did not turn the color of tallow."

"You turned the color of old candle wax. I've thought about it since. Tallow is generous."

Despite everything—the clock running down, the Crown's armies massing along the border, the three turns left on the thirteenth moon—I laughed. It came out of me sideways, startled, and Renn looked up with that expression he wore whenever he'd managed to surprise himself into making me laugh: a flicker of triumph quickly buried under wolfish indifference.

The embers sat in their iron case between us, eleven dull-red seeds in a bed of ash. Twelve had been recovered; the ninth, returned by a dying man's hand, and the rest won from drowned chapels and bone orchards and ice. One remained unaccounted for—the first ember, the keystone, which the ledger swore had never left the border wall itself. Which meant we would have to go back to where everything began.

But that was tomorrow's road.

Tonight there was only this one, the twelfth, warm through the cloth even before I touched it.

Renn went quiet in the way he always did when I prepared to take an ember. He didn't leave—he had stopped leaving after the bone orchard, after he'd watched me wake screaming from my own funeral procession and decided, apparently, that whatever came out of me next, he would rather be there for it than imagine it alone. But he withdrew to the far side of the fire and became very still, the way wolves go still, watching.

I unwrapped the ember.

It was smaller than the others, dense as a swallowed star, and the moment my bare skin met it the world folded shut like a book.

* * *

I stood in a hall I had never seen and knew perfectly.

It was built of black stone veined with silver—the covenant-stone, the same stuff as the shard that had burned my name into my own palm a lifetime of months ago—and it was not a hall so much as a throat, a hollowed place beneath the border wall where the original signatories had once gathered. Thirteen seats ringed the chamber. Twelve were empty, dust-sheeted, their occupants long dead or scattered. In the thirteenth seat sat a woman made of firelight and shadow, and though her face would not resolve, I knew her the way I knew my own heartbeat: she was the covenant itself, or the voice of it, or the last fragment of whoever had first spoken its terms into the world.

*I know why you've come,* she said, and her voice was every voice—mine, Renn's, the queen's, the guildmaster's, all braided together. *You've gathered the twelve. You mean to reforge.*

"Yes," I said, and heard how steady my voice was, and hated it a little. Even in memory I lied well. "Tell me the terms."

*Ah.* Something moved in the shadow of her face that might have been pity. *There it is. The question everyone asks too late.*

"The ledger lists them," I said. "Thirteen embers, thirteen oaths, blood on stone. I've read it until I can recite it."

*The ledger tells you what must be done,* the voice said. *It does not tell you what it costs. Your guild kept careful books, Charmwright. They wrote down every term except the one that mattered, because the one that mattered was never written. It was understood. And understanding dies with the people who held it.*

Cold walked up my spine. "Then tell me."

*The covenant is a bridge,* she said. *And bridges are built of weight borne willingly. When your guild forged the first pact, they paid in the only coin both realms hold sacred—memory. Every signatory gave up something true. The queen forgot her lover's face. The guildmaster forgot his oath. The wolves forgot the names of the men they'd spared. That is why the realms drifted, child. Not because the magic failed. Because the payment ran out, and no one remembered what they had bought with it.*

The fire-hall seemed to tilt around me. "The reforging—"

*Requires a signer who remembers everything,* she said gently. *That is why the embers return your memories to you. All of them. So that when you stand at the wall's heart and write the new pact in your blood, you will know exactly what you are giving away. A sacrifice made blind is worth nothing. The covenant does not accept stolen goods.*

"No." My voice cracked on it. "No, that's—that's not—"

*You will give up your memories of him,* the voice said, merciless as arithmetic. *Not all of them. Not your mind, not your self. Only him. Every hour of him. His name, his face, his voice, the sound of his laughter, the weight of his hand, every word he has ever said to you, every word you have ever said to him. All of it, poured into the new pact, so that the two realms may see each other clearly at last. Clarity is bought with clarity, Odile Winterbourne. You cannot make others see while you yourself are clouded by—*

"Stop."

*—by love.*

The word landed in me like a blade finding the gap in armor.

*I tell you this now,* the voice continued, softer, *so that you may choose with open eyes, as the first queen chose, as the first signer chose. There is no other way. There has never been another way. The thirteenth moon will turn whether you reforge or not, and if you do not, the realms drift apart forever, and everything you have gathered, everyone who has bled for this, becomes a story told about fools. If you do reforge—*

She paused, and in the pause I heard, faintly, impossibly, the sound of a man laughing somewhere behind her shadow, and I knew whose laugh it was, and the knowing was unbearable.

*—then you will save two worlds and lose one man. And the worst of it, child, the cruel mercy of it, is that you will not grieve him. You will not even know he is gone. You will look at him with kind eyes and feel nothing but a strangeness, a dream-echo, an ache with no name. He will remember. That is the shape of the bargain. One of you carries the whole weight of what you were, forever, alone.*

"Why?" I whispered. "Why him? Why not anything else? Why not—"

*Because the pact binds realms, and realms are only people multiplied. What joins Vael to the wilds is the same thing that joined you to him: the willingness to be bound. To prove you understand binding—its cost, its weight—you must cut away the bond you treasure most. The covenant has always demanded this. It demanded it of the queen. It will demand it of you.*

The hall began to dissolve, the thirteen seats sinking into silver-dark water, the woman of firelight thinning to a thread of gold.

*One more thing,* she said, almost gone. *He knows. He has known since the glacier, perhaps since the beginning. Ask him why he stayed.*

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees in the cold grass, the ember lying spent and gray in my open palm like a dead coal, like an eye that had finished seeing.

The hens had burned on their sticks. Renn was crouched an arm's length away, close enough to catch me and disciplined enough not to touch me yet, his gray eyes searching my face with an expression I had seen on him only twice before—once at the ford, once at the wall—and both times it had been some species of grief wearing patience as a mask.

"Odile."

"It demands him." My voice came out scraped raw, barely mine. "The reforging. It wants—it takes—" I couldn't get the sentence assembled. It kept collapsing under its own weight. "It takes my memories of you. All of them. That's the price. That's been the price all along, and the embers—they weren't healing me, they were stocking the shelf. Making sure I'd have everything to give. Everything to lose."

Renn didn't move. Didn't blink. The fire popped, and somewhere out in the dark a nightjar called its one dry note, over and over, like a stitch being pulled through cloth.

"How much do you remember," I said slowly, "right now?"

"Everything." Flat. Steady. "From the first day. You were nineteen. You were arguing with a guild clerk about the price of luck-charms and you were winning, and I thought, *there*, that's the one the shard will sing for."

"The voice said you've known. Since the glacier. Maybe longer." I climbed to my feet because sitting felt like drowning. "Ask him why he stayed, it said. So I'm asking. Why? Why would you stay and let me walk toward this? Why would you help me gather the very thing that's going to erase you?"

Something moved behind his eyes—hurt, old and deep, and underneath it a stubbornness like bedrock.

"Because the alternative is losing everything else instead." He rose too, and we faced each other across the dead fire with the spent ember between us. "Two worlds, Odile. The wards fall, the stars stay dark, the wilds starve, Vael chokes on its own fear, and the Crown seals the border with soldiers and stone until there's nothing left on either side but ruins. Against that—" his jaw worked— "against that, one man's heartbreak is a rounding error."

"Don't you dare make it small."

"I'm not making it small. I'm making it survivable." He stepped closer, and the firelight caught the scar on his cheekbone, the gray threading his temples that hadn't been there a year ago. "I made a promise on the ice, under the glacier, when I thought I was dying. I promised that if I lived, I would remember enough for both of us. That's the whole plan, Odile. That's been the whole plan since before you knew there was a plan. You reforge the covenant. You forget me. And I keep you."

"*Keep* me." The word came out of me like something coughed up. "Like a—a charm in a drawer. Like one of my own trinkets. You'll carry me around and I won't even—"

"You'll be free," he said quietly. "That's what you don't understand yet, because you haven't felt it. Forgetting isn't death. I watched my mother forget the names of the men who killed my father, and do you know what she said afterward? She said the world felt lighter. She could see the sky again. Grief is a room, Odile, and you've been locked in yours for twelve years without knowing the door existed. The covenant is going to open it. You'll walk out into a life where you're not haunted by a man you can't picture, and you'll be *glad*, and that's the part that will kill me—not the forgetting. The gladness."

I hit him.

Not hard—my fist glanced off his shoulder and he didn't even rock back, just absorbed it, standing there, letting me spend the blow the way you let a storm spend itself against a wall. Then my fist wasn't a fist anymore, it was my hand gripping the front of his shirt, and then I wasn't gripping, I was holding on, and he wrapped his arms around me and let me shake against his chest while the nightjar called and called in the dark.

"I won't do it," I said into his collarbone. "There has to be another way. Blood, years, name, I'll give anything else—"

"There isn't. You know there isn't. You felt it in the ember—the truth of it, the way it doesn't bend."

"I could refuse. Let the moons turn. Let the realms drift—"

"And spend the rest of your life selling charms in a city going slowly dark, knowing you were the one who could have saved it and chose a man instead?" He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "You wouldn't survive that either. I know you. I've known you for twelve years and five months, and you would rather break than live small, and that's why I loved you first, and that's why I love you now."

*Twelve years and five months.* He counted. He had counted every day.

I pulled back far enough to look at him, and the terrible thing, the thing I would carry into the wall's heart like a stone in my chest, was that I could already see the ending in his face. He had rehearsed this. Somewhere in twelve years of searching, he had imagined this exact conversation, and he had decided his answer in advance, and his answer was yes. Yes to being forgotten. Yes to being the only one who remembered. Yes to standing on the far side of the reforging, watching me look at him with kind, total strangeness, and smiling anyway.

"You'll tell me," I said. My voice was wrecked. I didn't care. "After. When I don't know you. You'll come to the shop, or the wall, or wherever I end up, and you'll tell me the story. All of it. From the beginning."

"Every time it takes," he said. "As many times as it takes."

"That's not a life, Renn. That's a punishment."

"It's a courtship," he said, and smiled, and the smile broke my heart in a way I understood, finally, completely, was about to be asked to forget how to understand. "The slowest one ever attempted. I'm patient. I'm a wolf. We know how to wait."

* * *

He slept eventually. I didn't.

I sat with my back against the fallen watchtower and watched the sky where the stars should have been—still empty, still wrong, a black cloth pulled over the world—and I turned the ember's truth over and over like a charm I was trying to price.

One turn left. One ember remaining, sleeping in the stones of the border wall where I had sworn my first oath at nineteen beside a man whose face the memories had blurred until tonight. Tomorrow we walked north, back to the beginning, to take the keystone and finish the count.

And then, at the wall's heart, with the thirteenth moon waning overhead and two realms watching, I would write the new pact in my own blood—and pay for it with him.

I looked at Renn sleeping by the banked fire, one arm flung over his eyes, his breathing slow and even, his scarred chest rising and falling. Twelve years and five months of him, and I was going to pour every hour of it into the gap between worlds like mortar into a wall.

Unless.

The thought arrived quietly, the way dangerous thoughts do—dressed in reason, wearing the face of hope. *The covenant demands a memory-sacrifice from its reforged signer. Its signer. But the ledger named me final signatory twelve years ago—and ledgers can be amended. Names can be added. The guildmaster sold his signature once; signatures can change hands.*

There might be another way to bear the weight. There might be someone willing to share it.

Or there might be nothing but a fool's errand between me and the end of everything, and one turn left to find out.

I watched the empty sky, and held his name in my mouth like a coal I refused to swallow, and waited for dawn.
