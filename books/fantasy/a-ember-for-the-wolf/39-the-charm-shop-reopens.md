## 39. The Charm Shop Reopens

The sign above the door read *Winterbourne & Kin*, and every morning when Odile unlocked the shop she touched the letters once, the way a person touches a scar to confirm it is still theirs.

The paint was still fresh. Halvard had commissioned it himself—Halvard, who had once stood in her doorway with an ember-glass pendant and a warrant behind his eyes, now wore a clerk's gray coat and carried petitions between Vael and the wolf-kin camps like a man doing penance by paperwork. *You kept your name,* he'd said when he handed her the sign. *That's more than most of us managed.* She hadn't known what he meant. She suspected she never would.

The shop sat on Lantern Row, which was no longer the last street before the border but the first street of somewhere new. The wall still stood—stone remembered longer than kings did—but its gates stayed open now from dawn to dusk, and through them came traders with frost-pale furs and honey-dark pelts, wolf-kin children chasing hoops down cobbles that had never heard their laughter before, and once, memorably, a wild pack's emissary who had inspected the entire market for insults before buying a wheel of cheese and leaving without a word. The city had learned to hold two kinds of silence: the old kind, of things unsaid, and the new kind, of things simply at peace.

Odile sold charms. Legal ones, licensed, stamped with the twin seal of Vael and the northern wilds—a flame and a paw print curled into one another like sleeping animals. Wards against rain. Charms for safe childbirth, for good harvests, for travelers' feet. Small magic. She had built her whole first life on small magic, staying small, and there was a comfort in returning to it that she did not examine too closely, the way she did not examine the way her hand sometimes rose to her collarbone at dusk, or why the thirteenth day of every month left her restless as a cat in a storm.

She was good at not examining things. She'd had practice. Twelve years of her life lived somewhere she could not follow, and the last year spent learning to carry the shape of that absence without letting it hollow her.

Most days, it worked.

* * *

Spring came late to Lantern Row that year, and when it came it came all at once, the way water comes through a cracked dam. Odile propped the shop door open to let the smell of thaw in—wet stone and green things—and began the morning ritual: banking the charm-fire, sorting the day's commissions, setting out the little brass bell that rang when someone crossed the threshold.

The bell rang mid-morning.

"Back again?" she said without looking up. "The dream-ease charms don't work better if you buy three. They work worse. Greed confuses the binding."

"I'll remember that."

The voice stopped her hands over the tray of moon-glass pendants.

It was low, unhurried, with a roughness at the bottom of it like wind moving through winter grass. She knew it. Not the way she knew her customers' voices—not filed away in the ordinary drawers of memory—but the way you know a note sung back to you after years, the body recognizing what the mind cannot place.

She looked up.

He was tall, broad through the shoulders in the way of people who had carried heavy things across long distances, and he wore a traveler's coat gone soft at the elbows, gray as river stone. His hair was dark, shot through early with silver at the temples. And his eyes—

Gray. Not the flat gray of slate or storm-cloud but a living gray, the color of ice over running water, light moving beneath it. They found hers across the shop and held, and something happened in Odile's chest that had no name in any language she spoke: a tightening, a loosening, both at once, like a knot being tied and cut in the same instant.

"You're new," she said, because her mouth needed to say something and that was the only true thing available.

"New to the city." He came further in, and she noticed he moved carefully, favoring his left side very slightly—an old wound, long healed, that the body had never quite forgiven. "I've been north. I'm told this is the place for charms."

"Told by whom?"

"A friend." Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under ice—grief, or the memory of grief, worn smooth by time. "Someone who said if I ever needed reminding how to sleep, this was the shop."

"We sell a great many things besides sleep."

"I know." He was looking at the shelves now, at the rows of small glass vials and knotted cords and pressed-flower wards, and his gaze moved over them with an attention that wasn't browsing. It was cataloguing. As though he were checking each item against some list only he possessed. "I know what you sell."

* * *

His name was Renn. He paid for a traveler's ward and a salve for old scars—"the kind that ache in cold weather," he said, and did not explain—and he stayed nearly an hour in a shop that should have taken him ten minutes, asking questions about this charm and that, and Odile found herself answering at length, more at length than she had spoken to any customer in months, telling him things about the craft she told no one, the little heresies of binding and the tricks of the ember-fire, and he listened the way people listen to songs they half remember, leaning slightly forward, his gray eyes never leaving her face longer than politeness strictly allowed.

When he finally turned to go, the bell chimed, and he paused on the threshold with the afternoon light behind him.

"Do you dream?" he asked suddenly.

"That depends on the day."

"The same dream. Over and over. A wall, maybe. Or a fire." His voice was even, but his hand had closed around the doorframe. "A fire on a high place, and stars coming back."

Odile's breath went strange in her chest.

Because she did. She dreamed it perhaps once a month, always the same, always ending at the same unbearable moment: a stone parapet under her palms, a great fire burning green-gold against a sky filling with stars, and beside her—beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched—someone whose face she could never turn to see. In the dream she always knew that if she turned, everything would be different. And in the dream she always woke just before turning, her heart hammering, her cheeks wet, weeping for something with no name and no face, grieving a loss she had no record of having suffered.

"I dream of a fire," she said slowly. "On a wall. With someone standing next to me."

Renn went very still. Stillness, she thought distantly, was clearly natural to him—the stillness of something that had once been prey or predator and had never unlearned either.

"Do you see their face?" he asked. Quietly. Carefully. The way a man asks a question whose answer he has waited a very long time to hear, and fears.

"No." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, frayed at the edges. "Isn't that absurd? I've mourned this person in my sleep for—I don't know how long. Years. And I couldn't pick them out of a crowd."

Something crossed his face then that she would spend a long time afterward trying to name. It was not surprise. It was closer to relief folded inside sorrow, a man setting down a weight and immediately missing it.

"No," he said. "I don't suppose you could."

He left. The bell chimed behind him, bright and small and final, and Odile stood in her empty shop with her heart beating too fast and the taste of salt in her mouth, and did not know why.

* * *

He came back the next day. And the day after.

He bought things he plainly did not need—a luck-knot for a journey he wasn't taking, a fever-charm "for a neighbor"—and Odile let him, because the alternative was admitting that she wanted him in the shop for reasons that had nothing to do with coin. He sat on the stool by the counter while she worked, and they talked, or rather she talked and he drew her out, question by patient question, until she had told him about the guild, about the Charmwrights and their slow dying, about the night the stars went out and the wards fell and her whole careful small life cracked open like river ice.

She did not tell him about the embers. She did not tell him about the ledger with her own name dated twelve years past, or the drowned chapel, or the bone orchard where she had watched her own funeral procession move through the trees. Those memories had come back to her in the reforging—or rather, all but one had come back, and the one that hadn't was the one that mattered, the hole at the center of the ring, the price written in her own blood on the border wall. She remembered choosing. She remembered the knife of it, the deliberate opening of her own hands. She remembered knowing exactly what she was giving up.

She just didn't know what it was.

That was the joke of it, the cruel elegant joke she had written for herself with her own blood: she remembered the sacrifice perfectly and had forgotten only the thing sacrificed. Some nights she lay awake trying to reason her way backward—if I gave up a memory of a person, then the person existed; if the person existed, who were they?—and the reasoning always circled the same empty room and came out the same door. There was nothing there. There was a shaped absence, a person-sized silence, and her heart broke against it nightly and healed by morning, the way hearts do when they are made to forget on purpose.

But with Renn sitting on her stool, the silence felt less like absence and more like company.

"You look at me strangely sometimes," he said one evening, near closing, the light going amber through the windows.

"Do I?"

"When I laugh. Or when I say certain words. Like you're hearing an echo and trying to find its source."

Odile set down the pendant she was wiring. Her hands had gone unsteady, and she resented them for it.

"Have we met?" she asked. "Before the city. Before—all of this. On the road, maybe. You have a face people might remember."

Something flickered in those gray eyes—that fish-under-ice movement again, faster now, the ice thinning.

"No," he said. "We haven't met."

And then, so softly she almost missed it, almost convinced herself she'd imagined it:

"Not yet."

* * *

The trouble with a shaped absence, Odile was learning, was that it fit whatever came near it.

She caught herself saving things for him. The last honey-cakes from the market woman, set aside under a cloth. The best chair, the one that caught the afternoon sun. She caught herself listening for his step on the cobbles—a particular step, unhurried, favoring the left—and hating herself a little for the listening, and doing it anyway. She was forty-one years old and had rebuilt herself twice from rubble and she was behaving like a girl with her first crush, except it wasn't that, it was nothing like that, it was older and stranger, it was her own heart reaching toward something her mind insisted was empty air.

Her customers noticed. Marta the dyer, who had been coming since the Lantern Row days, squinted at her one morning over a fertility charm and said, "You hum now. Did you know that? You never used to hum."

"Humming is legal," Odile said.

"It's more than legal. It's suspicious." But Marta smiled when she said it, and squeezed her hand across the counter, and Odile realized that everyone on Lantern Row had been waiting a year for her to hum.

At night the dream changed. That frightened her more than anything. The wall was still there, the green-gold fire, the stars flooding back—but now the figure beside her had begun to resolve at the edges, the way fog lifts off a valley in the morning. Dark hair. Silver at the temples. A coat gone soft at the elbows. She could almost see the line of a jaw, the set of shoulders, and every morning she woke reaching across the bed for someone who had never been there, saying a word she couldn't hear, a name worn smooth as sea glass by twelve years of dreaming.

Once, she wrote it down upon waking, chasing the sound of it. What came out on the paper was a single letter, inked and re-inked until the page tore:

*R*

She stared at it for a long time. Then she burned the page in the charm-fire and watched the ash rise, and told herself it meant nothing, and knew she was lying, and did not know to whom.

* * *

On the last day of spring, he came in soaked to the skin, laughing, shaking rain off his coat like a dog—which was an odd thought to have, she noted, an odd and oddly warm thought—and demanded, of all things, a sun-charm.

"For today? The sun gave up hours ago."

"For tomorrow." He dripped on her floorboards and didn't apologize. "I want to be the sort of man who carries sunlight in his pocket. Indulge me."

She made it while he watched, winding gold wire around a sliver of citrine, murmuring the binding words. It was simple work, work she could do blind, and yet under his gaze her fingers felt newly made, as though the craft itself were happening to her for the first time. When she finished, she held it out—and he took it, but he didn't take his hand away. His palm stayed under hers, warm and callused, and across the scarred ridge of it ran a mark she knew.

An ember-burn. Old, white, long-healed. The kind of scar left by covenant-stone.

The shop went very quiet. The rain filled the silence against the windows. Somewhere below her ribs, in the place where the shaped absence lived, something turned over like a sleeper disturbed.

"Where did you get this?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"A fire," Renn said. "A long time ago. On a high place." His gray eyes held hers, and beneath the calm of them was something vast and patient and breaking, a dam holding back a country's worth of water. "I've told you about my dreams, Odile. All these weeks, I've told you about my dreams. Haven't you wondered why I never asked about yours?"

The bell over the door did not chime. No one entered. The rain came down on Lantern Row, and the sun-charm glowed faintly between their hands, and Odile Winterbourne stood in her own shop feeling the ground shift beneath her the way it had shifted the night the stars went out—except this time, she understood dimly, wonderfully, terribly, that the ground was not falling away.

It was coming back.

"Renn," she said. His name. Just his name. And the moment she said it, the shaped absence in her chest flared like a coal breathed upon, and she knew—she did not remember, but she *knew*, the way the body knows fire, the way the shard had known her name—that she had said this name before. Many times. In another life. On a wall, beside a fire, under returning stars.

"Yes," he said softly. "That's the beginning. If you want it."

Outside, the rain was stopping. Through the parting clouds, the first star of the evening appeared over the rooftops of Vael—then a second, a third, the sky remembering itself—and Odile looked up at them, and then at the man holding her hand, and heard herself say:

"Tell me. Tell me all of it. From the beginning."
