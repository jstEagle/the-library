## 40. An Ember Kept Burning

* * *

**Renn**

Winter had come gently to Vael that year, as if the city itself were trying to make amends.

Renn stood across the Lanternway from the charm shop with a cup of spiced cider going cold in his hands, watching the light spill gold onto the cobbles through windows that no longer needed wards to keep them bright. The sign above the door was new-painted, the lettering still sharp: **WINTERBOURNE CHARMWRIGHT — LICENSED BY JOINT ACCORD OF VAEL AND THE WILDS.** Beneath it, smaller, in Odile's own careful hand: *Charms for luck, love, memory, and safe passage.*

Safe passage. She'd insisted on that one. He'd watched her paint it, tongue between her teeth, and said nothing, because some jokes were better left to ripen.

Snow was beginning again—the third snow since the thirteenth moon had come full and the stars had returned like a held breath finally let go. People still stopped in the streets to look up at them. Renn did too, sometimes. Twelve years of starless sky had taught him not to trust a clear night, and even now there were evenings when he woke in the narrow room above Halvard's old barracks-turned-office and lay listening for the silence where the stars should be. It always took a moment to remember that the silence was gone. That the sky sang again.

Across the street, the shop door opened, and a bell he had helped her hang rang its small bright note.

She stepped out into the falling snow to turn the sign—*CLOSED*, though the hour was early—and stood a moment in the doorway with her arms crossed against the cold, looking down the street the way she always did at closing time. Looking north. She didn't know why she did it. He had asked once, casually, and she had shrugged and said, "Habit, maybe. From before." And then she had looked at him a moment too long, the way she always did when *before* came up, gray eyes searching his face for something just out of reach, and had gone back inside without saying anything else.

That was how it was between them now. Kindness, and strangeness, and a door she kept almost opening.

He crossed the street. He had learned, over these months, the exact weight of steps that wouldn't startle a wolf-kin's instincts and wouldn't frighten a woman who had forgotten she'd ever loved him. There was a distance that was right. He knew it the way he knew the phases of the hidden moon.

"Closing early?" he said.

"The Wardens' Accord meeting is tonight," Odile said. "Halvard wants both of us there when they read the new border terms. He gets twitchy if we're late." A pause. "Well. Twitchier."

"He's earned his twitching."

"He's earned more than that," she said, and meant it, and Renn felt the old warmth move through him—not love exactly, not anymore, or not only; something quieter that had grown where love had been cut away, like grass through a floorboard. "Come in out of the snow. I made tea before I remembered I hate making tea."

The shop smelled of cedar and beeswax and the faint copper tang of working magic—the smell of the covenant itself, though she couldn't know that, and he would never tell her so. Shelves lined the walls where her charms hung in rows: little glass stars on leather cords, knotwork rings for travelers, ember-glass pendants that glowed faintly warm when someone you loved was thinking of you. Those sold fastest now. All of Vael wanted proof, these days, that its heart was connected to something.

On the counter by the register lay a book.

Renn saw it and went very still.

It was a plain thing—a ledger, half-burned, salvaged from what remained of the guildhall, its spine cracked and re-stitched with sinew in his own careful hand. He had repaired it months ago, meaning to give it to her whole, and had never managed it. Some gifts are too heavy to hand over all at once.

"That's the guild ledger," Odile said, following his gaze. Her voice had gone carefully light. "From the ruins. Halvard released it after the trial—I signed for it, it's legal, don't make that face." She turned it toward him. "I've been reading it. There are gaps. Whole pages burned away, and the ones left are... strange. Entries in my handwriting that I don't remember writing. Dates I can't account for. And—" she tapped a line near the front, where the ink had survived— "*Thirteen embers, thirteen memories, one signer.* I keep reading that line and feeling like I'm standing at the top of stairs in the dark. Like there's a step below me that isn't there."

"I know the feeling," Renn said quietly.

She looked at him. Outside, the snow thickened, softening the sound of the city until the shop felt like the last lit room in the world.

"You do, don't you," she said. Not a question. "You get that look. Every time I ask about the wall, or the wolves, or why you stayed in Vael when your pack offered you a place in the northern courts—you get this look like a man holding a door shut against wind." She pulled out the stool behind the counter and sat, and folded her hands, and looked at him with the steadiness she brought to everything now, everything except him. "So I'm going to ask you properly, once, and then I'll never ask again. Renn. What aren't you telling me?"

The bell over the door gave a small sympathetic shiver, though no draft had touched it.

Renn set down his cold cider. He thought of a night twelve years ago on a frozen wall, and a girl of nineteen with frost in her lashes swearing an oath beside him while the stars went out one by one behind her. He thought of a promise made in the dark before the reforging, when her memories were already slipping: *I will remember enough for both of us.*

He thought: *she has healed. She has built a life. Is it kind, to open the door?*

But then—he had watched her paint *safe passage* on the sign. He had seen her stop in doorways and look north. A person could heal around an absence the way a tree heals around a nail, but the nail was still there, and every year it grew harder to take out, and he had promised. He had promised.

"All right," he said. He pulled a chair from the corner and set it across the counter from her, close, the way two people sit when they have sat that way a thousand times and he alone remembers it. "All right. But it isn't a thing I can tell you in an evening. It's long, and parts of it will hurt, and some of it you won't believe until the second telling."

"The second telling?"

"Wolves tell stories in circles," he said. "We say them again and again until they're worn smooth as river stones and true all the way through. So that's how I'll do it. In circles. As many times as it takes."

"As what takes?" she whispered.

"Until you remember," Renn said. "Or until you don't need to."

He put his hand flat on the burned ledger, and began.

* * *

**Odile**

Later—much later, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the snow outside had buried the street sounds entirely—Odile would try to reconstruct how the story had begun. What she remembered was his voice first, low and unhurried, a voice made for winter nights. What she remembered was that the first sentence made the hair rise on her arms, though she couldn't have said why.

"On the night the stars went out," Renn said, "you were selling charms in the night market in Vael. You had a stall between a spice-seller and a woman who told fortunes with knucklebones, and business was bad, because your charms worked and people are afraid of things that work."

"That's—" Odile started, and stopped. Because it was true. She remembered that night; everyone remembered that night; it was the night the sky emptied. But she had never told anyone about the stall, or the fortune-teller, and she had certainly never told *him*. "How do you know that?"

"In circles," he said. "No interruptions yet. You can interrogate me at dawn."

And then he told it, and she forgot to breathe.

He told it from his side of it—which was a side she had never imagined, because on the night the stars vanished she had thought herself alone under the empty sky, and here was this stranger describing the same moment from beyond the fallen border: running through the wilds with a shard of covenant-stone burning in his fist, feeling the wards die, knowing in his bones that somewhere south a woman he had sworn himself to was standing in a market feeling the same silence fall.

"A woman you'd sworn yourself to," Odile repeated. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"Mm," he said, which was not an answer, and continued.

He told her about crossing the broken wards wounded, carrying the shard. He told her about a cellar beneath a charm shop, and a woman who stitched his wounds with shaking hands and insulted his hygiene the entire time. Odile laughed at that—one startled, cracked laugh—and then pressed her hand to her mouth, because she remembered the cellar. Of course she remembered the cellar; that was three days after the stars went out, well within the span of her living memory. But hearing it from the other side, from inside his head—*she scolded me like a sergeant and her hands never trembled once when they were on the wound, only everywhere else*—was like seeing her own house from the street for the first time. Familiar walls. A stranger's window.

He told her about the ledger, and the guildhall, and the name in it dated twelve years past. He told her about the ash market, and the raid, and the first time she had seen his true shape—here he paused, ears coloring faintly, and she realized with a jolt that the great silver wolf who had carried her through fire was sitting across the counter from her drinking cold tea, and had been all along, and some deep animal part of her had known it from the first moment, from the shard that burned her palm in the market and whispered a name she hadn't understood.

He told her about the drowned chapel, and the bone orchard, and the funeral procession she had apparently walked in. At that part Odile found tears sliding down her face without any feeling of having begun to cry—grief arriving ahead of understanding, like weather off the mountains.

He told her about Hollow Vale, and the ford, and the rite of submission he had performed before the wild pack, and Odile reached across the counter without deciding to and took his hand, and he let her, and neither of them remarked upon it, and their fingers fit together like a lock finding a key that had been cut for it.

He told her about the campfire where he had almost said it and hadn't. About the guildmaster's confession. About *I chose to forget*. His voice went rough there, and he stopped, and looked out the dark window a long moment, and Odile waited, and the waiting felt older than either of them.

"She asked for it," Odile said softly. "In your story. The woman erased her own memories."

"She did."

"Why would anyone—" And then, mid-word, she felt it: the missing stair. The step below her in the dark. Twelve years of unexplained habits rose up at once like water finding its level—the way she looked north at closing time, the way she flinched from the color of his eyes in certain lights, the way she had painted *safe passage* on her own sign without knowing why the word made her chest ache. The way she dreamed, nearly every night, of a wall under a vanishing sky, and a blurred figure beside her whose absence hurt worse than any presence ever had.

"Renn," she said. Her voice was very small in the little shop. "The man on the wall. In the memory the first ember gave me. The one whose face was taken."

"Yes," he said.

"It was you."

"It was me," he said. "It was always going to be me. That's the shape of the whole story, Odile. Everything else is just the road we took to get back to the wall."

She sat with that. The candle flames leaned, though the air was still. Somewhere beyond the snow, the thirteenth moon was rising over a border where wolves and humans now traded openly, where children ran messages across lines that had been death for a hundred years—and all of it, all of it, had been paid for with a piece of her that she had cut out with her own hands to save two worlds.

"You kept the promise," she said at last. "The one you made before the reforging. You're remembering for both of us."

"I'm trying."

"And you've been here all along. Months. Buying thread. Fixing my shutters. Letting me—" she laughed, wet and disbelieving, "—letting me *flirt with you*, Renn, at the Accord dinners, I called you *mysterious*, I said you had kind eyes—"

"They are kind eyes," he said gravely, and she threw a balled-up order slip at him, and caught his wrist as he deflected it, and then neither of them moved.

His pulse beat under her fingers. Steady, slow, wolf-calm—and underneath it, when she listened with whatever she was now, charmwright and ex-signatory and keeper of thirteen restored memories minus one, she heard it racing.

"I can't feel it," she whispered. "That's the cruelest part, isn't it? I know it the way you know a word on the tip of your tongue. I know there was love. I can't feel it. They took the feeling and left me the fact."

"Not cruel," Renn said. He turned his hand over and laced his fingers through hers, deliberate as an oath. "Cruel would be leaving you the feeling with no story under it. This way, we build it again. Wolves believe a thing told truly enough times becomes true all the way down." His mouth curved, and there was grief in it and hope in it and twelve patient years. "So I'll tell you in circles. Tonight I got as far as the ash market. Tomorrow, the marshes. Next week, the glacier, if your nerves are strong."

"My nerves are excellent."

"Liar. Your hands shook during the cellar chapter."

"How do you—"

"Twelve years," he said simply. "I remember enough for both of us."

Odile looked down at their joined hands on the counter, at the ledger with its burned pages and its line in her own handwriting—*thirteen embers, thirteen memories, one signer*—and understood suddenly what the book was for. Not a record. A script. Thirteen chapters, and he had been waiting, all these quiet months, for her to ask him to begin.

"There are pages missing," she said. "Burned out. What happened to them?"

Renn was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had changed—lower, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto thin ice.

"That's the last circle," he said. "The one I haven't told anyone. The pages aren't missing, Odile. I took them out. Because there's a fourteenth entry in that ledger, written the night after the reforging, in ink that wasn't ink, and it says something I haven't decided whether you're ready to hear."

Outside, the snow fell silent on a city that could see its stars. Inside, the candle flames stood perfectly straight, as if the room itself were holding its breath.

"Tell me tomorrow," Odile said. She did not let go of his hand. "With the marshes. We'll do it in circles."

"We'll do it in circles," Renn agreed—and beneath the counter, where the old guild ledger lay closed, the ember-glass clasp binding its cover glowed once, softly, like a coal breathing, like a story refusing to end.

*On the night the stars went out,* he had begun, *you were selling charms in Vael's night market.*

And tomorrow, he would begin again.
