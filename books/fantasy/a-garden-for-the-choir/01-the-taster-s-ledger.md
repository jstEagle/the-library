## 1. The Taster's Ledger

Twelve years at the king's table had taught Ansel Joubert to eat the way other men pray: with reverence, with discipline, and with the quiet certainty that something was listening.

The first course arrived under a silver dome, and Ansel leaned close before the lid was lifted, breathing in through the nose the way his grandmother had taught him to test milk—no, not milk, that was someone else's grandmother, a memory he kept misplacing—but the habit remained. Steam carried the smell of partridge and roasted quince, of butter browned almost to burning, and beneath it all, faint as a whisper in another room, the green bitterness of hellebore.

*Hellebore*, he noted, in the ledger behind his eyes. *Slow variant. Fourth appearance this season.*

He took the fork. He ate. He swallowed, set the utensil down with a soft click against porcelain, and folded his hands to wait.

The court waited with him, though they did not know they were waiting. That was the elegance of his office: no one ever announced it. The taster simply sat, three seats down from the king's right hand, and when forty minutes passed without his pulse stuttering or his skin going grey or his bowels turning to water, the servants lifted the domes and the feast proceeded, and the king ate, and no one thanked Ansel, because thanking him would mean remembering why he existed, and this court was very good at not remembering things.

The poison worked slowly by design. Slow poisons were the honest ones—they wanted the taster dead too, just late enough to seem like grief. Fast poisons could be aimed around him; slow ones soaked the whole table like spilled wine into linen. So Ansel sat with hellebore blooming gently along his nervous system and thought about nothing at all, which was its own discipline, until the tremor passed and the head cook's shoulders dropped half an inch and dinner began.

He recited the ledger while he waited. He always recited the ledger. It was not written anywhere—it could not be written anywhere, because a list of every poison a man has survived is also a confession, an accusation, and an inventory of everything the wrong person might want to know—but it lived in him, ordered and exact, each entry wearing its own taste like a name.

*Crown's Bitter, nine survivals, tastes of almonds gone wrong.* The first thing he'd ever been dosed with, on his third day, without warning, because Master Corvel had believed in instruction by immersion. Ansel had vomited for six hours and come back to work the next morning, and Corvel had looked at him the way a jeweler looks at a stone that turns out to be real.

*Sallowroot, four survivals, tastes of pond water and pennies.*

*Grey Widow, two survivals, no taste at all—that is its signature, so when you taste nothing, be afraid of what you tasted.*

*Lady's Mercy, one survival, tastes of honey and rot together, sweet over sweet, and I do not recommend it to anyone I like.*

*Widow's Kiss. Three. Like biting a candle flame.*

*Thornwine. Five. Green and sharp as a snapped branch.*

*The Pale Brother. One. Tastes of snow. I dreamed of my mother for a week after and could not tell you her face anymore, only the dreaming.*

On it went, entry after entry, twelve years deep, a ledger written in flesh and kept in the dark of him. Some men counted scars; Ansel counted dinners that should have killed him and hadn't. His body was a map of every attempt on the king's life, and he knew the map better than he knew his own face, which was fortunate, because lately he wasn't entirely sure he did know his own face. Mirrors had begun to feel unreliable. He suspected the mirrors less than he suspected himself.

The second course was a pike stuffed with chestnuts and thyme, and Ansel found *foxglove* in it, delicate and deliberate, and ate his portion while the strings played something in a minor key that everyone hummed afterward without knowing they were humming it. The hall blazed with candles. The court glittered and laughed and asked one another questions and, Ansel noticed more and more these days, did not wait for the answers. Laughter came at the right moments like bells rung on schedule.

King Aldric sat above the salt and did not blink. Ansel had been trying for weeks not to notice that either, and failing, and noticing his failure, and filing all of it away in a part of the ledger that had no business existing—the section headed with questions instead of poisons. *When did the king stop blinking? When did he stop finishing sentences? Why does he laugh at jokes made three years ago and stare blankly at ones made yesterday?*

Noticing things was dangerous. Ansel knew this the way he knew the poisons—not from books, but from outcomes. Cook Marta had noticed that the king's shadow fell wrong in the morning light. She had mentioned it over the fish stock, laughing, the way one mentions odd weather. Two days later her station was filled by a young man from the coast provinces, and no one, when asked, could quite recall her surname, and eventually no one could quite recall her at all, and Ansel had stood in the kitchen doorway feeling the shape of the absence like a tooth socket after the pull.

He had stopped mentioning things after that. He noticed them, and he ate them too, silently, like everything else served at that table.

* * *

The new servant came in with the fourth course.

Ansel registered him the way he registered everyone—a habit older than his post: young, perhaps twenty, hands too clean for a cupbearer, eyes that stayed down exactly the correct amount. Nothing about him rang false. That itself was worth a second look, because everything in the palace rang slightly false now, like instruments tuned to a pitch just below hearing. This boy rang true, or rang nothing, and Ansel could not decide which unsettled him more.

"Spiced red, my lord," the servant said, filling Ansel's glass first, before the king's—which was correct procedure, though almost no one remembered the correct procedure anymore.

The wine smelled of nothing.

Not of poor wine, not of corked wine, not of vinegar or lead or any of the hundred small corruptions a bottle suffers. It smelled of *nothing*, the way the inside of a sealed jar smells of nothing, and Ansel's tongue went into the ledger, flipping pages, searching—

—and finding no entry.

Twelve years. Four hundred-odd poisons catalogued, cross-referenced by taste, by onset, by the particular way each one walked through a man's blood. And here was a vintage that matched none of them, not because it was unfamiliar but because it was *empty*. Grey Widow had no taste, but it had weight; drinking it was like swallowing a held breath. This was different. This was wine-shaped silence.

He drank anyway. That was the whole of his office, the entire architecture of his life: he drank, and he waited, and his body told the truth even when nothing else did.

The servant moved on down the table, pouring. The candle flames bent toward the king, all of them, very slightly, like grass leaning under a wind Ansel could not feel. No one else seemed to see it. Or no one else could remember seeing it a moment later.

Ansel began counting his own pulse.

It was steady. Sixty-two beats, sixty-three. No heat climbing the throat. No numbness at the lips where fast toxins announce themselves. No sweetness curdling into rot. He reached the count of two hundred, then four hundred, and felt nothing but the ordinary warmth of wine spreading through him, and he thought—foolishly, later he would think, *foolishly*—that the ledger had simply met a harmless stranger. A bad vintage. A barrel rinsed wrong.

Then he tried to recall his morning, and it was not there.

Not blurred, not dim. *Gone*. He had woken, he knew that much, because he knew he had woken every day for thirty-nine years. But between waking and the great doors of the banquet hall there lay a stretch of hours as smooth and featureless as poured wax. Had he eaten? Had he spoken to anyone? He searched for the memory the way he searched for a taste, systematically, entry by entry—and the search came back empty, and worse than empty: the search itself felt watched, as though something on the other side of the absence felt him looking and turned politely to face him.

One hour. He could feel the shape of the missing hour precisely, the way a man feels the shape of a room he has stepped out of. Something had been taken out of him and taken *cleanly*, without scar or soreness, and the wine sat in his stomach tasting of nothing, and around him the feast went on, and the king raised his goblet, and the whole court rose with him and drank to midwinter's approach, and their voices said the words in unison, perfectly, as if rehearsed, as if the toast were being poured into them rather than out.

"The king, long may he keep us."

Long may he *keep* us. Not guard. Keep. Ansel had heard the toast a thousand times and never once heard the word underneath it until tonight, sitting alone in his missing hour with a glass of emptiness in his hand.

He looked up. The king was already watching him.

Not the court. Not the feast. Him. And the king smiled—the wide, warm, ceremonial smile—and did not blink, and across the whole blazing length of the table Ansel understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death in a dish, that whatever had drunk his morning had done it deliberately, and carefully, and had left the rest of him standing only the way a cook leaves the rest of the loaf standing after testing one slice.

*He knows I noticed something. He doesn't know what yet. But he will check again tomorrow.*

That night Ansel did not sleep. He sat in his narrow room above the tasting chambers, candle lit, and on the back of an old inventory sheet he wrote, in a cipher of his grandmother's devising—one of the few memories of her he still trusted—everything he could reconstruct of the last month. The unblinking. The shadows. Cook Marta's surname dissolving like sugar in warm water. The wine that tasted of nothing.

At the bottom of the page his hand stopped, hovering, and then wrote the only conclusion the ledger allowed:

*The poison at this table is not in the food. It never was. I am not here to protect the king from dying. I am the last thing in this palace that remembers the king was alive—and whatever wears his crown has begun to wonder how I taste.*

He blew out the candle and listened to the palace breathe around him, vast and content and forgetting, and far below, through stone floors and vaulted dark, he thought he could hear the kitchens already at work on tomorrow's feast—and among the clatter of knives, faintly, a sound like many voices singing in a language that made his armored, poisoned, twelve-years-surviving memory itch at the edges, as if something had finally found the one door in the house that had never once been opened.

In three days' time, a priestess would light a candle in a forgotten shrine, and everything Ansel had just written would become the least of what he needed to remember.

But that night he only sat in the dark, reciting the ledger like a prayer, and for the first time in twelve years, he was afraid one of the entries might answer him.
