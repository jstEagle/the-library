## 2. Prayers with Answers

The shrine of the Small Gods had not been swept in twenty years, and Sera Voss was the reason.

She told herself it was dust that made her eyes water when she came here on the last night of autumn, dust and nothing else. The capital did not know she came. No one knew anything about her anymore except that she had been stripped of her orders, forbidden to wear the gray-and-gold, forbidden even to speak the old names aloud in public—and so she had become very good at silence. Twenty years of practice. She could carry a secret the way other women carried water: balanced, steady, spilling nothing.

But once a month—sometimes more often, if the nights were long enough—she climbed the crooked stair behind the tanner's row, ducked under the collapsed lintel, and entered the shrine where no god had answered since before her mother died.

It was not a grand place. The Small Gods had never wanted grandeur. Nine niches were carved into the living rock of the hill, one for each of them, their faces worn soft by centuries of hands that no longer came. Someone—she liked to imagine a tanner's child, generations dead—had left chalk drawings on the lower walls: a dog with too many legs, a sun with a smile, nine little figures holding hands. The chalk had faded to the color of bone. Everything here was the color of bone.

Sera lit her candle at the first niche, as she always did. Veth, who kept thresholds. A stub of yellow wax she bought from a chandler who thought it was for reading in bed. She lit it out of habit, which was the only honest form of prayer left in the kingdom, and because her hands needed something to do that was not shaking.

"You kept me waiting again," she said to the niche, in the flat voice of a woman talking to stone. "Twenty years, and you've never once been on time. My mother said the gods were like farmers—slow to rise, but they always rose."

Her mother's name caught in her throat, as it always did. Maren Voss, priestess before her, dead eleven years and buried outside consecrated ground because there was no consecrated ground left. The old rites had been folded away like linens after a guest departs; the new faith of the Crown required no shrines, only attendance. The king asked nothing of his people but their love, and their love asked nothing back. It was, everyone agreed, a merciful arrangement.

Sera knelt on the cold floor and let the candle do its small work. The flame stood perfectly still. That should have warned her. In this shrine the air moved—came down through the broken roof in gusts that smelled of tanneries and river—but tonight the flame burned straight as a nail.

"Mother used to say you could tell what kind of year it would be by how the candle behaved," she murmured. "A dancing flame meant gossip. A blue flame meant a birth." She stopped herself from saying the rest. *A still flame meant someone listening.*

The flame leaned.

Not toward the door. Not with any wind. It bent toward the ninth niche—the one at the end, whose face had been worn away entirely, whom even her mother had only called the Listener—and it stretched thin and tall as a hand reaching.

Sera's whole body went cold, then hot, then cold again, the way a limb does when it wakes from sleep.

"No," she said aloud, to the shrine, to herself. "No, I'm not doing this. I've done my grieving. I've done my—" Her voice cracked on the word *faith*, and she hated it for cracking.

From the ninth niche came a sound like breath drawn through water. Then, gentle as a hand on the back of her neck:

*Seralei.*

Her mother's voice.

Not similar to. Not reminiscent of. It was Maren Voss's voice exactly—the slight lisp on the *l*, the habit of swallowing the ends of endearments, the particular warmth of a woman who had sung her daughter to sleep with the names of gods instead of lullabies because she could never remember any lullabies.

Sera did not fall to her knees. She was already on her knees. What happened instead was worse: everything inside her that she had packed away, boxed, buried under twenty years of dutiful silence, came up all at once like floodwater through a cellar stair. She wept without dignity, without sound, her fists pressed against her mouth so the tanners' dogs below would not hear a grown woman crying in a ruin.

"I buried you," she whispered when she could speak. "I buried you twice, Mother. Once in the ground and once in my heart, and both times I did it properly, and now you—you are not her. Whatever you are, you are not her."

*I am the shrine,* said the voice, patient, ancient, wearing her mother's patience like a borrowed coat. *Your mother gave me her voice, long ago, the way priests give. I keep all the voices ever offered here. I am sorry to use one that wounds you. It is the only one you would believe.*

"What do you want?" Sera dragged her sleeve across her face. The old anger helped. Anger was load-bearing; she had built much of her life on it. "Twenty years of silence. The whole kingdom forgot you. I was stripped and shamed for lighting candles, and you said nothing. Nothing. And now—now you choose tonight—"

*Because tonight the harvest began.*

The word went through her like a needle finding the old channel of a scar. Harvest. She knew the word in its ritual sense: the ingathering, midwinter, the feast where the court gave thanks. But the way the voice said it was older. Heavier.

"Explain."

*Look at your city, Seralei. Tell me what you see on the streets.*

"I don't understand—"

*Tell me.*

So she told, reluctantly, the way one recites evidence against a friend. "People are... cheerful. They're very cheerful. The king's peace is—everyone says it's the kindest reign in memory." She faltered on the phrase. *In memory.* "There are fewer complaints at the granaries. Fewer disputes. Fewer... names."

*Nine hundred and forty children in the weaver's quarter alone cannot say their own names without looking at their wrists,* said the voice in her mother's mouth, and it was terrible not because it was cruel but because it was calm. *They laugh easily. They obey beautifully. They have begun to call each other by the names of months. The thing upon the throne has eaten past the edges of forgetting, and this winter it will eat the center. Every grief, every grudge, every name of every mother's mother—it will swallow the kingdom's remembering down to the marrow, and then, Seralei, there will be nothing left to wake anyone. It will be loved by an empty country forever.*

The candle guttered and steadied. Sera found she had gripped the chalk-white wall, and the drawing of the nine small gods was smudged under her fingers.

"The king," she said slowly. "You're telling me the king—"

*Is three years dead. What wears him eats memories as you eat bread, and grows stronger with every course.* A pause, and in the pause her mother's voice became almost tender. *One soul in this kingdom is hidden from it. One table has been poisoned so often that the poisoner's servant has become a fortress. His memories are armored in swallowed deaths. He does not know what he is. He thinks he is only a man who has survived.*

"Who?" she asked, though some deep part of her had already begun to dread the answer, already felt the shape of it the way you feel rain coming in an old wound.

*Ansel Joubert. The taster.*

The name landed in the shrine like a dropped chalice.

She had not heard it spoken in twelve years—not since the day Ansel had stood in the temple courtyard with his new appointment in his fist and told her, in that dry careful voice of his, that a food-taster could not be married to a priestess of proscribed gods, that he would not watch her be stripped and shamed, that he was sorry, that he was sorry, that he was sorry. She had watched him walk out of her life toward the palace with his ledger-book and his careful tongue, and she had told herself for years that she hated him, until hating became simply another form of keeping him.

"He can't," she said. "He's nobody. He tastes soup."

*He is the only door,* said the voice. *And you, Seralei, are the only key that remembers where doors go. The unbinding rite still lives in you—in the parts of you the stripping could not take. You must carry it to him. You must teach him what he is, and you must do it before midwinter, when the last course is served.*

"Why me?" It came out raw, almost childish. "Why us? We paid. We both paid everything. Is that why? Do the gods collect the ones who've already bled?"

The silence lasted long enough that she thought the presence had withdrawn. Then, gently:

*Yes,* it said, in her mother's voice, unflinching. *The wounded remember best where the knife was held. Go to the valley, Seralei. Begin there.*

The flame went out.

Not blown—finished, cleanly, like a life. Darkness filled the shrine, thick and total, and in it Sera knelt with wet cheeks and burning hands and felt her faith return the way feeling returns to a frozen limb: agonizing, tingling, unwelcome, alive.

She stayed until the light through the broken roof turned the color of ash, and then she rose, took the cold stub of candle, and did what she had sworn twenty years ago never to do again. She swept the shrine. Found the old broom behind the third niche, and swept, weeping and furious and awake, until the dust of two decades lay in a gray drift at the threshold like snow.

At dawn she went down into the city, past the cheerful markets where mothers smiled too widely at children who checked their wrists before answering to their names, and hired a cart heading east.

Toward the valley that forbids.

Toward Ansel.
