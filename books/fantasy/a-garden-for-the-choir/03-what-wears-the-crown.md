## 3. What Wears the Crown

The summons came at dusk, carried by a page whose smile sat a half-inch too high on his face.

"His Majesty requests the taster," the boy said, and his voice had the pleasant flatness of a bell rung in an empty room. "Privately. After supper."

Ansel bowed, because bowing was a language he spoke fluently, and watched the boy skip away down the colonnade with that same too-wide grin. He stood a long moment in the fading light, one hand resting on the stone balustrade, and felt the old ledger of his body open itself unbidden, as it always did when dread arrived without a name.

Arsenic, slow, survived eleven years past. Aconite, twice, the second time nearly the end of him. Belladonna in a wedding pie when he was nineteen. Hemlock water. Lead, patiently, over a decade of glazed dishes. Mercury. The bitter patience of foxglove. Ergot in the harvest bread of a famine year. And the nameless ones—the compounds some ambitious cook or clever assassin had invented just for kings, which Ansel had swallowed so that no king ever would.

Twelve years. Every dish at every royal table passed through his mouth first, and his blood kept accounts like a miser, and he had come to think of himself as safe the way a man thinks of a fortress wall as sky.

*His Majesty requests the taster. Privately.*

Kings did not eat privately. Kings ate in state, surrounded by witnesses, because a king alone was a king already half-assassinated. In twelve years Ansel had tasted for Aldric IV at coronation feasts and war councils, at weddings and funerals and the small jealous suppers where alliances were strangled between courses. But never— not once —alone.

He went to his rooms first and did something he had not done since his apprenticeship: he took out the little kit sewn into the lining of his coat. Chalk-white antidote powders, a vial of activated charcoal, emetic salts wrapped in waxed paper. His fingers knew each packet by touch. It was a comfort, handling them, the way prayer is a comfort to a man who no longer believes but cannot stop kneeling.

Then he followed the page's footsteps up toward the high tower, where the king's private apartments smelled of cold stone and, lately, of nothing at all.

* * *

The chamber at the top of the tower was warm. That was the first wrongness. Every room in the palace leaked its heat through ancient windows and badly fitted doors; this room held its warmth like a held breath. A fire burned in the grate without crackling. Two candles stood lit on the table, their flames perfectly still, as if painted there.

And the king sat waiting, hands folded on the table before an untouched meal, and he did not blink.

Ansel noticed it within ten steps, the way a musician notices a false note. He had spent twelve years watching this man across tables—watching his throat, his hands, the subtle signals of appetite and distaste that told a taster everything about a dish before it touched the tongue. Aldric IV had blinked like any man, had wet his lips, had drummed fingers gone soft with age. This—the thing seated in the king's posture, wearing the king's face—simply *looked*. Its eyes moved from Ansel to the food and back with the smooth mechanical sweep of a weathervane finding the wind.

"Taster." The voice was the king's voice. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. "Come. Sit. I am told the pheasant requires your... attention."

Ansel crossed the room and took his place at the table's edge, close enough to serve, far enough to flee. Twelve years of protocol lived in his body and carried him through the motions: the small knife, the portion taken, the chewing, the pause, the swallow. Pheasant, roasted with rowan berries. Salt. Bread still warm. A sauce of dark wine.

Nothing. No bitterness under the tongue, no numbness blooming at the back of the throat, no copper, no almond ghost. Clean food, honestly cooked. He said so.

"You are certain?" The king leaned forward. The candle flames did not stir, though the movement should have pushed air against them. "You are quite certain? Taste again."

Ansel tasted again. Rowan berries have a faint bitter edge, astringent, like the memory of frost. There was none. The berries tasted of the idea of berries, the way a portrait tastes of nothing. He looked down at his plate and understood, with a lurch, that the food was perfect the way a corpse is peaceful.

"It is good, Majesty," he said carefully. "It is very good."

"Yes." The king sat back. "Everything is good now. Everything is exactly as it has always been."

There it was again, that sentence, that terrible little sentence Ansel had been hearing for months now from mouths all over the palace. *As it has always been.* Cooks saying it about recipes they'd changed. Chambermaids saying it about corridors they'd cleaned differently yesterday. The whole castle murmuring it like a rosary, like a spell, like a lie everyone had agreed to believe.

"Majesty," Ansel said, testing the ice before putting his weight on it, "the harvest festival approaches. Shall I begin planning the menu as in previous years?"

"What previous years?" The king smiled, and the smile reached every feature of his face except the eyes, which continued their slow weather-vane sweep. "I have reigned three years. There are no previous years."

Three years. Ansel felt the words go through him like a fishhook finding flesh. He had tasted for this king for twelve. He had stood at the coronation feast, twenty-nine years old, and swallowed arsenic-laced wine meant for a newly crowned man while the court applauded his devotion. He remembered the trumpets. He remembered the weight of the crown catching candlelight. He remembered—

He remembered it perfectly, and that was the problem, wasn't it. Because the man across the table remembered nothing, and the servants who had begun to vanish last spring had all been old servants, long-serving servants, servants who remembered things, and Ansel had told himself it was plague, or purge, or politics, the ordinary machinery by which palaces emptied themselves.

"The coronation," Ansel said, very quietly. "Your Majesty's coronation. Five years ago. Or—" he made himself count backward aloud, watching the king's face the way he watched a dish, "—seven. The snow came early that year. The lords of the eastern marches arrived late and drunk."

Something happened to the king's face then. Not a change of expression—a change of *substance*, brief as heat-shimmer, as though the face were a surface of water and something beneath it had turned over. When it stilled, the smile was back, wider.

"I do not recall snow," the king said pleasantly. "More wine, taster."

* * *

Ansel poured. His hands did not shake, and he noted their steadiness with distant gratitude, the way one notes the courage of strangers. The wine was from the southern vineyards, a vintage he had tasted a hundred times, and it tasted of nothing at all—not even grape, not even alcohol, just wet and red and *shaped* like wine, a costume of wine.

He drank it anyway. Habit was a kind of armor too.

"Tell me," the king said, watching him drink, "how long have you served this court?"

"Twelve years, Majesty."

"That seems excessive." The king tilted his head, and the gesture was almost childlike, almost curious, and for one vertiginous moment Ansel thought he saw hunger in it—vast, patient, bottomless hunger, the kind the sea has for shores. "You have been here longer than my reign. How can that be, if I have always been king?"

The question hung in the warm dead air. Ansel set down the cup with great care.

"Men misremember, Majesty," he said. "It is a common failing."

"Yes," the king agreed softly. "It is. Men misremember. And what is misremembered was never true. And what was never true need never be grieved." He unfolded his hands and placed them flat on the table, palms down, and Ansel saw that the fingernails had a faint gray cast, like the skin of someone long drowned. "You remember many things, taster. I can see them behind your eyes. So many years. So many *dishes*."

The temperature of the room had not changed, but Ansel was suddenly, desperately cold.

"I taste for Your Majesty's safety," he managed. "That is all I am. A servant."

"A servant." The king considered the word, rolling it like a coin across knuckles. "Yes. Servants. We have lost so many servants. They leave in the night, one after another, and no one grieves them, because—" the smile deepened, split, became something no human mouth could survive wearing, "—because what is forgotten was never loved."

One of the candles guttered. Just one. And in the instant of its faltering light, the king moved—and his goblet, gold-rimmed, heavy, ceremonial, caught the flame's dying gleam and threw it back.

Ansel had spent twelve years learning to see what others missed: the haze of powder in a wine's meniscus, the wrong sheen of a poisoned glaze, the tremor in a cook's wrist. He saw now what the polished gold held, and his heart stopped like a dropped glass.

The goblet reflected the table. The candles. The food. Ansel's own pale, terrified face, hovering at the table's edge like a moon over water.

Where the king sat, the goblet held only the empty chair, the empty air, the far wall with its tapestry of hunts long finished.

No reflection. No face. Nothing where a king should be.

The candle righted itself. The reflection—or the absence of it—was gone, smoothed over, and the king was smiling at him with the king's own mouth, and saying, in the king's own voice:

"You look unwell, taster. Perhaps you should go."

* * *

Ansel walked. He did not run; running would have been a confession, and confessions in that castle ended in vanishing. He walked down the tower stairs with his spine straight and his ledger open in his mind, reciting poisons like prayers, because the poisons were real, the poisons had weight and history and consequence, and he needed—desperately, bodily needed—to be near something true.

Aconite. Belladonna. Hemlock. Foxglove. Arsenic, slow and patient. Ergot. Lead. Mercury. The nameless ones, the invented ones, the deaths he had swallowed so that a king could live.

But the king was dead. Had been dead for years. And the thing that wore the crown ate memories the way he ate meals—course by course, kingdom by kingdom, beginning with the servants who noticed, smoothing each absence over with that gentle rosary: *as it has always been.*

He reached his rooms and barred the door and stood in the dark with his back against the wood, breathing.

The protector inverted. All those years he had stood between a king and death, and death had been sitting on the throne the entire time, dining patiently, growing fat on everything the kingdom used to be. He was not the shield anymore. He was the last unpoisoned thing in a poisoned house—and every person who still remembered a snowfall seven winters ago, a mother's face, a child's name, was a dish waiting to be served.

They would come for him soon. The page with the too-wide smile, or the guards with their hollowed eyes, or the king himself, hungry and curious, wondering how one small man had swallowed so much death and still remembered everything.

Ansel looked around his room—at the kit in his coat, the ledger written in scar tissue, the twelve years of survivals stacked inside him like stones in a wall—and understood, with terrible clarity, why the walls of his body had been built.

Not to keep poison out.

To keep *himself* in. Whole. Undigested.

He began, quietly, to pack.

And beneath the window, far below in the dark courtyard, he heard footsteps stop—many footsteps, moving together, unhurried, coming up the stair.
