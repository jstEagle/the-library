## 4. The Valley That Forbids

Sera had forgotten how loud silence could be.

For twenty years she had lived inside the capital's hum—the bells, the courtiers, the whisper of the crown-thing's hunger moving through the streets like weather. Even in the forgotten shrine, where no one prayed, there had been sound: rats in the walls, wind through broken glass, her own breath keeping time like a reluctant metronome.

The valley had none of it. She stood at the lip of the pass as dusk came down and felt the quiet press against her eardrums like water. No birdsong—nothing lived here to sing. No wind-song either; the air moved but carried nothing with it, no pollen, no prayer, no stray thought of anything greater than itself. The old gods could not hear into this place. Neither, thank every silent heaven, could the thing that wore the king's face.

She had walked three days to get here. Her boots were ruined. Her hands were cracked from cold, and the candle stubs in her pack—she'd brought a dozen, unable to help herself, though she knew they would never light here—felt heavier than the food she'd abandoned along the road to make room for them.

A priestess without a god. A woman carrying light into a country where light could not exist.

She laughed once, at herself, and the sound fell dead in the air, and even that frightened her.

* * *

She found him on the second day, or rather his fire found her—a smudge of smoke rising from behind a collapsed millhouse near the valley's heart, thin and gray and stubborn, the way everything about Ansel Joubert was thin and gray and stubborn.

He was thinner than she remembered. Twelve years since the night he'd walked out of her shrine with a royal warrant in his hand and duty in his throat, and grief had carved him down to essentials: sharp cheekbones, ink-stained fingers, eyes the color of river slate that watched everything twice. He sat wrapped in a shepherd's blanket beside a fire built small enough to hide from the sky, eating boiled barley from a tin cup, and when he saw her standing at the edge of the firelight he did not startle.

He simply looked at her for a long moment and said, "You're not supposed to be able to find this place."

"Your mother told me," Sera said. "Before she died. She said, if ever you run, you'll run home, and home is a hole in the world."

Ansel set down the cup. "My mother hated me."

"She loved you the way stone loves rain. Slowly, and by wearing down." She stepped closer. "May I?"

"It's your fire as much as mine. You lit half the candles in my childhood." He almost smiled. Almost. "Sit, Sera. Before I decide I'm dreaming and wake up somewhere worse."

She sat. The fire gave off heat and nothing else—no crackle-spirit dancing in it, no little salamander of warmth such as would have leapt up in any hearth beyond the valley's borders. Here fire was only chemistry, obedient and dull. Here, nothing answered.

"You look terrible," Ansel said.

"You should see the kingdom." And then, because there was no gentle way, because gentleness had never been their language: "It's begun, Ansel. The harvest. The shrine answered me. My mother's voice came out of a candle flame and told me midwinter is the end of it. The whole kingdom, remembered into its mouth and gone."

"I know."

"You *know*?" Her voice rose before she could cage it. "Then why are you sitting in a hole in the world eating barley? Why aren't you—"

"Because I dropped a goblet." He said it flatly, staring into the flames. "Twelve days ago. In front of the king. The wine went everywhere, and I knelt to gather the shards, and I looked up, and where his reflection should have been in the polished silver there was—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "There wasn't. There was a shape of absence, Sera. Like a hole cut in the world wearing purple velvet. And then he smiled at me with all those teeth and asked if I'd tasted anything unusual lately, and I said no, my lord, and I ran."

"And the servants who've vanished—"

"Noticed something. A reflection missing. A name they couldn't remember giving their own child. A moment gone from a morning." He turned to her at last, and his eyes were wet, which frightened her more than the goblet story had. "I noticed. For twelve years I've been noticing, cataloguing, tasting every slow poison that crossed that table—and it couldn't take my memories. Do you understand what that means? It tried. That morning with the wine that tasted of nothing—I lost an hour, just an hour, and then it came back whole, like a bone setting wrong and righting itself. Whatever is in me, whatever I swallowed to survive, it can't digest."

Sera felt the truth settle over her like snowfall, cold and enormous. "The poisons. Your ledger of survivals."

"My ledger." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "I used to think it was a curse. Every morning reciting the list—arsenic, henbane, monkshood, the gray death from the southern marshes—like a penance. Turns out it was armor."

"Or an inheritance," Sera said quietly. "My mother used to say the old gods mark their own. Maybe surviving death twelve years' worth of times left... fingerprints."

They sat in silence. Above them the stars came out, and Sera realized she could name none of the constellations' guardian spirits here—they were only stars, distant and dumb and beautiful, with nothing watching through them. It should have been restful. It felt like being buried alive with her eyes open.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down and the cold made honesty cheaper, she told him about the bargain.

"The valley," she said. "Do you know why it's empty? Not barren—*empty*. Nothing grows wild here. Nothing magical crosses the border. Your grandmother's kitchen had no hearth-god, no brownie in the walls, no blessing baked into the bread. Did you ever wonder why?"

"My grandmother wondered. She said the valley paid a price so the rest of the kingdom wouldn't have to."

"That's exactly what she said." Sera hugged her knees. "Generations ago, when the crown was first forged—or first *found*, we don't know which—the priests of the old gods struck a bargain. The thing beneath the crown feeds on memory. On devotion. On the small daily prayers of ordinary people, the ones who thank the hearth for warmth and the well for water. So the valley was given to it as a tithe-land: emptied of magic, emptied of gods, emptied of *attention*. And in exchange, the creature agreed to sleep. To feed slowly. To leave the kingdom dreaming."

"But it woke."

"It stopped pretending. Or maybe it never slept at all—maybe the bargain was always a leash on the other end of which stood no one." She stared into the coals. "The valley's emptiness bought the kingdom's blindness, Ansel. Everyone forgot the price because forgetting was the price. Except the valley folk, who kept their memories precisely because there was nothing here worth feeding on. Your grandmother remembered. My order remembered, in fragments, in hymns nobody sang anymore."

"And now it's harvesting the rest."

"Now it's harvesting the rest."

Ansel was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice had gone soft and dangerous. "And where were you, Sera? Twenty years. Where was the priesthood while the thing sat on the throne eating us?"

The accusation landed where he'd aimed it, under her ribs. "Doubting," she said. "Being punished for doubting. Being stripped of office and sent to sweep an empty shrine while the faithful sang hymns to a king who doesn't blink. I didn't forget, Ansel. I was *muzzled*. There's a difference, and it cost me everything too."

"I lost a career."

"I lost my god." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, and let it crack anyway. "Twenty years ago I stood in the sanctuary and called the old names and got silence, and I thought I'd failed. I thought the silence was my punishment for loving someone the temple forbade. It took me fifteen years to understand the gods hadn't abandoned me. They'd been *devoured*. Every prayer I spoke went straight into its mouth. I was feeding the thing that ate my faith, one candle at a time." She wiped her eyes roughly with the back of her hand. "So don't talk to me about payment, Ansel Joubert. I've been paying interest for two decades."

He reached across the dead fire and took her hand.

His fingers were cold and stained faintly with ink—he still wrote, then, still kept lists even in exile, some part of him refusing to trust memory alone. She looked at their hands together in the starlight, hers cracked and his inked, and felt twenty years collapse like a folded letter.

"I wrote to you," he said. "After the first year. Three letters. I burned them all."

"I know. Your mother told me that too."

"Did she tell you anything else?"

"She told me you still say grace before meals. To gods who can't hear you. Out of habit."

Ansel exhaled something between a laugh and a wound. "Habit," he said. "That's all any of us are now. Habits wearing down. But yours came back, Sera. The voice in the candle. If the god spoke to you—if the old ones are answering again—that means the harvest isn't finished. There's still something left to save."

"There's still *everything* left to save. Minus what it's already eaten." She gripped his hand harder. "But it can't be saved from in here. The valley protects you and paralyzes you both. Whatever the god wants—and it wants something, it warned me—it wants you outside these borders, walking toward that crown with your armored head full of survived deaths."

"Then the god can want," Ansel said bitterly. "I spent twelve years learning to survive. I'm not eager to unlearn it in a week."

"No," she agreed. "But you'll do it anyway."

"How can you be sure?"

"Because you're still saying grace." She lifted their joined hands, pressed her lips briefly to his ink-stained knuckles, and felt him tremble like a plucked string. "Because you tasted every poison rather than let one servant die in your place. Because you ran, yes—but you ran *home*, and home is the place that taught you to count what survives."

The valley held its perfect, awful silence around them. Somewhere far beyond its borders, she imagined the crown-thing turning its reflectionless face toward these hills, sniffing at the one mind it could not swallow, patient as winter.

"Sleep," she told him. "Tomorrow I'll tell you what the voice said. All of it."

"What did it say, Sera?"

She met his eyes across the ashes. "It said the taster must serve one last meal."
