## 5. Immunity as Inheritance

The dream began with the smell of bread.

Not the flat, obedient loaves of the palace ovens—those baked to a schedule and a specification—but the rough, dark rye of his grandmother's kitchen in the valley, the kind that cracked its own crust when it cooled because no one had told it to be polite. Ansel stood at the threshold of that kitchen as he had stood a thousand times as a boy, one hand on the doorframe worn smooth by three generations of leaning children, and waited for the dream to do what dreams did.

It did not dissolve. It deepened.

The stove ticked as iron cooled. A kettle muttered on the back ring. The window over the basin held the gray light of an autumn morning, and beyond the glass the valley rose in its steep green walls—the same walls that had swallowed him six days ago when Sera found him crouched in the ruins of his mother's house like a man hiding from his own name.

"Sit," said his grandmother.

She was at the table with her back to him, kneading, her forearms dusted white to the elbow, her hair bound in the same faded kerchief she had worn every day of the eleven years he had known her before the fever took her. She had been dead for thirty years. He knew this the way he knew the taste of arsenic: completely, and without any hope of being wrong about it.

"I know you're not her," Ansel said. His voice came out steady. Twelve years at the king's table had taught him that much—to keep his voice level while everything under it curdled.

"No," she agreed pleasantly, still kneading. "But I'm wearing her well, don't you think? Memory is a garment, taster. Some of us have very few left to choose from." She turned then, and her face was his grandmother's face and also something older behind it, the way a lamp is a flame and also the hand that lit it. Her eyes were the brown of river stones, patient, unhurried. "You've been hard to reach. Do you know that? Six nights I've knocked."

"The valley," Ansel said. "Nothing crosses it."

"Nothing *magical* crosses it. I am neither magical nor nothing. I am what's left." She wiped her hands on her apron and gestured to the chair across from her, the one with the mended rung he had always claimed as a child. "Sit. The bread needs twenty minutes and we need less."

He sat. It was absurd, sitting down to talk with a dead woman's shape in a dead woman's kitchen, but there was a second chair and his legs were tired, and besides—he had learned long ago that the safest place to meet a poison was seated, with your hands visible and your breathing slow.

"You're one of the old gods," he said. "The ones Sera lights candles for."

"We were never gods. That was the kingdom's flattery. We were keepers. Gardeners, if you like." Something moved across the borrowed face—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "Your priestess would explain it better. She was trained. I was only ever good at kitchens and small mercies." The stone-brown eyes fixed on him. "I'm here about the harvest, Ansel Joubert. And about why you are the only man in this kingdom who can stop it."

"Because I'm immune," he said. "To poison."

"To poison," the god agreed, "and to something far worse. But you don't understand what you are yet. You think of yourself as a ledger. A list of survivals. Arsenic, Tuesday. Belladonna, Lent. Wolfsbane twice, and once something you never learned the name of and had to invent a word for." Ansel's breath caught; those words had never been spoken aloud, not even to Sera. "Yes. I can read you. Forgive me—it's how I see. Now listen, because the bread is nearly ready and I will not say this twice."

The god leaned forward, and the kitchen seemed to lean with it, the shadows gathering close as if they too wanted to hear.

"When the thing in the crown eats a memory, it must first digest it. Break it down. Render it into something it can swallow—a fear, a forgetting, a soft warm blankness where a life used to be. Every soul in that court is digestible. Every soul in the kingdom. They made themselves so, generation by generation, trading their sharpness for safety, their remembering for peace. That was the bargain the valley's emptiness bought—or rather, the bargain the valley's emptiness *cost*, though your Sera has likely told you that part already, and with more bitterness than I could manage."

"She did," Ansel said quietly.

"But you—" the god's voice dropped, went almost tender, which was somehow worse than any threat— "you spent twelve years swallowing death in small doses. And every dose you survived wrote itself into you. Not just into your body, taster. Into your *memory*. The night you survived the arsenic, you remember the arsenic—the taste of it under the wine, the burn in your throat, the hour you spent on the cold floor counting your heartbeats and deciding whether to live. You remember it perfectly, because remembering it is the armor. Each survived poison built a wall around the memory of surviving it. Your mind is a fortress whose every stone is a death you refused."

Ansel looked down at his hands. They were his grandmother's table, his boyhood hands, smooth and unscarred—and beneath the dream-skin he could feel them as they truly were now: nicked, stained, faintly yellowed at the nails from years of handling things meant to kill kings.

"The creature tried to take your morning," the god said. "Six days ago. The wine that tasted of nothing. The missing hour."

"It took it anyway."

"It took the *hour*. It could not take the memory of the hour. Do you understand the difference? It swallowed and found nothing there to digest—only a sealed room, walled in belladonna and wolfsbane and every other death you carry. So it spat the hour back out, or rather it left the shell of it, and inside the shell your memory sits untouched, waiting for you to come collect it." The god smiled then, fully, and it was his grandmother's smile, crooked on the left side. "You are indigestible, Ansel Joubert. You are the only thing in this kingdom wearing a crown-ward of your own making. Which means you are the only vessel that can carry the unbinding past its defenses without being emptied first."

The kettle screamed. Neither of them moved to lift it.

"And the cost," Ansel said. There was always a cost. He had learned that at the king's table too: nothing arrived on silver that wasn't paid for in blood somewhere out of sight.

"Ah." The god sat back. Outside the window, the valley light had begun to change, going gold and then copper, time moving strangely, the way it does in kitchens when you are happy. "You already know the cost. Say it."

"If I leave the valley, I leave the ward. The creature can touch me. Can try to eat me."

"It will try. Constantly. From the moment you cross the boundary until the moment the crown comes off its head—if it ever comes off. Every day inside its reach is a day it presses against your walls looking for a loose stone." The god's voice gentled further. "And there is a second cost. The unbinding requires fuel. Protected memory burns beautifully, taster. Everything you armored—all twelve years of survivals, all the deaths that make you safe—will feed the fire. You will walk out of that valley armored and walk away from the crown unarmored. Mortal in the ordinary ways again. Forgetful. Open. Able to be hurt by anything, including the simple passage of time."

Ansel was silent for a long moment. In the dream, the silence had texture—the tick of the cooling stove, the mutter of the kettle, the smell of rye bread turning golden in the oven he could not see.

"I built myself into a weapon," he said finally, "and the weapon only works once."

"All true weapons work once," said the god. "That is what makes them true."

"And if I refuse?"

The god did not answer at once. Instead it rose, crossed to the oven, and drew out the loaf—impossibly soon, impossibly perfect, the crust split along its shoulder exactly the way his grandmother's loaves had split. It set the bread on the table between them, and the steam that came off it smelled like every winter morning of his childhood.

"If you refuse," the god said, "you will live. That is the cruelty of it. The valley will keep you. Sera will go alone, because she cannot help herself—you know this about her already, I think; you loved her partly for it. And she will fail, or she will succeed at a price you cannot imagine, and either way you will sit here in your safe empty valley eating safe empty bread, and the kingdom will forget itself course by course, child by child, until there is nothing left to harvest and the thing in the crown grows hungry enough to start wondering whether wards are really as absolute as everyone believes." The stone eyes held his. "Safety is a room, Ansel. It is not a life. You of all people should know—you've been tasting other people's food for twelve years so that they could pretend to live."

"That's unfair."

"Yes," the god agreed cheerfully. "Most truths are. Would you like some bread?"

"I don't eat in dreams."

"A wise rule. Broken only once, usually." The god cut two slices anyway and pushed one across the table, and Ansel found, to his horror, that his hand moved toward it—that some boyhood reflex deeper than caution still lived in him, reaching for warmth without checking it for poison first.

He stopped his hand. He looked at it. And in that look he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that this was the whole of it: twelve years of checking, twelve years of dying in small doses so that others wouldn't die in large ones, and the only question left was whether he would do it once more, at the largest scale, with no ledger left afterward to prove it had happened.

"I'll need to know the rite," he said.

"Sera knows the rite. She has known it since before she was disgraced; it's why she was disgraced. What she doesn't know is how to get it past the crown's wards. That part is yours. Your memory is the key, taster. The creature cannot digest what you carry, so it cannot stop you carrying it." The god wrapped the remaining loaf in cloth with brisk, domestic efficiency, as if packing provisions for a journey, which perhaps it was. "One more thing, and then the bread will wake you."

"What?"

"When you cross the valley's edge—and you will cross it, I've watched enough men decide to waste no time pretending otherwise—the first thing the creature will do is offer you back your missing hour. The morning it stole. It thinks the shell is empty. Let it believe that. And let Sera tell you what happened in that hour, because I suspect"—the borrowed face grew briefly, terribly kind— "that you will want to hear it from her."

The kitchen began to thin around the edges, the stove-light stretching, the copper light through the window bleeding into white.

"Wait," Ansel said. "Who are you? Whose voice—"

"Yours, mostly," said the god, already half-gone, folding up like heat off bread. "Grandmothers are just the shapes our courage wears when we're young enough to listen."

* * *

He woke on the pallet in his mother's ruined house with dawn coming gray through the broken roof, and the smell of bread so vivid in his nose that for three full seconds he was nine years old and safe.

Then it faded, and he was forty-one, and lying on a bedroll in a magic-dead valley at the bottom of the world, and the decision was already made—it had been made, he realized, somewhere in the middle of the dream, probably around the time he'd reached for the bread.

Sera was outside. He could hear her at the shrine-rubble below the house, doing whatever small stubborn devotions she still performed for gods who hadn't answered in twenty years. She didn't know yet that one of them had answered. She didn't know that her god had sat in his grandmother's kitchen and packed a loaf for the road.

Ansel rolled up the bedroll. He checked his boots, his knife, the little packet of antidotes he'd carried so long it had worn a groove in his coat. Then he stepped out into the cold morning air, where Sera knelt among the broken stones with her head bowed, and said:

"How much food can we carry?"

She looked up. Whatever she saw in his face made her rise slowly, brushing ash from her knees, her eyes widening by degrees.

"Ansel—"

"There's an hour you haven't told me about," he said. "From the morning the wine tasted of nothing. Tell me on the road. We should reach the valley's edge by dark."

Her mouth opened, closed, opened again. Behind her the mountains stood in their ancient silence, keeping their old blind bargain—and beyond them, far to the north, a crown waited on a head that did not blink, hungry for midwinter.

"Sera," Ansel said, and heard his own voice go quiet and certain, the voice of a man who had chosen his courage rather than felt it. "Tell me what I forgot."
