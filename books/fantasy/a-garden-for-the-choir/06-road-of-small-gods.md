## 6. Road of Small Gods

### Ansel

The road out of the valley was a road out of the world.

Ansel felt it happen at the tree line—the moment the valley's emptiness let go of him. It was like stepping from a cellar into weather. For twelve years he had lived inside that silence, that blessed nothing where magic could not cross, and now something brushed against his skin again: faint, cold, attentive. The world remembering he existed.

He did not look back at the valley. If he looked back, he would turn around, and if he turned around, everyone in the kingdom would keep smiling their too-wide smiles until there was no one left inside them to smile.

"You're counting your poisons," Sera said beside him. Not a question. She walked like a woman who had once walked processions—spine straight, hands loose, eyes moving over the hedgerows as though they might contain either god or soldier.

"Old habit."

"Good. Keep it. A ledger in the head is harder to harvest than one on paper." She said it lightly, but her knuckles were white around the strap of her satchel, where the rite lay folded in Ansel's handwriting, disguised as a recipe for honeyed quince.

They made the first village by dusk. Harrow Fen, the signpost said, though the paint had been scraped away by someone careful and unhurried. The houses were whole. The chimneys smoked. The people stood in their doorways as Ansel and Sera passed, and every one of them smiled.

It was not an unkind smile. That was the horror of it. It was the smile of someone who has forgotten what they were about to worry about.

A woman offered them bread at her gate. Her hands were flour-white and competent; her eyes were pleasant and vacant as two cups of milk. "You'll stay the night," she said. "Travelers should stay. It's nearly midwinter."

"We're expected in the capital," Sera said gently.

"Of course. Everyone is expected somewhere." The woman beamed. Behind her, in the warm doorway, a small boy peered around her skirts. Ansel crouched to his level, the way he had once crouched for the kitchen boys in the palace, and asked him his name.

The boy smiled too widely. He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He tried again, and this time a sound came, puzzled and small, like a bird striking glass. Then he laughed, because laughing was easier, and ran inside.

Ansel stood very still for a long moment.

"They take the names first," Sera said quietly when they had walked on. "Names are the oldest prayers we own. A mother speaks her child's name over the cradle—that's a prayer without knowing it. The creature eats those first. Everything else goes slower."

"And the ones who notice?"

"You've seen the empty houses along the road. Notice long enough, loudly enough—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. They had both spent the day counting doorways with no smoke rising from them.

That night they slept in a hay barn rather than accept any more bread, and Ansel lay awake reciting his ledger into the dark like a rosary. *Wolfsbane, aged three years in wine. Arsenic, thrice weekly for a season. Hemlock, the long lesson. Nightshade, the first, when I was nineteen and wept into the basin afterward.* Each name was a stone in a wall. Whatever came for his memory would have to climb it.

Somewhere past midnight, he heard Sera praying. Not to the crown-thing's silent heavens—to the old ones, the small gods, under her breath, in a language that sounded like water over stones. And faintly—so faintly he thought he dreamed it—something answered.

* * *

### Sera

Sera had forgotten how loud belief was.

For twenty years she had served a faith gone quiet, tending candles in a shrine the city had walled off, speaking to gods who gave back only the silence she deserved. Now, walking the road of small gods toward the capital, she heard them everywhere—in the well-water, in the turning of the wind through bare orchards, in the way certain crossroads felt *watchful*. They were weak, starved, half-faded. But they were awake, and they were afraid, and fear, she had learned, was a kind of company.

The believers found them before they found the believers.

At a shrine barely larger than a hearth—a niche in a field wall holding a chipped stone figure of the Mother-of-Bees—an old man rose from behind it with a sickle in his hand and terror all over his face, and then, seeing Sera's grey priestess cloak, began to weep.

"Priestess," he said. "Priestess, I kept the candle. Nobody told me to stop keeping the candle, so I kept it."

"I know," Sera said, and took his hands, sickle and all. "I know you did. What is your name?"

"Bram." He said it like a man proving he could still lift a weight. "Bram Ostler. My wife is Maret. My daughters are Liss and Tove. Say them back to me. Please. Everyone here—I say the names aloud every morning so I don't lose them, but it helps when someone else says them too."

"Liss and Tove," Sera said. "Daughters of Bram and Maret."

He sobbed once, quietly, and then became brisk, because grief and duty wear the same coat in winter. There were others, he told them. Eleven in Harrow Fen who still noticed things. A ferryman two villages east whose memories were "too mean to eat"—his phrase—who remembered every debt ever owed him and therefore everything else besides. A girl of sixteen called Wren who had begun hearing the small gods answer and hadn't yet learned to be frightened of it.

By the third day they were nine on the road: Ansel and Sera; old Bram with his sickle; the ferryman, Odger, who complained magnificently; Wren, who walked close to Sera and asked questions Sera was almost able to answer; and four farmers who had watched their neighbors forget their children and decided that forgetting was the only enemy worth walking toward.

Ansel moved among them like a man learning a language he'd spoken as a child. Sera watched him teach Wren to test mushrooms against a silver coin, watched him listen to Odger's grievances with genuine attention, watched him stand each night at the edge of the firelight, counting faces, guarding them the way he had guarded a king who never deserved it.

"You could have stayed in the valley," she said to him one evening, when the others slept. "Safe. Empty. Nothing to protect."

"The valley was safe the way a locked pantry is safe," he said. "Nothing gets in. Nothing lives." He turned his hands over in the firelight—taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips from twenty years of spoons and needles. "I spent twelve years keeping one man alive by dying a little each day. I find I'd rather spend what's left keeping many alive by living."

She wanted to say something large then. Instead she said, "You used to burn the honey cakes. Every time."

"I improved."

"You did not. You simply stopped being supervised." And he laughed—actually laughed—and the sound went through her like warmth through frozen ground, and she understood that they were not traveling toward the capital at all. They were traveling toward each other, the long way, by road, the way important things travel.

She reached across the fire and took his hand. He let her. His fingers were steady, which surprised her; hers were not.

"Wendell wrote once that courage isn't the absence of fear," Ansel murmured—no. That was wrong. That was some other life, some other book, bleeding through. Ansel Joubert had never written anything but poison ledgers. But the feeling underneath the words was true, and true things traveled between worlds like seeds on wind: *courage is fear that has agreed to keep walking.*

"Sleep," she told him, and did not let go of his hand until the fire burned down.

### Ansel

The ambush came at the ford.

Ansel knew it first as a wrongness in the birdsong—the reeds along the river had gone quiet in a way that meant something was standing in them. Then Odger, ahead with the cart, said cheerfully, "Visitors!" in exactly the tone the smiling villagers used, and Ansel's blood went cold, because Odger had not smiled like that yesterday.

Six figures stepped from the reeds. They wore the king's livery, or what had been the king's livery, dyed now a grey that matched no heraldry Ansel knew. Their faces were smooth and pleasant. Their smiles were wide. And their eyes—

Their eyes were the worst part. Empty as the boy's mouth in Harrow Fen. Hollowed guards, Sera had called them, back in the valley: men the creature had drunk down to the shape of men, leaving just enough to walk and smile and carry orders.

"Halt," said the foremost, pleasantly. "You are expected in the capital. You will come with us. It is nearly midwinter."

"Odger," Ansel said softly, "step back from them."

But Odger was already smiling wider, wider than a face should allow, and Ansel understood with a lurch that the ferryman's complaints had been a wall too thin, that the creature had found the crack in him days ago and left him walking like a loaded trap.

"Take the priestess," the hollow guard said. "Leave the taster. The taster is expected at table."

Then everything happened at once.

Bram's sickle flashed—not at the guards but at the cart rope, cutting the horses free so they bolted and broke the guards' neat line. One of the farmers drove a hay fork into a hollow chest, and the thing didn't bleed, didn't fall, only looked down at the tines with mild interest, which was somehow worse than screaming. Wren shrieked something in the old tongue—half-prayer, half-panic—and the river itself flinched, a sheet of water rising like a startled animal, and two guards staggered back into the ford.

And Sera moved.

Ansel had seen her pray. He had never seen her fight. She fought the way she prayed—low, grounded, certain—with a pilgrim's staff taken apart into two iron-capped halves, and she put one cap into the temple of the nearest guard with a sound like a mallet on wet wood. The thing dropped. It did not get up. Whatever animated it, it needed the body intact enough to smile.

Ansel, meanwhile, discovered what twelve years of poison had built in him. A hollow guard seized his arm, and its grip carried something—a residue, a command laid into the flesh, the same slow magic the creature used to unmake memory. Ansel felt it crawl up his arm toward his mind, hungry for his recollections, his name, his ledger.

It hit the wall and recoiled.

Every poison survived was a stone. Wolfsbane. Arsenic. Hemlock. Nightshade. The thing's hunger broke against them like a wave against a harbor mole, and Ansel—gasping, laughing, terrified and exultant—drove his elbow into the guard's throat and its grip loosened and Sera's staff came down once more and it was over.

Silence, except the river. Six bodies that had been men. Odger sitting in the shallows, weeping, his own face returned to him like a coat given back, saying *I told them, I told them everything I owed, I told them,* over and over.

Sera stood among the fallen, breathing hard, staff lowered. She looked at Ansel—at his arm, where the creature's touch had left a red mark like a brand fading even now.

"It tried to drink you," she said.

"It choked," Ansel said. "On me." He flexed his fingers. Under the fear, something fierce had kindled, something he hadn't felt since the first time he'd survived a dose meant to kill. "Sera. If my memory is armor to it—"

"Then at the crown, it won't be able to stop you." She crossed the trampled snow and gripped his shoulders, and her eyes were bright with something between dread and dawn. "It will *have* to let you reach it. Your immunity isn't just protection anymore, Ansel. It's the key."

Behind them, Wren stood at the river's edge, pale, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear. When she turned, her young face was old with news.

"The small gods say the harvest feast is set," she said. "Midwinter night. The whole court invited." She swallowed. "They say the guest list includes us."

Ansel looked north, toward the capital's distant smudge of smoke, where the thing wearing the crown was setting its table.

"Then we shouldn't be late," he said. "I've never once missed a seating."
