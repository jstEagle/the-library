## 7. The Price of the Priestess

They took her at the ford of the Aldiss, where the water ran thin as hammered tin and the reeds had forgotten how to bend in the wind.

Sera had felt them coming the way one feels a storm behind a hill—not seen, but tasted, a mineral wrongness on the tongue. She had time to hide the god's token against her breastbone, time to whisper half a prayer into the mud, and then the hollowed guards were everywhere, their lanterns burning without flicker, their hands gentle as sleepwalking children's when they bound her wrists.

They did not gag her. That was the first mercy, and mercies frightened her more than cruelties now. Cruelty meant a mind still home behind the eyes. Gentleness meant the house stood empty, lamps lit for no one.

The journey back to the capital took three days. Her captors did not eat. They sat their horses like coats hung on pegs, and at night they arranged themselves around her fire in a perfect ring, watching her with the patient blankness of icons. Once she tried to sing the old cadences—the ones that used to make candles lean toward her voice—and their heads tilted in unison, thirty degrees, all together, like wheat under one hand of wind. She stopped singing. Not because it failed.

Because it worked, and something *answered* through them, faint and pleased, the way a master taps a finger to a well-trained hound.

*It knows I'm useful,* she thought, staring into the flameless fire. *It doesn't know yet what for.*

She prayed anyway, silently, wordlessly, letting the old names move through her like water through a closed fist—held, felt, not kept. If Ansel was still walking the road of small gods, if he still carried the rite folded inside his skull like a knife wrapped in a supper cloth, then every hour they spent collecting *her* was an hour he gained. Let them think the priestess was the load-bearing wall of this rebellion. Let them gather her in like a harvest.

She only hoped she could be worth the gathering.

* * *

They brought her not to a dungeon but to a dressing room.

That was the second mercy, and it terrified her most of all. A small warm chamber off the royal apartments, mirrors shrouded in grey linen, a fire already lit, a gown laid across the bed in deep ceremonial blue—the color she had worn at her ordination, twenty years and one disgrace ago. Someone had remembered her size. Someone—or some *thing*—had been paying attention for a very long time.

Her wrists were unbound. The guards withdrew backward, bowing, eyes down, and the door closed without a sound.

She waited. She examined the mirrors' shrouds, the seams of the linen, looking for the shape beneath. She counted her heartbeat the way her mother had taught her to count breaths before prayer: not to calm herself, but to *mark the time*, because time marked was time owned, and she would not surrender a single second of it uncounted.

When the door opened again, it was her mother who came through it.

Not the thing wearing the king. Not a herald or a hollowed servant. Her mother—grey-streaked, flour-dusted, hands rough from kneading, the little crooked smile that had made Sera feel chosen by the universe simply for existing. Dead nineteen years, burned in the shrine riots, and here she stood in a borrowed body with candlelight moving through her slightly wrong, like light through water.

"Sera." Her mother's voice. Exactly her mother's voice, down to the way she softened the final vowel, down to the childhood nickname hidden inside it. "Look how you've grown. Look how tired."

Sera's knees nearly went. She gripped the bedframe. The token against her breastbone—a sliver of shrine-oak carved with a name older than kingdoms—grew cold, then warm, then cold, warning and steadying both.

"You're dead," Sera said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it.

"And whose fault is that?" her mother asked gently, and there it was, the blade inside the honey, the exact wound. "You prayed and no one answered. You lost your faith, and faithlessness is a kind of abandonment too, isn't it? But I'm here now, love. I've *always* been here. He keeps us. Every voice the kingdom forgot, he keeps, safe as coins in a vault. You only have to reach in and take."

"Us," Sera repeated.

Her mother smiled the crooked smile and gestured, and the shrouded mirrors unveiled themselves all at once.

Every glass was full of faces. Hundreds. Thousands. Priests she had trained beside. The archcantor who had taught her the seven descending modes. Village wise-women, candle-tenders, bell-ringers, children consecrated to shrines so minor no map recorded them. All the servants of the small gods, harvested over years, standing in the silvered depths of the mirrors with their mouths moving in silent, patient chorus. Not screaming. Not suffering. *Waiting.* That was somehow infinitely worse.

"He is not what you think," her mother said. "He didn't steal us. We were drowning in being forgotten, and he gave us an ocean to live in. And he offers you the same, Sera. More than the same. Come to him willingly and you need never lose another voice. Your mother's. Your gods'. He'll even give you back the *faith*—the real thing, the answering thing, not the dry husk you've been mouthing prayers into for twenty years. You lit a candle in the forgotten shrine. He noticed. He was *moved*. When has anything answered you before?"

*Twenty years ago,* Sera thought. *Once. In this palace. By a voice that was not my mother.*

She let her shoulders sag. She let her eyes fill. None of it was performance, exactly—that was the horror of it; the grief was real, the longing was real, she only chose which door to open and let the true thing pour out through it.

"If I come to him," she whispered, "what must I do?"

"Only tell us where the taster hides. What he carries. What the rite requires." Her mother's hands took hers, warm, calloused, impossibly familiar. "One name, love. One road. And the choir gets its priestess back."

And Sera wept, genuinely, and nodded as though breaking, and asked the question she had come all this way to ask, disguised as surrender:

"What does *he* fear from it? Tell me what the rite costs, so I know why he wants it buried. I can't give you Ansel unless I understand what we'd be saving everyone from."

Her mother hesitated. And in that hesitation, behind her mother's eyes, something vast leaned closer, like a great fish turning under lake ice, and the whole room grew cold, and the chorus in the mirrors went silent.

"Clever girl," said the voice of the king through her mother's mouth, layered now, resonant, a chord instead of a note. "Very well. Knowledge for knowledge. It changes nothing—you cannot use it. The rite requires fuel. Memory is the only coin it accepts. His immunity is a hoard, yes—but a hoard alone burns poorly. It must be *given*, knowingly, willingly, by one who holds protected memory and surrenders it freely at the crown. Do you understand? Willingness is the keystone. No theft, no trick, no coercion can complete the unbinding. Which is why I do not kill him. Why I *invite* him. Why I will set a table for him at midwinter and offer him everything he has ever wanted, exactly as I offer you." The crooked smile returned, wrong now, too wide. "Everyone betrays eventually, priestess. Not out of weakness. Out of *longing*. Longing is the door I live behind."

"Then you know I could be lying now," Sera said quietly. "Telling you I accept, while carrying his refusal in my heart."

"I know," said the creature, almost tenderly, releasing her hands. "But you will not lie forever. No one does. Midwinter is coming, and the longest night is long, and I am very patient, and very generous. Ask your mother's forgiveness on your way out. She hates these errands."

The mirrors shrouded themselves. The chamber emptied of cold. Sera was escorted—gently, bowingly—to a guest suite with a locked door and a window that looked out over the capital, where preparations for the midwinter feast were already climbing the walls like ivy: garlands, lanterns, long tables being carried into the great hall by servants who smiled too widely and moved too smoothly.

Behind her, faintly, fading, her mother's voice one last time—not the creature's, she was almost sure, but her own mother's, tired and sad:

*I'm sorry, love. Take the oak with you wherever it goes.*

* * *

They left her alone with a bible of the new liturgy and a window. They should not have left her the window. From it she could see the shrine district, ruined, roofless—and beyond it, far off at the valley's rim, the pale line of the magic-dead hills where Ansel had been born, where no answer could follow anyone.

She knelt on the cold floor with the oak token in her cupped palms and began the work of twenty years ago, the work she had sworn never to attempt again because the silence afterward had nearly killed her: she built a sending. Small, humble, shaped like a kitchen—because the god had first reached her through a memory of bread, and it seemed the kind of thing a small god would appreciate.

*Sera to Ansel. Sera to the armored man, the ledger-man, the boy who ate death so others wouldn't.*

*They have me. I am sorry. I am not broken. Hear what I bought with my pretending:*

*The rite needs more than your hoard. It needs willingness—it needs someone at the crown, giving away protected memory freely, gladly, choosing emptiness with open eyes. That is the keystone. That is the trap. He means to offer you yourself at the feast—he believes everyone's longing opens eventually.*

*So starve, my love. Starve beautifully. And know that whatever you give at that table, I will be singing beside you, and I do not want you whole—I want you free.*

*Midwinter. Be there. I will find you in the crowd of smiling faces, because you are the only face in this kingdom that still knows how to grieve.*

She pressed the sending out through the token until her fingertips bled with effort, a thread of prayer thin as spider-silk, thrown across the dark toward the hills.

Then she rose, wiped her eyes, arranged her face into wide compliance for the guards who would come to fetch her to rehearsal—for the creature would want its converted priestess visible at the feast, seated close to the throne—and looked out once more at the longest night assembling itself over the city.

Below, in the great hall, the first of the feast-tables was being carried in.

It was long enough to seat the entire court.

It was shaped like a crescent, open at one end—

and at the open end, being lowered onto a dais with ropes and reverence, hung a crown.
