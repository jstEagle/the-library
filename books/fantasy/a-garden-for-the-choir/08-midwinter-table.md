## 8. Midwinter Table

The longest night began with a bell.

Ansel heard it from the servants' stair, where he had been standing for the better part of an hour, learning again how to be furniture. Twelve years of tasting had taught him this: that invisibility was not a cloak but a posture, a way of holding one's shoulders, a face arranged into mild and useful blankness. The court streamed past him toward the great hall in furs and candlelight, laughing their too-wide laughs, and none of them looked at him twice. He was a taster. Tasters were beneath noticing. That had always been his armor, and tonight it was also his disguise.

The bell rang nine times. Midwinter had begun.

He descended into the heat of the hall.

The transformation stole his breath, though he did not let it reach his face. The great hall of the palace had always been grand, but someone—something—had spent the autumn making it monstrous. Banners of black silk hung where the king's colors should have been, worked with silver thread in patterns that hurt to follow, spirals that seemed to turn when he wasn't looking directly at them. A thousand candles burned without dripping. And down the length of the hall ran a single table, longer than any table had a right to be, set for two hundred guests who were also, Ansel understood with cold clarity, the first course.

The courtiers sat in their finery, smiling. Their smiles were wrong—not the strained smiles of people enduring a tedious ceremony, but wide, serene, vacant smiles, the smiles of sleepers dreaming pleasant dreams. Ansel had seen it on the road for a hundred miles: villages that smiled like this, mothers who could not name their children, farmers who greeted him warmly and then forgot him before he passed the well. The harvest was nearly complete. What remained of these people's memories sat inside them like marrow in bone, and tonight the thing at the head of the table would crack them open and drink.

At the far end, on a throne dragged from the dais to sit among its guests rather than above them, wore the crown the king.

It had grown more skillful since Ansel had fled its presence weeks ago. Where once there had been only the absence of blinking, now there was something almost like majesty—the gray-bearded face animated, the mouth moving in warm greeting, the eyes fixed on each courtier in turn with what any stranger would call affection. Only the reflection betrayed it. Ansel had checked, as he passed the great silver ewers: the polished metal gave back candle flame, gave back banners, gave back every seated guest—and where the king sat, gave back only the chair.

*You are a servant,* Ansel told himself, taking his place at the sideboard behind the throne, among six other tasters in matching gray. *You are nobody. You have outwaited kings before.*

That was not strictly true. He had outwaited poison, which was a kind of king—patient, invisible, certain of itself. He recited the ledger silently, the old litany, letting it steady him. *Arsenic, thrice. Belladonna, twice. Wolfsbane, once, and I dreamed of blue flowers for a week. Hemlock. Lead, slow and stupid. The wine of nothing, which took an hour of my morning and left a hole shaped like an hour.* Each survival was a stone in the wall around his memory. The creature could look at him all night and find nothing to eat.

One of the other tasters leaned close. "You're new," she whispered. "I don't remember you."

"I've served eleven years," Ansel said mildly. "You tasted beside me at the autumn feast."

Her brow furrowed—a small, human motion, the last of her fighting through the harvest's fog—and then smoothed. "Of course," she said, smiling her wide smile. "Forgive me."

Ansel's chest tightened. *Hold on,* he wanted to tell her. *Someone is coming to unmake the thing that is eating you.* But Sera was locked in the shrine cells beneath the east wing, and the rite was folded into a recipe in his skull, and the bell had rung, and there was no time left for comfort.

The first course came up from the kitchens on silver platters.

Ansel had spent three days in those kitchens, and they had been the strangest days of his life. The cooks worked in silence, faces slack, preparing dishes of impossible beauty—roasted quail glazed with honey and black pepper, a soup the color of garnets, bread that steamed when broken. But threaded through every recipe, invisible to any eye, ran the harvest. Not poison; the creature had no need of poison here. The food itself was the instrument, each dish steeped in memory drawn from the fields and granaries and birthing rooms of the kingdom, so that eating it would finish the dissolution, would pour the last of a person's past down their throat and leave them smooth as river stones. The creature was feeding its guests themselves.

Ansel had done nothing to stop it. That had been the hardest discipline of his life—harder than swallowing arsenic, harder than watching servants vanish. He had stood in that kitchen with his sleeves rolled and his hands steady and let the platters go out, because the rite required a moment, and the moment was now.

Because the rite was a recipe. That had been the old god's gift, delivered in the dream-kitchen of his grandmother's house, in her voice, over the smell of bread: *A rite hidden in a recipe cannot be found by wards built to catch prayers and spells. Write it as a cook writes. Speak it as a cook speaks. Let the creature swallow it whole.* So Ansel carried the unbinding in his memory, armored against digestion, disguised as instructions for a sauce—the proportions of things, the order of additions, the words that were both ingredients and incantation. He would speak it over the crown itself, at the moment the creature opened its mouth widest, and Sera would sing what he planted.

If Sera lived to sing it.

The king rose to speak.

"Friends," it said, and its voice filled the hall the way cold fills a valley, "the year dies tonight, and we die with it, and are reborn. Eat. Drink. Remember nothing."

Two hundred guests laughed together, one sound, one throat. Ansel felt the hair rise on his arms. The creature lifted its goblet, and the hall drank with it, and the harvest-feast began in earnest.

Course after course went out. Ansel tasted each one at the sideboard, as duty demanded, touching the spoon to his tongue while the armored walls of his memory turned the harvest away like rain off slate. He nodded to the steward. The platters proceeded. With each dish, the smiles in the hall grew wider, and the laughter grew thinner, and somewhere near the seventh course a woman began quietly weeping without knowing why, tears running down her serene face, some buried self drowning even as it died.

Ansel counted courses the way he had once counted poisons. *Three more. Two more.* His hands never shook. Cold clarity was a thing he knew how to wear; twelve years at a poisoned table taught a man to think clearly while dying slowly, and this was only another version of that, the long night version, the final sitting.

On the ninth course, the doors of the hall opened.

Every head turned—including, for the first time, the king's, moving with a jerk like a puppet correcting its line. Two guards dragged a woman between them, her priestess robes torn to gray rags, her wrists bound, her dark hair loose and wild. She had fought them; there was blood at her lip. But when her eyes found the sideboard, found Ansel, they were clear and burning and utterly unafraid.

Sera.

She had been supposed to break free later. Later meant during the twelfth course, meant after Ansel reached the dais, meant with time to spare. Instead she was here now, dragged before the throne, and the creature was looking at her with an expression Ansel recognized across forty feet of candlelight: hunger meeting the one meal that had refused to be eaten.

"You," the king said. Its voice had lost the warmth. Underneath, something vast and hollow spoke. "You were given everything."

"You offered," Sera said. Her voice cracked across the silent hall. "I didn't take." She smiled then, blood on her teeth, and it was the truest smile in the room. "And I remembered why not."

The creature rose from its throne. The candles guttered as one, a thousand flames bowing toward the crown. "Then you will watch," it said, "as I finish. You will spend eternity remembering that you chose this." It turned to the hall. "Bring the final course."

Ansel moved.

There was no decision in it—that was the strange mercy of the trained body. While the guards dragged Sera toward a chair at the king's right hand, while the kitchen doors swung open on the twelfth course, Ansel stepped from the sideboard with the carver's tray in his hands, the tool of his trade, and walked toward the throne as he had walked toward it a hundred times. A taster approaching the king's plate. No one stopped him. No one ever stopped the taster. Invisibility held even now, even here, at the end of the world.

Sera's eyes met his as he drew level with her chair. One heartbeat of contact—her gaze flicking to the crown, to his hands, back to his face—and she understood. She straightened in her bonds. Beneath the torn robe, her lips began already to move, warming the first note of the song.

The final course arrived: a single dish, covered with a silver dome, borne by four cooks walking in perfect step. The creature sat back down. Its gray beard caught the light. Its mouth opened wide, wider than a mouth should open, and Ansel saw down into it, past teeth, into a darkness full of drifting lights—every stolen memory of the kingdom, suspended there like stars in a drowned sky.

Ansel set down his tray. Lifted the dome. And began, in the voice of a cook reciting a recipe, to speak the unbinding.

The creature's head turned toward him very slowly, and the whole hall's breath stopped, and the crown began, softly, to scream.
