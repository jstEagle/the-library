## 9. Unbinding

**Ansel**

The crown sat on a cushion of black velvet at the center of the high table, and it was the only thing in the hall that did not shine.

Everything else gleamed—the gold plate, the candled candelabra, the courtiers' faces stretched into their too-wide smiles—but the crown drank the light. Ansel had carried the rite past three wards to reach this room: once as a psalm folded into a recipe for honeyed quince, once as salt sewn into his hem, and once as nothing at all, because Sera had told him the last ward fed on intention and he had walked through it thinking only of supper.

Now there was only the walking forward, dish by dish, taster's tray balanced on his arm, doing the one thing he had done twelve thousand mornings until his body knew it better than prayer.

He tasted the first course. Roast fowl glazed with winter cherries. Beneath the sweetness, faint as a bruise, he felt the harvest—memories steeped into the meat like marrow, the whole court's stolen years rendered down into flavor. The creature was eating its kingdom at its own feast.

"Is it good?" asked the thing that wore the king's face.

Ansel looked up. The king's eyes were wet and dark and never blinked, and where the candlelight should have doubled itself in them there was only depth, like looking down a well with no bottom.

"It is good," Ansel said, and meant it, which was the worst part. His body had learned every poison; it had never learned how to survive wanting to weep.

He set down the fork. He reached beneath the false bottom of the tray, where the recipe lay written in Sera's hand on paper made of ash and beeswax, and he began to unfold it.

The hall went quiet. Not silent—quiet, the way a held breath is quiet. Two hundred smiling faces turned toward him all at once, and the smiles stayed fixed while the eyes above them emptied, and Ansel understood that the guests were no longer guests. They were larder. They were walls. They were the creature's body, worn the way the king's corpse was worn.

"You brought me a gift," said the crown-thing, and its voice came from everywhere now, from the candles and the floorboards and the mouths of everyone who had ever forgotten their child's name.

"I brought you a ledger," Ansel said. "Every death I survived. Every memory you tried to eat and could not hold."

He opened the page. And offered it.

* * *

**Sera**

She had sung to gods who did not answer for twenty years, so she knew how to sing into silence. That was the mercy of it, she thought wildly, standing on the table among the spilled cherries with her wrists still bleeding from the ropes they'd cut: she had practiced her whole life for a god who would not come, and now that one had come—small and furious and old, riding her voice like a hand on a plow—it was almost easy.

Almost.

The unbinding was not a spell. It was an accounting. Every verse named something true, and the truth was the blade: *You are not the king. You are not crowned. You were never chosen.* Each line cost her breath, and each line cost the creature more, and between the lines she could hear it screaming through two hundred throats at once, a chord of stolen voices all trying to be louder than hers.

But the rite needed fuel. Faith alone was kindling; it needed heartwood. It needed what Ansel was unfolding on the page before him—twelve years of swallowed deaths, every poison survived, every armored memory the thing had broken its teeth against and could not digest.

"Ansel." Her voice cracked on the second syllable of his name. "Once you give it, you can't take it back. You'll forget your grandmother's kitchen. You'll forget the valley. You may forget—"

"I know what I may forget," he said, and smiled at her across the ruined feast, and it was the smile from before everything, from the shrine steps when they were young and stupid and certain, and she hated him for it and loved him for it in the same unbearable heartbeat.

"Then let go," she said, and sang the next verse, and the candles all bent toward him like grass in wind.

* * *

**Ansel**

The memories left him the way blood leaves a wound—not all at once but in pulses, warm and unstoppable.

The creature fought. Of course it fought. It could not digest his past, so it did the next cruel thing: it made him watch it. As each memory tore free to feed the unbinding, the thing seized it first and showed it to him, magnified, lit like a festival.

His father's hands, blue-lipped on the kitchen floor, and nine-year-old Ansel sent for the midwife too late because he'd stopped to catch frogs. He let it go. The grief went with it, and worse, the love underneath the grief, and he felt the shape of his father remain without the feeling, a coat with no one in it.

The first poison. Arsenite in a wedding soup, his mouth numb for three days, learning that survival was a skill and fear was a choice. He let it go, and with it went the pride, and with the pride went the person who had been proud.

Sera, at nineteen, saying *I can't follow you into that palace, Ansel, I won't watch you die by inches,* and the door closing, and the twelve years after. He held that one a moment longer than the rest. Then he let it go too, because captivity was keeping it, and he had chosen emptiness over captivity, and emptiness meant even this—even her, even now—held loosely enough to open his hand.

The hall was coming apart around him. The courtiers were falling out of their smiles like sleepers waking, weeping, clutching at names returning to their tongues. The walls themselves seemed thinner, the candlelight honest again. Through it all Sera's song rose, ragged and triumphant, naming truths faster than the creature could swallow them.

And the crown-thing, wearing its dead king, leaned close across the table and spoke in a voice suddenly small, suddenly only a voice:

"What will you be, taster, when the ledger is blank?"

"Free," Ansel said. "Ask me tomorrow. I won't remember why it matters. That's the point."

He turned the last page. The final entry was not a poison. It was the hour of blankness, that first morning, the wine that tasted of nothing—and he gave that back too, gave the creature its own theft, and the unbinding took it like a debt collected.

* * *

**Sera**

The last verse required no words. The old god sang it through her, and it sounded like her mother's voice, and she did not fight the tears because tears were also an accounting.

At the crown itself, Ansel stood empty-handed, swaying, his face washed clean of everything she had spent twelve years missing. She caught him as the light went out of the crown—not slowly, but all at once, like a snuffed wick—and the thing beneath it had nowhere left to wear.

The king's true corpse crumbled. It had been waiting a long time, patient as dust, and now it simply agreed to be dust: the royal body folding inward, collapsing into gray powder and yellowed bone and, at the last, a small sound like a sigh of relief, as if whatever had been imprisoned inside the wearing of it finally got to stop.

The crown rolled off the velvet cushion. It rang against the flagstones—a bright, ordinary, metallic sound, the sound of any object, of a pot lid, of a coin dropped in a market—and lay still.

Silence followed. Real silence, the kind that has room in it.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere—from the ashes of the shrine, from the valley, from under every threshold in the kingdom—came singing. Small voices. Old ones. The little gods the bargain had starved, waking one by one, tentative as birdsong after a long frost.

Sera knelt in the wreckage of the feast, holding Ansel, who was breathing and blinking and looking at her with the polite puzzlement of a stranger.

"Do I know you?" he asked. "You look like someone I was supposed to remember."

Her heart broke cleanly, like bread.

"Yes," she said. "I'm going to help you remember. Or I'm going to help you plant a garden instead. We'll decide in spring."

He frowned, considering this, and then—out of habit older than memory, out of the taster's reflex that outlived everything else—he picked up the fallen crown, sniffed it carefully, and handed it to her.

"This is safe," he said. "Whatever it is."

And somewhere very near, a small god laughed.
