## 10. A Garden for the Choir

Spring came to the valley the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once.

I woke on the first warm morning to the smell of thawed earth through the open shutter, and for a moment I did not know where I was. That was not unusual anymore. There are rooms in my mind where the furniture has been sold off; I walk through them and see the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures hung, and I know only that something was there once, something mine, and that I gave it away on purpose. I have learned not to grieve the shapes. Only the absence has a shape, and it fits me like an old coat.

I rose, dressed, and did what I do every morning, which is write.

The ledger sits on the sill of Sera's kitchen window—the shrine's kitchen now, though the roof over it is new timber and the hearth is mine to tend. *Ansel Joubert,* I wrote, in the careful hand that twelve years at a king's table taught me. *Woke. Slept whole. No fevers. Ate bread, honey, the last of the salt pork.* And then, as I do every day, the list: *Nightshade — survived. Ashroot — survived. Widow's milk — survived.* Names in a column, descending like stairs into a dark I can no longer see down. I remember writing them the first time. I remember the taste of some of them still, faintly, the way a bell remembers being struck. But the deaths themselves—the fevers, the hours of twisting, the mornings I woke alive and astonished—all of that went into the rite. Every poison I had ever swallowed became a stone in the wall we built against the crown, and when the wall was finished there was nothing left of the man who had swallowed them but his handwriting.

Habit outlives need. Sera says this gently, sometimes, watching me record a breakfast no one will ever try to poison. She does not understand, or she understands too well, and so says nothing more. But here is what I have come to believe: the ledger is not a record of survivals anymore. It is a record of days. *Woke. Slept whole. Ate bread.* For twelve years I wrote to prove I was still alive against all evidence. Now I write because the mornings keep arriving, and someone ought to answer them.

* * *

The shrine above the garden is ruined, and we have left it so.

Its walls stand to about the height of a man, pale stone furred with lichen, and the old god's statue lies face-down in the grass where it fell during the unbinding, its hands broken off at the wrist. Sera will not raise it. "It isn't sleeping," she told me once, kneeling beside the fallen face, tracing the serene carved eyelids with a thumb. "It's empty. They all are. Whatever lived in them is out now." She said it the way you'd speak of birds flown from an opened cage—not grief exactly, and not quite joy either. Something between. Something that made her want to sing.

And they do sing. That is the part I could not have imagined, back when the world held only one hungry thing wearing a crown.

The small gods came back with the thaw. Not the great ones—the fallen statue's kin stay wherever they went—but little gods, nameless ones, things that had been waiting in wellsps and hedgerows and the roots of the plum trees for longer than the kingdom had been forgetting. You do not see them. You hear them, if you listen the way Sera taught me: not with the ear but with the attention, the way you hear a choir in another room. A low sweet murmur under everything. The sound water makes coming up through soil. Some evenings, working the beds, I catch myself humming along to a melody I could not transcribe if my life depended on it, and I realize I've been singing with them for an hour without knowing.

Sera says the singing is gratitude. I think it might just be relief—the sound of a valley exhaling after holding its breath for three hundred years. But she is the priestess, and I am only the taster, and I have learned to let her be right about holy things while I am right about whether the plums will take.

They will take. I checked this morning. Six of the eight whips we planted have broken bud, tight green fists against the grey bark, and the third bed is turned and raked and waiting for onions. We planted the garden over the shrine's old floor deliberately—Sera's idea, though I agreed before she finished saying it. *Let the thing that fed on memory grow things instead,* she said. *Let the ground that heard the harvest learn planting.* The first year we will eat mostly greens and failure, she warns me. I have eaten worse on purpose, for years, and been paid in coin. Being paid in food I grew myself seems an astonishing promotion.

There is a moment each morning, when I kneel in the dirt with my hands full of the cold black earth, when the ledger in my head goes entirely quiet. No lists. No columns of survived deaths. Just the smell of the soil and the far-off choir and Sera's shadow falling across me from the ruined wall, thin and long in the early light. If forgetfulness feels like this always—if it is this exact peace, this dirt under the nails—I understand now why I paid for it. I would pay again. That surprises me most of all.

* * *

Sera sings Vespers at dusk now, out loud, in the open air, for anyone who comes.

And people come. Not many yet—a handful from the nearest farms, two of the believers who walked the road with us and chose the valley over returning to their villages, an old woman who cannot say how she knew to come but arrived one Tuesday with a sack of seed potatoes and never left. They stand among the ruined walls while the light fails, and Sera leads them in the old responses, and the small gods hum underneath the human voices like a river under ice. Last week the old woman wept through the entire service, and when Sera asked her afterward what was wrong, she said, "Nothing, child. Nothing is wrong. I'd forgotten nothing could feel like this."

That is the kingdom's inheritance, I think, though the kingdom doesn't know it yet. Word travels slowly out of the valley—slowly, because the roads are still confused, the capital still reeling, the regency council still arguing over a throne no one should sit upon until they understand what thrones cost. But travelers pass through. Pilgrims, eventually, Sera says. She is rebuilding more than a garden. She is rebuilding the habit of asking, and the wilder habit of believing the asking might be answered.

As for me, I am rebuilding the habit of wanting things.

This is harder than it sounds. A taster wants nothing—that was the discipline underneath the discipline. Want led to appetite, appetite to carelessness, carelessness to death by the kitchen door. For twelve years I wanted only to survive the next dish, and when that wanting burned away in the unbinding, I found beneath it not other wants but bare floorboards. So I practice. Small things. I want the plums to take. I want rain tomorrow, gentle rain, not the hammering kind. I want Sera to laugh tonight at whatever foolishness I say at supper—and she usually does, and the wanting fulfilled does not spoil the having, which is the discovery that undoes me daily. I had believed all my life that desire was a poison to be managed. It turns out it was only ever a seasoning. Everything needs just enough, and no more, and I spent twelve years learning doses and never learned this one.

* * *

On the last morning of April, I packed a bag.

It was not a large bag. Bread, cheese, the ledger, a spare shirt, a knife for cutting apples—which is to say, a knife for eating, which is to say, a knife for living rather than defending, and I stood in the doorway of the kitchen holding it and laughed at myself, quietly, at the strangeness of carrying a weapon whose only job was lunch.

Sera was waiting at the gate with her walking staff and her shawl, the one her mother wove, the one she nearly traded away to a lie in a throne room and won back by refusing the trade. She looked at my bag, and then at me, and her eyes were bright and wet and amused all at once.

"You don't have to," she said, which was her way of saying she was afraid I wouldn't.

"I know," I said. That was the whole of it, really, the whole miracle, the entire harvest of everything we'd bought: *I know.* The valley no longer needed me inside it. The wards were dust; the crown-thing was ash and silence and a story the regency council refused to believe; the magic-dead air was just air now, ordinary air, breathable by anyone. I could leave or stay, and neither choice would kill me, and neither choice would save anyone. For the first time since I was nineteen years old and swore my first oath over a spoonful of widow's milk, my survival was nobody's project but my own.

We walked down through the garden beds, past the turned earth and the budding plums, past the fallen god's serene stone face looking up at the sky it hadn't seen in centuries. At the gate I stopped once and looked back—the shrine in its ruins, the green rows, the smoke of the kitchen fire standing straight in the still morning—and I tried to memorize it, and then I smiled at myself and didn't. Memorizing was the old work. The new work is trusting that what matters will keep, and what doesn't was never mine to hold.

The small gods sang us out. I hummed along. I did not know the tune, and it did not matter.

The road ran east toward the lowlands, toward the towns where people were learning their children's names again, toward a capital with an empty throne and a hundred thousand half-returned memories sorting themselves like a library after a flood. Somewhere out there, letters were waiting to be answered, and tables needed honest tasters—or perhaps not tasters anymore, now that nothing wore the crown. Perhaps just cooks. Perhaps just men who liked bread and honey and the company of a woman who sang.

"Ready?" Sera asked.

I took out the ledger, opened it to today's page, and wrote the day's first line standing there in the gate, the ink drying fast in the warm wind coming up from the south:

*Left the valley. Both of us. On purpose.*

Then I closed the book, took her hand, and stepped through.
