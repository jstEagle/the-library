## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Ivory

The counting house of the Vellum Guild kept its own hours, and none of them belonged to the living. Isandro Dunmore worked the night shift because the night shift was where the Guild put the things it did not want witnessed, and because bone carved more honestly by candlelight — or so Isandro told themselves, on the nights when honesty was a comfort they could afford.

The lamp above the workbench burned low and yellow. Around it, the counting house rose in shelves and ledgers, tier upon tier of them, climbing into a dark that the lamplight only made deeper. Somewhere above, a clerk's pen scratched and stopped, scratched and stopped. Below, in the vaults, the ivory slept — or pretended to.

Isandro took up the tusk section assigned for tonight's ledger-seal and set it against the vice. It was good material: dense, cream-pale, taken — *harvested*, the requisition slips said — from an elephant that had died thirty years ago in a menagerie two kingdoms south. Elephants were easy. Elephants remembered, but they forgave. It was human bone that held a grudge.

The burin touched the surface and the voice came, as it always came, soft as dust settling.

*Cold,* said the elephant. *It is cold where I am, and the man with the hook has come again.*

"I know," Isandro murmured, working the first line of the guild-sigil. "I'm sorry. It's quick work, if you don't fight it."

*Nothing is quick,* the elephant said. *Nothing has been quick since the keepers stopped coming.* A long, grinding silence, like a weight shifting in deep water. *You are not the man with the hook.*

"No. I'm the one who writes down what you were. Someone should."

The elephant had no answer for that, and neither did Isandro. This was the gift, if you could call it that — the thing that had made them useful to the Guild and ruined at everything else: bone remembered its dead, and when Isandro carved it, the dead spoke through the carving-knife as though the burin were a needle run gently under the skin of the world. Other carvers produced seals and buttons and inlay and heard nothing but their own thoughts. Isandro produced the same and heard eulogies.

The Guild had discovered this when Isandro was nine years old, and had never once let them forget the value of it.

Isandro carved the sigil's outer ring and tried, as always, not to listen. It never worked. Listening was the job. The trick — learned over twenty years, paid for in nights like this one — was to listen without agreeing to anything the dead asked for. They wanted so many things. Restitution. Revenge. To be found. To be forgotten. Once, a rib that had belonged to a drowned sailor had begged, in a voice like seawater in a broken hull, for Isandro to write a letter to a woman in a harbor town, and Isandro had done it, and the woman had come to the counting house gate with grief in one hand and a knife in the other, asking how a stranger knew the things the letter knew.

Silence for survival. That was the bargain. Carve what you're given, hear what you hear, tell no one.

The sigil finished, Isandro reached for the ledger to stamp it and paused. The ledger lay open from the day's final entries, and the last page still faced upward, and Isandro had read enough of the Vellum Guild's accounts to know that sane markets were not what these recorded.

*One (1) year, unnamed, unwept, sold by the parish of Saint Ferren's to the Honorable Company of Bellfounders.* Paid in full, in bells. The bells of Saint Ferren's rang the hour even now, and every soul in the district swore they sounded like a child crying, and every soul was right.

*Three hundred miles of coastline, leased in perpetuity, less one league reserved for the Crown's convenience.* Leased from whom? The seller column held a single word, written small and careful: *Nobody.*

And at the bottom of the page, entered in ink so fresh it still shone: *Call—*, and then a line struck through the word so hard the nib had torn the paper, and beneath the ruin, in Corvane's own hand: *Account closed. Do not reopen.*

Isandro had been reading ledgers since before they could read anything else, and they knew what a closed account looked like. They also knew that accounts only needed closing instructions when someone, somewhere, might be foolish enough to open them.

The lamp guttered. Above, the clerk's pen had gone silent.

That was when the knocking started.

Not knocking, quite — a fist dragged down wood, once, twice, the sound of someone using the last of their strength to announce themselves rather than simply dying against the door. The counting house door was iron-banded oak and opened for no one after midnight; the Guild valued its goods. The dragging came again, weaker.

Isandro set down the burin. Every instinct they owned — and twenty years in the Guild's service had bred instincts like scar tissue — said the same three things in order: *not your business, not your name, not your night.* People who came bleeding to the Vellum Guild's door came because they owed the Guild or the Guild owed them, and both categories ended badly for whoever answered.

The handle turned anyway. Someone outside had a key, then — or the door had decided for itself, which with certain doors in this building was not impossible. It swung inward over Isandro's protests to the hinges, and a man fell through the gap.

He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, dressed as a courier in travel leathers that had once been fine and were now ruined — soaked dark across the left side, one boot sole flapping loose, road-dust and worse ground into everything. He got one arm against the floor and held himself off it by main strength, and his eyes found Isandro across the room with terrible accuracy, as if he had been told exactly who would be sitting here at this hour, in this light, beside this lamp.

"Door," he said. "Locked itself. Wouldn't—" A cough bent him nearly double. When he straightened, there was blood at the corner of his mouth, bright and arterial. "You're the carver."

"I'm nobody," Isandro said, already moving — not toward him, God help them, but toward the bell-pull that would wake the house wardens, because that was procedure, and procedure was the shape survival took. "Sit still. There are people whose work this is—"

"No." He said it without raising his voice, and somehow the word crossed the room like a thrown knife. "No wardens. Listen to me. Forty years." He swallowed blood; Isandro watched the effort cost him something visible. "Forty years the interest has run. You've seen the ledgers. You know what I mean."

Isandro's hand stopped an inch from the bell-pull.

They knew what he meant. They had read the torn word an hour ago.

"They'll say you imagined it," the man went on, and now he was crawling, actually crawling, across the counting-house floor, leaving a smear on boards that the Guild would make someone scrub, "the carver hearing things, seeing things, alone at night — that's the story they'll tell, and everyone will believe it because it's easier than the truth. So take it while you can still choose to." He reached the workbench and pulled himself up its side, and from inside his coat he drew out a packet wrapped in oilskin, tied with waxed cord, stained dark at one corner. He held it out. "Find the door out. Before the date. Or we're all collected — everything the Crown stole, plus the ones who carried it. Plus you."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Isandro said, and it came out steady, which surprised them; lying to strangers was another survival skill, older than the carving. "I stamp seals. I don't—"

"You listen." His eyes were going glassy and still fixed on Isandro with the focus of a man spending his final currency. "Everyone says so. Sef, they call you, the one who listens—" He swayed. "No. That's my name. Sef. I keep forgetting. It's been a long road."

The oilskin packet stayed extended between them, trembling faintly with the effort of the arm that held it.

Everything in Isandro recoiled. This was how it happened — this was exactly how it happened; the Guild taught its history in warnings — some dying stranger hands you a secret and the secret becomes yours, and then the debt collectors come for the secret and find you attached to it like a burr. Refuse. Refuse, call the wardens, be a witness and nothing more—

"I can't take that," Isandro said. "I won't. Whatever it is, find someone who—"

"There's no one left," Sef said, simply. "I'm the last of the route. There were eleven of us when we started."

He said it the way men report weather. And then his arm gave, and instinct did what instinct does, faster than sense, faster than self-preservation: Isandro caught the packet. Their fingers closed around oilskin and cord, and the weight of it settled into their palms — heavier than paper had any right to be, warm from a dying man's body, and underneath the warmth something else, a faint hum like a struck tuning fork pressed to the teeth. Bone. There was bone in the packet. Isandro knew the feel of it through any wrapping, the way a blind woman knows her own doorkey.

"Oh, you clever bastard," Isandro whispered. "You carried it in bone."

Sef smiled. It cracked the blood at the corner of his mouth. "Knew you'd feel it. Only you would." He slid down until he sat against the workbench leg, and the strangest calm came over his face, the specific peace of a man setting down a load carried too far. "Eleven of us. I'm the last. Don't let it stop with me."

"Stay awake. Help is—" But Isandro's hand had never actually pulled the bell, and they pulled it now, and somewhere above, the wire sang, and it was already, visibly, far too late. Sef's breathing had gone shallow and spaced, each breath a separate decision. His eyes stayed on Isandro.

"The due date," he said. Words were costing him breath now, and he spent them like a miser turned spendthrift, all at once. "It comes due soon. The map knows. She wrote it all down — she wrote—" A rattle. "Ask for the clerk. The one who signs wrong. Signs too old. Promise me you'll—"

"Who?" Isandro leaned close, lamp raised. "Which clerk? What name?"

But the light behind Sef's eyes had already gone somewhere else, and the last breath he spent was not on an answer. It went out of him slowly, deliberately, like a man exhaling before stepping through a doorway, and then the counting house was very quiet, and the oilskin packet sat in Isandro's hands like a coal that would not cool.

* * *

They came for the body within the quarter-hour — two wardens and the night clerk, professional and incurious, the way people become when curiosity is a terminal condition in their employment. Questions were asked of Isandro in flat voices. Had the man spoken? No. Named anyone? No. Given them anything?

"No," Isandro said, with the packet's weight pressing its shape through the lining of their coat, against their ribs, humming its low note against bones that had never yet lied to anyone. "Nothing at all. He was dead before he finished falling."

The wardens wrote it down. The body was carried out feet-first. The smear of blood on the floor was noted for cleaning. And the night clerk, pausing at the door, looked back at Isandro with an expression that lingered half a second past professional, and said, "Master Corvane will want the account closed by morning. He's very particular about closed accounts."

Then they were gone, and Isandro stood alone among the shelves and ledgers with forty years of forbidden arithmetic breathing around them in the dark.

Slowly, they drew the packet from their coat. The cord gave way under their thumbs. Oilskin peeled back from bone and paper, and lamplight fell across both: a finger-bone, old and polished by handling, wrapped in a scrap of linen embroidered with a pattern no guild of Isandro's acquaintance had ever used — and beneath it, folded in thirds, a sheet of heavy vellum covered edge to edge in fine, furious, beautiful handwriting.

A map. Coastlines Isandro had never seen, though they had copied every chart the Guild possessed. Rivers with no names, towns marked in tiny patient script, mountains drawn with the loving precision of someone drawing a place they had carried in their heart across an ocean of forgetting. In the corner, where every honest map declared its nation, the vellum had been scraped blank — scraped hard, over and over, the way one erases a name from a ledger — and written across the scar in the same fine hand:

*Callowmere. Which does not exist. Remember it anyway.*

And below that, smaller, the ink faded as though the words had been written first and waited longest:

*The door opens at the debt's due date. Find it before they do.*

The lamp guttered again. In the vaults below, faintly, unmistakably, something shifted — a whisper running shelf to shelf through all that sleeping ivory, like wind crossing a field of grain, like ten thousand throats beginning, at last, the same word.

Isandro stood holding a country that had been erased, and understood, with the terrible clarity of the sleepless, that the erasure had just acquired their name.
