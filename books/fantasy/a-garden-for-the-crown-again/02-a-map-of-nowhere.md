## 2. A Map of Nowhere

The counting house had rules for the dead, and Isandro knew them all. Rule the first: never answer a voice before it finishes speaking, or you'll hear only what you fear. Rule the second: never carve after midnight, when the bone remembers loudest. Rule the third — the one that mattered now, with a dead man's packet burning a hole in their apron pocket like a coal that refused to cool — rule the third was: what the Guild does not know cannot be carved into your skin.

Isandro did not sleep. They sat in their narrow room above the tally-hall with the oilskin packet on the table between two guttering candles, and they did not touch it for a long time. The courier's blood had dried to a brown crust along one seam. Beneath the blood, the wax seal was broken already — Sef had opened it, then sealed it again in haste, the wax smeared with a thumbprint that was still tacky at the edges, as if he'd meant to reseal it properly and run out of time.

*Run out of time.* Isandro almost laughed. The dead were always running out of time. It was the living who kept pretending there was more of it.

They worked the packet open with a bone folder, not their fingers. Habit. Some things you didn't want touching your skin until you knew what they wanted.

Inside lay three things.

The first was a letter of passage, guild-stamped, made out to *Sef Arden, courier, third circle*, and stamped with a route number Isandro had never seen. Routes had numbers the way graves had names; this one had neither. The stamp itself was wrong — pressed in ink the color of rust and old wine, an ink Isandro knew because they ground it themselves for the master ledger. Corvane's ink. Corvane's private seal, the one he used for nothing he ever admitted to.

The second thing was a finger-bone, wrapped in linen. Human, second digit of the right hand, old — the ivory gone amber at the core, the way bone goes when its owner has been dead a long while. Isandro set it down quickly, without unwrapping it further. Even through the cloth they could feel it humming against the table, patient as a held breath. Not tonight. Tonight it would keep.

The third thing was the map.

It was drawn on vellum so fine it seemed lit from within, and it showed a country Isandro had spent forty years learning not to see. Callowmere. The name was written across it in a hand that slanted leftward, urgent, the letters leaning like people pushing against a wind — and beneath the name, smaller, in the same hand:

*the door opens at the debt's due date.*

No signature. No date. Just that, and the country itself: rivers Isandro had no memory of but which felt familiar anyway, the way a scar feels familiar. Mountain ranges with their peaks named in a language the Guild had struck from every lexicon. A capital city marked with a small, careful star, and beside the star, in that same slanting hand, a single word: *here.*

And everywhere — margins, borders, the empty spaces where cartographers usually wrote their flattery to kings — annotations. Densities of trade. Dates of shipment. Figures in a currency Isandro recognized with a lurch of the stomach, because they entered those figures into ledgers every day of their working life. Vellum Guild marks. This map was not a map of Callowmere. It was a map of what the Guild had *taken* from Callowmere, itemized by someone who had watched it happen, and who had written her accounting in the hand of a woman who must have been forty years dead.

Because that was the other thing Isandro knew, the thing that sat in their chest like swallowed ice: Callowmere did not exist. It had been erased — not conquered, not absorbed, *erased*. Forty years ago, in the reign of the old King's grandfather, every record, every map, every register and roll and parish book that named the country had been gathered up and burned, and the land it had occupied had been quietly divided among neighbors, and the word itself had been forbidden, and within a generation even the people whose grandparents had been born there had stopped saying it, because a word no one will write is a word no one can hold onto.

Isandro knew this the way they knew most terrible things: from the bones. From a rib carved once, long ago, by a frightened apprentice — themselves — that had whispered of green hills and a bell tower and a daughter's name spoken in a language that made the carving knife tremble.

They looked at the map for a long time. The candles burned lower. Somewhere below, the tally-hall clock counted out the hour in its dry bone-clicking way, and Isandro thought: *he died carrying this. He ran from wherever he came from, bleeding, and he chose my door. Of all the doors in this city, mine.*

That was either luck or design, and Isandro had survived long enough in the Guild to know that luck was just design you hadn't identified yet.

They folded the map along its original creases, wrapped the finger-bone back in its linen, and hid both — the map inside the hollowed spine of a defunct ledger from the year of the erasure itself, a ledger whose pages Corvane believed blank and which Isandro alone knew still held ghosts of ink, impressions of entries burned away, legible only if you knew how to read absence. The letter of passage they considered burning, and did not. Evidence destroyed was evidence missed. Evidence hidden was evidence owned.

Then they went downstairs, past the sleeping clerks' bunks, past the great iron door of the counting floor, and stood a moment in the dark looking at the spot on the threshold stones where Sef Arden had died. The blood had been scrubbed before dawn, per protocol. But stone remembers too, in its slow mineral fashion, and Isandro could have sworn the air above that spot was still warm.

"Who sent you?" they asked the empty hall, very quietly.

The hall said nothing. But somewhere in the walls, in the shelved bones of the archive beyond, something turned over in its long sleep, and Isandro chose to believe it was listening.

* * *

The summons came at midmorning, which meant Corvane had known about the courier before the body was cold.

Guild Master Aldous Corvane received people standing, always, in his office at the top of the tally-tower, a room with one window that looked out over the city's rooftops toward the harbor and the gray line of the sea. He was a spare man, silver at the temples, with the particular stillness of someone who had learned early that motion spends energy and information is cheaper than energy. He did not look up when Isandro entered. He was writing.

"You lost a courier last night," he said.

"I heard." Isandro kept their voice level, their hands loose at their sides. "A stranger collapsed at our door. I sent for the physic. He was dead before she arrived."

"Mm." Corvane finished his line, blotted it, set down the pen with care, as though pens were things that might be offended. "He carried something."

"He carried nothing I saw. His pockets were emptied by the physic, standard procedure. A few coins. A tin of leaf. Nothing else."

Corvane looked up then, and Isandro met his gaze the way one meets a blade: fully, without flinching, knowing that flinching is what gets you cut. Corvane's eyes were pale, the color of watered milk, and they had the quality Isandro hated most in them, which was patience. He could wait out a lie the way a river waits out a stone.

"A man runs four hundred miles," Corvane said, "bleeding from a wound in his side that any physic in the border towns would have told him was fatal, and he passes six guildhouses, six doors, some of them finer than ours, and dies at *yours*. At the door of my bone-carver." He tilted his head slightly. "You have a reputation, Dunmore. People say you can hear the dead in the ivory. Superstition, mostly. But superstition draws the desperate the way honey draws flies."

"People say many things about me, Master Corvane. Most of them are lies I started myself, because a carver with a frightening reputation is left alone."

Something moved at the corner of Corvane's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment of good craft, perhaps, from one craftsman to another.

"The physic reported the body," he said. "She also reported that the man's right hand was clenched around something before rigor took it. Something the size of a folded letter. She couldn't open the fingers." He paused. "When the fingers were finally opened, they were empty."

The silence stretched. Isandro let it stretch. In the pause they performed, inwardly, the oldest arithmetic of their life: what does he know, what does he suspect, what does he merely fear? Corvane feared much and knew little; that had always been his weakness dressed as strength. A man who trusts nothing builds a fortress with no windows, and then wonders why he can't see out.

"Rigor opens eventually," Isandro said. "Whatever he held may have fallen in the street. Or been taken by whoever found him first. The streets here are quick, Master Corvane. Quicker than physicks."

"Quick enough." Corvane crossed to the window. Below, the city went about its morning, carts and gulls and smoke, indifferent. "Forty years ago," he said, conversationally, as though remarking on weather, "this Guild financed an acquisition. A large one. The particulars are not in any ledger you've handled, and they are not going to be. But obligations of that size generate... correspondence. And correspondence requires couriers. And couriers, occasionally, develop ideas above their station." He turned back. "If such a courier were to die with such correspondence unrecovered, it would be a matter of some urgency to recover it. You understand me, Dunmore?"

"I understand that you're telling me a story without naming anything in it."

"I'm telling you that if anything surfaces — anything at all — relating to that acquisition, you will bring it to me before you bring it to anyone, including yourself." He smiled then, briefly, and it was worse than the not-smiling. "You have a talent for reading what isn't written. Apply it to what I haven't said."

Isandro bowed. "There's nothing to bring, Master Corvane. A man died at our door. The Guild lost a courier. I'm sorry for it."

At the door, Corvane spoke again, without turning around.

"Dunmore. The physic noted something else. When they finally pried the courier's hand open, there was bone dust under his fingernails. Freshly ground." Now he did turn, and the watered-milk eyes were bright. "You grind bone dust. For your inks. For your memorial work. Tell me — has anything come through your workshop lately that you can't account for?"

Every survival instinct Isandro possessed rose up screaming the same single note: *give him something.* A lie with weight in it. A truth bent just far enough to pass his inspection and no farther.

"The courier's finger," Isandro said. "Broken in the fall, or before. Bone dust happens when bone breaks, Master Corvane. You know that better than anyone — half this Guild's fortune is ground bone."

A long moment. Then Corvane nodded, once, dismissal and warning in the same gesture. "See that it stays that way."

* * *

Isandro walked back down the tally-tower stairs on legs that felt borrowed, and did not stop walking until they reached the bone-vault, three levels down, where the Guild kept its inventory of the dead and where, alone among all the rooms in the building, no one listened at doors because everyone was afraid of what the shelves might say back.

They stood among the whispering shelves with their heart hammering and made themselves name the thing they had done. They had lied to Corvane. They had lied well — the broken-finger story was elegant, unfalsifiable, exactly the kind of misdirection that had kept them alive since childhood. And lying to Corvane was not like lying to anyone else. It was a skill, yes, practiced daily, honed over decades. But each successful lie raised the price of the next one, and Corvane never forgot a debt, and Isandro had just told him a lie he would someday collect on.

*The door opens at the debt's due date.*

They thought of the annotation again, the slanted leftward hand, urgent as wind-driven rain. Whoever had written it had known the Guild's own figures, had known the route numbers, had known enough to send a dying man four hundred miles to a bone-carver's door. That was not a stranger's knowledge. That was insider's knowledge, buried deep, waiting.

Which meant someone had planned this. Someone dead, or someone pretending to be. And the plan had a hole in it the size of a grave: Sef Arden was supposed to deliver the map to *someone*, and instead he'd delivered it to Isandro, and whatever instructions he'd carried about that someone had died with him.

Unless.

Isandro unwrapped the linen bundle there in the vault, careless now of protocol, and held the amber-cored finger-bone in their palm. Old bone. Patient bone. It hummed against their skin like a trapped moth, and beneath the hum, faint as a voice through a wall, something was trying to speak.

Not tonight, Isandro had told it. But Corvane had asked his questions at midmorning, and the due date was coming whether Isandro liked it or not, and there was one question the dead could answer that no living clerk could: *who was the map meant for?*

Isandro closed their hand around the bone, took up their carving knife, and set the first edge to the ivory.

The vault went quiet. Every shelf, every whispering relic, every sleeping dead thing leaned in to listen — and through the bone, clear and cold as water rising, a man's voice began to speak.
