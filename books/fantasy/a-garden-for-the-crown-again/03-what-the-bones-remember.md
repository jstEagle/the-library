## 3. What the Bones Remember

The finger-bone arrived wrapped in waxed linen the way the Guild sent everything: with a docket, a fee schedule, and no apology.

Isandro had paid a river-rat named Coss three crowns to fish it from the canal where the courier had fallen — or been dropped; the bruising on what remained of Sef's hand said *dropped* — and now it lay on their workbench under a single lamp, small and pale as a sliver of moon. A proximal phalanx, left index. The bone of a man's pointing finger. The bone he'd have used to press the map into a stranger's hands.

Isandro did not want to carve it.

That was the truth they sat with while the lamp guttered and the counting house settled around them, beams ticking as the night cooled. Every carver learned the first rule young: *you don't have to listen*. Bone held voices the way amber held flies, but only for those who cut it. Corvane himself could hold a carved rosary all day and hear nothing but his own ambition rattling. The gift was rare, and like everything rare in Vellamont, it was taxed.

Isandro picked up the graver.

"You don't have to," they told themselves aloud, the way they always did, and then did it anyway, because refusing was also a kind of listening, and they were tired of pretending otherwise.

The first cut went shallow along the shaft of the bone, lifting a curl of ivory no bigger than an eyelash.

The room changed.

Not the room — the *light*. It went the color of late afternoon in a country Isandro had never seen, gold falling through windows that belonged to no house in Vellamont, and there was dust in it, ordinary blessed dust, turning slow.

*I'm running,* said a voice, young and winded, amused at its own fear. *I'm actually running. Sef, you absolute idiot, you swore you'd die old and fat.*

Isandro's hands kept moving. That was the trick of it — the body worked while the ear drowned.

*They came at dusk,* the voice said. *Not soldiers. Accountants. That's the joke nobody laughs at anymore. They brought ledgers. They read our names off a page and where the name was, afterward, there wasn't anything. Not rubble. Not ash. Rubble you could point to. Aunt Vesha's street is just — sky. Sky and a road that goes somewhere else now.*

The graver curled another shaving. Isandro breathed through it. Forty years dead, and the bone still remembered the afternoon light of a country that had never existed.

*He gave me the map four days before the end.* A pause in the voice, the sound of a man catching breath against a wall. *Heir-Apparent Lioran, may his name outlive the paper they burned it off of. He said: "It isn't a place, Sef. Everyone looks for a door because doors are how we leave rooms. But this one opens inward. Find her. She'll know her grandmother's hand." And I said, "Whose hand?" and he smiled like I'd already failed the test, and then the bells started.*

*Bells. We didn't even have bells. He'd rung them himself. One ring for every name he could still say aloud.*

Isandro set down the graver. Their hands were steady, which felt like a betrayal. Outside the counting-house window, Vellamont went about its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the gaslamps coming up one by one along Cordwainer Street, each flame fed by Guild money, every coin in the city passing through ledgers Isandro kept.

Ledgers that recorded, on the third day of Harvestmonth, forty years past, a single entry in Corvane's own hand:

*Received in full settlement: Callowmere. Consideration: ongoing.*

Ongoing. Interest collected annually, quietly, in things the sane market would never list. Years. Names. The color of an afternoon.

"Who is *her*?" Isandro asked the bone, and hated how their voice cracked on it.

The bone answered the only way bone answers: by remembering harder.

*

*Sef's voice, closer now, as if the shavings were piling up around Isandro's feet like snow:*

*Day two. I've stopped sleeping. You can't sleep when you're the last address in a dead country. The map rides against my ribs and I keep checking it like a second heartbeat.*

*Here's what I know, and I'll say it plain so whoever finds this bone hears it plain: the door is not in a wall. The door has a pulse. Lioran said "the heir walks free among them, hidden in the ledger's own house" — his riddles, always his riddles — but I finally understand it. The debt can't be paid in coin because coin was never what they took. They took a *country*, and a country is people, so the payment is a person. The last one. The door out of erasure opens if she walks through it willingly — or closes forever if they take her unwilling. Either way the Guild wins unless someone gets her to choose first.*

*A clerk. Of all the hiding places. She files their papers. She sharpens their pens. Somewhere in that marble tomb on Cordwainer Street there's a girl whose grandmother wrote maps, and she signs her ledgers in a hand too old for her face because she learned her letters from a dead woman's examples, and none of those ink-stained vultures ever noticed.*

*I noticed. I noticed everything. It's why I'm going to die.*

*Day three. There are men following me with Guild badges under their coats. I've led them through the tannery district twice and lost them in the smoke both times, but they don't tire the way hired men tire. They collect. I've seen what collection looks like — my aunt's dog, gone; my neighbor's memory of his wedding, gone, his wife a stranger to him by breakfast. Interest payments. Forty years of interest, and the principal comes due soon. Lioran marked the date on the map's margin. I won't write it here. Bones get found by the wrong people.*

*If you're hearing this, carver — and I know you're hearing it, the Guild doesn't hire deaf bone-carvers — I'm sorry. I'm sorry for the weight. I chose you because your window faces the door and your hands shake only when you lie. Get it to her. Tell her the door opens inward. Tell her—*

The voice broke. Not faded — broke, the way a man's voice breaks when he's hit, and the next thing the bone remembered was cobblestones seen sideways, rain in a gutter, boots coming at the speed of men who are paid by the account rather than the hour, and a hand — *his* hand, this hand, this very finger — dragging the oilskin packet out from under his coat and holding it up toward lamplight and a stranger's shadow, toward the one person in Vellamont whose window faced the guildhouse door.

*Please,* said Sef the courier, dead forty years and dying again on the cobbles, *please, you keep ledgers. Then you know what a debt is. Don't let them close the book.*

*

Isandro came back to themselves with the lamp burned to a nub and nine pale curls of ivory arranged on the bench like a row of tiny question marks. Their cheeks were wet. They hadn't noticed the crying happen, which was its own familiar horror — the body doing grief's paperwork without consulting them.

A living person. The whole time, every scholar, every smuggler, every drunk who'd ever whispered about the vanished country in the Drowned Lantern tavern — they'd all hunted for a gate, a pass, a standing stone. And the answer had walked into the Guild every morning for years, hung up her coat, and balanced columns for Corvane's enforcers.

Merrin Vale.

The name surfaced complete, which frightened Isandro more than anything else tonight. They hadn't heard it spoken. But they had *read* it — ten thousand entries across forty years of ledgers, and somewhere in that ocean, a signature. Isandro closed their eyes and let the memory rise the way they'd let Sef's voice rise: the audit of last spring, the junior clerks' register, page after page of identical schoolroom hands until—

*Merrin Vale.*

A hand too old for its owner. Loops with a flourish nobody taught anymore, the descenders cut short the way scribes cut them when ink was dear — ink-economy habits from forty years ago and more, fossil handwriting, a style buried with the country that invented it.

She signed her ledgers in Callowmere's hand. In her grandmother's hand. Every single day, in front of Corvane, and he never saw it — because Corvane read numbers, not letters, and because guilt makes men illiterate.

Isandro looked down at the nine shavings of Sef's finger-bone. Memorial pieces, they'd promised Coss. Little charms, sold quiet, money to the family Sef probably didn't have. That had been the plan. That had been the *excuse*.

Now the shavings were something else. Evidence. A dead man's testimony, curl by curl.

They thought about Corvane's summons two nights ago, the Guild Master's soft voice behind the black desk: *"What did the courier carry, Dunmore?"* And the lie Isandro had told with steady hands — *nothing, Master, he was empty-handed, he died reaching for his purse* — and how Corvane had smiled and said *of course*, and how Isandro had known, walking out, that the smile meant the enforcers were already being counted out of the strongroom.

They would find the canal. They would find Coss. They might already be asking after anyone who'd bought river-finds this week.

There was no time to carve memorials. There was barely time to think.

But Isandro was a keeper of ledgers, and a ledger-keeper knows the oldest rule of accounts: a debt ignored does not shrink. It accrues. Forty years of interest had made this one monstrous — a whole country's worth of stolen afternoons, names, bell-towers, gold light — and tomorrow, or next week, some clerk would draw the line under it and call the balance due.

Unless someone contested the entry.

Isandro swept the shavings into a tin, wrapped the rest of the finger-bone in its linen, and pulled a fresh sheet of audit paper toward them. At the top, in their neatest official hand, they wrote: *Routine verification of junior clerk registers — Isandro Dunmore, Assistant Keeper.*

An audit. Clerks couldn't refuse an audit. Corvane encouraged them; thoroughness was piety, in the Guild.

And tomorrow morning, in the long gallery of desks beneath the portraits of every Guild Master back to the founding, Isandro would stand behind a girl with ink-stained fingers and watch her sign her name, and see whether the loops matched a dead woman's maps.

Outside, the gaslamps hissed. Somewhere across the city, boots were moving through the rain, asking questions in soft voices, collecting.

Isandro blew out the lamp and began, in the dark, to sharpen a pen.
