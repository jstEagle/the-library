## 4. The Clerk with Two Handwritings

Isandro spent two days learning Merrin Vale before daring to speak to her.

It was not difficult. The counting house ran on ledgers the way a body runs on blood, and every ledger passed through the clerks' gallery on the third floor, where forty desks stood in rows beneath windows of thick green glass that turned everyone's face the color of drowned things. Merrin Vale sat at the fourth desk from the stair, and Isandro learned her the way they had learned to read bone: by what she did not do.

She did not gossip. The other clerks traded rumors like small coins, but Merrin's pen moved in silence, hour after hour, her spine straight as a ruled line. She did not look up when the enforcers came through on their rounds — and Isandro noted that too, the way her shoulders stayed loose while every other clerk's tightened. Either she was very brave or very practiced at seeming harmless.

And she signed her entries twice.

Isandro saw it first in the margin of a shipping reconciliation: a small initialing beside each figure, *M.V.*, done in a hand so fine and old-fashioned it might have come off a tombstone. Then, when the page was countersigned at day's end, the same initials again — but broader, younger, the loops of the V squared off like a clerk who had been taught to write quickly and recently. Two hands. One person.

Isandro knew about two handwritings. They had kept their own for thirty years: the child's careful script they'd used to forge records during the purge, and the adult's hand they used now, and never once had they let the two appear on the same page.

*The dead give one clue,* Sef's voice had said, from inside the finger-bone Isandro wore against their sternum these days, warm as a swallowed coal. *A clerk named Merrin Vale. She signs in a hand too old for her.*

The dead had not lied. But the dead also could not tell Isandro whether Merrin Vale was the door, or merely the door's keeper, or bait Corvane had already salted and set.

There was only one way to find out, and it required paper.

* * *

Merrin Vale knew something was wrong before the summons reached her desk, because the summons was polite.

In eleven years at the Vellum Guild, no one had ever summoned Merrin politely. Audits arrived as reprimands; inquiries arrived as ambushes; the Master's office communicated exclusively through people who avoided your eyes. But the note that landed beside her inkwell on Tuesday morning was written in Isandro Dunmore's own hand — she recognized it from the colophons in the master ledgers, the small proud flourish on the final *e* — and it said: *Reconciliation of the Ashcombe freight accounts, discrepancies noted. Please attend the vault annex at the second bell. Bring nothing.*

Bring nothing. Not even a defense.

She went anyway, because refusing an audit was how clerks disappeared into the provincial houses, and because — though she would have bitten through her own tongue before admitting it — the name Dunmore had been sitting in her chest for three days like a stone swallowed whole. Ever since the courier died at the counting-house door. Ever since she'd heard the enforcers asking, in the low voices they thought clerks couldn't hear, *what did he carry, who did he pass it to.*

Ever since she'd dreamed, three nights running, of her grandmother's handwriting.

The vault annex was a room most clerks never saw: a low stone chamber off the bone-vault stairs, lit by one lamp, its walls lined with pigeonholes of rolled vellum. Isandro Dunmore stood at the central table with a single ledger open before them, and Merrin's first thought was that they looked like a knife someone had tried to make out of a person — all narrow lines and quiet edges, gray-eyed, watchful, their left hand resting flat on the table as if holding down something that wanted to fly.

"Sit," they said.

"I'd rather stand."

Something flickered across their face that was almost approval. "As you like." They turned the ledger toward her. "Ashcombe freight. Forty-one entries over six months. Every one of them initialed here—" their finger tapped the margins, "—and countersigned here. Two different hands."

"The second is my end-of-day signature. It's required."

"The second is *yours*. The first is not." Isandro's voice was level, unhurried, terrible in its mildness. "The first is a copperplate hand. Old style. The kind they taught in chancery schools forty years ago, and haven't taught since, because the chancery schools were all in Callowmere."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the stair. Merrin did not move. Eleven years of practice held her face still, held her breath even, held her voice exactly bored when she said:

"You've made an error. I learned my letters from my mother, here in the city. If you're accusing me of forgery—"

"I'm not accusing you of anything." Isandro closed the ledger. "I'm telling you that I audit ledgers for a living, and I know what I'm looking at, and so do you. And now we're both going to stand here in this room while you decide whether to keep lying, because I have all night, and you should know that the enforcers don't."

"They're not looking for me."

"No," Isandro agreed softly. "They're looking for a courier. A dead man named Sef Arden, who came to the counting house four nights ago carrying an oilskin packet, and who pressed it into my hands while he bled out on the step. They don't know yet that he's dead. When they find out, they'll want to know what he carried. And when they trace his route — and they will, Corvane traces everything — they'll find that his last living stop before the city was a waystation in the hill country, where the keeper's daughter remembers him buying bread and asking after work, and where the guest register bears a signature in a fine old copperplate hand that says *E. Vale.*"

The silence went on so long that Merrin could hear the building around them: the tick of the great clock above, the whisper of the archive stacks settling, and beneath it all — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable if you knew to listen — a sound like many voices breathing in the dark below the floor.

Her grandmother's name. In her grandmother's hand. In a register in the hills, four decades after her grandmother had died in a country that had never existed.

"That's impossible," Merrin said, and heard her own voice crack on the second syllable, and hated herself for it.

"Yes," Isandro said. "Most true things are."

* * *

Corvane received the report at midnight, standing at the window of his tower study with his back to the room, watching the city burn its small lights against the dark.

"The courier's body hasn't surfaced," said Vess, the senior enforcer, from the doorway. He was a man built like a filing cabinet and nearly as expressive. "No river recovery, no potter's field intake, no guild morgue entry. Which means either he left the city alive—"

"He didn't."

"—or someone made him disappear quietly. Quietly costs money. Money leaves marks." Vess consulted his slate. "We pulled the tollhouse registers along the north road. His route checks out to the last mile — except the waystation at Harrow Fen. Keeper's daughter swears he stopped there. Register shows no entry under his description."

"But?"

Vess hesitated. Corvane did not turn around, but the quality of his stillness changed, the way water changes just before it freezes. "But there's an older entry. Same night, earlier hour. A name in a hand the toll-archive clerks date to the old chancery style. *E. Vale.* We're pulling every Vale in the guild rolls now. There are nine. One of them is a third-floor clerk."

For the first time, Corvane moved. He crossed to his desk, opened a drawer with a small brass key he wore on a chain against his skin, and took out a sheet of paper so old it had gone the color of weak tea. On it, in a fine copperplate hand, was a column of figures — grain shipments, arms contracts, sums paid to men whose names had been struck through afterward, all of it annotated in the margin with a single word in the same elegant script: *Callowmere.*

"My grandmother's accountant wrote like that," Corvane said, almost fondly. "They all did, in the old days. Beautiful hands. Hands built for signing away nations." He returned the paper to the drawer and locked it. "Dunmore has the packet. Dunmore is clever, and clever people go to the archives when they're frightened. Put watchers on the third-floor gallery and the vault stairs. And Vess — if the Vale girl meets with anyone, anyone at all, I want to know within the hour. Don't take them. Follow them. A debt collector's first rule: never spook the debtor before you've counted what they're worth."

"And if they run?"

Corvane smiled at the window, at the city, at the dark country that wasn't there beyond it. "Then we'll learn something valuable about where they think they can go."

* * *

In the vault annex, Merrin finally sat down.

Not because she had decided anything, but because her legs had decided for her, and because Isandro had pushed a stool across the stones with one foot in a gesture that was, absurdly, gentle.

"Say it plainly," she said. "What do you want from me?"

"The truth. Just once, from the source." Isandro leaned forward, lamplight catching the hollows of their face. "Your grandmother's name. Where she was born. What happened to her."

"She was born in Ashcombe," Merrin said. "She raised me. She died when I was nine." Each fact was true, and each was a wall, and behind the walls was the thing she had never said aloud in her life, the thing her grandmother had made her swear to on her deathbed with a grip that left bruises: *Never write it. Never say it. A country that isn't remembered can't be hunted.*

But her grandmother had also taught her the copperplate hand. Had drilled her in it at the kitchen table, evening after evening, saying *a hand is a fingerprint, child, and yours must be able to pass for mine* — and Merrin had never understood why, until the dreams started, until the courier died, until tonight.

Isandro watched her not-answer, and then did something Merrin did not expect. They reached into their coat and laid something on the table between them: a finger-bone, carved all over with fine spiraling script, mounted on a cord.

"This belonged to Sef Arden," they said. "He was sworn to the heir-apparent of Callowmere. He spent his last year carrying messages for a country that doesn't exist, and he died on our doorstep trying to deliver the last one. I can hear him. It's my — gift. My curse. Whatever it is. The ivory remembers its dead." Their voice roughened, just slightly. "He asked me to find the door out. He said the door is a person. And every thread I pull leads to a clerk with two handwritings who signs her ledgers in a dead woman's script."

Merrin stared at the bone. From somewhere far below the floor, through the stone, through her boots, through the marrow of her, she felt — or imagined she felt — a pressure, like a crowd leaning close to listen.

"If I tell you," she said slowly, "what happens to me?"

"Tonight? Nothing. Tonight I'm the only one who knows, and I'm not Corvane." Isandro's mouth twisted. "Tomorrow is a different arithmetic. There are enforcers pulling the guild rolls right now — I watched them do it. Nine Vales. By morning there'll be watchers on your desk."

Merrin thought of her rooms on Candle Lane, of the loose floorboard under the bed where a bundle of letters lay wrapped in oilcloth, forty years old, in her grandmother's hand. Letters from a country that had never existed. Letters that would hang her.

"I didn't deny it fast enough," she said at last, and it was almost a laugh. "My grandmother always said I would be bad at lying. She said honest people lie too late."

"Mine said the same thing," Isandro said quietly. "About me."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the lamp burned between them, and below their feet the dead breathed in the dark.

"The map," Merrin said. "The courier's packet. You have it."

Isandro hesitated — and then, deliberately, let her see them hesitate, because they were tired of being a knife and because some instinct older than caution told them that trust, once faked, could not be un-faked. They drew the oilskin packet from their coat and set it beside the bone.

Merrin unwrapped it with hands that shook only a little. The map inside was drawn on calfskin, faded, annotated in a fine copperplate hand — her grandmother's hand, unmistakable, the hand that had taught her hers — and across the bottom border, in ink that had gone brown with age, a single line:

*The door opens at the debt's due date.*

Merrin read it three times. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious and more awake than they had been in eleven years of straight-backed silence.

"Fourth desk from the stair," she said. "Eleven years. I hid in plain sight in the den of the people who erased us, and I never knew why my grandmother wanted me there, and now—" She stopped. Pressed her palms flat against the table. "The due date. Do you know when it falls?"

Isandro nodded once. "Nine days."

Above them, muffled by floors of stone and vellum, the great clock began to strike the third bell — and beneath its sound, from the shelves of the bone-vault below, rose a whisper that both of them heard, and neither of them would admit to hearing until much later: hundreds of voices, in an old chancery accent, saying a single word.

*Soon.*
