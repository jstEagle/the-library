## 5. Debt Collectors

The knock came at the wrong hour and with the wrong weight.

Merrin Vale knew the counting house's rhythms the way she knew the backs of her own hands — the soft double-tap of the night auditors, the impatient heel-drum of the ledger runners, the polite scratch of the Guild's own clerks returning from supper. This was none of those. This was a fist, delivered flat and deliberate, three times, with a pause between each strike as though the knocker were counting her heartbeats for her.

She had been copying interest accruals by candlelight, and her pen did not stop moving. That was the first discipline of the Vellum Guild: whatever happens, the hand keeps working. A clerk who froze was a clerk with something to hide.

"Enter," she said, in the bored voice she had practiced for eleven years.

The door opened without her permission. Three men came through it the way water comes through a failed dam — not fast, but total. They wore the grey livery of the enforcers, sashes knotted at the left hip, and the one in front carried a folded paper he did not offer to her. He simply stood in her doorway, in her small room above the archive annex, and let his eyes do the ransacking first.

"Clerk Vale." He had a voice like a ledger line: flat, ruled, no room for annotation. "Master Corvane requires an inventory of your effects."

"At this hour?" She set down her pen with a small, deliberate click. "The audit season ended a fortnight ago. If there's a discrepancy in my columns, you may bring it to me in the morning and I'll reconcile it before the second bell."

"There's no discrepancy." The paper unfolded. "A courier died at the counting house four nights ago. The bone-carver Dunmore was the last to see him. Dunmore has since fled. We are tracing the courier's route backward, room by room, contact by contact. You signed for a delivery from the eastern post three days before he died."

"I sign for forty deliveries a week."

"You signed for one from the east."

Merrin kept her face in the register she used for tax assessments — attentive, faintly weary, entirely empty. Inside, her heart was doing arithmetic she hated: *they know the direction, they don't know the name, they don't know the map, they don't know me.* Yet.

The map was not in her room. She had sewn it into the spine of a defunct ledger years ago — no. Not years. Four nights. Four nights since she had found the calligraphy on a scrap Isandro Dunmore had let her glimpse, four nights since her hands had stopped being entirely her own. The map lay twenty feet below her floor, in a ledger shelved among eleven thousand others, wearing a false title in her own forged hand. She had told herself it was the safest place in the Guild.

She watched the enforcers' hands now, and understood she had told herself a lie.

"Stand away from the desk," the flat-voiced one said.

They went through her room the way Corvane's men went through everything — not searching so much as *disproving*. The mattress slit along its seams. The candlesticks weighed in the palm. Her spare clerical robes turned out pocket by pocket, her shoes pressed at the toe, her hairbrush examined for a hollow that did not exist. One of them took down the little framed sampler above her bed — *A PLACE FOR EVERY LEDGER, A LEDGER FOR EVERY PLACE*, stitched by her grandmother's hands in a country that no longer existed — and held it up to the candle as though the cloth might turn translucent.

"Careful," Merrin said, and was proud of how flat her voice stayed. "Guild property is charged against my wages if damaged."

The enforcer looked at her. For a moment something moved behind his eyes — not sympathy, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who had done this forty times tonight and was beginning to hear how it sounded.

He hung the sampler back on its nail.

"Where were you four nights ago, between the sixth and ninth bells?"

"In the annex, closing the quarter's salt-tariff columns. The night auditor can confirm it."

"The night auditor says he cannot."

The candle flame leaned. Merrin felt the room tilt with it. They had bought the auditor, or frightened him, or he had simply folded the way paper folds — and now there was no witness between her and whatever came next, and the flat-voiced man was opening his mouth to say the thing that came next, when the bell above the annex began to ring the fire-hour.

It was not the fire-hour.

The enforcers moved to the window. Below, in the yard between the annex and the great counting house, orange light stuttered against the walls — a brazier overturned, perhaps, or something set deliberately, because someone in this building understood that enforcers were dogs on a scent, and a dog could be pulled off a scent by a brighter one.

Merrin did not think. Thinking was for people with time. She dropped through the memory of her own training the way a clerk drops through a ledger to the root column: the panel behind the wardrobe, the crawl-way built for pipe-access a century ago and never once marked on any plan, because the Guild marked nothing it did not intend to tax.

She was into the wall before the first of them turned around.

* * *

The archive stacks at night were a country of their own, and Merrin was one of perhaps six living people who knew its borders.

She moved through them by touch and count. Nine shelves, turn left at the gap where the Hereditary Claims had been pulped — the pulped section was the trick, the gap exactly wide enough for a body, and she had discovered it at nineteen and told no one, because a secret passage you tell no one about is a door, and a door you tell everyone about is a wall. She went down the maintenance stair with its rope handrail worn to silk, past the climate-vaults where the Guild kept its oldest instruments of extraction — the salt ledgers, the tithe rolls, the bound confessions of three generations of debtors — and came at last to the iron door at the very bottom, the one whose lock she had picked twice before and sworn, both times, never to pick again.

The bone-vault breathed cold up at her, and it breathed voices.

She had heard them before, faintly, on her two previous descents — a sibilance like wind through reeds, which she had told herself was the ventilation. Tonight, with a map of a murdered country sewn into a ledger under her arm, the sibilance resolved into words.

She stood in the doorway and let it.

The vault was long and low, shelved in black oak, and every shelf held bone. Not raw bone — *worked* bone. Combs and buttons, dice and flute-stops, the handles of Guild seals, the inlay of counting tables, all of it carved from human remains and all of it catalogued, because the Vellum Guild catalogued everything. Isandro Dunmore's craft, she thought. The bone they carve speaks with the voices of its dead. She had thought it a guild legend, the kind clerks traded over supper.

The voices were saying numbers.

Not all of them. But enough. A flute-stop murmured *four hundred souls, assessed at nine crowns the head.* A seal-handle recited, in a woman's patient voice, *the eastern farms surrendered in the third week; the yield exceeded projection.* A set of dice, rattling faintly in their box with no hand near them, kept repeating a child's counting rhyme in an accent Merrin had not heard since she was six years old, an accent that had been erased from every map and every record and every tongue except the one her grandmother had used to sing her to sleep.

Callowmere.

Her legs took her down the aisle without consulting her. She read the shelf-labels as she went, and the labels were worse than the voices: *Callowmere campaign, year one — receivables.* *Callowmere campaign, year two — consolidation.* *Callowmere, year three — dissolution and interest schedule.* Forty years of interest schedules. The Guild had not merely profited from the erasure. The Guild had *financed* it — advanced the Crown the cost of the soldiers, the cost of the burning, the cost of the new maps — and had been collecting the interest ever since, quietly, in columns so dull no auditor had ever flagged them, on a debt whose principal was an entire nation.

*We are still collecting,* said a bone paperknife, in a man's voice worn down to a thread. *They think the war ended. We are still collecting.*

Merrin put her hand against the shelf to steady herself, and every piece of worked bone within reach went silent — then, softly, all together, turned toward her attention like flowers toward light. She felt them *looking*. She felt them recognize something in her, the way the dice had recognized the rhyme.

*The heir,* whispered a comb. *The heir is in the building.*

"Be quiet," she breathed. "Be quiet, be quiet—"

"She's here," said the flute-stop, and it was not a warning. It was a relief. Forty years of interest, forty years of numbers, and the dead had been waiting the way unpaid debts wait — patiently, and without any intention of forgetting.

"Merrin."

She spun. Isandro Dunmore stepped out of the dark end of the vault, candle in hand, their face hollowed and grey. They looked as though they had been living down here for days. Perhaps they had. They looked at the ledger under her arm, and then at the shelves, and then at her, and something in their expression broke and reformed into a terrible clarity.

"You've heard it too," they said. "What they did. What we all did — every column, every quiet interest payment, every clerk who balanced the books and never asked what the books were *for*." Their voice cracked. "I balanced those books for twenty years, Merrin. I thought I was keeping accounts. I was keeping *score*."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone and shelving, boots began to move across the annex floor. Many boots. The enforcers had found the overturned brazier, and the fire had bought her an hour, and the hour was spent.

"They'll search the vault," Isandro said. "Corvane will want the map, and Corvane will want *you*, and I am beginning to understand — from what the bones are saying, and they have not stopped saying it since I came down here — that those are the same want." They lifted the candle. "There's a way out through the salt-archives. It's not a good way. It's a way."

Merrin looked back once at the shelves — at the dice still counting their rhyme, at the paperknife still whispering its collections — and understood, with a horror that did not feel new so much as *confirmed*, that she had spent her whole life working for the men who had done this, and that the ledger under her arm was the only receipt of the theft in existence.

"Then we go," she said, "and we take the accounts with us."

Behind them, at the top of the stair, the iron door began to open.
