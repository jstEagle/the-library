## 7. Where the Country Went

The marsh did not want them.

Isandro felt it in the way the ground gave beneath each step, a soft refusal, like a hand withdrawing from a handshake. Three days they had walked out of the Guild's reach, following the map's faded ink through salt grass and channels that appeared and vanished with the tide, and still the land seemed to lean away from them, as if it remembered what had been done at its edge and wanted no part of their return.

Merrin walked ahead with the oilskin packet strapped against her chest. She had not spoken in an hour. She spoke less each day, Isandro had noticed, as though the country she was walking toward were drawing the words out of her, saving them up for some accounting of its own.

The border-stone, when they found it, was not where the map said it would be. The map said *at the old road's end, past the third channel*. The old road was gone — drowned, silted over, its paving stones lying loose and crooked six inches under the black water like teeth knocked from a jaw. But the stone itself stood where no stone should have survived forty years of tide: upright, unweathered, its carved face still sharp enough to read.

*CALLOWMERE,* said the stone. And below, in smaller letters: *HERE THE CROWN BEGAN TO COUNT.*

Merrin put her hand flat against the carving. "It's cold," she said. "In summer."

"The dead are holding it," Isandro said.

She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed but dry; she had stopped crying somewhere on the second day, Isandro thought, the way people stop bleeding when there is nothing left to lose. "You say things like that so easily now."

"I've spent my whole life listening to bones. It was never easy. I just got good at pretending." Isandro crouched at the waterline, where the marsh pooled dark and mirror-still despite the wind moving the grass behind them. Their reflection looked back, and beside it — faint, like breath on glass — another shape. Sef's voice came thin through the water, thinner than it had been since his finger-bone had gone quiet outside the archive:

*Near now. They'll come near. What you see, see all of it. That's what we ask. That's all we ever asked.*

"What do you mean, all?" Merrin asked. "All of what?"

The water answered her.

It rose without wind — a slow swell, a lifting, as though the whole marsh had inhaled — and from it they came. Not lunging. Not grasping. They rose the way reeds rise after a flood recedes, patient and vertical and inevitable: shapes in the black water, dozens of them, then hundreds, standing in every channel and pool as far as the flats ran, faces pale as birch bark, clothes heavy with four decades of drowning. A woman with a ledger-clerk's ink-stained cuffs. A boy holding a wooden horse by one leg. Old men in Callowmere grey. Soldiers whose uniforms Isandro recognized from the purge — not Callowmere's soldiers, but the Crown's, drowned here too, because the water had not cared whose side they'd counted for.

Merrin made a sound that was half her grandmother's name.

"They're not going to hurt us," Isandro said, and heard their own voice shake. "Sef told me. They don't want anything taken. They want something *seen*."

The nearest figure raised one dripping hand, and the world folded like a page.

* * *

Merrin had expected darkness. What came instead was light — lamplight, warm and ordinary, the light of a street she knew from her grandmother's stories though she had never walked it. Vellin Row, in the capital of Callowmere, forty years ago, on the last night the country existed.

She stood inside the memory as if inside a room. The dead moved around her and did not see her; this was a thing being shown, not a place she could touch. She watched her grandmother — younger than Merrin herself was now, laughing, carrying a basket of late plums — pass a shop window where a man was pricing ivory combs. She watched children run chalk games across cobbles. She watched an ordinary night begin.

Then the ledgers arrived.

They came in carts, escorted by men wearing the Vellum Guild's grey-and-gilt, and the clerks who followed them wore the same livery Merrin had worn every working day of her adult life. She watched them set up tables in the square. She watched them open enormous books and begin, calmly, methodically, to write — and wherever the pen wrote, the thing written ceased to be. A name crossed out became a house with no owner. A deed struck through became land that belonged to no one, which meant it belonged to the Crown. Street signs unhooked themselves and fell. The chalk games faded from the cobbles mid-hop.

And the people — the people went gently, because they had already been made into a debt. She watched her grandmother argue with a guild clerk, waving a document, shouting about title and birthright, and she watched the clerk look at her not with cruelty but with the mild patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a child: *You were collateral. The Crown borrowed against you thirty years ago. The note has come due. This is simply collection.*

"That's not how countries work," her grandmother said.

"It is exactly how countries work," the clerk said. "It is the only way they have ever worked. Someone pays. We only keep the books."

The memory shuddered. Fires began at the edges of it, and the dead around Merrin in the present turned their faces away, even now, even dead, unable to watch what came next. Merrin forced herself to watch all of it. *See all of it,* Sef had said. *That's all we ever asked.* So she saw the ships, and the roads emptied by decree, and the moment when the last Callowmere word was spoken aloud on Vellin Row and the sound of it went wrong in the air, like a bell with a crack in it.

When the vision released her she was on her knees in the salt water, sobbing, and Isandro's hand was on her shoulder, and the border-stone's carving had changed. Where the old letters had been, new ones showed, wet and gleaming, as if the stone itself had just been cut:

*THE DOOR OPENS ONLY FOR ONE WHO ENTERS THE LEDGER WILLINGLY. A DEBT PAID BY FORCE IS NOT PAID. IT IS ONLY MOVED.*

"No," Merrin said. She understood immediately, completely, the way you understand a blow before it lands. "No. That's why he needs me alive. That's why Corvane hasn't just—"

"He can't force you," Isandro said quietly. "He has to make you willing. Or make someone willing in your place."

Across the flats, behind them, torchlight.

* * *

Isandro counted the lights and hated how good they were at counting. Nine torches, moving in disciplined pairs across the causeway — enforcers did not travel in crowds, Corvane's doctrine said, because crowds suggested urgency and urgency suggested fear. Nine was a statement of confidence. Nine said *we know exactly where you are and exactly what you're worth.*

"Merrin." They pulled her up from the water. Her face was wrecked and resolute at once, and Isandro recognized the expression because they had worn it once themselves, as a child of eleven, signing false entries in a Guild ledger while their parents' names were still wet ink on the true page. "We can't outrun nine across open flats. We need the water."

"The water is full of them."

"The water is full of *witnesses*, and Corvane's people have spent forty years making sure nothing they do gets witnessed." Isandro took the packet from her hands and held the map open against the border-stone. In the dying light the annotations glowed faintly, the dead woman's hand steady even now: *the door opens at the debt's due date.* "When is the due date?"

"Tomorrow," Merrin whispered. "Midnight tomorrow. Grandmother's annotation — the numerals — I worked it out on the second night. Forty years to the day since the collection." She laughed, a terrible small sound. "We walked three days through a swamp to arrive one day early. All that time, and we're *early*."

"Then we have one day, and nine reasons to spend it in the deepest channel we can find." Isandro looked at the dead standing silent in the shallows, hundreds of them, patient as the stone itself. "Will they shelter us?"

Sef's voice rose one last time from the water, fainter now, a candle burned down to its nub:

*We hid once while they wrote us out. We will not hide again. But we will stand between you and the pens for as long as standing lasts. Go into the deep channel. And child — when midnight comes, whatever Corvane offers you, whatever bargain, remember: a door opens inward. Whoever enters must walk in wanting to.*

The torches were close enough now that Isandro could hear boots on the causeway stones. One of the enforcers called out, a voice like a filed hook: *"Guild property! Surrender the map and the heir, and the Guild will honor your accounts!"*

*Honor your accounts.* As if everything — the marsh, the dead, the two of them — could be settled with an entry in a book.

Isandro took Merrin's hand and stepped backward off the causeway into the black water, and the dead of Callowmere closed around them like a country remembering how to hold its own.
