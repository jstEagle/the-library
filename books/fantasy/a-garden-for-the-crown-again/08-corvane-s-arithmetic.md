## 8. Corvane's Arithmetic

They did not bind Isandro's hands.

That was the first cruelty of it — the courtesy. Six enforcers had taken them at the edge of the salt marshes, mud to the knee, Merrin's name still raw in their throat, and not one of them had touched a wrist or ankle. They simply formed a ring and walked them inland, patient as tide, because Guild Master Corvane had sent word ahead: *Bring the bone-carver gently. Broken tools cut crooked.*

So Isandro rode to the counting house in an open cart, hands free in their lap, watching the marsh fall away behind them and understanding that freedom of the fingers meant nothing when every road belonged to the man at the end of them.

The Vellum Guild's counting house rose out of the lowlands like a ledger standing upright — grey stone, narrow windows, a roofline ruled straight as a margin. Isandro had worked beneath that roof for twenty years. They knew which stair creaked on the fourth step, which cellar held the tallow stores, which window in the scriptorium caught the last honest light of the day. Now the enforcers walked them through the front door, past clerks who would not lift their eyes from their columns, and Isandro understood they were being shown off. A warning written in flesh: *this is what happens to ledgers that walk out of the building.*

* * *

Corvane received them in the map room.

Isandro had never been invited into it before; few had. It sat at the top of the counting house, round-walled, lit by a single lantern, and its floor was not stone but paper — great sheets of vellum laid and lacquered over one another until the whole room was a palimpsest of borders, every kingdom and province and disputed salt-flat the Guild had ever measured, bought, or unmade. Corvane stood at the center of it with his boots on the sea.

He was smaller than his reputation. That was the trick of him, Isandro thought — men expected a giant to hold a country's debts, and got instead a neat, dry, silvering clerk with ink stains permanent as birthmarks on three fingers. He poured two cups of wine without asking whether Isandro wanted one.

"You left your desk," Corvane said. "Eleven days. Your column is behind."

"The courier died at my door," Isandro said. "I went to bury him properly."

"Did you." Corvane handed over the cup. His eyes were mild and bottomless, the eyes of a man who had read so many figures he no longer saw people as anything other than entries. "We recovered what remained of him. Most of him. The finger was missing — the index, right hand. Curious thing, a missing finger."

Isandro drank. The wine was very good and tasted like nothing at all.

"You carved something from it," Corvane said. "Our enforcers found shavings in your kit. Bone dust in the seams of your gloves. You've never carved outside the vaults before, Isandro. In twenty years you have kept every rule I set for you. So tell me — what made this piece different?"

*Lie well,* Isandro told themselves. *You have practiced all your life.* But the room was paper under their feet, and every word spoken on it, Isandro suspected, stayed written somewhere.

"He asked me to," they said. "With his last breath. He pressed a packet into my hands and said *carry it*, and then he said *remember me*, and a bone-carver does not refuse the dead. You know that better than anyone. It's why you keep me."

Something moved behind Corvane's mildness — not anger. Appetite.

"A packet," he repeated. "And where is this packet now?"

"Gone," Isandro said. "Burned, before the marshes. Whatever he carried, it's ash."

Corvane turned away from them then, slowly, and looked down at the paper sea beneath his feet. When he spoke again his voice had changed — softer, almost fond, which was infinitely worse.

"Do you know why we are called the Vellum Guild? Not the Counting House. Not the Exchange. Vellum." He crouched and pressed two fingers flat against the lacquered floor. "Because vellum is skin, Isandro. Every sheet in this room was once alive. And everything written on skin can be scraped away and written anew. That is our trade. We do not sell goods. We sell *forgetting*. And we buy remembering back at ruinous interest."

He straightened.

"Forty years ago, a client nation defaulted. You will not find its name in any of our ledgers — I made certain of that myself, with my own knife, sheet by sheet. But debts do not vanish because the debtor's name does. The debt remains. It accrues. It waits." He faced Isandro again. "The courier carried the proof of it. A map. Annotations in a dead woman's hand. Am I warm?"

Isandro said nothing. Silence, too, was a survival skill.

"I thought so." Corvane sighed, and gestured, and an enforcer opened a case at the wall and brought out — Isandro's heart stopped — an oilskin packet, water-stained, salt-crusted. Empty. "Recovered from your camp at the border-stone. You burned a copy. A kindness — I assume you burned a *copy*." He smiled thinly. "It doesn't matter. I have had forty years to learn what's written there. What I lacked was the due date. The dead woman wrote it in cipher, and ciphers require either the key or the keyholder."

* * *

He walked Isandro then — across the paper world, around the curve of the room — and told them the arithmetic.

"The erased country owed us, at the time of erasure, a sum beyond its means to pay. Erasure was our remedy: strike the debtor, seize the collateral, close the account. Clean. Except the account refused to close. Do you know why?" He didn't wait. "Because the dead keep books too. Every soul we scraped from the maps went into *their* ledger, and theirs says the Guild owes. One life per life, one field per field, one bell-tower per bell-tower. Interest compounding for forty years. There is only one payment that settles a debt like that honestly, Isandro, and it is a payment no guild can survive making. So we must pay dishonestly. As one does."

"And the door," Isandro said. Their voice came out level. They were almost proud of that.

"The door." Corvane nodded, pleased, as if Isandro were a promising junior clerk finally grasping compound interest. "The dead woman's instruction. The account closes only if someone enters the ledger willingly — a willing payment seals it forever. Not seized. Not murdered. *Given*. The distinction matters enormously to the dead, apparently. They're sentimental about consent." He stopped walking. "The keyholder is the heir. The heir signs her ledgers in a hand too old for her. She has been sitting in my archive for six years, Isandro, cataloguing the very records that erased her country, and she never knew she was the collateral."

*Merrin.* The name rang in Isandro like a struck bone.

"Where is she?"

"Safe," Corvane said. "For the value of safe. She is in a comfortable room with a comfortable fire and two enforcers who think she's being protected. Tomorrow at dusk the due date arrives — yes, we cracked the cipher, eventually, from the courier's recovered effects and your own generous bone-dust trail through the marshes — and at dusk she and I will visit the drowned capital together, and she will be given a choice framed so beautifully that choosing it will feel like her own idea. That is the whole art of dishonest payment, Isandro: the victim must sign."

"Why tell me this?" Isandro asked. "Why bring me here, pour me wine, show me your arithmetic? You could have put a knife in me on the flats."

"Because I am offering you employment." Corvane returned to the lantern and refilled both cups, unhurried. "Your old bargain. Silence for survival. You made it once, as a child — false records for bread, and you've been solvent ever since. I'm renewing the terms. Tomorrow, when the heir asks you — and she will ask you, she trusts you, God knows why — you will tell her the truth as I have framed it. That the debt is real, that refusal dooms everyone, that the door takes one willing life and spares a nation's memory entire. You will help her sign. And when it is done, your column reopens. Your desk. Your vaults. Your bones, uncarved, your own." He held out the cup. "Or you can be entered in the ledger yourself, tonight, as a loss. I warn you the entry would be brief. Losses always are."

Isandro looked at the wine.

Twenty years of silence stretched out inside it like a reflection — every false record, every name scraped clean, every night they'd told themselves the alternative was worse. Corvane had built that bargain brick by brick around them since childhood, and now he was simply asking them to live in it one day longer.

They could say yes. They could go to Merrin at dusk and speak softly and watch her choose, and wake the morning after with their hands still their own. No one would ever know. No one was left to know — except the dead, and the dead kept books too.

*The dead keep books.*

Sef's voice, faint, from the finger-bone Isandro had sewn against their own ribs these eleven days: *Carry it. Remember me.*

Isandro took the cup. Corvane's face eased into satisfaction — and Isandro watched that satisfaction settle, and memorized it, and understood with terrible clarity that they were going to lie one more time, the largest lie of their life, to the most dangerous reader in the world.

"I'll speak to her," they said quietly. "She'll listen to me. I'll make her understand the terms."

"I knew you were solvent," Corvane said.

* * *

They took Isandro down — not to the cells, but to a guest room with a bolted shutter and a comfortable bed, because broken tools cut crooked. An enforcer locked the door from outside. Boots retreated down the stair. Fourth step creaked.

Isandro sat on the edge of the bed and drew out the small carving they'd hidden in the lining of their coat: Sef's memorial piece, barely the length of a thumb. A messenger running. They closed their hand around it and listened.

*Tomorrow,* the dead courier whispered through the ivory, thin as breath on a cold pane. *Dusk. She'll come to you first — she doesn't trust the room, she's already working the lock with a hairpin, she's her grandmother's blood all through. When she comes, carver, tell her the truth. All of it. Even the part you're afraid of.*

"What part am I afraid of?" Isandro murmured to the empty room.

The bone was silent a long moment. Then, soft and sorrowful, the way the dead are when they already know how the story ends:

*That you've already decided what it will cost you to save her. And that you haven't told her yet.*

Below, in the dark of the counting house, somewhere in the archive stacks, Isandro heard — or imagined, or hoped — the small, patient scrape of a hairpin against a lock.
