## 10. A Garden for the Crown Again

The city did not know her name.

Merrin walked out of the customs house at Greyharbor with a satchel on her shoulder and salt drying white along her hem, and the clerk behind the desk looked through her the way clerks look through everyone — politely, completely, as though she were a line item already balanced. He stamped her papers. He wished her a pleasant stay in the Crown's fair dominion. He did not ask where she had come from, because to ask would be to admit there was anywhere else.

That was how it worked now. That was how it had always worked. Callowmere had not been destroyed; it had been *unmentioned*, and an unmentioning is a wound that closes over its own mouth.

She carried Sef's bones home anyway.

They rode against her hip in a cedar box no longer than her forearm, wrapped in linen she had embroidered during the long passage — clumsy stitches, a border of marsh-grass and kingcups, the pattern copied from a map that no longer existed anywhere but in her memory and in the oilskin packet pressed flat beneath the bones. The customs officer at the quay had asked what was in the box. *Relics*, she said, and it was true enough that he waved her through. Every country is made of relics. Hers simply had to be carried by hand.

The road north from Greyharbor ran through country that remembered nothing and everything. She saw it in the shapes of things: a hedgerow planted in a line too straight for any English hedge, following the ghost of a boundary wall; a churchyard where the oldest stones had been turned face-down into the grass like children hiding; a bridge with a keystone chiseled smooth, the name of whatever town had built it scraped away by some diligent, obedient hand. Forty years of erasure, and still the land kept its posture. Still the fields tilted toward a capital that was now a drowned lake called, on the new maps, *the Reservoir*.

Merrin had been a clerk for eleven years. She knew what a ledger could do. She had spent eleven years learning that a ledger could do anything — could make a debt appear or vanish, could make a person solvent or ruined with one stroke, could make forty thousand lives into a rounding error under a column headed *ASSETS RECOVERED*. Corvane had understood it too. That was the horror of him, when she let herself think of him: not cruelty, but arithmetic. He had died — or worse than died, been *entered* — believing the columns balanced.

They did not balance. She intended to prove it.

* * *

The drowned border-stone stood where the map said it would, alone in a mile of salt marsh that no one farmed because no one remembered why it should be farmed. It had been a waymarker once, granite taller than a man, carved with the arms of two kingdoms facing each other across the stone like old friends pretending not to embrace. Now the Crown's side had been chiseled blank, and Callowmere's side — the seaward face — still showed a lion couchant under a rising sun, worn soft by tides but unmistakable, patient, waiting to be read.

Merrin came at low tide in the last week of harvest-month, when the light lay long and gold across the flats. She set down her pack. She unwrapped the cedar box. And she said, aloud, to the water and the mud and the stone:

"I'm back. I'm sorry it took so long."

The marsh did not answer. But the air changed the way air changes before rain — a pressure, a listening — and she felt them then, faint as heat off a summer road: the dead of Callowmere, standing in their thousands in the shallow places, not risen but *present*, the way a family is present in an empty house. They had shown her this once, on the worst night of her life, rising through the black water with their faces full of witness. Now they only waited. They had been waiting forty years for someone to come back and do the work.

She began to dig.

Not a grave. A garden.

It was Isandro's idea first — or rather, it was Isandro's hands she had learned it from. In those last days, before the ledger, before the door, Isandro had sat in the lee of the ruined customhouse at the drowned capital with a knife and a scrap of ivory, carving while Merrin slept, and when she woke there was a small gate in her palm: two posts, a lintel, a wicket door left deliberately ajar. *Every garden needs a gate,* Isandro had said. *Otherwise how does anyone know they're invited?*

She carried that gate now, wrapped in wool against her heart. Bone-white, small enough to close her fist around, and warm — always warm, though she told herself it was only her own blood warming it. On quiet nights, when the wind dropped and the tide stood slack, a voice came through it. Not Sef's. Not anyone's she could name. Just a voice, low and unhurried, saying fragments: *the east bed wants thinning. Mind the frost. You're doing fine.* Isandro had never gardened a day in their life. Whatever was left of them on the other side of the ledger had apparently decided to learn.

Merrin dug the first bed inside the shadow of the border-stone, where the ground was darkest. Salt marsh grows almost nothing — samphire, sea-lavender, the tough gold of gorse — but she had brought seed from three countries and cuttings from four, and she had brought something else besides: soil from the archive vaults of the Vellum Guild itself, a sack of it, taken from the foundation level where the oldest ledgers had moldered into earth. Let the Guild's own ground feed Callowmere's garden. Let the complicity grow something.

She planted by the old calendar, the one her grandmother had kept in the margins of her maps — the same hand, that slanted, patient calligraphy, that Merrin had recognized in a guild audit room with her whole body going cold. Kingcups by the gate. Thyme in the cracks of the stone, because thyme forgives everything. An apple pip from the last orchard row in the Reservoir's shallows, which Sef had carried in his coat pocket for nine hundred miles without knowing why, only that his mother had given it to him and said *plant it where you stop running.*

He had stopped here. So she planted it here.

* * *

That night she made camp against the border-stone and opened the new ledger for the first time.

She had bought it in Greyharbor from a stationer who sold her a fine vellum-bound book without asking questions, and she had spent the voyage ruling it herself, by lamplight, in the manner of the Vellum Guild — double columns, hairline rules, a title page. But the title was hers, and it read:

**A LEDGER OF CALLLOWMERE: WHAT WAS TAKEN, AND WHAT WAS GIVEN BACK**

Two columns. Two hands.

In the left-hand column, in her clerk's script — the small, exact, anonymous hand the Guild had trained into her — she entered the takings. She worked from memory, from the map, from the whispered testimony of the dead, and she worked the way Corvane had taught her to work, which was the cruelest inheritance of all: precisely.

*Taken: 41,206 souls, entered as ASSETS RECOVERED, year of the Erasure.*
*Taken: 1 capital, flooded under the name Reservoir.*
*Taken: 1 language, unrecorded; 1 calendar, discontinued; 1 lion couchant under a rising sun, defaced.*
*Taken: 1 courier, Sef Arden, died at a counting-house door carrying his country's last map.*
*Taken: 1 bone-carver, Isandro Dunmore, entered willingly as payment, account closed by force.*

Her pen stopped on that last line. It stopped every time. She had learned, in the Guild, that a clerk must never blot, never hesitate, never show feeling in the hand — and every night she broke the rule over Isandro's name, and every night she let the ink sit there trembling until it dried with a small halo of hesitation around it, and she decided that the halo could stay. Some entries should look like what they cost.

Then the right-hand column. For this she used her grandmother's hand — the slanted, older calligraphy, which she had practiced on scraps all the way across the sea until it came out of her fingers like a second pulse. In that hand she wrote the givings-back, and the list was shorter, and she meant to make it longer every year for the rest of her life.

*Given back: 1 map, restored to public knowledge.*
*Given back: 1 garden, begun on the drowned border-stone.*
*Given back: 1 apple tree, planted from the last orchard of the Reservoir.*
*Given back: these names, spoken aloud each evening:* — and here she listed them, page after page, the dead of Callowmere, all she could recover, beginning with Sef Arden and ending, for now, with a name she wrote slowly and in both hands at once, the letters overlapping like clasped fingers: *Isandro Dunmore, who paid.*

When she finished, the wind had gone utterly still. The tide stood slack. And through the little bone gate lying open on the ledger's spine, faint as breath on a winter pane, the voice said:

*You're doing fine. The east bed wants thinning.*

"I'll thin it tomorrow," Merrin said, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and laughed, because grief and laughter had long ago agreed to share a house in her chest. "And I've started the book. You'd hate my margins. They're a disgrace."

The voice didn't answer. It never answered. But the gate stayed warm against the vellum, and outside, in the dark, she heard — or believed she heard, and had decided long ago that belief was also a kind of record — the sound of many people moving gently through shallow water, going about the business of being remembered.

* * *

Spring came early to the marsh that year, or perhaps Merrin simply refused to admit it was late.

The apple pip sprouted. The kingcups came up yellow as small suns around the bone gate, which she had fixed at the head of the garden on a post of driftwood, door ajar, exactly as carved. Samphire greened the beds. She built a low wall of field-stone, and travelers began to leave things on it — this was the part she had not planned, the part that undid her weekly: a ribbon. A copper coin from a currency no mint had struck in forty years, kept secretly in someone's grandmother's box. A scrap of paper bearing a word in old Callowmere tongue, spelled wrong, spelled hopefully. People came walking out across the flats at low tide, alone or in twos, looking at the lion on the stone with expressions like people waking. They did not say the country's name yet. But they stood near it. Standing near is how remembering starts.

Each evening Merrin opened the ledger and made her entries. The left column grew slower now; there was less left to take. The right column grew fat and stubborn as a spring lamb. And on the first anniversary of the door, she ruled a fresh page, titled it in both hands, and wrote the entry she had been saving, the one she had promised herself she would not write until she was sure:

*Given back: 1 school, in the market town of Harrow Ford, teaching the old letters to eleven children, none of whom know why their grandparents wept at the sign above the door.*

She blotted it carefully. She closed the book. And as she laced the ties, the voice through the gate spoke — clearly, fully, for the first time — not a fragment about beds or frost, but a sentence, whole and warm and unmistakably shaped like a smile:

*Merrin. There's someone here who says he knows the way to your grandmother's house. He says he's very sorry he's late.*

She looked up. Beyond the border-stone, at the edge of the tide-line, the evening light was standing in a long gold column, and in it — faint, patient, wearing the grin she remembered from a counting-house doorway — someone was waiting to be written into the right-hand column.

Merrin picked up her pen.
