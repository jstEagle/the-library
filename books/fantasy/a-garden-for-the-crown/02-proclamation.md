## 2. Proclamation

The paper arrived before the sun did.

Silas knew it first by sound—the wet slap of paste against her door, the bristle of a brush worked by hands in a hurry. She had been awake for hours, as she always was, lying beneath the eaves of her attic room and counting the city's murmurs the way other people counted sheep: the milk cart's iron-rimmed wheels at the corner of Vetch Street, the cough of old Ferro two doors down, the pigeons quarreling in the gutter like creditors.

Then the crier's bell began, and every ordinary sound in Lowmarsh bent around it.

*"By order of His Majesty King Aldric the Second, of that name the second, protector of the realm—"*

She was down the stairs before the sentence finished, bare feet on cold boards, moving with the particular silence she had practiced for five years. The workshop below smelled of tallow and bone dust and the faint sweet rot of the river coming through the shutters. Her stock lay in its usual places: three mourning charms in the cedar box, half-finished knuckle-bones on the wheel, the little carved teeth she sold to midwives as fertility tokens—harmless, mostly harmless, only a whisper of luck in them—and beneath the loose floorboard by the hearth, wrapped in waxed linen, the one thing she had never meant to sell.

She did not read the proclamation pasted to her door. She had heard enough through the wood. Words like *unmaking* and *abomination* and *the crown's mercy* carried perfectly through cheap pine, and they were words she had rehearsed against all her life without ever believing she would hear them spoken into law.

So she did what carvers do. She made an ending.

* * *

The hearth took the stock grudgingly. Bone does not burn like wood; it burns like something reluctant, like a secret leaving the body slowly. Silas fed the fire piece by piece and watched each charm's shape soften, blacken, collapse into itself. The cedar box went in whole. The half-finished knuckles she broke across her knee first, because breaking them felt more honest than burning them intact—they had been listening material, practice bones, and it seemed wrong for them to die whole.

She saved the waxed linen bundle until last. It sat on the table where she'd left it, small as a loaf, heavy as a sin.

Inside was her mother's hum.

Not the woman herself—that woman was twenty years in a pauper's grave upcountry—but the charm Silas had carved from her finger bone at seventeen, the one thing she had taken from the burial cart when no one was looking. Press it to her ear and she could hear her mother humming the way she used to hum over the wash, tuneless and endless, the sound of a life too tired for words but not for music. Silas had never once let another soul hear it. It was not trade goods. It was the difference between being alone and being alone *with*.

The flames reached toward it like fingers.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice came out rusty, a door unused. "No. They'll have everything else."

She pushed the bundle deep into the lining of her coat instead, where it sat against her ribs like a second heartbeat she would have to explain to no one. Then she doused the fire, because a chimney smoking at dawn in a week of proclamations was a confession, and began to pack what fleeing required: knife, flint, bread wrapped in cloth, coin sewn into a hem, the good carving tools—six of them, whalebone handles worn to the shape of her hand—wrapped in an apron that had never once been used for flour.

Through the shutter slats she watched the street fill.

They came in threes. That was the pattern she catalogued even as fear climbed her throat: hunters moved in threes, one to knock, one to bind, one to watch the windows. Grey coats, ash-grey boots, the crowned-sword sigil stitched at the shoulder in thread that caught the light wrong, too bright, like it had been dyed with something that wasn't quite color. They were polite. That was the worst of it. In Lowmarsh nobody knocked politely, and so the knocking itself was the verdict.

At the Widow Ansel's, across and down. At the cooper's, where the cooper's daughter had fits that might have been fits or might have been something else. At each door, papers. At each door, questions asked in the gentle voice you use on livestock.

Silas counted doors between hers and theirs and understood she had perhaps an hour. Perhaps less. Perhaps already less than less.

* * *

There was one thing left to do, and she hated herself for needing to do it.

Old Tam lived in the ground-floor room behind her shop, a rag-and-bone man grown nearly indistinguishable from his wares, and he was dying. He had been dying for a month with the patient professionalism of the very poor, refusing the physician, refusing the priest, hoarding his strength for nothing in particular. This morning his room was dim and close and smelled of dust and vinegar, and he lay in his cot like laundry someone had forgotten.

"Tam," Silas said softly. "It's Silas."

His eyes found her, clouded but present. "Heard the bell," he rasped. "Bad morning for business, girl."

"The worst." She knelt by the cot. Her hands were already moving before her mind consented—this was the part of the gift that had never felt like a gift, this compulsion of her fingers toward bone. "I'm going away for a while. I need—I want to know something before I go. May I?"

He understood. Rag-and-bone men know bones the way priests know scripture, and he had known for years what she was, though neither of them had ever said it in daylight. Now he turned his wrist over, offering it, and closed his eyes. "Go on then. Mind, if you hear anything about money owed me, I don't want to know."

She almost smiled. She pressed her thumb to the thin bone of his forearm—not reading him, never him, but using his living bone the way one uses a tuning fork, to steady the frequency—and reached past him, out along the street's long memory, into the marrow of the city itself.

This was the deepest trick she had, the one she showed no one: bones remembered what passed near them. Every threshold, every gravestone, every old wall held its sediment of sound. And last night, in the square by the fountain, a royal crier had stood before a crowd with a parchment in his hands, and the cobblestones beneath eight hundred feet had drunk the moment whole.

Silas listened.

She heard the crowd first—the shuffling hush, a child asking its mother what *unmaking* meant, the mother not answering. Then the crier's voice, pitched high and official:

*"By order of His Majesty King Aldric the Second... all persons gifted with unlawful arts shall surrender themselves to the crown's officers within seven days... those who conceal the gifted shall share their punishment... the hunt is declared from this hour..."*

The words themselves were nothing new. She had heard their shape through her own front door.

But Silas Ravensworth had spent five years learning the grain of living voices, and living voices have a texture no dead thing can counterfeit. A living speaker breathes. Their voice rides on breath, rises and dips with the pulse, carries the small honest noises of a body doing its work—a catch, a swallow, the faint whistle of air through a nose not quite clear. Even a crier reading another man's words puts his own breath behind them, his own weariness, his own suppressed opinions.

The decree had none.

The king's words came off that parchment like coins from a mint—perfect, identical, weightless. No breath under them. No pulse. When the crier spoke *His Majesty*, his own throat produced the sounds of *Majesty*, but the cadence, the rhythm underneath, belonged to something that had never needed air. It was as if a puppet's strings had been recorded and played back through a man's mouth.

Silas withdrew so fast her head swam, and Tam's grip on her wrist startled her—his hand was still strong, grip of a man who'd hauled carts of scrap for fifty years.

"You've gone white as your merchandise," he said. "What'd you hear?"

"Tam." Her mouth was dry. "The proclamation. It was read in the king's name."

"Proclamations are."

"It wasn't read *by* anyone alive." She heard herself say it and heard how mad it sounded, and kept talking anyway, because five years of caution had just come apart in her hands like wet paper. "The words have no breath in them. I've heard dead men speak, Tam. Dead men have more life in their voices than whatever ordered this."

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then, wheezing, almost gently: "Then run, girl. Run far. What's a dead king to a girl with your ears except a very loud ghost, and ghosts were always your trade, weren't they? Let some other fool bury him."

Yes, said every survival instinct she had honed since her mother died. Yes. Out the back, over the wall, down to the river, north before noon, be a rumor by winter, be safe.

But underneath the fear, something else had stirred—the thing she had spent five years starving because it was more dangerous than the gift itself. Curiosity. The listener's hunger. Because she had heard a great many voices from beyond the veil, and none of them had ever issued decrees. The dead did not govern. The dead clung, and mourned, and repeated themselves; they did not organize hunts. Whatever wore the crown and spoke without breath was doing something no dead thing should be able to do—it was *acting* in the world, at scale, through instruments. Through hunters. Through paper and bells and grey coats going door to door.

And it had, in a single night, converted her entire life from invisible to criminal.

"Tam," she said slowly, "if I run, I run forever. It'll follow. Proclamations travel faster than fugitives."

"Or it forgets you. You're a bone-carver in Lowmarsh. Kings don't remember girls like you."

"That's just it." She stood, and her knees cracked, and she felt suddenly very old and strangely calm. "Kings don't. Something else might. Something that counts us."

She helped him drink, tucked his blanket like a daughter would, and lied to him beautifully about coming back. Then she went upstairs and finished packing, and while she cinched the straps she made herself say the thought out loud, alone, to the empty room, because thoughts spoken become real and can therefore be weighed:

"The king is dead. Something is wearing him."

The room absorbed it. The burnt smell of her livelihood hung in the rafters.

* * *

She left by the coal chute at the back, dropped into the alley that ran behind the rag shops, and had made it perhaps forty yards toward the river road when the crowd in the square ahead stopped her—not with violence, but with sound. A second reading. Another crier, or the same one circling, and this time she could see him: a small man on the fountain's rim, parchment raised, grey-coated hunters flanking him like fence posts.

And this time, standing in the open with the wind carrying his voice straight to her, Silas heard it with her naked ears as well as her gift, and the two listenings braided together into something worse than either.

Her naked ears heard a proclamation. Ordinary. Tyrannical, but ordinary—kingdoms had burned gifted folk before; there was precedent; history was a ledger of such mornings.

Her gift heard the *gap*. The words arriving pre-aged, as if carved rather than spoken, each syllable a little tomb. And threaded through the gap—so faint she had to stand absolutely still among the jostling crowd, eyes closed, one hand on a rain barrel to steady herself—threaded something else. Not the crier's voice. Under it. Beneath the decree like roots beneath a road.

A hunger. Patient, vast, and attentive. As if the words themselves were bait cast into the city, and somewhere behind them, something waited to feel which fish rose.

Silas opened her eyes. The crowd was dispersing, muttering, already folding the terror into their routines because that was what crowds did. A hunter at the fountain's edge was scanning faces, and for one suspended heartbeat his gaze crossed hers—

—and passed on. Grey coat, young face, eyes that looked tired in a way that didn't match the set of his jaw. He moved on down the line of onlookers, checking, cataloguing, missing her by the width of a prayer.

She walked. Not fast—fleeing people walk fast, and fast draws the eye—but at the pace of a woman late for honest work, out of the square, along Vetch Street, past the cooper's where a family stood weeping quietly around a doorway marked with a paste-and-parchment star, and out the Marsh Gate onto the river road, where the fog came off the water thick enough to hide in.

North lay the fens and freedom, such as it was. South, three days' hard travel, lay the capital. The palace. The crown.

Every sensible voice in her—her mother's memory loudest among them—said *north*.

But Silas stood in the fog at the fork of the road with her stolen heartbeat pressed against her ribs, and realized with something like horror that the decision had already been made somewhere deeper than sense. Five years she had survived by hearing everything and saying nothing, and now she had heard the one sound she could not put down: a kingdom speaking in a corpse's cadence, and no one else able to tell. No one else *able*. The words formed themselves unbidden, and they were her mother's words, from another lifetime, over the wash-tub:

*You've got ears for a reason, girl. Pity they're pointed inward.*

She turned south.

The fog swallowed the road behind her. Somewhere in Lowmarsh, hunters were nailing paper to doors, and somewhere ahead, past three days of ditches and hedgerows and checkpoints not yet built, a dead man sat on a living throne and spoke without breathing—and Silas Ravensworth, fugitive of one morning's standing, walked toward him with her tools wrapped in an apron, because for the first time in five years, she wanted to hear something more than she wanted to be safe.

She did not see the grey coat detach from the treeline a hundred paces back and follow.
