## 3. What the Bones Remember

The femur had come to Silas the way most bones did—wrapped in oilcloth, passed across a counter by someone who would not meet her eyes.

She had not gone looking for it. That was the rule she'd kept for five years: never seek, only receive. The dead came to her through whatever the living carried to her door, and she carved what they asked and asked nothing in return that could be traced. But tonight the oilcloth sat on her workbench like a debt she had already agreed to pay, and the city outside her shutters was still burning its own fear—she could smell the char of charm-shops three streets over, hear the boots of hunters moving in pairs, always in pairs now, as if loneliness itself had become a crime.

She unwrapped the bone.

It was a left femur, long-healed at the midshaft where a break had knitted crooked—a man who had limped through some part of his life and learned to make a joke of it, probably. Grave-robber's stock. The seller, a rat-faced man named Fennick who dealt in relics the way other men dealt in fish, had told her it came out of the paupers' field behind Saint Aldhelm's, but his hands had shaken when he said *Saint Aldhelm's*, and hands that shook were telling a different story than mouths.

Silas turned the bone under her lamp. It was yellowed but not ancient. Thirty years in the ground, perhaps less. Someone had loved this man once—the femur had been cleaned with care, the marrow drained properly, which was not what grave-robbers did. Grave-robbers took and ran. This bone had been *prepared*.

"Who were you?" she murmured, and then, because the night was thin and the hunters were elsewhere and curiosity had been growing in her chest like a weed since the crier nailed the Proclamation to her door—*since she'd held her neighbor's ribs and heard the king's voice speaking words with no breath in them*—she set down her carving knife, cupped the femur in both palms, and listened.

The dead did not speak the way the living did. There was no sound, not exactly. What came was more like heat rising from stone at dusk, or the memory of a melody you couldn't name. She closed her eyes and let it rise.

Cold first. Always cold first—the body's last complaint, filed away forever. Then mud, and the particular ache of a spine bent over digging, and—

*A lantern. A wall too fine for a pauper's field. Iron gates with gilt on them.*

Silas's breath slowed. She leaned into the sensation the way she'd once leaned into warm water, letting it carry her deeper.

The man whose bone this was had dug graves for a living. Not robbed them—*dug* them, honestly, in the great cemetery that served the palace district, where even the servants of the crown were buried with ceremony. And one night, thirty years past, he had been called to open a grave that was not yet a grave.

*A crypt beneath the chapel. Candles burning green. A woman in mourning grey who was not a widow.*

The fragments came the way they always did—not in order, not whole, but in shards sharp enough to cut. The gravedigger had been paid an obscene sum to keep silent about what he saw that night. He had kept it until he died, and now he poured it out into the dark of his own buried bone because there was no one left to tell, and the dead are terrible gossips when they finally have an audience.

Silas saw through his eyes: a bier, and on it a body she recognized from coins and portraits—the old king, Aldric's father, or rather the man before him, the grandfather-king whose face had faded from the currency a generation ago. She saw a physician kneeling beside the bier, sleeves rolled, hands stained to the wrist with something darker than blood. She heard the physician's voice, cracked and reverent and horrified all at once:

*"Her Majesty, I must say it plainly. This is not medicine. This is a binding, and bindings do not loosen. They only tighten."*

And the woman in grey—the queen, young still, her face wet but her voice dry as paper—answered:

*"Then let it tighten. He promised me he would not leave us. I am only holding him to it."*

Silas opened her eyes. Her heart was slamming against her ribs.

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were listening.

She looked down at the femur and understood, suddenly, why the bone had been prepared with such care. The gravedigger hadn't just buried the old king that night. He had helped carry something else down into the crypt—something small and golden that the queen carried against her breast like an infant, wrapped in silk, humming to it. Something that had been *made* that night from a dying man's last breath and a crown's cold metal and a rite the physician had sworn he would take to his grave.

He had. Mostly. But the gravedigger had heard the edges of it, and edges were enough.

*Bound into the crown,* the dead man's memory whispered, circling back like a moth. *Bound into the crown so he need never leave. Bound into the crown, and the crown upon the son's head, and the son's head bowed beneath his father—*

The vision guttered. Dead memories were shallow wells; you got what rain had gathered, nothing more. Silas sat back, shaking, the femur warm in her hands though the room was cold.

The decree had gone out in the king's voice. She had heard it herself through Marla's ribs—the neighbor dying of the lung-rot who'd begged Silas to listen to the proclamation read aloud in the square below, wanting to know if the world she was leaving was safe for anyone. And the king's voice had been beautiful and terrible and utterly without breath, the way a bell is without breath, the way a puppet is.

*Something was bound into the crown decades ago.*

She thought of the Proclamation of Unmaking. Of hunters sweeping the city for the gifted. Of a kingdom suddenly, frantically searching for people who could hear things others couldn't—people who might, given time, press their palms to a royal ribcage and find it silent as river stones.

The hunt wasn't superstition. It wasn't cruelty for cruelty's sake, though cruelty would come—it always came. It was a search. The thing wearing the crown was looking for anyone who might look back.

Silas rose and began pulling her remaining stock from its hiding places—not to burn it, not yet, but to sort it. The harmless pieces she could sell or abandon. The rest—the widow's husband's finger-bone charm she'd kept because his voice had made her laugh for the first time in years; her mother's knuckle, worn smooth as a river pebble; the sliver of rib she'd taken, guiltily, from Marla's coffin because the old woman had promised to haunt her and Silas had wanted to hold her to it—those went into a lead-lined box that had once held apothecary's poisons.

She was wrapping the box in oilcloth when she heard the boots again. Closer now. Two pairs, walking in that paired rhythm, passing her street without stopping—but passing *slowly*, the way a man walks when he is memorizing a door.

Curiosity had outweighed survival five nights ago. Tonight, survival and curiosity had finally agreed on a destination: the capital. The palace. Whatever wore the crown.

She just had to live long enough to get there.

Wren Calloway had been a hunter for six years, and in six years he had developed exactly one private ritual: every night, before sleep, he told himself the lie that he was good at his job.

Tonight the lie wouldn't take.

The child had been maybe nine years old, barefoot in the reeds outside Millbrook, doing something to the water that made it glow—a soft, green-gold light rising off the surface like heat shimmer, like the pond had remembered being a star. Wren had his crossbow up. Protocol said loose the bolt, or close and cut, depending on the age. For a child that small, protocol said *close and cut*, which meant a knife, which meant he would have to be near enough to see the light go out of the water and the terror go out of the child in the same moment.

His partner, Brannock, was circling the far side of the reeds. Wren had maybe forty heartbeats.

"You're seen," Wren had said quietly, not raising his voice, not lowering the bow. "Run."

The child had looked at him—looked at the crossbow, at the hunter's black-and-silver badge, at everything the badge meant—and then done the bravest thing Wren had ever witnessed, which was to believe him. The light went out. Small feet slapped mud. Gone into the dark like the water had swallowed her.

Now Wren sat on the edge of the millpond with his boots off, drying his feet by a fire he shouldn't have lit, and tried to reconstruct the moment when he had stopped being the man his orders described.

It wasn't tonight. Tonight was only the symptom.

It had started with the ledger. Hunters kept ledgers—every cleansing, every unmaking, every village entered and exited, recorded in a hand that was supposed to be neutral as weather. Wren had been transcribing the quarterly reports for the southern districts, copying them into the master record for the Marshal's office, when he noticed the pattern. Cleansings clustered. They weren't scattered across the realm the way random sin scatters. They clustered around the capital, around the palace district, around the roads leading to it—tightening year by year, like a fist closing.

Hunters were told the gifted flocked to cities because cities hid them. It was a sensible explanation. Wren had believed it for years.

But fists don't close around nothing. Somebody was searching for something specific, and using the hunt to do it, and the hunt was too blunt an instrument to ever find it—so the fist just kept tightening, crushing villages like Millbrook's neighbors, crushing families, crushing glowing ponds and frightened children, and finding nothing, and tightening again.

And then there was the voice.

Every hunter was required to attend the king's proclamations when they were read in their district—to stand witness, to swear afterward that they had heard the king's will with their own ears. Wren had stood in the square at Millbrook with four hundred others and heard King Aldric command the unmaking of the gifted, and his ears had heard a strong voice, a clear voice, a voice like a drawn sword.

But Wren had also, years ago, as a boy visiting the capital with his father, actually seen Aldric speak. A coronation anniversary. The king had been young then, and kind in a tired way, and Wren remembered thinking his voice sounded like a man apologizing for something.

The voice in the square had not apologized for anything. The voice in the square had not *breathed*. Wren had spent six years learning to read bodies—the flinch before a lie, the stillness before violence—and the king's proclamation had moved like a marionette moves, beautifully, in perfect time, with nothing behind it pulling except the string.

He had told no one. You didn't tell anyone a thing like that. You especially didn't tell Brannock, who wore his faith in the crown like armor plate and would report his own mother if her shadow fell wrong.

Brannock appeared out of the dark now, boots squelching, annoyed. "Lost her. Reeds go on half a mile, then marsh. She could be in the next county." He dropped onto a stump and glared at the fire. "You circled wide. Too wide."

"I covered my ground."

"Your ground had a hole in it shaped like a girl." Brannock spat. "Doesn't matter. Word came from the Marshal while you were playing at the water's edge. We're recalled. All southern companies."

Wren kept his face still. "Recalled where?"

"The capital. There's a priority list now—names, districts, descriptions. High-value unmakings." Brannock's grin showed teeth. "They've found something, Calloway. Some thread. The Marshal says we're to bring in a carver. Bone-worker, works alone, sells charms out of a shop in the low quarter. Woman." He pulled a folded paper from his coat and smoothed it against his knee. "There's a sketch."

Wren took it. The firelight was poor, but the sketch was good—better than good, better than any description a witness gives. Whoever had drawn it had studied the face. Dark hair pinned carelessly. A jaw set like a decision. Eyes that looked at the artist the way a person looks at a wall they're deciding whether to climb.

Something in Wren's chest went quiet and attentive, the way it had gone quiet at the millpond.

"What's she done?" he asked.

Brannock shrugged. "Listened to the wrong bones, maybe. Who knows what these ones do. Marshal wants her alive—that's marked twice, see? Alive and *intact*. Which means someone above the Marshal wants to ask her questions himself." He laughed. "Or something does."

*Something.* The word landed in Wren like a stone in a well, dropping a long way before it hit water.

He handed the sketch back and looked at the fire and thought about a girl running barefoot through reeds, and a voice with no breath in it, and a fist tightening around the throat of the realm, and how every road he'd ever walked as a hunter seemed lately to bend, however gently, toward the palace.

"We leave at dawn," Brannock said. "Get some sleep."

Wren slept badly. In the grey hour before morning, he lay listening to Brannock snore and admitted the thing he'd been circling for months: he no longer knew whether he was hunting monsters for the crown, or whether he was one of the monsters' hounds, being whistled home.

There was a woman in the low quarter who carved bone. By dawn, Wren had decided that when they reached the capital, he would be the one to knock on her door.

He told himself it was because he wanted first crack at questioning her.

He was getting better at lying to himself. But not good enough—not quite yet.
