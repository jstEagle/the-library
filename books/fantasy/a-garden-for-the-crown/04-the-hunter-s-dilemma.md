## 4. The Hunter's Dilemma

The knock came at dusk, and Silas knew before her hand touched the latch that it was death wearing a hunter's coat.

She had felt him for three streets. Hunters carried their own weather—a pressure in the air, a hush that pressed against the ears like the moment before snowfall. The gifted learned to feel it the way sailors felt a storm coming, and Silas had spent five years learning to feel everything, because feeling was how she survived. She set down her knife, laid a cloth over the half-carved sparrow on her bench, and looked at the door.

Through it, muffled by old oak, she heard his heartbeat. Steady. Slow. The heart of a man who had walked toward this door all day and did not intend to turn back.

She could run. There was a loose stone behind the hearth and a passage beneath the tannery that smelled of rot and freedom, and she could be gone before he finished knocking. But the bones were out. Three of them lay on her worktable under lamplight—the femur she'd taken from the grave-robber's hoard, still murmuring its fragments of court confession; a child's rib she'd been asked to quiet; and the small knuckle-bone of an old woman whose granddaughter had paid two coppers for one last word.

If she ran, they would find them. And what hunters did to charms of the dead—she had seen it once, from a window, years ago: a pile of carved bone broken with hammers in the market square while their makers' names were read aloud, each name followed by silence, because the makers themselves had already been unmade.

Silas opened the door.

He filled the frame the way winter fills a doorway—not large so much as *present*, taking up all the warmth at once. Grey coat, grey eyes, the silver badge of the Royal Hunt pinned at his throat like a small cold moon. Younger than she'd expected. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking.

"Silas Ravensworth." He said it flatly, reading from memory rather than paper. "Bone-charmer. Licensed as a carver of trinkets, third district."

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said. "You know my name, and I don't know yours."

"Wren Calloway." His hand rested near his belt, not on it. "Hunter, second rank."

"Hunter." She let the word sit between them like a blade laid flat on a table. "And here I thought you were a customer. I've just closed, but for a hunter I might make an exception."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "Step outside, Mistress Ravensworth. You're wanted for questioning."

"Questioning." She leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and made herself look bored, though her pulse ticked in her throat like a clock running fast. "In my experience, questioning by the Hunt begins with irons and ends with ash. Forgive me if I decline the invitation."

"That's the order," he said. "I'm required to bring you in."

"And are you *required* to succeed?"

The question landed harder than she'd meant it to. He went very still, the way animals go still when they've heard something in the brush they can't yet name.

Inside the workshop, unnoticed by either of them—or noticed only by Silas, who noticed everything—the femur on the table began, very softly, to speak.

* * *

Wren Calloway had tracked eleven gifted to their doors since the Proclamation. Eleven arrests. He could recite them the way other men recited prayers: the dyer with ink-stained fingers who could color cloth any shade she dreamed; the ferryman who whispered to rivers; the midwife whose hands knew illness before it showed. Each arrest had gone the same way—fear, then pleading, then the terrible quiet of people who understood that no one was coming to help them. Each night after, Wren had lain awake cataloguing their faces, and each morning the catalogue had grown heavier, until carrying it felt like carrying stones in his mouth.

This one was supposed to be simple. A bone-carver in the third district, small trade, small magic, small risk. His captain had marked her file with a single word: *minor*.

But when he stepped into her workshop—invited in, absurdly, by the woman herself, because she'd said something about the cold and he'd hesitated a half-second too long—he found her weeping.

Not performing. He knew performance; he'd grown up with a mother who could cry on command for coin. This was different. This was grief arriving like weather, sudden and total, bending her over her own worktable while her hands stayed steady on a yellowed bone, as though her body had split into two people: one drowning, one working.

"—tell her the blue coat," the bone was whispering. Wren heard it and his skin crawled, because bones should not whisper, except that this one was, clearly, gently, unmistakably. "Tell her the blue coat, the one with the torn pocket. She'll know. She always said I'd lose my head if it weren't sewn on. Tell her—"

The voice thinned to nothing. The woman beside it drew a breath like surfacing from deep water.

"He was a cooper," she said, without looking up. Her voice was rough. "Died in the river flood, four years back. His granddaughter saved for two years to buy him ten words. That was the ninth." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, impatient, businesslike, the way one wipes away rain. "The tenth is hers alone. I never listen to the tenth."

Wren stood in the doorway of a room full of small carved things—birds, moons, hands, a sparrow with its wings half-open—and understood that he had walked into something he had no training for. The Hunt taught you to find the gifted. It did not teach you what to do when you found them crying over a dead cooper's coat.

"You heard that," she said. Still not looking at him. "The bone spoke where you could hear. That means I'm careless, or it means I wanted you to hear. Guess which."

"I—" He stopped. Started again. "Mistress Ravensworth. By order of the Crown, I'm instructed to place you under arrest for the practice of unlicensed—"

"Sit down, Hunter Calloway."

To his lasting astonishment, he sat.

* * *

They regarded each other across the worktable like two chess players who had discovered mid-game that they were playing different boards.

"You're going to ask why I didn't run," Silas said. She had lit a second lamp, and the light made the workshop warmer than it had any right to be, shadows pooling gold in the corners among the hanging charms. "Everyone asks. Runners think everyone runs."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because of what you heard just now. If I run, they burn these." She gestured at the walls, the shelves, the hundred small voices sleeping in bone. "Every word I've ever carried for someone. A widow's husband saying goodbye. A mother's lullaby, kept for a daughter who couldn't sleep. You wouldn't burn letters, Hunter. These are letters. Letters from people who no longer have hands to write."

"They're also illegal."

"So is hunger, in some districts. So is grieving too loudly." She turned the cooper's femur slowly under the lamp, and the light ran along it like water. "Tell me about your eleven arrests, Calloway. The dyer. The ferryman. The midwife with the knowing hands. Did any of them fight?"

His jaw tightened. "How do you—"

"The ferryman told the river your name once, did he? Before they took him?" She smiled without warmth. "Rivers gossip. So do bones. Half this city's dead have passed through my bench, and the dead talk about everything except what matters." She set the bone down. "You've been counting their faces. At night. Am I wrong?"

He said nothing, which was answer enough.

"I can hear the dead," Silas said quietly. "That's my crime. Not fireballs, not curses, not raising armies of the buried. I listen. And lately—" she paused, weighing, and decided, because curiosity had already outweighed survival once this week and apparently intended to keep doing so, "—lately I've been listening to something I shouldn't be able to hear at all."

Wren frowned. "What?"

"A confession. Stolen out of a court physician's grave by a robber who died badly. Fragments only—the rest is broken or missing. But the pieces say that forty years ago, something was bound into the crown of Aldric's father. Bound there on purpose. And that the Proclamation—the decree ordering every gifted person hunted—" she watched his face as she said it, "—was spoken in the king's voice. But there was no breath in it. No living breath. I've heard dying men breathe their last words into my charms, Calloway. I know what living breath sounds like. The king's decree had none."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the door. Neither of them moved.

"That's treason," Wren said at last, but his voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they step onto ice.

"It's arithmetic. Something wears the king and speaks with his mouth, and it wants the gifted destroyed. Ask yourself why. What does a thing that lives in a crown fear? Not swords. Not armies. It fears *listeners*. It fears anyone who might hear it and recognize it for what it is." She spread her hands. "It's hunting us first because we would be the ones to notice."

Wren stood abruptly, chair scraping. He paced the length of the workshop twice, past the shelf of sleeping birds, past the sparrow with its half-open wings, and stopped with his back to her.

"When I was nine," he said, to the wall, "my sister had a fever. Our village healer pulled her back from it—everyone knew she had the gift, everyone pretended not to know, because pretending was cheaper than losing her. Then the first hunt came through, years later. Old law, before this Proclamation. They took her to the capital for questioning." His shoulders were very straight. "No one questioned her. They unmade her in the square, and my sister lived, and my parents never said her name again, and I joined the Hunt because I thought—if I was inside it, maybe I could be the difference between questioning and unmaking. For someone. Once."

Silas waited.

"Eleven arrests," Wren said. "Zero differences." He turned around. His grey eyes had a fever-light in them that hadn't been there at the door. "You say the crown is empty. You say you can prove it, or near enough. I say I've spent six years learning exactly how the Hunt finds people, and exactly where its records live, and exactly which gates open for a second-rank badge." He pulled the badge from his throat and set it on the table between them, silver catching lamplight, and did not pick it up again. "Alone, you can't reach the palace. Alone, I can't ask the questions I want to ask without becoming the eleventh-and-twelfth arrest. So."

"So," Silas echoed.

"You give me the truth about what's wearing the king. I give you a hunter's protection—my knowledge of the patrols, my access, my coat over your head when the lists come around. Uneven bargain. Ugly bargain. But a bargain." He held out his hand across the table, above the badge, above the bones. "Do we have one?"

Silas looked at his hand. She thought of five years of careful invisibility, of names never spoken twice, of charms kept deliberately small. She thought of the road to the capital, patrolled and posted, impossible for a hunted woman alone. She thought of the femur's broken confession, and the king's breathless decree, and the terrible, growing certainty that whatever sat upon the throne had been listening for her kind long before she was born.

Loneliness disguised as safety had kept her alive. It would not get her to the palace.

"We have one," she said, and took his hand.

His grip was warm and callused and human, and for a moment neither of them let go, both startled, perhaps, by how ordinary it felt to trust an enemy.

Then the femur on the table spoke again—louder now, urgent, the dead man's fragment pushing up through decades of silence like a flower through pavement:

*"—the binding holds while the vessel lives—God forgive me, the queen bound him in while the vessel still breathed—it knows when listeners come, it told me, it TOLD me, it knows when—"*

The voice cut off. The lamp guttered. In the ringing dark that followed, Wren said quietly:

"It knows when *what*, exactly?"

Silas stared at the bone, her blood gone cold, and realized the dead man had not been warning her at all.

He had been warning whoever came next.
