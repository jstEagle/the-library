## 5. Ashes in the Ledger

The road to the capital ran north through country Silas had not seen in eleven years, and she spent the first two days pretending it was any other road.

She kept her hood up and her hands still. Wren rode half a length behind her, as he had since they left the city gates—close enough to be protection, far enough to be deniable. He wore no hunter's colors now; they'd been traded for a drover's grey coat at the second market town, the silver pin of his office buried somewhere beneath the seat of his saddle. Without it he looked younger. Unfinished. A man whose outline had been drawn for one purpose and was now being sketched over.

"You could tell me where we're going," he said on the third morning, when the hills began to take on shapes she recognized against her will—the long shoulder of Hallowmere Fell, the birch stands that turned white as candle-wax in winter.

"North," Silas said.

"That's not a place."

"It's the only direction that matters."

He let it go. He was learning to do that with her, the way one learns to leave alone a door that sticks—not out of indifference but out of respect for whatever was behind it. She appreciated it the way she appreciated most things: silently, and too late to say so.

But the road did what roads do. It narrowed. It bent. And on the fourth day it delivered them, without asking her permission, to the top of the ridge above Millbrook, where the whole of her childhood lay spread out below like something pinned under glass.

She reined in so abruptly that Wren nearly rode into her.

"What is it?"

Silas didn't answer, because she didn't know yet. The village was there—the mill, the bridge, the crooked lane where Widow Anselm had once thrown a shoe at her for carving on her fence. The church tower stood. The elms along the green were wearing their autumn yellow, exactly as they should.

And there was smoke.

Not hearth-smoke, which rises lazy and domestic from chimneys, gray-blue and unhurried. This smoke came from the ground itself, thin columns of it rising from the foundations of houses like the earth exhaling after a long held breath. Burned-out houses didn't smoke on the fifth day or the tenth. Burned-out houses smoked only when they had been burned recently enough that the timbers underneath were still smoldering, still eating themselves in the dark.

"Wren." Her voice came out strange, flattened, like a charm carved by someone else's hand. "How fast can you ride?"

* * *

They came down into Millbrook at a canter, then a gallop, then no pace at all because the lane was choked with ash and fallen roof-beams and they had to pick their way through on foot, leading the horses.

The mill was gone first. Then the smithy. The church tower still stood, but its bells were down—one of them lay cracked in the grass of the churchyard like a split plum, its bronze mouth full of leaves.

"Rebels?" Wren said, and his voice had the careful flatness of a man hoping to be told yes. "Bandits? There's been unrest in the northern—"

"There's been no unrest here," Silas said. "There's never been unrest here. There's a mill and forty families and a harvest that comes in late because of the river fog."

She walked the lane. She made herself walk it slowly, though everything in her wanted to run, to check every doorway, to call names into the ruins. Ash rose around her boots with each step and hung in the air, fine as flour, tasting of oak and thatch and other things she refused to name. A cooking pot sat in the middle of the road, unbroken, blackened, upright—as if someone had set it down to rest and simply never picked it up again. A child's wooden horse lay near the well, its paint bubbled by heat. The well itself was filled with rubble.

No bodies. That was the thing she understood fourth or fifth, the understanding arriving in pieces like all her worst ones. No bodies anywhere. Not in the doorways, not in the lanes, not in the churchyard where the old graves stood open-mouthed among the new ruin.

"They took them," she said.

"Who?"

"The hunters." She turned to face him and saw him flinch—saw the flinch travel through him before he could stop it, the reflex of a man hearing his own order read back to him in a stranger's voice. "This is what a cleansing looks like, isn't it? You'd know. You've read the reports."

Wren said nothing. Which was answer enough.

At the far end of the green, apart from the burned houses, one structure remained untouched: the tithe barn, squat and stone-ribbed, its great doors shut and barred from the outside with fresh iron. New iron. Iron that gleamed.

They crossed to it together. Wren drew his knife and worked the bar—it was only a drop-latch, meant to hold people in, not force—and the doors swung inward onto darkness and a smell that Silas knew from a hundred workshops, a hundred markets, a hundred quiet trades: the smell of many dead gathered close.

* * *

There were perhaps sixty of them laid out on the barn floor, arranged in rows with a neatness that was worse than disorder would have been. Someone had carried them here and laid them straight, arms at their sides, faces covered with sacking. Someone had done this as a task. As a duty. As the final entry in an orderly day's work.

Silas stood in the doorway a long time.

Wren moved past her into the dark, lifting sacking, checking throats with two fingers, though both of them already knew. He worked his way down the rows with the mechanical thoroughness of a man who has been trained to assess casualties, and she watched his shoulders change shape as he went, watched the drover's coat grow heavier on him row by row.

"This one's warm," he said finally, from the far wall. "Hours, not days. Three days at most." A pause. "Silas. There are children in this row."

"I know," she said. "I can feel them."

Because she could. Even without touching bone, even standing ten feet away, the barn hummed against her gift like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing. Sixty voices, none of them speaking yet—all of them waiting, the way the newly dead wait, confused, pressed up against the membrane of the world, wanting only someone who can hear them to say: you're dead now, it happened fast, you're not alone.

She had done that work her whole life, one voice at a time, in doorways and back rooms, for coin small enough not to attract notice. She had never once done it at scale. She had never imagined scale like this.

"Give me your knife," she said.

Wren handed it across without question.

She knelt beside the nearest body—an old man, gaunt, a shepherd's knotted hands—and touched the blade to the bone of his wrist. Not to cut. Bone only needed to be opened to the air, a hairline kiss of steel, and then the listening charm did the rest. She carried three charms always, sewn into her cuff, and she used the smallest one now, the whisper-weight one, because sixty voices at full strength might drown her entirely.

The old man's last moments rose up through the bone like water through chalk.

*—the riders came at dusk, gray cloaks, the king's badge, and Tammer ran for the alarm bell and they put him down on the steps of the church, put him down like a dog, and they read it out loud, they read the decree in the king's own voice, his majesty commands, his majesty commands, and Marta screamed that we'd never even *seen* a gifted soul in Millbrook, and the officer said the king sees all, the king sees all, and they drove us into the barn like—*

Silas pulled back, gasping. The barn swayed around her. Wren caught her elbow.

"What did you see?"

"Not see. Heard." She steadied herself against a roof-post. "It was the decree. They came under the Proclamation of Unmaking. They read it aloud—in the king's voice."

"Then it's lawful. Sanctioned." He said it the way a man tests ice with one foot. "If the crown ordered—"

"Your crown ordered my city swept three weeks ago and found eleven gifted souls in a hundred thousand. This village has four hundred people and they killed every one of them." She heard her own voice rise and forced it down. "That's not a hunt, Wren. That's a harvest. Something up in that palace is hungry, and I want to know what."

Something shifted behind his eyes—she'd learned to read hunters' eyes the way other people read weather—and he went to the saddlebags on his horse and brought back a book. Leather-bound, brass-cornered, swollen with use. A hunter's field ledger.

"I shouldn't show you this," he said, and showed her anyway.

The pages were ruled and gridded, entries in a clerk's precise hand: dates, locations, numbers taken, disposition. Page after page. Town after town. And there, dated six days past, written in the same untroubled script as everything else:

**MILLBROOK — SUSPECTED GIFTED SYMPATHY (SOURCE: UNVERIFIED) — POPULATION 412 — CLEANSED.**

Cleansed. One word, between two ruled lines, and beneath it the officer's initials and a note in smaller script: *no further action required.*

"No further action required," Silas read aloud. Her voice sounded very calm to her own ears, which was how she knew she had passed beyond anything calm. "Four hundred and twelve people. My neighbors. The woman who taught me to bake. Old Tammer who rang the bell. And some clerk writes *cleansed* and goes to supper."

"Let me see the source line." Wren leaned over the page, and she watched him find it as she had found it: *source unverified.* "Someone reported sympathy. Someone maybe invented it. And the order went out anyway, and—" He closed the ledger gently, as if it might bruise. "I have carried this book for six years."

"I know."

"I thought I was keeping the peace."

"I know that too."

He looked at the rows of the dead, at the sackcloth over their faces, and when he spoke again his voice was very low. "My father was a hunter. His father before him. We keep the ledger because we believe the kingdom is worth the cost of the ink." He swallowed. "I don't know what I believe tonight."

"Good," Silas said. "Doubt is the only honest thing anyone's offered me since this started."

* * *

They buried whom they could, which was everyone, because there was no one else left to do it.

It took the better part of two days. Wren dug with a spade from the ruined smithy, working in silence, refusing every offer to trade off until his palms bled through his gloves. Silas walked the rows of the barn and listened to each of the dead in turn—properly, fully, with the deep charms she'd sworn never to waste—and gave each of them what she could: the words. *You died quickly. Your family knows. You are going into the ground with your name spoken.*

Some of them had messages. She carried those too, whisper by whisper, and spoke them into the earth above each grave: *tell Nellie the cellar money's under the loose stone.* *Tell my son the boat needs caulking on the port side.* *Tell—tell—* and sometimes nothing, just a name, repeated, the way the dying repeat the name of whoever they loved best, which was almost never who anyone expected.

On the second evening she came at last to the row she had been circling for two days, the way water circles a drain.

Her mother's grave was already there, of course. Eleven years old, the headstone soft with lichen, the letters worn to suggestions: **MARA RAVENSWORTH, WHO KEPT THE BEES.** Beside it, newer, cruder: a cairn of river stones her brother Joss had stacked the year he died, because the village had no mason anymore and no coin for one. Joss. Who had begged her to stay when she left. Who had stood at the crossroads with their mother's beeswax candle and said, *you'll come back for the planting, won't you,* and she had said yes, and meant it, and had not come back for eleven plantings.

And beside the cairn, fresh-turned, a grave the size and shape of a grown man.

Silas knelt. The ground was raw. Whoever had buried him—before the riders came, or in the chaos after—had done it hurriedly, badly, the way grief digs when there's no time to grieve properly. She pressed her palm flat to the soil, and the listening came unbidden, the way it always did with blood, and she heard Joss's last living hour the way you hear weather through a wall: muffled, immense, unstoppable.

*—she'll come back, Mam always said she'd come back, tell her the bees swarmed twice this summer, tell her I kept the hive from the frost like she showed me, tell her—*

Silas did not remember falling forward. She remembered the cold of the turned earth against her forehead, and the sound she was making, which was not crying exactly but something older than crying, something the body does when it has run out of more articulate grief. Wren appeared at the edge of her vision and, to his enormous credit, did not touch her. He stood in the long evening light with his bleeding hands at his sides and kept watch, the way you keep watch over something that is being born or something that is burning.

When she finally rose, her knees were stiff and the sky had gone the color of cooling iron.

"He kept the bees," she said. "Eleven years. He kept them from the frost."

Wren nodded slowly.

"And some clerk wrote *cleansed* in a ledger." She looked down at the grave, at the cairn, at her mother's worn stone with its little carved bee that she herself had chiseled at fourteen, her first true work, the piece that had told her what her hands were for. "They weren't even guilty of anything. Sympathy. Suspected sympathy. Unverified." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, broken-backed. "Do you know what I did to hide? Eleven years. I never said my name twice in the same city. I carved mourning charms small enough to fit inside a thimble. I let my brother die thinking I'd forgotten him, because forgetting him was safer than writing him a letter."

"Silas—"

"I chose invisibility." She turned to face him, and she felt something settle in her chest as she spoke, something locking into place like the last tumbler of a lock. "I chose it every single day, and I called it survival, and I told myself the kingdom was bigger than me and my fear was rational and someone else would fight the fight. Well. The kingdom came to Millbrook, Wren. The kingdom came to a village of beekeepers and millers and burned it to the ground over a rumor, and wrote one word about it, and moved on." She looked north, toward the distant haze where the capital sat, where something wore a dead king's face and fed on loyalty and lies. "So here is my vow, and you may write it in your ledger if you like, in your tidy clerk's hand: I am done hiding, and I am done being small, and I will unbind whatever wears that crown if it takes the gift out of me, or the life out of me, or both. Whatever it costs—I will pay it. Because I have already paid the other price. The price of doing nothing. And I know its weight to the ounce."

The wind moved through the elms on the green, carrying ash and the last warmth of the day. Somewhere in the ruined village, a shutter swung and knocked against a wall, patient, rhythmic, like a heart.

Wren opened the ledger.

She thought for a moment he was going to record her vow—that some part of the hunter in him would reach for the pen out of habit—and instead he tore the page free. The Millbrook entry. *Cleansed. No further action required.* He folded it once, twice, and held it out to her.

"Burn it," he said. "Or keep it. Either way, I'm not writing that word again." He paused, and something moved across his face that was not quite a smile and not quite a wound. "From here to the capital, this book records nothing unless I see it myself. And what I'm going to see, I suspect, is going to make me tear out a great many pages."

* * *

They rode north at dawn, past the crossroads where Joss had stood with the beeswax candle, past the boundary stone where the village lands ended. Silas did not look back. Looking back was a luxury of people with homes to return to, and she had just spent two days burying hers.

But at the top of the ridge she stopped once, and took out the folded page, and read it one last time in the early light.

Then she noticed what exhaustion had hidden from her the night before—a detail at the bottom of the torn page, in the officer's cramped initials and, beneath them, a routing mark. Every hunter's report bore the seal of the office that received it. Most went to the Marshalcy in the capital.

This one bore a different stamp: a crowned circle, bisected by a single vertical line, pressed in wax so old its recipe predated the current reign.

"Wren," she said quietly. "Where do these reports actually go?"

He leaned across, frowning. "That's not the Marshalcy's seal. That's..." He trailed off. His face did something complicated. "I've only ever seen that mark on documents from the inner court. The private household. The king's own—" He stopped.

"The king's own," Silas finished for him.

Six days ago, an officer had ridden into Millbrook on a rumor, slaughtered four hundred people, and sent his one-word report not to the hunters' marshal but directly to the thing that wore the crown. Directly to whatever was hungry enough to call this *cleansed* and consider the matter settled.

She folded the page into her cuff, next to her deepest charms, and turned her horse north.

"Hold on to your ledger, hunter," she said. "I think we're going to need every blank page it has."

Below them, Millbrook smoked gently in the morning light, and the bees—Joss's bees, someone's bees, escaped from their burned hives—rose in a golden cloud over the ruin and drifted north along the road ahead of them, as if they knew the way.
