## 6. The Gilded Court

The summons came on paper the color of old cream, sealed with wax the red of a wound that refuses to close.

*By order of His Majesty King Aldric, Third of That Name, the charm-smith "Maren Voss" of Thornhollow is commanded to present herself at the Gilded Court, there to demonstrate her craft before the throne.*

The name was not hers. Wren had written it in a careful clerk's hand two nights before, by candlelight, in a room that smelled of tallow and fear. *A dead woman's name,* he'd said. *No one checks a dead woman twice.* Silas had almost laughed — almost — because that was precisely how she had lived for five years: as a woman no one bothered to check. But the joke died somewhere between her ribs and her throat, because the seal was real, and real seals meant real roads, and real roads led to the one place in all the realm she had spent half a lifetime learning never to go.

Now the palace rose out of the morning haze like a mountain that had been taught to wear jewels.

Silas saw it first from the river road, where the barge carrying her and six other summoned artisans bent eastward with the current. It did not look built so much as grown — spires of white stone furred with gold leaf, terraces cascading down toward the water in tiers of gardens so green they seemed painted. Fountains threw silver ropes into the air. Peacocks screamed from the balustrades. Everything about the place insisted on its own beauty the way a drunk insists on his own sobriety, loudly, repeatedly, daring you to look too closely.

And beneath it all — beneath the water-lapped foundations, beneath the silt and the pilings and the bones of the laborers who had drowned pouring those foundations a century ago — something was listening.

She felt it before she could name it. A pressure against the inside of her skull, faint and patient, like a fingertip resting on a drumhead. Not a voice. The dead had voices; she had spent years learning their textures, the gravel of grandfathers, the wet whisper of the drowned, her mother's low humming hum. This was none of those. This was attention itself, vast and unhurried, sweeping across the river road the way a lighthouse sweeps the sea.

Silas folded her hands in her lap and became, with the discipline of long practice, a woman of no consequence.

Beside her on the bench, Wren sat disguised as her hired guard, chainmail borrowed, face carefully bored. He had not spoken since dawn. She could feel him wanting to — feel it the way one feels weather coming — but he kept his silence, and she was grateful. There were things neither of them could say aloud anymore without the words changing shape between them.

The barge kissed the dock. The court received them.

* * *

Everything inside the palace was gilded, and everything that was gilded was watching.

Silas was processed through a colonnade whose marble columns had been wrapped in gold to the height of a man's reach — as if even stone needed to be reminded of its rank. Servants glided past in livery of white and scarlet, their faces smoothed into expressions of perfect, bloodless courtesy. Courtiers clustered in alcoves like orchids, beautiful and rootless, their conversation a soft continuous music of nothing: the price of falcons, the cut of a rival's gown, the scandalous lateness of spring.

And through it all ran the pressure, the attention, the great slow turning of something that noticed.

She tested it carefully, the way one tests ice. In the colonnade she let her gift open a hair's width — barely enough to taste the air of the place — and what came back nearly buckled her knees.

Death here was not quiet. Death here was layered, sedimented, pressed flat like flowers in the pages of an enormous book. The palace had stood for four generations, and every generation had left its dead in the walls: servants who died in attics and were carried out through sculleries and forgotten before sundown; guardsmen whose names lived only in the grooves their boots wore into stair-stone; children — there were always children — lost to fevers, tucked into beds that became other children's beds. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They did not speak. They *hummed*, the way a struck bell hums after the clapper stills, and the sound of all that buried life went through Silas like cold water through cloth.

But under the hum, under everything, lay the silence.

It took her until the second antechamber to understand what she was hearing, and when she understood, her hands went cold inside her gloves. The palace dead avoided something. Their hum bent around a single point in the architecture the way a stream bends around a stone — and the stone was above her, high in the heart of the building, in whatever room the throne occupied. The dead would not go near it. Even in death, even as diffuse as fog, they gave it a wide berth.

*A thing the dead are afraid of.* She filed the thought away where she kept the thoughts that could kill her, and smiled at a passing chamberlain, and curtseyed when curtseyed to, and let herself be led.

The summoned artisans were assembled in a gallery adjoining the audience hall: a glassblower from the coast with burn-scarred forearms, a tapestry-weaver who kept touching her own sleeves as if to confirm they were real, a clockmaker, a bookbinder, two jewelers, and Silas, the charm-smith, holding a plain wooden case containing three carvings so harmless they might have been sold at any market stall in the kingdom. Love-knots. A luck-tooth. Nothing that whispered. Nothing that remembered. She had made them herself, honestly, which was somehow the strangest work she'd done in years.

They waited. The clockmaker's clocks ticked against each other, slightly out of step, and the sound filled the gallery like insects.

Then the doors opened, and trumpets said the king's name, and everyone sank to the floor, and Silas looked up because looking up was expected —

— and felt her gift tear wide open without her permission.

* * *

King Aldric came into his hall the way light comes through a window: beautifully, and without any evidence of a person behind it.

He was not an old man, though he had reigned long enough that older men called themselves young beside him. He moved down the length of the hall with a grace that made the watching courtiers lean like grass in wind — each gesture finished, each pause deliberate, his head turning at exactly the moments a head should turn. His face was handsome in the way coins are handsome. He smiled at the right people. He nodded at the right intervals. And every part of it was flawless.

And his bones were silent.

Silas had heard the bones of the living only rarely — a murmur under the voice, a second heartbeat made of memory, the animal knowledge the body keeps of its own ending. Everyone had it. Everyone alive rang, faintly, the way a full cup rings when tapped. But King Aldric, walking toward her down sixty feet of gilded floor, made no sound at all in that deeper register. No murmur. No ring. He passed across her opened senses like a boat over deep water: all surface, and underneath, nothing.

Not hidden. Not shielded. *Empty.* Silent as river stones.

Her breath went thin. Beside her, she heard rather than saw Wren shift his weight — he couldn't feel what she felt, but he could feel her feeling it, and some part of him had already drawn the same conclusion they had traveled a hundred miles to test. The king was dead. The king had been dead for years. And the thing wearing him walked, and waved, and smiled, and no one knew, and no one *could* know, except the dead, who would not come near it, and the one bone-carver kneeling on the cold marble with her heart trying to climb out through her teeth.

The king reached the dais. Sat. The crown was brought to him — not worn, Silas noticed, but borne on a velvet cushion and set upon a stand beside the throne, near his hand, the way a man sits near a fire.

Gold. Old gold, darker than the gilding everywhere else, worked into a circlet of intertwined branches. It did not gleam. It *absorbed*, drinking the light of the hall into itself and giving nothing back, and around it the palace dead fell silent so completely that Silas felt the absence like a hole in her ear.

The king raised one hand. The hall hushed.

"Artisans of the realm." His voice was lovely. That was the worst of it — the voice was *lovely*, warm and measured, pitched to carry comfort to the last row. "Your king is pleased by excellence. Show us your craft, and be rewarded."

One by one they presented. The glassblower offered a vase blown thin as breath. The weaver unrolled a hunting scene so lifelike the hounds seemed mid-cry. The king praised each in turn, and his praise was gracious and empty as rain on glass, and all the while Silas knelt with her eyes lowered and felt the attention rolling off the crown in slow tides — over the courtiers, over the artisans, over *her* — tasting, weighing, the way a tongue explores a sore tooth.

It was not searching for her. It was simply making sure. Making sure of everything. Loyalty, she thought suddenly, understanding arriving whole and terrible: *it feeds on loyalty.* Every bow in this hall, every murmured devotion, every lie of love — it drank them. The whole gilded court was a pasture, and the flock grazed willingly, and never once looked up.

"Charm-smith."

Her name — her false name — hung in the air. The hall waited. Her knees told her to rise and her body obeyed a half-second late, and she crossed to the dais with the wooden case held level before her, thirty feet, twenty, ten, each step bringing her closer to the silence that lived inside the king.

Up close, Aldric's beauty failed her entirely. His skin was fine. His eyes were blue and clear. But there was nothing behind the eyes — no flicker of thought arriving, no small human lag between seeing and knowing. He looked at her the way a mirror looks.

"You carve bone," the king said. It was not quite a question.

"Mourning pieces, Majesty." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "Remembrances. Small comforts."

"Comforts." Something moved behind the handsome face — not a smile, but the memory of where a smile lived. "The dead are a comfort, then."

"Their memory is, Majesty."

"And do your charms hold memories? Truly?" The blue eyes did not blink often enough. "Or is it superstition? Pretty trinkets for grieving fools?"

Every instinct screamed at her to give the safe answer, the dull answer, the answer that ended conversations. But the crown sat three feet from her hand, and the silence poured off it like cold off winter stone, and beneath her terror Silas felt the other thing rise — the thing that had outweighed survival once already, on the night she chose curiosity over flight. She wanted to know what it knew. She wanted to know if it knew *her*.

"Some say the bone remembers," she said quietly. "I only carve what I'm asked."

The hall had gone very still around her. Courtiers exchanged glances like cards. The king regarded her for a long moment — three seconds, five — and then laughed, warm and ringing, and the court laughed with him in relieved chorus, and the sound rolled through the hall like a wave washing something clean.

"A honest tradeswoman," the king declared. "We shall have one of your mourning pieces, then. For the queen dowager, who does not leave her garden. Carve her something gentle. She is easily distressed."

"As Your Majesty commands."

"See that she is paid," the king said pleasantly, to the chamberlain, and turned away, and the audience rolled on, and Silas backed away with her eyes down, and it was not until she had returned to the line of artisans that she understood what had happened.

The crown had not stopped attending to her. From the moment she'd risen, its attention had stayed on her like a hand resting on the back of her neck — light, proprietary, curious. *Carve her something gentle.* Was it a commission or a test? Did it know what she was, or did it merely enjoy the smell of gift the way a shark enjoys one drop of blood in a mile of sea?

She knelt. She breathed. The clockmaker was presenting a clock; the king was praising it; the world continued its pageant.

And then a gloved hand brushed hers, quick as a moth, and something slid between her fingers.

* * *

She found the note an hour later, in the antechamber where the summoned artisans waited for their escort out. She read it in the shadow of a pillar, cupped in both palms like a moth she was afraid would escape.

The handwriting was small, slanted, elegant, and urgent:

*You knelt too well. You listened too hard. I watched you listen.*

*It knows you came.*

Silas read it twice. Then she looked up, very slowly, along the length of the antechamber — at the servants gliding, the courtiers drifting, the gilded everything glittering its loud insistence on beauty —

— and found, across the room, a woman in dove-grey watching her. Not staring. Watching, the way one watches a bird one hopes will land. Dark-haired, perhaps forty, with the kind of composed face that has been composing itself for decades, and eyes that held no courtier's emptiness at all.

Lady Maren did not smile. She did not nod. She simply met Silas's gaze for the length of one breath — long enough to say *yes, I wrote it; yes, I meant it; yes, we must talk* — and then turned, gracefully, and dissolved into the traffic of the court.

Outside the high windows, the fountains threw their silver ropes into the air. Somewhere above, in a hall full of bows and lies, a dead king praised a clock.

Silas closed her fist around the note until the paper bit her palm, and thought: *It knows I came. And someone in this nest of vipers wants me to know that it knows.*

That was either salvation or bait.

She would find out which tonight — because Lady Maren had already sent, with the note, a second thing: a slip of card bearing a single word in the same slanted hand. *Midnight.* And beneath it, a number.

The number of a door in the west wing, where the dowager's faction slept with one eye open, and where — if the note spoke true and not knives — Silas would finally learn who else in this golden rot was willing to hear the truth.

If the truth didn't hear her first.
