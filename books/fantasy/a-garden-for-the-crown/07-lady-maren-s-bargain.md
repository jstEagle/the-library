## 7. Lady Maren's Bargain

The note had said *the third door past the tapestry of the hunt*, and so Silas counted doors as she walked, her palms damp against the folds of her borrowed gown. The palace at night was a different animal than the palace by day. By day it gleamed—polished marble, gilded cornices, servants moving like well-oiled clockwork—but at night it *breathed*. She could feel it in the walls, that slow arterial pulse of old stone and older secrets, and beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat heard through a feather pillow, the whispering.

Not the crown. Not yet. This was something else—the palace's own dead, murmuring in the mortar the way they murmured in everything she touched. Servants who had died in service, perhaps. Children born and lost in these corridors. She kept her hands tucked into her sleeves and did not touch anything she didn't have to.

*Third door past the tapestry.*

The tapestry itself was enormous, a hunting scene rendered in thread so fine it seemed to ripple when the torchlight moved: hounds, horses, a stag caught mid-leap, its glass-bead eye catching fire from the flame. Silas paused before it despite herself. Something about the stag's eye held her—the way it seemed less afraid than *watchful*.

*They hung their victories where everyone must see them,* her mother used to say. *That's how you know a house is built on fear.*

She moved on. One door. Two.

The third was oak, iron-banded, unmarked by any crest. It opened before she knocked—or rather, it opened a hand's width, and Lady Maren's voice came through the gap like silk drawn over a blade.

"You weren't followed."

It wasn't a question, but Silas answered anyway. "No one follows a charm-smith summoned to court. They assume she's already owned."

A pause, then a soft sound that might have been amusement. The door widened. "Enter quickly."

* * *

The room beyond was a study, but not the kind Silas had imagined nobles keeping. No gilded shelves, no portraits of stern ancestors. Instead: books stacked in precarious towers, maps pinned three deep to every wall, a cold fireplace heaped with papers instead of wood. A single candle burned on the desk, and beside it sat Lady Maren Vess—out of her court silks for once, dressed in plain grey wool that made her look younger and more dangerous, like a hawk that had decided to stop pretending to be a dove.

On the desk between them lay a folded square of linen. Maren nudged it forward with one finger.

"Your payment. As agreed. And before you ask—yes, I checked them myself. Unmarked coin, no serial numbers traceable to any house treasury. If you're searched on your way out, you're simply a smith who was paid too generously for her work."

Silas didn't reach for the coins. "You said you wanted a charm carved. You didn't say what kind."

"I'm getting to that." Maren rose and crossed to the window, standing where the candlelight couldn't reach her face. "How much do you know about the history of this court, Mistress Ravensworth?"

"Enough to carve mourning pieces without offending anyone. That's the extent of my ambition here."

"Your ambition is a lie you wear like armor. I've watched you since you arrived." Maren turned, and her eyes in the half-light were pale and level. "You flinched when the king looked at you yesterday. Not at his face—at his *crown*. Most people bow lower when frightened. You went very still, like prey deciding whether to bolt or freeze. I've only seen that stillness in two kinds of people: soldiers who've survived a massacre, and people who can hear things the rest of us cannot."

The room felt suddenly colder. Silas kept her voice flat. "I'm a bone-carver. Stillness is part of the craft."

"Of course it is." Maren returned to the desk and sat, folding her hands with the deliberate calm of someone laying out cards she already knows will win. "Then let me put my cards down plainly, carver, and you can decide whether to keep pretending. There are seven of us. Seven lords and ladies of this court who have served King Aldric faithfully for years—and who have begun, each in our own season, to suspect that the man we serve no longer exists."

Silas said nothing. The whispering in the walls seemed to lean closer.

"Lord Havelock noticed it first, eleven years ago. He was the king's boyhood tutor; he knew every habit of Aldric's mind. He told me once that the king stopped *aging* in his opinions—that a man of thirty should change his mind about something, even slightly, over twenty years, and ours never has. Not once. Lady Corvane noticed the letters. The king writes beautifully, but he writes in the same hand he wrote in at fifteen—no drift, no fatigue, no tremor. Ink does not remember, carver, but hands do. And Ser Dain—" Maren's mouth twisted. "Ser Dain carried the king's standard at Corvath Field. He says the king took an arrow through the throat that day. He *saw* it. And yet Aldric stood before the army three days later with his voice whole, reviewing troops, and when Dain asked him privately how he'd survived, the king smiled and said, 'Kings don't die.' Nothing since has ever been quite warm again. Not the smile. Not the voice."

"Why tell me this?" Silas asked quietly. "You don't know me."

"I know what you are." Maren leaned forward, and now there was nothing soft in her at all. "I don't know *how* you do it—I suspect you'd cut out my tongue if I guessed aloud—but I know you listen to the dead. And I am offering you a bargain, bone-carver, because we have spent eleven years gathering suspicions and have nowhere to take them. We need proof. Proof that can't be dismissed as ambition or madness. And I'm told—" her gaze dropped meaningfully to Silas's hands, "—that bones hold truth the way ledgers hold debts."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave with your coin and your secret, and we'll likely all die in the dark together. But consider what happens if the hunt finds you first. The decree names your gift. The crown *hunts* your gift. Ask yourself why, carver. Of all the magics in the world—weather-workers, dream-speakers, hedge-witches—why would a king decree death for those who speak with the dead?" Maren smiled, thin and cold. "Unless the dead had something to say about him."

The candle guttered. Somewhere far off in the palace, a door closed with a boom like a coffin lid.

"What do you want from me?" Silas asked.

"A listening charm." Maren drew a folded paper from her sleeve and flattened it on the desk—a floor plan, inked in a careful hand, of a chamber deep in the palace's east wing. "This is the old council annex. We meet there, when we can, because it's the one room in the palace the king never enters. We believe it's warded—we believe *he* avoids it—but belief is not certainty, and paranoia is a poor long-term strategy. We want a way to know if our meetings are heard. Not just by spies." Her finger tapped the paper, once. "By *him*."

"And you think bone can tell you that."

"I think *you* can tell us that. Carve a charm that listens—not to the dead, if you please, but to the living. To footsteps in the corridor. To breath behind doors. Something we can set on the table during our meetings, and which will warn us if we are not alone."

It was, Silas thought, almost laughably modest. What Maren was asking for was a child's toy compared to what Silas could build—and that, precisely, was the danger. Because Silas could build so much more than a listening charm, and Lady Maren was smart enough to suspect it.

"The price," Silas said. "You mentioned archives access."

Maren nodded once, briskly. "The sealed archive beneath the west tower. It requires the king's seal to enter—which none of us can obtain without questions being asked—but Lady Corvane holds a steward's key to the reading room above it, and the catalogue of the sealed collection is stored there. You would have access to the catalogue, and to whatever documents the archive's own indexing permits. Enough to research what you need, I judge, given what I've seen you do with a stranger's knucklebone."

Silas looked down at her own hands. Carver's hands, scarred and steady. Hands that had listened to the dead for fifteen years in hiding, alone, because being known meant being hunted—and now a noblewoman was offering to trade state secrets for exactly the gift that had kept her alive and friendless.

*Allies with agendas,* she thought. *Every ally has an agenda. The question is whether theirs kills you slower than yours kills them.*

"One condition," Silas said. "The charm hears what I build it to hear. I won't carve blind. If I'm making something that sits in a room with seven conspirators while they plot against a crowned thing that eats souls, I want to hear those meetings myself. All of them. Every word."

Something flickered across Maren's face—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "You want to spy on us."

"I want to *survive* you. Those aren't the same thing, but they're cousins." Silas met her eyes. "You get your warning charm. I get the archive. And I keep a thread of hearing in what I make, because I've trusted exactly one person in five years and she's dead. Forgive me if I don't hand seven strangers the power to summon me into a room I can't hear coming."

For a long moment Maren studied her, and then—unexpectedly—she laughed. It was a short, dry sound, genuine as a bone crack.

"Havelock said you'd be either a coward or a jewel. He owes me a bottle of Corvath red." She pushed the linen of coins across the desk. "Done. But understand the shape of the bargain, carver: if that charm ever tells *me* that you've been feeding our words to someone else—if I ever suspect your thread of hearing runs both ways toward the throne—there is no hole in this kingdom deep enough to hide you. Not from me, and certainly not from the thing wearing the king."

"It doesn't need to hide me," Silas said, rising, taking the coin at last. It was heavier than it should have been, or perhaps that was only the weight of the moment settling into her palms. "It already knows I came."

Maren's expression didn't change, but the candle flame bent sideways, as though something in the room had exhaled.

"Yes," the lady said softly. "Lady Corvane says the same thing about her mirrors. Go. The servant passage ends at the laundry stair. And Ravensworth—" Silas paused at the door. "Carve well. Whatever you put into that charm, know that I will spend the rest of my life trying to guess whether it was enough."

* * *

The workshop they'd assigned her was a converted storeroom off the charm-smiths' row, stone-floored and windowless, lit by a single lamp whose oil smelled of tallow and old prayers. It should have felt like a cage. Instead, closing the door behind her, Silas felt something she hadn't felt in years: the particular safety of a locked room where no one knew her name twice.

She laid her tools out on the bench in their worn leather roll—burins, rasps, the tiny hooked knife she used for hollowing. Then she unwrapped the material Maren had provided, and stopped.

Bone. A forearm's length of it, wrapped in oilcloth, dense and yellowed with age. Old bone, dead bone—but not empty. Even before her fingers closed around it, Silas could feel it humming faintly against her palm, like a struck bell that had forgotten how to go silent.

She peeled back the cloth and went very still.

It was a femur. Human. Heavy, thick-knuckled at the joint, the kind of bone that had carried a body up stairs and across battlefields and through forty years of ordinary days. And along the shaft, half-worn but unmistakable, someone had carved a mark she recognized from the archive catalogue she'd memorized in Maren's reading room: the sigil of the royal household guard.

*A guard's bone.* Someone had died in this palace wearing its colors, and someone—Maren? Corvane? one of the seven?—had thought to bring Silas the one material guaranteed to resonate with the building's own dead.

Or perhaps it wasn't a gift at all. Perhaps it was a test. Perhaps Lady Maren wanted to see exactly what the bone-carver would do with a piece of the palace's own history in her hands—how deep her hearing truly ran, how much she'd reveal, how much she'd conceal.

Silas sat with the femur across her knees for a long time, the lamp burning low, the walls whispering their patient, wordless whisper.

Then she picked up her finest burin, and began to carve.

She worked the way she always worked: slowly, reverently, letting the bone tell her what it wanted to become. And the bone wanted to become a warning bell—it practically sang with the desire to *alert*, to cry out at the presence of the living intruder, the listening ear, the crowned shadow at the door. That part was easy. That part she gave Maren honestly, shaping the resonance chambers with a craftsman's precision until the smallest footfall in the annex would set the bone humming like a plucked string.

But underneath it—in the marrow channel, in the hidden chambers only another carver would know to look for—Silas carved the other thing. The deeper thing. The thing she'd promised herself she'd hide.

A second set of chambers, tuned not to the living but to the dead. Tuned to carry voices *back* to her, wherever she was, across whatever distance the palace's own foundation-bones allowed. When Maren's faction met in the annex, Silas would hear every word—not through the charm's public function, but through the private one, the smuggled one, the thread of hearing running silent beneath the honest work like groundwater beneath a field.

And deeper still, where even she barely dared to carve, she left one chamber empty. Unshaped. A hollow waiting to be filled—with what, she didn't yet know. But her hands remembered the feel of the king's silence in the throne room, that terrible absence of living breath, and some instinct older than craft whispered that an empty chamber in a listening bone might someday be the safest place to keep a secret too dangerous to say aloud.

She finished near dawn. The charm was beautiful, in the way that useful weapons are beautiful: a slender rod of polished bone, banded with fine rings of carving, small enough to close in a fist. To Maren's eyes it would read as exactly what she'd asked for—a living-space listener, elegant and obedient.

To Silas's hands, it sang.

She wrapped it in fresh oilcloth, tied it with the plain twine of a tradeswoman, and set it on the bench. Then she sat back, exhausted, her shoulders aching, and let herself feel—for one unguarded moment—the full strangeness of her position. Alone in a locked room in a palace ruled by a dead man, having just carved a double agent out of a dead guard's leg, paid in unmarked coin by a conspiracy of the living.

*My mother would have laughed herself sick,* she thought. And then, unbidden, quieter: *I miss her voice. Not the ghost. Just her.*

Outside the storeroom, the palace woke around her—footsteps, water in pipes, the rattle of shutters, ten thousand living sounds that she had never once needed magic to hear.

Inside the oilcloth, the charm waited, holding its two truths in silence.

And somewhere below her, beneath the west tower, behind a door that required a king's seal, the sealed archive waited too—holding, among its thousand catalogued secrets, an entry Silas had copied out and hidden against her skin:

*Rite of Sovereign Binding — attributed to Queen Elira, Year 312. Status: forbidden. Contents withheld by order of the Crown.*

*Contents withheld by order of the Crown.*

Which meant the crown itself had made sure no one would ever read what was bound inside it.

Three days later, when Maren's page collected the charm, Silas pressed a slip of paper into the boy's hand along with the parcel. On it, in her careful carver's script, she had written only:

*The charm is delivered. Now show me the archive.*

The boy ran. The palace swallowed him.

And in the walls, faint and patient, the dead began—very softly—to sing.
