## 8. The Binding Rite

The archive door was made of ironwood, and it had not been opened in thirty years.

Silas knew this because Wren told her, and because the hinges screamed when he lifted them from their rusted cradles, and because the air that breathed out of the darkness beyond smelled of stopped time—dust and tallow and the faint sweet rot of vellum left too long in its own silence. She stood at the threshold with Maren's lamp held high and let that breath move over her face like a hand.

"Thirty years," she said. "Since the last archivist died."

"The position was never refilled." Maren's voice, behind her, was low and even, the voice of a woman who had learned to speak so that stone walls would carry it nowhere. "His Majesty finds questions... fatiguing. The council found it simpler to let the room sleep."

*His Majesty.* Silas turned the phrase over. Two days in the palace now, disguised as a charm-smith summoned to carve a trinket for the dowager's name-day, and she still could not say the words without feeling the crown listening through them. The king's body had moved beautifully before the court—gracious, measured, a man at the height of his powers—but she had pressed her palm to the marble floor beneath her skirts and heard nothing from him at all. No breath in the bones. No hum. Kings were supposed to leave echoes in the places they ruled, the way fires left smoke. Aldric left nothing. He was a coat hung on a hook, waiting for shoulders.

"Wait here," Silas said.

"I'll wait where I can see you," Maren answered. It was not a request. The lady had positioned herself by the door with her hands folded and her spine straight as a candle, and Silas understood that this was the posture of someone who expected to die standing and had made peace with it. "If the guards come, there is a servant's passage behind the third shelf of geographies. You are small enough for it. I am not. So do not be caught."

It was almost tender, in the way a knife is tender when it tells you which way to fall.

* * *

The archive was a cathedral of shelves.

They rose into darkness on all sides, oak and iron, stacked with ledgers and scrolls and boxes of correspondence tied in ribbon gone the color of old teeth. Silas walked the central aisle slowly, one hand trailing along the spines, and listened—not with her ears but with the deeper hearing, the one she had spent five years teaching herself to muffle. Dead things spoke to those who knew how to lean close. Paper remembered hands. Ink remembered the wrist that drove it. Even here, even after thirty years, the room was crowded.

But the voices here were strange. Faint. Court voices, trained out of all their roughness, speaking in cadences that had been dead before Silas's mother was born—and beneath them, threaded through everything, a silence shaped like a scream.

She felt it first as cold. Then as absence. Then, following it down an aisle marked only with a copper plate bearing a single letter—*R*, for *Rites*, though the plate had been scratched nearly smooth by some deliberate, patient thumb—she understood what she was hearing. Someone had tried to erase this shelf. Not burn it; burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. They had scraped it. Scratched the names off the plates and buried the volumes under mislabeled ones and hoped that silence would do the work of forgetting.

Silence never did. Silence was just a louder kind of speech.

The volume was bound in calfskin gone soft as cloth, its title eaten away. Inside, the pages were dense with two hands: an older, formal script, and a younger one cramped against the margins, writing fast, writing scared. Silas carried the book to the lamp, set it down, and read.

*The rite of continuance,* the old hand began, *set down at the command of Queen Elyssa, widow of King Osric the First, in the winter of his dying.*

She read the opening invocation twice, because the first time her eyes slid off it like rain off glass. It was beautiful. That was the horror of it—it was genuinely beautiful, a litany woven from images any bone-carver would weep over: *let the beloved remain, let the hearth keep its fire, let the crown remember the head that blessed it.* A wife's prayer, carved into law. The old queen had loved a dying man so much she had refused the shape of his leaving, and instead of burying her refusal she had built it into the succession itself, clause upon clause, so that no future king could ever simply die.

*Let the crown hold him. Let the crown hold whoever wears the crown. Let the holding continue until willingly released.*

*Until willingly released.*

Silas sat down on the floor without deciding to. Her legs simply folded, the way they do when the body understands something before the mind consents to.

There it was. In the oldest ink, in the queen's own marginal notes—the younger hand, the archivist's hand, annotating in terror:

*Note: the release requires a vessel both willing and aware. The bound spirit cannot be cast out; it must be *invited out*. The wearer must choose. But see the third appendix — the crown does not permit choice.*

She turned to the appendices with fingers that had begun to shake.

The third appendix was a coroner's record. Or rather, three of them, copied in sequence, spanning sixty years. King Osric the Second—Aldric's grandfather—had died at forty-one of a wasting illness that the physicians recorded with growing confusion, because his body had shown no disease at all. He had simply thinned. The second record belonged to King Osric the Third, who had drowned in a pond in the palace garden at nineteen, in water shallow enough to stand in, while four guards watched from twenty paces and swore afterward that they had seen nothing but a boy standing very still. And the third—

The third record was recent. The third record was a physician's private journal, smuggled into the archive by the last archivist, and it described in clinical detail how King Osric the Fourth—called Aldric since his crowning at age six—had ceased, over a period of years, to initiate speech, then to initiate movement, then, by every measure the physician could devise, to *be present*—while the body continued to reign, to hunt the gifted, to issue proclamations in a voice that carried no living breath.

Three kings' souls. Devoured. Each death recorded as illness, accident, mystery. Each one a child or a young man who had worn the crown because inheritance demanded it, and been eaten for it, slowly, in the most protected rooms in the kingdom.

And none of them had chosen it. That was the part that made Silas press the heel of her hand against her mouth. None of them had ever been given the chance to say *yes, leave me, take the thing out of me*—because the crown punished dissent. The queen's rite required willingness, and the crown ensured there would never be any. It was a lock that had eaten its own key. Sixty years of kings, each one a cage built inside a cage built inside a grieving woman's love, and the thing inside growing fatter and more fluent with every soul it swallowed, until it could wear a king so well that hunters marched out in its voice to unmake children who carved charms for widows.

Silas thought of the widow from her shop, months ago, holding the charm to her ear while her husband's voice said *the blue dress, love, you looked beautiful in the blue dress.* Grief could do this. This was what grief did, unchecked, ungrieved, given power. It kept the beloved past all loving. It made a prison and called it a marriage.

She looked up at Maren, who was watching her from the doorway with the lamp between them throwing their shadows long across the floor.

"Well?" Maren asked.

"It's worse than your faction thinks," Silas said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "You want the king freed. Freed implies he's being kept somewhere. He's being *eaten*. There have been three of them. Your king is the fourth meal, and the thing wearing him has been practicing for sixty years." She closed the book. "And it can't be driven out. The rite needs the wearer to choose. Willingly. Aware. And the crown makes choosing impossible—it punishes dissent with pain. Whoever tries this has to get a message through to a man who may not entirely exist anymore, and convince him to reach for freedom hard enough to matter, inside a prison designed to make reaching hurt."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere far above them, the palace went about its evening—footsteps, doors, the ordinary music of the lie continuing.

"There is one more thing you should know," she said finally. "Before you decide what to do with that book."

"What?"

"The queen dowager. The one whose name-day trinket you're supposedly carving." Maren's eyes did not waver. "Her name was Elyssa. Not the first Elyssa—her granddaughter. But she keeps the old queen's letters. She keeps *everything*. And she has not spoken to the court in eleven years. She tends a walled garden on the eastern grounds and lets no one in."

"A garden," Silas repeated.

"A garden." Maren pushed herself off the doorframe. "I've always found that interesting. Don't you? Of everyone in this palace, the one person who grows things instead of taking them." She paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Take the book. Copy what you need and return it—I'm not certain yet whose loyalty holds, and if that volume is found on me, we both hang. And Lady Ravensworth—"

"That's not my name."

"No," Maren agreed. "Which is why you're useful. Names can be hunted. Careful women cannot." She looked back once, from the dark of the aisle. "The dowager receives no petitions. But she might receive a bone-carver. Old women who grow gardens tend to remember other kinds of planting. Go carefully. And go soon—the feast is in six days, and I am no longer certain the crown doesn't already know your face."

Her footsteps faded up the aisle. Silas sat alone in the archive with the stolen rite open on her knees, and around her the scraped shelves held their breath, and somewhere above—through floors and floors of marble and ceremony—a king's hollow body moved through its evening ritual, wearing a crown full of someone else's hunger.

Six days.

She bent her head over the page and began, in her smallest hand, to copy.
