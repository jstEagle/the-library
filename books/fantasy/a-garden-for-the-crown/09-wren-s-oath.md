## 9. Wren's Oath

The square in Vellmark smelled of wet stone and tallow smoke, and Wren Calloway stood in it with his sword drawn because the writ said he must.

The healer was a girl. That was the first obscenity. Seventeen, perhaps eighteen, with flour still ground into the creases of her knuckles—she had been pulled from her father's bakery not two hours past, and no one had thought to wipe her hands before binding them. Her gift had been small things, the neighbors whispered afterward: a poultice that closed a wound in a night instead of a week, a fever broken by a touch to the brow. Small things. The kind of gift that made a village love you and, in this season of proclamation, made a village report you.

"Kneel," said Captain Drossard, who had a voice like a boot heel on gravel.

She knelt. She did not weep. She looked at the assembled crowd—at her father, held back by two hunters, at the baker's wife clutching her apron, at the children on the steps of the tithe-house—and she lifted her chin the way people do when they have decided that dignity is the only thing left they are permitted to carry.

Wren had executed four gifted since the Proclamation of Unmaking. He told himself each time that the writ was law, that law was the spine of the realm, and that without a spine the kingdom folded like wet paper. He had repeated it so often it had worn smooth as a river stone, and like a river stone it no longer caught anything—not grief, not doubt, not the memory of his own mother humming over a charm carved from his grandmother's finger bone, hidden in a drawer he had never opened again after the hunters came through his childhood town.

But the girl's hands were floured. The girl's hands were floured, and she was kneeling in the rain-washed square of Vellmark with her chin up, and something in Wren's chest cracked along an old seam.

"The writ," Drossard prompted, impatient.

*In the king's name.* Wren opened his mouth to say it. The words were there, worn smooth, ready.

What came out instead was silence, and then: "No."

Drossard turned his head slowly, like a man watching weather turn. "Hunter Calloway."

"She heals." His voice sounded strange to him, thin and far away, as though someone else were speaking through his mouth—which was, he understood dimly, exactly the sort of thing the crown feared most. "That is all she does. She heals."

"That is *enough*," Drossard said. "Gift is a door, and doors let in what we cannot see. Execute the writ."

And there it was—the whole rotten architecture of the last five years laid bare in a single sentence. *A door lets in what we cannot see.* Fear dressed as doctrine. Wren looked down at his own sword hand and found it steady, which surprised him; he had expected it to shake.

"I won't," he said.

The square went very quiet. Rain ticked against the cobbles. Somewhere a dog began to bark and was hushed.

Drossard did not shout. That was the terrible thing about the man—he never shouted. He simply drew his own blade with the sound of a whisper leaving a room, and said, "Then kneel beside her."

For one absurd heartbeat Wren considered it. Kneeling in the square next to a flour-handed girl, both of them waiting for steel. But then he saw her face change—a flicker of horror, not for herself but for him, as if his death would be a weight added to hers rather than shared—and he understood that kneeling would make her death mean nothing. It would be two bodies instead of a question.

So he ran.

He did not remember deciding to run. He remembered the moment of choice the way one remembers a door swinging open in wind: suddenly, and with a draft behind it. He knocked the nearest hunter sprawling, seized the girl's bound wrists and snapped the cord against his knee—her gasp of freed breath was the only payment he would ever receive for everything he was about to lose—and shoved her toward the bakery alley where her father already stood with the back door open.

"Go. Both of you. Don't stop until the marsh road."

"You'll be—" the girl began.

"*Go.*"

They went. And Wren turned to face the hunters he had trained beside, slept beside, bled beside, and saw in their faces not hatred but something worse: confusion. Grief, even. Drossard's expression alone was legible, and legible as a ledger.

"Traitor," the captain said, almost gently, marking the word into the air between them. "Every hunter in the province has your name now, Calloway. Run far."

Wren ran near. He ran into the oldest part of Vellmark, down stairs that predated the kingdom, into the warren beneath the granary row where the walls sweated and the light came green through grates. Because he had realized something in the square, in the half-second before his feet decided for him: if he fled to the borders, he would spend the rest of his life running from a lie. But there was a woman in a workshop above the chandlery who listened to bones, who had shown him a stranger's last words and wept over them as though they were scripture. She was going to the capital. She was going toward the truth of what wore the crown.

If he had to be hunted, he would be hunted *toward* something.

* * *

Silas Ravensworth had been careless, and carelessness, in her experience, was simply death arriving at a leisurely pace.

She had gone out after dark for lamp oil—lamp oil, of all the stupid errands, because she'd burned her stock of charms along with everything else and had sold her good candles weeks ago—and she had taken the long way around the crier's post out of pure spite, and spite, too, was a luxury the hunted could not afford. The blade came from the mouth of Tanner's Wynd, quick and low, meant for the kidney. She twisted enough that it opened her side instead of her back, white-hot, and then her shoulder struck the wall and the world tilted sideways and became a smear of lamplight and boots.

"Hold her—she's marked, the list says marked—"

Two of them. Hunter's grey cloaks. She got her palm against the cobbles and pushed, and the wound tore wider, and blood came hot and shocking down her hip, and she thought with strange clarity: *so this is how it ends, in a wynd that smells of tannin, over nothing.*

Then the lamplight broke apart. There was a sound like a door slamming in a cathedral—one of the hunters folding around a fist to the throat—and the other spun, sword clearing its sheath, and met a second sword coming down on his own with a crack that Silas felt in her teeth.

"Wren?" she said, because the world had gone strange enough that hallucination seemed reasonable.

"Can you walk?"

"No."

"Good," he said, and put her arm over his shoulders and lifted her anyway, one arm under her knees like she weighed nothing more than a bundle of kindling, and carried her at a dead run into the dark.

Behind them, shouting. A whistle—the hunters' three-note signal for quarry sighted, a sound Silas had heard in nightmares for weeks now. Wren took corners without slowing, using his shoulder off the walls to bank like a bird, and Silas pressed her bleeding side against his cloak and tried to decide whether the warmth spreading there was blood or fever or some third thing she didn't have a name for.

"In here," she managed, as they passed the rusted door of the old dye-works cellar. "I know these cellars. All of them. I've slept in worse."

He shouldered the door, dropped down three steps into blackness that smelled of oak gall and old iron, and set her down against the wall with a gentleness that did not match anything else about the last minute of her life.

"Lamp," he said.

"Left pocket. Careful—it's all I—"

He lit it. In its small gold circle she watched his face change as he saw the wound properly: the long opening along her flank, the blood soaking her shift, her hands pressed uselessly against it. He swore once, softly, in a dialect she didn't recognize—border-country words, from the years before his oath.

"How deep?"

"Deep enough to be honest about. Not deep enough to be interesting."

"It needs stitching."

"It needs a miracle, and I burned mine." She laughed, and hated how the laugh shook. "There's a needle in my kit. Left strap. Thread's waxed linen. You've field-stitched before—don't tell me you haven't, every hunter has."

He had. She knew it the moment his jaw tightened. She also knew, from the way he looked at the needle and then at her face, that he was calculating whether she would let him.

"I don't faint," she said. "Much."

He heated the needle in the lamp flame, and his hands, which had held a sword with such violence minutes ago, were careful as a carver's. She talked while he worked, because talking kept her above the pain, and because there were things that needed saying and this seemed as good a time as any to say them.

"They posted your name in the chandlery window. I read it on my way to buy oil. 'Wren Calloway, stripped of writ and rank, to be taken alive if convenient.'" She winced as the needle bit. "Alive if convenient. That's Drossard's phrasing. He wants to do it personally."

"He can want."

"Why?" The question came out harder than she intended. "Why throw it all away? You had five years of orders and a clean conscience—or whatever hunters keep in place of one. One girl in one square wasn't worth your life."

His hands stilled. The lamp guttered, and shadows moved across his face like water over stones.

"She had flour on her hands," he said finally. "Nobody wiped it off. They dragged her out of her father's bakery and nobody thought to—she'd been making bread. That morning she'd been doing the most ordinary holy thing there is, feeding people, and they were going to kill her for it in front of everyone she loved, and I was going to say *in the king's name*, and I realized—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I realized I couldn't remember the last time I'd heard the king speak and believed it. Not doubted it. Just... believed. There's a difference, Silas. Doubt you can live with. You just carry it. But somewhere in the last year the words stopped landing at all. Like listening to a bell with no clapper."

Silas went cold, and not from the wound.

"A bell with no clapper," she repeated.

"What?"

"Nothing. Keep sewing." But her mind was racing ahead of her mouth, down the same track it had run the night she'd held her neighbor's dying bones and heard the decree spoken in a voice with no living breath behind it. A king whose words carried nothing. A bell with no clapper, rung anyway, and the whole kingdom pretending to hear music. "You refused the writ. You know what happens to men who refuse. There's no road back for you. No pardon, no quiet retirement in some garrison town. They will hunt you until one of you is finished."

"Yes."

"And you came for me anyway. To the one place they'll certainly look, eventually, because I'm on the list and you're—"

"I'm not hunting you anymore," Wren said. "I'm asking to follow you."

The needle paused. In the lamplight his eyes were level and unflinching, and Silas, who had spent five years perfecting the art of reading living faces as carefully as dead bones, found nothing false in his. Only exhaustion, and a stubbornness like bedrock, and underneath both, something raw and newly opened that he was trying very hard not to bleed on her.

"Follow me where?"

"To the palace. To whatever made the decree come out of a dead man's mouth. You felt it—you told me you felt it. The words had no breath in them." He tied off the stitch, clipped the thread with his teeth. "I've spent my whole career believing the crown was the spine of the realm. If the spine's been hollow this whole time—if we've been executing children in the name of a corpse—then I want to see it. I need to see it with my own eyes, even if seeing it unmakes me too."

Silas leaned her head back against the damp stone and breathed through the throbbing of her flank. Around them the cellar held its old smells—dye-vats long dry, iron hoops rusting, the mineral breath of groundwater—and beneath it all, faint as a pulse through wool, she could feel the bones of the building, patient and indifferent, holding up a city that had forgotten they existed.

"If we do this," she said at last, "there are terms."

"Name them."

"One: you don't ask me to listen to anyone's bones without their leave. Not prisoners, not suspects, not you. The dead don't get a vote, and neither do you." 

"Agreed."

"Two: when we find the truth—and we will find it, because I am apparently incapable of walking away from a question, God help me—you don't get to decide what's done with it. No handing me to Maren's faction, no trading what I learn back to whichever lord offers you a pardon. What I hear belongs to the dead first and to me second and to no one else at all."

Something flickered across his face—hurt, maybe, that she would think it—but he nodded slowly. "Fair. And fair warning in return: I don't break twice. Once I choose a side, I stay chosen. Whatever you're walking into at that court, you won't shake me loose from it."

"And three," Silas said, and here her voice betrayed her, going rough at the edges, "you tell me why. Truly why. Not the girl with the flour—she's part of it, I believe that—but the rest. Before the capital. Before it matters. I need to know what I'm trusting."

Wren sat back on his heels. For a long moment the only sounds were the drip of groundwater and the small flutter of the lamp flame, and Silas had time to notice that the pain in her side had settled into something bearable, rhythmic, almost companionable, and that she was colder than she should be, and that his cloak was already coming off his shoulders and around her before she could protest.

"My mother hummed," he said quietly. "When she worked. Mending, cooking, digging in the garden—always humming, tunes with no names. When I was eight, a hunter found a charm in our house. My grandmother's work. My mother hadn't known what it did—she'd inherited it, kept it in a drawer, never used it. Didn't matter. Gifted kin counted as gifted, in those days. They took her in a cart on a Tuesday morning and I hummed her tunes for years afterward so I wouldn't forget them, and then one day I realized I'd forgotten, and I joined the hunters the following spring because I thought—" his jaw worked— "I thought if I became the thing that took her, I'd at least understand it. Understand her. Make it make sense."

"And did it?"

"No," he said simply. "It just taught me every way there is to look away."

The lamp flame steadied. Silas found, to her surprise, that her eyes were burning, and blamed the dye-smoke, and knew she was lying to herself, and let it go.

"Your mother's name," she said. "Tell me it."

"Cally. Cally Calloway—her husband's surname, she thought it was funny."

"If we live through this," Silas said, "if we reach the other side of whatever's wearing that crown—I'll carve her a charm. Bone, proper bone, done right. And if any part of her is anywhere a voice can reach, she'll hum for you again."

Wren stared at her. In the lamplight his eyes were bright and wet and he did not blink it away, and neither of them said anything else, because some promises are too large for speech and can only be held in silence, the way a cupped hand holds water.

It was much later—after he had banked the lamp, after she had drifted at the edge of fever-sleep with his cloak around her and his sword across his knees—that she heard him say it to the dark, thinking her asleep:

"I'm sorry I ever pointed a blade at you. I'm sorry for all of them. Every one."

*Forgiven,* said the cellar's old bones, in their wordless way. *Not by me. But somewhere.*

* * *

They traveled by night for six days, moving through culverts and coal-chutes and the buried spine of the old aqueduct, and Silas learned that Wren Calloway had a map of the underworld in his head that rivaled her own—hunter training, he admitted, included learning where the hunted hide. On the seventh night they surfaced in a drover's barn outside the market town of Hessel, and Silas, her wound knitting at last into an angry pink seam, sat in hay that smelled of summer despite the season and felt the strangest sensation.

Safety. Borrowed, temporary, conditional safety—and company.

Wren was sharpening his blade by the light of a shuttered lantern, the whetstone whispering in long strokes, and without looking up he said: "Maren's faction has a contact in Hessel. A wine merchant. If we're going to reach the capital as your escort-charter requires, we need papers, and papers need coin, and coin needs Lady Maren to be worth the trust you gave her."

"She gave me a note that said *it knows you came*. Trust is a strong word for what we have."

"Trust is a strong word for anything worth having." He tested the edge against his thumbnail. "There's something else. News from the capital, from the drovers—they're preparing for a state feast. The Feast of Loyalty, they're calling it. Every noble house summoned. The king will appear in person."

Silas felt the words land like stones dropped into still water, ripples spreading outward through everything she'd built these last weeks: the plan, the disguise, the slow careful approach. A feast. A hall full of nobles. A crown that listened.

"He'll be there," she said. "The thing wearing him."

"And you'll be there too," Wren said, "if we move fast enough. Which means we stop being travelers tonight and start being something else." He looked at her then across the small ring of lantern-light, and whatever either of them might have named the thing between them—partnership, debt, penance, the unnamed third thing that had been growing steadily since a cellar smelling of oak gall—neither of them reached for a word. Words could be confiscated. This could not.

"Drovers leave for the capital at dawn," Wren said. "Sleep. I'll take first watch."

She lay back in the hay, one hand resting lightly over the stitched seam in her side, and stared up at the rafters where swallows' nests hung like old charms, and listened—for habit, for comfort—to the great slow voice of the earth's bones turning beneath her.

And beneath the ordinary murmur of stone and root, faint but unmistakable, rising up from the direction of the capital like smoke from a distant fire, she heard something new.

Screaming. Many voices, layered and distant and wrong—screaming with no bodies attached, echoing off foundations she had never touched, as though the palace itself had begun, at last, to cry out.

"Silas?" Wren's voice, sharp with alarm. "What is it? What do you hear?"

She sat up in the hay, blood draining from her face, and said: "The walls of the palace are screaming. Wren—something's happened at court. Something's happened to Maren."
