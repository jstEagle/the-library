## 10. Voices in the Walls

The wound in Silas's side had opinions about everything.

It spoke when she rolled onto her right elbow, when she laughed—rarely, and only at Wren's terrible jokes, which she suspected he told deliberately to test whether laughing still hurt—and it spoke loudest of all in the hour before dawn, when the palace above Maren's wing went quiet and the pain rose like water in a locked room, finding every low place in her.

She had learned the wound's language in four days of convalescence. It said *lie still*. It said *you bled more than you let him see*. She lay in a bed too soft for a woman who had slept eleven years on a straw tick above her workshop, staring at a canopy embroidered with stag-horns and thinking about the seam of the world.

Because the palace had a seam. She had found it the first night, half-fevered, when she had pressed her palm flat against the cool stone of the wall beside her bed—the way she used to press her ear to her mother's ribs to hear the humming—and the stone had answered.

Not the way living things answered. The way old things answered. The way bone answered.

The palace was built on bones.

She had known this the way everyone in the realm knew it, the way you knew the sky was blue and bread was dear: the palace of Aldric's line stood on the Ossuary Foundations, the great spine-work of the first dynasty, when the builders had mixed the honored dead into the mortar so that the kingdom's house would literally rest upon its ancestors. A pious custom, the histories called it. A foundation of loyalty.

Silas had spent four days lying against that foundation, and she now knew what the histories did not say.

The dead in the walls were still there. All of them. And they had been waiting a very long time for someone who could hear.

* * *

On the fifth night, when the fever had finally broken and the wound had settled into a dull, negotiable grumble, Silas got out of bed.

The wing was dark. Somewhere beyond the door, a guard shifted his weight, boots creaking; beyond him, the long corridors of the palace breathed with the small sounds of the living—snoring kitchen boys, a cat's claws on flagstone, Lady Maren herself still awake three rooms away, turning a page, turning it back, unable to read. Silas catalogued all of it without trying, the way she catalogued everything now. The palace was a sea of living sound, and she floated in it.

But beneath the living sounds, there was the other thing.

She crossed the room barefoot, one hand pressed to her bandaged side, and knelt at the wall where the paneling met the stone. The mortar here was old—older than the rest of the wing, older than the pretty paneling some court carpenter had nailed over it a century ago. She could feel it through her fingertips before she ever reached out with the gift: a grain, a texture, a patience.

She laid her palm flat.

The world dropped away like a floor opening.

*—cold, cold, they pour us in cold and the master says it is an honor, says our bones will hold up the king's hall for a thousand years, and I am sixteen and I believe him—*

Silas gasped. The voice was not a voice. It was a texture, a pressure, a memory soaked into mineral the way blood soaks into linen and never quite washes out. But her gift translated it, as it always did, into something her mind could hold: a boy's voice, a mason's apprentice, mixed into the foundation itself two hundred years and more ago.

*—they said the mixing would be gentle. They did not tell me I would still be aware. They did not tell me the walls would dream—*

"I hear you," Silas whispered. Her breath fogged the cold stone. "I hear you."

The wall went still. Then, all along it, all through it, down and out in every direction, something vast and buried turned its attention toward her like a field of flowers turning toward an unexpected sun.

*—someone hears. Someone hears. Do you hear, Marta? Do you hear, old Tam? Someone hears us—*

They came then. Not all at once—there was no rush in the walls, nothing in the walls had been in a hurry for centuries—but one by one, the way the poor have always come to a woman with bread. A scullery maid who had fallen into a cistern in the third king's reign and been deemed not worth the trouble of retrieving. A laundress who died of lung-rot at thirty-one, her hands forever wrinkled with other people's wash. A gardener. Two guardsmen. A child—a child, a kitchen girl of six, who had hidden from an angry cook in a flour chest and suffocated there, and whose small presence had been folded into the palace's bones without ceremony, without name, without anyone at all being told.

*They put me in the wall with the flour still on me,* the little one said, wondering, unresentful. *It was in my hair. I can still feel it.*

Silas pressed her forehead against the stone and wept, quietly, the way she had learned to weep—without sound, without witness, the weeping of a woman who had spent her whole life being the only ear in a world full of mouths.

She had thought she understood loneliness. She had built her whole life inside it, a small careful architecture of silence, and she had believed she knew its every room.

She had not known this room. This was loneliness on the scale of centuries. This was a hundred dead servants, nameless in the mortar, who had lain in the dark beneath the glory of the crown for generations, hearing everything—the feasts, the prayers, the coronations—and never once being heard. The kingdom's house rested on its ancestors, the histories said. The histories did not say the ancestors were awake.

*You're the bone-listener,* said the laundress, whose name, it turned out, was Sefa, and who had been dead for a hundred and forty years and still remembered the smell of lye. *We felt you come. We felt you the way you feel weather. The walls have been talking about you for days.*

"What do the walls say?"

*They say you're going to the throne room eventually,* Sefa said. *Everyone who comes here with a wound and a purpose goes to the throne room eventually. And they say—* the laundress's voice hesitated, the first hesitation, and Silas felt the whole foundation gather itself, the way a household gathers itself to deliver terrible news— *they say you should speak to the one at the very bottom. The deepest one. The first.*

* * *

She went down the next night.

Maren's wing stood at the palace's edge, and beneath it the foundations descended farther than any servant's stair admitted. But the dead knew the ways. The little flour-girl led her—Silas followed the tug of the child's presence through a wine cellar, past a bricked-up archway, down a stair that had not felt a living foot in three hundred years, into a low chamber where the foundation stones were not dressed stone at all but something older, something that had been standing when the first king was crowned.

There, at the bottom, the bones were different.

Silas knelt. The wound pulled; she ignored it. The air in the chamber tasted of iron and candle-snuff, of very old time. She put both palms against the deepest stone and reached down through her gift the way she had never reached before, past the servants, past the masons, down into the dark where the oldest presence lay folded like a letter no one had opened.

And the first king spoke.

*—is it time? Is it finally—* The voice was a ruin. It was a voice that had been speaking into silence for so long that it had forgotten what answering sounded like, and it stumbled now, caught between hope and the grief of hoping. *—a listener. A true listener. Oh. Oh, I had begun to believe I had dreamed the world. Tell me your name.*

"Silas Ravensworth."

*Silas.* He said it as if setting it down somewhere safe. *Silas. I am Alderic. The first. They crowned me in this hall when it was open sky, before the roof, before the walls, before—* A long stillness. *Before my wife learned what love could be made to do.*

Silas's hands went cold against the stone. "The crown."

*You know of it.*

"I've read the rite. Or part of it. In the sealed archive." She had told Wren, in the worst hour of the night, everything she'd found—the old queen's binding, the spirit in the gold, the three kings devoured. Wren had sat with it the way a man sits with a wound of his own. "I came to ask you about it. You were there when it was made."

*There.* A sound that might have been laughter, in a voice that had forgotten laughter. *I was not there. I was it. I am the first of them, Silas Ravensworth. I am what the crown wore before it learned to wear kings.*

The chamber seemed to tilt. Silas gripped the stone. "You're bound in the foundation?"

*I am what was left over.* The old king's presence gathered, strained, a man trying to stand in a room too low for him. *When my wife bound my spirit into the crown, she took the whole of me—or she believed she did. But a soul is not a single thread. It is a weaving. She caught the warp of me, the part that loves and endures, and she pulled it into the gold. The weft remained. The part that remembers. The part that knows what was done. She could not bind my knowledge of her sin, because to bind it would have been to bind her own guilt, and her hands would not make the charm.* Another stillness, longer. *So she buried it. She had my body laid in the deepest foundation, and she spoke a quieting over it, and she believed she had buried my grief along with my bones.*

"And she didn't."

*I have been under this palace for two hundred years, child. I have felt every king sit down upon my grave. I felt my grandson wear the crown and I felt the thing inside it begin to eat him—slowly, so slowly, the way rot eats a beam, from the inside out. I felt the second king go. I felt the third. I felt my great-grandson Aldric—* the name caught, was repeated, *—Aldric, my namesake, a boy of nine years with his father's grave-dust still on his mourning clothes, crowned too young, crowned precisely because a child's soul is easiest to fold around a foreign thing. I have felt him for thirty years, Silas. I have felt the boy he was get smaller and smaller inside himself, like a light behind a closing door. And I have lain here in the dark and known, and been unable to tell a single living soul, because the quieting my wife laid on me was made of the same art that made the crown, and it does not break. It only thins.*

"It's thin now," Silas said. "I can hear you."

*Because you are the first true listener to press her hands to my stone since my wife died.* The old king's voice came closer, or seemed to, rising through the foundation like water rising through a well. *And so I will spend what thinness I have left on what matters. Listen to me. Listen as no one has listened to me. The crown cannot be unbound from outside. My wife's rite was built to hold against every force but one—the consent of the vessel. The thing in the gold knows this. It is the one law it cannot break, because it was written into it by a woman who, whatever else she was, loved me, and built the binding honest in this single way: it can be ended only from within, by the king who wears it, freely chosen, while the king still lives.*

"While he still lives," Silas repeated. Her heart had begun to beat strangely. "What happens if the vessel dies?"

*Then the binding passes on.* The words came heavy as stone settling. *To the next crowned head. It has been waiting thirty years for Aldric to fail, to sicken, to die of anything at all, so that it might fold itself around his heir. There is no heir yet. The court grows anxious about it. The court's anxiety feeds it—did you know? It eats loyalty and lies, and a court is nothing but loyalty and lies, and every year the harvest is richer. But an heir—a new young vessel—* the old king's voice frayed, *—that would be a feast. That would be centuries more.*

Silas sat back on her heels. The wound in her side throbbed in time with her heart, and for once she was grateful for it, grateful for the blunt animal insistence of a living body.

"The counter-rite," she said slowly. "The one I found in the archive. It says the vessel must choose. It doesn't say the vessel must be strong enough to choose."

*No,* the first king said. *It does not. And that is the cruelty of it, child, and the hope. Aldric cannot choose. Not now. The crown has worn him so long that his own will is a room with no windows—he does not remember what wanting feels like that is only his own. If you set the choice before him tomorrow, he could not take it. Not because he is a coward. Because there is almost nothing left of him to take it with.*

"Then he's lost."

*I did not say that.* And now the old king's presence did something she had never felt a dead presence do: it leaned toward her. It pressed against the thinning quieting with everything it had left, and for a moment Silas heard him not as a voice in bone but as a man, a father, a founder, urgent and mortal and afraid. *I said there is almost nothing left. Almost. I have felt him for thirty years. Once a year—once, only once, on the anniversary of his crowning, when they put the crown on his head again before the whole court and the binding is at its tightest and its most strained—once a year, the thing in the gold must grip him with its whole strength, and for one breath the grip is too tight, and the boy underneath feels it. Feels everything. Feels the cage. Once a year, Silas, for the length of a single breath, Aldric knows he is in a cage. I have felt that breath thirty times. It is the only hour of the year I do not grieve.*

Silas's mouth had gone dry. "The anniversary."

*They will crown him again soon. The court is already sewing for it. And if anyone stands close enough, in that breath—if someone with your gift is close enough, with a charm ready, with the counter-rite in her hands—* the old king's voice was going, thinning back into the stone, spending its last coins, *—he might find the door. One breath is not much. But I was a king, and I will tell you what I learned on my throne: a kingdom can turn on less. Go to him in that breath. Give him the choice in the only moment the cage has a door. And Silas—*

"Yes."

*Tell my namesake his ancestor forgives him. He will believe it is the madness they punish dissent with. Tell him anyway.*

The presence sank. The stone went ordinary. Silas knelt a long time in the dark of the buried chamber, her palms still against the foundation, listening to the hundred small dead of the walls murmur around her like a congregation after service—*did she hear him, did you hear what he said, someone finally heard*—and she felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders and find it fit.

She was the only one who could hear. That had always been her loneliness. She understood now that it was also her office.

* * *

Wren found her at dawn, sitting in the window of her room with her boots still dusty from the buried stair, watching the light come up over the capital.

"You were gone." He did not ask where. He had learned in three weeks of uneasy partnership that Silas went where the dead required, and that the dead required strange things. But he looked at her face and his hand drifted toward the knife at his belt, the way it did when the news was bad. "What happened?"

Silas turned from the window. Below, in the courtyards, she could see the preparations already underway: scaffolding going up along the ceremonial route, banners being unrolled, seamstresses' carts rattling in through the gates. The whole palace was beginning, in its living body, to prepare for the Feast of Loyalty and the re-crowning.

"The crown has to come off while the king still lives," she said. "And he can only choose it himself. And he can only choose it—" she heard the old king's voice one more time, frayed and urgent, a kingdom turning on less— "for one breath a year. Wren. When is the anniversary ceremony?"

Wren went very still. Then he looked out the window at the scaffolding, at the banners, at the palace dressing itself in gold, and Silas watched him do the same arithmetic she had already done, and arrive at the same terrible sum.

"Nine days," he said.
