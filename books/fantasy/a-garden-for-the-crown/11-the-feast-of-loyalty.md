## 11. The Feast of Loyalty

The invitation arrived on vellum thick as skin, sealed in wax the color of dried blood.

*By command of His Majesty King Aldric III, Silas Ravensworth, charm-smith of Thornhollow, is summoned to attend the Feast of Loyalty, in celebration of the thirty-first year of the King's coronation.*

Maren's hand had arranged it all—the forged credentials, the guild sponsorship, the story of a provincial carver whose work had caught a courtier's eye. But no amount of arrangement could soften what the summons meant. Silas held it over the candle flame in her borrowed chamber and watched the wax seal glisten, and thought: *It knows my name now. It chose to speak it.*

"They always invite the carvers," Maren had told her that morning, pacing the length of her silk-hung sitting room with the restless energy of a woman who had learned to think while appearing idle. "Every feast needs entertainment. A jeweler, a musician, a carver. It's tradition. No one looks twice at tradition."

"Everyone will look at me."

"No." Maren had stopped pacing, and her eyes—gray as winter slate—had held Silas with the flat certainty of someone who had survived twenty years at court by knowing exactly what people saw when they looked. "They'll look *through* you. You're a tradeswoman. A curiosity for the first quarter hour, then furniture. Your danger isn't their attention. Your danger is *His Majesty's*. He notices things. He has always noticed things." She had said it lightly, but the lightness was lacquer over rot. "Smile. Carve slowly. And whatever happens, whatever he asks of you—do not refuse him. No one refuses him. Not once in thirty-one years."

Now the hour came, and Silas dressed in the gown Maren's seamstress had made—a deep green that flattered without flattering, plain enough to be forgettable, fine enough to belong—and let a maid arrange her hair, and walked the long corridor toward the great hall on legs that felt borrowed from someone braver.

The palace bones sang beneath her feet.

She had been listening to them for three days now, learning their language the way she'd once learned her mother's humming: by patience, by repetition, by love. The palace was old, older than the dynasty that ruled it, its foundations laid on stones that remembered being riverbed, its deepest cellars shored with beams cut from trees that had stood when the first king was a boy. The dead clung to those old things like moss to north-facing stone. Servants mostly. Laundresses and scullery boys and a chamberlain who had served four kings and died in his sleep in a bed too grand for him, still wondering if he'd earned it. They murmured through the walls in voices worn soft as river pebbles, and Silas walked through their whispers like wading a shallow stream.

Tonight they were agitated.

*She comes, she comes,* breathed a scullery girl dead two hundred years, her voice threading up through the flagstones. *The one who hears. She walks above us and we are loud tonight, we are so loud—*

Silas kept her face smooth. She had spent five years perfecting the art of a blank expression; it had never been harder than now, walking into the lion's mouth with every dead thing in the building calling her name.

* * *

The great hall of Vaelcrest Palace had been built to make human beings feel like insects granted an audience with weather. The ceiling soared into painted clouds where golden gods wrestled golden monsters. Banners hung from iron brackets, one for each generation of Aldrics, their embroidery dark with age. Three hundred candles burned in chandeliers of wrought gold, and beneath them moved the court: ladies in silks the colors of crushed flowers, lords draped in chains of office, pages weaving between them with wine the color of garnets.

And at the far end, on a dais beneath a canopy of cloth-of-gold, sat the king.

Silas had seen him once before, from across a courtyard during her arrival—distant, robed, moving with the slow grace of a man carved from good timber. Now she saw him near. King Aldric III was not old. That was the first wrongness. Thirty-one years on the throne should have silvered him, stooped him, put weight around his eyes. He looked forty. His hair was brown shot with gray at the temples, precisely, decoratively, as though age had been applied by a painter who understood that a king required a certain dignity of years but not too many. His hands lay folded on the arms of the throne, elegant, motionless.

And his bones were silent.

Silas had known this would be so—had felt it from across the courtyard, a hole in the world's noise shaped like a man—but knowing did not blunt the horror. Every living body she had ever stood near carried a faint warmth in her gift's hearing, the hum of marrow, the whisper of blood remembering it would someday stop. Even the youngest child rang faintly, a struck glass. The king rang with nothing. He was a room with the fire gone out. He was a glove, beautifully tailored, with no hand inside.

*Don't look at him,* she told herself. *Look at your plate. Look at your wine. Be furniture.*

She was seated at the fourteenth table among minor functionaries and their spouses, close enough to see the dais clearly, far enough to be beneath notice. The feast unfolded around her in waves: consommé poured from silver tureens, quail glazed with honey and bitter orange, a whole sturgeon plated in scales of beaten gold leaf. Musicians played from a gallery, something sweet and forgettable. Conversation rippled. Candlelight turned every jewel into a small captive star.

Silas ate little and listened much.

The living were easy to read without her gift—she'd spent a lifetime studying faces because faces were safe, faces were the hearing of people who couldn't hear. Lady Vess, third from the left at the high table, was terrified; her laughter came a half-beat late and her fan trembled. Lord Cassel, beside her, was angry in the cold way of a man storing rage for later use. The Master of Coin was drunk and frightened of being drunk. And threaded through all of it, everywhere, like a current beneath a frozen lake: fear. The whole court was afraid. They laughed and toasted and feasted, and underneath, every one of them was listening for footsteps coming up their own stair.

The dead in the walls watched the feast too. They always had, Silas realized. Generations of them, pressed against the plaster behind the tapestries, watching the living eat and drink and scheme, unseen and unseeing-seen. An old steward dead ninety years murmured the names of the nobles as they passed the dais, a litany he must have recited ten thousand times in life: *Vess, Cassel, Orley, Maren—Lady Maren, looking well, looking pale, looking afraid, my lady, my lady—*

Silas found Maren at the second table, radiant in dove-gray, laughing at some ambassador's joke. Her eyes never stopped moving. When they crossed Silas's—for less than a heartbeat—something passed between them, wordless: *Steady. I'm here.*

Then the king stood, and the music died mid-phrase, and three hundred conversations collapsed into silence as neatly as a house of cards.

"My loyal subjects." His voice filled the hall without effort, warm as hearthsmoke, resonant as a bell. It was a beautiful voice. It was the most terrible voice Silas had ever heard, because there was nothing behind it—no breath-warmth, no mortal rasp, just sound produced by a mechanism that had studied how kings speak. "Thirty-one years ago, I took the crown of this realm and swore before gods and men to hold it. Tonight we celebrate not my reign but *yours*—the loyalty that holds me up, as I hold up the law. Drink. Feast. Tomorrow we return to our duties, strengthened by one another."

He raised his cup. Three hundred cups rose with it. And as the king drank, his gaze swept the hall—slowly, lazily, the gaze of a shepherd counting sheep—

—and stopped on Silas.

There was no reason for it to. She was one face among hundreds, seated in shadow at the fourteenth table. But the crown caught the candlelight as his head turned, and for an instant—an instant no one else could have perceived—Silas felt it *look* at her. Not the king. The crown. A pressure against her mind like a thumb pressing on a bruise, curious, cold, tasting the air around her the way a snake tastes with its tongue.

*There you are,* said something that did not use words. *There is a door in this one. A listening door. How long has it been open?*

Silas dropped her gaze to her plate and became, with every scrap of will she possessed, a woman thinking about sturgeon.

The moment stretched. One heartbeat. Two. Around her the feast resumed in a wash of relieved chatter, the toast concluded, the ritual complete. But the pressure lingered, patient as frost.

Then the king descended from the dais.

He did it beautifully. That was the horror of him—he did everything beautifully, each step measured, each turn of his head composed, a performance of kingship refined over three decades by something that never tired and never forgot. He moved down between the tables with his retinue trailing, pausing here to compliment a lady's gown, there to ask after a lord's gout, and the court parted before him like water, bowing, curtsying, glowing under the sun of his attention.

He was walking toward the fourteenth table.

Toward her.

*Stand up,* Silas commanded herself. *Bow. Do what everyone does.* She rose with the rest, heart slamming against her ribs hard enough that she feared he might hear it, and lowered her eyes in a curtsy, and waited.

"Lady—" The king paused, reading some inner ledger. "Forgive me. You are new to us."

"Silas Ravensworth, Your Majesty." Her voice came out steady. Five years of hiding had taught her that trick: fear folded small and tucked away, the voice left clean. "Charm-smith. Of Thornhollow."

"Charm-smith." He tasted the word. Up close, his face was worse than from afar—it was *handsome*, warmly lined, kind-eyed, and utterly vacant, a mask of a man painted by someone who had met men only in portraits. "A maker of small protections. Little charms against ill luck. Against sorrow." Something flickered behind the kind eyes—not malice, exactly. Appetite. "Rise, Mistress Ravensworth. Let me see your hands."

She raised them. Carver's hands, scarred and nicked, nails trimmed short. He took one—his touch cool, dry, precise—and turned it over as a buyer examines livestock.

"Good bones," he said, and the table laughed politely, and Silas laughed too because refusing to laugh was death, and inside her skull the pressure tightened like a closing fist. *Show me,* the crown purred through the king's grip, through his skin into hers. *Show me what you carry. Every craftsman carries something. Every gift leaves a residue. Let me read your hands, little door, little listening door—*

She slammed every shutter she had. Thought of nothing. Became the blank page she had trained herself to be since childhood: a woman holding a stranger's hand at a feast, mildly flattered, mildly nervous, nothing else. Beneath the blankness, desperately, silently, she reached for the walls—for the dead pressed into the plaster six feet away—and found the old steward, dead ninety years, and clung to his murmur like a drowning woman clinging to driftwood. *My lady, my lady, mind the steps, mind the steps, my lady—*

The king released her hand.

"You honor us," he said aloud, smiling his portrait smile. "And I would honor you in turn. A commission." He lifted his voice so the hall could hear, and every head turned, and Silas felt three hundred pairs of eyes settle on her like weights. "The queen dowager's name-day approaches. My mother has been long retired from public life, but she remains in our hearts—and in our duty. I would have her given a charm worthy of her. Wrought by your own hand, Mistress Ravensworth, here, before us all. So that the court may witness the skill of Thornhollow." He smiled wider. "Come. There is a carving station prepared. Bone, I am told, is your preferred medium."

*There it is.* The trap sprang shut with the elegance of everything this creature did. A public test. A carving performed under the eyes of the court and the crown itself, while the thing behind the king's eyes leaned close to watch what rose out of the bone—to listen for the whisper of the dead answering her knife. If she carved true, if any voice stirred in the finished piece, she would be unmade before the dessert course. If she refused a direct royal command, she would be unmade faster.

"I am honored, Your Majesty," Silas heard herself say.

* * *

They had prepared a table at the center of the hall: white linen, a carver's kit laid out in gleaming order, and upon a cushion of crimson velvet, a piece of bone.

Silas knew it before she touched it. She knew it the way you know a voice through a wall.

It was a femur. Human, old, polished smooth by decades of handling. Grave-stock—someone had robbed a barrow or bought from a robber, and the palace had it in its collection, and the crown had chosen it for her specifically. Because bone was her medium, yes. And because a femur like this one *sang*. She could feel it from a foot away, a low mournful drone like a wet finger circling a bowl's rim: a warrior, maybe, or a farmer with a warrior's temper, dead eight centuries, still muttering his grievances into the dark.

Carve anything true from this, and the dead man's voice would come through like water through a cracked dam.

The hall had rearranged itself around her. Chairs scraped forward; nobles crowded close, wine cups in hand, delighted by the spectacle. The musicians had gone quiet. Above it all, on the dais, the king had resumed his throne and leaned forward, chin on laced fingers, watching with the bright fixed attention of a cat at a mousehole.

*Begin,* said the pressure against her mind.

Silas picked up the bone. It was warm. Old bones shouldn't be warm. Inside it the dead man stirred, feeling her gift brush him like sunlight through a window, and began to gather himself to speak—eight hundred years of silence, and here at last was someone who could hear—

*Not yet,* she begged him silently. *Please. Not yet. Play dead for me. Please.*

She set the knife to the bone.

What followed was the longest hour of her life. She carved slowly—slowly was safe, slowly was craft—and she carved *nothing*. A border of interlocking leaves. A geometric fret. Patterns that meant nothing, invoked nothing, opened no doors. Her hands knew this work so deeply they could do it while her mind stood elsewhere, screaming. Sweat gathered between her shoulder blades. The crowd murmured appreciation—*lovely work, such precision, look how the lines flow*—and beneath the murmur she read the room's dead with the desperate thoroughness of a woman memorizing a house before it burns.

The old steward, cataloguing nobles. The scullery girl, humming a song about blackbirds. A chambermaid dead sixty years, watching the king with hatred so old it had polished itself smooth: *he wears my brother's face, they said he favored my brother, they said, and then my brother went into the crypt and the king came out of the mourning period unchanged, unaged, and I scrubbed his floors until I died and he never once aged, never once—*

Silas's knife did not slip. She would not permit it to.

But the crown was watching her carve, and the pressure against her mind grew sharper, more probing, a blade testing a lock. *Leaves,* it mused. *Frets. Pretty emptiness. Is this all you are? Or are you hiding the shape of your gift inside harmless geometry, the way a smuggler hides a blade in a bale of wool? Show me. Open. Open.*

*I am a tradeswoman,* she answered it, in the wordless grammar of a shielded mind. *I make pretty things for pretty people. There is nothing else.*

*Liar,* said the crown, almost fondly. *All the best ones lie.*

The bone fretwork grew under her knife: a circlet, she decided, a decorative band, suitable for an elderly lady's dressing table—leaves and vines and, at intervals, small carved flowers. Harmless. Empty. She hollowed nothing, whispered nothing, woke nothing. The dead man in the femur raged silently against her restraint—*I have waited, I have WAITED, girl, give me a mouth, give me one word*—and she bore his fury like a hot coal cupped in her palm, and carved another flower.

Halfway through, disaster nearly came from an unexpected quarter.

A young lord—drunk, bored, the dangerous combination—leaned over her shoulder with a wine-bright laugh. "Flowers! Charming. Tell me, mistress—do your charms truly work? My grandmother swore by a Thornhollow luck-piece. Said it spoke to her." He grinned at the surrounding nobles. "Spoke to her, didn't it! What did the bone ever say to you?"

The hall laughed. The pressure against Silas's mind went very, very still. Listening.

"A bone says whatever the carver puts into it, my lord," Silas said, not lifting her eyes from her knife. "Mine put flowers. Flowers say nothing at all."

More laughter. The young lord bowed, mocked and mocking, and withdrew. But the stillness in her skull remained, and Silas understood with cold clarity that she had just been weighed, and the measurement had come back ambiguous, and ambiguity would not protect her forever.

She finished the circlet as the clocks somewhere below struck the eleventh hour. Leaves, vines, twelve small roses—one for each month of the dowager's long life. She buffed it with wax until it glowed like moonlight, set down her knife, and lifted the finished piece in both hands.

"A trinket for Her Majesty the queen dowager," she said clearly. "For health, and rest, and gentle dreams. Nothing more."

Silence. The whole hall watching. On the dais, the king rose, descended, took the circlet from her hands with fingers cool as porcelain, and held it up to the candlelight.

"Exquisite," he pronounced. "Thornhollow shall be proud." He turned it once, twice, and Silas felt him tasting it, searching it for the ghost of a voice, finding only wax and geometry and a dead man's muffled, furious silence. For a long moment the mask-face hung above her, kind eyes empty as wells.

Then, softly, pitched for her alone beneath the hall's swelling applause:

"Well done, little door. We will speak again."

He set the circlet aside, returned to his throne, and called for the next entertainment. The feast roared back to life around Silas like a wave closing over a stone. She stood amid the noise with her hands trembling at last that no one could see, and made herself smile, and made herself sit, and made herself drink the toast when it came.

* * *

She found Maren afterward, in the antechamber where the ladies repaired their paint and the gentlemen loosened their collars. Maren dismissed her maid with a flick of two fingers and gripped Silas's arm above the elbow, hard, her smile never wavering for the benefit of the room.

"You were magnificent."

"He knows." Silas kept her voice below the chamber's murmur. "Not everything. But he knows there's something. He called me—" she hesitated— "*a door.*"

Maren's gray eyes went flat and cold. "Then we've run out of time. Whatever he suspected before, tonight confirmed it. He tested you publicly because he wanted the court to see a harmless carver. Which means he intends the court to keep seeing one—right up until the moment you disappear." She released Silas's arm and smoothed her sleeve, restoring the picture of composure. "You'll move to my wing tonight. Officially, you're my guest—I've wanted a personal carver for ages, everyone knows I collect oddities. Unofficially—"

"Maren." Silas caught her wrist. Through the antechamber's thin walls, through the palace's ancient bones, the dead were murmuring again, and what they murmured chilled her worse than the crown's attention had. The old steward's litany had changed. All evening he had named the passing nobles; now, over and over, softer and softer, he repeated a single phrase, the way the dying repeat a prayer:

*Mistress Ravensworth. Mistress Ravensworth. They're writing down her name. In the guard room they're writing down her name.*
