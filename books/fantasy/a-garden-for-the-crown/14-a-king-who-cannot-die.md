## 14. A King Who Cannot Die

The tooth arrived wrapped in waxed linen no larger than a thumbnail, carried to Silas's chamber in Maren's wing by a serving girl who would not meet her eyes and whose hands smelled of lye and fear. Inside the linen lay a sliver of milk-white shell, thin as a fingernail paring, its edges worn soft with age.

Silas held it to the candlelight and understood what she was holding before her mind consented to know it.

A baby tooth. A king's baby tooth, cut from some prince's gum sixty years ago when he was still only a child who cried and bit and grew, kept—she could guess by whom—in a box lined with velvet, in a drawer that locked, in a room where a mother told herself she was preserving something precious rather than preparing a weapon. The dowager had pressed it into Wren's hand at the garden gate with words Silas had not been permitted to hear. Now it lay in her palm like a chip of moon, and it hummed.

Not loudly. Not the way the palace dead hummed through the foundation stones, that vast slow chorus of servants buried under floors and forgotten in ledgers. This was a single note, small and stubborn, the way a seed hums with everything it has not yet become. The tooth remembered being part of a living boy. And because bone remembers—because bone was all Silas had ever been able to trust—it still carried the shape of the mouth it had grown in.

*He is in there,* she thought. *Somewhere under sixty years of crown, the boy who lost this tooth is still in there.*

Wren stood by the door with his arms crossed, watching her face the way he had learned to watch it: without interrupting. He had shed his hunter's colors three days ago; now he wore the gray wool of a carver's apprentice, borrowed from the underground network, and he kept touching his empty sword belt the way other men touched amulets.

"That's his?" he asked quietly.

"His." She closed her fingers around the sliver. It was warm. Teeth were always warm; they sat nearer to the blood than most bone. "A piece of him that never got worn away. Never crowned, never bound, never taught to lie. It's the truest part of Aldric that exists outside that throne room."

"And you can reach him through it."

Silas looked down at the sliver. Through it, faintly, she could feel the great dull weight of the palace above them—the crown's presence, that cold attentive hunger she had felt in the audience hall, listening even now to the walls. If she worked the charm wrong, if she reached for the boy inside the king and the crown noticed her reaching—

"I can try," she said. "But I need you to do something for me first."

* * *

They built the working in the old chapel beneath Maren's wing, a low stone room abandoned to dust and moths since the last queen's time, its altar stripped, its windows bricked over. Silas chose it because it was quiet in every sense: no bodies in the floor, no voices in the mortar. The dead had not gathered here. Even the crown's attention, she hoped, would slide past a room where nothing had ever happened worth remembering.

She carved for six hours.

She carved the way she had not carved since before the Proclamation—with total abandon, letting the bone tell her what it wanted to be instead of deciding in advance. The sliver of tooth was too small, too precious, to carve directly; instead she made a cradle for it, a palm-sized vessel of ox bone, hollowed and polished, scored on its underside with a spiral of listening-marks she had learned from her mother's hands and refined through fifteen years of secrets. When it was done she set the tooth fragment in the hollow, and it fit as though the cradle had been copied from it, and the whole thing began to hum against her palms like a struck bell that refused to fade.

"What does it sound like?" Wren asked. He had asked her this before—about the widow's husband, about the palace foundations—and each time she struggled anew.

"Like a room," she said, "with someone breathing in it."

Night had come down over the capital. Somewhere far above, torches moved along the walls; the anniversary preparations were beginning, scaffolds rising against the ceremony grounds, and the crown-thing's hunters walked the streets in threes. But here, under the earth, there was only the lamp, the bone, and the two of them.

Silas lay back against the chapel's cold stones and set the cradle-charm against her brow.

* * *

Falling into a mind was nothing like falling into a grave. Graves were finished things. What waited behind the tooth was unfinished—a house mid-collapse, rooms half-gutted, staircases that ended in air.

She came aware in a corridor. That was the first metaphor her own mind offered and she accepted it: a long corridor of pale stone, hung with portraits, lit by windows that showed not sky but years. In the nearest window snow fell on a garden; in the next, banners streamed across a coronation square; in the next, smoke. Time ran along this hallway like water along a gutter, and the walls themselves were soaked in it.

And the doors. Doors on either side, dozens of them, most hanging open on empty rooms—nurseries stripped bare, schoolrooms with the desks overturned, a music room where a harp's strings had been cut one by one. The crown had been thorough. Sixty years of possession does not simply sit atop a soul; it eats outward from the center, room by room, taking the memories it needs and burning the rest for fuel. Three kings' souls, the rite had said. It had devoured three kings' souls, and Aldric had been on the table longest.

Silas walked the corridor with her heart hammering and her gift stretched wide, listening the way only she could listen—not with ears but with the bone-deep patience of someone who had spent her life waiting for the dead to finish speaking.

At the corridor's end, one door remained closed.

It was small. Child-height. Painted with faded blue birds, the paint chipped by small impatient hands. Around its frame, in a pattern that made her stomach turn, someone had scratched tally marks—hundreds of them, thousands, gouged from the inside.

She knelt. Pressed her palm to the wood. It was warm the way the tooth had been warm.

"Aldric," she said. Her voice sounded enormous in the ruined hallway. "My name is Silas Ravensworth. I'm a carver of bone charms. Your grandmother sent me."

Nothing. Then—a sound so small she almost missed it. Not a voice. A breath. The kind of breath a person takes when they have spent decades learning not to be heard breathing.

"I know you're afraid," she said. "I know you've had visitors before. Things that wore friendly faces. Voices in your own voice, saying whatever would make you open the door." She swallowed. "I can't wear any face but mine. I can't promise I'm not another trick—that's the cruelty of what's been done to you, that trust itself became a trap. But I can give you something no trick can fake."

She drew the sliver of tooth from the cradle-charm and held it up to the door's keyhole.

"You lost this when you were six," she said. "It remembers. Bone always remembers. Let me show you what you sounded like before."

For a long moment the corridor held only its own silence. Then the door opened—not all the way, an inch, a child's cautious inch—and through the gap came a smell that did not belong in this ruined place: bread. Summer. The particular warm-animal smell of a boy who has spent the day climbing things he should not climb.

An eye appeared in the gap. Dark, red-rimmed, ancient and young at once.

"You're real," said a voice like a dry hinge. "You're really real. You're not one of its shapes."

"I'm real."

"It makes shapes." The eye flinched toward the windows, toward the running years. "It made my mother once. Made her stand at the end of this hall and ask me to come out, asked me so gently, and when I went to her it—" His voice broke and reformed somewhere older. "There is no mother anymore. There hasn't been anyone to go to for sixty years. Do you understand what that means? Everyone who knew Aldric is dead or it ate their faces out of my memory until they were strangers. You're asking me to believe in a person. I don't have anything left that knows how."

"Yes, you do." She turned her hand so the tooth caught the light of the impossible windows. "You believed in this. You kept it. Somewhere deeper than it could dig, you kept proof that you existed before it, and proof is belief, Aldric. It's the same muscle."

The door opened another inch.

* * *

What emerged was not a man. She had braced herself for that—the rite's warnings, the dowager's grief had prepared her—but preparation meant nothing against the actual sight of him: a figure the size of a boy of ten, translucent at the edges, flickering like a candle in wind, wearing the ghost of a child's tunic and carrying around his shoulders the terrible stoop of someone four times that size. Sixty years compressed into a body that had stopped growing the day the crown came down over his father's—no. Over *his* head. He had been crowned at thirty-one, the records said. But inside, the crown had found the child and pinned him there, and fed on everything else.

"You want to take it off me," Aldric said. Not a question.

"I want to give you the choice to take it off. Those are different things."

"Are they?" He laughed, and the laugh was the worst thing yet, because underneath the rust she could hear the king's public voice in it, the beautiful trained instrument the court heard every day, worn like a mask over this whisper. "Choice. Do you know what happens when I choose? I tried to choose once. Year nine of my reign. I stood in front of the mirror—I couldn't speak aloud, it doesn't allow that, but I thought it, I thought *no*, I thought *get out of me* with everything I had."

He pushed back the sleeve of his ghost-tunic. Beneath the translucency, scar tissue mapped his forearm in whorls and rivers, burn-scar, old and layered.

"It doesn't punish the body," he said. "The body is its glove. It punishes *here*. Every nerve I owned, it lit, for—I don't know how long. Days inside my own skull. And when it finally let me surface, I smiled at the court and gave a speech about harvest tariffs and my voice didn't shake, and that was worse than the burning. Because it proved the burning wasn't necessary anymore. It proved I'd already learned." He looked up at her, and his eyes were the eyes in every official portrait, and they were also nothing like them. "You cannot ask me to say yes. I want to. God, you cannot imagine how much I want to. But the wanting is the leash. It lets me want freedom precisely because it knows I'll never dare reach for it. That's the joke it tells itself, every day, for sixty years."

Silas sat down on the corridor floor, in the middle of that ruined hallway of years, and pulled her knees up like a girl. Something in the gesture made him stare—as if no one had ever been small in front of him before, as if smallness itself were a miracle.

"Then we won't ask," she said. "Asking is a door it guards. So we go around."

"How? There's no around. It's in my bones. It's in the—" He stopped. His flickering form went very still. "You're a listener. You hear the dead. My grandmother sent you because of what you hear."

"The crown is bound into the metal," Silas said slowly, feeling her way along the thought as though along a carving line. "The binding lives in the gold, and the gold rides your skull, and through the gold it owns your nerves. But Aldric—the binding is a *carving*. Someone cut those marks. Someone spoke that rite. Which means it has a shape, and every shape has a flaw, and every flaw can be found." She met his eyes. "But finding it isn't the problem. The problem is the moment of unbinding. The counter-rite requires the vessel to be willing—truly willing, freely choosing, in the instant the bond breaks. No coercion possible, no trickery. The dowager wrote it that way deliberately, God help us both. So the crown has only to keep you too afraid to consent, forever, and it wins."

"Then I've already lost," Aldric said simply. "Because I am afraid. I have been afraid so long that fear isn't a feeling anymore. It's the ground I stand on."

"No." Silas leaned forward. "Fear is the ground it stands on. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters, and I'm going to spend whatever time I have proving it to you." She reached into the cradle-charm and took out the tooth sliver, and held it out to him. "Here. Take this back. Hold it. Feel what you were before anyone put a crown on you. Not the king. Not the vessel. The boy who lost a tooth and kept it under his pillow and woke up richer by a coin."

His hands shook as he took it. The sliver sank into his translucent palm and glowed there, a coal of white, and for one moment the flickering steadied, and he stood taller—or the child-shape stood taller—and his face cleared the way fog clears from glass.

"Oh," he whispered. "Oh, I remember grass. I'd forgotten the word for grass."

* * *

The crown noticed.

Silas felt it before she saw any change: a pressure through the charm at her brow, vast and cold and patient, like deep water shifting overhead. The corridor's windows went dark, year by year, window by window, snuffed in sequence. The portraits on the walls turned their faces slowly toward her—not painted faces anymore but the smooth blank oval of the entity's attention, dozens of them, all looking down the length of the hall.

*GET OUT OF MY HOUSE,* said the whole corridor, in Aldric's voice.

The boy-figure cried out and folded around the glowing tooth like a moth around flame. The little blue-bird door slammed. And Silas, ears ringing with the force of a will that had eaten three kings, did the only thing she could think to do: she put both hands flat against the floor of that ruined mind and listened down, down through the metaphor, down to the true architecture beneath—and there, exactly where she had hoped, exactly where the dowager's confession had promised, she found the binding's root. A thread of gold woven through the deepest bone of him, and along the thread, marks. Carved marks.

Marks could be read.

Marks could be, someday, unmarked.

She tore herself free before the pressure could close over her like a fist, ripping back up through the tooth, through the cradle-charm, through six hours of careful carving, and gasped awake on the chapel floor with Wren's hands gripping her shoulders and his voice shouting her name.

"I'm here." Her own voice came out scraped. "I'm here. I found him, Wren. He's alive—he's *alive*, do you understand? Sixty years and he's alive in there, a boy with a tooth, hiding behind a door with birds painted on it."

Wren sank back on his heels. In the lamplight his face was doing something complicated—hope and horror wrestling for the same ground. "Can he be freed?"

"Yes." She sat up, pressing the cradle-charm between her palms, feeling the tooth's small stubborn warmth. "But he can't be asked. Asking is exactly what the crown has armored itself against for sixty years. It lets him want freedom because it knows fear will always outrun the wanting." She looked at the sealed chapel door, and beyond it, upward, toward the throne room where a beautiful body sat moving beautifully on a silent throne. "So we don't ask him. We build him a moment—public, loud, impossible to ignore—where reaching for freedom costs less than staying still. We make the whole kingdom watch. We give him witnesses."

Wren went very still. "Silas. What kind of moment?"

She thought of the scaffolds rising over the ceremony grounds. The banners already going up along the processional route. The date every clerk in the capital had inked into their ledgers months ago.

"A coronation," she said. "His coronation. They're going to crown him again in eleven days, Wren—before the entire court, the hunters, the nobles, the city. The crown-thing thinks the ceremony is its triumph." She opened her fingers and looked at the small white sliver gleaming in the cradle, humming its one unbroken note. "We're going to make it a door."
