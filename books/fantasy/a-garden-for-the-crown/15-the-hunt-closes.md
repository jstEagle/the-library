## 15. The Hunt Closes

The door in the cemetery wall looked like nothing at all—cracked stone, ivy-choked, the kind of thing the eye slides past because it has decided already that it is not worth seeing. Wren pressed his shoulder to it three times before it gave, groaning inward on hinges that had been greased recently enough to smell of tallow and rosemary.

Silas paused on the threshold, rain running off her chin, and listened.

She had been listening for hunters all night—the particular rhythm of boots that meant patrol, the wet click of a crossbow being unshouldered, the small human sounds of men who would rather be anywhere else but who would still put a blade through her if the ledger demanded it. What she heard instead stopped her breath.

Beneath the ground, faint as fish under ice, voices were singing.

"What is this place?" she whispered.

Wren glanced back at her, blood still crusted at his temple where the hunter's ring had caught him, exhaustion carved into every line of him. "The reason I never understood why the hunt took so long to find anyone," he said. "Come inside, Ravensworth. You're about to meet the people you've been hiding from your whole life."

The passage descended in a spiral so narrow their shoulders brushed the walls, and the walls were not plain stone. She felt them before she saw them—her fingers trailing the damp surface out of habit, the way she touched everything now, reading the world through its oldest bones—and they *answered*. Not words. Not yet. A low murmur of attention, like a room full of people going quiet when a stranger enters.

*Ossuary,* the bones murmured. *Ossuary, ossuary,* and then, softer, curious: *carver? Carver come home?*

Her hand jerked back. "Wren."

"I know." He didn't turn around. "First time I came down here, on an arrest warrant four years ago, I stood where you're standing and I heard nothing, because I'm not gifted, and I nearly went mad wondering what you all were hearing. Come on."

The spiral opened into light.

It was not torchlight, though there were lamps. It was the light of work—dozens of small lamps over dozens of small benches, arranged in tiers down the inside of what had once been a cistern or a catacomb vault, everything worn smooth and warm with use. And at the benches sat people. Old women with magnifiers strapped to their foreheads, drawing lines no thicker than a hair into slivers of femur. A boy of perhaps twelve, tongue between his teeth, coaxing a sparrow's breastbone flat. A broad-shouldered man whose hands were wrong for such fine work, farmer's hands, knuckles like walnuts—and yet the charm taking shape beneath them was the most delicate thing in the room.

They all looked up. Silas stood dripping at the top of the stairs, hunted, branded by association, five years of careful invisibility torn away at last—and thirty pairs of eyes looked at her and did not see a stranger.

They saw a carver.

"Bone-speaker," said the old woman at the nearest bench, setting down her graver. Her voice was unhurried, the voice of someone who had decided long ago what frightened her and what did not. "We felt you at the wall. The ossuary doesn't sing for just anyone."

"I don't—" Silas's voice caught. She started again. "I've worked alone. Always. I didn't know there were—"

"Others?" The old woman smiled, and there was grief folded inside the smile, old and well-aired grief, like linen kept in a chest. "Child, there have always been others. That's the first thing they take from us—not the gift. Each other."

* * *

They gave her tea in a chipped cup and a seat close to the central brazier, and slowly, in pieces, the shape of the underground assembled itself around her.

It was called, plainly, the network—no grander name than that, though some of the older members called it the garden, and no one quite remembered why. Cells in every city, threaded together by charms that whispered across distance: a knucklebone in one pocket that grew warm when its twin, a hundred miles away, was held by the right hand. Safe houses marked not by signs but by the way the mortar above a doorway had been carved with a line too fine to see. For two hundred years, while decrees came and went and hunts flared and faded, the carvers had simply continued—burying their dead properly, easing their dying, letting the grieving say goodbye.

"We knew of you," said the boy's mother, a sharp-faced woman named Odile who spoke with her hands as much as her mouth. "Ravensworth. The widow-charms out of Mill Lane, clean work, quiet work. Master Corvin wanted to reach out to you years ago."

"Why didn't he?"

"Because you'd made yourself invisible," Odile said, without judgment. "And we respect the craft of that. Some of us wear our gifts openly where we can; some of us have to disappear to survive. We don't drag anyone into the light who chose the dark." Her eyes flicked to Wren, sitting apart with his back against a pillar, arms around his knees, watching them all with the wariness of a man in a den of animals who had every reason to eat him. "Mostly."

Silas followed the look. "He's the reason I'm alive."

"He's the reason Maren is in the Black Cell," someone said quietly, from the second tier. "Hunters know hunters, Ravensworth. His refusal bought us days, maybe. Not more."

"Corvin," the old woman said, in the tone of a bell ending an argument, and Silas understood that this must be her. "Enough. The man is bleeding in our house and the woman he bled for is downstairs listening to us argue. Sit. Both of you."

So they told her, in the low even manner of people rehearsing bad news, how far the hunt had closed.

Maren had been taken three nights ago, in the raid that had scattered them—Silas through the servant passages, Wren holding the corridor until the cost of holding it became his own name. He had surrendered loudly, deliberately, buying her the hours she needed; by morning his face was on every proclamation post in the capital. *Traitor to the crown.* Beneath the inked likeness, someone official had added a line in a clerkly hand: *accessory to the crime of sorcery.* They were calling it a crime again, openly, the old word dragged out of the cellar where it had waited fifty years.

"And the dowager?" Silas asked. "The garden—is it—"

"Watched, not burned." Corvin drew a slow circle on the bench with one finger. "You don't burn the queen dowager's garden. But there are hunters on the lane, and the walled gate has not opened since the night you left it. Whatever she gave you, child, they suspect she gave it. She is old and beloved and half-legend; they will move slowly around her. We are not so protected." She looked around the vault, at the lamplight and the bowed heads, and Silas heard what the old woman did not say: *we are deciding, right now, whether to scatter.*

"The king," Silas said. "Aldric. Is there anything left of him?"

"Less every year," said a voice from the lowest tier. The farmer-handed man had not looked up from his charm, but he spoke now, quietly, as if to the bone itself. "My sister served in the palace kitchens until last spring. She said the king has not spoken an unprompted sentence in six years. That he eats what is put before him and sleeps when the candles are doused and smiles exactly twice at every feast, at precisely measured moments. She said the servants have a game—they try to catch his eyes changing. Sometimes, she said, for half a heartbeat, the eyes are *young*. Frightened. Then the crown settles him again, like a hand smoothing water." He set down his graver at last and looked up. "If there's a man in there, he's been screaming behind his own face for longer than most of us have been alive."

Silas thought of the baby tooth hidden in her bodice, carved with Aldric's own small name in a nurse's careful hand decades ago—a sliver of the boy before the crown, a keyhole into whatever prison remained. She thought of the fragmentary presence she had found there, curled small around a single surviving instinct: *not this. please, not this.*

Not a scream anymore. Just a flinch. But a flinch was proof.

"Then we go in at the coronation anniversary," she said.

The vault went very quiet. Even the bones seemed to lean in.

* * *

They argued for a long time, and Silas let them, because she understood something now that she had never understood alone: argument among one's own people was not danger. It was shelter. For five years she had made every decision in silence, weighed every risk with no voice but her own, and she had called that safety, and it had been loneliness wearing safety's clothes.

Odile laid out the facts with charcoal on slate. The Feast of the Second Crowning fell in nineteen days—the annual ceremony in which the realm reaffirmed its loyalty, the king recrowned publicly before the assembled court, the crown lowered onto his head by the Lord Protector's hands while bells rang in every tower. Nineteen days. Every noble house present, every hunter drawn to the palace like iron filings to a lodestone, security doubled, tripled—

"—and one moment," Silas finished, when Odile ran down, "when the crown comes off his brow before it goes back on. However briefly. There is a rite. It requires proximity. It requires—" she hesitated on the edge of the lie and stepped back from it, because these people would taste a lie the way she did— "it requires a willing vessel. The counter-binding cannot be forced. Aldric has to choose it, freely, in the space of a heartbeat, while the thing that has worn him for thirty years pours everything it has into stopping him."

"Can he choose?" asked the boy, Pip, bluntly, the way children ask. "If it hurts him to disobey?"

Silas looked at the child—at his thin wrists, at the sparrow bone cupped in his palm like something rescued—and told him the truth, because he deserved it and because everyone else in the room needed to hear her say it aloud.

"Not unless someone gives him a door. The crown punishes dissent with agony—that's the design, that's what it was *for*. But pain is a wall, not a lock. If, in that moment on the dais, with the crown lifted and the whole court watching, I can carve the final line against his skin—if I can press the choice into his hands at the exact instant the weight is gone—then for one breath he is free, and free things can choose even while terrified." She spread her hands. "One breath. That's what we need. One breath, at the loudest, most guarded moment of the royal year."

"That's madness," someone said.

"It's the only door there is."

Wren spoke for the first time in an hour. He had been sitting with his chin on his fists, following every word, and when he stood, the whole vault tensed—he was still, in body and bearing, a hunter, and would be until the day he died—but he did not reach for any weapon. He reached into his coat and drew out the hunter's badge he had carried since he was seventeen, the silver stamp of the Crown's Hunt, and set it on the nearest bench with a small final click.

"Nineteen days," he said. "Here's what I know, because I trained the men who'll be guarding that dais. The ceremony runs forty minutes. The recrowning takes eleven seconds. The guard doubles at the west doors and thins at the east gallery, because the protocol assumes treason comes from outside, and it has never once occurred to them that the traitor might be the crown itself." His voice was steady, but Silas could hear the fracture running under it—the sound of a man describing the dismantling of his own life's architecture. "I can't get you onto that dais. But I can make them look at me. When they brand me traitor, they expect me to run, and running men get chased, and chasing men stop watching doors." He looked at Silas, and something passed between them that neither of them moved to name—the same unnamed thing that had been passing between them since the sewer, growing heavier and warmer each time, like bread rising in the dark. "You'll need a diversion, Ravensworth. I'm offering to be a very loud one."

"No," Silas said.

"It wasn't a request."

"It wasn't permission." She stood, and the bones of the vault hummed faintly at the motion, thirty gifted people feeling the air change. "I've spent five years surviving by letting other people bleed for me. My neighbors. Maren." Her voice roughened on the name; she had heard Maren's screams through the palace walls for a night and a day, and they lived under her ribs now like swallowed coals. "I'm done paying for my life with other people's blood. If we do this, we do it together, or not at all."

For a moment Wren just looked at her. Then, slowly, the corner of his mouth moved—not quite a smile, but the place where one would build, given time.

"Together," he agreed. "Then we'd better start planning, because in nineteen days I intend to surrender spectacularly, and I want it on record that I planned it better than anyone in the history of treason."

Someone laughed—a short, startled bark of laughter in that dim vault—and the sound broke something open in the room. Shoulders eased. Benches scraped. Odile wiped her slate and began again, faster now, charcoal flying, and the network bent itself around the plan like water around a stone.

Silas sat back down among them, tea gone cold in her chipped cup, and felt the strangest sensation of her life settle over her: she was among her own kind, planning a war, and she was not afraid of the people beside her.

Too late, she thought. Found too late, after the village burned and Maren fell and her hearing hung by a thread over a rite's price. But late was not never. Late was nineteen days.

Late was enough, if it had to be.

* * *

Near dawn, when the others had gone to their pallets in the side alcoves and the lamps had been shuttered to embers, Silas climbed partway up the spiral stair and pressed her palm to the ossuary wall one more time.

*Carver,* the bones murmured sleepily. *Carver, carver, come home.*

"I am home," she whispered, and marveled that it was true, and grieved that it had a deadline.

Below, faint through the floor, she heard Wren turn in his sleep and mutter something—planning treason even in dreams, probably—and she smiled in the dark.

Then, from far above, muffled by earth and stone and ivy, came a sound that turned her blood to ice water.

Bells. Dozens of them, ringing out of pattern, ragged and urgent, the alarm-toll of the capital carrying even this deep. And beneath the bells, threading down through the bones of the city itself, a whisper she recognized—the crown's cold signature, riding every charm in the realm at once, speaking through dead material into living ears:

*I know where you are, little listener. Bring me the rite, or I will begin returning your friends to you. Piece by piece.*

In the alcove below, Wren's eyes snapped open, meeting hers across the dark.

Nineteen days had just become none.
