## 17. Ceremony of Chains

The bells began at dawn, and Silas felt them in her teeth.

She stood in the shadow of the coronation colonnade, wrapped in the grey wool of a palace laundress, watching the scaffolds bloom with banners. Ten years since Aldric's crowning. Ten years of a dead man walking in gold. The court had turned out in its finest silks, a garden of jewel-toned nobility arranged in tiers along the processional way, and above them all, on the high dais beneath the ancient canopy of state, waited the throne—and the crown that sat upon it like a spider on a web.

*It knows,* she thought. *It has always known.*

The guards had doubled overnight. She had counted them through the long dark hours from her hiding place in the undercroft: four at every gate where two had stood, hunters in their bone-grey cloaks moving through the crowd with the particular stillness of men searching faces rather than watching doors. They carried the new writs—she could smell the ink on them, sharp as vinegar—authorizing seizure on suspicion alone. No trial. No testimony. Just a face that looked wrong, a hand that trembled, a name spoken twice.

Wren stood three rows back among the hunter contingent, his cloak pulled up, his jaw set in a line she knew too well now. He would not look at her. That was the agreement. In ninety minutes, everything would depend on how well they had learned to be strangers to each other.

Her fingers found the charm at her wrist without her permission—a sliver of king's tooth carved with the counter-rite's first lines, hidden beneath the laundry-woman's coarse sleeve. Through it she could feel the crown even here, even at this distance: a low, patient hum, like a hive dreaming of winter. It was awake today. It was *hungry*. Ceremonies of loyalty were feasts for it, and it had called ten thousand souls to feed.

A trumpet split the morning.

* * *

They came for the king at the third bell.

The procession wound up through the heart of the palace, and Silas pressed herself against a pillar to watch him pass. Aldric moved beautifully—that had never stopped being true, and it had never stopped being horrifying. His body wore its robes of state the way a glove wears a hand. His smile arrived precisely when smiles were wanted. The lords and ladies bowed as he went by, and their love rose off them like heat off summer stone, and Silas watched the crown drink it.

She heard what they did not. Beneath the trumpets and the hymns, the crown-thing was singing to itself—a thin, satisfied sound, old as the rite that made it, wordless and vast. And threaded through that song, fainter than a heartbeat, another voice: Aldric. Still there. Still buried alive behind his own eyes after thirty years of chains.

*Hold on,* she thought at him, uselessly, the way you might speak to a drowning man across a frozen lake. *One more hour.*

The procession reached the dais. The king ascended. The crown was lifted from its cushion by the Lord Chamberlain with hands that shook only slightly, and set upon the head that had worn it so long it had grown into the shape of it.

And then the crown-thing opened its eyes.

Silas felt the attention sweep the courtyard like a lighthouse beam. It was not looking at the ceremony. It was looking *through* it—reading faces, reading hearts, hunting the small bright signature of a gift. She shrank into the pillar's shadow and did the thing her mother had taught her before either of them had words for it: she became furniture. She became stone. She became nothing worth noticing.

But the beam kept sweeping. Slower now. Closer.

*Ninety seconds,* Wren had said. *Give me ninety seconds, and then run for the throne.*

The beam found the tier where Lady Maren should have been sitting. Her seat was empty—the faction had scattered her to safety days ago, or lost her to the dungeons; no one knew which. The crown-thing lingered on that empty chair a long moment, and something in its attention curdled, and Silas understood with sick certainty that it was not merely suspicious anymore.

It was certain. It simply hadn't found her yet.

On the dais, the Archbishop began the Litany of Renewal, his voice rolling out over the courtyard: *"...and the crown shall find the head worthy, and the head shall bear the crown gladly, unto death—"*

Unto death. Silas almost laughed. If only they knew whose death, and how long ago.

The beam swept toward the servants' gallery.

*Now, Wren,* she prayed. *Now, now, now.*

* * *

He walked out of the ranks of his own brotherhood like a man walking into the sea.

Later, Silas would learn he had planned it down to the breath—had chosen his moment, his ground, his words the way she chose grain and grain direction in a piece of bone. But in the living moment, all she saw was a hunter stepping out of formation, pulling off his hood, and standing alone in the open floor of the coronation courtyard while ten thousand courtiers turned to stare.

"Captain Calloway," the Hunt-Master said sharply. "Return to your post."

"No." Wren's voice carried. He had been given a voice built for command, and he spent all of it at once. "I have a post. This is not it."

Every guard within twenty paces went for steel. Wren did not. He raised empty hands, and he raised his voice higher, and he gave them the spectacle they would never forgive and never forget.

"I surrender!" he shouted, to the king, to the crown, to the whole gilded rot of the court. "I confess! I am Wren Calloway, Hunter of the Third Order, and I am guilty of treason—treason against the thing that sits on that throne!"

The courtyard went silent enough to hear the banners snap in the wind.

"The enemy is not in the alleys," he went on, striding forward as guards closed in, making them drag him, making every step a scene. "The enemy is not the carver in her cellar or the healer in her kitchen or the child who talks to sparrows. The enemy wears gold. The enemy has worn it for thirty years, and it has eaten three kings, and it is eating this one while you bow to it!"

"Lies," the Hunt-Master breathed—but other voices were murmuring now, a rustle through the tiers like wind through wheat. Silas saw a countess clutch her son's arm. Saw an old duke go very still. Saw, in a hundred faces, the flicker of a question people had been forbidden to ask for a generation: *what if?*

"There is a woman in this city," Wren cried, as they forced him to his knees, "who can prove it! Who can hear the truth the crown has buried! Kill me if you like—I'm one man, and I'm already dead. But hear her. Hear the dead. Hear your own king, screaming inside his own skull—"

The crown-thing moved.

There was no other word for it. The king's body turned on the dais with a smoothness no living neck possessed, and the eyes—Aldric's eyes, blue and kind once, she had seen them in the baby tooth's memory—fixed on Wren kneeling in the courtyard. And the voice that came out of the beloved mouth was the king's voice, warm and reasonable and utterly wrong, the way a painted door is wrong.

"Hunter," it said gently. "You are ill. You have been deceived by a gifted witch. Kneel there a moment longer, and be forgiven."

And the love poured off the dais like a wave. Silas felt it strike the courtyard—a tide of manufactured devotion, loyalty weaponized, washing over the assembled court. Nobles wept. Guards lowered their swords and wept. Half the crowd swayed toward the throne like flowers toward sun, and the crown drank and drank and drank.

But half did not.

Half stood rigid, fighting the warmth, faces grey with horror—not at Wren's words, but at the *wave*, at the thing they had just felt move through their own hearts without permission. An old duchess crossed herself. A young lord whispered, "What was *that*?" And in the silence between trumpet calls, Silas heard the murmur spread, the terrible beautiful murmur:

*What was that. What did it do to us. What is sitting on our throne.*

The crown-thing felt it too. Its gaze snapped back across the courtyard, hunting, furious now, the lighthouse beam whipping through the crowd—

And found her.

* * *

There was no hiding from it. Not anymore. The attention landed on her like a hand on her shoulder, and through the sliver of tooth at her wrist she felt it recognize her—felt it taste the shape of her gift the way she tasted the shape of a soul in bone.

*Carver,* it said, inside her skull, in a voice like coins spilled on marble. *Little listener. You came all this way.*

Silas ran.

Not away. Ninety seconds were spent; the plan was ash and momentum now. She ran *forward*, through the servants' gallery, down the colonnade steps, into the open floor where Wren knelt bleeding from a rifle-butt to the mouth, still shouting, still naming names, still giving her every eye in the kingdom while the guards around him hesitated—hesitated, because half of them had felt the wave too, and their hands no longer trusted their orders.

"Wren!" she screamed, and threw him the knife from her sleeve.

He caught it left-handed, cut his own bonds, and came up swinging—not to escape, but to hold. To buy her the last forty feet to the dais stairs. Around them the courtyard detonated: nobles rising, guards turning on guards, the Hunt-Master bellowing contradictory commands, someone crying *"Seize the witch!"* and someone else, louder, answering *"Seize the *throne*!"*

The crown-thing poured fury through the king's body. Aldric rose from the throne, and his movements were beautiful and terrible, a marionette danced by rage, and the Archbishop fled, and the canopy of state tore loose in the wind like a ship's sail coming about.

Silas climbed the stairs.

Behind her, Wren took a spear meant for her back and did not fall. Ahead of her, the dead king's puppet raised one perfect hand, and the air itself thickened with borrowed loyalty, ten years of fed devotion condensing into a wall between her and the throne—

She put her head down and walked through it. It tore at her like briars. It whispered her mother's voice, her neighbor's voice, *turn back, little one, turn back, be safe, be invisible*—and she wept and kept walking, because she had spent five years being safe and invisible, and it had saved nothing and no one.

The king stood before her. The crown burned on his brow like a cage of light.

Silas drew out the sliver of his own baby tooth, carved edge to edge with the counter-rite, and reached for his face.

*One free breath,* she begged him, across the frozen lake, through thirty years of ice. *That's all I need. One breath where it isn't holding your tongue.*

The eyes fixed on her. Blue. Kind. Drowning.

And somewhere far beneath the crown's song, faint as a pulse in a wrist, Aldric Ravenmoor—crowned at nine years old, eaten alive by his grandmother's grief—opened his mouth, and fought for air.
