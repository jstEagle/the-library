## 18. Unbinding

The hall was screaming in three languages.

Silas heard all of them—the living ones with her ears and the dead ones with the deeper listening she had carried since childhood, the one she was about to lose. The Feast of Loyalty had become a riot in the time it took Wren to cross half the marble floor with his hands raised and his voice like a drawn blade: *the enemy sits on the throne, look at him, look at his eyes.* Half the court had looked. That was the trouble. Looking was the first act of treason, and once a courtier had committed it, there was no unseeing.

Chairs went over. Wine spread across stone like something spilled from a wound. Guards who had spent their whole lives obeying found themselves caught between two orders—*seize the traitor* and *protect the king*—and discovered that both commands came from the same mouth, and that the mouth did not breathe.

Silas did not watch any of it. She moved through the chaos the way water moves through a broken ship, finding the gaps, letting the panic part around her because panic always parts around a small woman carrying nothing visible. Her hand was closed around the charm. It was warm. Bone always warmed when it remembered what it was for.

The throne dais rose ahead of her, three steps of white marble veined with grey, and on those steps stood two guards who had not joined the riot. They were old men, both of them. She could hear their bones from here—not the voices, not yet, but the particular silence of bodies that had stood at attention so long their skeletons had forgotten any other posture.

She held up the charm Maren's faction had given her weeks ago, the forged warrant, and the guards hesitated exactly as long as she'd gambled they would—and in that hesitation she was past them, up the steps, standing before the king.

Aldric sat in the throne like a man nailed to weather.

That was the closest thing she could find to describe it, and Silas had spent her life finding words for things that resisted words. His body was beautiful still—the possession kept its house well, fed it, exercised it, dressed it in cloth-of-gold—but beauty without breath inside it. His chest rose and fell because the thing wearing him remembered that chests were supposed to do that. His eyes tracked her with a focus no human gaze should have: too steady, too patient, the stare of something that had watched forty years pass from behind glass.

*You,* it said through his mouth.

Not aloud. Or rather—aloud, but in the register below sound, the way the dead spoke, the way bones spoke, the frequency she had been born tuned to. The whole hall might have heard only a king's voice saying something ordinary. Silas heard the true sentence underneath, vast and cold and old as the rite itself:

*You are the carver. You have been digging in my roots.*

"I've come to take them out," she said.

The king's face smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, because Aldric's face knew how to smile beautifully, and the thing behind it used that beauty the way a soldier uses a shield. "Then you will kneel," it said, and this time the voice was audible, resonant, kingly, pitched to carry over the rioting hall, "and be judged."

Fury poured off the dais like heat off a forge. Not anger—fury, the entity's own substance, loyalty's dark twin, the pressure it had used for four decades to bend ten thousand wills into a single obedient shape. Silas felt it hit her and understood, finally, how it had ruled so long. It did not threaten. It made obedience feel like warmth, like belonging, like the safest place in the world. Every guard in the hall felt it and knelt a little straighter. Every noble who had begun to doubt felt doubt curdle into shame.

She had prepared for this. In the bone-carvers' tunnels, in the long nights of planning, she had carved herself an anchor—a small charm of her own finger bone, taken with a knife and a steadiness that had surprised even her—and she pressed it now against her sternum and let it hold her shape against the flood.

*I know what you feel,* she told it, in the deep register. *I've felt it too. That's why I'm dangerous.*

The king's eyes narrowed. And then, beneath her feet, the dais began to speak.

She hadn't expected that. The palace dead had warned her about many things—the hidden passages, the dowager's garden, the anniversary ceremony—but no one had thought to mention that the throne itself was marble cut from a quarry where men had died, or that the entity had been pouring itself into the palace's foundations for decades, feeding on the building the way ivy feeds on a wall. Now the stone under her boots hummed with its attention, and she felt it reaching up through the floor, through her soles, looking for the seam of her gift so it could pour itself in.

There was no more time for stealth.

Silas drew the counter-rite charm from her bodice—the final carving, six days of work, every line of the old queen's rite reversed and rendered in the antler-bone the dowager had given her—and stepped forward and pressed it to King Aldric's brow.

* * *

The world went white and then went elsewhere.

She was inside. She had known she would be—this was why she'd needed the sliver of baby tooth, the fragment of the king's original self, the keyhole into his buried mind—but knowing had not prepared her. Inside a possessed man was not emptiness. It was a country.

Aldric had been crowned at nine years old. He had been possessed at nine years old. Everything he had ever been had happened since in a space the size of a locked room at the bottom of his own soul, and Silas stood in that room now, and it was full.

Forty years of the prince. Drawings scratched into the walls—horses, ships, a mother's face going vague with forgetting. Tally marks by the thousands. A child's handwriting grown steadily into a man's, recording everything: *Year twelve. I have learned to move my left hand again when he sleeps.* *Year nineteen. There is a girl in the orchard who smiles at me. I cannot make my mouth answer.* *Year thirty-one. I think I am mostly gone. If anyone finds this, tell my brother I tried.*

Her eyes stung. There was no time. But she read one more line, near the bottom, fresh, the ink barely dry:

*Someone is coming. I can hear her carving.*

"I'm here," Silas said aloud, and her voice filled the little room the way dawn fills a cellar.

And the crown-thing noticed.

It came down on the room like winter. Not entering—the room was the last sealed place in Aldric's soul, and even after forty years the entity could not crack it—but pressing, crushing, pouring its fury against the walls until the scratched drawings shivered and the tally marks wept dust. Silas felt it testing the lock, feeling for the flaw, and she understood with cold clarity that if she stayed here, it would find the flaw. It had eaten three kings' souls. It was very good at doors.

So she didn't stay. She took the room with her.

This was the trick the dowager had described, the thing that made the counter-rite possible and terrible: the vessel's will and the carver's art had to occupy the same instant. Silas opened the door herself and carried Aldric's awareness up through the layers of his own possession—past the false memories the entity had laid down like sediment, past the puppet-strings of habit and duty and fear, up into the audience chamber, into his body, into the moment.

The hall came back around her. The riot had become something worse: Wren was on his knees in the center of the floor with three guards' pikes at his neck, shouting the truth anyway, his voice cracked and magnificent, and Lady Maren—free, furious, alive—was on the steps below the dais holding back nobles with nothing but her body and her rank. And the king's body stood before Silas, and the crown sat upon its head, black iron under gold leaf, and the entity looked out through Aldric's eyes and *knew*, and the fury it poured into the hall doubled, tripled, became a pressure that made chandeliers sway and strong men weep.

*Stop,* it said, in every register at once, audible and subterranean, and half the hall did stop, frozen mid-motion by pure force of will. Even Wren sagged against the pikes. Even Maren staggered.

Silas did not stop.

She began the rite.

Her knife was already in her hand—she did not remember drawing it—and she set its point to the antler-bone charm against the king's brow and began to carve, each line of the counter-rite scored in real time, because the dowager had warned her of this too: *it must be made in his presence, at his skin, while he watches. No binding can be forced undone. He must choose as you work.*

The first line was for witness. *What was bound by love may be loosed by truth.*

The second line was for return. *What was taken may be given back.*

With each stroke, the entity screamed. Not from the king's mouth—from everywhere. From the walls, from the floor, from the mouths of the kneeling courtiers who did not know their throats had been borrowed. The sound was the sound Silas imagined a forest makes when every tree is felled at once. Chandeliers burst. Wine turned black in cups. Somewhere a dog began to howl and could not stop.

The third line was the hard one. The dowager had written it in a shaking hand: *the vessel must open the door himself. No carver can carve a soul free. The soul must walk out.*

Silas reached into the king's mind one more time and found the little room, battered, its walls buckling, and she put her palm flat against the drawings inside it and said, in the voice she used for the dying, gentle and unhurried:

"Aldric. Your mother's name was Elira. Your brother sent you a wooden horse the winter you were crowned. You hid it from the crown for forty years and it is still under the floorboard in the nursery, and I know because the nursery floor told me." The room went very still. "I can't open this door. Only you can. He'll hurt you. He's hurting you now. But the pain ends, and the door doesn't have to close again unless you let it."

Silence, inside and out. The entity's fury hung suspended. The whole hall seemed to lean toward the dais.

And then, from the bottom of the ruined room, from forty years of tally marks and a wooden horse and a boy who had learned to move his left hand again when the thing slept—one breath.

One free breath. She felt it the way she felt the dead speak: a small, impossible movement in a place where nothing was supposed to move. The prince's will, thin as wire, worn nearly to nothing, and still there. Still choosing.

*Yes,* said Aldric of House Veyra, ninth of his name, who had never once in his adult life been asked. *Take it off. Please. Take it off.*

Silas carved the final line.

The knife bit deep and her blood ran into the groove—her own blood, freely given, the last component—and the antler-bone drank it and flared white, and the counter-rite completed itself in a single syllable older than the kingdom.

The crown cracked.

It did not shatter, not at first. A single fissure ran through the iron band from brow to nape, fine as a hair, and out of that hairline crack poured the entity—not shrieking now but *shrieked*, undone, unwound, forty years of stolen loyalty and devoured souls venting out of the breach in a column of darkness that bent the torchlight backward. The hall saw it. Everyone saw it. Ten thousand lies became visible at once, hanging in the air like smoke escaping a burning archive.

Inside the collapsing storm, Silas heard the dead.

Three kings' souls, freed mid-devour, incomplete and flickering. The first king—old Aldric the founder, the husband the queen could not bury—rose through the column like a bubble rising through dark water, and for one instant he looked at her, and his face was not angry. His face was the face of a man hearing his own name after forty years of silence.

*Thank you,* he said. Or perhaps he only shaped it. Perhaps she read it in his bones, which even now were turning, at last, toward rest.

And then the entity found its voice.

The shriek it loosed as it tore loose from the crown was not sound. It was un-sound, the negation of sound, and it passed through Silas like a blade of cold, and she felt it take something with it—felt her deep listening, the sense she had owned since before she could walk, tear along its length like silk catching on a nail.

No. Not yet. Not quite yet.

The crown split fully in two. Both halves rang against the marble steps with a note like a struck bell, and the darkness funneled upward, screaming, shrinking, and then—with a violence like the world inhaling—it was gone. Out of the world. Out of every world she had names for. The chandeliers guttered and steadied. The wine ran red again. The pressure that had crushed the hall for forty years lifted all at once, and three hundred people who had spent their lives leaning unknowingly into that weight stumbled forward, staggering under the strange lightness of freedom.

King Aldric fell.

Silas caught him. Forty-one years old and lighter than a child, hollowed, breathing in great ragged gulps that were entirely, unmistakably his own. His hands found her sleeves and gripped them, and his eyes—his own eyes now, unfocused, terrified, wet—tried to find her face.

"Is it—" His voice was rusted from disuse. He swallowed. Tried again. "Is it out?"

"It's out," Silas said. "It's done. You chose."

He began to cry, silently, the way a man cries who learned in a locked room that crying made noise and noise was punished. Around them the hall was dissolving into weeping and shouting and prayer; Wren had gotten to his feet and was staring at the broken crown halves as though expecting the darkness to reassemble; Maren was already moving, taking command of the nearest guards with the crisp authority of a woman who had rehearsed this hour for a decade.

Silas held the king and waited for the grief to come.

Because she knew what the rite had cost. She had known since the night she said goodbye to the voices—her mother's humming, the widow's husband, the palace's generations of unseen dead. The counter-rite demanded payment, and payment was owed, and she could feel the debt arriving now like tide coming up a beach, quiet and total and irreversible.

She listened, one last time, while she still could.

The hall's dead were singing—not words, just the sound souls make when a long cruelty ends. The first king's fragment was fading toward wherever fragments go, and it was humming, and the tune was the one the queen had written for him, the one the dowager still played on her clavichord in the walled garden. The servants in the walls were going quiet, gratefully, like children being tucked in. Somewhere far away, in a city she had fled five years ago, every grave was exhaling.

*Goodbye,* Silas told them all, in the register below sound, one last time. *Thank you for finding me. Thank you for making me less alone.*

The tide came in.

It did not hurt. That was the strangest thing. It was simply a door closing, gently, in a house she had lived in all her life—and then the house was gone, and she was standing in open air, and the silence where the voices had been was so vast and so clean that she gasped.

The hall's noise rushed in to fill it—real noise, living noise, human voices shouting and sobbing and laughing in disbelief—and Silas discovered, with a wonder that undid her more than the loss had, that she had never heard the living this clearly before. Every sound arrived whole, unmuddied by the whispers underneath. Wren's footsteps crossing the marble. Maren's voice, sharp and steady. The king's breath, hitching, human, alive.

"Carver," Aldric whispered, gripping her sleeve. "Your ears. Something happened to your—I can see it in your face. What did it cost?"

She almost laughed. Of course he would ask. Forty years in a locked room, and the first thing he did with his freedom was worry about someone else.

"Nothing I didn't choose," she said.

But even as she said it, even as Wren reached them and Maren shouted for physicians and the great doors banged open onto daylight, Silas felt the last echo of the entity's shriek still lodged somewhere behind her breastbone, faint and fading, like a bell that has been struck in an empty cathedral—

—and somewhere in the city below, in the bone-carvers' tunnels, in a hundred workshops and hiding places, every gifted person in the realm had felt the crown break, and was at this moment asking the same question the court was about to ask her:

*What comes next?*

The answer was walking toward her across the shattered feast on the legs of the king she had unmade, and it was carrying, in its hollowed hands, the two halves of the cracked crown.
