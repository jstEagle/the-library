## 19. The Silence After

Silence had a texture. Silas had never known that before.

It was not emptiness, the way a room is empty when a chair is carried out. It was weight, and grain, and pressure — like standing at the bottom of a deep, clear pool and looking up at a light she could no longer swim toward. For five years she had lived beneath a river of voices: the murmuring dead in their graves, the whispering dead in their bones, the palace itself thrumming with the uncounted servants who had died in its walls and been forgotten by everyone but the stone. She had complained of the noise the way one complains of rain. She had never once thought to miss it.

Now it was gone, and the silence pressed on her like snowfall.

She lay on a cot in a cell beneath the Hall of Judgment and listened to nothing. The nothing was absolute. Somewhere above her, boots crossed stone — she felt the vibration through the cot frame, through the floor, through her teeth — but the boots carried no voices with them, and neither did the stones, and neither did the small yellowed bone of a sparrow someone had once lost behind the wall and which she had been able to feel humming faintly, all night, like a struck bell gone quiet.

She had said goodbye to them. She had thought she had. The widow's husband with his dry, patient voice. The palace dead with their litany of small griefs. Her mother's hum, low and tuneless, the sound Silas had slept to as a child and chased through every bone she'd carved since. She had spent a whole night saying goodbye, and she had meant it, and she would have chosen it again a thousand times over — the crown was cracked, the thing inside was shrieking out of the world, Aldric was breathing — and still, waking in the dark of the cell, her hands went to her ears as if the silence were a blockage, a deafness, an illness, something a physician could draw out with a needle.

It was not. It was the price, paid in full.

She turned her face to the wall. The wall was cold and spoke of nothing at all, and she wept for a while, quietly, the way one weeps at a graveside for someone whose death was a mercy, and then she slept again, and dreamed of nothing, which was its own strange kindness.

* * *

They came for her in what she judged to be morning, though the cell had no window and her sense of hours had gone strange. Two guards, keys, a corridor. Her wrists were bound — gently, she noticed, the way one binds someone known to be dangerous only in the sense that they might break your heart — and she was led up through the palace's bones, past corridors she remembered hearing the dead sing in, past the great stair where a scullery maid had fallen in the reign of Aldric's grandfather and where her ghost had hummed for sixty years.

The hum was gone. The stair was just a stair.

They brought her into a chamber she had not seen before: smaller than the throne room, round, lit by high windows that threw long blades of light across a floor of pale stone. A council sat at a horseshoe of tables — lords and ladies, a general, a man in the gray robes of the Chantry with ink stains on his fingers. And at the center of the horseshoe, on a plain chair rather than any throne, sat King Aldric.

He looked hollowed. That was the only word she could find for it, and she had a carver's vocabulary for hollowness; she had spent her life hollowing things. His skin had the translucence of a man who had been poured out and refilled with something thinner than himself. His eyes were open and aware, but they moved slowly, as if each glance cost him. He was dressed simply, in a gray doublet with no chain of office, and his bare head — his bare head — looked strange and vulnerable, like a statue with its bronze stripped away.

When her guards stopped her before the horseshoe, the king looked at her for a long moment. His lips moved. No sound came that she could hear — no, that was wrong; sound came, a voice, thin and cracked and alive, and she realized with a lurch that this was the first living voice she had heard since the rite, and she had to *learn* it, the way one learns a language, parsing the shape of the words out of the air.

"—thank you," he was saying. "I don't know if they told you. I wanted to say it myself."

Silas's throat worked. "You're alive," she said, and her own voice sounded foreign to her, unaccompanied, a single instrument where there had always been a choir.

"Barely." Something moved across his face that was almost a smile. "There is a great deal of me that is... absent. The physicians say it may return. They say it may not." He looked down at his hands, which lay in his lap like tools set aside. "I was nine years old when it took me fully. I do not remember what I was like. I am told I laughed easily."

The chamber was silent. The living kind of silent, the kind she would have to grow used to — the silence of people deciding whether to breathe.

"You are not on trial," the king said. "I want that understood before anything else. What was done to me was done before I could walk. What you did was—" he paused, and the pause was long enough that she saw the lords shift, "—was the first free act committed in this hall in three generations. But the council must decide what the realm is told. And what the realm is told will decide what happens to you, and to the others like you, and I am—" his voice frayed, and he pressed his lips together, "—I am not yet well enough to fight for it as I intend to. So they will question you. Answer them as you see fit. I will hear everything."

He gestured, faintly, and the man in the Chantry robes opened a ledger, and the questioning began.

* * *

They asked her about the rite, and she told them. They asked her about the archive, the counter-rite, the dowager's garden, and she told them that too — all of it, the whole rotten inheritance of it, the old queen's grief and the old king's begging and the three kings devoured one after another like meals. She watched the lords' faces as she spoke. Some went gray. Some went hard, the hardness of men calculating what a truth costs against what it buys. The general's hand did not move from the table, but she saw the knuckles whiten when she described how the crown-thing had tasted loyalty the way a leech tastes blood, how it had *farmed* the court for decades, fattening the love of the realm and feeding on it, and how every cheer at every coronation had been a meal.

"Enough," said the Chantry man at last, closing his ledger. "The question before this council is not what happened. The question is what we *say* happened. There are options before us." He began to count on ink-stained fingers. "We may say the king suffered a long illness, now cured. We may say a foreign sorcery was discovered and destroyed, and the gifted hunted in connection with it — which has the advantage of justifying the decree already carried out. Or we may say nothing at all, and let the story settle as stories do."

"And the gifted?" said a voice from the side of the chamber. "The ones already unmade? The ones in your cells? Do they settle quietly too?"

Silas turned. Wren Calloway stood at the chamber's edge, out of the light, in a hunter's coat with the hunter's badges torn off. There was a bruise along his jaw and his left hand was splinted. She had not seen him since the ceremony — since he had walked into a square full of armed men and given himself up at the top of his voice, naming the true monster before the whole court, buying her the thirty seconds she needed. She had heard the chaos begin as she carved. She had not known until this moment that he had survived it.

"You are not entitled to address this council," the Chantry man said coldly.

"I'm entitled as a witness," Wren said, and walked forward into the light, and Silas saw that he moved like a man who had decided something and would not be moved from it by pain or protocol or anything under heaven. "I was a hunter for nine years. I carried the decree into eleven towns. I can tell this council exactly what your options cost, because I've already paid them." He stopped at the horseshoe's edge and did not bow. "I found a gifted girl of eight in a root cellar in Marrowfen. I let her run. I told myself she'd die in the winter anyway, that I was sparing her the unmaking. That was the first lie I told myself, and the decree made liars of every one of us. If this council buries what happened — if the story becomes *illness*, or *foreign sorcery* — then every family that lost someone to the hunt will spend the rest of their lives believing their dead deserved it. You will be feeding the same appetite the crown fed on. Loyalty built on a lie. You've seen what grows from that soil."

"You would have us confess that the monarchy itself has been a puppet for sixty years," said the general. "That every law, every war, every marriage of state—"

"Was made by a thing that ate kings, yes." Wren did not flinch. "I would have you say it out loud. Once. Clearly. In every market square. Because it will come out anyway — it always comes out — and a truth that is *dragged* into the light costs ten times what a truth that is *carried* costs."

The lords began to speak at once, and the chamber dissolved into the particular roar of powerful people defending their positions, and Silas stood in the middle of it, bound and exhausted, and found that she could not hear any of it properly. Not because of the silence in her — because of the noise. Living noise, all of it, overlapping and ugly and human, and she realized she had spent five years learning the grammar of the dead and had nearly forgotten how the living sounded when they argued: no resonance, no echo, no truth humming underneath the words. Just words. Just men.

Then the doors opened, and the noise stopped.

Lady Maren came through them leaning on a stick, and Silas's breath went out of her. The last time she had seen Maren, hunters were dragging her down a corridor by her arms, and her screams had come through the walls all that night, thinning toward morning into something Silas had prayed — for the first time in years — she would not have to hear. Now Maren was thin, and gray-faced, and walked as if each step were a negotiation, but her spine was straight as a blade and her eyes went around the horseshoe of lords with the unhurried contempt of a woman counting her enemies and finding them insufficient.

"They told me the council was deciding how much truth the realm could bear," she said. "How considerate. And how much did it decide *I* could bear, when it let them have me for nine days?"

"Lady Maren," the Chantry man began, "you stand accused of—"

"Of conspiracy, sedition, and trafficking with a bone-witch. Yes. I've read the charges. They're very tidy." She came forward, step by negotiated step, until she stood beside Wren, and the two of them — the hunter and the courtier, the two people who had bled for her — flanked Silas like a wall. "I was raised in this court. I was taught that the realm is a garden and we who serve it are gardeners, and that some things must be pruned in the dark so the roses keep their color. I believed it. I *liked* believing it; it made the dark work feel noble." Her voice did not rise, and somehow that made it carry further. "But I have spent nine days in a cell beneath this hall learning what the dark work costs, and I am done with it. So I will tell this council what I told the captain of your guard, who was decent enough to listen: I know about the rite. I know about the three kings. I have the archive seal, the dowager's testimony in her own hand, and the names of every noble in this room who dined with the thing in the crown and called it majesty — including, my lord Chancellor, your own father, whose letters I kept, because I am a careful woman."

The silence that followed had a texture too. Silas knew it at once, though she had never heard it with living ears alone. It was the silence of a foundation cracking.

"You would destroy the realm to save your own skin," the Chancellor said.

"No," Maren said. "I would *save* the realm by refusing, one time, at one crossroads, to do the thing that destroys realms — which is to choose the comfortable lie because the truth is heavy." She turned, slowly, and looked at the king. "Your Majesty. I served the thing that wore you. I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking you to make them say it. All of it. Let the history be heavy. Heavy things hold."

Aldric looked at her for a long time. Then he looked at Silas, and she understood that he was not asking her permission, exactly. He was asking her what the dead would have wanted — and then his face changed, a small grief moving through it, as he remembered that she could no longer ask them.

"Say it all," the king said.

* * *

They kept her another night in the cell, but the door was unlocked, and in the morning a servant brought bread and honey and did not look at her as if she were a curse. The decree of unmaking was read out in the square above — she heard it through the high slit of a window, a crier's voice cracking on the words *pardoned*, *free to go home* — and she sat on the cot with the bread going cold in her hands and felt the strangeness of peace settle over her like the snowfall-silence had, heavy and grainy and new.

Wren came at midday. He sat down on the floor across from her cell, his back against the bars, his splinted hand resting on his knee, and for a while neither of them said anything. The living silence between them was different from the others. It had room in it.

"They'll want to give you a title," he said at last. "Maren's already betting on it. She says the council can't stand an unhonored debt."

"I don't want a title."

"I know." He turned his head, and through the bars she could see the corner of his mouth. "What do you want?"

Silas looked at her hands. They were a carver's hands, scarred and capable, and they would carve again — trinkets, spoons, buttons, things with no voices in them at all. But there was another image underneath that one, one that had been growing in her since the dowager's walled garden, since she had knelt in that old woman's soil and understood what the queen had been trying to do all along and done so terribly wrong: that grief does not want a crown, or a throne, or a voice. It wants a place. It wants to be put in the ground and tended.

"There's a garden," she said. "Behind the dowager's wall. It's been waiting sixty years for something to be planted in it that isn't a lie."

Wren was quiet a moment. "The old king," he said. "They'll bury him properly now."

"They'll bury him properly now."

He nodded slowly, and she heard him shift against the bars, settling in, a man planning a journey. "I'm discharged," he said. "Struck from the rolls. No pension, no coat, no orders." A pause. "I've never had no orders before. It's very quiet."

Silas smiled at the wall — at the silence, at the whole vast snowfall of it, at the life she could hear at last, only hers, only living, only enough.

"Yes," she said. "It is."
