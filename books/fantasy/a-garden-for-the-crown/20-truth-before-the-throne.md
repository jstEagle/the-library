## 20. Truth Before the Throne

The bells of the capital rang wrong.

Silas had heard them all her life—first through her mother's stories, then through the bones of travelers who passed through her village, and finally, in five years of hiding, not at all. But she knew their shape the way she knew the shape of her own hands. They were meant to ring in patterns: coronation, mourning, war. This morning they rang in a pattern she had no name for, a slow and uneven tolling that seemed to be searching for itself, the way a man searches a dark room for a piece of furniture he has moved.

She stood at a window in Maren's wing—the same window through which she had once listened to servants' voices in the walls—and watched the plaza below fill with people. They came in the gray light before full morning, pouring out of tenement doors and merchant stalls and the mouths of alleys, gathering beneath the balcony where King Aldric would stand. Silas counted them the way she used to count heartbeats in a dying man's wrist: out of habit, out of an old instinct for measuring what was about to end.

They did not know yet. That was the thing that made her chest ache. Ten thousand people had come to see their king, and every one of them believed something different about what they would see. Some believed he had survived an illness. Some believed the rumors already spreading like spilled oil—that the hunters had turned on each other, that Wren Calloway had named a monster before the whole court, that the crown itself was cracked and being melted down even now. Most believed nothing yet. Most were simply hungry for a story to carry home.

Behind her, in the room's dimness, Maren moved among her ladies with the crisp economy of a woman who had slept four hours and intended no one to know it. Her wrists still bore the marks of her imprisonment, hidden under long sleeves, but her voice had recovered its edge.

"You'll watch from here," Maren said, without turning. "Not the balcony. Not the floor of the plaza."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask." Maren glanced back, and there was something almost warm in the dryness of it. "You ask by standing near doors."

Silas almost smiled. It was strange, this new world where people noticed her habits and named them aloud instead of pretending not to see them. Five years of careful invisibility, and now a countess tracked her movements like weather.

"The healers say he can stand for perhaps an hour," Maren went on. "Perhaps less. He insisted on no chair behind him. He said—" she paused, and for a moment her composure flickered, "—he said a king who has been carried for thirty years should stand on his own legs if it kills him."

* * *

Aldric stood on the balcony at mid-morning, and the plaza went quiet the way a forest goes quiet when a predator passes—not peace, but attention.

He looked older than his years and younger than his suffering, both at once. The possession had hollowed him over decades, and though the entity was gone, its absence had left him like a house after a fire: standing, sound-walled, but blackened inside. He wore no crown. That absence drew every eye. He wore plain wool, gray as river stone, and his hands gripped the balcony rail as if the rail were the only honest thing left in the kingdom.

Silas watched from the shadowed window, deaf now to everything but air and distance, and found she could read him better than she ever could have before. There was no ghost-whisper under his words to distract her. There was only the living language of the body: the tremor in his knuckles, the breath he took before speaking that was too long and too deliberate, the way his eyes found the far edge of the crowd rather than the near edge, because the far edge was easier.

When he spoke, his voice carried on trumpets of silence—the plaza was so still that his words reached the last rows unaided.

"People of Veyra. I have lied to you my whole life."

The words fell into the crowd like stones into deep water. Silas saw the ripples spread outward—heads turning, hands gripping, a woman near the fountain pressing her fingers to her mouth.

"Not my lies alone," Aldric continued. "I did not know, for most of it, that I was lying. That is not forgiveness I ask. It is only the truth, which is owed to you before anything else is asked of you." He steadied himself against the rail. "For thirty-one years, the king you have obeyed has not been a king. He has been a crown, worn by a boy who was put beneath it before he could walk."

The murmur that rose was not loud. It was worse than loud—it was the sound of ten thousand private worlds tilting at once, the sound of a nation's floor shifting a half-inch out of true. Silas heard a man near the gates laugh, one sharp bark of denial, and then stop laughing as if the laughter had been taken from him.

"The decree of Unmaking," Aldric said, "is ended. Today. Every hunter's commission is dissolved; every order given in my name against the gifted is void. Those of you who were hunted—" his voice caught, and he let it catch, did not smooth it away, "—those of you who hid your children's gifts, who burned your own work, who buried your dead in unmarked graves so that no one would ask questions—I am sorry. Sorry is a small coin. I will spend the rest of my reign trying to mint it into something larger."

He told them the rest. He told them about the old queen and the binding rite, about the spirit in the crown that had eaten three kings, about the hunt that had been, all along, a monster hunting its own reflection. He told them about Wren Calloway, who stood at the balcony's edge in hunter's blacks with his commission torn in half and pinned to his chest like a flower, and who did not flinch when the crowd's attention swung to him like a blade.

And then Aldric said the sentence Silas had helped him find, the one they had turned over together in the long nights of his recovery, testing it like a bone charm tested against the ear:

"A kingdom built on a comfortable lie is a grave with good lighting. I would rather give you an uncomfortable truth and a country that is alive."

* * *

The nobles did not take it well.

That evening, in the long gallery where the council had gathered, Silas watched the resistance assemble itself the way she had once watched infection spread in a wound—quietly, along existing lines, finding the places where the flesh was already weak.

Duke Halvard of the Eastern Marches was the center of it, a broad man with a voice built for parade grounds. He did not shout treason. Treason was for fools. He spoke of *stability*, of *continuity*, of the *sacred mystery* of kingship.

"A crown that admits it was empty," Halvard said, pacing the length of the council table, "is a crown no one will kneel to. You have not freed us, Majesty. You have shown every ambitious lord in this realm that the throne can be hollowed and refilled. You have taught the lesson yourself."

"And what lesson would you prefer I teach?" Aldric asked. His voice was still thin—he tired quickly, the healers warned—but there was iron under it now, the iron of a man who had spent thirty years being spoken through and would never again be spoken for. "That lies hold kingdoms up? I lived inside that lie, Duke Halvard. I know precisely how much weight it bears. None. It bears none. It only hides how little is bearing the weight."

"Superstition kept the borders peaceful—"

"Superstition kept *you* comfortable." Aldric leaned forward. "It burned children in villages whose names you cannot recall. It emptied houses while you dined. Do not lecture me on the price of stability. I have paid it since before I could speak."

Halvard's jaw worked. Around the table, the factions showed themselves: some lords staring at their folded hands, some nodding slowly, Lady Maren watching everything with the expression of a chess player three moves ahead, and old Lord Cassel—who had served three kings and been fooled by all of them—weeping openly into his sleeve without shame.

It was Cassel, when he could speak, who broke the deadlock. "I have spent my life believing I served a living king," he said, wet-voiced and steady. "I find I would rather serve a wounded true one than a beautiful false one. Let the record show it."

One by one, in ones and twos, the others came. Not all. Halvard left the chamber with his banner-men, and his departure had the sound of a door that would open again on worse things. But a majority remained, and Maren, counting them with her eyes, allowed herself one small exhale.

Silas stood by the gallery wall, unnoticed, hearing all of it clearly for the first time in her life. No whispers under the words. No dead murmuring their judgments from the corners. Just voices—tired, frightened, stubborn, human—and she discovered that living voices, heard plainly, were harder and more beautiful than any chorus of the dead.

* * *

They came at dawn three days later.

Silas was in the courtyard when the first of them arrived—a woman in a carter's smock with bone dust in the creases of her knuckles, walking through the palace gates as if she had walked through them all her life. Behind her came another, and another, and then dozens, a river of them flowing up from the lower city: carvers, listeners, hedge-healers, storm-readers, the gifted of Veyra emerging into daylight for the first time in generations.

The garden network. The underground Silas had never known existed until it saved her life. They had decided, in their meeting-halls beneath the tanneries and the bone-carvers' lanes, that if the king was telling the truth, then it was time they told theirs.

They came carrying their work. A stooped old man held up a charm carved in the shape of a lark, and when he touched it, it sang—not with a dead voice, Silas realized, but with memory, with the shape of a song his mother had sung, preserved in bone the way amber preserves an insect. A young girl held a carved mouse that scurried up her arm and sat on her shoulder, quick and warm with borrowed life. An entire family of river-listeners brought a net woven with charms that could feel fish moving in the dark water, and they laid it across the courtyard cobbles like a gift.

The guards did not know what to do. They looked to their captain; the captain looked to the palace; and Aldric, summoned, came out onto the steps in his plain gray wool and stood before them.

No one knelt. That was the remarkable thing. The carvers did not kneel—they had spent lifetimes learning what kneeling cost—and the king did not ask them to. He walked down the steps instead, until he stood among them at their own height, and he picked up the lark charm with hands that trembled slightly, and he listened to it sing.

"My grandmother's lullaby," the old carver said. "I kept her voice. All these years, I kept it."

"Then you have done," Aldric said, "what I could not do for myself." He looked up at the crowd of them, at the dust and the worn tools and the wary, waiting faces. "Come in out of the cold. All of you. And bring your work with you—we have thirty years of graves to mark properly, and I am told bone remembers names."

Silas stood at the courtyard's edge and watched them stream past her into the palace, and one of them—an old woman with silver braids and a carver's calluses identical to her own—paused beside her.

"You're Ravensworth," the woman said. Not a question. "The one who unbound it."

"I'm no one particular."

"That's what we all said, once." The old woman smiled, and it was a smile with decades of hiding in it, and underneath the hiding, something bright and unburied. "There's a hall below the tanneries with two hundred chairs in it. We used to meet in the dark and whisper. Come sit in the light with us sometime. First chair's yours, if you want it. We've been saving it since before you were born."

Silas did not answer. She could not, quite. But the old woman seemed to understand, and squeezed her hand once—callus against callus, the handshake of a trade—and moved on into the warmth of the palace.

* * *

That night, Silas climbed to the widow's walk above Maren's wing and stood in the wind with the city spread out below her.

Somewhere down there, in the tannery district, lamps were burning late in halls that had known only darkness. Somewhere beyond the walls, in the Eastern Marches, Duke Halvard's riders were moving between strongholds, and Maren's spies were counting them, and the word *civil* was being whispered in corridors with the word *war* attached to it like a tail. Somewhere a bell that had been a crown hung silent in a workshop, waiting to be hung.

And somewhere south, in a walled garden gone wild with forty years of neglect, an old queen dowager sat alone with her confession and her roses, and waited—for what, Silas was not sure. Forgiveness. Punishment. Spring.

Silas pressed her palms flat against the cold stone of the parapet and felt, for the thousandth time, the great silence where the voices used to be. It did not ache the way she had feared it would. It ached the way a healed break aches—in damp weather, in memory, a reminder of what had been mended at a cost.

But the living were enough. She was learning that. The living were louder than she had ever known, once the dead stopped talking over them.

Below, a lamp flared in the king's study—Aldric, awake again, writing letters he would send to every village that had lost someone to the hunt. And further off, at the city gate, a single rider on a tired horse presented papers to the night watch: a courier from the south, mud to the thighs, carrying a sealed letter marked with the dowager's personal seal.

The watch captain read the direction, frowned, and sent it up toward the palace at a run.

Silas watched the small flame of the courier's lantern climb the hill toward them, and felt the old instinct stir—the listener's instinct, the sense that a message was coming that would change the shape of everything, arriving the way all important things arrived: quietly, at night, carried by someone ordinary.

She turned from the parapet and went down to meet it.
