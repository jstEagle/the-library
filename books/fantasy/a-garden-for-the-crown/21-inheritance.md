## 21. Inheritance

The council chamber smelled of beeswax and old smoke, and Silas stood in it the way she had once stood in doorways—present enough to be seen, positioned so that leaving would be easy.

Three weeks had passed since the ceremony. Three weeks of physicians in the king's rooms, of clerks burning ledgers and rewriting them, of the city learning, street by street, that the hunt was over. The gifted carvers had come up out of the ground like crocuses. She had watched them from a window of Maren's wing, dozens of faces she had never known were hers, and had felt something turn over in her chest that she did not yet have a name for.

Now the king stood before her—not the puppet, not the beautiful hollow thing on the throne, but Aldric, thin as a winter branch, wrapped in a plain grey robe because he had refused ceremonial dress until the kingdom finished deciding what it wanted to be. His hands shook when he forgot to steady them. His eyes did not.

"Lady Ravensworth." He said it carefully, testing the name. "Or should I say Mistress Carver? I'm told you decline both."

"I'm told you're offering both," Silas said. "A title. A place at court. The letters arrived under seal, which felt excessive for an invitation."

"It wasn't an invitation." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one might live. "It was an apology with furniture attached. A seat on the council. Lands in the eastern provinces. A guard of your own choosing." He spread his shaking hands. "The realm owes you more than it can carve into stone, and stone is what we have. So we offer titles. It's the only currency kings are minted with."

Silas looked past him, out the high windows, to where the city climbed its hills in tiers of slate and chimney smoke. Somewhere down there, in a cellar off Tanner's Row, a woman named Odile was teaching children to carve whistle-bones openly, their small voices cracking with laughter instead of fear. Somewhere down there, hunters' badges were being melted down in the same crucibles that had once made charms illegal.

"You want me where the court can see me," she said. "Living proof. The woman who unbound the crown, kept close like a relic."

"I want you honored," Aldric said. "And yes. Perhaps those are the same thing. They usually have been, in my experience—which is limited, and mostly secondhand." He said it without flinching. He had been doing that lately: naming his own hollow years plainly, the way a man shows a scar to prove he survived it. "I spent thirty years as a coat someone else wore. I know what it is to be displayed. I would not do that to you."

"No," Silas agreed quietly. "You'd just do it to the office."

He laughed—a short, startled sound, as if laughter were a language he was relearning and had mispronounced. Then he sobered, and studied her with the particular attention of a man who had been looked *through* for three decades and had developed a taste for looking *at*.

"What do you want, then?" he asked. "Name it. If it's within a king's power, it's yours."

Silas had expected this question. She had rehearsed answers on the walk over—the clever ones, the modest ones, the ones that would sound right in the histories. But standing in the wax-and-smoke light, she found she only had the true one.

"The dowager's garden," she said. "The walled one behind the old queen's residence. And whatever land comes with it."

Aldric blinked. "The dowager is dead three months. Her household has been dispersed. The garden is—" He stopped, and she watched him arrive at it himself, the way understanding sometimes walks into a room ahead of the person carrying it. "That's where he'll be buried," he said. "The first king. That's what you're asking."

"He's been in a box under the archive floor for forty years," Silas said. "Bound to a crown, wearing his son and grandson and you like coats. He asked me—what was left of him asked—to let him end. The least the crown can give him now is a bed of soil and something green growing over him." She paused. "I'd like to be the one who plants it."

"You could have provinces," Aldric said, half-wondering. "You could have had a river named after you."

"I've spent five years being no one on purpose," Silas said. "Turns out I liked parts of it. I just want to choose which parts." She met his eyes. "Give me the wall and the gate and the ground inside it. Let me decide who comes through the gate. That's all the inheritance I ever wanted—one piece of earth that's mine because I chose it, not because it was left to me or awarded to me or carved out of someone else's ruin."

Aldric was quiet a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into something private, almost boyish.

"When I was small—before, I mean, truly before—I used to sneak into that garden. The dowager caught me stealing plums once. She didn't scold me. She sat me down and told me that everything worth keeping in this palace was either grown or mended, and everything else was just gilded." His hands trembled; he folded them. "I think I understood her better than anyone knew. I think that's why it hurt so much, later, when I couldn't remember why."

"She remembered why for both of you," Silas said gently. "Right up to the end."

"Yes." He looked away, toward the window, toward the garden neither of them could see. "She carried it alone for forty years. The least I can do is carry the truth loudly for the rest of mine." He turned back, and there was the king in him after all—not the puppet-king, not the gilded thing, but the man underneath, rusted and real. "The garden is yours, Silas Ravensworth. Wall, gate, and ground. Deeded in your name, freehold, in perpetuity—and if any clerk objects, tell them the king said the realm owes you a river and you settled for dirt."

* * *

They buried the first king on a morning in late autumn, when the light came slantwise through the garden wall and turned the bare plum trees to filigree.

There was no state procession. Aldric had offered one—full honors, a thousand mourners, bells—and Silas had asked him what the man in the box would have wanted, and Aldric had thought about it honestly, which was still new to him, and answered: *quiet*. So it was quiet. Seven people inside the wall: Silas, Wren, Aldric, Lady Maren leaning on a cane she pretended not to need, two of the bone-carvers from Tanner's Row whom Silas had asked by name, and old Mother Hesk, the dowager's last gardener, who had stayed on in the empty residence out of stubbornness and now found herself employed again by a deed signed in the king's hand.

The coffin was plain elm. Inside, wrapped in linen gone amber with age, lay what remained of King Edvard the First—the bones of him, and nothing else. Whatever had been *him* had screamed out of the world in a throne room in October, and the silence afterward had been the cleanest sound Silas had ever heard.

She had dug the grave herself. Not because there weren't men for it, but because some work doesn't transfer. The soil had come up dark and heavy, smelling of rain and rot and the sweet mineral cold underneath things, and she had worked it until her palms blistered, and the blisters had felt like a kind of honesty.

Wren stood beside her at the graveside, out of uniform for the first time since she'd known him. The hunter's grey hung in his wardrobe like a shed skin; today he wore a brown coat with a mended elbow, and he kept touching the mend absently, as if checking it was real.

"Do you think he's anywhere?" Wren murmured, as Aldric spoke the few words the rite required—words about rest, and debt, and a kingdom finally paying what it owed.

Silas listened inwardly, out of habit, to the silence where the dead used to live. It was a strange country, that silence. Vast. Level as a frozen lake. For twenty years her skull had been a crowded market; now it was an empty field at dawn, and she was still learning to walk in it without reaching for voices that would never come.

"I don't know," she said. "I used to know things like that. Now I get to wonder." She looked down at the coffin. "I think wondering might be better. Wondering means he gets to be a mystery instead of a message."

"That's very wise."

"It's very new. Ask me again in a year."

They lowered him together. Mother Hesk sang—not a hymn, but an old planting song about seeds sleeping in the dark until they forget they were ever anything else—and her voice cracked twice and nobody minded. Aldric wept without shame, standing at the head of the grave of the great-great-grandfather whose hunger had eaten his life, and Silas watched him and thought: *this is what the truth buys. Not comfort. But grief that lands on the right grave.*

When the last spadeful was tamped and the mound was dressed with cut plum branches, Maren touched Silas's arm.

"He'd have liked this," she said. "The old king. All those years in the dark, and now a garden." Her mouth twisted. "We spend our whole lives inheriting other people's sins and calling it duty. You're the first person I've met who inherited one and planted over it."

* * *

Winter came early that year, and Silas learned the garden the way she had once learned bones: by touch, in the dark, patiently.

The wall was older than the palace proper—fieldstone, lichened, its mortar patched by six generations of hands. The gate was iron and oak, and she had the key hung on a nail inside rather than carried, so that visitors always had to knock and wait and be *let in*, which she considered the entire point of walls. There was a potting shed with a sagging roof, a cold frame full of Mother Hesk's winter greens, a plum orchard asleep in its rows, and a stretch of raw ground along the south wall where the old king's grave lay flat and unmarked except for a river stone Silas had set at its head.

She worked every day that the frost allowed. Turning soil is slow theology. Each forkful came up holding something—old roots, a child's clay marble, a coin worn faceless, the pale threaded architecture of worms doing the world's oldest quiet work—and each day the ground grew looser, darker, more willing. She had spent her whole life handling death and making it speak. This was the opposite craft: taking a thing that had suffered enough and teaching it how to grow.

Her hearing was gone, but her hands remembered. Some evenings she caught herself humming while she worked—an old tune, wordless—and it took her weeks to recognize it as her mother's hum, carried all this way not in her ears but somewhere deeper, somewhere the crown's price hadn't reached. The dead were silent. What they'd given her stayed.

Wren came on Thursdays. He never announced it; he simply appeared at the gate at midmorning, knocking the particular knock—two, pause, one—that they had fallen into without discussing, carrying bread from the baker on Cooper's Lane and news from the city like a man bringing coals to a warm hearth.

On the first Thursday of December, he came with no bread at all and a paper in his fist, and Silas knew before he spoke.

"They discharged me," he said. "Formally. Honorably, even—the word feels like a joke. 'Services rendered beyond requirement.' Eleven years, and they gave me a certificate and a pension of forty crowns a year." He laughed, short and wrong. "Maren fought for back pay. She got it. I don't know what to do with money that came from hunting people I should have been protecting."

Silas wiped her hands on her apron and looked at him—at the brown coat, the mended elbow, the face that had changed more in three months than in the eleven years before it. Once he had been the most dangerous thing in her world. Now he stood in her garden holding a discharge paper like a man holding a map to a country that no longer existed.

"What will you do?" she asked.

"I don't know. That's the truth. I trained for one thing since I was fourteen, and the thing turned out to be a lie wearing armor." He folded the paper smaller and smaller between his fingers. "Some of the others are talking about forming a new order—guards for the carving districts, protection instead of pursuit. Odile says the underground needs watchers who've learned what watching costs. Maybe that. Maybe I'll just—" He gestured vaguely at the wall, the bare trees, the flat dark earth by the south wall. "I don't actually know what people do. With days. Ordinary ones."

"Neither do I," Silas said. "I'm finding out. It's slower than I expected and better than I feared."

He looked at her then, directly, the way he had learned to look at things since October—as though seeing were a discipline he intended to keep practicing. "And you? The king offers you a kingdom's gratitude and you take a walled garden. Everyone in the capital has a theory. Maren thinks you're hiding. Odile thinks you're mourning. The taverns think you're a saint, which is worse than either."

"What do you think?"

"I think," Wren said slowly, "that you spent your whole life belonging nowhere on purpose, and now you're building somewhere on purpose, and the difference between those two things is the whole story." He hesitated. "What will you do with all this quiet?"

Silas turned the question over. Beyond the wall, the city went on—hammers, cart wheels, the bright rude noise of the living, which she heard now more clearly than she had ever heard anything. Inside the wall, wind moved through the bare plums with a sound like long exhaling. Under her feet, the old king slept his first untroubled sleep in forty years, and around him the soil was already working, already softening, already beginning the patient process of turning an ending into a beginning.

"I'm going to plant it in spring," she said. "Plums, and apples, and herbs along the wall. Roses over the grave, maybe—Mother Hesk says the old queen loved damask roses, and I think he'd want to wake up under something she loved." She pulled off her gloves, finger by finger. "And I'm going to learn what my own voice sounds like when it's the only one. Twenty years of listening to everyone else, Wren. I never once asked myself what I wanted. Turns out the answer takes seasons."

"And if it's lonely?"

"Then it's mine," she said. "Lonely and mine beats crowded and borrowed. I did borrowed. I did it for five years, and it nearly killed me, and it nearly killed everyone." She smiled—small, real, new. "This is the first thing I've ever chosen with my eyes open. Walls, gate, ground. A grave to tend and a tree to prune and a Thursday visitor who brings bread and bad jokes. If that's inheritance, I'll take it."

Wren looked at her a moment longer, and something in his face eased, the way a knot eases—not undone, but loosened enough to breathe. "Thursdays," he said. "I can do Thursdays."

"I know," Silas said. "You've been doing them for a month."

He laughed then, properly, and the sound went up through the bare branches and out over the wall, and she thought: *the dead never laughed like that. Only the living do. Only the living.*

* * *

On the last day of winter, with frost still silvering the wall at dawn, Silas knelt by the south wall with a sack of damask rose roots across her knees and a spade in her blistered hands.

The ground over the old king's grave had settled into a gentle swell, grass just greening at its edges. She dug where Mother Hesk had shown her, three feet from the headstone-stone, deep enough for roots to find the cool dark and hold on. The rose roots were pale and tangled, alive in the ugly miraculous way that dormant things are alive, waiting in their burlap for someone to give them a reason.

"One season of sleep," she told them, and heard her own voice—rough, low, hers, the only voice in the garden, the only voice in her head, and for the first time in her life that felt like abundance instead of loss. "One season, and then you bloom over a king. He kept a kingdom waiting forty years. You can manage till summer."

She set the roots in, firmed the soil around them with her palms, and watered them from the rain barrel despite the cold, because hope is a thing you do before there's evidence.

Behind her, at the gate, came a knock. Two, pause, one—but lighter than usual, quicker, and beneath it another sound that made her go still: voices, several, young, excited, arguing happily about something. She rose, brushed the soil from her knees, and walked the path between the sleeping beds to the gate.

Through the iron bars she saw Wren—and behind him, half-hidden, four children from Tanner's Row in mismatched coats, clutching little bundles of carved bone whistles, peering up at the walled garden with wide eyes, as if it were a country they'd only heard sung about.

"Wren," she said, opening the gate. "You brought guests."

"They brought themselves," he said, grinning. "Odile's whole cellar heard you took the garden. They've been planning this for a week. Apparently—" he glanced down at the children, who were staring past Silas at the fresh-turned earth, the swelling grave-mound, the first green, "—apparently there's a tradition starting. Every carver in the city wants to plant something here. Over him. They say—" and his voice gentled, "—they say no one who was bound should sleep alone."

Silas looked at the children, at their small chapped hands full of bulbs and cuttings and seeds saved in twist of paper, at the living future crowding eagerly against her gate on the first warm morning of the year.

She stepped aside and held the gate wide.

"Well then," said Silas Ravensworth, gardener, carver, heir to nothing but her own choice. "Come in. There's ground enough for everyone—and spring's not going to wait."
