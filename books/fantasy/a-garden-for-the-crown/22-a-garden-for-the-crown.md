## 22. A Garden for the Crown

Spring came to the hill above the capital the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once.

Silas felt it first in her hands. All winter the soil had been stone, unyielding as any closed door, and she had worked it anyway, breaking clods with a fork until her palms cracked and healed and cracked again. But on the morning the thaw arrived, she knelt at the edge of the turned earth and felt the ground breathing under her fingers. Warmth rising from below. The old king's grave, warming like a sleeper's back.

She had chosen the plot in autumn, when the dowager's walled garden still stood empty and the council was still arguing over what to do with a queen's confession and a cracked crown. *The land is yours if you want it,* Aldric had said, in that new voice of his, thin and careful, a man learning to live inside his own body again. *I don't know what to do with grief that large. You seem to.*

She had wanted to tell him it wasn't grief she knew how to tend. It was what came after.

The wall around the garden was older than the city gates, moss-slicked granite furred with lichen the color of old coins. Inside it, the dowager's roses had gone feral decades ago, tangling into a thicket that Silas was clearing row by row, each cut cane leaving a bright scratch on her forearms like a signature. Beyond the wall, the city spilled down the hill in its spring noise—hammers, gulls, children, cart wheels, the market bell—and every sound arrived whole and ordinary, needing no interpretation. She was still learning how loud the living were.

* * *

The bell had come two months after the unbinding, hauled up the hill on a farmer's sledge by six carvers who refused payment and stayed for soup.

They had melted the crown in Wren's presence—she had insisted on witnesses, insisted that no piece of it survive unaccounted for—and poured the bronze into a mold Silas carved herself from applewood. The shape that emerged was nothing regal. A wide-mouthed bell, plain as a milk pail, its surface bearing the faint spiral of the wood grain transferred in metal, so that it looked less like an instrument of state than something grown. Where the crown's points had been, there was only a smooth lip. Where the binding rite had been etched inside the band, hidden against a king's skull for sixty years, the bronze now showed only the maker's mark: a small bird, wings folded.

Wren had asked, watching them hang it from the oak beam of the garden's arbor, whether it would ring.

"Only in wind," Silas said. She had shaped the clapper herself, filed it just shy of true, so that no hand could make it speak. "It has said everything it needs to say. Now it can just listen."

That had made him laugh—the short, startled laugh he still produced sometimes, as if surprised to find humor available to him. "A bell that listens. Only you, Ravensworth."

But it rang, in wind. Low and brief, a single soft note when the spring gusts came off the western hills, like a word spoken half in sleep. The carvers who visited claimed you could hear your own name in it if you wanted to badly enough. Silas never heard hers in it. She heard only wind through bronze, which was, she was discovering, its own kind of music.

* * *

She planted in the order the voices had taught her—no. Not the voices. She caught herself at it constantly, that first spring, reaching for counsel that no longer existed. She would stand with a tray of seedlings in her hands and realize she was waiting, head tilted, for advice that would never come, the way a person waits in an empty house for someone whose footsteps they used to know.

So she planted in the order her mother's almanac had taught her instead, which amounted to the same thing, because her mother had been the one whose hum she'd carried longest. Sweet peas along the south wall, where the dowager had grown them, because some things deserved continuity. Beans in the bed directly over the grave—nitrogen-fixing, generous, the kind of plant that gave more than it took. Root vegetables in the deep loam near the gate. And in the center, in the circle of ground where the old king's bones rested at last beneath three feet of honest dirt, she planted nothing at all. She left it bare and raked it smooth, a circle of dark earth like a held breath, and let it be a resting place rather than a bed.

He had asked her to. Or the fragment of him had—the ghost worn down to almost nothing, fewer words than a charm, begging her in the palace walls to end it, end it, end it. In the last night before the ceremony, when she had sat saying goodbye to every voice she would ever lose, his had been the last. It had gathered itself out of the foundation stones long enough to say one whole sentence, the only one it had left:

*Let there be something growing over me that I chose.*

Not his wife's choice. Not his kingdom's. His own. So she grew things there that he might have liked—a king who had hunted and hawked and, according to the servants' bones, once smuggled honey cakes to kitchen girls—fruit canes along the circle's edge, a young plum tree at its rim, planted the day the frost left. The plum was the first thing in the garden to bloom. It did so extravagantly, foolishly, covering itself in white while the rest of the beds were still brown stubble, and Silas had stood under it with petals in her hair and thought: *yes. That. That is the right kind of extravagant for a man who waited sixty years.*

* * *

The carvers came up the hill on Thursdays.

That had not been planned; it had simply accreted. The first week after the pardon, a woman named Odile appeared at the garden gate with a basket of bone blanks and an expression like someone testing ice. Silas knew her vaguely from the underground network—the bone-carvers' cells beneath the tannery quarter, where she had spent one bewildered, crowded week after the raid, surrounded for the first time in her life by people who could hear what she heard. People who had looked at her scarred hands and known exactly what they were.

Odile had brought the blanks because her brother had died in winter and the family wanted a mourning charm carved properly, openly, with a priest present and no fear in the room. Silas had carved it on the arbor bench in full daylight while Odile's family watched, and when the husband's voice came through the whorled femur—thin, astonished, saying *I'm here? I'm still here?*—nobody flinched. They wept. The priest took notes for his records, because the Church had decided, after some agonizing, that the dead speaking through bone was not sorcery but sacrament, and there were forms now, paperwork for miracles.

After that, Thursdays happened. Three carvers, then nine, then a dozen, spreading their work along the garden wall where the light was good. They carved mourning charms mostly, and memory charms for families scattering, and small listening bones that midwives used to hear an unborn child's heartbeat double itself in the shell of a hazelnut. Some of them sold their work in the market now, openly, with little stamped certificates from the Crown Guild of Hearths and Holdings—an absurd name nobody used, printed on paper Aldric had signed with a hand that still trembled. Most days the hill path was busy as a street, apprentices hauling water, old men arguing about grain direction, somebody always singing.

Silas carved with them on Thursdays but took no students. Her gift was gone; what she had now was craft, forty years of knowing how bone wants to be cut, and she taught that freely. But when a finished charm began to whisper in a student's hands, she heard only the room—the scrape of knives, the wind bell, Odile humming. She had stopped minding. Mostly.

* * *

Wren came on a Tuesday in late spring, carrying bread.

This was their arrangement without ever having been arranged: he arrived when he arrived, always with food, always with no reason. He had been discharged from the hunters with honor—*restored to civilian standing*, the document read, as though he had been a building under repair—and had spent a strange, restless winter trying to be a civilian before admitting to Silas, over her table, that he had no idea what he was for anymore. He was helping rebuild the hunters' corps into something else, a watch that answered to the council instead of the crown, but the work moved slowly and was mostly meetings, and Wren was not built for meetings. He was built for walking roads and carrying heavy things and standing between people and harm. So on the days the meetings defeated him, he walked the hill road with bread or cheese or, once, a completely inexplicable crate of ducklings, and he worked in her garden with his sleeves rolled up until the restlessness drained out of him.

Today it was bread, dark and dense, still warm through the cloth. He broke it on the bench without asking, the way you do when a place has become yours enough.

"The plum's showing off," he said.

"It earned it."

He ate, looking down the hill at the city. From here you could see the tannery quarter, and past it the market square where the Proclamation of Unmaking had been nailed to posts five years ago, and where last month the same crier—it was the same man, Silas had checked, unable to help herself—had read the Repeal aloud to a crowd that went silent and then made a sound she had never heard a crowd make before, somewhere between a sob and a cheer.

"They're debating the archive," Wren said. "Whether to open the binding rites to public study. Maren's pushing for it."

"Maren nearly died in those archives."

"Maren nearly died everywhere. It's her favorite way to live." He tore another piece of bread. "Council's scared. Half of them think if people read the rite, someone will try to use it."

Silas considered this, turning a seedling pot in her hands. "Someone will. Someday. Someone who can't let go, the way the dowager couldn't." She had visited the old woman twice more before her death that first winter—quiet visits, tea in the walled garden, the confession already given and therefore no longer heavy, just true. *My husband begged me to let him go,* the dowager had said once, weeding with slow, precise hands. *And I heard him. That's the part I cannot forgive myself for. I heard him beg and I loved the sound of his voice more than I loved him.* "But better it be read in daylight than found in the dark. That's the whole lesson of the last sixty years, isn't it? Secrets grow teeth."

Wren grunted, which from him was agreement. Then, after a while: "How's the quiet?"

She understood what he was asking. He asked it maybe once a season, never pressed, and she had learned it was not pity but inventory—he was checking on her the way she checked the beans, gently, regularly, for blight.

She listened inward, as she did now and then, to the place where the voices had lived. It was not empty, exactly. It was quiet the way a well-kept room is quiet: furnished, warmed, awaiting nothing. For the first months the silence had grieved her like an amputation, phantom voices arriving at the hours she used to hear them—the widow's husband at dusk, her mother's hum at the edge of sleep. But grief, she had learned in this garden, does not leave. It composts. It goes down into whatever you're trying to grow and feeds it, if you turn it often enough, if you don't let it sit and sour.

"It's mine," she said. "All of it. Every sound I get now, I get because it's actually there." She looked at him. "Do you know how much of my life I spent interpreting? Reading echoes, guessing at what was meant instead of what was said. I heard the dead clearly and the living not at all. Now—" she gestured at the hillside, the hammer-noise of the city, the wind bell giving its low single note as a gust came through, Wren's breath, her own pulse, "—now it's just the world. Unfiltered. Loud and rude and real. I find I prefer it."

"You prefer anything rude," Wren said. "It's why you tolerate me."

"It's why I *keep* you," she corrected, and had the rare pleasure of watching him go slightly pink.

* * *

They planted the last of the beans together as the afternoon slanted gold, his big hands making surprisingly neat furrows, hers setting the seed. Over the grave-circle she scattered nothing today, only walked its border as she did each evening, one slow circuit, the way you'd check on someone sleeping. The plum tree dropped petals across the smooth dark soil. Somewhere beyond the wall the city went on being rebuilt—not just the buildings; the whole idea of it, the assumption underneath everything that some truths were too dangerous to say. It was going badly in places. It was going. She had decided that was what freedom was: not peace, but motion, messy and loud and alive.

At the gate, when he left, Wren paused the way he always did, one hand on the latch.

"Thursday," he said. "I'll bring—"

"Whatever you bring," Silas said, "bring no agenda with it."

"Never have."

"I know," she said. "That's the point of you."

He grinned and went down the hill, and she stood a moment longer in the gate's shadow, listening. Wind in the plum blossom. The bell's low note. A cart, gulls, children racing somewhere below, Odile's apprentices laughing on the road. The living world, arriving in waves, asking nothing of her but attention.

She turned back to the garden, picked up her fork, and put her hands in the earth.
