## 1. Charms for Small Coin

There are three rules to staying invisible in Vell, and I have spent five years learning all of them the hard way, which is to say: by nearly dying of each one in turn.

Rule one: never let them see you work. Rule two: never work for free, because charity gets remembered and memory gets people burned. Rule three — the one I invented myself, after the fever winter, after the night I held a dying dockhand's heart steady with both hands and had to look his widow in the eye and lie — never, under any circumstances, let them see you *care*.

Caring is a kind of light. Light is a kind of confession.

So on Tallowday mornings, when the market quarter of Vell smells like wet wool and frying onions and the particular desperation of people about to spend money they don't have, I am nobody. I am a small woman with a crooked stall at the crooked end of Cordwainer's Row, past the fishmongers and before the man who sells birds that cannot sing. I have chapped hands and a seller's smile and nothing in my eyes but commerce.

"Charms," I call, in a voice I keep deliberately flat, deliberately dull. "Luck-knots, fever-strings, safe-passage braids. Woven fresh, woven fair."

*Woven fresh, woven fair.* It's even true, which helps. The best lies are load-bearing, and you build them out of true things.

The knots themselves are nothing special to look at — cord and thread, mostly, with a bead or a button worked in where the pattern wants it. A luck-knot is three strands braided over a whispered word. A fever-charm is red thread wound nine times around a splinter of rowan. A safe-passage braid takes me an hour and I only make those for people I like, which is a short list, which is the point.

What they don't see — what *can't* see, because I take precautions — is the ember.

That's what my grandmother called it, the gift, before the word *gift* became a legal category and then a death sentence. The ember sits somewhere behind my sternum, or in my blood, or in the part of me that isn't strictly flesh; I've never been able to pin it down and I've stopped trying. When I work a knot, I feed a thread of it into the weave, and the weave holds it, and the person wearing it gets a little warmth against bad luck. A fever that breaks a day early. A foot that finds the dry cobblestone. Small mercies. Coin-sized mercies.

The gloves are iron-thread. Real iron, spun fine and worked into grey wool, and they cost me a month's earnings and a favor I still owe a very unpleasant woman in the tannery district. Iron doesn't stop the ember — nothing stops the ember — but it *muffles* it, the way a heavy curtain muffles a lamp. Through the gloves, my glow reads as nothing. Body heat. Nerves. The ordinary warmth of an ordinary woman selling ordinary string.

I have not taken the gloves off in public in three years. I have not taken them off in private in one.

This is what I want you to understand about invisibility: it is not freedom. It is a job. It is a job you work every hour of every day, with no holidays, no wages, and a penalty clause written in fire. I dream about the gloves. I dream that they're growing into my skin, that the wool is knitting itself to my knuckles, and I wake up and put them on again because the alternative is a pyre in Coronation Square and my name read out by a crier like a list of debts.

"Charms," I call. "Woven fresh, woven fair."

* * *

The child arrives an hour past midday, when the market is at its thickest and I am at my most tired, which is a dangerous combination I should have seen coming.

She's maybe eight, maybe nine, with a scab on her knee and a copper coin clenched in her fist so hard her knuckles have gone white. She stands at the edge of my stall for a long moment, doing that thing children do when they're deciding whether you're a person or an obstacle.

"Fever-charm," she says. "For my mum. She's got the lung-rot."

The lung-rot. I look at her — scabby knee, secondhand coat two sizes too big, a coin that won't cover a fever-charm at my prices, let alone a doctor's — and every rule I have stands up in a row and looks at me sternly.

"One copper," I say, because rule two exists for a reason, and because a charm given free gets remembered, and memory gets people burned.

She puts the coin down like it's an egg she's afraid of dropping. I take it. My hands, gloved and steady, select a red thread and a rowan splinter, and I begin to wind.

Here is the thing about working tired. You get sloppy. Not with the craft — the craft is in my bones, older than my fear — but with the *gates*. Every worker learns to build gates between the ember and the weave, little valves that let power through in a trickle instead of a flood. Tired gates stick. Tired gates jam open.

The child is watching my hands with her whole face, the way children watch, and she says, "My gran had one of these. Before. She said you could feel it working, like a bird in your pocket."

"Gran was a liar," I say pleasantly, "or a romantic, and those are the same thing."

And then the gate jams.

I feel it happen — feel the ember lurch through me like a swallowed spark, feel the charm drink too deep, and I clamp down, I clamp down *hard*, but the red thread is already drinking, already glowing, and when I hold it up to hand it to her it is not red thread anymore. It is a filament of soft gold light, bright as a candle-flame, bright as a confession, humming against my gloved fingers like something alive.

The child's eyes go wide. Not afraid. *Wondering.* That's worse.

"It's singing," she breathes.

"Take it," I hiss, "and go."

She takes it. The glow dims as it leaves my hands, settling into the thread, banked but not out — a coal wrapped in wool, and any warden with a sight-glass or half a witch's training would read it for exactly what it is. She stuffs it in her coat pocket and runs, and I am already moving, sweeping my stall's cloth over the remaining charms, my heart going like a hammer in a burning house.

Because across the Row, by the fishmongers, a grey coat has turned.

Wardens don't patrol Cordwainer's Row. That's why I'm here. Wardens patrol Coronation Square and the palace district and the rich streets where paranoia is a fashion accessory. But today — Tallowday, market day, an ordinary day — a warden in a grey coat has stopped between the fish stalls and is looking down the Row with his head tilted, the way a dog tilts its head at a sound it can't quite place.

I do the math. Thirty feet between us. One crooked stall, one cloth-covered pile of charms that glow like banked coals, one exit through the tannery lane that smells bad enough to hide anything, including me.

The warden takes a step. Then another.

And that's when the child — gods bless her and gods curse her — trips. Right there in the middle of the Row, scabby knee meeting cobblestone, and the fever-charm flies out of her pocket and lands glowing in the open air like a dropped star, and the warden's head snaps toward it, and the whole market seems to hold its breath.

I am already through the tannery lane before the screaming starts. I don't look back. Looking back is how you get caught, and getting caught is how you get burned, and I have spent five years learning that too.

But here is what I carry out of the market with me, past the tannery vats, through the reeking alleys, all the way to my rented room with its one window and its iron-thread curtains: not fear. Fear I know. Fear is an old coat; it fits me fine.

What I carry is the child's face. That wide, wondering look. *It's singing,* she said, like it was the best thing she'd ever seen. Like my whole hidden, halved, glove-bound life was something worth marveling at.

I sit on my cot in the dark and I pull my gloves off, finally, and my hands glow faintly in the gloom — gold at the fingertips, gold in the veins of my wrists, the ember breathing its slow light like a thing that has been holding its breath for five years.

And I think: *they saw. Someone saw, and the warden saw, and by tomorrow there will be a description, and descriptions become lists, and lists become pyres.*

I should run. I know all the roads out of Vell. I've walked them in my head a hundred times, packed my imaginary bag, said my imaginary goodbyes to the short list of people I like.

But the child is out there with a fever-charm and a sick mother and my light in her pocket, and the wardens will find her before they find me, and children say names. Children always say names.

I put the gloves back on.

In the morning, the criers are in the streets before the fishmongers are, and their bells are ringing the pattern I have only ever heard in nightmares: the long-short-short of royal proclamation.

*"By order of His Majesty King Aldric,"* they cry, *"all persons bearing the tainted gift shall present themselves for registry within seven days. Those who conceal themselves shall be sought. Those who are found shall be cleansed by fire."*

I stand at my window with my gloved hands pressed flat against the sill, and I think of Maren, my old teacher, who lives — who *lived*, who had better still be living — in a cottage on the Mill Road with blue shutters and a herb garden and no idea what's coming.

Seven days. The criers say seven days.

Maren won't even hear the bells until noon.
