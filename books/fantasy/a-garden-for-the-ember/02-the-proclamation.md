## 2. The Proclamation

The bells woke me before the criers did, which should have been my first warning. Vell's bells rang for weddings, for funerals, for the turning of the year — never at dawn on an ordinary grey Tuesday. Bells at dawn meant someone in a tall tower had decided the day belonged to him.

I lay in my rented room above the dye-shop and listened to the city grumble awake beneath the racket, and I thought, with the particular clarity of the not-yet-caffeinated: *someone has died, or someone is about to.*

Then the criers started.

"By decree of His Majesty King Aldric!" The voice carried up through my shutters, brass-bright, rehearsed. "All persons gifted with witchcraft, hedge-craft, ember-craft, or any art of the old blood shall present themselves to the Registry of Loyal Subjects within seven days!"

I sat up so fast I cracked my head on the low beam and didn't even feel it.

"Those who register shall be examined, catalogued, and bound to lawful service under the Crown." A pause, professionally timed. "Those who do not register *shall be burned.*"

Burned. Not exiled, not fined, not imprisoned pending paperwork. Burned. With the cheerful briskness of a man reading out grain prices.

I dressed in the dark, which is a skill five years of hiding teaches you — gloves first, always gloves first, iron-thread woven through the knuckles so that if my gift ever woke restless in the night it would find its glow smothered before it reached the window — and I was down the stairs past a bewildered dyer's apprentice and into the street while the crier was still working through the penalties for harbouring the unregistered.

*Harbouring shall be accounted as the crime itself,* he said to a market square gone very quiet.

Well. That was most of the market square condemned, wasn't it. Half of Vell had bought something from somebody who smelled faintly of herbs. They just hadn't known it.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world tilts: I went to work. Habit is a kind of armour, and mine had kept me alive this long. I set out my tray of luck-knots and fever-charms in my usual corner near the fountain, and I watched the crowd instead of selling, because today the crowd was the weather report.

They weren't panicking. That was the strange thing. They clustered around the criers the way people cluster around a street fight — hungry for spectacle, careful not to touch it. A baker's wife asked me, not meeting my eyes, whether my fever-charms still worked. A man I'd sold a luck-knot to last autumn looked at me a beat too long and then walked away very fast.

Seven days, I thought. Seven days for everyone in Vell who had ever whispered over a kettle or hung a charm above a cradle to decide whether they were brave or lucky or simply tired enough to stop pretending. And every one of them would be doing arithmetic with my face. *Do I know her? Did she sell to me? Will reporting her shorten my own list?*

I packed up my tray before noon. First time in five years I'd left a pitch early. My hands were steady. I want that noted somewhere, by someone: my hands were perfectly steady, and it cost me everything I had.

Maren. Get to Maren.

Because here is the thing about Maren Ostell, herbalist, hedge-witch, the woman who taught me that magic isn't fire you hide but breath you ration: she would not run. She would stand in her cottage doorway with her arms crossed and inform the wardens, in her flat Fenmark accent, that she had been healing this district since before their mothers learned to walk, and that if the king wanted her registered he could come inside and have some soup while he filled out the forms.

Maren did not run. Maren argued. And arguing, as of dawn today, was a burning offence.

* * *

The Tanners' Row stairs were slick with the morning's drizzle, and I took them two at a time, which at my age and level of cardiovascular honesty is less running than controlled falling. Her cottage sits — sat — at the top where the row opens onto the old orchard wall, smoke curling from its chimney at all hours because Maren believed a hearth should be warm whether anyone needed it or not.

No smoke.

I told myself: she's at market. She overslept. She's sulking about the criers and letting her tea go cold out of principle.

I told myself a great many things on those stairs, and none of them survived the open door.

The door wasn't broken. That was the detail that stopped me on the threshold with rain dripping off my nose. It stood ajar, politely, almost invitingly, and inside the cottage had been turned over with the same courtesy. Drawers opened, contents lifted out and laid on the table in neat rows. Jars of dried yarrow and feverfew sorted by species, lids replaced. Her chair pushed in. Her teacup on its saucer.

Wardens don't tidy. Wardens kick doors and leave splinters and take what's valuable. This was something else. This was a search conducted by someone who wanted the room to say *we know exactly what we're looking for, and we found it.*

Her herb bundles were gone. Every one — the sage for cleansing, the valerian for sleep, the little twist of rowan she kept over the lintel "for sentiment," she said, though we both knew better. All the tools of her craft, removed with care. And the floorboards near the hearth had been pried up and set back down just slightly wrong, a seam of dark air between plank and joist, and I knew without looking what had been under there: her mother's grimoire, the one she swore she'd burned years ago.

Gone too.

I stood in the middle of my mentor's ransacked-and-tidied kitchen feeling the specific vertigo of a person whose worst guess has been confirmed by the furniture. Then I saw it.

On the table, weighted down with her salt cellar — her salt cellar, left behind like a signature — lay a strip of linen. On it, drawn in something brown and deliberate, was a mark I knew better than my own handwriting: a circle crossed by three lines, the apprentice sigil Maren had pressed into my palm with her thumb when I was sixteen and terrified and certain I was a monster.

She had marked me as her apprentice. Deliberately. Left it where any searching hand would find it, or where only one particular pair of eyes would understand it.

Because it wasn't a confession. Look closer, Freya. It was a message.

The circle was broken — a gap cut clean through the ring at the top. In our craft, a broken circle means *the path is open; follow.* The three lines weren't evenly spaced. Two close together, one apart. Two-and-one. The old signal we used when I was learning wards: *two safe roads closed, one remains.* And the mark itself was drawn pointing — the gap in the circle angled toward the orchard wall, toward the hills beyond, toward—

Toward the palace outskirts. Toward the old royal gardens, the ones nobody tended anymore, the ones Maren had once told me — drunk on plum wine at midwinter, laughing — held "a well worth more than the king knows."

I had thought she was joking. Maren joked the way cats blink: rarely, and meaning it.

*Two-and-one. Follow the broken circle.*

And underneath the relief of understanding it, rising slow and cold as floodwater, came the other thing. The guilt. Because here was the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do for five years: I had hidden. I had gloved myself and charmed my stall and paid my rent and kept my head down, and I had called it survival, and it was, and I had also — let's be honest, Freya, no one else will be — I had also left her alone with it. I visited. I brought honey. I let her feed me and fuss over me and tell me stories about the old days, and then I went home behind my locked door and my iron-thread gloves, and I never once said the words *come away with me, teach me the deep wards, let me stand between you and whatever comes.*

Running was never the same as being safe. Running was just fear with good posture. And now the wardens had been through her cottage with white gloves and tidy hands, and she was gone, and the only trail she could trust anyone to read was written for me.

Or — the colder voice, the one I'd spent five years feeding and was now starting to suspect of treason — the trail was written *to* draw me. Left deliberately, the room had said. We know what we're looking for. Maybe what they were looking for wasn't a grimoire.

Maybe it was an apprentice.

I folded the linen into my glove, against my palm, where my pulse could argue with it. Outside, the drizzle had thickened into honest rain, and across the rooftops another crier was bellowing the same bright sentence — *shall be burned, shall be burned* — with the tireless good cheer of a man who had memorized his text and skipped the meaning entirely.

Seven days. One broken circle pointing at a dead garden above a well worth more than the king knew.

I pulled my hood up, checked that my glow slept quiet beneath the iron thread, and ran — properly, this time, no pretense of falling — down through the Tanners' Row and out along the orchard wall, toward the one road Maren said remained.

Behind me, at the mouth of the lane, a warden in grey turned and began to follow.
