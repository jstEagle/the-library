## 3. What Wears the Crown

The mark Maren left was a lily bent at the stem, and it pointed east.

That's the trouble with apprenticeships. You spend ten years learning that every gesture means something—that a crooked knot is a warning, that salt scattered in a doorway is an invitation, that a flower snapped wrong-way-up on a ransacked table is a compass—and then you can never look at anything ordinary again. I once spent three days convinced a dead sparrow outside my window was a death-oath from the Lamplighters' Guild. It was a dead sparrow. But I checked the omen books anyway, because Maren trained me thorough.

The lily pointed east, toward Gildrow, where the palace sprawls over the hill like a fat cat that owns everything it sees. Which meant Maren had either been dragged toward the seat of the purge, or she'd gone there on purpose, leaving me a trail because she knew I'd follow and wanted me to do it *smart*.

I intended to be smart. Truly. I had a plan involving side streets, my dullest cloak, and a face arranged into the particular blankness of someone thinking only about bread.

Then I heard the bells, and the crowd, and I did what I always do at crowds: I went toward them like an idiot.

Gildrow Square had been cleared of stalls for the occasion. In their place: a scaffold of pale new timber, two iron posts, and chains. Between the posts stood a woman in a grey dress, her wrists bound above her head, her hair loose. She was swaying slightly, the way people sway when they've stopped fighting and started conserving.

A warden stood at each corner of the scaffold. And before the scaffold, on a raised drape of black velvet, sat a chair.

The king sat in it.

I want to tell you what I felt first. I've tried, in the years since, to get the order right. Fear, obviously—fear should have come first, fear is my oldest companion, we're practically married. But what came first was something worse: recognition. The specific, stomach-dropping recognition of seeing a thing that is wearing a shape correctly and knowing, anyway, that nothing is home.

It was cold that morning. Proper cold, breath-fog cold, the kind where the whole square exhales together in little ghosts. Every person in that crowd smoked white from the mouth. The wardens. The woman in chains. Me.

Not the king.

He sat perfectly still, hands folded, watching his own cleansing with the mild attention of a man watching rain. His face was Aldric's face—I'd seen it stamped on coin and painted on tavern walls all my life—but it hung on him strangely, like a coat worn by someone with different shoulders. And around him, the light did something I still can't describe without my hands going cold. The shadows didn't fall away from him. They leaned *in*. All of them, all at once, like courtiers bowing to something they liked better than the sun.

"Gifted one," a herald read out, in the flat recitation of a man who has done this too many times this week, "you are found unregistered. By the king's mercy, you will be cleansed, and your gift returned to the crown."

*Returned to the crown.* I remember thinking: that's odd phrasing. Gifts aren't returned. Gifts aren't property. That's rather the point of them.

The woman lifted her head. From thirty feet away I saw her lips move—not screaming, not begging. Praying, maybe. Or counting. Some witches count when they're frightened; Maren taught me that. She said the numbers keep you in your body.

They lit the pyre at the base of the scaffold.

Here is what I expected: smoke, screaming, the ugly ordinary horror of burning. Here is what happened instead. As the fire climbed toward her feet, something came off the woman. Not smoke. A light—a soft gold shimmer, like heat-haze in reverse, rising off her skin the way steam rises off tea. It drifted upward, unhurried, almost gentle.

And the king breathed it in.

He didn't stand. He didn't move at all. He just opened his mouth slightly, the way you'd taste soup on the air, and the golden shimmer curved across the square, against the wind, into him. Where it touched him his skin brightened for a moment—lit from inside, briefly, like a lantern behind paper—and then dimmed again, hungry again, already looking for more.

The woman screamed then. Late, but who could blame her. What was left to burn wasn't the fire's doing.

I got out of the square. I don't remember deciding to. My body made an executive decision and my legs ratified it, and some part of me—the charm-seller part, the survivor part—had the presence of mind to keep my head down and walk, not run, until the crowd swallowed me into an alley and I was sick behind a cooper's barrel with my gloves pulled up past my wrists like bandages.

Because here's the thought that wouldn't stop, the one I crouched there holding like something venomous:

*I know that light.*

Five years selling luck-knots, and I'd never let myself see it plainly—my own ember, reflected back in a flare or a flicker, always smothered fast, always shameful. But I knew its color. Gold-amber, warm as a hearth. The same color that had just been poured out of a stranger and drunk by a corpse in a crown.

The purge wasn't cruelty. Cruelty has reasons—politics, fear, spite. This was simpler and infinitely worse. This was a *table being set*. Every gifted person in Vell wasn't a criminal to the Crown. We were food.

Maren hadn't been arrested. She'd been *collected*.

* *

Captain Ilsa Voss counted the cleansings the way other officers counted casualties: precisely, and with a ledger no one would ever see.

Three this week. Eleven since the edict. Her quill moved through the report in her neat, joyless hand—*subject secured, subject cleansed, gift yield: satisfactory*—and she wrote the word *satisfactory* without letting herself think about how her sister had tasted to whatever wore the king's face. Petra had been loud about everything: her opinions, her laugh, her small green magic that made bread rise twice as high. Voss had spent twenty years building a career on never being loud about anything. When the edict came, she had done the arithmetic every warden in Vell did—report Petra or lose her post, lose the access, lose any chance of protecting anyone—and she had reported a false address instead, and Petra had been found anyway, because the Crown always found them, and Voss had stood at the edge of the square with her helmet on and her face like weathered stone while her sister's gold went up in the air and *in*.

She had not looked away. Looking away was a luxury. She kept her eyes open so that someday, if there was a someday, she could describe exactly what she'd seen to exactly the right person.

Today she watched the king leave the square—or watch the body leave, carried in a litter he didn't need, shadows pooling under it like spilled ink. Today's yield had been thin. She'd heard the Crown say so, afterward, to Chancellor Merek, in a voice like dry pages turning: *"Thin. They hide too well. Find me the old teacher. An ember-bearer's line runs through her students."*

Voss had written that sentence on the inside of her skull, where she kept everything that mattered.

An ember-bearer. Rare lineage. Somewhere in this city, someone the Crown considered a delicacy, and someone Maren Ostell had taught.

Voss closed her ledger and gave the order to sweep the eastern quarters.

* *

By dusk I'd picked up the trail again, and it was getting worse.

Maren's marks were getting cruder. The second lily was scratched into a doorpost instead of laid as a flower, which meant she'd been moving fast, or restrained, or both. The third was pressed into candle wax on a windowsill in Cooper's Row, and it was shaking—her fine engraver's control gone ragged. Whatever they were doing to her between waypoints, it cost her.

And the waypoints were leading somewhere deliberate. Not away from the palace. Toward its outskirts. Toward the old garden wall.

Which meant she wasn't leaving me a trail to safety. She was leaving me a trail to *her*, and she was choosing ground. Maren Ostell had forgotten more hedge-craft than I'll ever learn, and if she was steering her own capture toward a specific patch of dirt, then that patch of dirt mattered.

The problem was that I wasn't the only one following the trail.

I spotted them at the mouth of Vine Street: two wardens in the grey-and-silver, walking the lilies with their heads down, reading the same message I was. Behind them, further back, a third. And beyond the third, at the street's end, a fourth figure who wasn't in uniform at all—tall, mud-booted, moving with the easy silence of someone who knows every crack in these streets, keeping pace with the wardens the way a shadow keeps pace with a lantern.

I flattened into a doorway and did arithmetic. Wardens ahead, hunter behind, Maren somewhere between me and a garden I couldn't see yet, and night coming down cold enough to fog every breath in Vell except, presumably, the king's.

The smart move was to go home, bar the door, and mourn my mentor from a safe distance, the way I'd mourned everything else for five years.

But I'd seen the light come off that woman in the square. I'd seen it curve, obedient, into a dead man's mouth. And I'd realized, crouched behind that cooper's barrel, that I recognized the color because it was mine—that somewhere in the Crown's endless appetite there was a place reserved, a place set, a plate waiting with my name on it.

Running had never been the same as safe. I'd just spent five years pretending otherwise.

So when the mud-booted figure glanced back down the street—glanced directly at my doorway, unhurried, unsurprised, as if he'd known I was there since Vine Street—and tilted his head toward the garden wall, I did the least smart thing available.

I followed him.
