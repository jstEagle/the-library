## 4. The Gardener's Gate

I had a plan for being hunted, in the way that I had plans for most disasters: keep moving, keep small, keep the gloves on. It was not a good plan. It was the plan of a woman who has spent five years perfecting the art of being furniture. Furniture does not survive a purge. Furniture just gets thrown on the fire with everything else.

The palace outskirts of Vell are a geography of forgetting—old servants' lanes behind the terraced gardens, walls furred with moss, gates that haven't opened since Aldric's father was king. I'd chosen them because nobody walks there. That was the point. Nobody walking there is exactly what makes it a good place to be caught.

The warden patrol found me within an hour.

Not because of anything I did. Because of what I was: a person moving with purpose through streets built for idleness, gloved hands clenched, hood up in weather that didn't warrant it. In Vell, guilt has a particular gait. I'd seen it in the mirror for years.

"Halt." The voice came flat off the stone. Two wardens, lanterns swinging despite the grey afternoon, and behind them the low murmur of the city proper—the cleansing still going on, smoke standing over the market district like a second skyline.

I ran.

I want to tell you it was heroic, that I vaulted a wall or turned my ember into something bright and cutting. What actually happened was that I sprinted down a servants' lane like a rabbit with opinions, knocked over someone's rain barrel, and discovered that five years of hunching your magic into a knot small enough to hide does absolutely nothing for your cardio.

They were gaining. Of course they were gaining. They hadn't spent their whole lives apologizing to their own lungs.

A gate appeared on my left—a gap in the wall where a door had once been and then thought better of it—and a hand shot out of the dark of it and closed around my wrist and *pulled*.

I went through the gap the way water goes down a drain, which is to say gracelessly, and landed against someone's chest. A smell of loam and crushed mint. The hand released me, and a second hand reached past me and eased the door shut without letting it click. Then nothing. Darkness that smelled like a greenhouse. My heart hammering so loud I was certain it would give us both away.

Outside, boots slowed. Stopped. One warden muttered something to the other—I caught "gone to ground," "the terraces," and a word that made my skin crawl: *"ember-scent."*

Then the boots moved on.

In the dark, the stranger breathed slowly, deliberately, the way you breathe when you're teaching someone else how. I matched him without meaning to. In. Out. Until the lantern-glow beyond the door dimmed and died.

"Well," he said quietly. "You're either very brave or very stupid."

"I've been told it's a blend."

He struck no light. He didn't need to; my eyes were adjusting, and I made out a tall man, somewhere past fifty, with dirt worked permanently into his knuckles and a face like a map that had been folded wrong too many times. His clothes were plain wool gone soft with age, but I noticed—the habit of noticing is another thing five years teaches you—that they'd been fine once. Cut for palace service. The shoulders taken in by his own needle.

"You're the one from the cleansing," he said. Not a question.

"How—"

"Ember-scent." He said it with distaste, as if the wardens had invented a rude word for something lovely. "It clings. Yours especially. You burn hot under all that iron thread, girl. Or—" he tilted his head, reconsidering, "—not girl. Forgive me."

"Freya," I said. "Either's fine. Neither's fine too."

"Freya, then." He nodded, filing it away without ceremony, and I decided in that instant that I liked him—not because he was kind, though he might have been, but because he corrected himself the way people correct a mislabeled seed packet: important, but not worth agonizing over. "Tam," he said. "Formerly of His Majesty's gardens. Formerly," he added, "of quite a lot of things."

* * *

He led me deeper into the gap between wall and world, along a path only he could see. We came up inside what I took, at first, for an abandoned nursery—cold frames with broken glass, pots stacked like sleeping turtles, a trellis holding up nothing but ivy and regret. But the air was warmer than it should have been. Warmer by degrees, and sweeter, and underneath the sweetness something mineral, like snowmelt over stone.

"The palace outskirts," I said. "We're still—"

"Gardeners' ground." Tam lifted a latch hidden under a shelf of empty pots. "Nobody purges the gardens. The Crown keeps them." Something moved across his face, quick as a shrike. "That should tell you something, if you think about it. Why would a thing that hates the gift bother keeping a garden alive?"

"Why does it?"

"That," he said, "is the question I've been carrying for six years like a stone in my boot."

Beyond the latch, the ground changed. The path became older—older than Vell's brickwork, older than anything the city had built—and it curved downward, gently, the way roots curve, following some logic older than roads. And then the walls opened, and I stopped walking.

I have seen the royal gardens from the outside, same as everyone: the terraces above the river, clipped and formal, the kind of beauty that takes forty gardeners and a hundred slaves-of-schedule. This was not that. This was a garden that had never been designed, only *grown*—and grown around a single tree.

I don't have words that are equal to it, but I'll try, because Maren taught me that failing to describe a thing is its own kind of cowardice. The tree stood at the center of a hollow, vast and silver-barked, and its leaves were the color of banked coals—not burning, never burning, just holding light the way a cupped hand holds water. The air around it hummed. Not sound, exactly. Pressure. The hum of a held breath six hundred years long.

And it was dying.

Once you saw it, you couldn't unsee it. Whole limbs bare. Leaves curling brown at the coal-bright edges. The hum itself faltered every few breaths, like a heart skipping beats.

"This is why the Crown keeps the gardens," Tam said. His voice had gone very quiet, the way voices do in sickrooms. "This is the garden. Everything else is hedge-trimming around it."

"What is it?"

"A seal." He walked toward the tree with the unhurried step of a man who had done it ten thousand times and never once gotten used to it. "Before there was a Vell, there was a wellspring here. All the gift in this land rises out of it—or used to. Every charm, every ember, every luck-knot sold for a copper in the market quarter—it's all downstream of this." He laid his palm against the trunk, gently, the way you'd touch a fevered child. "Six centuries ago, something came up out of the deep places and started drinking. The witches of that age couldn't kill it. So they bound it instead—in gold and iron and a dead man's crown—and they planted a garden over the lock. Living wood, growing roots around the chains. Every gardener who served the crown tended it, whether he knew what he was tending or not."

"And now?"

"Now the binding is failing. Has been for years." He looked back at me, and his eyes were terrible with old knowledge. "You asked why the Hollow Crown keeps the gardens alive while it burns every gifted soul in the city. Here's your answer. The garden isn't decoration. The garden is the cage. And the thing wearing King Aldric has been starving it on purpose—letting it die slow, leaf by leaf—because when the last root rots, the lock opens."

The hum faltered again. I felt it in my teeth.

"But that's not even the part that matters," Tam went on, and now his voice roughened. "The part that matters is what feeds the seal. It was never iron. Iron's just the frame. The seal drinks the gift—freely given. There's a hollow in the roots, a place shaped like an open hand. Six hundred years ago, the first witches each gave a measure of themselves to start it, and it's been living on the interest ever since. But interest runs out." He drew a breath. "The Crown doesn't just burn witches for sport, Freya, whatever the criers say. It burns them *here*. Feeds them to the fire and lets the ash fall on these roots, because stolen power poisons the wellspring and kills the tree faster. Every cleansing brings the end closer. It is farming us."

The word sat in the cold air between us.

I thought of the child in the market, her charm flaring gold. I thought of Maren's apprentice mark left on the ruined floorboards like a note pinned to a door. I thought of the king on his scaffold-plain, casting a shadow that moved against the sun, and how I'd understood in one vertiginous moment that I wasn't looking at cruelty. Cruelty wants you to suffer. Hunger just wants you *in*.

"You fled," I said. "Six years ago."

"I fled." He didn't dress it up. "I was the keeper of the inner beds. I saw the queen give her gift to the seal every season—she was bound to it, her mother before her. And one night the king came down to the garden, except it wasn't the king, and I watched it take her gift out of her like pulling a thread from a hem. She looked at me while it happened. She didn't scream. That was the worst part. It drank her *quietly*, the way you drink water, and when it was done she smiled at me with a mouth that wasn't hers, and I ran, and I have been running ever since, growing other people's flowers under other people's names." He flexed his dirt-dark fingers. "Keeping my head down. Sound familiar?"

It did. It sounded like my whole life.

"So why stop now?" I asked. "Why grab me? You could have let them take me through that gate and gone back to your pots."

He was quiet a long moment. Above us the heart-tree shivered, shedding a single coal-colored leaf that fell like something falling slowly out of love.

"Because I watched the queen die polite and quiet," he said finally, "and I told myself survival was the same as virtue so long as I kept the beds weeded. And then three days ago they burned a witch on the market plain, and she was nineteen, and the crowd cheered, and I realized the tree is dying and I am the last person alive who knows how it's fed." He turned to face me fully. "I'm done surviving, Freya. It's a poor trade. I want to spend what's left."

"The tree needs a freely given gift to live," I said slowly, working it through. "But if the Crown catches anyone gifted, it steals their gift and poisons the roots anyway. So the seal dies either way. Unless—"

"Unless someone gives freely before they're taken," Tam finished. "Which is a martyr's arithmetic, and I won't ask it of you. I pulled you off the street to hide you, not to spend you. But you needed to see it. You needed to know what the game actually is, because I don't think you did. I think you thought you were hiding from the fire." His eyes were very steady. "You weren't hiding from the fire. You were ripening."

The hum beneath the earth stuttered, caught, held.

And that was when we heard it: bells, from the direction of the palace, ringing fast and ragged—an alarm pattern I'd never heard but understood in my bones. Then hooves on the old service road, many of them, and Tam's head snapped up, and all the blood left his face.

"They're not hunting you anymore," he said. "That's a full mustering. They're coming for the garden."

Behind us, in the hollow, the dying heart-tree dropped another leaf—and this time, I swear on Maren's life, it fell upward.
