## 5. Roots and Ashes

I have kept a garden alive for eleven years by pretending it was already dead.

That's the trick of it, if anyone ever asks. Wardens don't burn dead gardens. They don't question a man who walks the rows at dawn muttering about blight and rot, who lets the outer beds go to weed so the whole place reads as abandoned. Grief is excellent camouflage. I wore mine like a uniform for a decade, and no one ever once looked past it.

Then I hauled a witch over my wall by the collar of their coat, and the camouflage started cracking like spring ice.

* * *

The stranger—Freya, they'd said, in a voice that tried to be steady and mostly managed it—slept in the potting shed until well past noon. I let them. You don't wake someone who's been hunted through the palace outskirts all night; you let them sleep, and you make tea, and you think about how badly your life has just gone sideways.

The tea was chamomile from the third bed, which is to say: the one bed that still grew anything worth picking.

When Freya finally emerged, blinking, they looked younger than I'd guessed. Late twenties, maybe. Iron-thread gloves, fingerless and worn soft at the palms—the kind you buy when you want to muffle a gift without strangling it entirely. I knew those gloves. My wife had owned a pair.

"You're the gardener," Freya said, looking around at the dying beds with an expression I recognized. Pity, poorly hidden.

"I'm the gardener," I agreed. "And before you say anything kind and useless—yes, it's dying. Not from neglect." I held up a hand before they could apologize. "Come see. Then decide what you're sorry about."

* * *

The heart-tree stood at the center of everything, behind a yew hedge I'd let grow tall enough to hide it from the palace windows. It had been an emberwood once—I say *had been*, because calling what remained an emberwood was like calling ash a fire. The trunk still held its shape, silver-grey and smooth as bone, but there was no warmth in it. No glow. When I pressed my palm against the bark each morning, the way I'd done every morning since I took this post, it gave back nothing. Cold as flagstone. Colder, some days.

Freya stopped three paces away and went very still.

"It's a seal," they said. Not a question.

"Planted the year the king was crowned. Before, actually—my grandmother helped set the rootstock, and her mother before her. There's a wellspring under this ground. Old magic, older than Vell, older than the crown on Aldric's head. When the first binding was done, they planted a tree over it to hold the knot tight." I crouched and pulled back a handful of mulch, showing them the soil beneath: grey, dry, dead as the tree above it. "A living lock needs a living key. As long as the heart-tree thrived, the thing below stayed bound."

"And now?"

"Now it's starving." I stood, brushing off my knees. "You felt it last night, didn't you? In the streets. That pull, like something drinking through a straw."

Freya's jaw tightened. "Every gifted person in this city has felt it for months. We thought it was the purges. Fear does strange things to the gift."

"The purges are the symptom, not the sickness." I walked to the far side of the trunk, where the bark had split in a long vertical seam, and I made myself look at it while I said the next part. I'd earned the right to look away from it years ago; I'd also learned that looking away is how things like this win. "The Crown isn't a symbol, Freya. It never was. It's a parasite—a spirit old enough that the founders bound it rather than kill it, because killing it would have poisoned the wellspring for a hundred miles. And bindings don't last forever. Ours lasted four hundred years. It started failing maybe two decades back. Slowly. A little seep here, a little hunger there."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I was there," I said, "when it finished failing."

* * *

Here is what people forget about court gardeners: we are invisible. Kings look through us. Queens nod at us the way you nod at furniture. Which means we see everything.

I was thirty-one the night Queen Elira came down to the garden alone, without her ladies, without her guards, wearing a plain grey cloak like a woman going to meet a lover. She came to the heart-tree, and she knelt in the dead soil, and she put both hands flat against the trunk, and she wept.

I should have left. Any sensible man would have left. But she was crying the way you cry when there's no one left to perform strength for, and I had loved that woman since the day she was born—had carried her on my shoulders through these very rows when she was small enough to fit between the beanpoles. So I stepped out of the hedge shadow and asked her what was wrong.

She told me the truth, because she thought I already knew half of it.

She told me the king had died in his sleep eight months before. Quietly, in his bed, with his hand in hers. She told me that the body that woke the next morning had his face and his voice and none of him inside it—that she'd known within a week, from the way it watched her eat, the way it never slept, the way the shadows in its chambers moved wrong, leaning toward it like plants toward light. She told me the physicians were replaced, then the priests, then the captains who noticed too much. She told me the thing wearing her husband had begun to feed—not on bread, not on blood, but on gift. On magic. That it could reach into a person and drink them hollow, and that it had started with the court witches, quietly, one by one, and called their emptying a wasting illness.

And then she told me why she'd come to the tree.

"The binding is loose," she said. "It's been leaking for years, and now it's wide enough to walk through. If I pour my own gift into the rootstock—if I feed the seal—it might close again. Buy another century. Maybe less."

"Might," I said.

"Might," she agreed, and smiled at me with her father's smile, and knelt down, and began.

I will not describe what happened next in detail. Some nights I can almost not-describe it successfully. But the shape of it was this: she opened herself to the tree, and the tree was too weak to hold what she offered, and the thing below the wellspring took it instead. All of it. Her gift, her warmth, her color—drunk through the roots like rain through sand. She didn't scream. That's the part I can't stop hearing, the absence of it. She just went quiet in the middle, like a candle pinched out, and slumped against the trunk, and when I got to her she was breathing but nobody was home. The body lived nine days. The woman was gone in a minute.

And the thing in the crown grew stronger that night. Everyone felt it. The candles burned brighter in the palace; the wards hummed; the king-thing smiled at breakfast for the first time in months.

I ran the next evening. Took nothing but my tools and my guilt, slipped out through the gardener's gate, and spent eleven years telling myself I'd done all I could. Telling myself a gardener couldn't fight a spirit. Telling myself survival was its own kind of loyalty.

Telling myself stories, in other words. Gardeners are good at growing things. Lies grow fastest of all.

* * *

Freya hadn't spoken through any of it. Now they stood with one gloved hand hovering near the split bark, not quite touching, the way you hover near a wound.

"She tried to save everyone," Freya said finally. "Alone. Because she thought she was the only one who knew."

"She was the only one who knew and had access to the tree. Different problem, same grave."

"No." Freya turned, and there was something in their face I hadn't seen yet—something hot under all that careful control, banked coals finally getting air. "No, listen. Maren taught me everything I know about hiding. About dampening charms, iron thread, keeping your head down. She's been doing it forty years. She's *good*. Better than me. If the wardens took her in an afternoon, if they ransacked her cottage and left her mark behind like a calling card—" They stopped. Started again, quieter, worse. "They didn't catch her. They were sent for her. Specifically. And she's still alive, Tam. Witches who get taken in the cleansings die publicly, within days. Maren's been gone a week and there's been no burning. No announcement. Nothing."

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the dying tree.

"Bait," I said.

"Or fuel." Freya's voice cracked on the word and they pushed through it. "The Crown feeds on gift. Maren's strong—strong enough to keep it fed for weeks without killing her. Strong enough to draw in anyone who might come looking. And who comes looking?" They laughed, short and bitter. "An ember-bearer. That's what she called my line, once. Rare lineage, she said, rare enough that I should never, ever let anyone know. The Crown doesn't want a market witch or a hedge-healer. It wants what the queen had. What I have."

We stood there in the shadow of the dead tree, two people who had built entire lives out of not being noticed, and the truth sat between us like a stone neither of us wanted to pick up: hiding hadn't saved the queen. Hiding hadn't saved Maren. Hiding had only arranged the menu.

"They're saving you for last," I said. "Or saving you for the main course."

"Either way, waiting is cooperating." Freya pulled their glove off—one hand, bare to the air—and I saw it then, faint as banked embers under skin: the glow. The real thing, unhidden, trembling slightly. "So I'm done waiting. Tell me how to get into the registry."

"That's a fortress."

"So was this garden, once. Look at it now." They met my eyes. "I'm asking for your help, Tam. Not your permission."

And here's the thing I've had eleven years to learn about guilt: it keeps, but it spoils. Mine had gone rotten in the dark, feeding nothing. But grief—grief transmuted, grief pointed at something—grief is compost. It makes things grow.

I reached into my coat and brought out the ring I'd taken the night I fled: the gardener's signet, the one key that still opened every service door in the palace, because no one had ever thought to change the locks for a dead man's servant. I'd worn it under my shirt for a decade like a stone around my neck.

"Service tunnels run under the east wall," I said, and heard my own voice come out steadier than it had any right to. "There's one that surfaces inside the registry block. I swore an oath to that family when the queen was in swaddling clothes." I closed my fist around the ring. "Time I stopped breaking it by running."

Freya smiled—tired, fierce, the first true thing I'd seen on them.

"Then let's go steal a witch back."
