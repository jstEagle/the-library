## 6. The Registry

The writ took me three nights and one small act of theft, which I choose to think of as poetic justice.

Captain Ilsa Voss had a seal. Of course she did—wardens all carried them, a stamp of black iron shaped like a closed fist, pressed into grey wax that smelled faintly of ash. What she didn't know was that her second-in-command, a soft-bellied man named Dree with a fondness for dice and drink, kept his in the outer pocket of his coat while he lost money at the Gilded Hen. And what *he* didn't know was that pressing a stolen warden's seal into wax softened by candle heat leaves an impression deep enough to cast, if you happen to have iron-thread gloves, a steady hand, and five years of practice making things look like what they are not.

I'd made luck-knots for coin. A forged writ was only a larger knot, tied around a larger lie: *By order of Captain Voss, the bearer is authorized to inspect the Registry holdings on matters internal to the Crown.*

Matters internal to the Crown. I nearly laughed when I wrote it. Nothing was more internal than what lived inside that crown, though I hadn't yet found the words for it—not even in my own head, where I kept my thoughts locked up tighter than anything else.

The Registry stood at the end of Warden's Row, a building that had once been a customs house back when Vell traded in silk instead of fear. They hadn't even bothered to repaint it. The old carved sign still read *ENTREPÔT DU ROI* above the door, half the letters rotted away, so it greeted every witch dragged through its doors with the king's own name, worn down to almost nothing. There was a joke in there somewhere. I couldn't find it. My hands wouldn't stop shaking long enough to look.

*You can walk away,* said the voice in me that had kept me alive for five years. The sensible voice. The coward's voice, if I'm honest, though I'd spent five years calling it prudence because prudence sounded better at night. *You have a forged writ and a plan and no idea what you're walking into. Maren would tell you to run.*

Maren would also have told me, once, that a teacher's first duty is to outlive her students' mistakes. But Maren had left her apprentice mark behind in a ransacked cottage, laid deliberately on the table where anyone could see it—a message, and messages from Maren were never suggestions. *Come find me* was written in that mark as clearly as if she'd inked it. Or else *don't be foolish.* With Maren it had always been hard to tell the difference.

I went through the doors.

* * *

The Registry inside was worse than I'd imagined, and I had imagined it generously, which is to say I'd imagined cruelty with some craft to it. There was none. Just efficiency. Rows of desks where clerks recorded names, origins, the nature of each gift, the date of "processing." A corridor of cells behind iron grating, each cell marked not with a number but with a small painted sigil indicating the kind of magic its occupant had been caught carrying. Weather-workers. Hedge-healers. A whole cell block, three doors deep, marked with the coiled symbol of dream-speakers, and I let myself hope it was empty until I heard someone humming inside, very softly, a lullaby in a dialect from the southern fens.

The clerk at the front desk looked up at my writ, then at me, then at the writ again. He was maybe nineteen, with ink-stained fingers and the particular exhaustion of a boy who had learned too quickly what his salary purchased.

"Voss's office," he said. Not a question.

"Internal inspection," I said, and pitched my voice into the flat register I used for customers who haggled too hard. Bored. Slightly annoyed. A person whose time was being wasted and who wanted everyone to know it. "There's been a discrepancy in the intake records."

"Discrepancy," he repeated, and something flickered across his face—too quick to name, gone before I could catch it. He stamped the writ without another word and waved me toward the corridor. "Third block. If you're looking for the ember lineage, they keep those separate."

The floor did not drop out from under me, though it considered it.

"The what?" I said, carefully, in the tone of a woman who has never heard the term.

He shrugged, already looking past me at the next visitor in line. "Ember-bearers. Old blood. Intake records flag them special. Third block, end of the hall."

* * *

Here is what I knew about my own gift, before that hallway: that it glowed. That it came from my mother, who came from hers, back through a line of women and men and people like me who had carried fire under their skin in a country that kept deciding fire was a crime. That I had spent five years binding it beneath iron thread and silence and charm-work so careful it had become its own kind of prison. That I had told myself, every single day, that I was invisible. Unremarkable. One more face in the market quarter, selling luck to people who needed it.

Here is what I learned in that hallway: the Registry had a file on me.

I didn't see it. I want to be clear about that—I didn't see the file itself, didn't read my own name in a clerk's careful hand. What I saw was the third block's ledger, left open on a warder's desk at the end of the hall, because wardens get careless in buildings where the prisoners can't leave. And there, in a column headed *HELD FOR ASSESSMENT*, was a list of names, and beside them, in different ink, newer, a single word repeated:

*Reserved.*

And below the list, in the same newer hand, a note: *Ember line confirmed. Subject R. located. Do not process. His Majesty requires the vessel intact.*

Subject R.

Five years of hiding, and I had been a subject. A *vessel*. Every charm I'd woven, every flare I'd smothered, every morning I'd wound the iron thread around my fingers until they ached—and the whole time, somewhere in a clerk's neat ledger, someone had written *located* and underlined it twice.

I stood in that hallway longer than was safe. Long enough that a warder glanced at me twice. I made myself move, made myself put one foot in front of the other, because the alternative was to stand there forever while the truth rearranged everything I thought I knew about my own life.

They hadn't missed me. They had been *saving* me.

* * *

Maren was in the last cell, and they had not been gentle, and I will not describe what they had been, except to say that when I saw her I understood why the clerks wrote *reserved* rather than *processed.* You don't burn a candle you mean to use later.

She was sitting upright against the wall anyway. Back straight, hands folded, gray hair pinned as neatly as if she were receiving students in her parlor instead of rotting in a cell. When she saw me her eyes went wide, then narrow, then—worst of all—fond.

"Oh, you *foolish* child," she said, and her voice was hoarse but level. "That's Voss's seal. Where did you get Voss's seal?"

"I copied it," I whispered through the grate. "From Dree. He loses at dice."

"Dree always did." She laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and I gripped the grate until my knuckles went white. "Freya. Listen to me. Why are you here?"

"You left the mark. I thought—" My voice cracked. Five years of composure, and it cracked on the first sentence. "I thought you were calling me."

"I was telling you to *run*, you impossible girl." She reached through the grate, and I took her hands, and they were cold and lighter than I remembered, like holding a bundle of dry sticks. "But since you're here, and since we're both fools now, listen. We don't have long. The warders change shift at the bell, and the thing that wears the king comes to this building every third night. It comes to *look* at us. To feed a little. Tonight is a third night."

My stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, into whatever well lay beneath this city. "Then I get you out now."

"No." Her grip tightened, surprisingly strong. "No, Freya, listen. It doesn't want me. I'm bait. I've known since the second week—it keeps me alive the way a fisherman keeps a lure bright. It wants *you*. Ember-blood. Your mother's line, and hers before her. Do you understand? The Hollow Crown was bound once, long ago, in a place beneath the palace gardens, and the binding is failing, and it needs an ember to make itself permanent. Not any ember. Yours. It has your name in a ledger, child. It has had your name for years."

The world went very quiet. Somewhere far off, a bell began to toll—the changing of the shift, or perhaps just the hour; I couldn't have said.

"Why mine?" I asked. "Why our line?"

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen, the smile of a woman who has carried a secret for twenty years and finally gets to set it down. "Because your grandmother helped bind it the first time. Because embers don't just burn, Freya—they remember. And because the Crown has been patient, and hungry, and it does not intend to consume you." Her cold hands squeezed mine. "It intends to *wear* you. The way it wears the king."

Above us, faintly, the bells finished tolling.

And beneath them—so soft I might have imagined it, except that Maren heard it too, and her face went white as paper—came the sound of the great doors at the end of the hall opening, unhurried, and footsteps that made no echo at all coming down the corridor toward us.

"It's early," Maren breathed. "Freya. *Hide.*"

But there was nowhere to hide. There never had been.
