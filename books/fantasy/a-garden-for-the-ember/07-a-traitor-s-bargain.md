## 7. A Traitor's Bargain

My sister burned on a Tuesday.

I have thought about that a great deal since — the ordinariness of it. Not a feast day, not an omen-laden solstice. A Tuesday, with the fish carts still in the square and a child crying two rows back because he'd dropped his candied apple. The kindling was damp and took too long. I stood at my post at the edge of the cleansing ground in full warden black, and I did not look away, because looking away would have been the one mercy I couldn't afford anyone to see me give.

They told us afterward that Sera had failed to declare her gift. That she had chosen concealment over honesty, and that the Crown's justice was only fairness wearing armor. They used words like *hygiene*. As if my sister were a spoiled cask of wine rather than a woman who could make bread rise by humming to it.

I signed the report confirming her execution. My own name, in my own hand, in the tidy script they teach you at the warden academy along with how to hold a pike so your arm doesn't shake.

That is what duty looks like, children. Write it down.

* * *

The ember-bearer had been in my cells for three days when I finally went down to look at them.

I say *finally* as though I hadn't been circling the decision like a gull over a fish market. I'd read the intake report twice. Freya Reyes. Luck-knots and fever-charms, market quarter, minor infractions, nothing that should have put them on any list at all — except that someone upstairs wanted them on a list, and when the palace wants a name on a list, wardens find reasons. We're very good at finding reasons. It's most of the job.

The cells under the registry are not cruel by design, which is somehow worse than cruelty. They are clean, gray, and evenly lit, so that nothing in them casts a shadow worth hiding in, and time stops meaning anything. Freya sat against the far wall with their iron-thread gloves still on, though we'd taken everything else, and watched me come down the stairs with the expression of someone doing arithmetic about doors.

"There's water," I said, because protocol demanded I open with something.

"I noticed." Their voice was flat. Tired. "It tastes like the inside of a boot."

"It's the pipes."

"It's ambition, is what it is. Someone worked hard on that flavor."

Despite myself — and despite everything, which I will get to — something in my chest unclenched half a degree. You can learn a lot about a prisoner from whether they joke. The ones who go quiet are already broken. The ones who joke are still deciding whether to be afraid of you, which means they haven't decided yet, which means there was still a conversation to be had.

"Freya Reyes," I said. "Apprenticed six years to Maren Corvane. Charged with concealment of gift, obstruction of registry, and—" I glanced at the page, savoring the absurdity, "—unlicensed charm-vending."

"The luck-knots were licensed," they said. "Mostly. There was a paperwork issue with the knot part."

"Captain Voss." Maren's voice came from the next cell, thin but unmistakably amused. Through the grate between them I could see the old witch folded onto her cot like a heron standing in shallow water. "Don't let her bait you, girl. She's here for another reason. Nobody reads intake reports aloud to prisoners for fun."

I looked at her a long moment. Maren Corvane, who had taught half the hidden children of Vell their letters and their wards both. Who was supposed to be fuel by now and instead sat in her cell radiating patience like a banked stove.

"You know why I'm here," I said.

"I suspect," she said, "that you want to talk about the king."

* * *

Here is what nobody says out loud in the barracks, though every warden above the rank of sergeant has noticed: the king does not breathe.

You see him at ceremonies, at cleansings, that tall gray ruin of a man in the crown that never leaves his head, and he speaks, he gestures, his face makes the shapes of a face. But in winter, when the rest of us stand fogging the air like horses, his mouth opens and nothing comes out. His shadow falls wrong. I've watched it lean east at dusk when every other shadow in the square stretched west, patient and patient, like something waiting to be told which way the light goes.

I reported it once. Early. Back when I still believed the chain of command led somewhere other than into its own mouth. My commanding officer thanked me, and three days later my file gained a commendation I hadn't earned, and the following spring Sera was on a list.

I understand now that the list was a message. I understand that everything in this city is a message if you bleed on it right.

So when I unlocked those cell doors at the dead hour before dawn — when I put the forged writ in Freya's hands and the keys in Maren's — understand that I was not being brave. Bravery is what other people call it afterward, if it works. What it actually was, in that stairwell, was five years of Tuesdays reaching critical mass.

"Why?" Freya asked me. Fair question, all things considered. I'd helped put them in that cell.

"My sister burned in the first cleansing," I said. "Sera Voss. Bread-witch. Terrible liar, excellent baker." I kept my voice level because if I didn't it was going to do something else entirely. "The thing wearing the king killed her for a scrap of gift she could have lit a candle with. And everyone who carried out the order got a citation." I looked at Freya until they looked back. "I don't want a citation. I want it ended. Your mentor tells me you know how."

"I know a garden," Freya said slowly. "And a wellspring. I don't know if—"

"That's more than I knew yesterday. Take it."

Maren rose from her cot with the care of an old woman and the eyes of a much younger one. "Captain," she said, "the servants' tunnel past the cistern — is it still watched?"

"By rotation. Third watch changed an hour ago; you have perhaps forty minutes before anyone misses these keys, and less before Dren notices."

"And Dren is?"

"The reason this plan has a fifty-percent chance instead of ninety," I said grimly. "Dren Hale. Sergeant. Good soldier, bad man, and lately — richer than a sergeant ought to be. He reports to someone in the palace beyond me. Possibly beyond all of us. Stay away from Dren."

Famous last words. I've had occasion to think of them often since.

* * *

The cistern tunnel smelled of wet stone and older fears. I went first with a hand lantern hooded to a coin of light; Maren followed, moving better than she pretended to; Freya came last, and I will note for the record that the ember-bearer complained about the smell in whispers the entire way, which takes a particular kind of courage.

At the third junction the tunnel forked — left toward the river gate, right up toward the old gardener's stair. That was the route: up through ground Tam Ashworth knew better than anyone living, into the garden, down to whatever slept beneath it. Simple. Everything is simple at four in the morning.

Then the lantern-light ahead doubled, and tripled, and Dren Hale stepped around the corner with six wardens at his back and a smile like a key turning.

"Captain," he said pleasantly. "Lost?"

Every hair on my arms stood up. Not at him. At the arithmetic. He was early. He was exactly where a man would be only if he'd known where to be, and there were exactly three people who'd known where to be tonight, and I was looking at the other two.

"Dren," I said. "This is a registry transfer. Papers are in order."

"Mm." He strolled forward, boots ringing on the wet stone. "See, that's funny, Captain, because I checked the transfer log before I came down. There isn't one." He stopped just inside lantern range and looked at Freya the way a man looks at produce. "There's no log at all. Just a message that came up from the palace, saying to be here, now, and to bring friends." His smile widened. "Someone upstairs wants the ember-bearer very badly indeed. Wants the old woman too, as it happens. Doesn't much mention you."

I felt it then — the exact moment the floor gives way beneath a life. My writ was forged. My seals were stolen. My word, against a sergeant with direct lines to the Crown, was worth the air it took to speak it. I had handed them everything and built our escape on the assumption that the rot stopped below my rank.

It had never once, in five years, stopped below my rank.

"Run," I said, and drew my pike, and turned to face six men in the uniform I had worn with pride for fifteen years.

To their credit — to this day, to their eternal credit — neither of them ran. Maren's hands came up trailing pale threads of working; Freya tore off their gloves, and the dark tunnel bloomed ember-orange, and I remembered too late that the Crown *wanted* that light, wanted it like a drunk wants the bottle, and that Dren's orders surely said *alive*, and mine said nothing anymore at all.

The fight was short and ugly. I broke a man's wrist and Dren's nose before something heavy found the back of my skull, and the stone floor came up generous and fast.

The last thing I saw before the dark took me: Freya dragged backward down the left-hand passage, glowing like a forge, screaming Maren's name, while Maren fought threads of binding light alone in the dark.

The last thing I heard was Dren Hale, wheezing through his ruined nose, laughing.

* * *

I woke to cold water and a lamp swinging, and Corvane's heron-face above me, bloodied at the temple but free, which meant she'd won her fight or been left for leverage, and knowing the Crown, leverage.

"They have the girl," she said. No preamble. Witches don't waste breath on the obvious.

"Yes."

"They're taking her to the palace."

"Yes."

I sat up. My head objected loudly and I overruled it. Somewhere in the last quarter hour — I couldn't tell you when, couldn't name the moment any more than you can name the moment a rope frays through — the arithmetic had finished. Duty, chain of command, the tidy script they teach you at the academy: all of it lay at the bottom of the cistern with the rest of the drowned things. What was left was simpler. My sister's hands in flour. The king who does not breathe. A frightened kid in fingerless gloves who joked about the taste of the water, going up to the palace in chains because I'd opened the wrong door for the wrong man.

"Corvane," I said. "The gardener. Ashworth. Does the ember-bearer know him? Is there a place in the palace grounds — some way in from outside?"

Maren studied me, and whatever she saw in my face seemed to satisfy her. "There's a gate," she said carefully. "An old one. Why?"

"Because," I said, hauling myself upright against the wall, "they'll take Freya in through the service postern before noon, under guard of four, and Dren will want to boast about it to somebody, which means the schedule will leak if I squeeze the right throat." I picked my pike up off the wet stone. Fifteen years of habit; it weighed nothing. "And because I am done being the hand that holds the torch, old woman. Find your gardener. I'll get them the gate."

She caught my sleeve. For a small motion it stopped me entirely.

"You understand," Maren said quietly, "that there's no coming back from this. Whatever happens at that garden — win or lose — the woman who signs tonight's report does not exist tomorrow."

I looked at her, and I thought of Sera, and I laughed — actually laughed, first time since the Tuesday, a short ugly sound that echoed beautifully off the stone.

"Madam," I said, "she barely exists now. Let's go commit treason."

We went up the stair together, the witch and the turncoat, into the gray beginning of the day, toward the palace where a dead king waited with my prisoner and my name.

Behind us, far below, the cistern water settled back into stillness, patient as a shadow leaning east.
