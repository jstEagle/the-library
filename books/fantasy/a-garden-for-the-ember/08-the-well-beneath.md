## 8. The Well Beneath

They say drowning is peaceful at the end. I can't speak to that. What I can tell you is that being dragged through the palace of Vell by two wardens with hands like iron bands and a third walking ahead with a lantern he didn't need — that is not peaceful at any stage.

The corridors went down. That was the first thing I noticed, because everything else hurt too much to notice anything. Down past the state rooms with their gilded ceilings, down past the wine cellars where the king's ancestors slept in casks older than the city walls, down a stair worn hollow in the middle by feet that had been climbing and descending for centuries before anyone thought to put a crown on anyone.

"You're bleeding on the king's floor," said the warden on my left. He sounded almost apologetic.

"Send me the bill," I managed.

He actually laughed. Wardens, it turns out, are people. It would have been comforting if his grip hadn't tightened right after, the way people's hands do when they remember what they're supposed to be doing.

The lantern-light gave out before we reached the bottom. Not dimmed — *gave out*, like the dark down there was simply more authoritative than fire. And in that dark, I felt it.

I'd been feeling it for days, honestly, without knowing what it was: a pressure behind my sternum, a pull like low tide in my chest, ever since Maren was taken. Every witch in Vell had felt it, I suspected, and told themselves it was grief. It wasn't grief. Grief doesn't *lean*.

The ember under my skin leaned toward that darkness the way a candle leans toward an open window.

*There you are,* something said, without sound, in the register of a voice you use on yourself at three in the morning. *We've been waiting so long.*

"Freya Reyes." Captain Voss stood at the foot of the stairs, or a version of her did — her face was a gray smudge against the black. Her voice was flat as slate. "By order of the Crown, you are presented for judgment."

"Judgment," I repeated. "That's a new word for it. In the market quarter we just say 'burning.'"

Nobody laughed this time. The wardens shoved me forward, and the dark opened like a throat.

* * *

The chamber beneath the palace was not built. I understood that immediately, in the way you understand a thing with your teeth rather than your mind. The walls were bedrock, but they were *worn*, grooved and scalloped, as if water had shaped them over ten thousand years — except no water ran down here. The air was dry as old paper. At the center of the chamber stood a ring of standing stones, each taller than a man, each carved with script I half-recognized from Maren's oldest lessons and half-recognized from nowhere at all, from somewhere deeper than lessons.

And above the ring, suspended where a chandelier should have been, hung the Hollow Crown.

It looked like a crown. That was the obscenity of it. Gold worked into oak leaves and antler tines, delicate as frost, turning slowly in the air with nothing holding it up. If you squinted, if you wanted very badly to see a king's regalia, you could see a king's regalia.

If you didn't squint, you saw the space inside it, and the space inside it was *hungry*. It wasn't empty the way a cup is empty. It was empty the way a wound is empty.

And below it, kneeling in the ring of stones, was Maren.

She was thinner than she'd been five days ago, which shouldn't have been possible; Maren had always been built like a doorframe, all angles and stubbornness. Her hair had gone from iron-gray to white. But her eyes found mine across that terrible chamber, and they were exactly the same eyes that had looked at me over a workbench twelve years ago and said, "You knot your charms like you're apologizing to them. Stop apologizing."

"Don't," she said. Just that. One word, cracked down the middle. *Don't come closer. Don't listen. Don't.*

The wardens set me on my feet at the edge of the stone ring and stepped back, and I noticed then that they were afraid. Good, some small vicious part of me said. Then the Crown turned its absence toward me, and the vicious part shut up along with everything else.

*Freya,* it said. My name, but pronounced correctly — better than I pronounced it, with all the sounds I'd spent my whole life rounding off. *Ember-bearer. Last of the line of the wellspring's own. Do you know how long we have waited for you?*

"Five days?" I said. "Maren only got taken five days ago."

*Maren.* The name moved through the chamber like a cold draft. *A candle. A taper lit to draw the moth. Forgive us the crudeness — we are old, and old things grow impatient. Five hundred years, little ember. Five hundred years since they bound us here, since they buried our wellspring under dirt and roots and called it a garden. Since they taught your kind to be ashamed of what burns in them.*

"It wasn't hard," I said. "You'd be amazed what shame people can teach."

*I know.* And here the thing did something dreadful: it softened. The hunger in the air didn't vanish, but it folded itself around me like a blanket, like the specific warmth of being known. *We watched you in the market. We watched you hide your light behind iron thread and sell luck to people who never knew your name. We watched you flinch when a child's charm flared. Do you know what we felt, watching?*

"Disapproval?"

*Sorrow.* The word landed in my chest like a dropped stone. *You have spent your whole life making yourself smaller than a candle flame. You have knotted your gift into trinkets and sold it for copper because copper is safe. You learned to walk so quietly that even you lost track of where you were going. We could give you back every step of that road. Belonging, Freya. Purpose. A place where the light you carry is not a crime but a crown. No more gloves. No more knots. No more being half a person in a city full of strangers.*

And here is the thing I am not proud of, the thing I will carry longer than the scars from tonight: it nearly worked.

Not because I believed it loved me. Because I was *tired*. Five years of vigilance, five years of counting exits in every room, five years of watching Tam's hands shake and Maren's door stay locked and Ilsa Voss's face learn to show nothing — five years of being a rumor in my own life. The Crown didn't offer me power. Power I could have refused. It offered me *rest*. It offered to hold my identity the way I'd never managed to hold it myself, to tell me who I was so I could finally put the question down.

For one long moment I stood in that hungry dark and felt my own edges soften. I felt the ember rise in me, eager, unclenching after years of being held tight, ready to pour itself into whatever shape was offered—

"Don't you dare apologize to it," Maren rasped.

The sound snapped like a whipcrack across the chamber. My edges hardened. I remembered a workbench, and a woman telling me to stop apologizing to my own magic, and I understood — suddenly, completely — that the Crown had just done the same thing Maren had scolded me for. It had apologized. It had made itself small and gentle and palatable, because that is what predators do when they want to be let close.

"You're lying," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Not about the watching. About the rest. You don't give rest. You take. I've seen what's left of the witches you've burned — Voss showed me the registry ledgers. They're not at peace. They're *digestible*. That's what belonging means to you, isn't it? Everything inside you. Nothing left outside."

The softness fell away. All of it, at once, like a mask dropping — and what stood in the air above the stones was not a crown and not a king and not anything that had ever loved anything. The temperature dropped. The carved stones began to sing, a low note I felt in my molars.

*Then we will take the ember unshared,* it said, *and your ashes will keep their own counsel.*

Chains of dark rose from the bedrock. I felt my knees buckle. Somewhere far away, Maren screamed my name, and I thought, absurdly, of the fever-charms I used to sell for three coppers, and how none of them worked on this.

* * *

But Maren had not spent twelve years teaching apprentices to stop apologizing for nothing.

She broke the circle from the inside.

I saw it happen and I still barely believe it: a woman with nothing left, drained white as bone, who should have had maybe a spark of strength remaining — she took that last spark, the one keeping her heart beating, and she drove it into the binding-script at her knees. The stones' song shattered into discord. The chains of dark stuttered, wavered, lost their grip on me by a finger's width — enough.

"Maren, *no*—"

"The circle needs a focus," she gasped, blood at the corner of her mouth, grinning like this was the best day of her life. "It's been using *me*. Take away the focus, and—" She coughed. "Take away the focus, boy. Girl. Whatever you've decided to be today. Just *run*."

"I'm not running."

"Then you're even stupider than I was at your age," she said fondly, and collapsed.

The Crown shrieked — there was no other word for it — a sound like a hinge the size of the sky. The chamber lurched. And above us, impossibly, through forty feet of bedrock and palace foundations, came another sound: birdsong.

Dawn birds. In a garden that was supposed to be dead.

The wall of the chamber split along a seam no one had seen in five hundred years, and light poured down the broken stair like water finding its old channel — green-gold, living light, the smell of turned earth and cut stems. Standing in it, silhouetted, dirt to the elbows and a pruning knife in his fist, was Tam.

"You planted a garden over this thing," he called down, breathless, wild-eyed, alive in a way I'd never seen him. "Your ancestors. They knew what they were doing — the roots *hold* it, Freya, they've always held it, it just needed someone to remember how. The gate opens from within. Always has. Come *up*."

The Crown's hunger roared around me, coiling, desperate now, offering everything — kingdoms, names, rest, rest, *rest* —

I looked at Maren's crumpled body. At the stair full of morning. At the space inside the crown, which was very old, and very empty, and had mistaken hunger for love for so long it no longer knew the difference.

"No," I told it. "I know who I am. I've always known. I was just waiting for permission."

I took the first step toward the garden, and the Crown came apart above me like a storm deciding where to strike.

The last thing I heard before the light took us both was its voice, and it wasn't angry anymore. It was curious. Almost — I'll deny saying this — hopeful.

*Then show me,* it said, *what freely given looks like.*

The garden gate stood open. I walked through.
