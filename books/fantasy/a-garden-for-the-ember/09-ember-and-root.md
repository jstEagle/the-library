## 9. Ember and Root

The garden was dying, and it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen.

That sounds like the sort of thing a poet says before doing something stupid, and I'll admit the two often go together. But I mean it plainly. Every bed in the royal garden had gone to gray ash-bloom, every hedge stood like a skeleton wearing last winter's coat, and yet the whole place still *hurt* with intention. Someone had loved this ground once. Someone had knelt in this dirt with bleeding hands and planted a seal out of living things—roots as wards, petals as prayers—and whatever love goes into a working like that doesn't leave just because the leaves do.

Tam walked ahead of me through the ruin, and even now, half-dead on his feet with warden blood drying on his sleeve, he couldn't stop himself from wincing at every broken branch.

"Left," he said hoarsely. "Always left at the sundial. The paths are part of it."

"The paths are part of a seal?"

"Everything is part of a seal when you're standing inside one." He glanced back at me, and there was something raw in his face—not hope exactly; hope is expensive and he'd clearly gone years without paying for any—but something adjacent. Something that wanted to be. "You understand what we're about to do?"

"I'm about to offer my magic to an eldritch parasite that eats witches for breakfast." I turned my hands over. Under the ruined iron-thread gloves, my ember sat coiled and furious, hotter than it had ever been, as if five years of hiding had been a held breath and something in me had finally decided to exhale. "So no. Not entirely. But Maren taught me that the things you don't entirely understand are usually the ones worth being terrified of."

"That's terrible teaching."

"She also told me fear is just respect paid in advance." I shrugged. "She has range."

We came around the sundial—its gnomon snapped, its face furred with ash—and the garden opened into its heart, and there it was.

The wellspring didn't look like water anymore. It looked like a wound. A circular depression maybe thirty feet across, ringed by the blackened stumps of what must once have been nine trees, and in its center a light that wasn't light: a slow, oily shimmer, like moonlight poured over tar. The Hollow Crown's first prison. The place where someone—some circle of witches who loved their king and feared what wore him—had bound a hunger so old it had forgotten its own name.

And above it, on a throne of roots that had grown up out of the wellspring like the garden itself had been forced to kneel, sat the king.

Or what wore the king.

He looked worse than he had in the square, if such a thing were possible. The body of Aldric had always been gaunt, but here, away from the crowds and the ceremony, the wrongness showed through like rot through paint. The skin hung too loose. The eyes were lamps with nothing behind them but appetite. And the crown—the actual crown, black iron worked into thorns—sat on his head and *moved*, subtly, endlessly, like something breathing.

"Freya Reyes."

Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which I suppose is what happens when your mouth is borrowed. It said my name the way you'd say the name of a wine you've been saving.

"You brought yourself," it said. "How considerate. We have spent such effort arranging your invitation, and here you are, declining the escort, choosing the long road through the garden. There is a stubbornness in your line. We remember it. Your grandmother had it too."

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "You knew her."

"We tasted her." The thing smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was the worst thing I have ever seen, and I include everything else I saw that week, and I saw a great deal. "She burned slowly. Embers do. That is why we saved your kind for last. A hearth-fire gutters in an hour. An ember can warm us for a year."

Behind me, Tam made a sound low in his throat. Ahead, at the edge of the wellspring, I could see them: the wardens who hadn't betrayed us, forming a crescent, and among them—held upright between two guards by sheer bureaucratic stubbornness—Maren.

She was gray as the garden. Whatever she'd spent breaking my binding circle back in the registry, she'd spent all of it and then some. But her eyes found mine across the dying ground, and they were clear, and they were *hers*, and she did not mouth *run*. She did not mouth anything at all. She just looked at me with the expression she used to wear when I brought her a charm that was technically functional and aesthetically criminal: *well? Show me what you've learned.*

I laughed. I want that on record. Standing in front of an ancient hunger wearing a dead king, hunted, outnumbered, my mentor dying ten yards away—I laughed, because apparently that's what my line does instead of screaming.

"You keep saying 'we,'" I called to the Crown. "But it's just you, isn't it? One very old, very lonely appetite. Everything you eat, you eat alone. Nobody shares a meal like that."

"It will not bargain," Tam murmured. "It never bargains."

"I'm not bargaining." I pulled off my gloves.

The ember rose.

Five years I'd kept it leashed behind iron thread and small charms and the careful performance of ordinariness, and letting go felt less like igniting and more like *remembering*—like I'd been walking around with a sun folded up small in my chest and had simply forgotten the folding. Light poured off my skin. The ash on the nearest hedges stirred. Somewhere behind me a warden swore in a way that suggested a religious experience.

"There," the Crown breathed, and Aldric's ruined body leaned forward on its root-throne, and every shadow in the garden leaned with it. "There it is. Come, little ember. Come and be *known*. You have hidden so long. Hidden is only another word for alone. We can give you a city that sees you. A name that means something. We can make your fire *matter*—"

"Here's the thing," I said, and started walking toward the wellspring. My voice shook. I let it shake. Hiding was over; I was done spending strength on pretending otherwise. "You're offering me belonging. And I know exactly how much that's worth, coming from you, because belonging that's *given* to you by something that eats people isn't belonging. It's dinnerware."

"Freya—" Tam's hand closed on empty air where my sleeve had been.

"You eat power that's taken," I went on, closer now, close enough to feel the wellspring's cold radiating up through my boots, close enough to see that Aldric's chest did not rise, did not fall, had probably not risen or fallen in years. "Every witch you've burned—you took them. Their gift dragged out by force. And taken things taste a certain way, don't they? Like stolen bread. Filling, and filthy. But nobody ever *gave* you anything, did they? In all your long hungry centuries, nobody ever once handed you their fire and said, *here, this is yours too, welcome to the table.*"

The Crown went very still.

Even a thing that ancient knows the shape of a trap when it hears one. But it was starving—it had starved since the garden began to die, since the seal weakened, and starving things are bad mathematicians—and beneath the caution I watched something worse wake up in those lamp-eyes. Want. Stupid, vast, bottomless want.

"You would share freely," it said. Testing the words. Tasting them before tasting me.

"Freely," I said. "No strings. Which means you can't digest it."

I stepped down into the wellspring.

* * *

Cold. Cold like a memory of cold, cold that went through the meat of me looking for the ember and recoiled, hissing, because the ember was *hot*, was blazing, was the one warm thing in a wellspring full of dark—and the Crown rose out of Aldric's body like smoke given ambition, pouring down into the wound-light around me, wrapping me in coils of not-dark, and it *took*.

Of course it took. It didn't know how to do anything else.

It seized my gift the way it had seized a thousand gifts, wrenching, drinking, hauling at the ember with the greedy practiced pull of five years of burnings and five hundred years of hunger—

—and choked.

Because the ember wasn't resisting. That was the trick of it, the beautiful, simple, infuriating trick Maren must have seen the seeds of back when I was fifteen and giving away charms for free in the market because it never occurred to me to charge. Taken power fights. Taken power is a fist around a knife. But *given* power—given power comes with the giver's hand still open, comes with *welcome* braided through it, comes rooted like Tam's garden, and a root does not fight the thing that drinks it.

A root *holds on*.

The Crown drank, and the drink became a tether. It pulled, and the pull became a circuit, and down that circuit poured not just my fire but everything the fire meant: five years of market mornings, luck-knots tied for children who couldn't pay, fever-charms pressed into shaking hands, the whole long quiet ledger of small kindnesses I'd thought were nothing, thought were *hiding*, and none of it was hiding, none of it had ever been hiding, it was all *practice*—

"You cannot—" the Crown shrieked, and its voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "This is not—power does not *give*—"

"Somebody should have told you sooner," I said. "It's really the best part."

I reached out my open hand and took hold of the tether myself.

I don't have words for what happened next that would satisfy anyone who wasn't there, so I'll use the honest ones: the binding reversed. All of it. Every burning, every stolen gift, every scrap of the Crown's hoarded centuries came pouring back along the line I'd offered, and the Crown discovered the one meal it could not digest—a gift—and discovered it was already swallowed. The not-light flared white. The shadows in the garden tore free of the ground and scattered like startled birds. And the spirit that had worn a kingdom like a coat screamed with a sound that was, underneath all the rage, unmistakably and terribly, *relief*.

It unraveled. Slowly, then all at once, thread by hungry thread, until all that remained of the Hollow Crown was a black iron circlet clattering onto the stones of the wellspring, and silence, and my own ragged breathing, and the ember in my chest still burning—undimmed. Untouched. Given away and somehow more mine than it had ever been.

Then the air above the wellspring filled with a faint silver light, and a man stood there.

Not Aldric-as-he-was—the gaunt puppet, the loose skin. This man was broad-shouldered and weathered, with soil under his fingernails and laugh lines carved deep, and I knew instantly, the way you know a face from a statue only better, that this was how Aldric had actually been. A gardener's build. A gardener's hands.

His ghost looked at Tam. Tam had fallen to his knees at the wellspring's edge, and his mouth was working, and nothing was coming out.

"Your Majesty—" Tam managed at last.

"Don't." The ghost's voice was warm and rough and real. "I've been dead a long while, Tam. Longer than you fled. Don't spend your apology on me—spend it on the garden. It was always yours as much as mine." His gaze moved to me, and he inclined his head with a formality that was almost playful. "And you. Ember-bearer. I felt every year of that binding, and every year I hoped someone would come along foolish enough to try *that*."

"Foolish seems to be hereditary," I said.

"Good." The ghost smiled, and the smile was the truest thing in that garden. "Keep the ember lit. Keep it *shared*. That's the whole secret, and it took my kingdom five hundred years to forget." He was fading even as he spoke, going soft at the edges like morning mist, and he seemed entirely unbothered by it. He looked past me, at the gray beds and the blackened heart-trees, and his voice gentled. "Plant something. Will you plant something? For both of us."

"I will," Tam said, hoarse and fierce. "On my life, Majesty."

"Better than on mine," said the dead king, laughing, "mine's rather spoken for—"

And he was gone. Not tragically, not terribly—gone the way a candle goes when the dawn arrives, glad of the work, finished. The silver light settled over the wellspring like a blessing, and then the wellspring was just water again, dark and clean and cold, reflecting a sky that was beginning, impossibly, to show the pink edge of morning.

* * *

Maren reached me before I finished climbing out.

She moved like a woman three times her age, which for Maren meant roughly her actual age finally catching up, and she grabbed my face in both hands and looked at me with an expression I'd never seen on her: unguarded. Undone.

"You absolute idiot," she whispered.

"You taught me everything I know."

"I know. That's what frightens me." She pressed her forehead against mine, and her hands were shaking, and I let her shake for both of us. "I felt it, you know. When you gave it. Everyone in Vell felt it, I'd wager. Like a door opening after five hundred years." She pulled back and studied me, and her eyes went wet and sharp all at once. "The ember didn't dim."

"No."

"And you're certain you can't do it again."

"Maren—"

"Because there are forty-one witches in the registry cells below the palace," she said, "and Captain Voss is currently holding the keys and asking, in a voice I can only describe as dangerously reasonable, whether the person who unmade the Hollow Crown might be persuaded to come downstairs and teach forty-one terrified people that their fire doesn't have to be a secret anymore."

I looked at Tam, kneeling in the ash with the blackened cutting of the heart-tree cradled in his arms like a sleeping child. I looked at the wellspring, quiet and clean. I looked east, where the sun was coming up over a city that had spent five years learning to be afraid, and which was about to wake up and discover that the oldest lie in Vell had died in the night.

"Well," I said, pulling my ruined gloves off entirely and letting the light show. "I suppose I'd better start charging for lessons."

Somewhere behind us, a garden that had been dying for five hundred years put out its first green shoot.
