## 10. A Garden for the Ember

The cutting weighed almost nothing. That was the thing that kept undoing me—a piece of a tree older than the kingdom, a splinter of the only thing that had ever held a hungry god in place, and it sat in my palm light as a promise, thin as a pencil, with two pale leaves at the top like it hadn't heard about everything it had survived.

"It's heavier when you worry at it," Tam said beside me. He was on his knees in the turned earth of the restored garden bed, sleeves rolled, forearms mapped with dirt and old scars and one new burn from the night we'd unmade a king. "You're worrying at it."

"I'm appraising."

"You're fretting with style. I'll allow it."

Three weeks since the well beneath the palace had filled with morning instead of hunger. Three weeks since a dead man's ghost had looked at me with the last of his borrowed eyes and said *thank you* like it was a whole will and testament. Three weeks since I'd stood in the ruin of the Hollow Crown's unraveling, ember still burning in my chest where I'd offered it up, and understood that it had not been taken because it had not been given to the Crown at all—it had been given to the garden, to the roots, to Maren's shaking hands, to Tam, to the city itself, and power freely shared does not go out. It goes around.

I'd spent five years learning how to be small. Nobody tells you that unlearning is louder.

"Here?" I asked, kneeling. The garden had been a corpse when Tam first brought me through the gardener's gate—the heart-tree blackened, beds gone to salt and ash, the seal above the wellspring worn thin as wet paper. Now green came back the way rumor travels: unevenly, then everywhere at once. The heart-tree's stump had put out shoots like fingers testing a bath.

"A hand's depth deeper than you think," Tam said. "Roots are honest. They go where it's dark until they find what they need."

"You've been waiting weeks to say something like that."

"I've been waiting *years*. You have no idea what it's like being the only gardener in Vell with nobody to lecture." He dug the hole himself, though, because apparently there were things even a lecturing ex-royal gardener did not delegate to reformed fugitive witches, and set the cutting in with the tenderness most people reserve for sleeping infants and good bread.

I put my bare hands into the soil. No gloves. That was its own small revolution—my iron-thread gloves hung on a hook by the door of the cottage Tam had refused to call a cottage ("It's a *garden house*, it has dignity") and I had not worn them once since. My ember sat under my skin the way a hearth sits under a kettle: warm, visible, doing its work in the open.

Light bled from my fingertips into the ground. Not the panicked flare of a market charm going wrong—just a slow, deliberate glow, the color of coals at dawn, sinking into the roots.

"There," Tam said quietly, as if I'd done something difficult. Which, fine. I had. It had taken me twenty-seven years and one apocalyptic possession-crisis to learn that showing my light wasn't the same as burning down.

The garden gate creaked. It always creaked; Tam swore he could oil it and refused to, on principle I never got out of him.

Pip arrived the way Pip arrives everywhere—sideways, at speed, already talking. She was eleven, gap-toothed, fearless in the specific way of children who have been invisible themselves and have decided to make it everyone else's problem. Her luck-knot charm, the one whose flare had started this entire catastrophe, hung at her throat where she refused to stop touching it.

"You said sunrise," she accused. "Sunrise was *ages* ago."

"Sunrise was an hour ago. Some of us slept."

"Some of us *practiced*." She held out her hand, and there—faint as breath on glass, but steady—a little coil of gold light turned over her palm. Three weeks ago she couldn't hold it for a heartbeat without it spilling everywhere. Now it turned like a coin.

"That's better than yesterday," I said, and meant it so hard my voice went rough.

"Told you." She pocketed the light like a marble and knelt next to the cutting, studying it with the ferocity of a general reviewing troops. "Is it sad? Being a baby after being huge?"

"Pip," Tam said gently.

"What? Trees have feelings. Freya says so."

"I say a lot of things," I said. But she wasn't wrong, exactly. The cutting was a child of the heart-tree, and the heart-tree had spent centuries holding shut the lid on a hungry thing, and now its child would grow over a well that held nothing but water and quiet. I put my hand on the little trunk. *Grow slow,* I didn't say, because trees don't need telling in words. *Grow free.*

Maren arrived second, which was correct; Maren had always believed in arriving precisely when she intended to and never before. She walked with a cane now, and probably would forever—the Crown had drunk deep of her before we pulled her out—but she walked upright and she walked alone and she wore her apprentice-mark pendant openly, the silver leaf catching the sun, because three weeks ago the regency council had repealed the Registration Edict in full and declared magic legal, licensed, and *taxable*, which Maren said proved the revolution was truly over.

"They're calling it the Ember Reformation," she said by way of greeting. "There's a mural going up on Cooper's Row. You've got very flattering shoulders in it."

"I don't have shoulders. I'm a smear of orange light and moral ambiguity."

"In the mural you do. Take it up with the artist." She lowered herself onto the bench Tam had built her—wider, lower, hers—and looked at the cutting a long moment. Her eyes were the same as ever: sharp, brown, capable of seeing straight through a lie or a glove. "Well," she said. "You planted it in the open ground."

"Where else?"

"Mm." She said nothing else, which from Maren was a eulogy, a benediction, and a raised glass all at once.

We'd had the fight already, her and I. Two weeks ago, over soup, when she'd asked me—casual as asking about weather—when I meant to come back to her cottage and resume my apprenticeship properly. And I'd said, before I could stop myself, that I wasn't sure I wanted to resume anything. That the garden needed tending. That the gifted kids surfacing all over Vell like green shoots after fire needed someone who remembered what hiding cost. That maybe I'd spent five years becoming excellent at survival and I wanted to spend the rest of my life getting good at something else.

She'd put down her spoon and cried, briefly and furiously, and then said, "Good. I was afraid you'd come back and be my shadow again. I have a cane now. Shadows are redundant."

So here we were. Four people and a tree and a morning.

Tam stayed on his knees, working compost around the cutting's base, and I watched him and thought—not for the first time—that staying was its own kind of courage, and harder to spot. He had served the true king, fled the true king's murder-by-inches, hidden in plain sight as a disgraced groundskeeper for six years, and then walked back into the palace through a garden gate that should have killed him, because someone had to open it from within while the Crown was busy trying to swallow me. He could leave now. The council had offered him the head groundskeeper's post for the whole royal estate, title and salary and a hat. He'd declined the title and the hat and kept the job at the garden only. When I'd asked him why, he'd said, "Because this is where the work is," and gone back to pruning, and I had understood approximately everything about him.

"Freya." Pip tugged my sleeve. "When do I get a real lesson? Not just the coin trick. A *real* one."

"When your parents finish signing the licensing papers."

"They signed them. Papa says if the crown can tax it, it can't burn it, and he laughed like it was the funniest thing ever said."

From the mouths of babes. That was more or less the argument Ilsa had made too, in the end.

I thought about Ilsa Voss a great deal these days—more than was probably efficient. She'd vanished into the machinery of the new order the way she'd moved through the old one: fast, silent, useful. The wardens had been reorganized into something called the Gifted Affairs Office, which sounded like a tax bureau and functioned like a shield, and she ran half of it. I'd seen her twice since the well. Both times she'd nodded at me like we shared a debt neither of us wanted named. Then, four days ago, a sealed letter had arrived at the garden house, heavy paper, warden's wax, and I'd put it in a drawer because I am still, at root, a person who hides things, and I hadn't opened it yet.

Maren watched me look at the drawer. Said nothing. Maren's silences had always been load-bearing.

The rest of the morning passed the way good mornings do, without my noticing most of it. More apprentices came—there were seven now, ranging from Pip's precise little flames to a boy of sixteen whose gift was so large and so shapeless that mostly we worked on breathing. Tam taught them the beds between lessons, on the theory that magic and gardening failed for the same reasons: impatience, poor soil, refusal to prune. By noon the cutting's leaves had turned toward the sun and my chest ached in the way I was slowly learning meant happiness rather than dread.

At midday I sent them all home and sat on Maren's bench with the letter.

Wax. Seal. My name in a hand like a drawn blade: *To the Keeper of the Garden.* I hadn't told anyone I was calling myself that. I hadn't called myself anything yet, honestly. Apparently Ilsa Voss had decided for me, which was either infuriating or the funniest joke in Vell.

I broke the seal.

---

*Ilsa Voss, Office of Gifted Affairs, to Freya Reyes, Keeper of the Garden—*

*Forgive the formality. I have only one register and it is the wrong one.*

*You will have heard the reports: registrations proceeding, no incidents, the Registry building under conversion to a school. I signed the order converting it myself. There is a satisfaction in that I lack the vocabulary for, so I will not attempt it.*

*Two matters. First: the records. When the Registry fell, certain files did not burn—the ones marked with the ash-leaf sigil, the ones cataloguing bloodlines the Crown hunted longest. Your family's file among them. I have read it. I will say only this: the Crown did not save you for last out of patience. It saved you last because embers of your line are the only fires it was ever afraid of. It knew what you were before you did. I thought you should hear that from someone who isn't flattered by you.*

*Second: there have been irregularities. Three registered gifts gone quiet in the past fortnight—no deaths, no burns, just gifts gone dim, holders reporting dreams of cold and a voice offering to tell them who to be. Small numbers. Possibly coincidence. I have spent my career not believing in coincidence, and I do not intend to retire from the habit.*

*The sites of all three dreams form a line on the map. The line points away from the city, north, toward somewhere old. I am riding out tomorrow to look. I would not ask for help—I have proven I can break my own traps—but I am given to understand that gardens require keepers, and keepers notice when a weed returns.*

*Come or don't. The light you carry belongs to you now. I was present when you learned that, and I do not intend to be the reason you forget it.*

*But if you come—bring the gardener. He knows what lives in old places. And bring your apprentices nowhere near any of this, or I will write you a strongly worded second letter.*

*—V.*

*P.S. Your shoulders in the Cooper's Row mural are, in fact, flattering. I have seen it. This concludes the personal portion of our correspondence.*

---

I read it twice. Then I folded it, and sat, and listened to the garden breathe—the leaves, the bees, the faint mineral whisper of the well beneath, holding nothing but water.

"Well?" Tam asked from the beds, not looking up. He'd seen my face. He always saw my face.

"Weeds," I said, standing, tucking the letter into my coat. "Up north. Old roots."

He sighed the sigh of a man who had known peace for exactly twenty-one days. "I'll get my spade."

I looked back once, before the gate—looked at the cutting standing small and bright in the open ground, at Maren asleep upright on her bench in the sun, at the garden house with its hook of unused gloves by the door. Five years I'd kept my light buried and called it living. This—this was living. And living, it turned out, included whatever came next, north and cold and hungry as it might be.

I closed the gate behind us. It didn't creak, this time.

Some things, it seemed, were ready to be oiled.
