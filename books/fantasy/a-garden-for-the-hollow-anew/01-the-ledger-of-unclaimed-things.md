## 1. The Ledger of Unclaimed Things

The counting house smelled of tallow and old salt, the way everything in Verrin's Deep smelled of something drowned. Yara Nystrom had worked beneath its low green ceiling for eleven years, long enough to know that the damp came not from the sea above them but from the country below—the drowned one, the erased one, the one whose name you could still taste on certain nights if you licked your lips at the wrong window.

They did not lick their lips at windows anymore.

Yara sat at the third bench from the wall, the one with the worn hollow where a hundred carvers before them had rested their elbows, and turned a fingerbone between thumb and forefinger. It was a woman's bone, second digit of the left hand, delivered that morning in a lead-lined box with a paper tag: *Unclaimed. Audit lot forty-one. Process by quarter's end.*

The bone was warm. They always were, at first. The dead did not cool so much as they quieted, and it was Yara's particular misfortune—though they would never have called it that aloud—to be present for the quieting.

They set the bone against the bench's leather rest and took up the finest burin, the one with the whale-ivory handle worn smooth as a river stone. Around them, the other carvers bent over their own lots: Master Oduya shaping a rib into a ledger-clasp, the twins Ferren and Ferro grinding knucklebones into counters for the weighing room upstairs. No one spoke. Speaking was for the upper floors. Down here, the Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in silence, and silence was the first thing they taught you—not as virtue but as arithmetic. A carver who talked was a carver who heard their own voice instead of the bone's.

And the bone always had something to say.

Yara made the first cut along the shaft, a shallow channel no deeper than an eyelash, and the dead woman's voice rose through the ivory like breath through a reed.

*—told him, I told him, don't take the river road, the tolls have changed—*

Yara breathed out slowly and let the voice settle around them like silt. This was the work. This was what the Guild paid for, though *paid* was a generous word for the roof, the ration chits, the small room with the lock on the inside. The Guild collected bones the way other institutions collected debts, and every bone carried its last hours folded inside it like a letter never sent. A skilled carver could open that letter without tearing it. Could read the name, the debt, the dying wish—and enter all three into the Ledger of Unclaimed Things, where they would sit in careful script until someone upstairs decided what an unclaimed life was worth.

Mostly, nothing. Mostly the entries gathered dust. But sometimes—Yara had seen it twice in eleven years—sometimes a name in the ledger was read aloud in a high chamber, and then men in grey came down the stairs, and the bone was taken away, and the carver who had processed it was asked gentle questions about what they had heard.

The first time, the carver was Tam Wexler, who had laughed too loudly at supper and hummed while he worked. He answered the questions honestly. He was reassigned to the tanneries in the lower warrens, and everyone knew what the tanneries meant, and no one said so, because saying so was also a kind of humming.

The second time, the carver was Yara's sister.

Yara made the second cut and did not think about Brenna. Thinking about Brenna was a door they kept painted shut, and paint, applied thickly enough, held for years.

*—the tolls have changed and he won't listen, he never listens, Maretto, Maretto, if you find this—if anyone finds this—I owe the ferryman at Coldharbour nine coppers and my sister owes me a wedding, she promised, she promised she'd come home for it—*

A name. A debt. And there, coiled at the end like a hair in milk: a door. Not a real door—the dead woman meant the door of her mother's house, the one she'd left slammed on some old quarrel—but the word landed in Yara's chest with a weight that had nothing to do with her meaning. Doors. The Guild had opinions about doors. The Guild had whole vaults of opinions about doors, locked behind the green door at the end of the corridor that even senior carvers pretended not to see.

Yara carved, and listened, and wrote.

The ledger lay open to their left, its pages ruled in faded violet ink. *Name:* unknown; the woman had died outside the registry, which made her doubly unclaimed—unclaimed by family, unclaimed by nation. *Last words:* transcribed in the carver's hand, verified by the carver's oath. *Disposition:* pending.

Eleven years of pending. The ledger held four thousand names in Yara's handwriting alone, and every one of them waited, patient as sediment, for a disposition that would never come. That was the quiet genius of the Guild, Yara thought, making the third cut, the one that began the key's bit. You did not need to burn a history if you simply filed it. You did not need to silence a voice if you shelved it. The drowned country below the city had not been destroyed, whatever the schoolbooks said. It had been *processed*. Audit lot after audit lot, quarter after quarter, until an entire nation fit in boxes with paper tags, and the people who remembered it learned to keep their mouths full of water.

*—Maretto, love, tell her the blue door was never locked, I only ever wanted her to knock—*

Yara's hand slipped.

It was a small slip, a hair's width, but in bone-carving a hair's width is a shout. The burin skated sideways and cut a notch where no notch belonged, and the dead woman's voice cracked open like an egg, spilling something that was not her last hour but older, deeper—a memory pressed down into the marrow the way flowers are pressed into books:

*A kitchen. Steam on glass. A child drawing shapes in the fog of a window, and outside the window, a street with lamps shaped like lilies, and beyond the street, hills with windmills turning, turning, and someone singing in a language Yara did not know and yet recognized the way you recognize your own face in a dark glass—*

Yara set down the burin.

Their hands were steady—that was the horror of it, the hands never shook—but beneath the sternum something turned over, slow and cold, like a body shifting in shallow water. They knew those hills. Everyone knew those hills, or had known them once, before the maps were redrawn and the redrawn maps were burned and the burning was declared a mercy. Forty years ago the country below had been erased from every registry, every atlas, every tongue that mattered, and the city of Verrin's Deep had been built on top of it like a lid on a pot, and the Guild of Impossible Things kept the ledgers that proved the pot had once held anything at all.

The Guild did not talk about the old country. The Guild *processed* it.

"Lot forty-one."

Master Oduya's voice came from the far bench, mild as milk. He had not looked up. His burin had not stopped moving. That was how the counting house worked: attention here was a currency spent carefully, and Oduya had just spent some of his on Yara, which meant Yara had done something worth noticing.

"The bone," Oduya said. "Its quality."

"Good density," Yara said. "Elderly donor. Clear resonance." Their voice came out flat and professional, a coin rubbed smooth of all features. Eleven years of practice. "I've begun the key blank."

"Audit lot forty-one," Oduya repeated, "was flagged this morning. Priority processing. The finished piece goes up to the weighing room by Friday's bell, not quarter's end." Now he looked up, and his eyes were the pale grey of things that live in caves. "There's a buyer."

Buyers were rare. Buyers were *whispers*. In eleven years Yara had finished perhaps two hundred keys, clasps, counters, seals—small beautiful instruments carved from the dead—and exactly three had been marked for buyers, and each time the grey men had come down the stairs afterward, and each time a carver stopped humming.

"Understood," Yara said.

Oduya returned to his rib. The silence closed back over the benches like water over a dropped stone.

Yara picked up the burin and made the fourth cut, and the fifth, and gave the dead woman's fingerbone the shape of a key, because that was what the tag specified, because keys were what the Guild made from its unclaimed dead—keys that opened what, Yara had never been told, and had never asked, because asking was a form of humming.

But the woman's voice kept leaking through the cuts, thin and stubborn as root through pavement.

*—nine coppers to the ferryman at Coldharbour, and tell her the blue door was never locked—*

*Tell her.* As if the bone knew it would be carved. As if the dead knew that their last words would pass through hands like Yara's, hands that could carry a message further than any mouth. Yara had wondered about that, over the years, in the privacy of the space behind their eyes where unsanctioned thoughts were allowed to pace. Did the dead choose their final words knowing? Was a dying whisper, in this city, secretly a letter addressed to whoever held the burin?

If so, then Yara was a postman for the erased. Four thousand letters, undelivered. Four thousand names in violet ink, gathering dust in a ledger nobody read, in a basement nobody visited, in a city built like a lid.

Yara finished the key near the evening bell. It was lovely work, even by their standards—the bit cut clean, the bow shaped into a curling wave because the bone had wanted waves, had *insisted* on waves, and a carver who fought the bone's insistence got cracks. They polished it with chalk and oil until it gleamed like something pulled living from the sea, and they entered it in the ledger—*one (1) key, elder female donor, priority, buyer pending*—and they wrote beneath it, in the column reserved for the carver's private notation, the only rebellion permitted them, the smallest possible act of witness:

*Her name might have been Maretto's wife. She owed nine coppers. She forgave a door.*

Then, because the notation column was checked, and honesty in that column was dangerous, they blotted it out with a fingertip of tallow until the words vanished under a smooth grey film, legible only to someone who knew to look, which was no one, which was Yara alone, which was the point.

That was the strategy. That had always been the strategy. Be the third bench from the wall. Be flat, be competent, be forgettable. Give the Guild nothing to weigh. A carver who wanted to survive in the counting house made themselves into a bone: dense, silent, resonant only when held. Yara had spent eleven years learning to be held without being heard.

They wrapped the key in flannel, placed it in the delivery tray, and blew out their lamp.

In the dark, the dead woman's voice lingered a moment longer, the way warmth lingers in a chair. *She promised she'd come home,* it said, to no one, to the tallow-blotted page, to the city above with its ten thousand lit windows built over a country with no name. *She promised.*

Yara climbed the stairs to their small room with the lock on the inside, and ate their ration, and did not think about Brenna, and slept the way they always slept: lightly, evenly, invisibly, a stone at the bottom of a very deep pond.

Three days later, during the quarterly audit, a stranger bled out on the floor of the weighing room—and everything Yara had buried rose up through the water after them.
