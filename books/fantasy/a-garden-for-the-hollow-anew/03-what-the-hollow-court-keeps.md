## 3. What the Hollow Court Keeps

The counting house smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath it all, the faint sweet rot of bone that had been worked too long without rest. Yara Nystrom knew that smell the way a fisherman knows weather. It meant the shelves were full. It meant the Court had been busy.

They arrived before dawn, as they always did, letting themselves in through the side door whose lock they had picked so many years ago that the mechanism had learned their touch and surrendered without complaint. The quarterly audit was three days off. Three days of quiet ledgers, of tallying what had come in and what had gone out, of keeping their head down and their hands moving and their name spoken as rarely as possible.

Invisibility was not a gift. It was a discipline. Yara had practiced it for fifteen years, since the day their sister's name was struck from the registry and written into a different kind of book entirely, and they had gotten very good at it. Good at walking the corridors of the Guild of Impossible Things like a draft that had learned to carry a lantern. Good at answering questions with just enough truth to satisfy and no more. Good at being *useful*, which was the only shelter the Guild ever offered anyone.

The courier's body was gone by now—taken down to the cold room, weighed, measured, entered into the ledger under *unclaimed*. Yara had watched them carry him out on a plank, his boots knocking softly against the doorframe, once on each side, like a man apologizing for the trouble even in death. They had reported the death, of course. That was procedure. A stranger bleeding out in the weighing room during audit season was exactly the sort of irregularity that got noticed if you tried to hide it, and Yara did not hide things. Yara hid *one* thing, folded small and sleeping inside a hollowed knucklebone in their coat pocket, and everything else about them was open as a windowless room.

What they had not reported was the map.

They thought about it now as they lit the lamps along the counting wall, one by one, each flame catching with a sound like a held breath released. Forty-one lamps. One for every year since the erasure. The Guild liked its symbols where they could be counted.

*The doors are real.*

The words kept surfacing in Yara's mind like drowned things rising. They had heard ten thousand last words in their career—the dead spoke through the bone when you carved it, always, whether you wanted them to or not—and most of them were small. *Tell my daughter.* *I never paid the baker.* *It doesn't hurt anymore.* Last words were mostly the ordinary debris of an ordinary life, and Yara had learned to treat them as cargo: received, recorded, delivered where possible, forgotten where not.

But the courier's words had been different. Not because they were strange—a dying man saying *the doors are real* was strange the way a fire in winter is strange, sudden and bright and demanding immediate attention—but because of how he had said them. He had looked at Yara. Not past them, not through them, not at the uniform or the ledger or the title. At *them*. And he had pressed the folded map into their hands with the desperate precision of a man spending his final coin on exactly the right purchase.

Nobody looked at Yara. That was the whole architecture of their life.

They set the map from their mind with effort, the way you set down something hot, and turned to the morning's work: a tray of fingerbones from a woman who had died in the flood-district two weeks back, unclaimed, her remains routed to the carving floor per standard practice. The Court used the dead. Everyone knew it and nobody said it, because the dead could not object and the living had learned not to try. Bones became keys, seals, hinges, instruments. Bones became the small machinery of a city built atop a drowned country, where nothing was wasted and no one was permitted to be finished.

Yara took up the first fingerbone and the finest blade, and began to carve.

The woman came through immediately, the way some of them did—eager, or perhaps simply unable to wait any longer. Her voice was dry and papery in Yara's skull, a sensation like reading a letter by someone else's candlelight.

*Cold,* she said. *It's cold down here. Is anyone—*

*You're being carved,* Yara told her, in the way of the trade, the professional courtesy that was also a kind of anesthesia. *I'll be careful. You'll become something useful. Most people find it restful, after.*

*Useful.* A pause, and then, with the flat bitterness of the recently dead: *That's what they told my son, when they registered him. Useful. He believed them.*

Yara's blade paused for half a heartbeat. Then continued. This was the danger of the work—not the hearing, but the listening. There was a difference, and fifteen years ago they had crossed it, and the crossing had cost them everything they were still paying for. Hear the dead. Do not listen. Record the debt, deliver the name, close the ledger.

*His name?* Yara asked, because names were part of the protocol, the one mercy the process allowed.

*Tomas Vey. He owes a woman named Aldric four silver for a boat passage he never took. Tell her I said the debt dies with me, if she wants it to. She won't, though. She's not the type.*

*I'll record it.*

*And tell him—* The voice wavered, thinned, gathered itself. *Tell him the country wasn't always like this. Tell him there were doors once, real ones, and we walked through them laughing—*

The voice cut off. Not faded—cut, clean as a snipped thread, the way voices sometimes did when the carving reached the marrow and whatever remained of the person settled into the bone like sediment into still water. Yara sat back, flexing their fingers, and looked at the half-finished key in their hand.

There were doors once, real ones.

Everyone knew about the doors. That was the joke of it, the terrible civic joke everyone learned as children: the old country had been erased forty-one years ago, drowned officially in the great flood that was actually a decree, and the people who had lived there were scattered and renamed and re-registered, and the doors—the famous doors, the hundred doors of the old country, carved into cliffsides and cellar walls and the roots of mountains—had been sealed. Officially sealed. Ceremonially, permanently, with documents and proclamations and a national holiday called Stillness Day on which schoolchildren were taught that the doors had never led anywhere at all, that the whole idea of a country behind the country was a fable, a comfort story, a thing the weak had needed to believe.

Yara had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold: constantly, unconsciously, until something made you look down.

A dying courier, bleeding out on the audit-room floor, pressing a folded map against their chest. *The doors are real.*

Yara reached up and touched the hollowed knucklebone through their coat, feeling its small weight, its secret geometry. They should burn it. They knew that. They had known it since the moment they'd hidden it instead of reporting it, in that first wild half-second when instinct had overruled fifteen years of discipline. An unreported artifact was not a mistake in the Guild; it was a sentence waiting to be transcribed. The Court did not punish theft. The Court punished *concealment*, because concealment implied a self, an interior, a place inside a person where the Court's eye did not reach—and an unreachable place was the one crime the Hollow Court could not forgive.

But they didn't burn it. Their hands, steady as they were with other people's bones, would not perform the task on their own decision. So the map stayed where it was, sleeping in its bone, and Yara went back to work, and the woman's key took shape under their blades—teeth cut true, wards carved fine, her name etched along the shank in letters too small for any eye but a carver's.

By midmorning they had finished eleven keys and delivered six messages—to a baker's widow, a boat-woman named Aldric (who, as predicted, refused to forgive the debt), a mother, two brothers, and a man who had died owing an apology to no one in particular and receiving one anyway. Routine. Rhythm. The safe, small music of an invisible life.

Then the bell above the counting-house door rang, and Guildmaster Vess came in, and the music stopped.

* * *

Vess did not walk so much as arrive gradually, the way fog arrives, occupying a space before you quite register that it has begun to. She was a tall woman gone soft at the edges, with the pale, well-fed complexion of someone who had spent decades indoors among records, and she wore the gray sash of the inner Court over her Guild robes—the only person in the building entitled to it. Her hands were remarkable. Everyone noticed them eventually: long, immaculate, unscarred, the hands of a woman who had never once done actual work, and yet they moved with a carver's economy, as if all that work had been done *through* them, by others, elsewhere.

"Yara Nystrom." She said the name the way she said all names—correctly, completely, with a faint relish, as though tasting whether it rang true. "Working early."

"The audit's in three days, Guildmaster."

"The audit is always in three days. That's rather the point of audits." She drifted along the counting wall, trailing one immaculate fingertip across the shelf-edges without touching anything, and stopped at the tray of fingerbones. Eleven keys lay finished beside it. She regarded them for a moment. "The flood-district woman?"

"Yes, Guildmaster."

"She had a son, I believe. Registered."

"Tomas Vey. I've recorded a debt of his, four silver, to a woman named Aldric."

"Mm." Vess turned. Her eyes were light gray, almost colorless, and they rested on Yara with the particular patience of someone who had never once needed to raise her voice. "You hear them clearly, then. The dead."

"I hear what the bone holds, Guildmaster."

"What the bone holds," Vess repeated softly, as if filing the phrase somewhere. She moved toward the window, where the morning light came in thin and gray off the drowned quarter below—the rooftops just breaking the black water, the old streets visible in shadow beneath, streets that officially had never existed. "A terrible thing happened here yesterday. During your shift."

"A courier collapsed in the weighing room. I reported it."

"You reported it within the hour. Promptly." She smiled, and the smile did not involve any part of her eyes. "Promptness is a form of honesty, wouldn't you say? Or perhaps only its costume."

Yara kept their hands still on the table. Under the table, out of sight, they let one thumb press against the knucklebone in their pocket—feeling it, confirming it, the way you press your tongue to a cracked tooth. "I don't follow, Guildmaster."

"No. You rarely do. It's one of your virtues." Vess examined her own fingernails, immaculate, unhurried. "The courier carried nothing when he was weighed. No papers, no seals, no tokens. A courier, Yara, with empty pockets. His guild badge was genuine. His route logs show him carrying freight on every prior run for six years. And yesterday, on the most important delivery of his career, he arrives carrying *nothing*, and dies reaching for—" she paused delicately— "well. Reaching for you, by several accounts."

"He was dying. Dying people reach for whoever's nearest."

"Do they?" Vess tilted her head. "In my experience, dying people reach for what matters. Whatever that happens to be." She let the silence sit between them, fat and patient, and then sighed, a small theatrical breath, and changed registers entirely—warm now, almost maternal, which was infinitely worse. "You're a good carver, Nystrom. Quiet, exact, no ambitions, no entanglements. Fifteen years and not one blemish on your record. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what I could do with a dozen more like you?" She stepped closer. Up close she smelled of ink and something faintly mineral, like wet stone deep underground. "Which is why it troubles me when irregularities occur near you. Because irregularities, around a person like you, are never accidents. Accidents happen to people who are *seen*."

"I was seen yesterday, Guildmaster. By a dying man."

"Yes." Something flickered behind the colorless eyes—satisfaction, or appetite, hard to say. "He was seen too, before he died. By the wrong people. Which means that whatever he carried, others know it existed, and those others will come asking, and when they come asking, I will need to account for every second of that evening in my house." She straightened her sash with two precise tugs. "The vault inventory is tomorrow, Nystrom. You'll assist. Consider it an honor—it's rare that the counting floor sees the inner collection."

The word landed in Yara's stomach like a stone dropped down a well. *The vault.* The inner vault. The place even senior carvers spoke of only in lowered voices, if at all.

"Of course, Guildmaster."

"Wear gloves," Vess said pleasantly, from the doorway. "The collection is delicate. And Nystrom—" she paused, half-turned, lamplight pooling in the hollows of her face— "if anything irregular should present itself during the inventory, you'll bring it to me directly. Before you bring it anywhere else. Before you take it *anywhere* else. We understand each other, I'm sure. We always have."

The door closed behind her with a click soft as a knuckle cracking.

Yara stood alone among the forty-one lamps, heart beating slow and enormous, and understood two things with perfect clarity. First: Vess suspected. Not knew—Vess never dealt in knowing when suspicion served better—but suspected, and had arranged this inventory the way a carver arranges a vice around a piece of bone: to hold it still while the blade comes down.

Second: tomorrow they would see the inner vault. The place where the Court kept what it silenced. Every instinct of fifteen years screamed at them to request exemption, to plead illness, to be invisible.

Instead, Yara picked up the next fingerbone, and began to carve, and listened to the dead woman's voice rise thin and papery out of the marrow—

*—and the door in the cliff at Vell's Landing, don't let them tell you it was never real, I went through it as a girl, I went through it laughing—*

—and thought: *tomorrow I will see what they keep.*

**From the private ledger of Guildmaster Vess, Inner Court of the Hollow**

*Entry 4,112. Audit minus two days.*

*The Nystrom child continues to interest me, which is itself an irregularity—I do not permit myself interests, as a rule. Interests are hooks, and hooks catch the one who casts them.*

*Facts: a courier of six years' clean service dies in my weighing room carrying nothing, having carried something for the entirety of his approach, per the toll-keeper at Graybridge (who remembers his pack-weight to the ounce; toll-keepers always do). Facts: the courier's final gesture was toward the carver Nystrom, who reports it promptly and hides nothing, which is precisely what a person hiding something would arrange to do. Facts: the courier's ghost has not settled. I have checked the resonance-plates twice since the death. There is a residue in the weighing room that will not disperse—an unsettled voice, restless, *looking for something*. Dead men do not linger unless they have unfinished business, and dead couriers do not have unfinished business unless their delivery failed.*

*So. Either the map exists and the Nystrom child holds it, or the map exists and passed beyond my walls in the hour after the death, in which case someone helped it pass, and there are only nine souls with access to that corridor, and eight of them fear me correctly.*

*Tomorrow I take the child into the vault. Let them stand among the shelves. Let them feel what the Court keeps, and what the Court does to those who keep things from it. Fear is such an honest instrument. It rings true even in the hands of liars.*

*One more matter. The masters have asked after the old seal—the broken-country seal, the one not used in forty-one years. Asked casually, which from the masters is never casual. Something has moved behind the doors, or someone believes it has. The ember in the eastern mountains gutters, they say. The borders soften.*

*If the doors are waking, then everything the Court has built on top of the erasure—all of it, the registries, the reassigned lands, the forty-one years of official silence—is standing on a floor that may open beneath it.*

*I intend to be holding the ledger when it does.*
