## 4. The Ghost Who Won't Settle

The courier's knucklebone sat in Yara's palm for three days before it spoke.

Not that Yara had been idle those three days. They had done everything a careful person does with a thing that should not be in their possession: they had hidden it, moved it, hidden it again. It spent one night wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards of their sleeping cell. One night inside the hollowed marrow of an old ox femur, among the Guild's stock of blank bone waiting to be carved into ledgers. One night—this was desperation, not method—in a jar of pickled eels in the counting house kitchen, where no one with functioning senses would go looking.

The map itself had gone into the deepest hollow Yara owned: the false joint of their left hip, a defect of birth they had carved themselves into a hiding place at fifteen, when they first understood that in the city of Vharn, anything visible was anything losable. The folded paper rode against their femur now like a second heart, thin and patient and damning.

But the knucklebone—the courier's own bone, taken from his hand when Yara prepared him for the Guild's unclaimed pyre—it would not stay anywhere.

It was not that it moved. Bones do not move. But every time Yara reached for it, meaning to sink it in the river or grind it to meal or do any of the sensible things a carver does with a relic that carries more voice than silence, their fingers found some other task first. Ledger entries. Knife-sharpening. The long, slow inventory of the dead that made up ninety percent of their working life. The bone sat where it sat, and Yara's hands went elsewhere, and three days passed like water through open fingers.

On the fourth night, Yara gave up pretending and took it out.

They worked late in the counting house, as always, when the Guild slept and the lamps burned low and the only company was the soft tick of tallow and the smell of tanned hide and lye. Their bench faced the window, and beyond the window Vharn did what Vharn always did at night: it settled another inch onto the drowned country beneath it, groaning like an old man rolling over in bed. Somewhere below the pilings, streets with other names lay under black water, and everyone agreed—had agreed for forty years, loudly, in schools and proclamations and the small print of property deeds—that there had never been any such streets at all.

Yara clamped the courier's knucklebone in the vise and picked up the finest of their burins.

They told themselves this was procedure. An unclaimed dead man's bones were Guild property, and Guild property was either catalogued or rendered useful, and a knucklebone was too small for a ledger-plate and too dense for glue-stock, which left carving. A name, perhaps. A death-date. The little epitaphs Yara cut into the unclaimed so that somewhere, in some ledger nobody read, the record would show that this person had existed—not who they were, never that, but the bare fact of it, *someone was here*, which in Vharn was sometimes the most dangerous sentence you could write.

The burin touched the bone.

Yara heard him before the first chip fell.

*Don't.*

The word arrived the way all the voices arrived—not in the ear but in the teeth, in the small hollow behind the breastbone where sound becomes memory. But it was wrong, all wrong, and Yara's hands went still as pond water.

Dead voices were finished things. That was the whole nature of them, the whole mercy and horror of the gift Yara had been born with and had spent thirty-one years pretending was a minor ailment: when Yara carved a dead person's bone, they heard the last of that person, the residue, the dregs. A name said once. A debt muttered. The final door-slam of a life closing. Voices like footprints in ash—there, going nowhere, fading. You could no more argue with them than you could argue with weather.

*Don't carve me yet,* said the courier's voice, clear and present and annoyed. *I'm still deciding what I want on the stone.*

Yara set down the burin very carefully, the way one sets down a live coal.

"That isn't possible," they said aloud, to the empty room, because aloud was how they kept their sanity on the nights the voices came thick. "You're dead. I carried you to the pyre myself. There is ash in the harbor with your name on the wind."

*There's ash in the harbor,* the voice agreed. It sounded young. Tired in the way of someone tired across years rather than hours. *Ash is cheap. I'm talking about the rest of me.*

The lamp guttered. In its flare Yara saw—or almost saw—a shape sitting on the edge of their workbench, where nothing sat: a suggestion of shoulders, a smear of travel-dust, boots worn through at the heel. Then it was gone, and the room was lamplight and bone dust again, and Yara's pulse was a drum in their ears.

"You don't have a rest of you," Yara said. "Nobody does. That's what death is. I hear the last of people, and then I put them down, and that is the arrangement."

*Whose arrangement?*

"Mine." The word came out harder than intended. Yara picked the burin back up, mostly to have something between themselves and the air. "I've carved four thousand of you. Couriers, dockhands, tax-collectors, thieves, one archbishop whose last thought was about soup. Four thousand voices, and every one of them said their piece and went quiet, and I am very good at putting them down. So whatever you are doing—" they gestured at the room, at the shape that wasn't there, at the whole insolent fact of him— "*stop it.*"

Silence. Long enough that Yara almost believed it had worked.

Then, mildly: *You counted. Four thousand.*

"It's my work. I count my work."

*You count your dead like sacks of grain.* No anger in it. Something worse—curiosity, gentle and terrible. *Four thousand people, and you remember the archbishop's soup.*

"The soup was memorable." Yara hated that their voice cracked on it. "A man facing eternity, and his whole soul narrows to wanting barley broth. What was I supposed to do, forget that?"

*No.* And now the voice was quiet too. *That's exactly the kind of thing you shouldn't forget. See? We agree on something already.*

"We agree on nothing. You're a residue. Residues don't agree, they repeat." Yara pressed the burin to the bone—a test, a defiance—and the courier's voice came sharp:

*I said don't.*

And the bone moved.

Not much. A shiver, a hair's breadth roll in the vise, the way a sleeper turns. But bone does not move, and Yara had spent their whole life standing on the certainty that it did not, and the certainty went out from under them like ice off a lake.

They stepped back. Knocked over the lamp-oil, caught it, righted it. Their hands, which were famous in three districts for being steady, were not steady.

"What do you want," Yara said. Not a question. A surrender wearing a question's clothes.

*Finally,* said Oren—for he was Oren; the name surfaced in Yara's mind with the taste of river-water and road-dust, the way names did when the dead gave them up—*finally. I want you to deliver my map.*

"No."

*I haven't told you where yet.*

"No. Whatever it is, no. I know what happens to people who carry things for dead men in this city. I watched it happen to you. You came through the weighing-room door leaking from six places, and the last thing you did was make me complicit, and I have spent four days being quietly terrified in ways I haven't been since I was fifteen, and it ends here." Yara heard their own voice rising and forced it down to the flat register the Guildmistresses called *ledger-tone*, the tone that noticed nothing. "You're dead. Your errand died with you. The Court will ask questions about you soon enough without me handing them answers."

*The Court,* Oren repeated, and something in his voice changed—cooled, the way a room cools when a window opens in winter. *You've seen what they keep.*

"I keep my eyes down and my knives clean."

*That's not an answer.*

"It's the only answer worth having in Vharn." Yara sat, because their legs had opinions, and looked at the knucklebone in the vise—at the small, ordinary, indecent piece of a man who would not lie down. "You want to know what I do? I carve. I listen to the last words of the unclaimed, and I write down enough to prove they lived and not enough to prove anything else, and I file it, and I go home, and nobody knows my face. Forty years ago this country decided a whole nation had never existed—streets, songs, graves, grandmothers, all of it, *unwritten*—and the people who did that are still in charge, and they stay in charge by noticing things. By noticing *people*. I survive by being impossible to notice. Do you understand? You are asking me to become a thing that gets noticed."

*Yes,* Oren said simply.

The honesty of it stopped Yara cold. The dead lied sometimes—last words curdled with shame, confessions shaped to flatter—but this ghost had none of that fog on him. He was as plain as a nail.

"Why," Yara said.

*Because I spent eleven years filing people the way you file bones,* Oren said. *In the Court's registry. Names in, names out. And I filed one page too many, and I saw what the erasure actually was, and now I'm dead, and the map is still real, and there is a keeper of the last fire who has been waiting nine years for it.* A pause, and when he spoke again there was gravel in his voice. *You held me while I died. Did you think I didn't feel that? Six holes in me and someone holds my hand like it matters whether I'm scared. You can call us cargo all you want, carver. Your hands disagree with you.*

Yara stood up so fast the stool went over.

They crossed to the window. Below, Vharn breathed its wet dark breath; the drowned country slept under the pilings, unwritten, patient. Forty years of patience. Forty years of a whole nation agreeing to be a rumor, and the rumor getting quieter every year, and soon—everyone said soon—it would be gone entirely, and then the doors, if they had ever been doors, would be nothing anyone remembered how to knock on.

"I don't have hands in this," Yara said to the glass. "That's the point of me."

*The keeper of the last fire,* Oren said, and now his voice had gone soft, the way it must have gone soft when he was alive and saying this same thing to himself on some cold road, keeping it warm. *In Ashfall. Under the mountain. She kept our borders real when the Court drew them out of lies, and she's dying, and when she goes, the last true thing about the old country goes with her. Unless someone brings her the map. Unless someone walks out of this city past the Court's puppets and the checkpoints and the forty years of *never happened*, carrying a dead man's paper in their hip.* A breath that was not a breath. *I know what I'm asking. I died carrying it. But I'm asking you because you're the only one in this city who listens to the unclaimed, and I am extremely unclaimed, and you're the only one whose hands disagreed with them.*

"They'll send the puppets," Yara whispered. "If I'm missed. If the map is missed. They wear faces, Oren. I've carved half the faces in this district—they'll wear people I know."

*I know,* he said. *I wore a face too, near the end. It didn't fit.*

The lamp burned low. Yara stood at the window a long time, and the knucklebone sat in its vise, and the map lay against their thigh like a small warm animal, and the drowned country under the city waited with the patience of things that intend to outlast everyone.

When Yara finally turned, they were already unbuckling their tool-roll, and they hated how natural it felt, hated the way thirty-one years of careful invisibility peeled back like skin off fruit to show something underneath that had apparently been waiting all along.

"If I do this," Yara said, ledger-flat, "we do it by my rules. I carve you a traveling-bone tonight—one piece, small, something I can swallow or burn if it comes to that. You speak only when spoken to. You do not rattle, hum, glow, or otherwise behave like a haunted object in front of a single living soul. And when this is done—whatever this is—you settle. You go where the dead go, and you stop talking to me forever."

*A bargain,* Oren said. *Spoken like a merchant.*

"Spoken like a survivor."

*Same thing, in Vharn.* And then, quieter, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he'd carried eleven years: *Thank you, Yara Nystrom.*

Yara froze. "I never told you my name."

*You wrote it in my unclaimed ledger,* Oren said, and Yara could *hear* the smile, insufferable, alive. *Small letters. Under 'recovered effects.' You gave me back my name before you'd even decided to help me. Carver—I told you. Your hands disagree with you.*

Yara said nothing. They clamped fresh bone in the vise, chose a fine burin, and began to carve, and the voice of the dead courier hummed directions in their teeth—*east, always east, toward the mountain, toward the fire*—and outside the window the tide came in over the streets that had never existed, and somewhere far away, under ash, an old dragon's heart beat slower, and slower, and slower.

* * *

It took until dawn to finish the traveling-bone: a finger's length of ox rib, hollowed and sealed, with Oren's name cut along it in the deep strokes that made a voice carry. Yara hid the courier's knucklebone inside it, waxed the seams, and strung it on a cord against their sternum, where it lay like a second, colder heartbeat.

At the first grey light, Yara took a blank ledger-page from the drawer, wrote *Gone east on Guild business — return date uncertain* in their own flawless imitation of Guildmistress Corvel's hand, and pinned it to their bench.

Then they shouldered their pack, checked the map against their hip one last time, and walked out of the counting house into a city that had never erased anything, heading east, toward Ashfall, toward the last fire—

—and did not see the pale figure standing across the canal, watching them go, wearing the dead courier's face.
