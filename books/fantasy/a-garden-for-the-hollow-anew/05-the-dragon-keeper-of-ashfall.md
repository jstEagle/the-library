## 5. The Dragon-Keeper of Ashfall

The map did not want to be read.

Yara had discovered this on the third night out of the city, when they spread it by candlelight in a room above a ferry-house and the paper seemed to lean away from their eyes, the ink going soft and gray like breath on glass. It was not enchanted in any way Yara knew. It simply resisted being known, the way some bones resist the knife—holding their secrets in the grain until the carver stops trying to force them and begins, instead, to listen.

So Yara listened.

They held the map the way they held bone: flat against the breastbone, both palms over it, eyes half-closed. The courier's ghost had gone quiet since the weighing room—Oren came and went like weather—but his last words still lived in the paper's fibers somewhere, and when Yara pressed close enough, they could feel them humming. Not words. Directions. The map was less a picture than a pull, a tug behind the sternum that said *north, and higher than north*, and after a while Yara understood that the roads drawn on it were not roads at all but the memory of a country that no longer appeared on any chart sold in the Guild's own shops.

Ashfall sat where three trade roads gave up and became goat tracks: a town of slate roofs and smoke-stained eaves stacked against the mountain's shoulder like barnacles on a hull. It smelled of sulfur and wet stone and, underneath everything, faintly of cinders—as if the whole place were a fire that had agreed to smolder instead of blaze. The people moved slowly and looked at nothing, which Yara recognized as the posture of a town that had learned what happened to those who looked too long at anything.

They arrived at dusk, wearing the face they wore best: nobody's. A carver's traveling coat with the Guild sigil unpicked from the collar, leaving only the pale ghost of stitching. A satchel of honest wares—mourning rings, remembrance beads, a child's first-tooth charm. A ledger-keeper's stoop. Invisibility, Yara had learned long ago, was not the absence of presence but the careful management of it. You made yourself a thing people's eyes slid off, like rain on oiled leather.

The innkeeper took their coin without taking their measure. Good. But at the corner table near the hearth, a woman in a drover's oilskin watched Yara set down her satchel, and her gaze did not slide. It snagged, and stayed, and Yara felt it the way one feels a splinter working toward the skin.

She was broad through the shoulders, sun-browned, with hands that had clearly done rougher work than driving cattle—the knuckles swollen, the left thumb ending in a burn scar old enough to have gone smooth and silver. She sat alone. Nobody sat alone in Ashfall unless they wanted to be alone, or wanted to be feared, and something about the set of her jaw suggested both.

Yara ordered stew, ate it slowly, and listened—not to the woman, but to the town. To the walls. Ashfall was old enough to have bones in its foundations, and bones always remembered what they'd been part of. Most of what they murmured was ordinary: harvests, quarrels, a wedding forty years gone, a funeral. But beneath it, deeper than foundations, there ran another sound entirely. A slow rhythm. A vast, labored breathing, felt more than heard, like the tide inside a sea cave.

Something on this mountain was alive, and enormous, and dying.

*The keeper of the last fire,* Oren had said—or would say; tense meant nothing to ghosts. Yara paid for the stew, shouldered the satchel, and walked out into the blue dusk toward the breathing.

* * *

The map pulled them up past the last houses, along a track of black slag where nothing grew, through a stand of trees so scorched they looked like ironwork. The air warmed as they climbed, which was wrong for the season and wronger for the altitude, and the smell of cinders thickened until Yara could taste it on their teeth. Twice they passed carved waystones, and both times the stones flinched from their touch—not in fear, Yara realized, but in recognition. These stones had been carved by someone who worked bone. Someone who knew the old craft, the pre-Guild craft, the kind you could be registered out of existence for practicing.

Near midnight, the mountain exhaled.

It was not wind. It came up from a fissure ahead—a seam of orange light in the rockface, breathing heat—and it carried with it a sound Yara had never heard outside of legend and had heard once, terribly, inside a dying man's rib: the voice of a dragon. Not a roar. A groan, long and low and cracked down the middle, the sound of something vast trying to be small enough to die quietly.

Beside the fissure, a door. Rough-hewn planks, unremarkable, except that every inch of them was carved—and Yara's hands began to ache before they even touched it, because the carving was *good*. Old-style good. Each plank told a piece of a story Yara didn't know but recognized the grammar of: a country rendered in relief, its rivers and roofs and market days, its people mid-laugh, mid-grief, mid-work. And across the whole of it, worked so finely it was nearly invisible, a seal. A circle broken by nine lines, like a wheel with nine spokes snapped off at the rim.

The same seal, Yara saw with a lurch, as the one stamped in faded wax on Oren's map.

They knocked. It seemed the only civilized thing to do, though every instinct honed in the Guild's counting house screamed at them to turn around, go back downhill, become invisible again.

The door opened six inches. The burn-scarred woman from the inn stood in the gap, and she was holding a dragon-hook—a long pole tipped with a curved blade, the traditional tool of the keeper's trade and, at need, a fine weapon.

"You followed me," she said. "From the inn."

"I did."

"Guild?"

"No." Yara turned back their collar to show the unpicked stitches. "Not anymore. Not officially ever, really."

The woman's eyes did the arithmetic quickly and coldly. "Unofficially is worse," she said. "Officially, I know what you're capable of. Unofficially, I have to guess." She swung the door wide anyway, which Yara found interesting, and stepped aside to let them in, which Yara found more interesting still. "You'll take your boots off. And you'll tell me why your hands are bandaged, and why you smell like grave-dirt and lamp-oil, and why—" she stopped. Her gaze had dropped to the satchel, where the map's corner protruded, and the wax seal showed.

Everything in her face closed like a fist.

"That's not yours," she said.

"It isn't," Yara admitted. "A dead man gave it to me."

"Then you stole it off a corpse."

"He gave it while he was dying. His hands around mine. There's a difference, though I grant you it's a thin one." Yara set down the satchel, slowly, and stepped back from it, palms open. "I'm Yara Nystrom. Bone-carver. And I've come a long way to find the keeper of the last fire, because a dead courier asked me to, and because I don't think I have a choice anymore."

The woman looked at the map for a long time. Then at Yara. Then, briefly, at something behind Yara—over their shoulder, into the dark of the room, as if checking whether someone else had an opinion. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register: quieter, harder, like a blade being drawn rather than sharpened.

"The Hollow Court has been looking for that seal for forty years," she said. "And now it walks up my mountain in the hands of a stranger with grave-dirt under her nails. Do you know what happens to me if I let you leave here? If I let you go back down and tell anyone what's breathing in my cellar?" She lifted the dragon-hook slightly, not quite aiming it. "There's a Court weigh-station in Vell's Crossing, two days east. I could have you there by dawn. They pay for recovered Court property. They pay very well."

"Yes," Yara said. "They do."

"They'd pay you more than you'd make in ten years of carving mourning beads."

"They would."

"And you came anyway. To the one person who could sell you."

"I came," Yara said, "because the dead don't lie to me, and the living mostly do, and I needed to bet on somebody. The dead pointed me here. That makes you either my ally or my executioner, and I'd genuinely rather know which before breakfast."

For a moment—just a moment—the hard mouth twitched. Then it firmed again, and the woman planted the hook butt-down on the floor like a staff.

"Sef," she said. "Sef Marrowgate. Keeper, formerly of the Ashfall eyrie, formerly of anything at all." She jerked her chin toward the inner dark. "Come and meet her, then. Since the dead sent you. But understand something first, bone-carver." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "What's in that cellar is the last of her kind, and she is dying, and I have spent eleven years making sure the world forgets she exists. If you are a trap—if there's so much as a listening-charm stitched in your coat—I will put you in the ground myself and tell the mountain you fell."

"I believe you," Yara said, and found, to their surprise, that they meant it as a compliment.

* * *

The cellar was not a cellar. It was the mountain's throat.

Sef led them down a stair cut into living basalt, past shelves of jars—rendered fat, salves, char-wrapped dressings—and the heat rose around them like water, until Yara's shirt clung and the bandages on their hands prickled. The breathing grew until it wasn't sound anymore but architecture, a rhythm the whole cavern moved to. And then the passage opened, and Yara stopped walking, and forgot, for the first time in years, to manage their own face.

Vhalor filled the cavern the way night fills a valley.

She lay on her side, scaled in bronze gone dull as old pennies, wings folded like a cathedral brought low. She was smaller than the dragons in the old tapestries—everything real was smaller than its legends—but smallness meant nothing here; she still occupied space the way grief occupies a house. Her chest rose. Fell. Rose. Between the great plates of her ribs, her skin glowed a dim banked orange, guttering, and each breath pushed a little heat-smell out of her nostrils like the last warmth off a hearth at dawn.

"She was the fire-warden of the eastern border," Sef said quietly. Yara hadn't heard her come up beside them. "Before. When there was an eastern border. When there was a country to have a border of." She crossed to a rack of long-handled brushes and began, with the tenderness of habit, to work oil into the scales along Vhalor's jaw. The dragon's eye opened—one amber coin, clouded at the edge—and regarded Yara with an attention that made their skin prickle. Dragons, the stories said, could smell lies.

"She knows what you're carrying," Sef said.

"Can she—"

"No. She can't see the map. She hasn't flown in years. But she smells the Court on Court things. That wax seal was made in their counting house, however old it looks." Sef set down the brush. "Now. Tell me exactly how it came to you, and don't soften it, because I'll know."

So Yara told it: the quarterly audit, the weighing room, the courier bleeding out between the brass scales. The folded map pressed into their hands. *The doors are real.* The decision—not a decision, really, more a reflex of survival—to hide the map inside a hollowed femur rather than log it. The ghost that wouldn't settle. *The keeper of the last fire.*

When they finished, Sef had gone very still, her hand resting flat against Vhalor's neck, feeling the slow pulse there.

"Doors," she repeated. "He said doors."

"So the ghost claims."

"There were doors." Sef's voice had gone strange, flattened, the tone of someone reading from a memory she'd tried to burn. "Before the erasure. Nine of them. Ways out of the old country that didn't go through any checkpoint, any registry, any ledger. My grandmother went through one, the last night. She used to say they weren't passages, exactly. They were—" she frowned, hunting the word. "*Permissions.* Like the country itself deciding, one last time, who it would save." Her hand curled against the dragon's hide. "Everyone said the doors died with the country. Sealed, collapsed, myth. The Court certainly says so. Loudly. Often."

"And yet a Court-sealed map of them just walked up your mountain."

"Yes." Sef turned. In the dragon-light her face looked hewn rather than born. "And here's what I can't get past, Nystrom. The Court wants that map back so badly they've burned three of my informants in the lowlands this year just asking about seals like it. Which means it isn't a curiosity to them. It's a threat." She stepped closer. "So before I decide whether you're a messenger or a bomb—I need to know what you felt. When you touched it. Carvers feel things. What did the map feel like?"

Yara considered lying. Considered how easy it would be, how practiced. Then they looked at Vhalor's open eye, amber and ancient and unfooled, and decided that some audiences you did not lie to.

"It felt," Yara said slowly, "like a debt."

"A debt."

"Not a treasure. Not a weapon. Every powerful thing I've ever handled feels like power—hot, greedy, wanting to be used. This felt like something owed. To someone. By someone." Yara flexed their bandaged fingers. "Like restitution waiting for a recipient."

Sef stared at them. Behind her, Vhalor breathed, and in the pause between breaths the whole cavern seemed to hold itself, listening.

"My grandmother used to say that," Sef said at last, barely audible. "About the doors. She said they wouldn't open for the strong or the clever or the rich. Only for the owed." She laughed once, short and bitter, without humor. "Eleven years I've kept her alive in secret, feeding her, oiling her, watching her gutter, telling myself it mattered, telling myself someone would come. And now the dead send me a grave-robbing carver with a dead man's map and a story about debts." She rubbed her burn-scarred thumb across her knuckles, thinking. Deciding. Yara watched the war move through her face like weather.

"Here's my problem, bone-carver," Sef said finally. "If I help you, and the Court traces you here—and they will trace you, they trace everything—I lose her. Not to death. Death I can manage. To *them*. They'll take her bones and carve them into wardens and border-posts, the way they carved all the others, and the last free dragon in the world will spend eternity standing guard over the theft that killed her." Her jaw tightened. "So I need to know you're worth that risk. And I don't know you. I've known you for one hour."

"You've known me for one hour," Yara agreed, "and you haven't turned me in. That's data."

"That's exhaustion."

"It's also data."

Sex—Sef—laughed again despite herself, and this time it cracked something open, just slightly, in the fortress of her face. She looked back at the dragon, whose eye had drifted shut again, whose glow had dimmed another shade since Yara entered the cavern. How long, Yara wondered, did a fire like that have? Weeks? Days?

"Three nights," Sef said, as if hearing the question. "Maybe four. I've been stretching it for years with fat-render and heat-stitching, and it's not working anymore. She's choosing to go, and I've been too much of a coward to let her." She straightened. "So. Here's what happens tonight. You sleep in the eyrie loft. Tomorrow you show me the whole map—every fold, every mark—and you tell me everything the ghost said, word for word, and we see whether your courier's errand and my dragon's death are the same errand. And if they are—" she exhaled, long and unsteady, the first unsteady breath Yara had seen her take— "then I suppose I'm in. Gods help us both."

She turned toward the stair, then paused.

"One more thing. The seal on that map—the broken wheel. Where exactly did the courier press it into your hands? Show me the grip."

Yara demonstrated, hands remembering the dying man's fingers over theirs. Sef watched, and went white.

"That's a keeper's hand-sign," she whispered. "That's the *old* hand-sign. He wasn't just a courier, Nystrom. Whoever he was—he was trained the same as me. Before the erasure." She stared at Yara's empty hands. "Which means the Court didn't kill him for stealing a map."

The dragon-light guttered, and steadied, and in its flicker Sef's eyes were very bright.

"They killed him for knowing the doors were real."
