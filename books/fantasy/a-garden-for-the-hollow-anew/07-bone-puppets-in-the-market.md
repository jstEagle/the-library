## 7. Bone Puppets in the Market

The market at Ashfall opened before dawn, the way markets do in towns that live under a mountain's bad temper. By the time the first grey light came down through the ash-haze, the stalls were already groaning under smoked fish and iron nails, wool dyed the color of rust, honey from hives kept in the warm cracks of the lower slopes. The vendors called their prices in voices worn smooth as river stones. Children ran between the legs of oxen. Somewhere a fiddle was being tuned, badly, with great enthusiasm.

Yara stood at the edge of it all with a cup of bitter tea going cold in their hands, and tried very hard to be no one.

It was a discipline, invisibility. Yara had practiced it for fifteen years the way other people practiced scales or swordwork: shoulders rounded just enough to suggest apology, eyes fixed on the middle distance, hands tucked into sleeves so the calluses—the telltale ridges of a carver—stayed hidden. In the Guild's counting house, being no one had been survival. Here, three hundred miles from the city and one map deeper into treason, it had become something closer to prayer.

*They haven't found us,* Yara told themselves, the way you tell yourself the ice will hold. *The courier died quietly. The map is a hollow bone against my ribs. No one knows.*

Then the fiddle stopped.

Not faltered—stopped, mid-phrase, the way a heart stops. And into the silence that followed came a sound Yara knew better than their own heartbeat: the dry, deliberate click of bone on cobblestone. Many bones. Walking in step.

Yara set down the tea.

They came up the market road in a line of six, and the crowd parted for them the way crowds part for anything that moves wrong—not running, not yet, but leaning away, the way wheat leans from wind. At a distance they might have been pilgrims in grey traveling cloaks. Up close there was no mistaking them. Their gait had the metronome precision of things that did not need to breathe. Their cloaks hung from shoulders that were too narrow for human frames, frames built of lashed femur and wired rib, padded out with wool and lies.

And their faces—

Their faces were the worst part. Because they were not masks, not exactly. They were faces Yara had made.

The Court had taken its puppets to the best carvers in the Guild, and Yara had been the best for years without ever asking what happened to the work afterward. A jaw here that Yara had carved from the fingerbone of a drowned fisherman, delicate as lacework. Brow ridges there shaped from the skull of an old woman whose last words, spoken through her own carved knuckle, had been *tell my son the debt is paid*. Yara stood in the ash-smelling morning and looked at six dead strangers wearing their own deaths, and understood, with a nausea that started in the teeth, that the Hollow Court had never needed to commission monsters.

It had only needed to pay its invoices.

The puppets walked to the center of the square, where the weighing scales stood—the great brass ones Ashfall used for grain disputes—and stopped. One of them turned its head. Not smoothly, like a person looking around. In increments, like a lock testing tumblers, until its face found Yara across the crowded square.

It was a face Yara knew intimately. They had carved it eleven days ago, from the fingerbones of a man who bled out on the Guild's weighing-room floor with a folded map pressed into a stranger's hands and four words left in his mouth: *the doors are real.*

The puppet opened Oren's mouth.

"The doors are real," it said.

Oren's voice. Not an imitation—the actual voice, drawn out of the bones Yara had carved, resonating in the chambers Yara's chisel had made. It carried across the whole square, clear as a bell over water, and every vendor and child and fiddler heard it. *The doors are real.* Spoken by a dead man's stolen throat in a town where such words could get a whole street registered and erased.

"Yara Nystrom," the puppet said, in Oren's voice, with Oren's cadence, and Yara felt the name land on them like a brand. "You have Guild property. Return it."

The tea cup shattered on the cobbles. Yara didn't remember dropping it.

They moved without deciding to move—fifteen years of practice collapsing into pure instinct, the invisibility abandoned because it was already burned. They shouldered through the market crowd, past the honey stall, past the oxen, toward the tanner's row where Sef had lodgings above the smoke-sheds. Behind them the puppet's voice pursued, patient as arithmetic:

"You have Guild property. Return it. You have Guild property. Return it."

*It doesn't know where I am,* Yara told themselves, climbing the tanner's stairs two at a time. *It knows the name. It's fishing. It's—*

But the face had turned. It had turned and found them, in a crowd of two hundred people, and whatever sense had guided it, it hadn't been sight.

Sef was awake, of course. Dragon-keepers slept like cats, in snatches, always half-listening for fire. They had a knife out before Yara finished the second sentence.

"Puppets." Sef said the word the way you'd say *landslide*. "Here? This far out?"

"Wearing faces I carved. Wearing—" Yara's voice cracked and they hated it. "Wearing his. It spoke with his voice, Sef. It said *return the property*, like he was a ledger entry, like everything he was fits in a—"

"Stop." Sef was already pulling a travel-bag from under the bed, already sweeping its contents onto the floor—flint, dried meat, a coil of oiled rope, a horn vial of dragon-salve that Yara had learned cost more than the house. "Where's Pip?"

The question went through Yara like cold water.

Pip had gone to the market at dawn. Pip went every morning, to buy bread with coins Sef pretended not to count out, because Pip liked the noise and the color and the small sovereignty of carrying money. Pip was in the square. Pip had been in the square when the fiddle stopped.

Yara was back down the stairs before Sef finished saying the child's name.

* *

The square had changed texture. Fear does that—it thickens air the way fat thickens broth. The vendors were still at their stalls but no one was selling; the children had been gathered in doorways by mothers whose hands shook; the fiddler sat holding his silent instrument like a wounded bird. And in the center, the six puppets had begun to walk.

Not toward Yara. Toward the bread stall.

Toward the small figure standing in front of it, clutching a loaf, too still in the way children go still when they understand, wordlessly and completely, that the rules of the world have just changed.

"No," Yara said, aloud, to no one, and ran.

What happened next happened in pieces. Yara remembered the smell of the honey cart they knocked over, sweetness flooding the cobbles. They remembered Sef appearing from nowhere with the economy of motion of someone who had spent a lifetime working around a creature that could incinerate a mistake. They remembered putting themselves between Pip and the lead puppet, arms spread, feeling absurd and mortal and furious.

And they remembered the puppet stopping, three paces away, and tilting Oren's borrowed face at them with something horribly like curiosity.

"You carved me," it said. Still his voice. His exact voice, down to the slight roughness on the vowels that Yara had preserved without thinking, the way you preserve anything beautiful you're given custody of. "I know your hands. I have been inside your hands."

"You're not him," Yara said. Their voice came out steadier than they deserved. "He's dead. You're a theft wearing his shape."

"I am what remains when the Guild is finished," the puppet said. "You know this. You have made forty-one of us." A pause, the head tilting further, tendons of wire creaking. "Forty-one. We counted."

Somewhere behind Yara, Pip made a small sound. The puppet's attention slid past Yara's shoulder toward the sound, and Yara saw—saw with absolute clarity—what the Court had sent these things to do. Not to retrieve a map. Maps could be waited for. The puppets were here to establish a fact: that nowhere was far enough, that anyone who touched the property would be found, and that the finding would be done publicly, in front of children, in front of a market, until the story spread on its own and did the Court's work forever.

"You won't touch the child," Yara said.

"I don't want the child," the puppet said, in Oren's gentle voice, and that gentleness was the most obscene thing Yara had ever heard. "I want you to come home, carver. The masters miss your hands."

Sef hit it with a full flour sack.

It shouldn't have worked. It worked anyway—not because the puppet was fragile but because it was balanced, engineered for walking and speaking, not for being tackled by a dragon-keeper who had spent thirty years hauling feed sacks up a mountainside. The puppet went down in a white explosion, and Sef was already moving to the next one, and Yara grabbed Pip's wrist and pulled.

"Run," Yara said. "Don't look back. Don't—"

"I wasn't going to look back," Pip said, with the scorn only a child can produce while fleeing for their life, and ran.

* *

The ash-fields began where the town's fences gave up.

No one farmed them; nothing grew there but a grey, stubborn moss that drank the mountain's fallout and asked for nothing. From a distance the fields looked flat. Up close they were a broken country of hummocks and old lava channels, ground that swallowed footsteps and gave back nothing, and Sef navigated it the way Yara read ledgers—fluently, contemptuously, without looking down.

"They'll follow," Sef said. They had Pip on their back, the child's arms locked around the keeper's neck, the loaf of bread still clutched in one small fist like a talisman. "Not fast. Puppets don't hurry. But they don't stop either, and they don't eat, and they don't sleep, and eventually that wins."

"Then we don't let it win eventually."

"Oh, good plan." Sef's breath came hard on the uphill stretches, but their voice stayed dry as the moss. "Where exactly are we running *to*, carver? Because I've been assuming you had one, and I've noticed you haven't mentioned it."

Yara hadn't. That was the truth of it. They had a map of doors pressed in a hollowed bone against their ribs, a ghost who wouldn't settle, a child who dreamed of streets that officially did not exist—and no destination, only direction. Away. Always away, since the weighing room, since the fingerbone, since the key.

Except.

"The keeper of the last fire," Yara said slowly. "That's what Oren wanted. He made me swear the map would go to the keeper of the last fire, and I thought—I thought it was a riddle, or a title, some Court office—" They stopped on a hummock of grey moss and looked up, really looked, at the mountain that had been sitting on the horizon all morning like a sleeping animal. Smoke rose from its flanks in thin, tired threads. Not the vigorous plume of a healthy volcano. The exhausted wisps of something dying. "Sef. What's up there?"

Sef walked three more steps before answering. When they did, they didn't turn around.

"You know what's up there."

"I know what the maps say. I'm asking what's true."

Sef set Pip down on a flat shelf of rock and crouched, suddenly very busy adjusting the child's bootlace, which did not need adjusting. "There's a caldera," they said, to the bootlace. "High up. Old lava tubes, warm even in winter. And there's a dragon."

Yara waited.

"She's old," Sef said. "Older than the erasure. Older than the Court. She was the border once—her fire drew the lines that the Court's maps pretend were always theirs. And she's dying, has been dying for years, slow as a candle in a draft, and I—" The bootlace was perfect. Sef stood. "I keep her alive. Illegally. Alone. For nine years I have climbed that mountain twice a week with salve and goat meat and lies, and if you tell anyone, they will kill her and me and probably you, so let's agree that you won't."

The wind came off the ash-fields and carried the smell of sulphur and cold.

"The keeper of the last fire," Yara repeated.

"That would be me," Sef said. "Disgraced, unregistered, and currently fleeing bone puppets with a stolen child and a stranger. Yes. That's the keeper the dead man wanted." They finally looked at Yara, and behind all the dryness there was something raw and frightened and fierce. "So give me the map, carver. Or tell me why I shouldn't take it."

Yara's hand went to their ribs—to the hollowed bone, to the folded weight inside it—and stopped.

Because behind them, faint but unmistakable, drifting up from the direction of Ashfall, came a voice. Oren's voice, carried on the wind across miles of grey moss, patient as arithmetic:

"You have Guild property. Return it."

And beneath it, woven through it, dozens of other voices now—the drowned fisherman, the old woman with the paid debt, all forty-one of Yara's dead, calling in chorus across the ash-fields:

"Return it. Return it. Return it."

Pip's hand found Yara's. The child's grip was small and strong and absolutely certain.

"They're wrong," Pip said, in the matter-of-fact tone of someone correcting a simple error. "It's not Guild property. It's ours."

Yara looked down at the child who remembered a country that had been erased, then at Sef, who kept a dying fire alive with smuggled salve and stubbornness, and made the decision that would unravel everything.

"It's not enough to run," Yara said. "We have to go up. We have to show him the fire."

Sef stared. "Show *who* the—"

Behind them, faint as breath, the ghost's voice joined the wind.

"*Yes,*" Oren whispered. "*Take it to the keeper.*"
