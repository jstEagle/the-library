## 8. The Rival Carver

The border refuge announced itself by smell before sight: tallow, wet stone, and beneath it all the faint sweet rot of old bone shavings, a scent I knew the way I knew my own hands. Sef smelled it too. They stopped in the middle of the flooded road, Pip heavy against their hip, and said nothing, but their jaw did the thing it did when they were deciding whether to tell me bad news.

"You know this place," I said.

"I know *of* it." Sef shifted Pip's weight. "The Unregistered take refuge along the drowned border. There's a woman who forges papers out of bone and birchbark. They say she can carve a person into existence so well the Court's own registries accept the counterfeit." They looked at me sideways. "They say she learned from the best carver the Guild ever broke."

The ash-fields were three days behind us, and the bone-puppets' footsteps still echoed in my sleep—*click, click, click* of femur on cobbles, and Oren's stolen voice coming out of a face I had carved myself, saying my name like it was a debt I owed. Three days of walking through fog that tasted of the drowned country's old orchards. Pip had slept the whole first day and half the second, and woken asking questions I didn't have the courage to answer: *Was the old country pretty? Did the doors have colors? Why did the lady puppet sound sad?*

"The best carver the Guild ever broke," I repeated. "That's one way to describe her."

"You know her."

"I was apprenticed to her." I kept walking. "Ilsabet Vane. She taught me everything I know about listening to bone. And then she taught me the other lesson, the one they don't put in the Guild's primers." The fog thickened around my ankles. "That testimony is a knife, and it cuts whoever's holding it, eventually."

Oren's knucklebone, strung at my throat, had been quiet for an hour. When I touched it, it warmed faintly, the way it did when he was gathering himself to speak. *Not yet,* I thought at it. *Not in front of the child.* He had learned, in his stubborn dead way, to read my silences.

The refuge was a drowned granary, half-sunk, its upper floor tilted above the waterline like a ship run aground. Candles burned in the windows—real candles, wasteful, defiant. Sef knocked a rhythm on the door: two, pause, three, pause, one. From inside, a voice I had spent fifteen years not hearing answered.

"Who taught you that knock?"

"Does it matter?" Sef said. "We're cold, the child's hungry, and the Court is behind us."

The door opened on a chain. One grey eye in a face I had memorized in candlelight a lifetime ago—lined now, the strong jaw gone soft, but the hands. The hands were the same. Carver's hands, knuckles like river stones, a white scar across the left thumb where a chisel had slipped the day she taught me to hollow a bone without cracking it.

Ilsabet Vane looked at Sef, at Pip, and last at me. Her eye widened. The chain rattled off.

"Yara Nystrom," she said. "As I live and breathe and am not yet shelved."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the voice. Don't do the *old friends* thing. The child is cold and we need a floor for the night. That's all this is."

Something closed in her face—not hurt, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a debt she'd been expecting to be presented with for fifteen years. She stood aside and let us in.

* * *

The refuge's interior was warm in the way of places built by the desperate: a stove made from a ship's boiler, pallets everywhere, and along one whole wall, shelves. I knew what was on those shelves before I looked. Bones. Not the Court's kind of collection—hers were labeled, wrapped in waxed cloth, each one a person she was in the middle of forging into existence. A whole shelf of people who had never officially lived, waiting for her chisel to give them papers, names, a past the Court couldn't argue with.

"You're still doing it," I said. "The counterfeits."

"It's not counterfeiting," she said, pouring broth into a tin cup for Pip. "The Court calls it that. I call it testimony. Every bone I carve, I carve the person back *in*. Their mother's name. The street they were born on. The song their grandmother sang. The Court erased a country, Yara. Somebody has to remember it out loud, in the only material they can't burn." She set the cup in Pip's hands. "There. Careful, it's hot, little ghost."

Pip looked up at her with that unnerving directness children have. "Are you the lady who makes people real again?"

Ilsabet's hands stilled. "Something like that."

"Then you can make the old country real again. It's in my dreams. It's all still there, under the water, the doors are—" Pip stopped, glancing at me, having learned already that some words were dangerous. "The map lady has the doors."

Ilsabet looked at me then. Really looked, the way she used to look at a fresh bone before the first cut, and I felt fifteen years collapse like wet paper.

"Show me," she said quietly.

* * *

I didn't want to. That's the truth of it. I sat on her pallet with the hollowed femur in my hands and I wanted, more than I had wanted anything in years, to be somewhere else. Because the moment I showed her the map, she would know what it was. She had been the Court's finest carver once, before her own fall. She would know what the doors meant, and what they would cost, and she would say yes, and I would have to spend more days in the company of the woman whose testimony had put my sister's name in the registry that swallowed her.

But Oren's knucklebone warmed against my throat, and Pip was watching me with broth on their chin, and Sef was standing by the stove with their arms crossed, wearing the expression of a person who has already decided the answer and is waiting for me to catch up.

I unstoppered the femur and shook the map out onto the table.

Ilsabet went very still. Not the stillness of surprise. The stillness of a carver who has just felt a bone hum under her fingers in a way it should not.

"Where did you get this?"

"A dying courier put it in my hands during the quarterly audit. The Hollow Court wants it back. They've sent puppets wearing the faces of people I've carved." I kept my voice flat, ledger-flat, the voice I used for counting. "That's the whole of it."

"That is *not* the whole of it." She touched the map's edge, not quite touching the seal. "Do you know what this seal is? This is the seal of the Office of Borders. The office that drew the line across the old country and declared it never existed. This map shows the doors *out* of a country that officially has no outs. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means the erasure was never finished," Sef said from the stove.

"It means the erasure was never *meant* to be finished." Ilsabet's voice dropped. "You don't build doors out of a mercy. You build doors out of a country you intend to keep empty. They erased Vhalor's Reach on paper, but they left the doors, because paper is not the only country there is." She looked up at me, and her grey eye was wet. "Yara. Do you know what I would have given, fifteen years ago, for this map? What I could have—"

"Don't." The word came out of me with a carver's precision, clean through the joint. "Don't you dare make this about your penance. My sister is *in the registry*. Her name, her voice, her *self*, shelved in the Court's vault because you stood up at her hearing and told the truth. You told the truth, Ilsabet. That's the worst part. You didn't lie. You just didn't understand that the truth, in that room, was a knife, and they'd put it in your hand and point it at her and you *carved with it*."

"I was trying to save you."

"I know." And I did know, had always known, that was the poison of it. "You traded one sibling for another. You registered Maren to keep them from registering me. And Maren has been a bone on a shelf for fifteen years while you carved other people's sisters back into the world." I stood. The knucklebone was hot at my throat now, Oren pressing against the inside of it, wanting in, and I pressed my hand flat over it. *Not yet.* "I'll take the floor for the night. In the morning, if you have a way across the border that the puppets can't follow, tell Sef. Not me. I can't—"

My voice did something I hadn't authorized. It cracked.

Ilsabet rose too, slowly, the way you move near a frightened animal. "Yara—"

"Don't touch me." I stepped back. "You don't get to comfort me. That's not how betrayal works. You don't get to be sorry *and* helpful *and* forgiven all in one night. Forgiveness is a carving, Ilsabet. It takes time. It takes *material*. And right now all I have is the bone you broke."

The refuge was silent. Pip had stopped eating. Sef had gone still by the stove. And on the shelf behind Ilsabet, the waxed-wrapped bones—her people, her remembered ones—seemed, in the candlelight, to lean toward me like an audience.

Ilsabet sat back down. She picked up her chisel. And she said, quietly, to the bone in her hands, not to me:

"Your sister's fingerbone. The little finger, left hand. Do you know where it is?"

The floor tilted. "What?"

"In the vault. The Court shelves people whole, usually. But when they registered Maren, they took a token. They always take a token—it's how the silence-charms anchor. I know because I helped write the procedure." She looked up, and there was no softness in her face, only the terrible competence I had loved and feared as an apprentice. "I know which shelf. I know the vault's turning-days. And I know that if your map's doors are real, then the vault can be opened, and everything in it can be *heard* again." She set down the chisel. "I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight, Yara. I'm asking you to let me be useful. It's the only apology I know how to carve."

I stood there with my hand over a dead man's knucklebone, in a room full of the bones of the unremembered, and I hated her, and I needed her, and the two feelings sat in my chest like two hands trying to hold the same chisel.

Above the stove, the candle flames all bent sideways at once, though there was no wind. On the shelf, one of the waxed bundles shifted—a small, deliberate movement, like a sleeper turning over.

Ilsabet went white. "That's never happened before," she whispered.

At my throat, Oren's knucklebone burned hot as a coal, and through it his voice came, urgent, ragged, the voice of a man who had just understood something terrible:

*Yara. They're not following the map. They're following the child.*
