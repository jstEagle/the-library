## 9. Doors Are for Those Who Belong

The border refuge had been a chapel once, back when the drowned country still had borders worth drawing. Now it was a waystation for the unregistered: a low stone room with a leaking roof, a hearth that smoked more than it warmed, and walls papered with the names of the disappeared, written in charcoal by hands that had passed through and never come back. Ilsabet Vane had been coming here for fifteen years, and every time she crossed the threshold she read one new name and let herself grieve it for exactly as long as it took to bank the fire.

She was banking the fire now when Yara Nystrom came in out of the ash-rain with a dragon-keeper, a child, and a ghost's knucklebone in their pocket, and Ilsabet felt the old grief arrive early, wearing a familiar face.

Fifteen years since she had last stood in a room with Yara. Fifteen years since the registry office in Corvess, since her testimony, since the clerk's dry voice saying *the sibling Yara Nystrom is hereby entered into the Court's permanent custody* — since the look on Yara's face that Ilsabet had rehearsed forgetting every night since and never once managed to forget.

Yara looked at her the way a carver looks at flawed stock: measuring where the crack ran, deciding whether the piece was worth working at all.

"You're thinner," Ilsabet said, because someone had to say something, and cowardice had always dressed itself in practicality on her.

"You're older," Yara said.

"I'm fifty-one."

"You were supposed to be dead," Sef said from the doorway, shaking ash off their coat. "That's what the story was. The great carver Ilsabet Vane, purged in the third sweep, bones ground into registry dust." They said it without heat, but Ilsabet heard the audit in it, the careful weighing. Dragon-keepers, she would learn, were accountants of breath; they counted everything.

"Dead is a registration status," Ilsabet said. "I've held it for years. It's very restful. No one audits a dead woman's ledgers." She nodded at the child half-hidden behind Sef's leg — small, sharp-eyed, with the soot-shadow of the ash-fields still ground into the creases of their knuckles. "And this?"

"Pip," Sef said. "They're with us."

*With us.* Two words, and Ilsabet felt them land in her chest like coins dropped down a well. She had spent fifteen years building a life out of such small plural pronouns, and she knew exactly how much weight they could bear before they broke.

"Well then," she said. "Pip, Sef, Yara. Sit down. There's barley soup if you don't mind it burnt, and there's a rule in this house you should hear before anything else gets said." She took the poker and tapped it against the mantel, an old habit, a bell for a congregation of no one. "In this refuge we do not say the name of the erased country. Not because it's forbidden — everything true is forbidden somewhere — but because names are doors, and I've spent a decade and a half learning what comes through doors."

Pip, to their credit, did not flinch. They only tilted their head, the way children do when an adult has accidentally said something interesting. "What comes through?"

"Everything," Ilsabet said. "Sit."

* * *

The soup was, as promised, burnt. They ate it anyway, hunched around the hearth while the ash-rain ticked against the shutters like a slow clock counting down something none of them wanted to name. Oren's knucklebone sat on the mantel in a dish of salt, and every few minutes it turned slightly on its own, the way a sleeping person turns toward a window.

Yara had not explained the courier yet. Ilsabet had not asked. Some debts you let the debtor arrange themselves.

It was Pip who broke first, because children always do, because patience is a callus that grows over years and theirs were still soft. "You said names are doors," they said, staring into the fire. "Our door has a name. We just can't say it."

"We?" Sef asked gently.

"The people in my dreams." Pip's hands curled around their bowl. "There's a street with blue doors. All the doors are blue, different blues, like — like a scale, like someone painted the whole street listening to one song. And there's a well in the middle with a roof on it, and old women sit on the roof edge even though it's clearly going to collapse, and they argue about fish. Every night. Same argument. About whether the silverfish come upriver before or after the first frost." They looked up, defiant, daring anyone to laugh. "After. It's after. Everyone knows it's after. But Marta says before, and she's been wrong for forty years and she *still* argues—"

The bowl slipped from their hands.

Sef caught it — quick, keeper's reflexes — but the soup was already across the floorboards, and Pip was standing rigid, staring at Ilsabet as if she had struck them.

"How do you know Marta's name," Pip whispered.

Ilsabet set down her own bowl. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were perfectly steady, which was its own kind of obscenity. Fifteen years of practice at steadiness, and here it was, betraying her by working.

"There's a street in the old country called Ferrow Lane," she said. "Every door on it is painted a different shade of blue. The well has a roof. And Marta Ostrevik has been arguing about silverfish since before you were born, before your parents were born, probably before the frost itself was born. She'll be arguing about it long after we're all registered dead." She swallowed. "How do you know her, child? You were born twenty years after the erasure. You've never seen the old country. No one living has."

"I dream it," Pip said. Their chin came up, and Ilsabet saw the stubbornness arrive, the same stubbornness she'd once loved in another face across another table. "Every night. I've dreamed it my whole life. Sef says it's just dreams. But it's not *just* — I know where things are. I know the bakery on Ferrow Lane burns on Thursdays because the oven's cracked, and everyone just accepts it, and the bread tastes better for the smoke. I know the blue door third from the well sticks in winter and you have to lift the handle while you push. I've never *been* anywhere. How can I know how a door sticks in a country that doesn't exist?"

The fire popped. In the salt dish, Oren's knucklebone turned — deliberately now, urgently, a finger tapping glass.

Ilsabet looked at Yara. Yara was watching Pip with an expression Ilsabet recognized from the old days, from the workshop in Corvess: the total absorption of a carver who has just found the grain running true in a piece of bone everyone else had given up on.

"It's not nothing," Yara said quietly. "I've handled relics from the erasure. Registry bones, the ones the Court carved to silence people. When I touch them, there's — a shape underneath. A country-shaped absence. Like a socket where a tooth was." They nodded at Pip. "This child is the tooth."

"That," said Ilsabet, "is either poetry or heresy, and in this nation those are the same genre." She rose, joints cracking, and went to the chest beneath the window. "But you're not wrong. And if we're going to talk about what this child might be, you're going to need to see what I've spent fifteen years stealing."

* * *

The chest was iron-banded oak, and it opened on paper.

Dozens of sheets, layered like fallen leaves, each covered in fine cartographic linework — and each, to any eye trained by the Guild, obviously forged. Ilsabet watched Yara recognize it, watched the professional contempt start to form and then falter as the details resolved: the ink matched period pigments exactly; the creases were aged with smoke and handling, not tea-staining; the seals were pressed from molds cut from genuine wax impressions, and the mold-cutting was flawless.

"These are counterfeits," Yara said slowly. "Travel passes. Old-country passports. These haven't been issued in forty years."

"No," Ilsabet agreed. "They're issued every week. By me." She lifted one and held it to the firelight. "There are perhaps three hundred people left alive who were born in the old country and escaped the registries. Fishers, smugglers, one mad archivist, a woman who sells eels in the delta and has never once paid a tax because officially she does not exist. For fifteen years I have forged them papers proving they exist somewhere else. It's a strange trade, forging existence. Most counterfeiters fake money. I fake *belonging*."

"And the Court?"

"The Court knows documents exist. They can't prove they're false, because the true registers burned in the third sweep — I made sure of that myself, and it cost—" She stopped. Set the paper down. "It cost what it cost. Without the registers, the Court can't check my forgeries against anything. A lie with no truth behind it is indistinguishable from the truth. That's the whole architecture of the erasure, Yara. They didn't destroy the old country. They destroyed the *records* of it, and then dared anyone to remember differently."

Sef had gone very still by the fire. "You were Court-trained," they said. "Registry carver. Third rank. Before the disgrace."

"Before the *disgrace*, yes." Ilsabet smiled, and knew it didn't reach anywhere useful. "I was good at it. That was the problem. You can't carve silence into a person's bones day after day and stay blind to what you're carving away. Eventually you start reading what's under the chisel marks. And under all of it — under every silenced tongue, every unmade name — I found the same thing the courier found, I'd wager." She glanced at the salt dish. The knucklebone had stopped turning. It lay pointing, unmistakably, at Pip.

"What did he find?" Yara asked.

"He found that the erasure wasn't mercy." Ilsabet said it flatly, because she had said it to herself so many nights that it had worn smooth as river stone. "The official history is that the old country was drowning — literally, that the floods had won, that the land was unsalvageable, and the Court absorbed it out of compassion and resettled the survivors for their own good. It's a beautiful story. It's even mostly true about the water." She crouched and began laying the forged papers out on the floorboards, one by one, until they formed a rough mosaic of a coastline. "But the land wasn't unsalvageable. The land was *valuable*. Deepwater ports. River tolls. The only known seams of moonstone between here and the sea. The Court didn't rescue a drowning country. They drowned it on paper so they could own it without heirs. Every person they registered out of existence was a claim extinguished. Every silenced voice was a witness removed. It's not a tragedy, Yara. It's a *theft*. The largest theft in recorded history, committed with ledgers instead of armies."

The rain ticked against the shutters. Pip had come to stand at the edge of the paper-coastline, bare feet on the map of a country they'd never seen, and Ilsabet watched the child's toes find the gaps between the pages as naturally as finding cobbles.

"So the map," Yara said. "Oren's map. The doors."

"Ah." Ilsabet sat back on her heels. "Yes. The doors. Here is where I tell you the rule, and you're going to hate the rule, because everyone hates the rule, including me, and I've had fifteen years to make peace with it." She drew a breath. "The doors out of the old country are real. Seven of them, that I know of — Oren's map shows nine, which means he knew things I don't, which frightens me more than the puppets do. They were built in the last generation before the erasure, by people who saw what was coming. Not escape hatches. *Thresholds.* And they are not locked with keys."

"They open for bloodlines," Sef said. "Everyone says that. Born-of-the-old-soil, and all that folklore."

"Folklore, yes. And folklore is usually a rumor that got tired and sat down." Ilsabet shook her head. "No. I've studied the door at Greywater Crossing — through a spyglass, at distance, for two years, so believe me when I tell you what I watched. The doors don't ask what you are. They ask what you *belong to*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Blood is one way of belonging, the crudest way, the way that requires no effort and therefore proves nothing. But belonging — real belonging — is a made thing. It's built. Given. Sworn. Carved." She looked up at Yara, and let fifteen years of guilt sit plainly in her face for the first time since the registry office. "You of all people should understand that. What do you do, every day, in that counting house? You take the dead stranger's bone and you make it yours. You hold it. You listen to it. You give it a place in your hands. That's not biology, carver. That's adoption."

Yara's hand had gone, unconsciously, to their pocket — to the hollowed bone where the map slept. "The doors open for people who belong to the old country," they said slowly. "And belonging can be *given*."

"Given, sworn, or carved. Those are the three ways the old law recognized. Giving is simplest — a citizen of the old country declares you theirs, publicly, before witnesses, and you belong where they belong. Swearing is the formal rite, oath-bound, hard to undo. And carving—" Ilsabet touched the scarred bench beside the hearth, where her own knife had worn grooves over years of midnight work. "Carving is the deepest. A piece of yourself, freely given, worked into a token for another person. Bone remembers what it's part of. Give someone your bone and they carry a piece of your belonging inside their body forever. The old country's law called it *sworn-in-bone*, and the Court banned the rite within a year of the erasure, which tells you precisely how much it frightened them."

"Why?" Pip asked. "Why would it scare them?"

"Because, little one, the entire erasure runs on one assumption: that belonging can be destroyed by destroying records. Delete the registers, burn the maps, silence the voices, and the country stops existing — because a country is only ever the sum of who belongs to it." Ilsabet rose, knees protesting, and looked down at the paper-coastline, at the child standing in the middle of it like a lighthouse. "But if belonging can be *given* — if a living person can adopt another into a nation that exists nowhere but in memory and bone — then the erasure has a hole in it. Then the country can regrow. One sworn person at a time. The Court didn't ban the rite because it was superstition. They banned it because it works."

Silence, except the rain. In the salt dish, Oren's knucklebone had begun turning again, fast, urgent, a compass needle finding north.

Yara stood. Walked to the dish. Held their palm above the bone without touching it, the way they must have done ten thousand times in the counting house, reading the air around the dead. "He wants to say something," they murmured. "He's been trying since she said 'carved.'" A pause. Their eyes widened slightly. "He says — he says the map isn't a map of exits. He says he stole it because—" Yara frowned, pressing closer to the bone's silence. "It's fragmented. He keeps circling one phrase. *Give it back. Give it back. Give the country back.*"

"Give it back to whom?" Sef asked. "There's almost no one left to give it to."

"Not almost no one," Yara said. And then they all turned, in the same slow moment, the way iron filings turn, and looked at Pip.

The child stood in the middle of the forged coastline with the firelight on their face, and Ilsabet watched understanding arrive in them — watched it land the way heavy things land, without grace, without gentleness. "Me," Pip said. "The doors would open for me. Because I dream it. Because I — because some part of the old country is already in me, somehow, and I don't even know how—"

"Because you belong there," Ilsabet said softly. "However it happened — born of registered parents, dreamed into you, carried in the blood the Court thought it had drained — you belong, child. And belonging is the key. Not a key. *The* key. Oren didn't steal a map of escape routes. He stole a map of restoration. Nine doors, and a child who can open them, and a country waiting on the other side of its own disappearance to be given back."

"But I'm just—" Pip's voice cracked. "I'm nobody. I'm not even registered. I'm not allowed to be anybody."

Ilsabet crouched to the child's height, and the words came out of her before she could weigh them, fifteen years overdue, addressed as much to the ghost of another conversation as to the small fierce face in front of her. "Listen to me. I once told someone that being registered was the same as being safe. I traded one person's freedom for another's and called it arithmetic, and I have been carving penance ever since. So hear the correction, and hear it clean: *nobody* is nobody. Unregistered doesn't mean unreal. It means unwritten — and unwritten things are the only things left in this nation that the Court hasn't figured out how to own." She reached out and, when Pip didn't pull away, tucked a strand of hair behind the child's ear with fingers that smelled of bone-dust and woodsmoke. "You may be the most real person in this room."

Outside, the ash-rain thickened, and somewhere down the valley a dog began to bark — not the panicked barking of strangers, but the flat, rhythmic alarm of an animal that had scented something wrong and uniform and many.

Sef was at the shutter in three strides, one eye to the gap. Their shoulders changed. Anyone who had kept dragons would have read it instantly: the stillness of a keeper watching something large move on the horizon.

"Lights," Sef said quietly. "Four of them. Coming up the refuge road. Moving wrong — too even, too spaced. Nothing human walks that evenly in weather like this." They turned, and the firelight caught the old fear in their face, polished by use. "We need to decide what we are to each other in the next five minutes, because whatever's climbing this hill, it's going to ask."

Ilsabet snuffed the lamp and stood in the dark with her forged country spread on the floorboards around her feet, her past walking up the road toward her in four even lights, and the future standing barefoot in the middle of it all, asking to be taught how to belong.

"Then let's decide fast," she said. "Yara — get the map. Pip — behind the hearthstone, there's a space, go. And someone had better pray that whatever the Hollow Court sent loves company, because I have fifteen years of crimes in this chest and I intend to introduce them properly."

The knucklebone in its dish of salt spun once, twice, and pointed at the door.
