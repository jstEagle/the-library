## 10. The Court's Offer

The border refuge smelled of tallow and wet stone, and for three days it had almost felt like safety.

Yara had learned to distrust that feeling the way other people learned to distrust ice in springtime—by falling through it once and remembering the cold all their life. Safety was a thing the Guild sold, like keys and silence and clean deaths, and nothing the Guild sold was ever what it claimed to be on the label.

Still. Ilsabet's refuge was a cellar beneath a dyer's shop in a town whose name had been changed so many times no one bothered saying it anymore. The dye-vats overhead stained the ceiling in slow blooms of rust and indigo, so that lying awake at night Yara could imagine they were sleeping under a drowned sky. Pip slept curled against Sef's side with the map-bone tucked under their cheek like a talisman. Oren's knucklebone sat in Yara's pocket, warm as a held hand, muttering in his sleep the way the dead did when they dreamed of being alive.

Three days of quiet. Yara should have known better than to believe in it.

They knew something had changed before they understood what. It came up through the floor first—a wrongness in the way the house settled, the particular hush of a building holding its breath. Then the dye-women upstairs stopped singing mid-verse, all at once, like a cut thread. Then the light in the room went strange: not dimmer, but *flatter*, as though something enormous had stepped between the world and its sun.

Sef was on his feet with a fire-iron in his hand before Yara finished sitting up. "Pip," he said softly, "behind me."

"I'm not going behind you," Pip said, but they went, small fingers closing in the back of Sef's shirt.

The door at the top of the stairs opened without sound. That was the worst part. Doors in poor houses complained; they dragged and stuck and needed shoulders. This one swung inward on oiled hinges, and down the stairs came a woman in dove-grey robes with her hands folded in front of her like a woman arriving at a funeral she had personally arranged.

Guildmaster Vess.

Yara had seen her perhaps six times in eleven years of quiet ledgers, and every time had been from across a counting-house floor, at a distance carefully calculated to be respectful without being memorable. Up close, Vess was smaller than her reputation and older than her voice. Her face was unlined in the way of faces that had been *arranged*, and her eyes were the pale grey of bone that has been boiled clean.

"Yara Nystrom," she said, and her voice filled the cellar the way water fills a cup. "You've been difficult to find. I want you to know how much I've enjoyed looking."

* * *

There was no escape and everyone in the room knew it. Sef's fire-iron might as well have been a knitting needle. The stairs were occupied by two figures who stood too still—the stillness Yara knew intimately, the stillness of things that moved only because someone else remembered moving for them. Bone-puppets. But they made no move toward anyone. They simply stood at the top of the stairs like ushers, which was somehow worse.

"You're not wearing your chains," Yara said, because their mouth had always gone ahead of their fear. It was true: Vess wore no badge of office, no ledger-chain, none of the ironwork that marked a Guildmaster in public. She looked, absurdly, like a grandmother come to deliver a casserole and a warning.

"No," Vess agreed pleasantly. "This isn't Guild business. If this were Guild business, you'd already be in a vault." She glanced around the cellar with mild curiosity—at the dye-stained ceiling, at Pip half-hidden behind Sef, at the knucklebone-shaped bulge in Yara's pocket—and Yara watched her catalog everything with the ease of long practice. "I came alone. Well. Nearly alone." A gesture at the puppets. "They're not here to take anything. They're here to make sure no one interrupts us. I find interruptions ruin negotiations."

"We have nothing to negotiate," Sef said. His voice was steady but his knuckles were white around the fire-iron. "We're nobody. We're keeping nobody's ledgers anymore."

"Everyone negotiates," Vess said. "Most people just don't know what currency they're holding." She turned back to Yara, and her voice changed—not softer, exactly, but more careful, the way a carver's hands go careful near a fault line. "May I sit? I've been walking since Ashfall. My knees are seventy years old and they hold opinions."

It was such an ordinary request, so human, that Yara heard themselves say, "There's a stool," before they could stop.

Vess sat. She arranged her grey robes with a few economical motions and folded her hands again, and for a moment she simply breathed, and the cellar breathed with her, and Yara hated how much the room wanted to trust her.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Vess said. "You may interrupt whenever you like. Most people don't."

* * *

"There was a girl," Vess began, "born in the Tidemark quarter, father a net-mender, mother dead of the lung-rot. Two daughters. The elder was registered at birth, standard practice, one silver piece to the registry clerk. The younger—" Vess paused delicately, "—the fee was never paid. Whether from poverty or oversight, the record doesn't say. The record only says *unregistered*, and unregistered children are, as you know better than anyone, invisible. They don't exist. They can't inherit, can't marry, can't be missed."

Yara's hands had gone cold. They knew this story. They had spent eleven years not telling themselves this story.

"The elder sister grew up," Vess continued, "and took work where unregistered people take work—in the margins, in the cash economy, in the gray places. And the younger grew up too, and turned out to have a gift. A rare gift. The kind of gift the Guild notices." Vess smiled faintly. "The Guild notices everything, Yara. That's the whole point of the Guild. But we noticed quietly. We let the girl believe she'd hidden herself well. We gave her quiet work, quiet rooms, a quiet life. And in exchange we asked only one thing—that she stay quiet. That she remain invisible. Because an invisible carver is useful, you see, but a visible one is a problem. Visible people have attachments. Attachments are levers."

"Say her name," Yara said. Their voice came out flat and strange.

"Maren Nystrom," Vess said gently. "Registered retroactively, fifteen years ago, into the Court's protected registry. Do you know what the protected registry is? Of course you do—you keep our ledgers. It's where we put people we're *keeping*. Not prisoners. Prisoners have sentences, terms, paperwork. The protected registry is simpler. We protect them from everything. From freedom, from contact, from time. Maren Nystrom has been protected for fifteen years. She was twenty-two when she entered it." Vess tilted her head. "She would be thirty-seven now. I'm told she still sings while she works. She works in our bindery. She'll never see the books she binds, but she keeps them beautifully."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above, a dye-woman had started singing again, some small defiant fragment of melody, and it fell through the floorboards like water through a crack.

"Fifteen years ago," Yara said slowly, "was when Ilsabet testified."

Something flickered across Vess's arranged face—surprise, quickly filed away. "You know more than I expected. Good. I dislike explaining things twice." She leaned forward. "Here is my offer, Yara, and I'll make it only once, and I'll make it honestly, because honest offers bind better than dishonest ones and I am a creature who honors her bindings.

"Return the map. Return the courier's bones—all of them, whatever he left in your keeping. Come back to the counting house and resume your ledgers, and I will strike Maren Nystrom from the protected registry this week. Not freed into nothing—freed into *citizenship*. Papers, a stipend, a trade, a name in the rolls where any clerk can read it. She walks out of our bindery and into her own life, and no one follows her, ever. You have my oath on it, and my oath is the only currency in this city that has never defaulted."

"And if I refuse?"

Vess spread her hands, rueful. "Then Maren remains protected. Indefinitely. She's comfortable, I promise you—more comfortable than most registered citizens. We're not monsters, whatever the margins say about us. But she will grow old shelving books she'll never read, and she will die in that registry, and she will be buried in a plot marked with a number, and the last person who could have spoken her name out loud to her will have chosen a map over a sister." Vess's voice didn't harden. It didn't need to. "That's all. No threats. The arithmetic does the threatening for me."

* * *

Afterward, Yara could never remember how Vess left—only that the flat light lifted, and the stairs emptied, and the dye-women started singing again, and there was a stool standing in the middle of the cellar at a slight angle, evidence that the whole visit had happened and wasn't a fever dream.

They sat on the floor with their back against the wall and their knees drawn up, and they did the arithmetic.

That was the trap of it, Yara thought. Not the threat. Anyone could refuse a threat; threats hardened people, threats were easy. The trap was that Vess hadn't threatened. She had *offered*. She had walked into a cellar alone, asked politely to sit, complained about her knees, and offered to give a sister back. Fifteen years of imagining Maren—Maren at twenty-two, furious and laughing, Maren teaching Yara to gut fish and sing off-key—and here was a door with her name on it, and all it cost was a folded map and a dead man's bones and the whole impossible country that had never done Yara anything except get erased.

*A map over a sister.* Vess had chosen those words like a carver choosing grain.

"You're doing the thing," Sef said. He had lowered himself onto the floor beside them, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. He didn't look at them. Looking would have made it harder for both of them.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you decide you're a burden and start pricing yourself out of your own family." He turned the fire-iron over in his hands. "I kept a dragon alive for nine years past her time because I couldn't stand the arithmetic of letting go. I know the look."

"She's real," Yara said. It came out cracked. "Maren. Vess doesn't lie about bindings—everyone knows that, it's the one law even the Court keeps. If I give back the map, Maren walks free. Actually free. Papers and a stipend and a *life*, Sef. She's been shelved for fifteen years because of me. Because I was invisible and she was visible, and visible people are levers." Their laugh came out wrong. "Vess is right. That's the worst part. The arithmetic is right. One map against one living woman. One maybe-country against one certain person. What kind of monster hesitates?"

"The kind who's met the people on the other side of the map," Sef said quietly.

From the corner, Pip spoke. They hadn't spoken since Vess arrived; Yara realized now they'd been absolutely silent through the entire negotiation, watching with those old-country eyes that saw too much for a child of eight.

"She lied," Pip said.

"Pip—" Sef began.

"She lied about the sister." Pip crawled forward, stubborn as a burr. "Not about the offer. About the *sister*. She said Maren still sings. How would she know?" The child's chin came up. "My ma used to say the Court can't hear anything unless it's written down. If Maren sang in the bindery, who wrote it down? Who told the Guildmaster? Nobody tells the Guildmaster things like that. Things like that aren't in ledgers." Pip's voice wobbled and steadied. "She said it because it's what you'd want to hear. She studied you. She knew which words would fit."

Yara stared at the child. In their pocket, the knucklebone had gone suddenly, urgently warm.

"That's not proof," Sef said gently. "Pip, love, that's hope wearing a clever coat."

"It's more than she deserves," Pip said, and crossed their arms, and would not be moved.

Oren's voice rose from the pocket, thin and clear, the way it got when he was frightened: *Don't. Don't do the sums, carver. The sums are hers. She built the abacus.*

"I need to think," Yara said, and no one argued, because there was nothing to argue with. Thinking was the one country they could still enter freely.

* * *

Night came down through the dye-stained ceiling in slow indigo blooms. Sef slept with one arm around Pip. The house creaked its ordinary creaks. And Yara sat by the single candle with the map-bone in their lap and Maren's name in their mouth like a stone they couldn't swallow or spit out.

They tried to remember her face and found the memory had been carved smooth by fifteen years of handling. Twenty-two forever. Furious and laughing forever. Singing, allegedly, in a bindery, forever. Vess had handed them a ghost and called it a rescue, and the cruelest part—the part that made Yara's hands shake—was that it might not be a lie. That somewhere in the Court's grey machinery a woman named Maren Nystrom really did exist, shelved and breathing, waiting for a sibling who had spent fifteen years perfecting invisibility instead of spending one day becoming visible enough to save her.

*I could do it,* Yara thought. *Give it back. Walk in tomorrow. The map opens doors for people who belong—but Maren belongs to me, and I belong to her, and that's a belonging older than any erased country. Let the others find another way. Ilsabet knows the rites. Sef knows the mountain. Pip—*

Pip. The word snagged like a nail.

Because Pip was eight years old and unregistered and dreaming of streets that officially did not exist, and if Yara handed the map to Vess, then Pip stayed exactly where they were: invisible, unregisterable, one bad winter away from the margins taking them the way the margins took everyone. And Sef, disgraced, harboring a dying dragon in secret. And Ilsabet, with fifteen years of counterfeits in her workshop, all of it hanging on a map that was the only proof the old country had ever existed at all.

One certain woman against four uncertain ones. Vess's arithmetic again. It was a good abacus. It was built to give the answer she wanted.

But the answer it gave—

Yara pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. *The sums are hers. She built the abacus.*

And underneath the sums, quieter, a question Vess had not put on the table: *what did the erasure cost, in Marens?* Not one sister in one bindery. A whole country of sisters, shelved, numbered, buried under plots with no names. Vess had offered to free one hostage. The map was the ransom note for ten thousand.

The candle guttered. In their pocket, the knucklebone pulsed warm, and Oren's voice came thin and urgent, half-dream, half-memory, words tumbling like something breaking loose after years of being carried:

*"I have to tell you—I remembered—I wasn't supposed to carry it, I was supposed to file it, I saw the requisitions, Yara, I saw who signed them—it was never mercy, it was never mercy, the land under the old country, they wanted the land, the doors were mine to steal because I kept the door-ledgers, I kept them, I can show you the names, I can show you who signed—"*

Yara went very still.

"Oren," they whispered. "What did you carry?"

The knucklebone burned like a coal. And far away, at the edge of hearing, in the direction of the mountains where a last fire was guttering toward its end, something answered—not Oren, not yet, but the map itself, humming against Yara's knee like a struck bell that had finally decided to speak.
