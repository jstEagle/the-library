## 12. The Fire Gutters

The mountain had a smell in the years after the fires went out, and it was not the smell of fire at all. It was the smell of what fire leaves behind: cold ash, wet stone, the mineral breath of snowmelt finding its way down through the old burn scars. Sef had lived in that smell so long they had stopped noticing it, the way a person stops noticing their own heartbeat, until someone new arrived and wrinkled their nose at the gate and said, *what is that?* and Sef had to think about it, really think, before they could answer.

Yara did not wrinkle their nose. Yara stood at the mouth of the ashfall terrace with the wind pulling at their coat and looked up at the cave mouth above them — the great arched throat of it, tall as a cathedral door — and said nothing at all. Which was, in Sef's experience, the mark of a person who already understood that some places asked you to be quiet before they explained themselves.

"You can go up," Sef said. "She won't mind. She stopped minding about strangers around the time I stopped feeding her properly."

"That's not what I'd call reassuring."

"No," Sef agreed. "It isn't."

They climbed the switchback path together, the one Sef had cut by hand over eleven winters because the old stair had cracked in the quake and the Court's surveyors had come through twice, counting ruins, and Sef could not afford to have a well-trodden way leading anywhere in particular. The path was narrow and deliberately dull. It wound through dead orchards — the trees still standing, black and patient, their roots sunk in ash that had once been topsoil — and past a shrine with its face worn smooth, and only at the very top, behind a curtain of hanging roots that Sef had trained across the rock like a curtain across a window, did it open onto the terrace where Vhalor lay.

She was smaller than the stories said. That was the first thing everyone noticed and the last thing anyone said out loud. The stories had her blotting out the moon; the truth was that she filled perhaps half the cave, and even that was a generosity of measurement, because so much of what she had been was now hollow — the great wings folded like a tent struck by weather, the ribs showing through the hide along her flanks in ridges that had not shown when Sef was young. Her scales had gone the color of old pewter. Her eyes, when she opened them — and she opened them now, because she always knew when Sef came up the path, had known it for eleven years, would have known it if she had been deaf and blind and buried — her eyes were the color of the last coal in a grate. Not fire. The memory of fire, banked and patient and running out.

"There you are," she said.

Her voice did not boom anymore. It had once been a thing that rearranged the air in your chest, that made your teeth sing. Now it was a low rolling murmur, like a cart going over a distant bridge. But she still said it the same way she always had, the first words of every one of their days together, and Sef still felt it land in the same place, under the ribs, where all the things they could not say out loud were stored.

"Here I am," Sef said. "Brought a guest. Yara Nystrom. Bone-carver. The one the courier's map was meant for."

Vhalor's gaze slid past Sef and settled on Yara with the weight of a hand. Yara, to their credit, did not flinch. They bowed — an old-country bow, deep, palm pressed flat over the heart, the bow that had not been made in this country for forty years because the country had been made not to have happened — and Vhalor made a sound that was almost a laugh, a sound like a landslide deciding not to happen.

"Where did a bone-carver learn the old bow?"

"My grandmother," Yara said. "She was from the river towns. She taught it to me when I was small and told me never to use it where anyone could see."

"And you use it here."

"There's no one here to see," Yara said, "but you. And you're the one it's for."

Vhalor closed her eyes. When she opened them again there was something in them that had not been there before, and Sef recognized it with a lurch of something between pride and grief, because it was the look of a creature deciding to hope, which is the most expensive decision there is.

* * *

They sat with her through the afternoon, the way Sef sat with her every afternoon, and Sef talked, the way Sef talked every afternoon, filling the cave with small reports — the weather, the state of the shrine, the price of lamp-oil in the valley town, the antics of a marmot that had taken up residence in the woodpile and considered itself the true keeper of Ashfall. This was the liturgy of their days. Sef had built it carefully, stone by stone, the way you build a dam, and for eleven years it had held.

But today the dam had a crack in it, and the crack had a name, and the name was *map*.

Yara sat against Vhalor's foreleg with the courier's map unrolled between them — carefully, on a cloth, because the thing was older than it looked and its ink was made of something that moved when you weren't watching. Oren's ghost sat a little apart, as he always did, a thin gray suggestion of a man, watching the dragon with an expression Sef could not read and did not try to. Pip was below, asleep in Sef's bed after a night of dreaming the old streets aloud, and Sef had left the child with strict instructions not to burn anything, which Pip had received with the solemn nod of a person who had absolutely already burned something.

"The borders," Yara said, tracing the map's edge with one finger. "Oren says they're held. That they don't collapse because she—" a glance up at the vast folded shape of the dragon "—because her fire keeps them. Is that true?"

Sef looked at the map. The map showed a country with edges drawn in ink that shimmered like heat-haze, and beyond the edges, in the margins, the Court's survey-lines ran straight and greedy, the geometry of people measuring a thing they intended to own. Forty years of survey-lines. Forty years of the old country being officially nothing, a cartographic apology, a blank space with a footnote.

"It's true," Sef said.

And then, because the crack was in the dam now and the water was already through it, Sef heard themself keep talking.

"She was burned out of the sky in the last year of the erasure. You know that much from the songs, if you know the songs — everyone pretends not to know the songs. What the songs don't say is that she didn't die. Dragons don't die the way things die. They die the way a country dies, slowly, and only if you let them. She fell in the high pass above Ashfall and the Court's masons came up with their chalk and their instruments to draw the new border through her body, because a border drawn over a living dragon doesn't hold — the ink won't take, the lines crawl, the land argues back. But a border drawn over a dead one—" Sef's hands had closed into fists without their permission. "A dead dragon is just geography. They were waiting for her to die. They had a ceremony planned. There are records. I've read them. They had the ink mixed."

The cave was very quiet. Even the wind at the cave mouth seemed to be leaning in.

"So I didn't let her die," Sef said. "That's the whole of it. That's the confession, if you want it. Eleven years. I found the old husbandry texts — the ones the Court burned, but they missed the copies in the ash-temple, because nobody thinks to burn a temple that's already ash. And I learned what keeps a dragon alive when she wants to go. Not feeding her — that's the story they tell, that the last keeper starved her, and it's a convenient story because it makes the Court innocent. It's the opposite. You keep her *hungry*. Hunger is a kind of holding on. You give her just enough to keep the fire banked, never enough to let it rise, because if it rises she'll burn the way she burned before, and burning is dying for her kind, it's how they spend the last of themselves. So I starved her slowly and called it care. I kept her fire at a coal instead of a flame, and the coal is enough — it's been enough for eleven years — the borders hold, the survey-lines crawl and won't take, and the Court's ink dries crooked every time they try." Sef heard their own voice go flat and careful, the voice of a person reading out a ledger. "Every morning I decide it again. Every morning I look at her and I decide that she should keep being this. Half a dragon in a cave. In pain — don't look at me like it isn't pain, carver, you of all people know what a body is when it's being held past its own consent. I know. I've always known. And every morning I decide it anyway, because the alternative is the country dies with her, and I loved her too much to let her go and too much to let her live properly, and those turned out to be the same love, and it is the most selfish thing I have ever done, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I hate that I would do it again tomorrow."

The words ran out. Sef sat with their hands open on their knees, palms up, the way you sit when you have shown someone the whole of a wound and there is nothing left to do but let them look.

Vhalor had not moved. But her breathing had changed — slower, deeper, the rhythm of a creature who has been listening to a truth said aloud for the first time in eleven years.

"Keeper," she said.

"I know."

"You say it every morning as if it were a choice you make alone."

Sef's throat closed.

"You decide," she said, in that low rolling murmur, "as if I were a thing being kept. As if I were the lamp and you the hand on the wick. Little keeper. I have been choosing this every morning longer than you have been alive. I choose to be the coal. I choose to be the border. Do you think a dragon does not know her own dying? I fell out of the sky knowing exactly what I was spending. I have spent eleven years watching you carry the guilt of a decision we made *together*, because you would not put it on me, because you thought love meant carrying it for me. That is not love. That is theft. You stole my death the way the Court stole my country, and you did it out of love, which is the only reason it isn't unforgivable." Her coal-colored eyes found Sef's and held them, and there was no cruelty in them at all, which was somehow worse. "I forgive you. Now forgive me. Forgive me for wanting to finish."

Sef did not remember deciding to weep. They were simply weeping, the way a wall simply comes down, all at once and after years, and Vhalor lowered her great head until her brow rested against Sef's shoulder, pewter-warm, and stayed there while the sobs shook them both.

* * *

Later — much later, when the light in the cave mouth had gone the color of weak tea and the first stars were out over the burn scars — Yara spoke, quietly, from where they still sat by the map.

"The ember," they said. "Oren's map says the first door is forged in the last flame. When she goes. When she finally spends herself." They looked up. "Sef. If you let her go — if you both let go — the borders come down. The Court takes the whole country inside a season. Unless the door opens first. Unless we're ready."

Sef wiped their face with the heel of their hand, an unglamorous gesture, a keeper's gesture, and looked at the dragon who had forgiven them, and at the ghost who was watching with something like recognition — a man who had also carried a secret past its proper time — and at the carver with their ink-stained, bone-dusted hands, and Sef felt the strange lightness that comes when a weight you have carried so long it became your skeleton is finally set down and you discover you can stand up straight.

"She can't rise," Sef said. "Not to fly, not to fight. But a dragon's last fire doesn't need her to rise. It needs her to *give*. And she'll only give it to people the country belongs to — that's the old law, older than the Court, older than the maps. Which means before she spends herself, we need to be sure of who belongs." Sef looked at Yara, and at the thin gray shape of Oren, and thought of a child asleep downstairs who dreamed in the streets of a country that officially did not exist. "And I think," Sef said slowly, "that we're going to have to make sure of it in bone."

Yara went very still.

"You've heard of the rite," Yara said. It wasn't a question.

"I keep the last dragon alive in a cave behind a dead orchard," Sef said. "You think I don't know about forbidden things?"

Above them, Vhalor's eyes had closed, but the coal at the center of them had brightened — just slightly, just for a moment, like a thing taking a breath — and in the dark of the cave it was almost, almost like dawn.
