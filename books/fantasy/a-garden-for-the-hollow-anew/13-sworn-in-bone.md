## 13. Sworn in Bone

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Yara finished explaining what they intended to do.

They had chosen the ruined chapel at the edge of Ilsabet's border refuge deliberately—a place where the roof had half-fallen and the stars came through the gaps like scattered coins, a place that belonged to no registry, no parish, no nation. If the rite was forbidden—and it was forbidden, forbidden in the oldest ledgers, forbidden with the particular venom the Court reserved for anything it could not tax or own—then let it be done nowhere. Let it be done in a gap.

Sef sat on a fallen beam with their arms around their knees, watching Yara with the flat wariness of someone who has already lost too much to believe easily in gifts. Pip knelt close to the fire, close enough that the heat made a shimmering wall between them and the dark, and did not understand yet. That was the worst of it. The child was watching Yara's hands the way children watch hands—with delight, expecting a trick, a carved bird, something small and wonderful.

Ilsabet stood apart, near the broken doorway, her back to the room. She had not spoken since Yara laid out the tools: the bone-saw, the fine gravers wrapped in oilcloth, the bowl of saltwater, the strip of clean linen. She knew. Of course she knew. She had been a carver longer than Yara had been alive; she had taught Yara which bones remembered best, which grains held a voice, which held only silence. She knew exactly what Yara meant to take out of their own body, and she could not look at it.

"You don't have to do this," Sef said at last. "There are other ways. There must be."

"There aren't," Yara said. "You know there aren't."

"The Court—"

"The Court forbade the rite because it works." Yara unwrapped the first graver. Its edge caught the firelight and threw a thin line of gold across the chapel floor. "They can't register what's sworn. They can't erase what's been given. A child who belongs to the old country by adoption of the living isn't an entry in anyone's ledger. They're a fact. Facts are harder to burn than names."

Pip looked up. "What's a rite?"

Yara set down the graver. They made themselves turn and face the child fully, because this deserved facing, all of it, no part of it hidden behind clever hands.

"A promise," they said. "Made out of bone, so it can't be taken back."

* * *

They had found the passage three nights before, in Ilsabet's smuggled books—the ones she kept beneath the false floor of the refuge's pantry, wrapped in waxed cloth against damp. *Rites of Belonging, Being an Account of the Old Country's Sworn Kinships*, printed forty-five years ago, one year before the erasure, when such things were still merely frowned upon rather than punishable by unmaking. Yara had read it by candlelight while the others slept, and read it again, and again, until the words had worn grooves in them deeper than any graver could cut.

The old country had understood something the Court never had: that belonging is not a birthright but a construction, built joint by joint the way a house is built stone by stone. A stranger could be made kin. An orphan could be given a country. All it required was bone freely offered, carved with intent, and sworn over fire by the one giving and received by the one receiving. The living adopting the living. Not blood. Choice.

The Court had banned it within a decade of the erasure, and Yara understood now why with a cold, clarifying fury. Because if belonging could be given—if it could be *made*, by anyone, for anyone—then the erasure itself was meaningless. You could not starve a country into forgetting itself if its people could simply keep adopting each other back into existence. The rite was not sentimentality. It was architecture. It was a door that no army could close, because it opened inside a person and stayed open there.

And Pip—who dreamed the streets of a city that had been struck from every map, who woke weeping for a fountain that had never existed in this world's memory—Pip needed a door inside them more than anyone Yara had ever met.

"I want you to hear everything first," Yara said to the child now. "All of it. Before I ask. Because once it's done, it's done, and I won't have you carrying something you didn't choose."

Pip nodded solemnly, the way children nod when they sense that gravity matters even if they cannot yet weigh it.

"The rite makes us family," Yara said. "Not pretend-family. Real family, the kind the old country recognized, the kind that shows up in the old ledgers next to blood. I carve a piece of my own bone into a token—a token shaped for you—and I give it to you, and you carry it, and as long as you carry it, you belong to me and I belong to you. And through me—" they glanced at Oren's knucklebone, riding in their breast pocket, faintly warm as it always was these days, "—through everyone here who swears witness, you belong to the old country. Not because you were born there. Because you were *given* there. Do you understand the difference?"

Pip's brow furrowed. Then, slowly: "Born is something that happens to you."

"Yes."

"Given is something somebody does."

"Yes."

Pip considered this with the terrible seriousness only children bring to moral arithmetic. "Did anybody ever give you anything?"

Yara's hands went still on the graver. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the ash-fields, and the ruined chapel breathed around them, dust sifting down through starlight.

"Once," Yara said quietly. "A long time ago. Someone gave me my sister's freedom, though I didn't know that's what it was." Their eyes flicked to Ilsabet's back. "And recently, a dead man gave me a map. And a dragon-keeper gave me shelter. And a child gave me—" they stopped, recalibrated, started again. "People keep giving me things I didn't think I'd get. So. It's your turn to receive, if you want it. That's how it works. Giving goes around."

"What do you have to cut?" Pip asked. Direct, unsentimental, appalled in exactly the right amount. "Off yourself."

Yara turned their left hand palm-up in the firelight. Along the outer edge of the forearm, just below the wrist, ran a ridge of bone that carvers called the honest rib—the one place in the body where the bone lay close enough to skin to be reached without grave danger, if one knew the anatomy as intimately as a carver knows their own trade.

"Here," Yara said. "A splinter of it, no bigger than a grain of rice. It will hurt. I won't pretend otherwise. But I've cut worse for less worthy reasons."

"Like what?"

"Ledgers," said Yara, and Pip laughed, and the sound went up through the broken roof and out among the stars, and Yara thought: *this. This is what the Court cannot register.*

* * *

Sef spoke then, from the beam, their voice low and rough. "The last time someone swore something in this region, it got six hundred people registered and erased. You'll forgive me if I flinch at ceremonies."

"This isn't registration," Yara said. "Registration is what they do to you. Swearing is what we do for each other. That's the whole difference, Sef. That's the entire war."

Sef was quiet a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice had changed—cracked open somewhere along its length. "Vhalor used to say dragons didn't recognize nations. Only fires. Wherever a fire was tended for you, that was home." They slid off the beam, crossed the chapel, and knelt down on Pip's other side. "Then I'm witness. If witnessing counts."

"It counts," said Oren's voice, thin and bright, from Yara's pocket. The ghost had been silent all evening, which from Oren was itself a kind of reverence. "In the old country it counted double if the witness was dead. Lucky for you, you've got me."

Ilsabet still hadn't turned around.

Yara rose, crossed to the doorway, and stood beside her mentor in the cold. Below the refuge's slope the ash-fields stretched away silver-gray under starlight, and far off, barely visible, the mountain that held Vhalor's guttering fire smoldered against the sky like a dying coal.

"You taught me every cut I know," Yara said.

"Don't."

"You taught me that bone remembers intent. That a carving made in love holds love forever, and a carving made in fear holds fear, and that's why the Court's puppets walk wrong—they're all fear, all the way down."

"*Don't,*" Ilsabet said again, sharper, and then, wrecked: "You're going to scar. Carvers need their hands, their arms, their—everything you have is your instrument, Yara, and you're going to put a notch in it for a child you've known a season."

"For a child I've known a season," Yara agreed, "who dreams a country whole, and whose dreams match a dead courier's map line for line, and whom the Court will hollow out without losing sleep the moment they learn she exists. Yes." They looked at their mentor's profile, hard-edged against the stars. "Fifteen years you've carved counterfeits. Fifteen years you've smuggled strangers out of erasure. Don't tell me you don't know exactly what this costs and exactly what it's worth. You knew it before I did. You paid it first."

Ilsabet's jaw worked. For a moment Yara thought she would strike them—old habit, old grief—and then she reached out instead and gripped Yara's forearm, hard, right over the honest rib, as if testing whether the bone beneath would hold.

"Cut shallow," she said hoarsely. "Grain runs diagonal there; go with it, never across, or you'll chip the ridge and lose feeling in two fingers." Her voice broke and rebuilt itself. "I taught you that. Use it. Use everything I taught you, and then forgive me someday, or don't, but *live*, Yara. Live long enough for it to matter either way."

She went back inside and knelt down on Pip's third side, completing the circle, and said nothing else, and that was forgiveness beginning—not finished, not even really started, but beginning, the way a crack begins in ice before the thaw.

* * *

Yara prepared.

Saltwater in the bowl, for cleansing. Linen torn in strips. The finest graver, honed that morning on riverstone until its edge could split a hair. The fire fed up to a proper blaze, because the old book had been specific: the oath required flame, living flame, tended by willing hands, and so Sef fed the chapel fire with broken pew-wood until it roared and threw all four of their shadows huge against the standing wall.

Then Yara rolled up their sleeve, set their forearm on the flat altar-stone that had survived the chapel's ruin, and took up the saw.

"Wait," Pip said.

Yara waited.

"Does it work backwards?" The child's eyes were enormous in the firelight. "If I belong to you—can you belong to me? Both ways?"

Something in Yara's chest, something long calcified, shifted and ground like a hinge unused for years.

"That's the secret of it," they managed. "That's the thing the Court never understood when they wrote the ban. It only works both ways. I can't make you mine without becoming yours. The old carvers called it the loop. The bond doesn't point anywhere. It just—holds."

"Good," said Pip, with the fierce finality of childhood, and nodded once, permission granted, and Yara had to look away for a moment at the fire because their eyes had gone hot and untrustworthy.

Oren began, very softly, to hum. An old tune none of them knew and all of them somehow recognized—the kind of melody a country keeps in its bones after the maps have burned. Sef joined it, low. Then Ilsabet. The song had no words, or its words were worn away, and it rose through the ruined roof into the indifferent stars, and it sounded, Yara thought, like a door being built.

They cut.

The pain was white and absolute and then it settled into a deep ringing ache, and Yara worked through it with the steadiness their sister had once teased them for, their sister who was a name in a Court registry now, their sister for whom this too, someday, if there was a someday. Grain diagonal. With the ridge, never across. The splinter of bone came free no bigger than a grain of rice, and Yara lifted it on the graver's tip into the firelight—living bone, their bone, still warm—and did not let themselves feel the horror of it, only the rightness, only the weight of it, heavier than lead, heavier than the map, heavier than every ledger they had ever kept.

Then they carved.

They carved for an hour while the others watched and the fire sang. Into that grain of bone they put everything: Pip's laughter in the ash-fields, the child's sleeping face smudged with coal, the fierce grip of small fingers on theirs during the flight from the market, the way Pip had insisted the dead deserved to be heard. They carved the shape of a promise. They carved the old country's forgotten knot-patterns from the pages of the smuggled book, the interlacing cords that meant *kinship chosen*. And at the center, because the rite demanded it, they carved a door—a tiny door, no taller than a hair's width, with its hinges rendered so finely they were invisible, so that the door appeared to stand unsupported, opening onto nothing, onto possibility, onto whatever was on the other side of being claimed.

When it was done, Yara strung it on a cord of braided linen and leather and held it out.

"Pip," they said, and their voice was a carver's voice, steady, level, the voice that did not shake because it could not afford to. "This is my bone. It grew in my arm, and I cut it out for you, and I carved it into a door. While you wear it, you are mine and I am yours, and through our witnesses—through the keeper of the last fire, through the carver of fifteen years' smuggling, through the dead man who carried the map—you are a child of the old country, adopted of the living, and no registry, no ledger, no Court can unmake what is freely given and freely worn." They swallowed. The ringing pain in their arm had become a kind of music. "Do you take it?"

"I take it," Pip said, and the words came out too big for the child's mouth, ancient and new at once, as if the country itself were speaking through her, as if forty years of erasure leaned forward into that broken chapel to hear itself answered. "Both ways. Forever."

The cord went over the small head. The token settled against the small chest. And Yara felt it happen—felt it as a physical event, a soft detonation behind the sternum, the loop closing, the bond taking hold—felt Pip become theirs and felt, simultaneous and irreversible, themselves become Pip's, owned by a child, answerable to a child, bound to a future they had never planned for and now could not imagine living without.

Oren's humming stopped on a note that hung in the air like light.

"Oh," the ghost said softly. "Oh, that's what it sounds like. I'd forgotten. I clerked for the Court twenty years and I never once heard it *done*, only banned." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice was thick with something that ghosts should not have been able to feel. "Yara. The map just changed."

Yara's hand went to their pocket. The folded map was warm—warmer than Oren's knucklebone, warmer than body heat—and when they drew it out and unfolded it, the ink had shifted. Lines had redrawn themselves. A route that had ended in blankness now continued, fine and sure, toward the mountain, toward the fire, toward a door—and at the end of the route, where before there had been only a symbol, there was now a word, small and precise, in the old country's script.

Yara couldn't read it. But Ilsabet could. The older carver leaned close, and Yara watched her face as she read it, watched the color leave it and something else arrive in its place—wonder, and terror, and underneath both, unmistakably, hope.

"What does it say?" Sef asked.

Ilsabet looked up at Yara, at the bandaged arm, at the child wearing a piece of Yara's skeleton against her heart.

"It says *the bearer*," she whispered. "Not the courier. Not Oren. *The bearer.*" Her finger trembled above the fresh ink. "The doors have chosen a new key. And we have until the dragon's last ember cools to reach them."

Outside, far off in the dark, the mountain flickered—once, twice—like a lamp running low on oil.

And Vhalor's fire, everyone in the chapel understood at once, was burning faster now. As if it knew. As if it had felt the swearing, and was glad, and was spending itself gladly, and had not much gladness left to spend.
