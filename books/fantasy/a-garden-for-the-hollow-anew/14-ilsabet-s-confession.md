## 14. Ilsabet's Confession

The border refuge smelled of tallow and wet stone, the way all hiding places smell when they have been lived in too long by someone afraid.

Ilsabet Vane had been afraid for fifteen years, and her rooms beneath the ruined chapel of Saint Ferren had taken on the character of that fear: narrow, low-ceilinged, every surface crowded with the tools of a trade practiced in whispers. Bone shavings in clay pots. Knives wrapped in wool to keep them from singing against each other. A cold hearth she never lit after dark, because light through a broken window was a signature as legible as any carved name.

She had been carving since moonrise. A knucklebone tonight, a woman's, worn smooth at the joint from years of kneeling on stone floors—she could read that much before the knife ever touched it. The dead told their lives through their bones the way rivers told theirs through silt, and Ilsabet had learned long ago to listen with her hands. This woman had prayed. This woman had worked. This woman had died badly, somewhere in the flooded districts south of the Weir, and now her finger would become a registry token good enough to pass inspection in three of the Court's nine counting houses.

Good enough. Not perfect. Nothing was perfect; perfection was how you got caught. The Court's auditors trusted flaws the way honest men trusted calluses.

She heard them coming down the chapel steps before she heard the door—three sets of feet, one dragging slightly, one too quick, one measured and careful as a carver's knife. Her hands went still over the workbench. Fifteen years had taught her the arithmetic of footsteps. These were hers. These were the ones she had been waiting for, dreading, rehearsing speeches toward for two days since Sef's raven-scratched note had come wrapped around a river stone: *We have Yara. We have the child. We need the map explained.*

*I have Yara.* As if Yara Nystrom were a thing that could be *had*, like a bolt of cloth or a bag of nails. But then, Ilsabet supposed she herself had once thought so. Had once held Yara's apprenticeship contract the way a jailer holds a key, and called it protection.

The door opened without a knock. Of course it did. She'd taught them that too.

* * *

Yara looked older. That was the first cruelty of it—not changed, not hardened into someone else, but simply older, the way a scar is older than the wound. The same narrow face. The same habit of standing with their weight on their heels, ready to step back. The same eyes, grey as river-fog, that had once watched Ilsabet across a workbench with something close to worship and now watched her with nothing at all. That was worse than hatred. Hatred was a ledger still open. This was a ledger balanced and closed.

Behind Yara stood the dragon-keeper—Sef Ashfall, ash in their braids and a burn-scar climbing their throat like ivy—and half-hidden behind Sef, a child. Small. Maybe eight years. Watching everything with the ferocious attention of someone who had learned early that watching was survival.

"You got my note," Ilsabet said.

"We got your note." Yara's voice was flat as slate. "Sef wanted to come alone. Pip wanted to see inside a smuggler's den. I came because if anyone is going to decide whether to trust you, it should be me."

"And?"

"And I don't know yet." Yara pulled out the stool by the door—the one Ilsabet had left there deliberately, angled toward the light, because some invitations had to be physical before they could be anything else—and sat down without taking off their coat. "So talk."

Ilsabet looked at the child. "Not in front of—"

"Pip stays," Yara said. "Pip hears everything everyone else hears. That's the rule now."

Something moved in Ilsabet's chest, old and unnameable. *That's the rule now.* As if Yara had spent these fifteen years building a family out of the wreckage of the one the Court had taken, and had learned to guard it like a fire in wind.

"Then I'll start with the night your sister was registered," Ilsabet said. "And you can decide what I'm worth afterward."

* * *

"The purge of 941 wasn't announced," she said. "It was scheduled. There's a difference, and the difference is that an announcement gives people time to run, and a schedule only gives the Court time to prepare its lists."

She turned her back on them—deliberately, trusting them not to put a knife between her ribs while she did it, which was itself a kind of confession—and took down a box from the high shelf. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a bundle of papers gone soft and brown at the edges. Registry copies. Forty years old and older, kept against every law she had ever sworn to uphold.

"I was twenty-three. Junior carver, third bench, Guildhouse Meridian. You know the rank—you held it yourself, once. What you don't know is what the third bench saw during the purge weeks. Every unregistered person the Court processed came past our knives. Bones to be catalogued. Names to be struck. And every junior carver on the third bench understood, without anyone saying it, that our own names were provisional. That we were registered the way livestock is registered. One bad audit and the knife turned around."

Yara said nothing. Sef had gone very still by the wall. The child, Pip, had climbed onto the second stool and sat with their chin on their knees, listening the way children listen to stories about monsters—with hunger and dread at once.

"I had a sister too," Ilsabet said. "Did you know that? No. Of course you didn't. Maren. Two years older than me. Unregistered—our parents crossed the Weir during the flooding years and lost their papers in the water, and by the time the Court's clerks got around to us, the paperwork that proved we'd been born *before* the erasure line was drawn had drowned with everything else. So Maren grew up a ghost in her own city. Couldn't hold work. Couldn't walk the main avenues after dark. Couldn't exist loudly."

She laid the papers on the bench, smoothing them flat with both hands, needing something under her palms that wasn't memory.

"In the last week of the purge, the Court issued a quota directive. Every registered household harboring an unregistered relative had forty-eight hours to surrender that relative for processing—or be struck themselves, whole family, whole line. Striking meant de-registration. De-registration meant—" She stopped. Started again. "You know what it meant. Your family taught you."

"They came for us at dawn," Yara said quietly. It was not a question. It was a wound being shown.

"They came for everyone at dawn. Dawn is efficient." Ilsabet finally turned back around. "I want you to understand what I am about to tell you, because I have rehearsed it for fifteen years and it has never once sounded like enough, and it will not sound like enough tonight either. But you will have it true, or you will have nothing."

* * *

"There were two Nystrom girls in the Guildhouse rolls that week," she said. "You, aged eleven, apprentice-track, third-bench candidate—I had marked your bone-work myself, at the winter exhibition. A fisher-girl who carved a gull's wing from a deer's shoulder blade, and made the wing *remember flying*. Do you remember? You wouldn't look at the judges. You stared at the floor the whole presentation."

"I remember the wing," Yara said. "I don't remember you."

"No. I was one of seven judges, and we wore masks that year—plague protocols. Convenient, plague protocols. They let us do terrible things anonymously." She drew a breath. "Your sister Wrenna was fourteen. Unregistered. Born four months after the erasure line, in a house the Court had already decided belonged to no one. Under the quota directive, registering you meant striking your parents. Registering Wrenna was impossible—her birth fell on the wrong side of every line. And leaving either of you unclaimed meant the dawn cart."

"So you registered Wrenna anyway." Sef's voice, low. "Falsified the birth date."

"I falsified everything." The words came out of her like teeth being pulled, one at a time, each with its root. "I went into the registry vault at night with a stolen countersignature and I moved Wrenna Nystrom's birth across the line. Four months. One line of ink. And to balance the ledger—because the vault audits balances, always balances—I struck a registration elsewhere. Another child. Another girl. Fourteen years old. Born on the right side of the line, healthy, unremarkable, with a mother who had paid her fees every year and never missed a season."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the broken chapel roof with a sound like slow breathing.

"I didn't know her," Ilsabet said. "I never learned more than the vault required. Name, district, mother's mark. I chose her because her file was thin. Thin files attract less attention." Her hands had begun to shake, and she pressed them flat against the bench to stop them, the way she pressed bones flat before the first cut. "The dawn cart came for her instead of Wrenna. And your family stayed whole, and you stayed on the apprentice track, and you became the finest carver I ever trained or ever failed, and I told myself for fifteen years that I had traded one grief for another and that arithmetic is what mercy looks like when you're poor. And then I told myself it wasn't arithmetic at all. It was just cowardice wearing mercy's coat. Both things are true. I've stopped trying to decide which one is truer."

"That girl," Yara said. Her voice had not risen at all, which was somehow worse than shouting. "What was her name."

"Liesse Corvan." Ilsabet gave it plainly, without flinching, because flinching would have been another theft. "Her mother's name was Odile. I have paid the Corvan family's seasonal fees for fifteen years, under three false names. It buys nothing. It buys graves maintenance, maybe. But I pay it."

* * *

Pip spoke for the first time. "Is that why you carve the fakes?"

Everyone looked at the child. Pip sat hunched on the stool, small hands locked around one knee, and repeated, with the terrible directness of children: "The tokens. In the pots. Is that for her? Are you trying to give it back?"

Ilsabet felt something crack open in her chest that fifteen years of careful carving had not touched. Because yes. Yes, that was it, said plain by an eight-year-old in six words: she had been trying to give it back. Token by token, night by night, she had been smuggling the erased out of the country the Court insisted did not exist—forging registrations, counterfeiting lineage-bones, slipping the unregistered through checkpoints wearing other people's legitimate pasts. Three hundred and forty-one souls, at last count. She kept the count scratched inside the lid of her knife-box, because she did not trust herself to keep it anywhere softer.

"Not only for her," she said. "But she's in every one of them. Every token carries her file-number hidden in the grain, where no auditor looks. Liesse Corvan travels with every person I get out. That isn't penance. Penance would be easy. It's—" She searched for the word and found only the true one, the ugly one. "It's debt. Debts don't forgive themselves. They just wait."

"Three hundred and forty-one," Yara said.

Ilsabet blinked. "How did you—"

"The knife-box lid. You used to scratch counts in lids when you were nervous. You did it through the entire purge week. I was eleven, not blind." Yara stood, and for a moment they only looked at the workbench, at the half-carved knucklebone, at the papers gone soft with age. When they spoke again their voice had a fracture in it, fine as a hairline in glaze. "I spent fifteen years hating you. Did you know that? I built a whole life on the shape of that hate. It fit my hands better than any tool. And now you tell me you broke another family to save mine, and you've spent every night since paying for it, and I don't know where to put any of this. Hate needs a monster. You won't even let me have the monster."

"No," Ilsabet said. "I won't."

"I'm not forgiving you tonight."

"I know."

"But." Yara picked up the knucklebone, turned it in fingers that read bone the way Ilsabet's read paper—faster, deeper, hearing the dead where Ilsabet only heard the past. Their expression shifted, sharpened, went distant in the way that meant they were listening to something no one else in the room could hear. "This woman. The one whose finger you're carving. She didn't die in the floods."

"She—what?"

"She walked into the Weir on purpose. She says—" Yara closed their eyes. "She says she'd heard there was a place the Court couldn't follow. She says she was looking for a door." Their eyes opened, fixed on Ilsabet, and the fracture in their voice had widened into something like awe, or alarm. "Ilsabet. She knew about the doors. Before Oren. Before the map. How many of the erased have died looking for them? How many bones in the river are *pointing*?"

Ilsabet stared at her former apprentice—at the child beside them, at the dragon-keeper with ash still in their hair—and felt fifteen years of solitary, secret work suddenly tilt toward something vast and terrible and almost holy.

"More than you could carve," she said. "Which is why you're going to need every counterfeit I've ever made. Sit down, Yara. All of you. I'll show you the routes—and then I'll show you the vault-copy I stole the night I moved Wrenna's name. Because the Court's original registry doesn't just record the living." Her hand rested on the brown papers, trembling now, and did not stop. "It records what they erased. Every street. Every name. And I think—it's the thing Oren died carrying. I think it's the map."

Outside, far off toward the border, something howled that was not a wolf, and the three hundred and forty-first name waited unfinished on the bench between them.
