## 19. Who Stays

The door stood in the throat of the mountain like a held breath.

It was not beautiful, not yet. Vhalor's death-heat had hammered it out of raw basalt and old country iron, and its surface still smoked faintly, the way a forge-smith's work smokes when it is newly quenched. Through its seam came a light that none of them had a name for — not firelight, not dawn, but something like the color of a place remembered correctly. The air that leaked through it smelled of rye fields and river mud and woodsmoke from chimneys that had burned nothing for forty years.

Yara Nystrom stood before it with a carver's stillness, hands folded, and did the arithmetic they had been refusing to do since the ember of Vhalor's last flame had settled into its iron cradle at the door's hinge.

The ember was cooling. They could feel it dying the way they felt everything dying — as a thinning of the voice. The mountain itself had a voice, low and mineral and patient, and beneath it the door had a voice too, a bright new note like a struck bowl still ringing. The note was fading. When it stopped, the door would close, and it would close forever, because there would be no fire left in the world hot enough to sing it open again.

And behind them, coming up the switchback road with torches and chains and the borrowed faces of the dead, was the Hollow Court's army.

Yara turned. Their family stood in a half-circle in the ash, lit by the door's strange remembered light, and every one of them was already doing the same arithmetic.

* * *

Sef spoke first, because Sef always spoke first when the silence got heavy enough to hurt.

"I'll stay." The dragon-keeper's voice was rough as scale, cracked from years of breathing ash. They had not stopped smelling of smoke since Vhalor died; it clung to their clothes, their hair, the creases of their knuckles, and Yara suspected it would never wash out. "I held her for eleven years. I know how to keep something alive past its time. That's the whole of my skill, and it's the skill this needs." They jerked their chin at the ember in its cradle, guttering orange at the door's hinge like a heart that refused to admit what it was. "I can bank it. Feed it. Buy hours. Maybe a day."

"You can't." Yara said it gently, and hated how gently. "The ember isn't a dragon, Sef. It doesn't want to live. It's already given everything. All you'd be doing is what you did with Vhalor — prolonging a death out of love, and calling it keeping." They saw the words land, saw Sef's jaw tighten around the old grief, and did not take them back. Kindness now would cost more than honesty. "And the door doesn't need tending. It needs holding. The hinge is a thing of voice and bone, not fire. Fire made it. Voice keeps it."

"Then I'll learn," Sef said stubbornly. "Teach me."

"You can't hear them." Yara spread their hands. "The dead I woke in the vault — they came with me. All of them. A thousand voices, and they're in the stone of this mountain now, waiting for someone to give them shape. The puppets are wearing the faces of the silenced, Sef. The only way to stop them is to call the true voices louder than the stolen ones, and I am the only pair of ears in this world that can hear which is which."

Sef opened their mouth. Closed it. Looked at the door, and Yara watched them understand — really understand, for the first time — what Yara had done in the vault, and what it had cost, and what it meant. The dragon-keeper had spent eleven years talking to a dying creature no one else could hear. They knew what it was to be the only listener in a silent world.

They did not argue again. They just nodded, once, the way you nod at a grave.

* * *

Ilsabet came next, and she did not offer to stay.

She offered something worse. She took off her carver's satchel — the one Yara had known for fifteen years, the one that had hung at her hip in the Guild's workrooms, in the border refuges, in every counterfeit-smuggling run she'd never confessed to until three nights ago — and she set it down in the ash between them.

"Everything's in it," she said. "The gravers. The bone stock. My notes on the Court's charm-work, every silence-sigil I've ever copied or broken. And the ledger of names." Her voice caught on it. The ledger of names — four decades of the erased, every person the Court had unmade from history, copied out in Ilsabet's small precise hand because someone had to remember them even when remembering was treason. "Take it through. When you're over, start carving. A country that's been erased needs its names cut somewhere permanent, and there's no one alive better at making bone hold a truth." She looked up, and her eyes were dry in the way of eyes that had spent all their water years ago and learned to grieve without it. "I stay."

"No," Yara said.

"Yara—"

"No." The word came out with an edge they hadn't meant to put on it, and they let it stay. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to trade yourself again. That's the whole shape of your life, Ilsabet — you decide what the sacrifice is, you make it quietly, and you let everyone else live with the arithmetic. You traded my sister for me. You've been trading yourself for strangers for fifteen years. I'm done being on the receiving end of your ledgers."

Ilsabet flinched. It was a small thing, a flicker around the mouth, but Yara had been carved by the same teacher and knew every joint in the woman's composure.

"Then hear the logistics," Ilsabet said, and her voice went flat and professional, the voice she used for hard truths at workbenches. "You're the only one who can hold the door. Agreed. But someone has to get them through it. The puppets will be here within the hour, and the crossing will be a rout, not a walk. Someone has to stand at the threshold and keep the line orderly, keep the panicked ones moving, keep the children from turning back. That's not a voice-work. That's a body in a doorway. Mine is the oldest body here and the least useful on the far side." She spread her hands. "I'm not trading myself, Yara. I'm spending myself where I'm worth the most. It's what you taught me."

"I never taught you that."

"You taught it by living it. Every day in that Guild, invisible, unclaimed, taking nothing for yourself so no one would ever look at you." Ilsabet's voice softened by a degree, no more. "I learned to disappear from watching you. Let me spend it well."

Yara turned away. The door hummed at their back, its note a little thinner than it had been an hour ago.

* * *

It was Pip who broke them all.

The child had been quiet through the whole argument — quieter than Pip had ever been, which was itself a kind of alarm. The child who remembered streets that no longer existed, who dreamed in the grammar of a drowned country, who had walked through bone-puppet markets and vault-dark and dragon-death without once losing that ferocious bright curiosity — that child sat now on a fallen lintel with their knees pulled up, watching the door the way you watch a thing you love that is leaving.

When Yara finally turned to begin the terrible logistics — the counting of who goes, the ordering of the line — Pip was standing in front of the door.

"No," Pip said.

"Pip—"

"No." The child's voice cracked and they let it crack, let it be a child's voice for once, because they had spent so long being the old country's memory that they had almost forgotten to be eight years old. "I'm not going without you. You carved me. You *carved* me." Pip pressed a hand flat against their own sternum, against the small token of Yara's own fingerbone that hung there on its cord, warm from the child's skin, sworn in bone, irreversible. "You said it makes us family. You said belonging is built. You built me into you. So if you stay, I stay, because I *belong* to you, and the door only opens for people who belong, and I belong *here*, with *you*—"

"Pip." Yara knelt. It was the carver's kneel, the one they used at the workbench, steady, unhurried, the posture that said *I have all the time in the world and both my hands are sure.* They had learned it from Ilsabet, and Ilsabet had learned it from no one, and it was the only inheritance Yara had ever wanted to pass on. "Look at me."

Pip looked. Their eyes were wet and furious and eight years old.

"Your dreams," Yara said quietly. "The streets. The river with the nine bridges. The bakery on Coriander Row where the ghost-lady taught you the words for *good morning* in a language that isn't taught anywhere anymore. Whose are those?"

Pip's chin trembled. "Mine."

"They're yours," Yara agreed. "And whose are they going to be, if you don't carry them across?"

The child stared. Yara watched the question land, watched it turn over, watched Pip — sharp, ferocious Pip, who had matched their dreams against Oren's map line for line — begin to understand the shape of the trap Yara was building out of the child's own dearest things.

"Everyone over there is old," Yara went on, still in the carver's steady voice, hands resting open on their knees, no tremor in them, none. "The ones who remember are old, Pip. Ilsabet's notes are just ink. The names in the ledger are just ink. Dreams fade when the dreamer dies — you know that better than anyone, you've heard it happen, you've felt the streets in your sleep getting quieter every year as the old ones go." They let a silence stand, one beat, two. "You're not a child who remembers the old country. You're the old country's memory. And a memory that stays in a dying land with a dying door is a memory that dies with it. You have to go be the memory *there*. Grow up there. Walk the real streets your dreams came from. Learn the language from living mouths instead of ghosts. And when you're grown — when you know it all, every bridge, every street, every song —"

Yara stopped. The next words were the lie, and the lie had to be laid down with a carver's steady hands or not at all.

"— then you come back for me," they said. "You find me. You carve the door open from your side, and you come back, and you tell me everything I missed. That's the work. That's *your* work, and no one else can do it, and it's the most important work there is." They smiled, and the smile was steady too, because they had spent forty years practicing invisibility and had never once needed it more than now. "You can't do it if you stay here and die with me in a doorway. So you go. That's not leaving me, Pip. That's *coming back*, started early."

It was a beautiful lie. It was, in its way, even a true one — Yara did not know whether the door could ever be reopened, whether any carving of theirs could sing cold iron back to life. They had told the child the one future they could not promise, in the steadiest voice they owned, and they watched the lie do its work: watched hope move into Pip's face like light moving into a room, watched the child's fists unclench, watched the eight-year-old begin, against every instinct, to believe.

"You swear," Pip whispered. "Sworn in bone."

Yara drew the small knife. They pressed the edge to the back of their own hand, hard enough to bead red, and held it out to Pip. The child pressed their palm to it, and their blood mixed, and Yara said the old words — *what is carved is carried, what is carried is kept, what is kept is come-back-for* — and hated themselves with a purity that felt almost like peace.

"Sworn," they said.

* * *

Oren came last, because Oren was dead, and dead things are patient.

The courier's ghost had thinned to little more than a brightness in the door's light, a suggestion of shoulders and a courier's stoop, but his voice was still his own — the voice the Court had stolen for their puppets and could never quite counterfeit, because a voice is not a sound, it is a *way of meaning*.

"You know I can't cross with them," he said. "I'm already on the other side of every door there is. I just wanted—" the brightness flickered, and Yara understood, with the strange double sight they had for the dead, that he was doing what they were doing: arranging his face. "I wanted to say the clerk thing. One more time. In case it helps."

"Say it."

"I was a coward for thirty years," Oren said. "I filed the erasure orders. I alphabetized a country into nonexistence. And the only brave thing I ever did was steal a map and die in a weighing room." The brightness steadied. "And it was enough. That's the whole lesson, Yara. It doesn't take a life of courage. It takes one door, held open, once. Whatever you're about to decide — you've already done the brave part. Everything after this is just logistics."

He laughed, a small dry ghost-laugh. "Logistics," he said again, softer. "That's what we called the lists of the erased. File under logistics. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Go be the door."

And he was gone, or folded into the door's light, which might be the same thing.

* * *

The torches came around the last switchback then, a hundred small fires in the dark, and behind them the puppets walked in the faces of the dead — Yara's dead, the vault's dead, the silenced wearing their own stolen faces like ill-fitting masks. Yara stood, and rolled their shoulders the way they did before a long carving, and gave the orders in a voice that did not shake.

"Sef — the line. Get them moving, the old ones first. Ilsabet — the satchel, and you walk at the rear, and if you look back even once I will carve your name into something embarrassing. Pip—" they crouched one last time, and took the child's face in their hands, and pressed their forehead to the child's forehead, bone to bone, sworn to sworn. "Coriander Row," they whispered. "Nine bridges. *Good morning.* Don't forget a single one."

"I won't," Pip said. "I'll come back."

"I know," Yara lied, with a carver's steady hands, and stood, and turned to face the door.

The ember at the hinge had gone from orange to red. The puppets were close enough now that Yara could hear the stolen voices chattering in their borrowed throats. Behind them, the first of the fleeing ones — the old, the unregistered, the erased who had spent forty years being no one — began to pass through into the light that smelled of rye and river mud.

Yara opened their ears, and reached down into the mountain, and called the thousand dead up out of the stone.

They came like a tide. They came like dawn. And Yara Nystrom, invisible no longer, planted their feet on either side of the door's hinge and began, with both steady hands, to hold.
