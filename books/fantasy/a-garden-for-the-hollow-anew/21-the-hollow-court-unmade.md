## 21. The Hollow Court, Unmade

The door closed behind my family and did not close at all.

That is the trick of a hinge held by living hands: it swings neither way. It simply *is*, an open thing, a wound in the air that breathes. I stood with my palm flat against the warm grain of it—Vhalor's last fire still humming in the wood like a struck bell that would not finish its note—and I listened to the ember cooling behind me, and I listened to the dead.

There were so many dead.

They were not puppets. That had been the Court's great blasphemy, the thing Vess and her masters had never understood about bone: that bone remembers *personhood*, not shape. When the Hollow Court carved their servants, they hollowed first—they scraped out the marrow of memory, the honeycomb of a life—and what was left was a shell that walked. But when I shattered the silence-charms in the vault beneath the counting house, I had not hollowed anything. I had *unstoppered*. And now the dead of the erased country stood around me in the vault's green lamplight, not as bodies but as voices wearing the suggestion of bodies, translucent as breath on glass, patient as only the dead can be patient.

"You held the door," said Oren. He was beside me, of course he was beside me; he had been beside me since a weighing room floor and a fistful of blood. His ghost-shape flickered where the ember-light touched it. "You can let go now, carver. The arithmetic is finished."

"The arithmetic," I said, "is just beginning."

Because here is what no one tells you about holding a door open while the people you love walk through it: the moment they pass, the grief arrives to collect its fee, and it does not accept payment in tears alone. It wants work. My hands wanted work. They had been wanting work since I was a girl in Ilsabet's workshop, learning that a bone held to the ear will speak if you are quiet enough, and that being quiet enough is a kind of loneliness you can build a life inside.

I looked down at the ledger in my other hand.

The Court's ledger. I had taken it from the masters' reading room while the risen dead sang their thousand voices through the corridors above and the enforcers dropped their bone-blades one by one, weeping with stolen mouths, remembering whose faces they wore. It was a fat book bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, and its pages were dense with the Court's beautiful, terrible script—their accounting script, the one they used for inventories, because that is all the erasure had ever been to them. An inventory.

*Country: unregistered. Persons: reassigned. Names: withdrawn from circulation.*

Withdrawn. As if a name were a coin clipped from circulation, melted down, recast into something more useful.

"They kept records," I said aloud, and my voice came out strange in the vault, small against the murmur of the dead. "Forty years of erasure, and they kept *records*."

"We always suspected clerks were involved," Oren said dryly. He had been a clerk himself, once, before he was a courier, before he was a ghost who refused to settle. "No atrocity survives without excellent bookkeeping."

And that was when I understood what my hands wanted.

* * *

The counting house above the vault was empty now. Its clerks had fled or joined the singing or simply sat down in the middle of the marble floor with their heads in their hands, undone by forty years of complicity arriving all at once. The great brass scales in the weighing room—where a bleeding courier had once pressed a folded map into my hands and died believing doors were real—stood silent, their pans perfectly level, weighing nothing.

I laid the ledger on the master carver's table. My table, now, I supposed. Mine by conquest, which is the worst way to own anything, but mine.

The dead gathered.

Not all of them—the vault held thousands, and thousands cannot fit in a room—but representatives, in the way of crowds. An old woman who had been a ferryman's wife on the drowned coast. A boy of perhaps twelve who had died in the first year of the erasure and had therefore stayed twelve for forty years, and spoke with the weariness of someone much older wearing a child's impatience like a borrowed coat. A man with a surveyor's chains still ghosted across his shoulders. Oren. Always Oren.

"Read them," said the old woman. "Read us."

So I read.

The ledger gave me names. Not all of them—many entries read only *unclaimed, unregistered, uncounted*—but enough. Enough to make my gorge rise and my hands shake and my vision blur until the beautiful terrible script swam like fish in dark water. Here was the master Vess answered to, a name I had never heard spoken aloud in ten years of service, written plainly: *Architect of Reassignment, First Chair*. Here were the dates the false border was drawn across land that did not belong to the Court. Here were the shipments—not of goods, never of goods; of *persons*, processed, reassigned, and where reassignment failed, *silenced*, and where silencing failed, *shelved*.

Shelved. Like jars of preserves. Like the bones in the vault, ranked and labeled and dusted twice a year by clerks who never once wondered what they were keeping quiet.

"They counted everything," I said. "They counted every person they erased, and then they erased the count."

"Then we will count again," said the ferryman's wife, simply, as if it were a thing as easy as bread. Perhaps to the dead it is.

I went down to the vault and I began to choose bones.

This is the part I will not prettify, because prettiness is the Court's language and I am done speaking it. The bones in that vault belonged to people. Some of those people were standing near me as voices, watching me lift the physical remainder of themselves out of cedar drawers with silk-lined grooves, and I asked each one first. *May I?* It seemed the barest courtesy the world owed them, and the world had never paid even that, so I paid it now, over and over, a thousand times, until the words wore grooves in my own throat.

*May I?*

"Yes," they said, every one. "Yes. Say it. Say my name."

Bone takes carving differently depending on what it has endured. Femurs that carried a body through hard years go dense and ivory-pale. Fingerbones are delicate as reeds. Skulls—I could not, not yet; there are limits even to necessity, and I set the skulls aside with a promise I meant to keep. For the ledgers I chose ribs mostly, broad and curved, and scapulae, wide as small shields. Surfaces enough for a name and the facts of a life. *Maren Ostrevik, ferryman's wife, mended nets and sang off-key, refused three times to sign the renunciation.* Surface enough for a crime, too, when the crimes came due.

Because that was the second half of the work, the part my hands circled like gulls over a harbor: the masters.

* * *

They came for me on the ninth night.

I should say they *tried* to come for me. The Hollow Court was not yet unmade—it was dying, but institutions die the way dragons die, slowly and in stages, dangerous long after the light goes out of them. Vess arrived with the last of the enforcers she could still bind, six hollow shells walking in the clothes of dead men, and she stood in the doorway of the counting house with the lamp-glow behind her and said, in her reasonable voice, the voice that had signed my permits and praised my discretion for a decade:

"Nystrom. You are committing theft from the registry."

"I am committing *publication*," I said. "You should know the difference. You built an entire country's disappearance on it."

She looked older than she had a month ago. The Court's power had always been a kind of borrowed stillness, the stillness of a held breath, and the vault's unmaking had let the breath out of her too. But her eyes were steady, and her hands did not tremble, and I want to be honest in this account: I was afraid of her. Fear is not cowardice. Fear is just the body telling the truth.

"Give me the ledger," she said, "and the bones, and your silence-charm keys, and I will see that you are remembered kindly. There is a version of this where you are a footnote, Nystrom. A regrettable incident. The Court forgives its instruments."

"The Court is four old people and a filing system," I said, "and I have read the filing system." I turned the rib in my hands so she could see the fresh-cut letters catching the lamplight. "Do you know what bone cannot do, Vess? Bone cannot be silenced. That was your whole trade—you proved it yourself, forty years of proof. Charms fade. Paper burns. Registries can be rewritten." I set the rib down among its brothers, rows of them now, a growing choir of curved white pages on the master's table. "But carved bone speaks as long as bone exists. You taught me that. You should have killed me when you had the chance."

"I nearly did," she agreed, and something in her face moved—not remorse, nothing so clean as remorse, but the shadow where remorse lives in better houses. "Fifteen years ago. Your mentor begged for your life, did you know? Kneeling on this very floor. She offered us her counterfeit network in exchange. We took the network and left you alive, and I have wondered since whether she won or lost that bargain."

Ilsabet. Fifteen years kneeling on marble so that I would live, and I had needed a border refuge and a confession to learn it. Grief and anger arrived together, twin couriers, both pressing things into my hands.

"She won," I said. "But not for your reasons."

Vess's enforcers shifted. Six shells, and behind them in the doorway, more shadows that were not shadows—my dead, drifting up through the floorboards, gathering at my back the way weather gathers. I had not called them. They came anyway. That is what belonging does; it shows up uninvited and stays past the end of the argument.

"Look at them, Vess," I said quietly. "Really look. Every one of them is wearing a face your clerks catalogued. Every one of them has a page in your ledger. And every one of them is going to stand in the public square of this city, in front of the fountain, in daylight, while I read their names and their crimes—" I tapped the ledger, the masters' own beautiful script, "—and yours. The Court kept records. Records cut both ways."

For a long moment the only sound was the ember-cool tick of the brass scales and the whisper of the dead breathing air they no longer needed.

Then Vess did the thing I will think about for the rest of my life, however long the dead allow it to be. She took off her signet ring—the First Chair's ring, the hollow-circle sigil, the key to every charm in the building—and she set it on the master's table next to a ferryman's wife's rib, and she said:

"Then read accurately, carver. I will not have my crimes miscounted. That would be *sloppy*."

And she sat down to wait for the square, and the daylight, and the reading, like a woman attending the funeral of a nation she had helped bury—which, in the end, was exactly what it was.

* * *

It took eleven days to carve everyone.

Eleven days on my knees at the master's table, and the dead taking turns sitting with me—not to help; bone-carving is a solitary art, and they knew it, having been its subject—but to talk. To tell me the small things the ledger never kept. How the boy had liked his eggs. What song the ferryman's wife had sung off-key (everything, apparently; the sea itself had complained). The name of the surveyor's dog. The dead do not ask for monuments. They ask for witnesses, and witnessing turns out to be mostly listening, which is the one skill my whole strange life had given me in surplus.

By the eighth day, living people began to come. Word moves through a drowned city the way water moves through limestone—invisibly and unstoppably. Old women who had half-remembered a grandmother's accent. Men who had signed renunciations under threat and wanted, God help them, to unsign. Children of the unregistered, born after the erasure, coming to look at the bones with the same expression Pip wore in dreams: recognition without memory, homesickness for a place they'd never stood. I gave them chairs. I gave them work. There was always more bone, God forgive me, always more drawers.

On the eleventh day we read the names in the square.

I will not render the whole of it here—there are eleven days of names, and this chapter is not large enough to hold a country, which is rather the point. But I will tell you that the fountain ran all day and nobody minded the noise. I will tell you that when I read *Maren Ostrevik, ferryman's wife, mended nets and sang off-key, refused three times to sign the renunciation*, a woman in the crowd fainted, and her granddaughter caught her, and both of them wept, and Maren's voice-ghost stood beside them with her hands folded, looking like sunrise looks: impossible, and ordinary. I will tell you that when I reached the masters' crimes, Vess stood when her name was read, because accuracy mattered to her, and that accuracy, in the end, was the only integrity the Hollow Court had ever possessed, and it was not enough, and it fell with the rest.

The Court did not collapse dramatically. Institutions rarely do. It collapsed the way a shelf collapses when you remove the books one by one: a tilt, a slippage, a slow thunderous honesty. The registries were opened. The unregistered were registered—into a new book, one I started myself, with a rule burned into its cover in dragon-forged iron: *no entry may ever be withdrawn.* The erased were named, and counted, and mourned. The mourning took longer than the naming. It is still taking. Mourning forty years is not a task; it is a landscape, and we are all learning to farm it.

And when it was done—when the last name was read and the last rib hung in the memorial gallery where the vault had been, cedar drawers replaced by open light—I went back to the master's table, and I sat down in the chair that no longer felt like conquest but still did not feel like mine, and I picked up a piece of Vhalor's forge-bone that Sef had pressed into my hands at the mountain, months ago, in another life.

Dragon-bone. Dense as destiny, warm as a held hand, and stubborn past all sense. The last of the last.

"What are you making?" Oren asked. He was fading now, gently, the way morning fog fades—his delivery complete, his rest earned, staying out of stubbornness and love and the sheer clerkly joy of watching a ledger balance at last.

I turned the dragon-bone in the lamp-light. Through the window I could hear the city: carts, arguments, someone laughing, a child—some registered, some not, all counted—shrieking happily in the fountain square. Somewhere far away, on the far side of a door I had held open with these same scarred hands, my family was walking through a country that existed again. Pip was probably arguing with a garden. Sef was probably letting her.

"A door," I said. "A small one. A practice one." I fitted the graver to the bone, and the bone hummed under it, faint and familiar, like a bell deciding whether to ring. "Because the map had a rule, Oren. Doors open only for those who belong somewhere. And belonging must be built." I made the first cut. "So I'm going to build one that opens both ways. From this side. All the way from this side."

Oren smiled his thin, tired, unfinished smile. "A garden for the hollow anew," he said, quoting something I hadn't yet written, and faded before I could ask him how he knew.

The dragon-bone hummed. Outside, the city counted itself, softly, all night long.
