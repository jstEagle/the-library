## 22. A Garden for the Hollow Anew

The mornings in the old country came up gold.

Pip had learned that in the first year, when they still woke before dawn out of habit — the old habit of the border towns, where you learned to be small and quiet and unregistered before the sun could find you. But here there was no registry, no weighing house, no ledger of names that could be struck through with a clerk's careful pen. Here the sun simply rose over the terraced hills of a country that existed again, and it fell on Pip's face like a hand laid gently on their head, and Pip learned to sleep until the light woke them instead of fear.

This morning the light came through the shutters in long amber bars, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone and something sweeter underneath — the particular sweetness of dragon-ash soil after rain. Vhalor's soil. The whole valley smelled of her, if you knew how to listen with your nose. Sef said you never stopped smelling her. Sef said that was the point.

Pip dressed quickly, pulling on the patched coat that had been too big six years ago and fit perfectly now, and went down the stairs two at a time, because today was a counting day, and counting days belonged to them.

* * *

The garden lay below the house in a wide bowl of land where the mountain's shoulder sloped down toward the river. It had not always been a garden. Six years ago it had been a field of grey — Vhalor's ashes spread across an acre of scorched earth, fine as flour, deep as snow, still warm in places for a full season after her death. Sef had walked out into it the first week and stood there until dark, and Pip had stood at the edge and watched, too young then to understand that grief could look like a person refusing to come inside.

But ash is only another kind of soil, Ilsabet had said, in her dry way, already turning the earth with a borrowed spade. Ash remembers being wood. Everything remembers what it was, if you give it leave to.

So they had given it leave. And the country — the old country, the true one, the one that had been erased from every map and every ledger and every official memory for forty years — had answered.

Now the bowl of the valley was green past all sense. Bean rows climbed their trellises in fat green ropes. The apple trees Sef had grafted from cuttings carried down through the door in a bundle wrapped in oilcloth were heavy this year, so heavy that Pip had propped three branches with forked sticks just yesterday. There were beds of yarrow — Yarrow, named by Pip, who had been eleven and grieving and allowed to name things however they liked — and beds of hollowroot, which grew nowhere else in the world anymore except here, in ground that had held a dragon's ending. The hollowroot bloomed white bells that rang faintly in wind, a sound like very small doors opening, and Pip had never once gotten tired of hearing it.

At the center of the garden, ringed by a low wall of fitted stone that Sef had built without mortar, in the old style, the old country's style, stood the ash-tree.

It had grown from the deepest part of the burn, where Vhalor's heart-fire had settled into the earth and cooled into something between mineral and memory. The tree was silver-barked and black-leaved, and its leaves did not fall in autumn; they glowed instead, dimly, all winter, like coals banked for morning. The village children — and there were children now, dozens of them, born free in a country that remembered itself — swore the tree whispered. Pip knew better. It didn't whisper. It listened. That was different, and anyone who had ever sat under it with something heavy in their chest knew the difference in their bones.

Pip filled the watering cans at the cistern and went down the rows, doing the morning count. Beans: sound. Apples: heavy, good. Yarrow: flowering early. Hollowroot: ringing. They counted the way Yara had taught them to count, back when — everything named, everything noted, nothing lost. A carver's habit. You keep ledgers, Yara used to say, because the world will try to make some things uncounted, and your job is to refuse.

Pip kept the garden's ledger in a little book bound in grey leather, and every entry ended the same way, in their own hand, in ink mixed with a pinch of ash: *counted, and kept.*

* * *

Sef found them at the ash-tree around midmorning. Pip heard the uneven footfall first — Sef's left knee had never forgiven the climb to the mountain pass, all those years ago, carrying a child on their back while the sky behind them filled with the Court's puppets — and then the smell of the forge, because Sef still worked metal, still made hinges and hooks and small bells, still, every spring, forged one nail and buried it at the tree's roots. Nobody asked why anymore. Everybody knew.

"Your beans are showing off," Sef said, lowering themselves onto the stone wall with the groan of a body that had argued with dragons and lost gracefully.

"They're allowed. She'd want them showing off."

Sef huffed — the laugh they made instead of laughing, most days, though they laughed properly more now than they used to — and looked out over the garden with the expression Pip had spent years learning to read. It wasn't sadness, exactly. It was the look of someone standing watch over a thing they loved, out of a habit so old it had become the shape of their soul.

"Vhalor would have eaten the beans," Sef said. "Whole plants. Pods, stakes, trellis and all."

"And then she'd have burned the outhouse."

"And then I'd have apologized to the Hendersons for the rest of my natural life." Sef closed their eyes briefly against the sun. When they opened them, they were looking at the far end of the garden — at the door.

It stood where the meadow met the tree line, half a mile off, alone in a stand of birches that had grown up around it as if the land itself wanted to shelter it. The first door. Vhalor's death-heat had forged it; Oren's ghost had passed through it; and for six years it had stood cold and grey-green, its surface weathered to look almost like bark, almost like stone, almost like nothing at all. Pilgrims came to it sometimes — people from the villages, people from across the sea, people who had lost someone to the erasure and wanted to put a hand on the place where the impossible had happened. They touched it. It stayed cold. They went home.

Every morning, without fail, Pip walked to it and laid a palm against it. Every morning it was cold as riverbed stone. Ilsabet said that was right, that was how it should be, that a door should be cold until it has a reason to be otherwise.

"Ilsabet's coming tonight," Sef said. "Bringing the new counterfeits — well." They smiled crookedly. "I suppose we don't call them counterfeits anymore. Documents. Legitimate documents, for the families still trickling through the southern passes. Forty years of erasure doesn't undo itself in six."

"She'll stay for supper?"

"She'll complain about my cooking and eat three helpings. Same as ever." Sef stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and rested a hand briefly on Pip's head — the gesture that had become, over the years, the whole of what needed saying between them. Then they paused. Their hand tightened, just slightly.

"Pip," they said quietly. "Come look at the smoke."

Pip followed their gaze to the chimney of the house, expecting trouble — a blocked flue, a spark on the thatch. But Sef wasn't looking at the chimney. They were looking past it, south, toward the high pass where the mountains folded together and the sky beyond them was the sky of the other country, the one with the counting houses and the Guild and the Court's ruins.

There was smoke rising there. Not the dirty brown of a wildfire. Not the black of burning bone.

White. Thin. Deliberate. Rising in three slow columns, spaced evenly apart, the way signal fires had been lit in the old country before the erasure — the way, Pip knew from the dream-memories that had never left them, travelers announced themselves at a border: *we come in peace, we come known, we come home.*

Pip's heart began to hammer somewhere up under their ribs, in the place where hope lived, the place they had learned to keep padded and quiet, because hope in a child who remembers erased things is a dangerous animal.

"That's not possible," Pip said. Their voice came out small.

"No," Sef agreed softly. "Probably not." And then, gruffly, hiding the tremor badly: "Go on, then. Go check the door. I'll get Ilsabet's kettle on. If we're wrong, we'll want tea. If we're right—" They didn't finish. They turned toward the house very fast, wiping at their face with a forearm, pretending to check the bean trellises on the way.

Pip ran.

* * *

They ran the half-mile through the birches with their lungs burning and the leaves strobing gold above them, and the whole time some old, patient voice in their chest kept saying *it will be cold, it will be cold, it is always cold,* and Pip ran anyway, because that was the thing about hope — you fed it even when it bit you.

The birch stand opened. The door stood among the trees.

And it was warm.

Not glowing — not yet — but warm. Pip saw it from ten paces away, saw the faint shimmer rising off its surface the way heat rises off a forge-stone at dusk, saw the grey-green weathering of six years fading like frost off a windowpane, grain by grain, revealing underneath it the carved surface that had always been there: interlocking patterns of vine and hinge and wing, a dragon's tail curled around a lintel, and along the bottom edge, small enough that you had to kneel to see it, a row of tiny figures holding hands.

Carved by hand. Carved slowly, patiently, over years. Carved by someone who kept ledgers because the world tries to make things uncounted.

Pip knelt and pressed both palms flat against the door, and the warmth soaked into them like sunlight into water, and beneath their hands they felt — not heard, felt, the way you feel a heartbeat through a floorboard — a rhythm. Steady. Patient. The rhythm of a chisel working bone, or wood, or the boundary between countries. Working. Still working. All these years, still working.

"You stubborn, wonderful, impossible person," Pip whispered, and their breath fogged on the warm wood, and they laughed, and they cried, and they did both at once because nobody had ever taught them those had to be separate things.

Behind them, footsteps — Sef, running as fast as the bad knee allowed, and further back, smaller figures, villagers, drawn by the smoke signals and the strange brightening in the air. Above the trees the light had changed. Pip couldn't have said how. It was simply that the morning had become the kind of morning that happens once in a lifetime, the kind of morning the old songs tried and failed to describe.

The door shivered.

Not moved — shivered, the way a sleeping animal shivers before waking, and along its edges the carving began to glow, a soft ember-light tracing vine and hinge and wing, and the row of small figures at the bottom lit up last of all, each one kindling in turn, left to right, like names being read aloud.

Then the door opened inward — toward the old country, toward the garden, toward home — and the air that came through it smelled of rain on counting-house stone, of lamp-oil and bone-dust and yarrow, of a city built atop a drowned country, of six years of patience and grief and work that no one had witnessed.

Yara Nystrom stood in the doorway.

They were thinner than Pip remembered, and greyer, and there was a new scar along their jaw, and their hands — their wonderful, terrible, gentle hands — were wrapped in fresh bandages stained with carving-dust. Behind them, filling the doorway's frame and spilling out into the birches like light through a cracked shutter, stood shapes. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not puppets, not shells, not the silenced things the Court had made. Voices, given form at last, the risen dead of the vault and the ledgers and the forty erased years, and every one of them was quiet — not silenced, but *settled*, the way a room settles after a storm passes, the way grief settles when it is finally, finally allowed to speak.

Oren stood nearest the door. He looked older than his ghost had ever looked, and younger too, and he was smiling the smile of a man whose delivery had finally arrived.

"Special courier," he said, and his voice was his own again, no one else's, no puppet's stolen echo. "One package. Long overdue. Signature required."

Yara stepped through the door.

Their legs nearly gave — six years on the far side of a closing world, and the crossing took something out of even the living — and Pip caught them, arms around the thin ribs, face pressed into the dust-smelling coat, and Yara's bandaged hands came up and held the back of Pip's head the way they had on the last night, the lying night, the night of steady carver's hands and a promise made of necessary falsehood.

"I told you it could be reopened," Yara murmured into their hair. "I told you I'd finish the carving."

"You're late," Pip said, which was what they had planned to say for six years, and which came out as a sob anyway.

"I know. Bone is slow. And there were—" Yara paused, looking past Pip at the garden, at the ash-tree with its banked-coal leaves, at Sef standing frozen at the garden gate with a watering can still in their hand and tears cutting clean lines through the forge-grime on their face. At the country, green and real and existing, everywhere at once. "There were a great many things to count," Yara finished softly. "I refused to lose any of them."

Sef dropped the watering can. It rolled, spilled, forgotten. They crossed the garden at a speed their knee would bill them for tomorrow and hit Yara like a landslide, and the three of them went down together into the bean rows, laughing and wrecked, and the risen dead filed gently out of the doorway and fanned across the valley like seeds on wind — going, at last, wherever the dead of a restored country go, which is everywhere, which is home.

* * *

They ate supper in the garden, all of them, the living at the long table and the dead seated among them in the manner of the old country, present and welcome and requiring no plate. Ilsabet arrived at dusk, took one look at the open door glowing among the birches, and sat down hard on the grass, and for the first time in anyone's memory, said absolutely nothing at all.

Later, when the candles were guttering and Pip was drowsing against Sef's shoulder, Yara drew them aside to the ash-tree, and pressed something into their hands.

A key. Carved of pale bone, worn smooth at the grip, warm as a held hand. Along its shaft ran a row of tiny figures holding hands — and at the end of the row, newest, brightest, a figure with a garden spade in one hand and a watering can in the other.

"The door opens both ways," Yara said. "That was always the point. But a door that opens both ways needs a keeper on each side." They looked out at the valley, at the country breathing under the stars, at the family scattered around the dying firelight. "I've held mine for six years. I'm asking if you'll hold yours."

Pip turned the key over. Counted the figures. Found their own at the end, and kept it.

"Yes," they said. "Counted, and kept."

And far away, in the birches, the door glowed steadily on — not cooling, not closing, but waiting, the way all true doors wait: patiently, warmly, for the next hand ready to build what comes after.
