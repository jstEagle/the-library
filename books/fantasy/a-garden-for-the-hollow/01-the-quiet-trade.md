## 1. The Quiet Trade

The bell above the door of Villalobos Charms & Curios had a crack in it, and Juniper had never fixed it, because a cracked bell rang half a tone flat, and a half-tone-flat bell sounded like an apology. In Corvane, where speaking power aloud was a hanging crime and even the *suggestion* of magic could earn you a cart ride to the gallows square, it paid to sound apologetic about everything.

"Morning, mistress," said the fishmonger's boy, ducking in out of the drizzle to buy a luck knot for his mother's stall. He was maybe twelve, all elbows and river smell.

"Morning, Pip. That'll be two coppers."

He counted them out with the gravity of a man settling a debt of honor, took his knot — a small tangle of blue thread Juniper had tied that morning while reciting, silently, absolutely silently, nothing at all — and paused by the door. "Does it actually work?"

"It works exactly as well as your mother believes it does," Juniper said, "and belief is worth more than copper in this city."

Pip considered this, nodded as though she'd handed him a coin instead of wisdom, and left. The cracked bell apologized behind him.

Juniper exhaled.

That was the whole of her morning trade, most days. Luck knots. Fever whispers — which were not whispers at all but willow bark and honey, sold with instructions to administer them *while murmuring something soothing*, because the murmur did no harm and the willow did the work, and if a mother wanted to believe her own voice was the medicine, who was Juniper to correct her? Dried herbs labeled in careful, boring Latin. Candle stubs "blessed" by nobody. A shelf of river stones that were just stones, though she'd arranged them in a spiral pattern so they'd *look* like they meant something, and customers bought them by the fistful.

Nothing on any shelf could hang her. She had made sure of it the way other shopkeepers made sure of their locks — nightly, obsessively, with a little prayer to whatever god handled small mercies.

Because here was the thing about Juniper Villalobos, the thing no one in Corvane knew, the thing she intended to die knowing alone if she had to: she was a swordspeaker. Not a hedge-witch, not a cunning woman with a knucklebone and a recipe. A swordspeaker — one of the Order of the Spoken Blade, sworn to the Hollow itself, the deep place beneath the world from which all true speech flowed. When Juniper spoke a word of power, doors unmade themselves. Rivers listened. The dead sat up slightly straighter.

Seven years ago there had been forty-one swordspeakers in the world.

Now there was one, and she sold river stones.

* * *

She spent the quiet hours doing inventory, which was to say she spent them not thinking. This was a skill she had refined like any other craft. Not-thinking had forms and disciplines. There was the counting form, in which she counted everything twice and let numbers be a wall. There was the mending form, in which she re-tied every luck knot in the display case whether it needed it or not, fingers busy so the mind could idle. And there was the dangerous form, the one she permitted herself only on grey afternoons when the rain kept customers home: the remembering form, in which she let herself think of the cloister at Thornfell, of forty voices rising at dawn, of her oath-sisters laughing over burnt porridge —

No. Not today. Today she counted.

The trouble with burying a gift as deep as hers was that it didn't stay buried unless you dug the grave fresh every single day. Magic like hers wasn't a thing you had; it was a thing you *were*, and it pressed upward through the soil of her like a crocus in February, patient, certain, absurdly hopeful. Some mornings she woke with a word already halfway up her throat — a word that would have lit the stove without coal, or called the rain off the washing, or simply *named* the day and made it mean something — and she had to swallow it, physically swallow it, like a fishbone going down wrong.

Seven years of swallowing fishbones.

"You could stop," said the small voice in her head, the one that sounded annoyingly like her old teacher, Magistra Corvel, who had been dead seven years and still found time to be right about things. "You could speak one word. Just one. See what happens."

"And what happens is the gallows, Magistra," Juniper muttered into her tea. "Every time. It's always the gallows. You'd think after four hundred repetitions you'd remember the ending."

The tea didn't answer. The tea was just tea. Everything in her life was just what it was, and that was the entire architecture of her survival.

* * *

The wardens came at midday, as they always did on the second Tuesday of the month, because routine inspections were only useful if they were routine, and terror ran best on a schedule.

There were three of them. The tall one was Warden Hesk, who had been inspecting her shop for six years and had never once found anything, and who Juniper privately suspected knew it and came anyway for the warmth and the smell of cinnamon candles. The short one was new — Warden Pell, young, earnest, with the hungry eyes of a man who wanted to find something and would settle for finding nothing loudly. The third was a woman, Warden Ashe, who said almost nothing and missed almost nothing, and whom Juniper watched the way a rabbit watches a hawk-shaped cloud: constantly, casually, with her heart doing arithmetic.

"Afternoon, Mistress Villalobos." Hesk tipped his hat at the exact angle of a man performing friendliness for an audience. "Routine."

"Of course it is, Warden. Can I offer you tea? I've got willow bark if anyone's feverish." A beat. "Feverish people come in sometimes. For the whispers."

Pell wrote that down. Juniper watched him write it down and felt the particular exhaustion of being interesting against her will.

They moved through the shop the way they always did. Hesk lifted the candle stubs and put them down. Pell examined the river stones with the intensity of a man expecting one of them to confess. Ashe drifted along the herb shelves, reading labels, and stopped at the fever-whisper jar.

"This is honey," she said.

"With willow bark steeped in it," Juniper agreed. "The whispering's free."

Ashe looked at her. Juniper looked back with the face she wore for inspections — open, pleasant, faintly dim, the face of a woman whose most dangerous thought was about rent. It was a good face. She'd practiced it in a shard of mirror for a month until it stopped feeling like wearing someone else's skin.

"The murmur," Ashe said. "In the instructions. What do you tell them to murmur?"

"Whatever comforts them. 'Hush now.' 'You're safe.' 'Mama's here.'" Juniper shrugged. "Words are cheap, Warden. That's rather my point. If words could do anything, I'd be out of business, wouldn't I? Everyone would just talk themselves well."

A silence. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, and the cracked bell trembled on its hook without ringing.

"That's the law's position exactly," Ashe said at last, and wrote nothing down.

They finished in eleven minutes. Hesk bought a luck knot — "for the wife, she worries" — and overpaid by a copper and pretended he hadn't, and Pell knocked over a basket of dried lavender and apologized so fervently that Juniper almost felt bad for him, this boy who had joined the silencing out of conviction and was discovering that mostly it meant smelling herbs. At the door, Ashe paused.

"Quiet street," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Quietest in Corvane," Juniper said. "That's why the rent's cheap."

Ashe held her gaze one second longer than comfortable, then left. The bell apologized. Juniper stood very still until the wardens' boots faded around the corner, then walked to the door, turned the sign to CLOSED, and leaned her forehead against the wood.

Eleven minutes. Twice a month, plus the random ones, plus the festivals when the Crown got nervous, plus the nights she woke at the sound of footsteps that turned out to be cats. Eleven minutes of being a dull woman selling dull things, and then the rest of the day, every day, being the last of something, alone, in a city that would kill her for the truth of her.

"I'm fine," she told the door, in the ordinary way, meaning none of it. "This is fine. This is a life. Half of one. A life's worth of life at half volume."

The door, being a door, declined to argue.

* * *

She reopened at two and sold a fever whisper to a grandmother, three river stones to a bride ("for the threshold, dear, grooms trip"), and a luck knot to a man who was clearly a smuggler and clearly terrified and clearly not worth reporting, because everyone in Corvane was smuggling something, even if it was only hope across the border of their own despair.

By dusk the rain had come on properly — the autumn kind, the kind that fell as though it had a grudge. Juniper closed early, banked the stove, and allowed herself the remembering form after all, because grey evenings were the appointed time and discipline was discipline.

She thought of Thornfell. Of dawn-speech, forty voices braided into one word that made the orchards bloom weeks early. Of Sarel — no. Not tonight. Tonight she thought of the others, the safe ones, the dead ones, and let grief sit in her chest like a stone she'd grown around, the way an oyster does its work.

The rain thickened. The lamp guttered. Corvane went about its silenced evening, ten thousand hearths burning, ten thousand households keeping their words small and warm and harmless, and above it all the sky pressed down like a lid on a pot.

And then, somewhere between the last light and the lamplighting, Juniper heard a sound she had not heard in seven years.

Not a word. Nothing so obvious. A *shape* of a word — a rhythm under the rain, brief as a struck bell, gone before her mind could pretend she hadn't heard it. It came from nowhere and everywhere, the way First Speech did, the way the Hollow spoke when it spoke through things: through rivers, through roots, through the bones of old stones.

Her wrist burned.

She looked down. Under three layers of sleeve-wrap and a leather cuff she wore summer and winter, the oath-mark — the blade-and-briar sigil inked into her skin the day she swore — had woken. It glowed faintly through the leather, gold-green, like foxfire, like the light off deep water, and it pulsed once, twice, in time with that vanished syllable.

"No," Juniper said aloud, uselessly, in plain trade-tongue, to herself, to the mark, to the night. "No, no. Seven years. You don't get to—"

The pulse came again, stronger. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, threaded through the drumming rain like a single silver wire pulled through grey wool:

Someone was speaking. Out there, in the storm, in the silenced city, someone was speaking the oldest language in the world.

Juniper stood in her dark shop with her heart slamming and her oath-mark burning like a brand, and every buried year of her rose up at once, forty voices, one voice, the Hollow's long silence cracking like river ice in spring.

Then came the knock.

Three small knocks at her door. Soft. Patient. The kind of knock a child makes when knocking is the only thing left to try.

Juniper crossed the shop in four strides and opened the door onto the rain — and standing in it, soaked to the bone, barefoot, looking up at her with eyes the color of deep water, was a child of perhaps eight years old.

The child said one word. Only one. But it was in the First Speech, the tongue of the Hollow itself, the language Juniper had sworn she would never hear again, and it went through her like a blade finding the old scar it had been forged from.

The mark on her wrist flared white.

"Well," Juniper whispered, in the ordinary tongue, to the child, to the rain, to the seven careful years collapsing quietly around her. "Come in out of the wet, then. Quickly now. Before the whole city hears what you just said."
