## 2. A Knock in Old Tongue

The child stepped over the threshold the way water steps over a stone—without hesitation, without weight, as if doors had never once been an obstacle worth considering. She left a trail of rain across Juniper's clean floor and stood dripping in the middle of the shop, small and grave and utterly unbothered, looking at the shelves of luck knots and fever charms with the expression of someone auditing a very bad library.

Juniper shut the door behind her, threw both bolts, and leaned against it.

Seven years. Seven years of careful living, of luck knots tied with deliberate clumsiness so they'd hold no more power than a grandmother's blessing, of fever whispers that were just words said kindly into a hot ear, of a whole life built to be too dull to hang. Seven years, and now there was a child standing on her floor who had spoken one syllable of the First Speech loud enough to make her oath-mark burn like a coal sewn under her skin.

"You can't do that," Juniper said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You can't say things like that. Not here. Not anywhere in Corvane, do you understand me? There are people whose entire job is listening for exactly that sound, and when they find it, they put a rope around the neck of whoever made it."

The child looked up at her. She was perhaps eight, perhaps younger—the kind of age that was hard to pin down because hunger and rain had been at work on her for a while. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, someone's knife-work rather than anyone's care. Big eyes, gray as river fog. She was thin in the way that suggested she'd been thin for longer than any child ought to be thin for anything.

And she did not look frightened. That was the strangest part. Juniper had just threatened her, more or less, with a hanging, and the child regarded her with the mild, patient attention of a cat watching a door it intended to open eventually anyway.

"I don't know what you are," Juniper said, softer now. "But you'd better be worth dying for."

The child tilted her head, considering this, and then pointed one small finger at the mark on Juniper's wrist, where the sleeve had ridden up during the bolt-throwing. The oath-mark showed dark against her skin even now—a line of script in no alphabet any kingdom used, coiling twice around the wrist like a serpent that had decided to stay. It had been inked in silence and blood on the day of her swearing, and it had been quiet for seven years. Dormant as a stone. Dead as her order.

It was still glowing. Faintly, sullenly, like embers refusing a doused fire, but glowing.

The child said something else.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the First Speech: it does not work like language works. Ordinary words sit on top of meaning like paint on wood. The First Speech *was* meaning, poured directly into the ear, and every syllable carried its own truth whether the speaker wanted it or not. A swordspeaker could lie in the common tongue all day—that was half the survival—but in First Speech, a lie was impossible. The tongue simply refused.

Juniper hadn't heard it spoken aloud since the Night of Silencing. She had heard it screamed that night. She had heard it die.

So when the child opened her mouth and spoke again—that same impossible syllable, and then three more after it, gentle as rain finding a window—it was not like hearing a language. It was like being called by name by someone long buried.

*The door was heavy,* the words meant. *The door was sad. I am sorry your wrist hurts.*

"That's—" Juniper's voice cracked and she had to start again. "That's not an answer to anything I asked."

The child shrugged. In First Speech, apparently, shrugging could carry meaning too, because Juniper understood it perfectly: *I answered what mattered.*

She pushed wet hair out of her eyes with the back of her hand and looked around the shop again, and this time she looked at it properly—at the bundles of dried herbs labeled in Juniper's careful merchant's hand, at the horse-shoe charms that were genuinely just horseshoes, at the little drawer of "blessed buttons" that Juniper sold to grandmothers at two coppers each and which were, in fact, blessed, but only in the way that anything is blessed when you say something warm over it and mean it. Harmless. All of it harmless. That had been the whole design of her life.

The child looked at all this counterfeit quiet and then back at Juniper, and her expression did something complicated. If Juniper had to name it—and she would rather not have had to—it looked like recognition. Like grief. Like someone walking through a house they used to live in and finding all the furniture wrong.

Then the child's stomach growled. Loudly. With real, ordinary, mortal hunger, the kind that had nothing to do with covenants or ancient tongues, and the solemnity broke for just a moment into something young and embarrassed.

"Oh," Juniper said. And felt something in her chest come loose, seven years rusted shut. "Well, that we can do something about."

* * *

She fed her, because that was the first thing you did. It was what Odessa would have said—feed them first, ask questions second, because nobody ever thought clearly hungry, and most people never thought clearly full either. Juniper heated the tail end of last night's stew and cut bread that was only slightly past its best and set it down in front of the child, who ate the way small children eat when they have known real want: carefully, completely, wasting nothing, one eye always on whether more might be coming.

"There's more," Juniper told her. "Eat slowly anyway. Your belly will thank you."

The child ate slowly anyway. The stove ticked. Rain worked at the windows with ten thousand small fingers. Juniper sat across from her with her untouched tea and watched the oath-mark dim by slow degrees, settling from ember to glow to something merely warm, and thought about all the reasons she should march this child straight out into the storm and bar the door behind her.

She counted them on her fingers, internally. One: whatever spoke the First Speech belonged to the Hollow, and everything belonging to the Hollow had been declared an enemy of the Crown, and harboring an enemy of the Crown was a hanging offense with extras. Two: the wardens came through this district every third day, and tomorrow was a third day. Three: she had spent seven years building a life so small and so gray that even she sometimes struggled to find herself inside it, and this child was a bonfire thrown through the window of it.

Four—and this was the one she kept stumbling over—there had been a prophecy. Not a grand parchment-and-trumpets prophecy; her order had never gone in for those. Just an old teaching, murmured by old mouths: *When the Hollow has need, it will send what it has left, and it will be small, and you will know it by the truth in its mouth.*

Small. Check. Truth in its mouth. Check, check, check, every word out of this child landing in Juniper's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"Who sent you?" Juniper asked quietly, in the common tongue, knowing the question was useless before she finished asking it.

The child swallowed her bread and answered anyway, and the First Speech moved through the kitchen-warm room like light through water:

*Nobody sent me. I walked. The road was long and my feet complained. Then the road ended here, where you are.*

"Where I am," Juniper repeated. "Why here? Of all the doors in Corvane—"

The child frowned, concentrating, and Juniper realized with a lurch that she was trying to explain something large with a language that admitted no vagueness, and that the effort of it tired her visibly. What she finally managed was simple, and it stopped Juniper's heart with the accuracy of an arrow:

*Because you still smell of the garden. The others lost the smell. You didn't.*

The garden. Every swordspeaker knew about the garden—not a place you could walk to, but the shape the Hollow took when it appeared in dreams, endless and green and going gold at the edges, full of paths that rearranged themselves kindly. Juniper had dreamed in that garden every night of her training. She had assumed, these seven years, that she'd been locked out forever.

"You've been there," Juniper whispered. "You've been *inside.*"

The child didn't answer. Instead her gaze drifted sideways, toward the shuttered window, and her whole body went still in a new way—not calm anymore but *listening*, ears-of-a-deer still, and Juniper felt the temperature of the room change.

Because outside, faint under the rain, came the sound of boots. More than two pairs. Moving unhurried along the lane, the particular unhurried pace of people being paid to be thorough.

* * *

Juniper crossed the shop floor without breathing and put her eye to the shutter-slit.

Three wardens in oilcloth cloaks, lanterns hooded against the rain, hunched together at the corner of Weaver's Row. Checking doorways. One of them raised his fist and knocked at the chandler's across the lane—routine, probably, the sort of knock that ended in a nod and a move-along. Probably.

Probably was the unit of currency Juniper had been living on for seven years, and she had learned never to spend more than she had.

Behind her, the child had drawn back into the shadow between shelf and wall, silent as furniture, and Juniper noticed that she had done it instinctively, beautifully, the way a soldier falls into stance. Someone had trained this child, or something had. Something had taught a girl of eight how to disappear in a stranger's shop while wardens knocked on doors down the street.

"All right," Juniper breathed, more to herself than anyone. "All right. Think."

If the wardens knocked, they would find a charm-shop keeper and a mute child she'd taken in out of the storm—charitable, foolish, entirely in character for the woman who sold lucky buttons. That story might hold. It might hold if the child stayed mute, and the child seemed incapable of speech in anything but a language that would get them both hanged before the questions finished.

Unless.

Juniper crouched in front of her, close enough to see the rain still beaded in her eyelashes, and held up one finger.

"Listen to me. Those men outside—they mustn't hear you. Whatever happens, whatever they say to you, however frightened you get—you cannot speak your words aloud. Not one. Can you keep quiet?"

The child looked at her for a long moment. Then, deliberately, she reached out and took Juniper's marked wrist in both small cold hands, turned it over, and pressed her thumb flat against the coiled script.

The mark flared—warm, not painful this time—and the child spoke, soft as breath, directly into Juniper's skin, and the words rose up through bone and blood instead of air, so that only Juniper heard them, intimate as her own heartbeat:

*I know how to be silent. I was born in the silence. It is speaking that is new to me.*

And underneath that, arriving a half-second later like a wave behind its ripple, another truth, smaller and heavier, the kind of thing a child says without understanding it is enormous:

*You are frightened. But you smell of the garden even when you're frightened. It is a good smell. I will stand behind your fear with you.*

Juniper closed her eyes for exactly one second. One second to mourn the quiet life, the gray life, the safe and small and suffocating life. Then she opened them.

The knock came at 10:43 by the church bell, three measured raps, and Juniper smoothed her face into the mild bored mask of a shopkeeper interrupted and unbolted the door.

"Warden Hask." She knew his name; he'd inspected her twice a year for five years and enjoyed the knowing. Rain blew in around him, and behind him his two colleagues stood in the lane with their lanterns hooded, and beyond them the night was very black and very full of consequences. "Bit late for button sales."

"Storm brought us round early," Hask said. His eyes went past her shoulder into the shop, warden-trained, cataloguing. "Heard anything strange tonight? Sounds out of place?"

"Only the thunder," said Juniper Villalobos, who had lied to worse men on worse nights, "and the thunder's been rude all evening."

Hask almost smiled. Almost. Then his gaze snagged, just briefly, on the small damp shape standing in the shadows by the herb shelf, and Juniper felt the whole night balance on its edge like a coin.

"A child," Hask said. Not a question. Wardens never asked questions; they stated facts until the facts confessed something.

"My sister's girl," said Juniper, smooth as poured cream, and marveled distantly at how easily the old skill came back, how the lie fit her mouth like a sword fits a hand. "Mute since birth, poor mite. Deaf as a post too, mostly. Come in out of the rain an hour ago and I couldn't turn her back into it."

Something flickered across Hask's face. Pity, maybe, or the memory of a report he'd once read, or—she could not tell—suspicion. Behind him, lightning stitched the sky white, and in the flash Juniper saw, imprinted for an instant on the wet cobbles of the lane, the shimmer of many footprints leading away down the alley behind her shop. Small footprints. Bare ones. Leading *away*, though the child had come in the front and never left—

—and in the same instant, pressed warm against her back where the child had silently moved to hide, she felt small hands grip her skirts, and heard, felt through cloth and bone and blood, one final truth, offered like a gift, like a key, like a lit fuse:

*They cannot follow me unless I wish it. But the ones who hunt me do not need to follow. They can smell the garden too. And one of them smells of it the way you do—old, and sad, and familiar. She is coming, swordspeaker. She is already coming.*

The mark on Juniper's wrist burned white-hot, and Warden Hask, blinking against the rain, said: "Your hand. What's wrong with your hand?"
