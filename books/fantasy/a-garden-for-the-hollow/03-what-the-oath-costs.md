## 3. What the Oath Costs

The child slept in the chair by the cold hearth, and Juniper did not sleep at all.

She had tried. She had banked the fire to nothing, drawn the shutters, sat on her own bed with her boots off like a person who intended, in good faith, to rest. But every time she closed her eyes she heard it again—that small clear voice speaking a language that had no business existing anymore, and her wrist burned under its wrappings like a coal someone had breathed on.

*You could have turned her out,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had kept her alive for seven years. *A mute child at your door in a storm. Wardens would have found her by morning. It would have been cleaner.*

But the child had looked up at her with eyes the color of deep water over stone, and had spoken, and the words had gone through Juniper like a blade going through wet paper—no resistance at all, because there was nothing left in her trained to resist them.

She got up. She lit a single candle and carried it to the chair, and stood over the sleeping girl, and let herself look properly for the first time.

Small. Thin in the way of children who have walked farther than they should have. Mud to the knee, hair like a crow's nest after a bad winter. Ordinary, except for the ordinary things being wrong: no chilblains on bare feet that had clearly walked through snowmelt, no shivering despite the cold room, and—Juniper leaned closer, heart beginning its slow sick drop—a faint luminescence under the skin of her throat, like moonlight held in milk.

The oath-mark on Juniper's wrist pulsed once, twice, keeping time with the child's breathing.

"Oh, you're not a child at all," Juniper whispered. "Or you're not *only* one."

The girl's eyes opened. Not startled—she had been awake the whole time, Juniper realized. Watching her back.

Then the small mouth moved, and the First Speech came out of it, soft as water over gravel, and Juniper's whole body went rigid, because she understood every word, and the words were:

*"Your candle is shaking."*

Juniper looked down. The candle was, in fact, shaking.

"I don't—" She stopped. Swallowed. Seven years of discipline told her to say nothing, say nothing, say *nothing*, but the discipline was fraying like everything else frayed these days, and what came out instead was hoarse and human: "What are you?"

The child considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she answered, and here was the thing about First Speech, the thing they had taught the novices first, before anything else: *in the Hollow's tongue, you cannot lie.* The language simply refused it. A lie spoken in First Speech curdled on the tongue, turned to smoke, meant nothing. Every word was true or it was not spoken at all.

*"I am Wren,"* the child said. *"I was stolen. I ran away. I am hungry."*

Three sentences. Three truths. And then, tilting her head like a bird regarding something shiny and slightly alarming:

*"You are afraid. You were brave before. You are sorry about something very large."*

"I think," said Juniper Villalobos, last swordspeaker of an order that no longer existed, "that you should go back to sleep."

*"That is not true,"* said Wren placidly, and closed her eyes, and was asleep again within moments, breathing slow, throat glowing faintly, while Juniper stood in the dark holding a trembling candle and feeling seven years of careful burial begin, all at once, to heave upward like a drowned thing rising toward light.

* * *

She made tea she didn't drink and sat across from the sleeping child and remembered.

It always started the same way: with the smell of rain on hot stone.

The cloister at Vael's Rest had been built over a spring, and in summer storms the whole courtyard smelled of rain striking warm rock, and the novices used to say you could smell the Hollow thinking. Juniper had been twenty-three, newly sworn, and she had believed—with the easy, unexamined belief of the young—that the world would go on needing swordspeakers forever.

Because that was the covenant. That was the whole shape of it, simple as bread and salt: the Hollow was the deep place beneath the world where speech had been born, the first voice, the wellspring. Everything humans said drew, however thinly, on that source. And most speech was just speech—words spent and gone. But some people were born with a deeper draught of it, born able to *speak power aloud*: to say *open* and have doors obey, to say *heal* and watch flesh remember itself, to speak a sword awake so that it sang and cut like dawn.

Swordspeakers. That had been the order's old name, from the wars, though by Juniper's time they were healers and wardens of thresholds and keepers of oaths more than soldiers. Their gift was bound by vow: never to speak power in anger, never to chain the Hollow's gift into service, never to take more than they gave. In exchange, the Hollow spoke through them, and the covenant between the two realms held—the deep place fed the upper world its language, and the upper world kept faith.

And then the Crown had decided that power which could not be owned was power that must be destroyed.

Juniper had been in the eastern dormitory the night it happened. She remembered absurd details: a half-mended cloak over her chair. Sister Maren's snoring through the wall, rhythmic as surf. The smell of rain on hot stone.

She remembered the alarm—not a bell, because the order had no bells; their alarm was a word, *the word of gathering*, spoken by the sentinel on the wall—and she remembered that the word never came. What came instead was screaming, and the smell of pitch, and torchlight crawling up the inside of the shutters like something alive.

She remembered running out into the courtyard with her sword unsheathed and unspoken—dormant, dumb steel, because she had not yet learned to wake it—and seeing the Cloister Mother standing alone on the steps with thirty wardens closing in, and hearing the old woman say, in a voice pitched to carry, in plain trade-tongue so there could be no mistaking it:

"Put down your steel, children. Tonight we are not permitted to be dangerous."

They had been given the choice, afterward. That was the part Juniper could never make anyone understand, the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed stone. There was no mass execution. There was a *choice*. Renounce the gift, take the Crown's quieting—iron bands that sealed the voice's depths forever, leaving you alive and hollowed—or refuse, and hang.

Most renounced. She could still see them kneeling in rows in the mud, still see the smiths coming down the line with their iron, still hear the sound the quieting made—not a scream, worse, a *sigh*, each sister exhaling something that would never come back.

Some refused. Sister Maren, who snored like surf, spat at the Inquisitor's feet and died with her chin up. Old Brother Callow recited the covenant's first verse as they put the rope on him, and half the wardens wept, and it changed nothing.

And Juniper—

Juniper had run.

Not knelt, not refused, not fought. Run. Through the postern gate she'd known since childhood, down the cliff path in the rain, with her oath burning on her wrist and her sword silent on her hip, while behind her the cloister burned and her family died or went quiet one by one. She had run because in the moment they put the choice to her, some animal thing in her guts had looked at death and chosen otherwise, and she had hated herself for it every single day since, with a hatred so practiced it had worn smooth, almost comfortable, like a river stone she carried in her mouth.

Seven years. Seven years of luck knots and fever whispers and charm-work shallow enough to pass inspection, of flinching at warden boots on cobblestones, of a life half-lived in a city that had outlawed her voice. She had told herself it was survival. She had told herself the order needed a remnant, that someone had to carry the covenant's memory. All true, probably. Also true: she had run, and she had kept running, and the running had become the whole architecture of her days.

The drowned thing rose another foot.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the shutter seams. Wren woke like a cat—all at once, no transition—and regarded Juniper across the dead hearth with those deep-water eyes.

Juniper had decided, somewhere around the fourth hour of not sleeping, what she had to do. It was a terrible plan. It was the only plan.

"You're hungry," she said. "You said so. It was true, apparently, the way everything you say is true."

*"Bread,"* Wren agreed, with sudden and devastating sincerity. *"Warm bread. With the crackly outside."*

Despite everything—despite the dread sitting in her stomach like a fist—Juniper felt her mouth twitch. "Crackly outside. You have opinions about crust."

*"All bread is a promise,"* said Wren, as if explaining rain to a fool. *"The crackly kind keeps it best."*

"Well. I know exactly one person in Corvane who can be trusted with a promise like that, and visiting her is going to be either the smartest or the stupidest thing I've done in seven years." Juniper crouched to the child's level, and made herself hold the deep-water gaze. "Before we go anywhere, I need you to understand something. Out there"—she nodded toward the shuttered window, toward the city beyond it—"there are men whose whole work is listening. If they hear what you are, if they hear what *I* was, we both hang. So you cannot speak your language where anyone can hear. Can you do that? Can you stay silent?"

Wren considered the question with terrible thoroughness. Then she shook her head.

"No?" Juniper's stomach dropped. "Wren, this isn't—"

*"Staying silent is a lie,"* the child said simply. *"I am full of words. They want to come out. Pretending they don't would not be true."* She touched her own glowing throat, matter-of-fact as a child reporting a scraped knee. *"But I can make them small. Whisper-small. If you carry me close, they will stay whisper-small."*

"And if they don't?"

*"Then they come out,"* said Wren, with the serene honesty of her impossible tongue, *"and something true happens, and truth is loud."*

Juniper sat back on her heels. Somewhere in the walls of Corvane, she thought, there were men paid to listen, and the last living anchor of the Hollow was going to walk through their city announcing herself in whispers, because lying was against her nature the way flight was against a stone's.

"You," she said, "are going to be the death of me."

*"No,"* said Wren, and frowned slightly, and corrected herself with care, the way one does when being precise matters: *"Something else will be the death of you. Something far north. But not today, and not me, and not the hanging."*

The room went very quiet. Rain ticked against the shutters. On Juniper's wrist, under its wrappings, the oath-mark burned like a brand fresh from the forge.

"What did you just say?"

But Wren had already turned away, nose lifted, following the ghost of some scent only she could find, and when she answered she was speaking of bread again, of warm bread with the crackly outside, of a woman who smelled of yeast and secrets three streets east—and she would say nothing more about the far north, or the death, no matter how Juniper asked, because the question Juniper was asking was the wrong shape, and First Speech only answered questions asked truly.

Which left Juniper standing in her little shop full of harmless charms, with a glowing-throated child tugging at her sleeve, knowing two things with absolute certainty: that she could not stay in Corvane another hour, and that Odessa Brann owed her a debt large enough to buy one day of shelter—one day in which Juniper would have to decide whether she was still the woman who ran.

The drowned thing had surfaced now. It had her name in its mouth.

She blew out the candle, though the room was already full of gray light, and reached for her cloak.
