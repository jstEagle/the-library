## 4. The Baker Who Remembers

The rain had followed them for three streets, patient as a debt collector, and Juniper had stopped pretending it wasn't an omen.

She kept Wren's hood up with one hand and her own head down with the other, threading through the morning market crowd the way water threads through cracks—present everywhere, noticed nowhere. The child moved like a small ghost at her side, bare feet silent on wet cobbles even though Juniper had bought her boots two days ago. The boots were somewhere behind them now. Wren had taken them off at the mouth of Tanner's Row and would not say why, because Wren could not say why, and Juniper was beginning to understand that the child's silences were not empty. They were full. They were so full they overflowed out of her eyes sometimes, and last night she had cried without sound at all, which was somehow worse than any crying Juniper had ever heard.

*Brann's Bakery, Candlemaker's Lane,* the note had said, tucked into a sack of flour Juniper had purchased every week for seven years from a woman who never once asked why Juniper always chose the sack nearest the wall. *If ever you need a door that doesn't ask questions, mine is the one that smells like Thursday bread.*

Odessa Brann owed her a debt. It was not a debt of coin. Coin was easy; coin could be counted and forgotten. This was the other kind—the kind that sat in a person's chest like a swallowed coal, warm and uncomfortable, until the day it was called in.

Juniper had spent seven years hoping that day would never come, because calling in debts meant admitting you needed help, and needing help, in Corvane, was how people ended up swinging from the Hookmarket gallows with their tongues publicly noted as *the cause of death.*

But last night Wren had slept sitting upright against the shop's back wall, knees drawn up, and in her sleep she had spoken—not First Speech this time, but something older, something that made the candle flames lean toward her like flowers toward sun—and Juniper had watched the wax pool into shapes that looked, unmistakably, like open hands.

The Hollow wanted its anchor back. And things that wanted things in Corvane eventually sent wardens to collect.

"Stay close," Juniper murmured, which was a waste of breath, because Wren stayed closer than close. The child had developed a habit of gripping two fingers of Juniper's hand when they walked, not hard, just anchored, as if Juniper might dissolve into the rain if she didn't hold on. Juniper had not held hands with anyone in seven years. She had not known, until now, how much of her body remembered how.

Candlemaker's Lane smelled of tallow and smoke and, beneath it, rising like a promise, the smell of bread.

Odessa Brann's bakery squatted at the lane's end like a fat brown hen, steam pluming from its chimney into the grey morning, windows fogged golden from within. A hand-painted sign swung above the door: *BRANN'S — BREAD FOR THE HUNGRY AND THE JUST PLAIN GREEDY.* Someone had recently added, in different paint, smaller: *NO REFUNDS ON CHARACTER.*

Juniper stood across the lane for a long moment, rain running down her collar, and made herself look at the door. Just a door. Oak, iron-banded, painted green once upon a time. Doors were safe. Doors were just doors.

Except that two nights ago she had watched one unmake itself at a word, and except that the child beside her was a living threshold, and except that every instinct Juniper had spent seven years sharpening was screaming at her that walking through any door today was a mistake.

Wren tugged her fingers. Once. Gently. The way you'd tug the sleeve of someone standing too long at a grave.

"All right," Juniper said. "All right."

* * *

The bell over the bakery door had been muffled with wool—a hedge-witch's habit, deadening sound, keeping the room's business its own—and so their entrance made no announcement, just a soft shush of warmth and the smell of yeast and woodsmoke closing over them like a blanket.

The woman behind the counter was built like her ovens: broad, blackened at the edges, radiating heat. Odessa Brann had flour in her eyebrows and forearms like ship's cable, and when she saw Juniper her face did three things in quick succession—recognition, alarm, and then a carefully arranged blankness that was itself an answer.

"Morning," Odessa said loudly, for the benefit of the two customers hunched over porridge by the window. "Loaf? We've got Thursday bread, Friday bread, and if you're feeling ambitious, the seed loaf what ruined my back."

"I need—" Juniper began.

"*Thursday* bread," Odessa said, louder still, "comes with a free consultation. Back room. Family rate." She was already untying her apron. "Marta! Counter!"

A girl of perhaps twelve emerged from the flour-dust depths, wiping her hands. Odessa pressed the counter into her custody with the gravity of a general surrendering a city, then jerked her chin toward the back and marched, leaving Juniper no choice but to follow, Wren's fingers tightening around hers.

The back room was low-ceilinged and crammed: sacks of grain stacked like fortifications, a wash basin, a table scarred by forty years of knives, and a window so thick with grime it turned daylight the color of weak tea. Odessa shut the door, stood with her ear against it for a count of ten, then turned around and let her face do whatever it wanted.

It wanted, mostly, to be afraid.

"Seven years," she said. "Seven years you buy your flour and never once come past the counter, and now you walk in with a child I can *feel* from here, Juniper Villalobos, and you have the nerve to look surprised that I'm frightened."

"You can feel her?"

"I was a hedge-witch before the Silencing, girl, not a houseplant. My gifts got pruned down to nubs, but nubs still itch." Odessa crossed her arms, and her arms crossing was like watching a drawbridge go up. "That child hums. Like a struck bell that won't finish ringing. You bring a thing like that into my bakery, into my *city*, and every ear in Corvane that's ever been touched by the old ways is going to hear it sooner or later."

"That's why I came." Juniper's voice came out lower than she intended. She made herself stand straight under Odessa's stare. "I need shelter for her. A few days. Maybe more. Somewhere the wardens don't look."

"And why wouldn't they look here?"

"Because you owe me."

The words dropped between them like a stone into a well. Odessa's jaw worked. Outside, muffled through walls and years, the city went about its morning: cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, the ordinary music of the unfrightened.

"I do," Odessa admitted at last, heavily. "I do owe you. Fever took my Marta when she was six. Not the warden's fever—the real kind, the kind that burns a child hollow in three days. And you came at midnight with a fever whisper and sat by her bed till dawn saying words soft enough they barely counted as sound, and by morning the burning was gone and you never told me what it cost you." Her eyes narrowed. "What did it cost you, Juniper?"

"A night's sleep."

"Liar."

"A great deal of night's sleep."

Odessa snorted. Then the snort collapsed into something wearier. She looked past Juniper, at the small hooded figure who had stationed herself against the grain sacks with the stillness of a bird deciding whether the cat was asleep.

"This her?" Odessa said quietly. "This the one making the city itch?"

"She can't speak. Not our tongue. Only—" Juniper hesitated. Saying it aloud, even here, felt like striking flint in a powder store. "Only the First Speech."

Odessa went very still. Flour drifted off her forearm and hung in the air a half-second longer than flour should.

"No," she said.

"Yes."

"The Hollow-tongue. The speech before speech. The thing they burned Master Aldous over, and he only ever *wrote about* it." Odessa pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I retired, Juniper. I hung up my herbs and married a man with a bad back and good heart and I make *bread*. Bread is honest. Bread rises or it doesn't and nobody hangs you for it."

"I know."

"There's a boy," Odessa said abruptly, changing the subject with the grace of a cart changing ruts. "Tam. He'll be underfoot. He's—well, he's mine, near enough. Found him picking pockets in the Hookmarket when he was six, quick as a weasel and twice as hungry. He don't talk much either, but that's because he's listening. Always listening, that one." She sighed, a sound like a banked fire settling. "You'll stay in the loft above the ovens. Warm, dry, one ladder, one exit. Nobody comes up here but family, and nobody asks family questions."

"Odessa—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking makes it real." She pointed a flour-white finger at Juniper. "And hear me plain, since you've made me say the rest plain: that child's arrival was *felt*. Whatever woke her, whatever sent her knocking on your door—it rang through this city like a crack in a bell. There are ears in Corvane that exist solely to listen for cracks. When they find her, and they will find her, they will also find everyone who helped her. So understand what you're asking me to stitch my name into."

Juniper understood. She had understood since the moment she'd opened her door on a rain-lashed night and seen a child standing in the lightning with words on her lips that should have died seven years ago.

"I'm asking anyway," she said.

Odessa looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached out and gripped Juniper's shoulder with a hand that could have cracked walnuts, and her voice, when it came, was rough as bran.

"Aye. Well. Debt's a debt." She turned to the door. "Come on, then, little hummingbird. Let's get you fed before you fade away to nothing, and gods know what my Tam will make of you."

* * *

What Tam made of Wren was, within the hour, apparent: he adored her.

He was eleven, maybe twelve—all elbows and watchfulness, with a shock of black hair he cut himself, badly, and a smudge of soot on his nose that seemed less like dirt than decoration. He materialized in the loft that afternoon while Juniper was showing Wren where the blankets were, appearing out of the chimney-shadow so suddenly that Juniper's hand went to her knife before her brain caught up.

"Easy," said the boy, palms out. "Just checking the new stock."

"I'm not stock."

"Everyone's stock. Question's whether you're the spending kind or the keeping kind." He crouched, studying Wren with the frank appraisal of a merchant examining goods. Wren studied him back. For a long moment neither moved. Then Wren reached out and, with grave deliberation, touched the soot smudge on his nose.

Tam went cross-eyed looking at it. Then he grinned—a sudden, crooked, entirely disarming grin—and Juniper felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was clenched.

"She's all right," he announced to the room, to the world, to Juniper specifically. "She's definitely the keeping kind."

"She can't talk," Juniper said. "Not properly."

"So?" Tam shrugged, producing from somewhere inside his coat a battered wooden top, a bent nail, and half a honeycomb, laying them out on the floorboards like treasure. "Talking's overrated. Most of what people say is lies anyway. I can tell." He tapped his temple. "Faces leak."

Wren knelt beside the treasures. She picked up the top, turned it over, and set it down again with the care of someone handling an egg. Then she looked at Tam and pointed at the honeycomb, then at herself, then raised her eyebrows.

"That's mine," Tam said instantly.

Wren pointed at the honeycomb, then at him, then mimed, with heartbreaking precision, breaking it in two.

Tam stared at her. Juniper watched him work through it—the calculation, the resistance, the slow collapse. "...Half," he negotiated. "You get the sticky end."

Wren nodded solemnly, and Tam broke the honeycomb, and that, apparently, was that. By evening the two of them were established on the loft floor in a circle of candlelight, playing a game that involved the top, the bent nail, and a system of gestures so elaborate and so obviously invented on the spot that Juniper gave up trying to follow it. Wren laughed twice—silent laughs, her shoulders shaking, her mouth open on sound that never came—and each time, the candle flames stood up straight and tall, and the shadows in the corners leaned gently inward, as if the darkness itself had drawn closer to listen.

Juniper sat on the ladder's bottom rung, pretending to mend a cloak, and watched them, and felt the loneliness she had carried for seven years shift its weight.

This was the dangerous part. Not the wardens, not the Inquisition, not the gallows. This. The way a found thing becomes a family. The way two children sharing stolen honeycomb could make a woman start believing, after seven careful years, that she had something left to lose.

* * *

She went down near midnight for water and found Odessa alone in the kitchen, proofing tomorrow's dough by lamplight, her huge hands working the mass of it with a tenderness that sat strangely on arms like hers.

"He's asleep up there with her," Odessa said, without turning. "Curled up by the door like a dog. Told him three times to come down to his cot. He says—" her voice did something complicated— "he says somebody ought to keep watch over the hummingbird."

Juniper took down two cups. Poured the dregs of yesterday's cider into both. Handed one over. For a while they drank in silence, and the dough rose, and the oven ticked as it cooled, and outside Corvane slept its watchful sleep.

"It was felt, you said," Juniper said finally. "Her arrival. Across the city."

"Aye."

"How widely?"

Odessa set down her cup. She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, buying time the way bakers buy time—with patience and repetition.

"When the fever took Marta," she said, "you told me something. You said magic's like smoke. It don't travel far, but it gets into everything, and it lingers, and the ones who know how to smell it—" She met Juniper's eyes. "There's a woman who smells better than anyone. They say she can taste a lie in a man's sweat from across a room. They say she knew the Silencing was coming a year before it did, because she could smell the fear coming off the Crown like rot off fish. They call her the Inquisitor now. Sarel Voss."

The name landed in Juniper's chest like a knife finding an old scar.

"I know the name," she managed.

"Do you." Odessa's gaze was steady and pitying and terrible. "Funny thing about names. The way you said that one, girl—you said it like a person says the name of a sister."

Juniper looked down into her cup. The cider was bitter and cheap and exactly right.

"Get some sleep," Odessa said gently. "Bread waits for no one, and neither, I'm thinking, does whatever's coming. But tonight, at least, the children are warm, and the door is barred, and that's more than most of Corvane's got." She turned back to her dough. "Go on. Family rate."

Juniper climbed the ladder in the dark. At the top, she paused.

The loft was dim, lit by one guttering candle Wren had insisted on keeping. Tam lay curled by the door exactly as promised, one arm flung over his face, the bent nail still clutched in his fist. And Wren lay on her side facing the door, awake, her strange pale eyes catching the light, watching Juniper with an expression that needed no translation in any tongue, first or last.

Safe. Here. With you.

Juniper blew out her own lantern, lay down on the far pallet, and listened to the bakery breathe around them—the tick of cooling iron, the sigh of settling grain, the small sounds of two sleeping children—and told herself, firmly, the way you tell yourself anything you desperately need to believe:

One night. One safe night. That's all this is.

Three streets away, unbeknownst to any of them, a rider in Inquisition grey reined in his horse beneath a guttering lamp, consulted a slip of paper, and looked up at the fogged golden windows of Brann's Bakery with narrow, patient eyes.
