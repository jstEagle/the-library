## 5. Inquisitor Voss

The smuggler's name was Pell Wick, and he had been crying for the better part of an hour.

Sarel Voss let him. Tears were useful. They loosened the tongue the way rain loosened soil, and she had learned long ago that a man weeping would tell you anything you wanted to hear just to be forgiven, even if there was nothing in him worth forgiving. She sat across the interrogation cell's scarred table with her hands folded and her ledger open to a blank page, and she waited with the patience of a woman who had nothing else in the world to do.

"Master Wick," she said at last, gently, the way one speaks to a frightened animal. "Let's begin again. The crate seized at the Rivergate. Cedar wood, iron-banded. What was in it?"

"Flour," Pell Wick sobbed. "On my mother's grave, Inquisitor, it was flour—"

"Your mother is alive. I checked." Sarel turned the page, though there was nothing to turn to. "The crate was lined with wool soaked in tallow and ash-of-roses. Do you know what ash-of-roses is used for, Master Wick?"

He shook his head. His chains clinked with the motion, a small dull music.

"Neither do most people," Sarel said. "That's rather the point of it. It's used for keeping things quiet. Charms that don't want to be found. So either you are the most sophisticated flour merchant in the history of Corvane, or someone paid you very well to ask no questions." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something almost kind. "I don't actually care which. I care about the name."

"There wasn't a name—"

"There is always a name."

He broke, as they all broke, and gave her a name that was nothing—a cutout, a dead end, a man who had never existed—and Sarel wrote it down in her neat, upright hand because a ledger with nothing in it was its own kind of confession, and she was not in the habit of confessing.

When the wardens came to take Pell Wick back to his cell, she remained seated a moment longer, alone in the lamplight, and pressed two fingers flat against the table's surface.

The silence of Corvane had a texture. Most people couldn't feel it—they lived their whole lives inside the Crown's Great Quieting the way fish lived in water, never noticing the wet—but Sarel had been trained in a different school. The order she had trained in, before there was an order to train in anymore, had taught its initiates to listen to the absence of speech the way musicians listened for rests. A city at peace sounded like a held breath. A city with magic in it sounded like a breath held by someone hiding in a closet.

For seven years, Corvane had sounded like nothing at all.

Tonight it sounded like a struck bell.

Faint. Fading. But unmistakable—a ripple moving outward through the quiet, the way a stone moves through still water, and at the center of that ripple, somewhere in the tangle of streets south of the Tanner's Canal, something had *spoken*.

Not loudly. Not a working, not a word of power loosed into the air. Smaller than that. Older. The sensation was so faint and so strange that Sarel sat very still for a long moment, wondering if she had imagined it, wondering—though she would never have admitted this under torture—whether she had *hoped* it.

Then it came again, thinner, and she knew.

*First Speech.*

Sarel Voss closed her ledger, snuffed the lamp, and stood in the dark of the interrogation cell with her heart going like a war drum, and she was ashamed of it. She was forty-one years old. She had broken men twice her size with nothing but her voice. She had signed, in the last seven years, one hundred and twelve death warrants, and she slept—this was the part she never told the priests—she slept *well*, in a soft bed, in a warm house, with the window latched against the rain.

And one whisper of the First Speech, one ghost of it, and her hands would not stop shaking.

* * *

She had been twenty-two the first time she heard it spoken properly.

The cloister at Vael's Rest had no roof over its speaking circle—deliberately, so that the words went up and not into walls, where they could fester—and on the morning of her initiation the dawn had come up grey and gold over the Thornfell hills, and the Grandmistress had stood in the center of the circle with her grey braid down her back, and she had said, in the First Speech, the word for *beginning*.

Sarel had felt it in her teeth. In her bones. In the place behind her sternum where she kept everything she was afraid to look at directly. The word had not been loud. It had not needed to be. It had simply been *true*, and truth, spoken in the First Speech, rearranged the world around it the way a keystone rearranges an arch.

Beside her in the circle, seventeen years old and vibrating with excitement, Juniper Villalobos had grabbed her hand without asking, the way Juniper did everything without asking, and squeezed it, and whispered in ordinary Common, "Did you *feel* that? Sarel. *Sarel.* Did you feel it?"

"I felt it."

"We're going to be *speakers*, Sarel. Real ones. Oath-bound. The Hollow itself is going to know our names."

"It knows everyone's names," Sarel had said, because she was twenty-two and determined to be unimpressed by miracles.

But she had squeezed back.

They had been sworn the same hour, kneeling on the same stone, and the oath-mark had risen on their wrists like a brand of dawn—hers a pale silver, Juniper's a deeper gold, because the Hollow gave each speaker its own light, and the Grandmistress had smiled down at them and said, *Two blades from one forge. The Hollow is generous today.*

Two blades from one forge.

Sarel stood now at the window of her office in the Inquisition citadel, watching rain crawl down the glass, and turned her left wrist over. Beneath the leather cuff she wore—worn smooth now from seven years of daily wearing—the oath-mark was still there. It had never faded. That was the cruelty of it. The Hollow did not take back its gifts when you betrayed them; it simply let you keep them, the way a scarred man keeps his scars, as evidence.

She had told herself a hundred versions of the story. In the version she told the Crown, she had discovered the order's treason and acted to save the realm. In the version she told herself, on the nights when sleep came slowly, she had simply been afraid, and fear was a reasonable thing, and anyone who said otherwise had never stood in a doorway with soldiers at their back and a choice like a knife against the throat.

In the true version—the one she kept in the deepest room of herself, behind three locked doors and a wall—she had stood in that doorway, and she had looked at the Grandmistress's face, and she had given the signal anyway.

The Night of Silencing had taken forty-one speakers. She had counted them every year on the anniversary, alone, with a bottle she never finished. Forty-one. She knew their names. She had trained beside nine of them. She had loved two of them, in the way you love the people who knew you before you became a weapon.

One of them was still alive.

She was sure of it the way you are sure of a stone in your boot—small, constant, impossible to ignore. The charm shop in the Weavers' Quarter, the one that sold luck knots and fever whispers and nothing that could hang anyone, run by a woman with careful hands and careful eyes and a laugh she only used on customers. Sarel had walked past it eleven times in seven years. She had gone in twice, once to buy a luck knot for a warden's wedding and once on no pretext at all, and both times Juniper Villalobos had wrapped her purchase in brown paper and smiled her shopkeeper's smile and said, *Come again, mistress,* and both times Sarel had walked out with her heart hammering and her warrant unsigned and told herself: *next time. Next time I'll have proof. Next time it won't be murder.*

It had been seven years of next time.

* * *

"Inquisitor."

Her lieutenant, Brakkus, filled her doorway the way weather fills a valley—large, grey, inevitable. He had been a soldier before he was a witch-finder, and it showed in the way he treated all magic as an enemy fortification to be starved out.

"The ash-of-roses," he said. "We traced the wool. It came from a chandler in the Weavers' Quarter."

"Weavers' Quarter," Sarel repeated. The bell-ripple of the First Speech had come from the south, near the canal. Her pulse ticked upward. "Which street?"

"Ropemaker's Row. The chandler's being brought in now, but—" Brakkus hesitated. Brakkus hesitating was like a mountain reconsidering; it made the room feel unsafe. "There's something else, Inquisitor. The sensor-wardens on the night rotation. Three of them reported the same thing, independent. A disturbance in the quiet. South quarter. They said it felt like—" he consulted his slate, embarrassed— "like a word they couldn't hear. Old. They used the word *old*."

Sarel looked at him for a long moment.

"Old," she said.

"Yes, Inquisitor. And there's a child. A mute child, seen two nights running near the canal, asking—well, not asking, they can't ask, but begging at doors. The night wardens tried to move it along and it looked at them and—" Brakkus's jaw worked. "One of them, Warden Cetch, a steady man, twenty years' service, says the child looked at him and he *heard* his own mother's voice saying his name. Inside his head. Just once. He's been sick since."

Sarel turned back to the window. Rain. Rain everywhere, softening the city into a grey smudge, and somewhere in that smudge, a child speaking the language of the Hollow, and somewhere else, a woman selling luck knots and pretending, with every careful hour of her careful life, that she had never knelt on the speaking stone at all.

The disturbance had come from the south quarter. The charm shop was in the south quarter.

It could be nothing. It could be a hedge-witch with a stolen word, a smuggler's charm gone loud. Coincidences happened. She had built a career on the fact that most people's sins were small and their explanations smaller.

But *two blades from one forge*, and a bell ringing in the silence of Corvane, and a mute child who could speak a language older than kingdoms, and Sarel Voss had spent seven years learning exactly one lesson, over and over, the way you learn a prayer: *the past does not knock politely. It breaks down the door.*

"Brakkus," she said. "I want the south quarter canvassed. Every shop, every cellar, every rented room. Quietly—no raids, no badges, not yet. I want to know who's bought ash-of-roses, who's paid in old coin, who's suddenly afraid." She paused. "And I want a list of every licensed charm-seller in the Weavers' Quarter."

"We have that list already, Inquisitor. It's short. Four names." He produced the slate. "There's a woman on Ropemaker's Row. Villalobos. Sells luck charms. Seven years licensed, never a complaint, never a flag. The wardens call her *the quiet one*—she passes inspection before they finish knocking."

Of course she did. Sarel almost smiled. Juniper had always been the best of them at the hardest discipline—the order had trained them to wield words that could crack stone, and Juniper had chosen, every day for seven years, to hold all of it behind her teeth. Sarel had chosen differently. She had chosen to speak, and to make others silent, and the difference between them was the difference between a blade and its sheath, and she no longer knew which of them the Hollow would call the weapon.

"Leave her on the list," Sarel said. "Unmarked. I'll inspect that shop myself."

Brakkus's eyebrows climbed. "Inquisitor, protocol suggests—"

"Protocol," Sarel said, "suggests a great many things. This one is mine."

When he had gone, she stood a while longer at the window, and then she did a thing she had not done in seven years. She unclasped the leather cuff from her wrist and looked at the oath-mark beneath it.

The silver had not faded. If anything, in the last two nights, it had *brightened*—a faint pulse of light beneath the skin, like a coal breathing, keeping time with something far away. With something that had begun, very softly, to call.

She thought of Juniper at seventeen, grabbing her hand in the speaking circle. *We're going to be speakers. The Hollow itself is going to know our names.*

It knew her name still. That was the horror of it. She could hang every speaker left in the world and burn the cloisters down to their foundations, and the Hollow would go on knowing her name, patiently, the way the sea knows the shape of every stone it has ever swallowed.

Sarel clasped the cuff back on. She took her coat from its hook, and her warrant book, and her pen, and she went down into the rain to inspect a charm shop she had inspected twice before, and to decide, one final time, whether the woman behind the counter was a criminal to be taken or a sister to be saved—or whether, after seven years, those had stopped being different things.

She did not know, as she stepped out into the wet grey street, that the child had already gone.

She did not know that the bell she had heard ringing in the city's silence was not a warning.

It was a beginning.
