## 7. The Undermarket

The way down was through a dead man's chimney.

"Are you *sure* about this?" Tam asked, peering into the soot-black throat of the abandoned smokehouse. He had Wren's hand in his, and he hadn't let go since the walls of Corvane had swallowed them an hour ago. "Because it looks like a place where people go to stop being people."

"That's the general idea," Juniper said. "Down there, nobody's anybody. That's what keeps it safe."

She struck a taper and held it to the wick of a stub candle she'd bought from a shrine alcove three streets back—paid double, left no change, touched nothing. Old habits. The flame caught with a small, ordinary *whup*, and Juniper felt a flicker of absurd pride at how much comfort one honest little fire could give.

Wren tugged her sleeve. When Juniper looked down, the girl pointed into the dark and then made a shape with both hands: a slow opening, like a book.

*Yes,* Juniper signed back, one of the few gestures they'd managed to build between them. *We're going down.*

Wren considered this. Then she pointed at Tam, pointed at herself, and shook her head with the solemn gravity of a queen refusing a treaty.

"She says we're not going," Tam translated, delighted. "I'm getting good at her."

"You're getting *inventive* at her." Juniper crouched to Wren's level. Rain dripped off the eaves behind them; somewhere above, a warden's whistle cut the night, still hunting the ripple Juniper's one spoken word had sent through the city. Her wrist throbbed under its wrappings, the oath-mark sullen as a bruise. "Sweetheart, I know you don't like small dark places. I don't either. But up here, there are men whose whole job is finding us, and down there—" she tapped the chimney stones "—there are only people who don't want to be found. We'll be invisible together. Like smoke."

Wren looked at the chimney for a long moment. Then she leaned forward and whispered—not First Speech, just breath shaped into almost-nothing—and the darkness inside seemed to lean back.

Juniper's skin prickled. The child wasn't commanding anything. She was *greeting* it. And the dark, God help them all, had greeted her in return.

"All right," Juniper said quietly. "Apparently the dark likes you better than me. Let's not make it jealous."

They went down hand over hand, through soot and old spider silk, past the bones of a fire that had died before any of them were born, until the chimney spat them out into a corridor of brick that sloped beneath the city like the gullet of something patient.

And then the Undermarket opened around them, and Tam forgot to be afraid.

* * *

It was not a market so much as a rumor that had decided to put down roots. It filled a vaulted cistern older than Corvane itself, and it was lit by a thousand hanging lanterns of colored glass, none of them casting quite enough light, all of them casting exactly enough. Stalls grew from the brickwork like fungus: a woman selling bottled laughter with labels in six languages; a man whose entire stock was keys to doors that no longer existed; a corner where someone was auctioning, in hushed tones, a mirror that showed you your mother's opinion of you. (A stout gentleman was bidding fiercely. Nobody asked.)

The air smelled of wet stone, frying onions, and old magic—the suppressed kind, the buried kind, the kind that hummed under cloth covers and pretended to be candlesticks.

"Right," Juniper murmured. "Rules. Don't touch anything. Don't taste anything. If anyone offers you a bargain that sounds too good, it's because they've already decided what you're worth, and it isn't flattering."

"What if someone offers me pie?" Tam asked.

"...Use judgment."

They moved through the crowd, and Juniper watched the crowd watch them back. That was the thing about the Undermarket: everyone here was running from something, which meant everyone here was an expert on other people running. She kept her hood up and her hands visible and her voice low, and she felt Wren pressed close against her side like a warm coal.

The child drew looks. Of course she did. There was something about Wren that the lantern-light couldn't quite hold—her edges went soft, her outline wandered, as though she were painted on glass and someone kept breathing on the other side. Juniper had been telling herself for two days that it was exhaustion. Down here, under a hundred colored lamps, she could no longer pretend. In the reflection of a polished copper pot, Wren's outline shimmered like heat-haze.

Juniper's stomach dropped. *Later*, she told herself. *Survive first. Understand later.*

They found him—or he found them, which was more accurate—in the center of the cistern, where a stall stood apart from all the others with nothing for sale on it at all. Just a table, two chairs, and a teapot.

The man behind the table was unremarkable in the specific way that made Juniper's neck prickle. Medium height. Pleasant face. Brown hair, brown eyes, a smile like a shopkeeper's handshake. She looked away and looked back and could not have described him to save her life. She tried again. Nothing stuck. It was like trying to remember the exact shape of water.

"Juniper Villalobos," he said warmly, pouring four cups of tea that had not been poured a moment ago. "The last swordspeaker. Sit down, sit down. You look terrible, which under the circumstances means you look sensible. Tea?"

"I didn't tell anyone my name down here."

"You didn't have to. Names are my trade, dear. Yours has been sitting in my ledger since the Night of Silencing, gathering dust and interest." He gestured to the chairs. "Master Quill, at your service. Which is to say, at your considerable expense."

Tam had gone very still beside her—the particular stillness of a boy who knew every kind of predator and ranked them by smile. But Wren stepped forward first. She looked up at Quill, tilted her head, and made a sound. Not a word. A note—a single clear tone, like a finger run round the rim of a glass.

Quill went white.

Not pale. *White.* For half a heartbeat his pleasant face simply failed, like a mask slipping sideways, and underneath there was—nothing. Not horror. Not a face at all. Then the pleasantness snapped back into place, seamless, and Quill laughed, and it was almost a real laugh.

"Well," he said. "Well, well. So that's what you are." He looked at Wren the way a man looks at a debt come due after twenty years. "Sit down, child. All of you. The tea's on the house, which is the most expensive thing I'll say tonight."

* * *

They sat. The tea was chamomile and something else, something that tasted the way the color green sounds. Tam drank his in three gulps and immediately regretted having no more. Wren wrapped both hands around her cup and did not drink, but held it, the way you'd hold something alive.

"Now," Quill said, lacing his fingers. "You came down here because you need to know things. I deal in knowing things. Before we begin the unpleasant business of prices, let me save us time by telling you what you already suspect, and then telling you what you don't."

He nodded at Wren.

"The Hollow is starving."

Juniper's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "Explain."

"Gladly. It's my favorite lecture." Quill leaned back. "Every story they teach children says magic was given to mankind as a gift, sealed in a covenant, blah blah, crowns and oaths and so forth. Stories are useful lies. Here is the truth: speech itself comes from the Hollow. Every word ever spoken is drawn, thin as thread, from that place beneath Thornfell. Most words use so little that no one notices—a blessing over bread, a mother's hush, a lie told kindly. But some people, your people, learned to draw deep. Swordspeakers. Oath-speakers. They pulled great draughts of the Hollow's voice through their own bodies and gave it shape."

"And the covenant?" Juniper asked. "What was it *for*?"

"A promise. The oldest one. The Hollow gives speech freely, endlessly, to everything that lives—and in return, it keeps an anchor. A living thing, bound to both sides, that stands at the threshold and lets the voice flow both ways. Without an anchor, the flow doesn't stop. It *frays*. The Hollow pulls harder and harder on a world that can't pull back, and the connection wears thin, and everything downstream starts to starve." He spread his hands. "Seven years ago, someone killed the old anchors. All of them. Your order died keeping theirs, Juniper. But anchors aren't only made. Sometimes they're *born*."

The cistern noise seemed to recede. Juniper became aware of her own heartbeat, of Wren's small hands around the teacup, of the lantern light sliding off the girl's uncertain edges.

"No," Juniper said. It came out flat. "No. She's a child. She's a *child* who likes bread crusts and hides from thunder."

"Yes," Quill said gently. "All true. And she is also the last anchor of the Hollow, stolen out of it seven years ago by people who thought they were saving her, and every day she spends in this world, the Hollow reaches for her a little more desperately, and the world's voice grows a little thinner. Look at her edges, swordspeaker. She's fraying too. The tether runs both ways."

Tam had gone gray. "That's why she goes see-through. At night. When she sleeps."

Quill's eyebrows rose, genuinely pleased. "Oh, the pickpocket observes. Excellent. Yes. She pours back toward her origin when her guard is down. Soon she won't need to sleep to do it."

Wren set down her cup. Very carefully. Then she looked at Quill and spoke—one word of First Speech, low and clear, and the word landed in Juniper's chest like a stone dropped down a well, because she understood it without wanting to:

*Lie.*

The word hung there. Quill flinched—actually flinched, the mask slipping again, that blankness beneath—and for a moment nobody breathed.

"No," Quill said softly. "I never lie. It's bad for business. Everything I've told you is true, child, and you know it's true, because you can hear truth the way fish hear water. What I did *not* tell you is who took you from the Hollow, or why, or what they intended. Those are separate questions. Separate *prices*." He smiled, and it was almost kind. "I am not your enemy. I am simply not your friend. There is a difference, and it will matter."

Juniper's mind was moving fast now, cold and clear. "Who else knows? About her."

"Everyone who matters, soon. The Crown has been feeling the famine for years—that's why the silencing laws, why the hangings. Not cruelty. *Rationing.* They think if they choke off small magics, the big ones will keep flowing to those who need them. Fools. You can't ration a river by strangling the streams." He turned his cup slowly. "But there are cleverer fools than the Crown working on the problem. People building machines. And an Inquisitor who felt your door come unmade two nights ago and has not slept since."

"Sarel," Juniper said.

"Sarel," Quill agreed pleasantly. "Your oath-sister. How is her grief these days? I hear it's wearing armor now."

Juniper stood up so fast her chair scraped, and half the market went quiet in that particular way quiet markets do—everyone listening while pretending not to.

"Sit down," Quill said, without any warmth at all. "Anger is free. Information is not. And you need information, Villalobos, because here is the last thing, the thing you really came for, whether you knew it or not: the threshold at Thornfell still stands. Barely. If the anchor returns to it willingly, the covenant can be reforged—but a covenant needs a speaker. Someone to stand at the threshold and speak the terms aloud, forever. Someone who stays." His brown eyes—she still could not remember their color—found hers. "The old rites are very clear about it. One voice, given wholly. Name, oath, memory. The Hollow does not take. It *receives*. There's a difference, and it will matter."

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the cistern, the mirror auction ended in applause.

Wren reached up and took Juniper's hand. Her grip was warm and small and slightly translucent at the fingertips, like paper held against a lamp, and Juniper realized she had already known—had known since the chimney, since the rain, since the knock—what the price would be, and had knocked anyway.

"There's the bill," Quill said, rising, gathering his impossible teapot. "For tonight's consultation: one favor, unspecified, redeemable at a time of my choosing. Standard rates. Oh—and take the north road out of the city, not the east. The east road has ears." He paused, and for just a moment his pleasant face looked tired, ancient, and terribly sad. "And swordspeaker? Feed the child. Whatever she asks for. Anchors fray slower when they're loved properly. That one's free."

He was gone between one blink and the next, and his chair sat empty, and no one at the neighboring stalls could quite recall who had been sitting there.

* * *

They walked out along the cistern wall in silence, following the lanterns toward the far passage. Tam carried the teapot Quill had, apparently, slipped into his pack without any of them noticing. ("Do we keep it?" "We keep it. Arguing with that man is how you end up owing him twice.")

It was Wren who stopped them, tugging Juniper's sleeve near a stall of bottled laughter, where the colored light fell warmest. She pointed at a food cart—actual food, skewers of spiced root vegetable sizzling over coals, run by a one-eyed woman who was pretending not to watch them.

Juniper bought six. Wren ate hers in fierce, deliberate bites, watching Juniper the whole time, as if eating were a message and Juniper the intended reader.

*I'm staying,* the message said. *I'm here. I'm real.*

"I know," Juniper said aloud, her throat tight. "I know, sweetheart."

Tam, mouth full: "So what's the plan? Because the scary tea man basically said the plan is you live under a mountain forever and forget our names, and I hated it, for the record."

"We're going to find another way," Juniper said.

"Is there another way?"

"I don't know yet." She looked at Wren's fading fingertips, at the lantern light passing through them like light through honey, and felt the old oath-mark burn under its wrappings—not in warning, this time, but in something horribly like agreement. "But I've spent seven years learning how to hide a miracle in plain sight. I'm not out of practice yet."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of brick and stone, a bell began to ring in the city of Corvane—three strokes, a pause, three strokes. The signal every wardens' post knew. Not a fire. Not a riot.

*A sighting.*

And by the way the lantern-light in the Undermarket suddenly dimmed, stall by stall, like eyes closing one after another, Juniper understood that whatever had been sighted—

—it had been seen coming *this* way.
