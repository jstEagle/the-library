## 8. Hollow-Sick

Tam had three rules, and he'd kept them alive longer than most people kept pets.

One: nobody's word was worth more than their actions. Two: never sleep deeper than one ear. Three: if a thing looked too kind to be true, it was bait.

He'd made the rules at seven, the year his mother sold him for a sack of flour and didn't come back for the flour's worth of trouble. He'd refined them in Odessa Brann's bakery, where the bread was honest but the world outside wasn't, and he'd held to them through every inspection, every raid-drill, every night he spent on the roof counting exits instead of stars.

So it was a personal insult, really, when Wren started fading and Tam found himself breaking all three rules inside a single week.

* * *

It began with her hands. Or rather, it began with Tam noticing her hands, which was its own small miracle, because noticing things about people other than their escape routes was not a skill he'd ever bothered to grow.

They were sitting on the bakery's back steps—Wren, Odessa shelling peas in the doorway, and Tam pretending he wasn't keeping watch, which was his way of keeping watch without admitting it. Evening light came slantwise through the alley, and where it passed through Wren's fingers, it did not stop.

Tam stared. The light went *through*, the way it went through the dust motes, the way it went through the gap under a door when someone stood on the other side deciding whether to knock.

"Odessa," he said, and his voice came out wrong, too careful, like the time he'd stepped on a board he knew was rotten. "Look at her hands."

Odessa looked. The peas stopped rattling. For a long moment the only sound in the alley was the city breathing its evening noise—carts, a drunk singing two streets over, the bell of the Vigil chapel—and then Odessa set down the bowl very gently, as if the bowl might break, and crossed the stones to kneel in front of the child.

"Wren, little bird. Give me your hand."

Wren gave it. And up close, in shadow as well as light, you could see it: her edges had gone soft. Not wounded-soft, not bruised. *Thinned*. Like paper held too long against a candle's warmth from a room away. Her fingertips were the worst—the pads gone translucent as sea-glass, so that when she pressed them against Odessa's palm, Odessa's lifeline showed faintly through them, pink as a secret.

Wren looked at her own fingers with an expression that wasn't fear. That was the part that got under Tam's skin and stayed there. She looked almost *relieved*, the way you'd look at a door you'd been knocking on for years finally opening from the other side. Then she said something in the First Speech—three soft syllables, falling notes, like water finding the lowest place—and pressed her thinning hand flat against her own chest, and then pointed, through the wall, through the whole city, north.

"No," Odessa said, quiet and fierce. "Whatever's calling you, you're staying put until Juniper gets back. She said—"

The door banged open behind them and Juniper came through it still fastening her coat, smelling of river fog and the Undermarket, and whatever she saw in their faces made her stop with one arm half into her sleeve.

She looked at Wren's hands for a long time. Tam watched her do something complicated with her jaw, the way people did when they were swallowing words too big to go down whole.

"How long?" was what she finally said.

"Just noticed," Tam said.

Juniper crouched, took Wren's hand in both of hers, and turned it in the light with the tenderness of someone handling a blade they respected. "I felt this coming," she said. "Quill told me enough. The Hollow is starving, and Wren is—" She stopped. Started again. "Wren is its anchor. Its last living anchor. It's pulling on her from far away, the way a drowning man pulls on a rope."

"And the rope wins," Tam said, "eventually."

"The rope wins eventually," Juniper agreed, not looking at him, which was its own kind of honesty.

* * *

That first night, Tam did not sleep.

He told himself it was rule two. Never sleep deeper than one ear. But the truth was uglier and simpler: Wren had been given the cot by the oven, because the oven held heat till morning and Odessa wouldn't hear argument, and Tam lay on his pallet across the kitchen with both ears wide open, listening to a child breathe.

Except she breathed strangely now. Shallow, and slow, and every few breaths there was a pause—a hitch, like a singer reaching for a high note and finding the note missing. In those pauses the kitchen went very quiet, quieter than kitchens should, and once Tam would have sworn the fire in the banked oven dimmed, just slightly, like something inhaling.

Around the third hour he gave up pretending, got up, and sat on the floor beside the cot where he could see her face in the ember-light.

She was awake. Of course she was. Her eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling beams, and when she saw him she didn't startle—she just shifted, making room on the narrow cot, an offer so casual it undid him more than any plea would have.

"I'm fine here," Tam whispered.

She patted the cot again. Insistent. Then she said something in First Speech, low and patient, the syllables round as river stones, and pointed at him, then at the space beside her, then made a shape with her hands like—like holding something. Keeping something. Guarding something, maybe.

"That's not how guarding works," Tam muttered. "Guarding means you watch from a distance so nothing sees you doing it."

But he climbed onto the cot anyway, boots off, back against the wall, because rule one said actions were worth more than words and apparently his own rules were going to hang him. Wren arranged herself against his side with the unselfconscious practicality of a cat claiming a warm spot, and within minutes she was asleep, breathing her strange hitching breaths against his shoulder.

Tam stayed awake and watched the door.

Somewhere in the smallest hours, her hand found his sleeve and gripped it—not tight, barely any pressure at all, and he understood with a cold drop in his stomach that soon there might be even less of her to grip with. He didn't move his arm the rest of the night. By morning it ached like a borrowed thing, and he decided he didn't mind, and then he decided he minded that he didn't mind, and after that he stopped thinking about it altogether, which was its own kind of surrender.

* * *

By the third day she couldn't hold a spoon.

Not because she was weak—though she was weak, her meals shrinking to broth and honey-bread torn into pieces she could worry at like a bird—but because the spoon kept slipping *through* her fingers before her grip closed. Odessa solved it by carving her a wooden cup with a handle thick as a rolling pin, and Wren drank from it with grave dignity, and Tam laughed, and then hated himself for laughing, except Wren looked pleased, so he decided laughing was allowed.

Juniper spent those days in the shop below and the library of Master Quill above everything, coming back with her eyes red-rimmed and her arms full of books no sane person owned. Tam heard the arguments through the floorboards at night—Juniper and Odessa, low and urgent, the words *threshold* and *Thornfell* and *too slow, she's too slow* recurring like a fever.

"We leave in four days," he heard Juniper say on the second night. "Five at the latest. I'm buying us time the only way left to buy it."

"With what coin?"

"With the last thing I own." A pause. "My voice. There are words that can anchor her a while, if I speak them true. Every true word costs me. I'll pay."

Silence, then, and Tam lay in the dark hating the arithmetic of it: days bought at the price of Juniper's voice, spent one syllable at a time, and no one asking Wren whether she wanted to be kept.

He asked her himself, the next morning, in the only language they shared properly now—which was to say, badly. He'd been learning First Speech from her in scraps, stolen minutes on the steps, her correcting his mangled syllables with the patience of a tutor who found her student hopeless but amusing.

"You want to be kept?" he said, and then tried it her way, shaping the sounds: *kept-want-you?*

Wren tilted her head. Considered. Then she took his hand—her fingers cool as pondwater now, and he could see the lines of his own palm ghosting through them—and drew, on his skin, with one thin fingertip: a house. A crooked little house with a chimney. Then a stick figure inside it. Then another figure, smaller. Then she pointed at herself, then at the house, then shook her head slowly.

"Not yet," Tam translated, his throat tight. "You want to go home. But not... not alone?"

She nodded. Then she drew again: the house, and around it, many small figures. All the stick people. She tapped each one, and then touched his chest, and Odessa's, and—after a hesitation, a real hesitation, like the word itself was heavy—drew a fourth figure standing apart, sword-shaped line at its side, and tapped that one too.

"Oh," Tam said, stupidly. "Oh, you great soppy little—"

She grinned at him. Even thinned, even translucent at the edges, her grin went all the way through, and Tam thought, with the particular horror of a boy who has spent nine years trusting no one, *this is what it feels like when someone keeps you.* He'd been on the giving end once, long ago, and the memory had rotted. This was the other direction. It didn't rot.

* * *

The stories started on the fourth night, and it was entirely Wren's fault.

She couldn't sleep well anymore—the Hollow pulled at her hardest in the dark hours, Tam could feel it through the cot sometimes, a faint tug like current under still water—and so she'd taken to waking him, poking his shoulder until he grumbled awake, and then signing at him expectantly. Story, the gesture said. Tell story.

"I don't tell stories," Tam had said the first time. "I know exits. Exits aren't stories."

Poke.

"They're *useful*. Nobody ever escaped danger via plot."

Poke, poke.

And so, because he was weak and she was persistent and the kitchen was warm, he told her the story of how he'd pickpocketed a warden's purse and replaced it with a turnip, and how the warden had walked four streets cradling the turnip tenderly before anyone dared tell him. Wren laughed so hard she went transparent to the elbow, which frightened them both, and afterward she taught him how to say *funny* in First Speech—two quick notes rising, like a question that answered itself—and made him say it back until he got it right.

After that, it became theirs. Every night, a story. And because she could not lie—because nothing spoken in First Speech could be false, which Tam found both beautiful and terrifying—her corrections of his pronunciation were always truthful, and some of her words, spoken sleepy and unguarded in the dark, were about him.

*Brave*, she called him once, and he snorted and said brave people were just scared people with bad planning, and she repeated it—*scared, bad-planning, brave*—in her round stone syllables until the words sounded like a spell, and maybe they were, because he slept better that night than he had in years.

*Kept*, she said another night, touching his chest, and he pretended to be asleep.

But the nights were getting harder to fill. Each day she faded a little more. By the fifth day her hair had gone from brown to the color of moonlit water, and when she walked past the window the light leaned through her like she was a curtain drawn aside. She tired quickly. Words cost her visibly now; you could watch her thin a shade with every sentence, spending herself syllable by syllable, and Tam began to understand with a sick lurch why she'd been so sparing of speech when he first met her. She hadn't been mute out of damage. She'd been *rationing.*

"Don't talk," he told her that night, when she woke him for a story. "Just—lie there. I'll do the talking. Both parts."

She raised an eyebrow, which required no substance whatsoever to be expressive.

"I mean it. You keep spending yourself on words and there won't be enough of you left to carry to Thornfell." He scrubbed his hands through his hair. "So. Here's the deal. I tell the story, and I'll tell it in your language. Badly. And you're not allowed to correct me unless my mistake is dangerous."

Wren's eyebrow climbed higher.

"Fine. Unless it's *very* funny. But you have to laugh quietly."

And so Tam told her, in broken, stumbling First Speech, the story of the turnip warden, and then the story of the cat he'd fed on the bakery roof for a winter (the cat, he admitted truthfully, had been eating better than him), and then—because she signed *more*, insistent, greedy for it, and because it was the middle of the night and no one could see—he told her the one he'd never told anyone. About the mother. About the flour sack. About waiting by the market gate until dark, and the next dark, and the dark after that, telling himself each dusk that she'd come tomorrow, and how he'd finally understood, somewhere around the fourth dusk, that *tomorrow* was a country he'd been exiled from.

His First Speech fell apart halfway through. He didn't have the words for exile, or for waiting, or for the specific weight of a flour sack. So he said the pieces he could say—*waited, dark, no-come, waited*—and let the silences carry the rest, and Wren listened with her luminous eyes and did not pity him, which was the mercy he hadn't known he needed. Pity would have broken rule three. She just reached over and took his sleeve in her pond-cool grip, and held on, and when he finally ran out of fragments she spoke one soft phrase in return, careful and true because it had to be true:

*Stay-here. Kept-now.*

And Tam, who trusted no one, lay there in the dark with a fading child's hand on his arm and thought: *I'm going to lose her anyway. Either to the Hollow, or to the road, or to whatever Juniper isn't telling us about what reforging a covenant costs.* And then he thought, immediately after, with the clarity of the truly doomed: *and I'd do all of it again. Every night. Every story.*

Tenderness, he was discovering, was not a feeling. It was an act of courage performed daily by people who knew better.

* * *

On the sixth morning, Odessa found them asleep on the cot, Tam's arm around the child like a drawbridge raised, and she didn't wake them. She stood in the doorway a long moment with a tray of honey-bread, watching the boy who checked exits watch the door even in his sleep, his body curled between Wren and the room.

Then she looked at Wren—or at where Wren mostly was—and the tray trembled.

Because in the ember-light of the cooling oven, Wren had gone nearly clear. Her outline shimmered like heat over a road. Through her chest you could see the blanket's weave. And her lips were moving, though no sound came—she was speaking in her sleep, speaking *northward*, syllable after syllable poured out into the dark like water down a drain, and with every word she grew fainter, thinner, more spent, a lamp burning its own oil.

Odessa set down the tray with shaking hands and went to find Juniper, and her feet on the stairs made no sound at all, because grief, like thieves and wardens, knows how to walk quietly.

And in the kitchen by the dead oven, Wren dreamed of a garden under a hill, and a door of root and starlight standing open, and something vast on the other side calling her name in a voice like every voice she'd ever loved—

—while downstairs, Juniper Villalobos read the last page of Quill's stolen book, closed it, and said to the empty room, in a voice gone rough as gravel: "We leave tonight. Before she pours herself out entirely. Gods forgive me—I know now what the covenant asks, and I'm going to pay it, and none of them can stop me."

She blew out the candle, and the dark took the shop, and somewhere far to the north beneath the Thornfell hills, something ancient felt a speaker make a promise, and began, very slowly, to hope.
