## 9. The Road Out of Silence

The Thornfell road began where the king's road ended, which was to say it began in mud.

Juniper had always thought there should be something more ceremonial about fleeing for your life. In the old stories—the ones Master Ellery used to read aloud by candlelight until his voice went hoarse—fugitives rode through moonlight on swift horses with cloaks snapping like banners. Nobody ever mentioned that after three days of rain your boots weighed as much as a sleeping cat each, or that Tam complained with such artistry and stamina that Juniper privately considered petitioning to have it declared a form of magic.

"—and I'm just saying," Tam was saying, from his perch atop Odessa's delivery cart, "if we're going to die somewhere, it could at least be somewhere with a roof. Or a tavern. Ideally both."

"We're not going to die," Odessa said, without looking up from the reins. She said it the way she kneaded dough: firmly, repeatedly, until it became true.

"You can't know that."

"I'm a baker, love. I know things rise when you don't expect them to." She flicked the reins over the mule, whose name was Duchess and whose opinion of all present was a matter of long-standing grievance. "Besides, if we die, who's going to finish my bread? It would go stale. That's a sin against everything holy."

Juniper walked at the back of the cart, because someone had to watch the road behind them, and because walking gave her hands something to do besides shake. Wren walked beside her, small and solemn under an enormous hooded cloak Odessa had cut down for her, her pale eyes fixed on the ruts ahead as though reading a story written in mud.

Behind them, Corvane's smoke sat on the horizon like a bruise that wouldn't heal.

Three days since the door unmade itself. Three days since Juniper spoke one true word aloud and felt the whole city flinch around her like a struck bell. Every remaining sensor in Corvane knew exactly what she was now, and where she'd been, and roughly where she was going. Sarel Voss did not chase. Sarel Voss *shadowed*—Juniper had caught the riders twice at the edge of sight, patient as weather, letting distance do their work for them. Her sister-by-oath had always understood that fear travels faster than horses. Why run down your quarry when she'll wear herself out running from you?

*She's not your sister anymore,* Juniper reminded herself. *Sisters don't sound the alarm while the order burns.*

But some treacherous part of her kept answering: *sisters also don't take seven years to stop writing letters in their head.*

"Juniper." Wren tugged her sleeve. When the child wanted attention she didn't point or wave; she simply said your name in First Speech, and hearing it was like being touched on the back of the neck. *"Teach me the word for walls."*

Juniper glanced up at the cart. Tam had mercifully paused his lamentations to eat a heel of yesterday's loaf, and Odessa's attention was on the road. Still, Juniper lowered her voice until it was barely more than breath. They didn't speak power aloud—not out here, not yet—but First Speech itself was only a language unless you put weight behind it, and Wren needed to know the difference the way other children needed to know which mushrooms killed.

"Walls," Juniper murmured in the old tongue. "*Sera-ven.* But listen—say it soft and it's just a word. Say it *true*, and it remembers being stone."

Wren's brow furrowed. "*How* does it remember?"

That was the question, wasn't it. That was the whole question, the one the order had spent four hundred years arguing about around tables Juniper would never sit at again. She crouched so they were eye to eye, and picked up a pebble from the road, turning it between her fingers.

"When you speak, you're not making something happen," she said. "You're reminding the world what it promised to be, back before anything had a name. The Hollow heard every promise ever made, and it keeps them. Speech is just... asking it politely to keep this one."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she held out her hand, palm up, asking without words—a habit Juniper was trying very hard not to find heartbreaking.

Juniper set the pebble in it. "Small words first. *Kel.* 'Hold.' If anyone grabs you—if anyone tries to carry you off—you say *kel* and mean it, and they will find you heavier than mountains. Do you understand? Heavier than mountains."

The child nodded once, and whispered *kel*, and the pebble in her palm suddenly weighed like a fistful of lead. Her eyes went wide with delight rather than alarm, which told Juniper more than she wanted to know about how the Hollow raised its daughter.

"That's it," Juniper said, and had to clear her throat. "Again tomorrow. Small words first."

*Because you won't be here for the big ones.*

The thought arrived without permission, the way it had been arriving since the Undermarket, since Quill had smiled his forgettable smile and said, in the tone of a man mentioning the price of tea: *Of course, reforging the covenant requires a speaker to remain behind. It always has. Where did you think anchors came from?*

She hadn't told the others. She told herself this was mercy. She suspected it was cowardice wearing mercy's coat.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a shepherd's cot, its roof long collapsed, its stones furred with moss. Odessa built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and produced from her seemingly bottomless basket a stew that tasted of onions and stubbornness. Tam ate three bowls and complained that the fourth was too far away.

It was, Juniper reflected, watching steam curl into the dark, the strangest company she had ever kept. A baker who remembered too much. A thief with a poet's gift for grievance. A child made of old light, thinning at the edges like paper held near a flame—and Juniper tried not to look at those edges, at the way firelight passed faintly through Wren's wrist when she lifted her spoon, but she looked anyway, and her chest did something painful about it.

"Tell the one about the fox," Wren signed—or rather, she said it in First Speech, but she'd learned that Tam preferred signs when her words got too old and strange. Tam had invented half the signs himself, badly, and Wren had adopted them out of kindness.

"The fox story," Tam groaned. "She asks for the fox story every night. There are only two stories I know and one of them is about a fox."

"It's a good story," Wren said, with the serene authority of someone who had never once been wrong in a language that forbade lying.

Tam shot Juniper a look of pure betrayal, then sighed the sigh of the profoundly put-upon, and told the story. It was a street story, a Corvane gutter tale about a fox who stole the moon out of the sky because nobody had asked it nicely to shine on him, and how he wore it under his coat for a year until he grew lonely and gave it back, and the moon forgave him because moons understand hoarding better than most. He told it badly and with great feeling, mangling his borrowed First Speech phrases, and Wren corrected his grammar twice, and Odessa laughed until she had to wipe her eyes.

Juniper laughed too. That was the terrible part. She laughed, and watched them in the firelight, and loved them with a fierceness that felt less like warmth every day and more like a blade being sharpened.

Because Quill had let one other thing slip, almost as an afterthought, the way cruel men drop knives: *And the speaker who remains must surrender everything, dear lady. Name. Memory. Oath. The Hollow doesn't want your body—it wants your selfhood. You'd be a voice in a garden, forever. No one would remember you. Not even you.*

*Not even me.*

"Juniper." Odessa's voice, quiet, beside her. The others had gone to sleep; Tam lay sprawled against Wren's side like a guard dog that had never been trained and didn't care. "You've been staring at that fire like it owes you money. Talk."

"There's nothing to talk about."

"Mm." Odessa fed a twig to the flames. "Seven years I ran a bakery next to a charm-shop, and in seven years you never once bought flour from me, which any fool could see was because buying flour means coming back, and coming back means being known. So I know what silence looks like on a person who's good at talking. Talk."

Juniper looked at her. Odessa Brann had a face like a well-used cutting board—all nicks and grain and honesty—and eyes that had seen hedge-work burned out of three villages before she'd buried her own gifts under bread and never dug them up again. She was not a woman who could be lied to comfortably.

"The covenant needs an anchor," Juniper said finally. "A living one. Wren was stolen from the Hollow—that's why it's starving, why the magic's gone sour and wild at the edges. To reforge it, the anchor goes home." She turned the pebble—Wren's pebble, still in her pocket—over in her fingers. "Or a speaker takes her place. Willingly. Permanently."

Odessa was quiet for a moment. "Permanently how?"

"The Hollow doesn't want a body standing at a gate. It wants a voice woven into itself. Name given up. Memories—" Juniper stopped. Started again. "Everything that makes me *me* stays behind. What walks out of that garden, if anything does, won't know your name. Won't know hers."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"And you've known this," Odessa said, "since the Undermarket."

"Yes."

"And you weren't going to tell us."

"No."

Odessa nodded slowly, and Juniper braced for anger, but what came instead was worse—it was understanding, worn like armor. "I buried my magic under a hearthstone in Year of Embers," Odessa said. "I didn't tell anyone either. Not because I was ashamed. Because telling people means they get a vote, and some sacrifices you have to make alone or you'll talk yourself out of them." She looked at Juniper with those cutting-board eyes. "So here's my vote, cast anyway, because I love you enough to be rude about it. Don't decide yet. Deciding early isn't bravery, Juniper. It's just dying ahead of schedule."

Juniper opened her mouth to say something clever and found nothing there, and closed it again.

Across the dead fire, Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something in the old tongue. Juniper caught it, and wished she hadn't:

*"Stay."*

Children talked in their sleep. Children dreamed nonsense. But Wren could not lie even dreaming, and the word settled over Juniper like snowfall, gentle and burying, and she sat awake a long time after Odessa slept, holding a pebble that remembered being stone.

* * *

On the fifth day the land changed. The farms thinned, the woods thickened, and the road narrowed into a track that wound upward through country the maps called the Thornfell and the people who lived near it called nothing at all, because they'd moved away.

The threshold was close. Juniper could feel it now, the way you feel a storm building behind hills—a pressure in the ears, a taste like iron and honey at the back of the throat. And Wren felt it more. The child had begun stopping mid-stride to stare north, her translucent edges catching light strangely, and once, at noon, she'd said, in a voice that wasn't entirely her own age: *"It's hungry. It's so hungry, Juniper. It's calling me home for supper."*

They made camp early, in a hollow ringed by hawthorn, and it was there that Juniper saw the riders clearly for the first time.

Six of them, on the ridgeline to the south, black against a bruised-orange sky. Not advancing. Watching. One of them raised an arm—not a weapon, just a hand, open-palmed, almost a greeting—and then they were gone behind the ridge like ink drawn back into a bottle.

"Sarel?" Tam asked quietly. He'd come to stand at her shoulder without her noticing; the boy moved like spilled water.

"She's counting our fires. Learning our pace." Juniper's jaw tightened. "She wants us to reach the threshold. She wants us to lead her to it."

"Why?"

"Because the Crown doesn't know where all the doors are. We do." Or Sarel did, once—had known them all, had traced them on Juniper's skin with a fingertip during study hours, laughing about how they'd visit every one someday. Juniper strangled the memory before it finished forming. "Get some sleep, Tam. Tomorrow we move fast."

He went, but slowly, pausing at the camp's edge. "For what it's worth," he said, not quite looking at her, "the fox gave the moon back. In the story. Just—people do that. Give things back." He shrugged, mortified by his own sincerity, and fled to his bedroll before she could answer.

Juniper stood a long time under the hawthorns, listening to the Hollow's hunger hum up through the soles of her boots.

Then Wren appeared beside her, silent as falling leaves, and took her hand—the left one, the marked one—and pressed her small thumb against the oath-scar on Juniper's wrist. The old brand woke beneath it, warm as a living thing.

*"You're going to give it yourself,"* Wren said. Not a question. Truth, spoken plain, because she could do nothing else.

The night went very still. Juniper knelt, taking the small face in her hands, feeling the frightening delicacy of it, the way light passed through the child's cheekbones like through sea-glass.

"Wren. Listen to me. Whatever you see, whatever the Hollow shows you, whatever you *know*—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and went on anyway, "—you are a little girl who likes fox stories and Odessa's bread, and you are not a key, and you are not an anchor, and you are not going anywhere I don't walk first. Do you hear me?"

Wren looked at her with those pale, ancient, eight-year-old eyes. And then she did the worst thing. She smiled—small, sad, knowing—and answered in First Speech, and every word was true:

*"Yes. That's why it has to be you."*

Far to the south, on the dark ridgeline, a single horn sounded—low, mournful, unmistakable. Sarel's riders, no longer content to shadow.

Coming at dawn.
