## 10. Ashes of the Order

They smelled the cloister before they saw it.

Not smoke—seven years was too long for smoke—but something older than smoke, a smell like burnt prayers, like the memory of burning soaked into stone. Juniper reined in at the lip of the hill and stood there a while with her hand on Wren's shoulder, letting the wind come up off the valley and tell her what it always told her.

*You came back. You always come back.*

"You said we'd reach Thornfell by week's end," Tam said behind her, in the tone of a boy who had learned that detours were how adults confessed things. "This is not north."

"No," Juniper said. "It isn't."

Wren's small hand found hers and squeezed once. The child didn't speak—not in Corvanish, which she couldn't manage, and not even in First Speech, though Juniper had heard her murmuring it in her sleep lately, soft as rain on a window. She only squeezed, and looked up, and waited, because Wren had a way of waiting that made silence feel like a question you'd already agreed to answer.

"The Order of the Spoken Blade kept its cloister here," Juniper said. "Forty years I trained within those walls. And seven years ago, in one night, every sister I had died in them." She swallowed. "I'm going to tell you both the whole truth of it now. But not from the road. There's a rite owed to the dead, and I've put it off long enough. Come."

* * *

The cloister did not look like a ruin so much as like a wound that had learned to live with itself. The outer wall still stood in places, blackened granite furred with lichen, arched gateways opening onto nothing. The bell tower had fallen inward, a broken ribcage of masonry, and grass grew through the refectory floor in green rivers. Somewhere a bird had made a home in what remained of the scriptorium's window, and sang there, cheerfully indifferent to history.

"Was it big?" Tam asked, hushed in the way he was never hushed anywhere else.

"Big enough." Juniper walked them along paths she could have walked blindfolded. "Two hundred swordspeakers at its height. We weren't soldiers, whatever the songs say. A sword is just a mouth, in our hands. The blade is the oath; the voice is the edge. We spoke the world into fairness where fairness had worn thin. Broke sieges by unmaking gates. Healed fevers by singing them out of children. Stood between kings and their worst ideas." Her mouth pulled sideways. "And swept our own floors, and grew our own vegetables, and argued about whose turn it was to churn butter. Sister Maerin once hexed the laundry line over a stolen shift. It took three of us a day to untangle her words from the sheets."

Tam grinned despite himself. "Hexed *laundry*?"

"She was ninety-one and felt she'd earned it."

At the center of the cloister stood the chapter house, or what was left of it: four walls roofed with open sky, the great hearth cold, the circular floor where the order had once gathered scored and scarred. Someone had dragged the dead here afterward—the Crown's men, efficient even in atrocity—and burned them in a pyre whose ash had sunk so deep into the flagstones that the stones themselves seemed grey with grief.

Juniper set down her pack.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. This will take as long as it takes."

* * *

She began, as the rite required, with water. There was a well in the eastern court, still sweet, and she drew a bucket and carried it back with both hands, and poured a thin ring of it around the scorched circle of the floor.

"For washing the names," she said. "So they can be spoken clean."

Then she knelt, and pressed her bare wrist against the stones, and let the old words come.

They were not power. That was the first thing Wren needed to understand, and Juniper watched the child's eyes widen as she listened and understood it anyway—understood faster than any adult would have. The remembrance-rite was only language, plain and human, arranged the way grief arranges things when grief is given time. Names. Deeds. Small jokes. The shape of a life in a breath.

"Sister Alenna of the Riverlands, who could quiet a stampeding horse with one word and never once raised her voice to a person. Sister Dova, who kept bees and swore they understood First Speech better than she did. Brother Callum—" her voice snagged, steadied— "Brother Callum, who was nineteen, who was supposed to be on kitchen duty, who ran toward the fire instead of away from it."

She went on. Forty-one names. She knew all forty-one. She had recited them every year alone in her shop with the shutters barred, whispering into a charm-jar so that even the walls couldn't carry them, and every year it had felt like drowning politely.

Wren sat cross-legged at the ring's edge, absolutely still, tears running down her face without any change in her expression at all, as if crying were simply weather moving through her. Tam had gone pale. Halfway through he whispered, "You *knew* all of them?"

"I trained beside them. I ate beside them. I loved—" Juniper paused at the last name, the hardest one. "I loved most of them. Speak the name with the deed, the rite says. Otherwise the dead are only a list."

When the last name faded, she stayed kneeling. The wind moved through the roofless room. The bird in the scriptorium sang on, oblivious, and somehow that helped.

"Now," Juniper said, "the rest of it. The part I've never said aloud to anyone."

* * *

"You know the story the city tells," she began. "That the Order conspired against the Crown. That we hoarded magic, plotted rebellion, and were rightly put down in the Night of Silencing. Some of it is even shaped like truth. We *did* refuse the King's edict. When the Silencing Laws passed—when speaking was made a hanging crime—we did not disband. We did not go quietly into shops and bakeries and pretend to be luck-knot sellers." She glanced at them both, wry and sad at once. "We kept speaking. Openly. Because an oath doesn't have an exit clause, and because two hundred armed consciences in one valley seemed to us like a reasonable thing for a kingdom to tolerate."

"And the King disagreed," Tam said.

"The King's advisors disagreed. Loudly. But no—that's not the truth either, not the heart of it." Juniper drew a breath and found, to her surprise, that it shook. Seven years of rehearsing this moment, and it still shook. "The truth is that the Crown didn't destroy us in one night. They couldn't have. Two hundred swordspeakers could have unmade any army sent against us. What destroyed us was that someone inside the walls opened the gate."

Silence. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

"There were three of us on the night watch," Juniper said. "Myself. Sister Maren of the east tower. And Sarel Voss, my oath-sister, my—my closest thing to blood since I was eleven years old. We had joined the same season. We'd taken the vow with our palms cut and pressed together. She knew my thoughts sometimes before I'd finished having them."

"We were meant to rotate the ward-words at midnight. Three speakers, three keys, renewed nightly so no outside word could slip the gate. That night I went up to the east tower and found Maren already dead. No wound. Just—emptied. Her mouth open, and nothing left in her, like a cup turned upside down." Juniper's hands had closed into fists without her permission; she opened them deliberately, finger by finger. "And standing over her, holding the ward-key she'd torn out of the air like a thread off a loom, was Sarel."

"She didn't look mad. That's what I need you to understand. She looked *certain*. She said—I can still hear it, word for word, because some things etch themselves—she said, 'They're going to burn the Hollow out of the world, Juni. The engineers already have the designs. If the Order fights, everyone dies tonight and the Hollow dies anyway. If I open the gate, maybe some of us live. Maybe enough of us live to matter later.' She thought she was saving us." Juniper laughed, and it came out wrong. "She opened the gate. Two hundred soldiers came through it. And I ran."

"You *ran*?" Tam's voice cracked with eight-year-old outrage on someone else's behalf.

"I ran." She met his eyes and did not flinch from it. "I was third blade of the Order. I had unmade siege gates. I could have stood in that doorway and spoken until my voice gave out, and I might have bought twenty sisters time to reach the river. Instead I heard Sarel say 'live,' and I heard myself agree, and I went out through the drainage culvert like smoke. I hid in a wine cart. I was in Corvane before dawn, buying a shop lease with money I'd saved for a garden I was going to plant when I retired."

The confession hung in the ruined air. Juniper found she couldn't look at either of them, so she looked at the scorched stones instead, at the ring of well-water slowly darkening them.

"The rite says the past should be acknowledged so it can be set down," she said quietly. "I've been carrying mine in a jar behind a false shelf for seven years. So. There it is. Your guide, your protector, your luck-knot seller: a woman who lived by abandoning everything her oath told her to hold."

* * *

It was Wren who moved first.

The child crossed the ring of water without waiting for permission—which was very like her—and knelt, and took Juniper's clenched hand in both of her small ones. Then, looking up, she spoke.

One word. First Speech, low and clear, the syllables falling into the ruined chapter house like stones into deep water.

Juniper's breath left her. "What did she say?"

"She says—" Tam frowned, working it out with the halting grammar Wren had been feeding him on the road. "She says... 'a door that opens is not a door that fails'? Something like—wait. No." His brow furrowed harder. "'A door that lets someone through has done its work.'"

"It's an old proverb of the Order," Juniper said, barely above a whisper. "Sister Alenna used to say it. About mercy. About retreat." She stared at the child. "How do you—"

But of course she knew how. Wren could not lie in First Speech; everything the girl said in that tongue simply *was*, the way stone is stone. Which meant the proverb wasn't comfort. It was verdict. The Hollow itself, speaking through its last anchor, had weighed Juniper Villalobos and found her not guilty—or at least, found the charge mislaid.

"That's cheating," Juniper managed, wet-eyed. "Using the literal voice of creation to win an argument."

Wren shrugged, the universal gesture of children everywhere: *and yet.*

Tam cleared his throat. He had gone pink and fierce, the way he did when feeling outran his vocabulary. "For what it's worth," he said, "I've done bad things too. Worse than running, probably. I stole from people who couldn't afford it, back when Odessa found me, because the man who owned me said I had to or he'd—" He stopped, jaw working. "Point is. You fed me. Both of you. People who run still get to be people. That's just true. I don't need a magic language for it."

"No," Juniper said, and her voice came out steady after all. "No, you don't."

She rose. The rite was nearly done; only the setting-down remained. She walked the ring of water one last time, and at each quarter of the circle she spoke the closing formula—*what was given, return; what was broken, release; what was loved, keep*—and with each phrase she felt something in her chest loosen by a degree, like a knot worked loose after years.

At the final quarter, she stopped.

Because the wind had changed.

* * *

It came down off the northern hills thin and cold and smelling faintly of iron, and Juniper knew that smell. Every swordspeaker knew it. Oath-marked skin knew it the way a scar knows coming rain.

Her wrist flared—dully, distantly, but unmistakably. Not Wren's presence this time. Something else. Something *hunting-shaped*.

"Wake up, both of you," she said, already reaching for her pack. "We're leaving."

"What is it?" Tam was on his feet instantly, all street-instinct, eyes flicking to the broken gateway.

"Riders on the north road, or my wrist is a liar." Juniper swung her pack up. "Voss's patrols have been shadowing us since Millbrook county. She knows where we're bound. And now she'll know we stopped here—this place is a beacon to anyone with old training." She hesitated one heartbeat at the ring of water, at the forty-one names settled into the stones, then turned away. "I'm sorry. We'll finish the rite properly someday, when there's time to be decent about it."

Wren tugged her sleeve. When Juniper looked down, the child was pointing—not at the road, not at the hills, but up, at the empty sky where the roof had been. Her face wore an expression Juniper had never seen on her: urgent, almost angry. Then she spoke again, quick syllables tumbling, and Tam scrambled to keep up.

"She says—'the Hollow is louder today.' And..." He faltered. "And 'it's counting down.' Like—like a bell? Like something ringing?"

Juniper felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs. *Counting down.* Quill's voice slithered back to her across the miles of the Undermarket: *the covenant is fraying, swordspeaker, and fraying cloth does not wait politely for your convenience.*

"Then we stop being careful," she said. "Thornfell, straight north, no more detours. We walk through the night if we have to." She looked back once—at the scorched floor, at the water-darkened stones, at the little bird still singing its indifferent song—and something in her that had been bent for seven years finally straightened.

"Grief keeps," she said, half to herself, half to the dead. "Purpose doesn't. Let's go."

They went out through the western arch, three small figures on a hill of ashes, and behind them the wind moved through the ruined chapter house and stirred the forty-one names like leaves.

None of them saw the rider on the far ridge lower his spyglass, turn his horse, and ride hard south with a message that would reach Inquisitor Voss by morning:

*The last swordspeaker has returned to the grave. She is heading for the threshold.*
