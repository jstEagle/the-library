## 11. The Trap at Millbrook

Sarel Voss had learned long ago that the best traps required patience and a certain amount of theater.

The ford at Millbrook was ideal for the first and offered nothing for the second—just a broad, brown river splitting the north road, willows drooping along its banks like mourners who had given up on the funeral. But it was the only crossing within thirty miles that a fugitive party with a fading child could manage, and so here she had waited three days, camped in the ruins of the old tollhouse with forty picked riders and a net of silence-wards strung through the reeds like a fisherman's lines.

She had expected them tonight. The boy's theft of bread from a farmstead two miles back had been reported by a trembling widow who described him as "a small horror with quick hands," which was accurate enough to confirm her suspicions. Fugitives fed themselves badly. Fugitives crossed rivers badly. And fugitives led by Juniper Villalobos crossed rivers *predictably*, because Juniper had always been the kind of soldier who chose the safe route even when the safe route walked her into a knife.

Sarel stood at the tollhouse window as dusk came down blue over the water, and she let herself feel, briefly, the thing she had not allowed herself to feel in seven years: the approach of her oath-sister.

*Former* oath-sister. She corrected herself the way one corrects a limp.

"They'll come at full dark," said Captain Ives behind her, a solid woman with a face like a closed door. "Wards are set. Your orders?"

"Take the child alive. Take Villalobos alive if it can be done without absurdity." Sarel turned the signet ring on her finger—the Crown's eye stamped in silver, cold against skin that remembered a different mark entirely. "The others may be treated gently or roughly according to their behavior."

"And if Villalobos resists?"

"She won't." Sarel heard how certain she sounded and wondered, not for the first time, whether certainty was just grief wearing armor. "She'll fight to protect the child. She'll surrender to protect the others. It's the order she comes from." It was the order we both came from. Only one of us kept it.

The wards would tell her when they came. Until then there was nothing but waiting, and the river talking to itself, and the memory of a cloister burning.

* * *

They came at full dark, exactly as predicted, and the wards sang their thin silver note, and Sarel gave the signal, and everything went wrong in precisely the ways she had feared.

Not the capture itself—that went smoothly enough. The lanterns bloomed along both banks at once, the riders closed from the trees, and the three fugitives froze in the shallows like startled deer: a broad baker's wife with flour still somehow under her nails despite a week on the run, a skinny boy clutching the child's hand, and Juniper, standing hip-deep in the Millbrook water with her hand already on her sword hilt.

No. That was what went wrong. Because when Juniper saw her—not the wardens, not the crossbows, but Sarel herself, stepping into the lantern light—she did not draw.

She looked *tired*. Not defeated-tired. The other kind. The kind that had been tired for years and had simply gotten used to carrying it, the way you get used to a stone in your boot.

"Sarel," Juniper said. Just her name. No curse attached to it. And the river made its small sounds around all their legs, and somewhere a nightjar called, and Sarel felt seven years of carefully constructed purpose shift under her feet like the gravel of the ford.

"Juniper Villalobos," she answered, because ceremony was a weapon too, and she needed one right now. "In the name of the Crown, surrender the child."

"The child has a name."

"Wren." Sarel had read every report twice. "The anchor. You know what she is."

"I know she's eight years old and frightened and hasn't eaten properly since Tuesday," said the baker's wife, Odessa Brann, with the magnificent indignation of a woman facing forty crossbows who had apparently decided the real outrage was the catering. "Which is more than any of you seem to know, since you're planning to drag her to whatever it is you're building in that citadel. I've heard the rumors, Inquisitor. Engines. Cages. Is that what the Crown calls salvation now?"

"Silence, hedge-witch."

"Baker," Odessa corrected. "Hedge-witch was my mother. I only ever got as far as bread."

Despite everything—despite the wards humming, despite the crossbows, despite the child peering out from behind Juniper's cloak with eyes the color of deep water—something in Sarel's chest twitched toward laughter, and she killed it immediately. Laughter was a door. Doors were dangerous.

"Juniper." She stepped closer, boots splashing, and made her voice the voice she used in interrogation rooms, level and cold and final. "You have run well. Better than I expected. But it ends here, at this ugly little river, and you can end it with dignity or you can end it bleeding. Those are the options. I've been generous with the options."

"You were always generous with the options," Juniper said. "You were never once generous with the reasons."

And there it was. Seven years compressed into one sentence, delivered without heat, without hatred—delivered almost gently—and it cut deeper than any curse the old order had ever taught them.

Because Sarel had expected hatred. She had rehearsed for hatred. Hatred was clean; it justified itself; it let you do what needed doing with a clear ledger. She had built the whole architecture of the last seven years on the assumption that if she ever found Juniper again, Juniper would look at her the way the survivors of the Night of Silencing must look at her name in their nightmares.

She had not prepared for compassion. Compassion from the woman whose family she had sold was obscene. It was worse than a blade. It made her feel *seen*, and being seen was the one thing the Inquisitor Voss could not survive.

"You sound the alarm yourself," Juniper continued, quiet now, just for the two of them, though the whole bank leaned in to listen anyway. Rivers carry voices strangely. "That night. Before they came. I've had seven years to make excuses for you—I told myself you were forced, that they held something over you, that anyone breaks eventually. But you weren't forced, were you, Sare. You *chose*. And you've been choosing it every day since, because stopping would mean admitting what the first choice cost."

"Careful," Sarel said, and her voice cracked on the word, which was unforgivable, which was catastrophic.

"There she is," Juniper murmured. "There you are. Under all that gray wool and the Crown's little eye. Do you sleep, Sarel? Do you actually sleep?"

"Take them," Sarel said.

* * *

It should have been simple. Wardens waded in; Odessa raised her hands with theatrical slowness; the boy Tam spat curses with impressive vocabulary for his age; the child Wren stood motionless in the water, watching Sarel with those impossible eyes, and did not struggle at all.

What was not simple was the moment the wardens reached Juniper, and she looked past all of them—at the lanterns, at the willows, at Sarel—and began, very softly, to laugh.

Not bitter laughter. Something else. Something almost fond.

"What," Sarel demanded, "could possibly amuse you."

"You waited three days at a tollhouse." Juniper lifted her wrists to be bound, cooperative as a saint, infuriating as sunrise. "Three days. In a ruin. With forty men and a fisherman's worth of wards. You always hated camping, Sarel. You complained about the mud at Thornfell for a month. You wrote a *letter* about the mud."

"That was fifteen years ago."

"You hated the food on campaign. You used to trade your rations to me for the pickled things nobody wanted, because at least pickled things tasted like the capital." Juniper's voice stayed gentle, which was the worst part; gentleness was the cruelest register she owned. "You've spent seven years becoming someone who waits in ruins, and you didn't even notice it happening. Did you? Nobody notices. That's the trick of it. You just wake up one day and you're the person who does this."

"Bind her mouth if she keeps talking," Sarel snapped, and hated herself for the order, and hated Juniper more for making it necessary, and understood with terrible clarity that neither hatred was aimed correctly.

They bound her hands but left her mouth—Sarel could not bring herself to give the countermand aloud, but she also did not enforce the gag, and everyone present noticed both facts. The company was marched up the bank. Odessa went quietly while delivering a running critique of the wardens' rope work. Tam fought until a warden cuffed him lightly and he subsided with the wounded dignity of a cat in a bath.

And then the child turned around.

Wren looked up at Sarel Voss—inquisitor, witch-finder, breaker of oaths—and did not scream, did not cry, did not plead. She looked at her the way one looks at a locked room whose key one has just found under a loose floorboard.

Then she spoke. One word. Not aloud—Sarel heard it *inside*, the way she had once heard the First Speech inside the cloister walls, before she had learned to build doors against it.

The word was *sister*.

Sarel stopped walking.

The column stopped with her, confused. The lanterns swung. The river went on saying whatever rivers say.

"It speaks to people," Captain Ives said uncertainly. "The mute ones sometimes—it's a trick of the Hollow, Inquisitor, it gets in your—"

"I know what it does," Sarel said, more harshly than intended. "Move."

But she did not move. For three heartbeats she stood in the mud of Millbrook with a dead order's word lodged behind her ribs like a swallowed coal, and the child watched her take it, and there was no triumph in the small face at all. Only a kind of sorrowing attention, the look of someone reading a letter meant for another woman—a younger one, an oath-sister, one who had once sat up all night learning the shapes of the First Speech because her friend couldn't sleep and the stars were good company.

Sarel had buried that girl under seven years of gray wool and the Crown's silver eye. The child dug her up with a single word, casually, the way children dig up anything.

"Chain the child's hands," Sarel said. Her voice was perfectly steady. Somewhere beneath it, something was not. "Gently. We ride for Corvane before dawn."

* * *

They made camp on the high ground above the ford, prisoners and captors alike sharing firelight because there was no practical alternative, and Sarel sat apart in the ruined shell of the old mill, doing what she did instead of sleeping: reviewing the operation, cataloguing errors, confirming conclusions.

Conclusion one: the capture was successful. Conclusion two: the anchor was secured. Conclusion three: the realm would be saved, because the Crown's engineers had promised that the engine could draw the Hollow's power through a living anchor and ration it—orderly, lawful, controlled—instead of letting wild speech rot the world's foundations. A covenant was anarchy wearing robes. An engine was law.

These were excellent conclusions. They had been excellent for months. So it was intolerable that they would not sit still in her mind tonight.

The flap of the mill rustled, and she knew before turning who it was. Guards had been posted. Guards were irrelevant where Juniper was concerned; they had never once been relevant.

"You're supposed to be bound," Sarel said.

"I picked the knots. Odessa ties sentimental knots." Juniper settled against the doorpost, unbound hands loose at her sides, making no move whatsoever. A prisoner-shaped ghost. "I could walk out of here, you know. Past your sentries, past your wards. You've seen me do harder things."

"If you could leave, you'd have left."

"I'm staying because Wren is asleep ten feet from that fire, and because Tam finally stopped crying twenty minutes ago and I'm not going to restart it, and because—" Juniper exhaled. "Because I want five minutes with you before whatever comes next takes the option away. That's all. Five minutes. Then I'll go back to my rope and you can go back to whatever it is you do instead of sleeping."

Sarel said nothing. Silence was her profession. But silence, tonight, felt less like armor and more like an open wound being asked to hold a conversation.

"The engine," Juniper said quietly. "Do you know what it actually does? Not the pamphlet version. The real one."

"It gives the Crown control of spoken power. Orderly allocation. An end to witch-burnings, an end to purges—an end to *nights like the one that took our order*." Sarel heard her own voice rise and mastered it. "You think I enjoyed the silencing laws? I wrote half the amendments that keep hedge-craft from being a hanging offense. I have spent seven years trying to make the cage humane, Juniper, because the alternative was chaos, and chaos is what killed everyone we loved."

"A covenant isn't chaos."

"The covenant failed. It frayed. It let the Hollow starve and leak and whisper into children born without tongues—" Sarel caught herself, appalled at what had slipped out, at how much she knew, and pressed on because retreat was worse. "The old ways died at that cloister. All of them. What the Crown builds now is the only future left that doesn't require trusting magic to behave."

Juniper was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft and terrible.

"Do you hear yourself? You're describing a leash. You're proposing to chain the source of every blessing that ever kept a village alive through winter, and feed it drop by drop to whoever holds the keys. And you think that's the *humane* cage." She shook her head slowly. "You didn't betray us because you loved the Crown, Sare. I finally understand that. You betrayed us because you were afraid, and fear does arithmetic, and your arithmetic always rounds down to 'control it before it hurts me.' You'd have made a wonderful engineer."

"Careful."

"No. You never once let me be careful with you. Fifteen years of 'say what you mean, Juniper, honesty is the first discipline.'" Juniper pushed off from the doorpost, and for a heartbeat she was close enough that Sarel could see the gray in her hair that hadn't been there seven years ago, the new scar at her temple, the ordinary human cost of the years between them. "So here it is, honestly. I don't hate you. I tried. Hate needs a person at the far end of it, and the person I find when I look down this road is a scared girl sitting up with me at Thornfell, trading rations, learning a language that was older than kingdoms because it made her friend happy. I can't hate her. I've spent seven years trying and I can't do it. And I think—" her voice caught, steadied— "I think some part of you has spent those same seven years trying to hate me, and failing just as badly, and building the engine-sized cage around that failure so it can't get out."

The silence afterward lasted four of Sarel's heartbeats, which was three more than she permitted anything.

"Go back to your rope," she said at last.

Juniper went. At the flap she paused, not turning. "When the engine starts, watch Wren's face. Not the machine's. Hers. If you can still fail to hate us after that, then at least you'll know what you are." And she was gone, a ghost returning to her shape by the fire.

* * *

Sarel did not sleep. She stood at the mill window instead, watching the fire burn low, watching the small shape of the child curled between the baker and the boy, guarded by enemies more faithfully than any warden had ever guarded anything.

Two answers. Both right, or both wrong—she genuinely could no longer tell. Save the realm by chaining its voice, or save it by giving the voice away. Control, or surrender. The cage, or the garden.

Her certainty had come apart at Millbrook like a knot picked by sentimental fingers, and what remained underneath was not resolve. It was a scared girl, and a river, and a word planted behind her ribs that burned like a swallowed coal:

*Sister.*

At dawn they would ride for Corvane. At dawn she would deliver the last anchor of the Hollow into the hands of engineers, and the engine would drink, and the world would be ordered forever.

Unless.

Captain Ives found her there at first light, already saddling. "Orders, Inquisitor?"

Sarel looked east, where the road ran home to the citadel, and then—briefly, traitorously—north, toward Thornfell, toward thresholds, toward everything she had spent seven years burying.

"We ride for Corvane," she said. "Full speed."

And beneath her breath, so low that only the river heard, she added the second part—the part that was either treachery or the first honest sentence she'd spoken in seven years:

"—and pray to whatever's left of the Hollow that one of us is right."
