## 12. Cage of Quiet

The cage was made of quiet.

That was the first thing Wren understood about it, before she understood the walls or the chains or the woman with the tired eyes who had carried her here like a sack of turnips. The citadel did not keep her in with bars. It kept her in with *nothing*—a room where sound went to die, where every word she tried to speak fell out of her mouth and lay on the floor like a dropped egg.

She had never been anywhere so loud with silence in her whole small life.

Wren sat cross-legged on a cot that smelled of other people's fear and considered the problem. She was eight years old, more or less—time worked strangely when you were made partly of a place that had no clocks—and she had been stolen from her father's house once before, long ago, by people who wanted to use her. This was not that. This was worse, because this time she could feel what they were doing to him.

*Him* being the Hollow. Her father. The vast dark garden underneath everything, the place where all words were born before they climbed up into mouths.

He was screaming.

Not with sound. Sound was for the world above; he had given all of that away at the beginning, when the first covenant was struck, when the first speaker stood at his threshold and said *we will trade you our voices for your gifts*. He screamed the way roots scream under frost, the way a river screams inside ice: silently, endlessly, through every stone of this citadel. Wren could hear it because she was made of the same stuff as the stones' dreams.

They had built an engine, the iron-thoughted men below. She had seen it only in glimpses, dragged past it in the grey hours, but she knew it the way you know a toothache. It was a machine shaped like a question no one should ever ask: *what if we took the voice and gave nothing back?*

And they had begun to drink from her.

It had started the second night. Men in leather aprons came with copper cups and long needles and words like *extraction* and *yield*, which were ugly words, words with elbows. They pressed the needles into the soft places of her arms, and something in her—not blood, something older than blood, something that was mostly *meaning*—flowed up the tubes and away into the engine's throat.

The engine drank and grew warm. The Hollow grew colder.

Wren lay on the cot afterward and thought about it with the particular seriousness of children, who are the only people honest enough to think clearly about terrible things.

Here was the trouble: she was the anchor. That was the word Quill had used, back in the Undermarket, back when Tam's hand was holding hers and Juniper's face was doing the thing it did when it was frightened but pretending to be a shopkeeper. An anchor holds a boat to the bottom of the sea. Wren held the world to her father. As long as she lived and remembered herself, the covenant held, thin as it was. And now these men were pulling her apart thread by thread to feed their machine, and every thread they pulled was a thread pulled out of everything.

If she simply stopped feeding them—if she curled up inside herself like a fern at dusk—they would have nothing. The engine would choke. Her father would have time.

But then the engine-men would get angry. And angry men with needles do not stop; they press harder. And there was another thing, a smaller thing, a thing she kept in the pocket of her heart where she kept Tam's story about the fox who was afraid of chickens:

Every drop they took from her hurt her father less than it would have if she let them take it from the world instead.

Because that was the secret the engine-men had not understood, being men who thought in ledgers: the Hollow was not a well. He was a heart. And a heart does not care where its strength goes, only that it keeps going somewhere. When they bled Wren into the machine, the machine spent her gift on chains and rationing and the cold arithmetic of power—but some small part of it leaked, always leaked, the way any bucket leaks, and the leaks fell back down through the floor of the world and into the dark garden, and her father caught every drop and saved it.

So Wren made her decision the way she made all decisions: completely, quietly, and without telling anyone, because there was no one to tell.

She would let them take. She would pour. She would be a cup turned upside down, and she would smile while she emptied, because smiling confused the apron-men wonderfully, and confusing cruel men is one of the few free pleasures left in the world.

* * *

The days in the cage of quiet had a shape, and the shape was this:

Morning: the door opened. A woman brought bread and water. The woman never looked at Wren, because looking at Wren made people feel things they had been trained not to feel, and feeling things in a citadel was dangerous, like striking a match in a powder store. Wren always said thank you anyway, in First Speech, and watched the thank-you fall to the floor and break, and felt sorry for it. Unspoken thanks were the saddest words. They just lay there, wanting to be delivered.

Midday: the needle-men came. She had names for them. There was Tall Sorrow, who was gentle and hated himself, and Short Anger, who was gentle to no one including himself, and the Ledger, who counted everything twice and believed in nothing once. They strapped her arm to a board and opened the little gate in her and let her run out into the copper cups.

She had learned a trick. She could not stop the taking, but she could choose what they took. Meaning came in flavors. So when the cups filled, Wren filled them with the plainest, most stubborn meanings she owned: the taste of Odessa's bread. The weight of Tam's hand. The exact brown of Juniper's eyes when Juniper lied badly about the price of luck knots. Let the engine chew on *bread*. Let it try to build a kingdom out of *warm*. Somewhere deep in its iron thoughts, against its will, it would learn to miss things.

Evening: the quiet got loudest, and Wren listened to her father scream, and she answered him.

This was the part no one saw, and it was the part that mattered most. She could not speak aloud—the cage ate her words—but she did not need her mouth. She was her father's daughter. She spoke down through the floor, through the foundations, through the old bones of the mountain, in the way trees speak to trees. *I'm here,* she told him. *I'm still here. Hold on.*

And he answered, faint as starlight on water: *Little root. Little song. You are spending yourself.*

*I know.*

*It will make you thin.*

*I know that too.* She had learned from watching Juniper that the best answer to a warning you intend to ignore is to agree with it quickly and change the subject. *Tell me about the garden. Tell me about the first flower.*

And he would tell her, in the wordless way, and she would listen until sleep took her, and in the morning the needle-men would come again, and she would open the gate.

She was getting thinner. She could see it when they brought the copper basin for washing. Not thinner the way hungry children get thin—thinner the way morning mist gets thin, her edges going soft and translucent, lamplight passing faintly through her wrist. Sometimes she held her hand up to the candle and played a game: how many fingers could she count through her own palm? Three was a good day. Last night it had been four, and one of those was blurry.

Tam would have made a joke. Tam was very good at jokes in dark places; it was his way of carrying heavy things, the way other people used carts. *You're going transparent, Wren, soon I'll be able to keep an eye on you by watching the wall behind you.* She missed him with a specific ache, in the place behind her ribs where his hand fit. She missed Odessa's kitchen, which always smelled like tomorrow. She missed Juniper most of all, in a way she did not have words for even in First Speech, which was saying something, because First Speech had words for eleven different kinds of missing.

But missing people was fuel too. She banked it carefully. You could not pour out of an empty cup, and Wren intended to go on pouring for a long time yet.

* * *

On the ninth day—or maybe the eleventh; days were slippery in the cage of quiet—the tired-eyed woman came back.

Sarel Voss. Inquisitor. Oath-breaker. Juniper's sister, though neither of them said the word anymore, and both of them said it constantly in the spaces around their other words.

Wren had been afraid of her at Millbrook, the way you're afraid of a storm. But storms, Wren had learned from her father, are just weather having feelings it can't express. And now, locked in a cell with nothing to do but listen, Wren listened—and heard what was underneath the Inquisitor the way you hear water under a frozen pond.

Grief. Enormous, ancient, carefully buried grief, packed down under seven years of duty like bones under a road.

Everyone in the citadel thought Sarel Voss was made of iron. Wren knew better. Iron rusts from the inside when salt water gets in, and Sarel was full of salt water. She had been crying for years without tears. She had sounded the alarm on the Night of Silencing—Wren had seen it in the echo of Juniper's telling, tasted it in the air of the ruined cloister—and she had done it believing she was saving her sisters, and they had died anyway, and she had spent every day since building a tower of reasons why it had been necessary, and towers built over graves lean.

The strange thing—the thing Wren found herself turning over like a smooth stone—was that Sarel was not wrong about everything. That was the trap of it. The Crown's new covenant, the engine, the rationing of speech to the powerful: it would work, in the way a dam works. It would hold back the flood. It would also drown the valley and call the drowning *order*. Sarel genuinely believed she was saving the realm. She had taken the worst job in the world on purpose, so that someone with a conscience would be holding the whip, and the whip had grown into her hand.

People like that, Wren's father had told her once, in the garden-time, in the before—you cannot fight them with fire. Fire only proves them right. You have to show them their own reflection, gently, at an hour when they cannot look away.

Wren was small. She was getting thinner. She had no army, no blade, no voice the cage would let her keep.

But she had dreams. Dreams were doors, and doors were her family's oldest craft.

* * *

That night, when the needle-men had gone and the lamp outside her cell had burned down to a red seed, Wren did her work.

She lay on the cot and slowed her breathing until it matched the breathing of the citadel itself—the great stone lungs of the place, inhaling torch-smoke, exhaling rumor. Then she reached, delicately, the way you reach into a bird's nest you don't want to frighten, up through the floor and along the corridors and into the Inquisitor's tower, where Sarel Voss sat awake at a desk she never slept beside, writing reports full of words she did not believe.

Wren could not make Sarel sleep. But she could wait. Everyone sleeps eventually. Even towers lean enough to rest.

Near the hour when the candles give up, Sarel finally lay down on her narrow bed, boots off, sword in reach, as always. And as she sank under, Wren slipped in after her, light as a thought, carrying one word.

She had chosen it carefully. It had taken her three nights to choose. Not *guilt*—guilt would only make Sarel armor up. Not *forgiveness*—forgiveness offered too early is just another kind of violence. Not even *Juniper*, though the name sat in Wren's mouth like honey all day, tempting her.

The word was *sister*.

Just that. Spoken in First Speech, where words are true whether you want them to be or not. Wren set it down in the soft soil of Sarel's dreaming, the way Odessa set seedlings in trays, and patted it in, and whispered the only spell her father's garden had ever needed: *grow.*

Then she withdrew, and lay in the dark of her cage, and listened to the Inquisitor's breath change.

In her dream, Sarel Voss was standing in a cloister that no longer existed, and seven women were laughing around a table, and one of them looked up and said her name the old way, the fond way, the way you say a word you've owned since childhood—and Sarel, in her dream, made a sound Wren had never heard her make awake, a small cracked sound like ice going out in spring.

Wren smiled in the dark and counted her fingers through her own hand. Four. One blurry.

*Hold on, Father,* she said down through the stones. *I've planted something. Things are going to start growing soon.*

Far beneath the world, vast and gentle and starving, the Hollow held her a little closer, the way you hold a candle flame cupped in two hands against the wind.

And three floors up, an Inquisitor woke before dawn with wet cheeks and no memory of why, and found, written on the fogged window glass in letters of melting frost, a single word in a language older than any kingdom:

*Sister.*

She stared at it for a long time. Then she did not wipe it away.

Down in her cage of quiet, Wren felt the word take root, felt the first hair-fine thread of it push down through seven years of frozen ground—and she closed her eyes, thinner than mist, smiling like a girl who has hidden a seed where no gardener can dig it out, and waited for spring.
