## 13. The Hollow-Engine

The citadel of Corvane had never smelled of anything but stone and ink and the tallow of a thousand candles burned by men writing down other men's sins. Sarel Voss had walked its corridors for seven years and thought she knew every smell it owned.

She was wrong. The engine smelled of copper and milk.

That was the detail that unsettled her most, in the long climb down the spiral stair behind Architect Halden Marn—the milk. Somewhere below them, something vast was being fed, and it was being fed the way one fed an infant: warm, white, patiently, from a source that did not consent.

"Mind the third step," Marn said over his shoulder. He was a small man, precise as a compass point, with ink-stained fingers and the cheerful manner of someone who had never once doubted himself. "It's been loose since Tuesday. The masons are terribly sorry."

"The masons built a machine that drinks children," Sarel said, "and they're sorry about a step."

Marn laughed—a bright, unbothered sound. "Inquisitor Voss. You have such a gift for framing." He patted the wall affectionately as they descended. "You'll feel better when you see her. Everyone does. There's something about her that—" he searched for the word, found it, delivered it with genuine warmth— "*reassures.*"

*Her.* The child. Wren. Who had sat in Sarel's custody for three days now, silent except in that terrible old language, watching Sarel with eyes that saw too much, who had put a word in Sarel's dreams that Sarel still could not dig out, the way you cannot dig out a splinter you cannot find.

They reached the bottom. The doors were iron-banded oak, taller than any door had a right to be, and carved with the sigil of the Crown: a closed mouth above an open hand.

"Behold," said Marn, and pushed them open, "the future."

* * *

Sarel had imagined many things on the stairs. A furnace. A forge. Some vast brass heart, pistons hammering.

What she saw instead made her think, absurdly, of a cathedral and a well having a child together.

The chamber was enormous, a hollowed vault beneath the citadel older than the Crown itself—and Sarel knew, with a sick lurch, exactly how old. She had stood in this shape of space before, once, in another life, at a threshold like the one her order had guarded. The Crown hadn't built the engine here. They'd built it *around* something that was already here.

At the chamber's center stood the engine: a ring of black iron thirty feet across, upright as a coin on its edge, inscribed with script Sarel's body recognized before her mind did. First Speech. Thousands of characters of it, cut into the metal, arranged not as words but as *chains*—each phrase a shackle, each clause a lock. The old tongue of creation, repurposed into grammar that bound instead of spoke.

Within the ring hung the Hollow's light—or what the engineers had caught of it. It looked like a slow silver mist, dimming and brightening like breath, pressing against the iron as though against glass. Every few seconds it surged, struck the ring, and fell back. It reminded Sarel of nothing so much as a bird testing the bars of a cage, over and over, with the patience of something that did not yet understand the bars would never open.

And beneath the ring, at its base, was the cradle.

Wren lay in it.

She was strapped gently—*gently*, that was the horror of it, everything here was gentle—cushioned, warmed, tended by three attendants in clean aprons who moved about her with the soft efficiency of nurses. Thin lines of copper ran from the cradle up into the ring's base, and along those lines ran the milk-silver light, steady as veins, carrying something out of the child and into the cage.

"You see," Marn said, spreading his hands like a man unveiling a bride, "the problem with the old covenant is that it was a *conversation*. Freely given, freely withdrawn. Unreliable. Democratic, if you'll forgive the word. One farmer's blessing worth as much as a king's." He clicked his tongue. "Chaos. But the Hollow needs an anchor to remain stable in our world—a living anchor, a willing one, or so the old texts say. And we have," he beamed, "found one."

"She's eight years old," Sarel said.

"She's the last anchor of a dying realm, Inquisitor. Age is a technicality of meat." Marn strolled toward the cradle, and his voice softened into something almost tender. "We don't even need her to *suffer*, which I know concerns your people. The extraction is painless. She sleeps most of the time. We feed her well. When the process completes—when the Hollow is fully held within the ring—we won't need her anymore at all, and she can live out her life however she likes. A pension, even. I've argued for it."

"And until then?"

"Until then, the Crown rations speech as the Crown rations grain. To those who can use it wisely." He smiled at Sarel, and there was nothing cruel in it, which was the worst thing about him. "No more hedge-witches blessing bread in cellars. No more swordspeakers cutting down honest soldiers with a shouted syllable. Order. *Provenance* for power. Every miracle stamped with a seal. Isn't that what you've spent seven years bleeding for, Voss? An end to the chaos?"

Sarel looked at Wren.

The child's eyes were half-open. They found Sarel's across the chamber, and they were not afraid, and they were not angry, and somehow both of those absences were worse than screaming. Wren looked at her the way one looks at weather. At something large and inevitable and sad.

Then, slowly, the child raised one small hand off the cradle's edge—as far as the straps allowed—and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart.

And Sarel heard, inside her own skull, in a voice like water finding a crack:

*It doesn't hurt. That's what should frighten you.*

* * *

Sarel did not remember climbing back up the stairs. She remembered standing afterward in the records room with a cup of wine she wasn't drinking, watching the candle flame do its ordinary faithful work, and hating herself for the ordinary comfort of it.

Three days. Three days the child had been in her keeping, and Sarel had told herself the story she always told: the story of necessity. The order had failed. The covenant had failed. Seven years ago she had watched the Night of Silencing burn through the cloister, had watched Juniper Villalobos—her sister, her *sworn* sister, the best of them—vanish into the smoke rather than die for a dying promise, and Sarel had made her choice in that fire and lived inside it ever since. The silencing kept the peace. The peace kept the people. If magic was a knife, then take away every knife; it was simple, it was clean, and it had the great advantage of requiring no one to look at what Sarel had done to buy it.

But the engine was not the absence of knives. The engine was one knife, kept, and sharpened, and pointed downward at everyone else.

*Freely given,* the old oath went. *Never taken.*

Marn's people took.

A knock at the door. "Enter," she said, and Master Quill came in.

He was, as always, difficult to hold in memory. Tall? Perhaps. Pleasant-faced in a way that slid off the eye like rain off glass. He wore good boots and carried himself like a man who had never once been turned away from a door, and he had arrived at the citadel two days after Wren's capture with letters of introduction so impeccable they were nearly insulting.

"Inquisitor." He bowed slightly. "You sent for me."

"I want to know who you are."

"I'm hurt. I'm your intelligence liaison. I'm the reason your wardens found the Brann woman's bakery." He settled into the chair opposite without being asked, which Sarel noted, and filed, and resented. "I'm the reason you'll catch Villalobos before week's end. Really, I'd think that would be answer enough."

"It isn't. You move like a man whose employer is very far away and very patient. Wardens don't move like that. The Inquisition doesn't move like that." She set down the cup. "Who built the engine, Quill? Not Marn—he's a craftsman. Craftsmen have masters. Who is paying for thirty feet of chained First Speech?"

For just a moment—half a heartbeat, no more—something moved behind Quill's forgettable face. Something old, and cold, and amused.

"Clever," he said. "They did say you were clever. It's why I requested this posting." He leaned forward, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—not deeper, but *denser*, the way air changes before a storm. "You're right that I serve someone. You wouldn't believe me if I told you who, so I'll tell you what: my patron believes the engine will work. Believes it completely. Which is delightful, because my patron also knows it won't—or rather, that what it does instead will be so much more instructive. You see, Inquisitor, the Hollow cannot truly be caged. But it can be *wounded*. And a wounded god, bleeding out through a machine, dripping power for whoever holds the valves—" he spread his hands, mirroring Marn's proud gesture from hours ago, and made it obscene— "well. My patron finds that arrangement preferable. To everyone."

Sarel's hand had gone to her sword without her permission. Quill observed this with polite interest, the way one observes a dog bark at thunder.

"Here is what happens next," he continued pleasantly. "Villalobos is coming. Of course she's coming—the child is in the building, and whatever else that woman is, she is loyal past the point of sense; I've read her file, or what remains of it. She has gathered her little collection of strays. The baker. The boy. A warden named Ferren who has been feeding them road reports for six days—yes, we know, we've let him run, he's a lovely marker of their position. They intend to strike during the activation, two nights hence, when the ring draws hardest on the anchor and the garrison is stretched thinnest. Their plan is competent. Their plan will fail, because I am going to make certain the garrison is not thin at all, and because your sister-in-oath"—he watched Sarel flinch and catalogued it, she could see him catalogue it, filed away like a coin into a purse—"will walk directly into the one room in this city where she cannot win."

"Why are you telling me this?" Her voice came out low. "Why tell me *anything*? What do you need from me?"

Quill rose, smoothed his coat, and paused at the door. For an instant, absurdly, he looked tired.

"The activation requires a voice to command the ring's opening sequence," he said. "Marn intends it to be yours. Your oath-breaking, you see—it left a shape in you. A hole where the vow used to sit. The ring speaks through holes." He smiled his sliding smile. "Congratulations, Inquisitor Voss. After seven years of hunting witches, you get to be the witch."

The door closed behind him.

Sarel stood alone in the candlelight, and finally drank the wine, and found it tasted of copper and milk.

* * *

She went down again that night, past midnight, past the sleeping attendants' shift-change, past every rule she had spent seven years building into armor. She told herself it was inspection. She told herself it was vigilance. She had gotten very good, these seven years, at telling herself things.

The chamber was quiet. The ring breathed its slow silver breath. The attendants had left a single lamp burning by the cradle, and by its light Sarel saw that Wren was awake, watching the ceiling, lips moving faintly—counting, maybe. Children count. It's how they make the dark manageable.

Sarel stopped beside the cradle. For a long moment neither of them moved.

"I don't expect you to forgive me," Sarel said. Her voice sounded strange in the vast room, stripped of rank, stripped of purpose. "I don't expect anything. I wanted—I need to know if it hurts. Truly. Not what Marn says. What you know."

Wren turned her head. Considered her, with that terrible unafraid patience.

Then she lifted her hand again, and this time she did not press it to her own chest.

She reached out—straining against the straps, fingertips just brushing Sarel's wrist, right over the faded scar where an oath-mark had once burned—and pressed her palm there instead.

And Sarel felt it: not words, not quite. A sensation. Warmth, moving outward. The feeling of a kitchen. Of bread. Of someone laughing in another room. Of being *held* by a place rather than a person.

*This is what it takes,* the water-crack voice said, gentle as ever. *It takes the warm places. That's all it eats. That's all it will ever eat.*

Wren's hand withdrew. Her eyes drifted closed. Within the ring, the silver light surged once against its iron, softly, like a sleeper turning over, and settled.

Sarel stood in the dark a long while.

Then she walked back up the stairs, past the loose third step, out into the cold corridor where the candles burned their obedient thousands, and she stood at the narrow window that faced north, toward the Thornfell, toward the ruins of everything she had helped bury.

Two nights, Quill had said. They would come in two nights, and they would fail, and she was expected to be the voice that opened the cage.

Sarel looked at her own reflection in the black glass—a woman-shaped shadow wearing the Crown's gray—and found she could not remember, for the life of her, which side of the window she was supposed to be standing on.

Far below, faint through forty feet of stone, she heard the ring hum as the night cycle began.

And underneath the hum—so faint she might have imagined it, so faint only a trained ear could have caught it at all—she heard something else. A second rhythm, out in the dark beyond the walls. Footsteps. Many. Moving north along the river road, quick and quiet and hopelessly brave.

Toward her.

Toward the engine.

Toward whatever Juniper Villalobos had decided she was willing to lose.
