## 15. Storming the Citadel

Tam had stolen a lot of things in his twelve years: bread, buttons, a silver spoon once (he'd given it back; it felt unlucky), an entire wheel of cheese that he and three other boys ate behind the tannery until they were sick. But he had never stolen a girl out of a fortress before, and as he crouched in the fog at the base of the citadel wall, listening to Juniper Villalobos lay out the plan in her quiet, terrifying voice, he decided that this was going to be his masterpiece.

"Three ways in," she said. "The gates, which Sarel controls. The river culverts, which are barred. And the old air-vents from when this was a granary—before the Inquisition moved in."

Everyone looked at the vents. Everyone looked at Tam.

"Oh, come on," Tam said. "You're all thinking it. I can see you thinking it."

"You're the only one who'd fit," Odessa said, not unkindly, adjusting the enormous basket strapped to her back. It smelled, even through the wicker, like yeast and warm sugar. "You're also the only one who's been inside. You said so yourself."

"I said I got *caught* inside. When I was nine. They threw me out. That's not reconnaissance, that's just being a nuisance."

"It's exactly reconnaissance," said Warden Pell, the defector, who had the look of a man perpetually surprised to find himself on the wrong side of a door. "You know where the guards stand because they stood over you while they did it."

Tam opened his mouth to argue further and then closed it, because down the street, the citadel loomed up out of the fog like a headstone for something very large, and somewhere inside it Wren was thinning away into nothing, and arguing about ventilation suddenly seemed like a waste of the night.

"Fine," he said. "But if I die in there, somebody tell my gang I went down doing something legendary. Not 'died in a vent.' Write it down. Swear it."

"We swear," said eleven voices, and one of them—Odessa's—cracked a little on the word *swear*, and Tam felt something twist under his ribs that he decided not to think about.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been assembled out of scraps, the way Tam assembled most things. Odessa would walk up to the armory gate with her delivery basket, because the citadel still bought its bread from the city's bakers—the Inquisition, apparently, was not above sourdough—and what was in the baskets was not bread. Or rather, it was bread, but dough that Odessa had fed and coaxed and whispered over for two days straight, dough that had been told, in the old hedge-witch way, to *rise*.

"How much is it going to rise?" Tam had asked.

Odessa had considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions about baking. "Do you remember the flood year? When the mill dam broke?"

"No."

"Neither do I, but my mother talked about it constantly. Imagine that, but indoors, and made of rye."

The warden would turn the gates—he still had friends among the watch, men who owed him money or apologies. Juniper and the hedge-witches would take the courtyard. And Tam would go up the drainpipe on the north face, through the vent behind the old granary loft, and down into the engine chamber, where a girl made mostly of moonlight was holding the world shut with her bare hands.

Simple. Tam had survived on simple plans his whole life. The complicated ones were the ones that got you hanged.

He watched Odessa walk toward the armory gate, broad and unhurried, her basket swinging, nodding pleasantly at the guards like a woman delivering rolls. One guard waved her off. Another sniffed the air. Then the second guard's eyebrows went up, and he said something to the first, and they both leaned toward the basket—

And the basket opened.

Later, people in Corvane would tell the story differently. Some said the dough exploded. Some said it climbed. What actually happened, according to Tam, who saw all of it from thirty feet up a drainpipe with his heart hammering against his teeth, was that the dough simply *rose*, the way dough does, except that it did not stop rising, and it did not care about the shape of the basket, and within the space of ten breaths there was a swelling golden mass the size of a hay wagon pouring across the armory floor like the world's most delicious lava, swallowing weapon racks, swallowing a startled sergeant, swallowing, with a kind of gentle inevitability, the entire rack of crossbows, which disappeared into the crust with a sound like spoons dropped in porridge.

A guard fought free of it up to his waist, hacking at the crust with a knife. The knife stuck. The crust healed around it, warm and patient and faintly smelling of caraway.

"That's my girl's work," Odessa said loudly, to no one, hands on hips, in the tone of a proud mother at a fair, and then she turned and walked away whistling while the armory drowned in bread behind her.

Tam laughed so hard he nearly fell off the drainpipe.

* * *

The vent was exactly where he remembered it, behind a loose grate furred with pigeon mess, and it was exactly as horrible as he remembered. He went in on his elbows, dragging his satchel of Quill's borrowed tools, breathing through his mouth because the alternative was breathing through his nose.

The dark in there was total. He counted his movements the way Wren had taught him—not words, she couldn't say ordinary words anymore without them costing her, but she'd press his fingers, one-two-three-four, a rhythm, a heartbeat to crawl by. *One-two-three-four.* Elbows. Knees. *One-two-three-four.*

Somewhere below him, muffled by stone, the citadel woke up. Bells. Shouting. A horn blowing the long note that meant *breach*, and then another horn answering it from the gates, where Pell's friends were presumably doing whatever turning-the-gates looked like from the inside. Tam heard boots running beneath him, dozens of them, all going away from where he wanted to go, which was the finest music he'd ever heard.

Then the floor shuddered.

Not footsteps. Something deeper, a groan in the bones of the building, and the dust around him trembled, and through the vent slats below he saw light—not lamplight but a cold white glare, pulsing, rhythmic, like the citadel had grown a heart and the heart was wrong.

The engine. Starting up.

Tam crawled faster.

* * *

The vent spat him out into a loft full of old grain sacks, exactly as advertised, and from there it was one ladder, one corridor, and one locked door between him and the engine chamber—which was one more locked door than he liked. He knelt at it, ears burning, and got out Quill's picks, and his hands were shaking so badly that the first attempt just clicked uselessly against the keyhole.

"One-two-three-four," he whispered. It wasn't First Speech. It was better. It was Wren's counting, the rhythm she'd tapped against his palm every night since the road, teaching him patience the only way a mute girl teaches anything: by touch, by repetition, by refusing to give up on him.

The lock gave on the third try.

The room beyond was enormous and round and full of light, and Tam stopped dead in the doorway because his body simply refused to take another step until his eyes understood what they were seeing.

The Hollow-engine filled the center of the chamber like a captive cathedral. Rings of iron and glass, taller than Odessa's bakery, turning slowly around a core of white fire, and chained to the core—actually chained, great black links driven into the floor and ceiling—was a column of darkness that writhed and pressed against its bonds like something drowning. Every time the rings completed a turn, the chains glowed, and the darkness screamed. Tam couldn't hear the scream with his ears. He heard it in his back teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the scar on his knee from when he was six. Everything in him that had ever been hungry or small or afraid sat up and howled along with it.

And at the base of the machine, in a cradle of iron bands, was Wren.

She was almost gone.

That was the thought that arrived first, flat and terrible: *she is almost gone.* She was translucent now, truly translucent, a girl-shaped window onto the machinery behind her. He could see the glow of the core through her chest. Her hair moved in a wind that wasn't there. Her eyes were open, fixed on nothing, on everything, and streams of pale light poured off her fingertips and into the engine's intake throats the way water pours off a squeezed cloth.

She was feeding it. She was feeding it everything she had, and it was drinking, and it was still hungry, and she was smiling.

That was the worst part. She was smiling like someone relieved, like someone finally allowed to set down something heavy, and Tam understood with a lurch that she didn't think she was dying. She thought she was *working*. This was what she was for. This was what everyone had always told her she was for—an anchor, a doorstop, a plug in the world's drain—and she was doing it with a smile because no one had ever taught her that she was allowed to do anything else.

"Well," Tam said aloud, to the empty air, to the screaming dark, to himself. "That's not acceptable."

* * *

Getting to her meant crossing the chamber floor, and the chamber floor was patrolled by two engineers and a priest of the Inquisition who kept consulting a brass instrument and looking worried. Tam did what he did best. He became uninteresting.

Uninteresting was an art. Uninteresting was a boy in a scullery smock—liberated from a hook two corridors back—with his sleeves rolled and a bucket in his hand, walking with the particular slouch of someone who has been ordered to mop something and resents it. Nobody looks at that boy. Kings have been assassinated by less.

He mopped. He mopped a stretch of floor near the engine with genuine resentment. He let one engineer bark at him—"not now, you little gutter-rat"—and shuffled off, and came back by a different arc, closer, bucket slopping. The priest frowned at his instrument. The rings turned. The chains glowed. Wren poured herself out, thinner and thinner, and smiled her awful relieved smile.

On his fourth pass, Tam was close enough to touch the cradle.

"Wren," he whispered.

Her eyes moved. Slowly, like heavy doors. They found him, and for a moment something flickered in them—recognition, and then grief, and then something that might have been *go away, you'll be hurt*—and Tam had seen that exact look before, on Odessa, on Juniper, on every adult who had ever tried to protect him by deciding things for him, and he was sick to death of it.

"Nope," he said. "Don't even start. I didn't crawl through a pigeon vent to be sent home."

He pulled Quill's tools from the bucket's false bottom—a pry bar, a file, and a small vial of grey grit that Quill had called "chain-salt" and refused to explain—and went to work on the iron band at her wrist.

The metal was cold in a way metal shouldn't be. His fingers numbed on contact. The file skated off it twice before biting, and each stroke shrieked, and the engineers shouted, and Tam stopped caring entirely. Let them shout. Let the whole Inquisition shout. He filed, and the band cracked, and a thread of pale light whipped free like a cut rope and wrapped itself around his arm instead—cold, then warm, then singing.

Wren made a sound. Not a word. A sound, high and cracked, the first noise he'd ever heard her make, and it went through him like a hook through a fish.

"I know," he said, working on the second band. "I know it hurts. Hold on. One-two-three-four."

Boots behind him. The priest, shouting now, and the engineers coming with their long brass prods that sparked at the tips. Tam didn't turn around. Turning around was for people with options.

The second band cracked. The third. Light spilled everywhere, pooling on the floor, climbing the walls, and the great chained darkness at the engine's heart stopped screaming and started—listening. The whole room tilted toward Wren like flowers toward sun.

The last band wouldn't break. The file was worn to a nub. The priest's shadow fell over him, and the prod came up sparking, and Tam did the only thing left, the thing he'd sworn he'd never do again because it had gotten him caught when he was nine:

He spoke to the machine.

Not First Speech. He didn't have it. He spoke street, and kitchen, and the language of locked things, the dialect every thief is born fluent in, and what he said—pressing both palms flat against the final band, forehead against the freezing iron—was:

"Please."

It wasn't power. It wasn't magic. It was just true, and maybe that was enough, or maybe the light pouring off Wren heard it and carried it down into the engine's roots, because the band sighed—there was no other word—and split open from clasp to clasp like a pod in summer.

Wren fell forward into his arms, and she weighed nothing. Less than nothing. She was a girl made of morning fog, and she was looking up at him, and her mouth moved, shaping a rhythm against his shoulder: *one-two-three-four.*

"Yeah," Tam said, and his voice broke in the middle, and he didn't care. "Yeah, that's right. Counting. We're going. Both of us."

The prod came down. The priest shouted a word of command. The rings began to turn faster, dragging at the freed light, hauling it back—

And every door in the chamber burst open at once.

Juniper stood in the largest doorway with her sword drawn and eleven impossible people at her back, and her voice, when it came, was the voice Tam had heard only once before, the tide-and-moon voice, and it said one word that none of them could afterward repeat, and the engine's rings stopped turning as if the whole machine had been caught eavesdropping and knew it.

Tam held Wren tighter and started to laugh, because the plan had gone completely sideways, and that, in his experience, was when the real plan usually started.

Juniper's eyes found his across the chamber—found Wren, found the broken bands, found the boy in the stolen smock holding a ghost of a girl—and she nodded, once, the smallest nod in the world.

"Hold on to her," she said. "Whatever happens next—don't let go."
