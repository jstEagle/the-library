## 16. Sisters at the Engine

The heart of the Hollow-engine beat like a drum made of grief.

Juniper felt it through the soles of her boots before she saw it—a slow, arrhythmic thrum that climbed the citadel's iron bones and shook the catwalk where she stood, forty feet above a floor full of running engineers and screaming brass. The engine filled the great central chamber like a cathedral's dark god: coils of silver wire wound around a core of black glass, and inside the glass—

Inside the glass was Wren.

She hung suspended in that impossible sphere, small as a moth in amber, her hair drifting around her head as if underwater. Her edges had gone from translucent to nearly gone. Every few seconds the engine *pulled*, and Juniper watched the child's outline flicker like a candle flame in a draft, and every pull came out through the machine as a thin, high whistle of stolen speech piped into copper throats and fed to the speaking-tubes that ran—Quill's stolen maps had shown them—to every noble house and garrison in Corvane.

Rationed magic. Bottled covenant. The Hollow on a leash, and its daughter as the collar.

"You're blocking my light," said a voice behind her, dry as old paper and twice as brittle. "Though I suppose you always did."

Juniper turned.

Sarel Voss stood at the far end of the catwalk in her Inquisitor's grays, sword drawn, hat gone, white-streaked hair coming loose from its braid in the steam rising from the vents below. Behind her visor of office she looked tired in a way Juniper had never seen—not the tiredness of a long hunt but the tiredness of someone who had been carrying something heavy up a very long hill and had finally run out of hill.

"The light's not yours either," Juniper said. "None of this is."

"No." Sarel walked toward her, unhurried, boots ringing on the grate. Below them, chaos bloomed—she could hear Odessa's distant, triumphant bellow, the groan of the armory doors surrendering under a tide of risen dough, a defected warden's whistle turning half the garrison against the other half. Tam was somewhere in the walls; she had to believe that. "None of it is. And yet here we are, you climbing my citadel like ivy, me standing here with a sword, because apparently neither of us knows how to have an argument indoors like civilized people."

"We tried indoors once," Juniper said. "You burned it down."

The flinch was small—the tiniest contraction around Sarel's eyes—but Juniper had spent eleven years learning to read that face across training yards and campfires and one unforgettable winter in a flooded bothy, and she saw it land exactly where she'd aimed.

"I did what kept me alive," Sarel said.

"I know." Juniper drew her own blade. It wasn't much of a sword—an Undermarket relic Quill had thrown in for free, muttering about sentiment—and her wrist ached where the oath-mark had burned steady now for three days, hot as a held coal. "That was always the problem, Sar. You survived everything. That's different from living."

"Draw or climb down," Sarel said. "I won't ask twice."

"You never do." Juniper settled her grip. "All right. But we're going to talk while we do this. It's traditional."

"It is not traditional."

"Our whole order fought while talking. Swordspeaking, remember? The word and the edge together?" She smiled, and knew it was the smile that had gotten her slapped exactly twice in her life, both times by the woman now leveling a blade at her heart. "You used to say I couldn't shut up long enough to die."

* *

The first exchange rang out over the chamber, steel on steel, and the engineers below scattered like startled pigeons.

Sarel fought the way she always had: economical, cold, every movement arriving exactly where it intended to be, no flourish wasted, no anger wasted either—which was what made fighting her terrifying, because there was no fury to bait and no rhythm to steal. Juniper gave ground along the catwalk, parried, retreated, gave another step, and talked.

"Do you remember the oath-yard at Corvane Cloister? The day we swore?"

"I remember you fainted."

"I did *not* faint, I knelt very suddenly and stayed down for strategic reasons—" Junipper caught a thrust and turned it, boots skidding on iron. "Two hundred of us, Sar. Two hundred voices saying the words together, and the Hollow heard us, you could *feel* it hearing us, like standing in front of an open door with summer coming through it. You were third in line. I could see the back of your neck. You'd braided a ribbon into your hair because you said the oath deserved your best."

"Stop."

"And seven years ago you stood in that same yard and told the King's men where all two hundred of us slept."

The next exchange was not clean. Sarel drove forward with something other than technique behind it, and Juniper gave ground faster, back and back until her shoulder blades met the railing and the drop yawned behind her and the engine's pull hummed through the metal into her spine.

"They were going to burn the city," Sarel said, low, her blade at Juniper's throat, trembling minutely. "They had proof the order sheltered the anchor-child. Voss the elder—you remember him, he of the pleasant voice—he showed me the lists. The pyres they'd already built. Give me the names, he said, and we take only the guilty. Only the speakers." Her jaw worked. "I thought—I *thought*—"

"You thought you could save some of us by naming all of us."

"I thought I could save *you*." It came out cracked down the middle. "Your name was last on the list. Last. I put it there myself. I told them the last swordspeaker was wounded, dying, no threat, and by the time they believed otherwise you were over the wall and gone, and I have spent seven years telling myself that arithmetic. Ninety-nine names for one."

"Ninety-nine names," Juniper echoed softly, "and every single night since, the one."

Sarel struck at her.

Juniper slipped it—not dodged, exactly; more refused to be where the blade needed her to be—and the moment stretched, both of them breathing hard, steam curling between them, and far below someone's rising bread burst the armory doors with a sound like the world's most cheerful cannon.

"Sarel." Juniper didn't raise her guard. She let her sword hang. "I don't hate you. I want you to hear that, because I think you've been waiting seven years for someone to hate you properly, and it would make everything so much simpler if I did. It would let you stop counting."

"Don't."

"Ninety-nine people who trusted you with their sleep. Maren, who taught you to hold a whetstone. Old Cobble, who called you *sparrow* because you ate like one. The twins—"

"*Don't.*"

"—and every night you lay in whatever fine bed they gave you and recited the list like a rosary, and the Crown called it vigilance, and you called it penance, and neither of those words is true. It's just grief wearing armor." Juniper stepped forward, into reach, which was the maddest thing she had ever done, and there had been competition. "And now they've got a child in a bottle, Sar. A child who cannot lie and has never once been cruel, and they are pouring her into that thing a spoonful at a time so that lords can have firewords and farmers can't have fever-charms. Is *that* the arithmetic? Does she go on the list too?"

Sarel's sword point dropped. Just an inch. But from Sarel Voss, an inch was a landslide.

"She's not a child," Sarel whispered. "She's an anchor. The realm needs—"

"So we keep hearing." A new voice, smooth as oiled walnut, from the stairhead: Master Quill, descending with his forgettable face arranged into regret, four citadel guards fanning out behind him with the ugly short crossbows the Inquisition favored. "Inquisitor, splendid work pinning the intruder. Architect's orders: the speaker is to be taken alive. The child's extraction schedule cannot be delayed."

Something happened in Sarel's face then that Juniper would carry for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be. It was the look of a woman hearing, clearly and for the first time, the exact pitch of the cage she'd been living in.

"Extraction schedule," Sarel repeated.

"Time is speech, Inquisitor." Quill smiled at everyone and no one. "Guards, the woman first."

The guards raised their crossbows.

And Sarel Voss, chief witch-finder of Corvane, traitor to her order, keeper of ninety-nine names, moved.

It was not the cold economy Juniper knew. It was fast and graceless and furious, the sword-work of a woman with nothing left to protect about herself, and she cut through all four guards before Quill finished his sentence—disarmed the first, hamstrung the second, took the third's crossbow bolt on her pauldron and put him over the railing into a vat of cooling flux, and caught the fourth's wrist and spoke one word, one flat gray word of command-speech that Juniper hadn't known she still carried, and the fourth guard sat down on the catwalk and began to cry.

Quill was already backing up the stairs, face smoothing itself blank, forgetting itself even as he went, and Juniper lunged past Sarel and shouted "*Stay*" in First Speech, and the word caught Quill like a hook in a fish's jaw. He froze, mid-flight, mouth opening and closing, entirely present and entirely unable to leave.

"Oh, that's *deeply* satisfying," Juniper said. "Sarel. The core. Now, before they cut power or reinforcements—"

But Sarel was already at the rail, looking down at the black glass heart of the engine, at the small dim shape hanging inside it, and her sword hand was shaking.

"I can't," she said. "If I break it wrong, the surge—"

"You won't break it wrong." Juniper came to stand beside her, shoulder to shoulder at the rail, the way they had stood at a hundred watches. "You were second-best swordspeaker of our year."

"You were second-best."

"Mm, and who was first?"

"...Maren." Sarel's mouth twitched, horribly, into something almost like a laugh. "Maren was first."

"Maren was first, and she'd tell you exactly where to strike, and she'd say—" Juniper put her hand over Sarel's gauntlet, over the shaking, and spoke the rest in First Speech, the old tongue, the true one, the language that cannot lie: *Strike like you are sorry, sister. It will listen.*

Sarel closed her eyes. Then she stepped up onto the rail, balanced above the humming dark, and drove her blade into the engine's core with her whole history behind it.

* *

The world went white, then silent, then loud.

The black glass split along a seam no eye could see, and the Hollow came out of it—not flooding, nothing so simple—it *unbent*, like a fist unclenching after years, and every speaking-tube in the citadel exhaled at once. Wren fell into the space where the sphere had been, and Juniper was already over the rail, dropping the last fifteen feet, rolling badly, coming up with her shoulder screaming, catching the child before the floor did.

Wren weighed almost nothing. She was light the way paper is light, edges still fraying, eyes open and fixed on some vast distance only she could see, and her lips were moving.

"What is she saying?" Sarel demanded, sliding down a maintenance ladder, bleeding from somewhere under her pauldron and not caring. "Villalobos, what is she—"

"She's saying thank you." Juniper's throat closed. "To you. Both of us. She says—" Listening to First Speech was like remembering a smell from childhood; the meaning arrived whole, ahead of the words. "She says the Hollow isn't angry. She says it's *dying*, and it wants to go home, and it wants her home, and it's pulling."

As if summoned, the air above the shattered core folded. Not opened—folded, the way cloth folds when fingers pinch it, and through the fold came a sound like wind over the mouth of a cave, vast and patient and terribly sad, and every loose hair on Juniper's arms stood toward it.

Wren's translucent fingers curled toward it. Her body wanted to answer. Juniper could see the wanting, the terrible gravity of it, the daughter called by the mother.

"Wren," Juniper said, and then, because the girl's eyes weren't focusing, "Wren. Little bird. Look at me."

Slowly, the child turned her head. Her gaze found Juniper's face and held it, and in it was knowledge no eight-year-old should carry—knowledge of what was being asked of everyone in this room, of what it would cost and who would pay, though how much of it Juniper could only guess at.

"She can't refuse it," Sarel said hoarsely. "Can she. Whatever it asks, she'll give, because she's made of giving. Look at her."

"I know." Juniper gathered the girl up—paper-light, moth-still—and looked at the fold in the air, and beyond it, in imagination, north to Thornfell where the old threshold stood, where Quill's maps and Odessa's hedge-lore and a dead order's last writings all pointed to the same terrible footnote: *a covenant requires a speaker, and the speaker stays.*

Seven years of hiding her voice. One week of using it. And now it wanted everything.

"Hold her," Juniper said, passing Wren into Sarel's arms—the first time in eleven years she had handed her sworn sister anything, and the look on Sarel's face said she knew it. "Both hands. Don't let her drift toward the fold. I'm getting the others."

"Villalobos." Sarel's voice stopped her at the stairs. It sounded scraped raw, new-made, like a bell recast. "The list. The ninety-nine. If we get through what's coming—"

"Then you'll start a different list," Juniper said gently. "Of children taught instead of burned. I've seen your arithmetic, Sar. You were always better at sums than you let yourself believe."

Above them, the fold widened, patient as winter, and somewhere in the walls Tam's voice was yelling Wren's name, and Odessa's footsteps shook the floor like a kind earthquake, and Juniper Villalobos ran toward all of it—toward the found and the broken and the gathering—already rehearsing, in the oldest language there is, the shape of the goodbye she was beginning to understand she would have to say.
