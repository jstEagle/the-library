## 17. The Threshold of Thornfell

They carried Wren north in Odessa's bread cart, because it was the only vehicle in Corvane that smelled of anything other than fear.

That had been Odessa's idea, delivered in the flat, unarguable tone she used for everything. "A dying child needs warmth and the smell of yeast," she'd said, hauling the great wicker baskets into the cart bed as if she were preparing for a festival rather than a flight. "And I am not leaving her in the cold like a parcel. Get in, all of you, and try not to crush the rolls."

So they rode north through three days of thinning winter light: Wren laid across the baskets like a sleeping queen, Tam curled against her side with his hand fisted in her sleeve as though the wind might steal her if he let go, Sarel riding behind on a requisitioned Inquisition horse with her hands bound to the cart rail by her own request and her face doing something Juniper had never seen it do in twenty years of knowing her.

It was, Juniper thought, watching her over her shoulder, the face of a woman who had burned down her own house and only now turned around to look at the ashes.

The Hollow-engine was a day behind them, a ruin of blackened brass and screaming iron, and Sarel had not spoken more than ten words since she'd driven her blade through its core. Juniper didn't press her. Some silences were wounds, and wounds needed air.

Wren, on the other hand, spoke constantly.

That was the horror of it, the thing that made Juniper's chest ache every hour of the journey. The child was fading—her edges gone soft as wet paper, her skin translucent at the wrists and throat so that when the light caught her, Juniper could see the faint blue lattice of veins like frost on a window—and still she talked. In First Speech, in the trade-tongue, in the half-language of gestures she and Tam had built between them on the road. She narrated the clouds. She named the crows. She asked Odessa eleven times how bread knew when to rise, and each time Odessa gave a different answer, and each time Wren considered it with the gravity of a magistrate.

"She's saying goodbye," Tam said quietly, on the second evening, when Wren had finally gone still. He didn't look at Juniper. He was sharpening a knife he had no intention of using, because sharpening was what Tam did with his hands when his heart was in his throat. "I've heard people do that. On the street, when the fever takes someone. They talk and talk so nobody notices them getting quiet."

Juniper reached over and stilled his hand. "Then we'll be loud enough for both of us."

* * *

The Thornfell announced itself long before they saw it.

The land began to hush. That was the only word Juniper had for it, and she hated how inadequate it was. It wasn't silence—silence was absence, an empty room. This was presence. The birds stopped singing not because they were frightened but because they were listening. The wind moved through the bare trees without sound, as though the trees had agreed, long ago, to hear something more important. Even the cart wheels seemed to roll on wool.

Wren lifted her head for the first time in hours.

*Home,* she said, in First Speech, and the word went through Juniper like a bell through the body.

The oath-mark on her wrist had been burning since Corvane, a low coal-heat she'd learned to ignore, but now it flared—not pain, exactly. Recognition. Like a dog hearing its name after years of being called *dog*.

"There," Sarel said, and her voice cracked on the single syllable.

The threshold stood in a hollow between two hills that leaned toward each other like old friends sharing a secret. Juniper had read about it in the order's oldest books, back when she'd been young and certain and full of words: the First Door, older than any kingdom, older than the order that had guarded its lesser cousins. She had expected a ruin. She had expected stone.

It was a garden.

That was the only way her eyes could render it. A circle of ground where the frost had never reached, where grass grew green and deep in the teeth of winter, where flowers opened in shapes Juniper had no names for—blooms like folded wings, like cupped hands, like the inside of a word. At its center, the air itself stood slightly ajar. Not a door. A *leaning*. The world there was tilted toward somewhere else, and through the tilt Juniper could see—not with her eyes, she realized, but with the oath-mark, with the seven buried years of her—something vast and dim and breathing.

The Hollow. Starving. Waiting.

"Oh," Odessa said softly. She had stopped the cart and climbed down and was standing with her flour-white hands pressed to her mouth. "Oh, I remember this. My grandmother's grandmother used to sing about a green place in the north where the winter couldn't follow. I always thought it was a song about spring." She laughed, a wet, wondering sound. "It was a song about *this*."

Tam was already lifting Wren from the baskets, gentle as a boy carrying a bird with a broken wing. "Almost there," he told her. "Almost home. You can sleep after. Odessa's making that honey bread, the one with the crust like—"

*Tam.* Wren's hand found his cheek. Her fingers were nearly transparent; he could see the dark of her sleeve through them, and he pretended he couldn't, and Juniper loved him so fiercely in that moment that it frightened her. *Listen. It wants me to come in. It's been calling for so long, and I'm so tired, and if I go in I can rest.*

"You're not going in," Tam said. "You're going to eat bread and get better and be annoying forever. That's the plan. I decided."

*It's a bad plan.*

"It's the only plan I've got. Don't make me improvise."

Juniper climbed down from the cart and walked toward the threshold, and with every step the Hollow's voice grew clearer—not words, not yet, but the *shape* of words, the way a melody is the shape of a song. It was a voice like her own voice, if her own voice had been stretched across centuries. It was tired. It was starving. It was so *grateful* to see the child coming that its gratitude had weight, a pressure on the air, a leaning of the whole green hollow toward Wren like a starving man toward bread.

And Juniper understood, with a clarity so terrible it was almost peaceful, that Wren was going to walk in.

Not because anyone would force her. Because she was kind. Because she was eight years old and had spent her whole strange life being pulled at from both sides of the world, and the Hollow was calling her home the way a wound calls a tongue, and she was too worn thin to refuse. She would pour herself into that dim breathing vastness the way she had poured herself into the engine, and she would hold the covenant together from inside, and she would never eat honey bread again, and Tam would sharpen his knives in an empty room for the rest of his life.

No, Juniper thought.

*No.*

* * *

She had read the old rites. That was the thing no one else here had done—Sarel had burned her share of the order's books, Odessa had been a hedge-witch who worked in charms and crumbs, and Tam couldn't read at all until Odessa taught him, and Wren read only in First Speech, which was not a language you could find written down. But Juniper had spent seven years in a shop full of harmless trinkets with a locked chest beneath the floorboards, and in that chest were the order's books, and she had read every one of them so many times she could recite them in the dark.

The rites of reforging were fragmentary. The order had never performed them; the covenant had held for a thousand years without needing repair. But the fragments said things. The fragments said the Hollow needed a *voice*—an anchor, a speaker, someone to stand between it and the world and translate. And the fragments said, in one marginal note in a dead woman's handwriting, in ink gone brown as old blood:

*The anchor need not be of the Hollow. The anchor need only be willing. But oh, the cost. Oh, the cost.*

Juniper had read that note a hundred times and never understood it. Standing at the edge of the green hollow, with the Hollow's hunger pressing against her skin and the child's fading hand in Tam's, she understood it completely.

The cost was everything. But it was *hers* to pay, and Wren's was not.

"Put her down," Juniper said.

Tam looked up, stricken. "Juniper—"

"Put her down gently, and then hold on to her, because she's going to want to walk, and she's too tired to fight you, and I need about a minute." She was already pulling off her gloves. Her hands looked strange to her, bare in the cold—ordinary hands, shopkeeper's hands, hands that had tied luck knots and sold fever whispers and buried a sword under the floorboards of a life half-lived. "Sarel. Come here."

Sarel came. She moved like a woman wading through deep water, and when she stood before Juniper she could not meet her eyes.

"Twenty years ago," Juniper said, "you and I swore an oath together under the winter moon. *One voice, one blade, one keeping.* Do you remember the words?"

"Don't," Sarel whispered.

"I remember them. I've had seven years to remember them, and I've hated every one of those years, and I've hated you for most of them, and I want you to know that none of that matters now, because you're the only other person alive who knows what I'm about to do." Juniper took her sworn sister's face in her hands, the way they used to when they were girls and one of them had a fever, and made her look up. "The Hollow needs a speaker to stay. Wren is too spent to refuse it. So I'm going to refuse it *for* her. The old rites allow it. A willing voice. Mine."

"No." Sarel's composure, that cold beautiful armor she'd worn since the Night of Silencing, came apart all at once, like ice off a branch in spring. "Juniper, no, there has to be another—"

"There isn't. There's an engine in Corvane full of dead engineers and a realm full of people who deserve to speak, and there is one door and one price, and I have spent seven years paying the wrong price—hiding, hoarding, half-living—and I am *done* paying it." She was surprised to find she was smiling. It felt true. "You were right, once, you know. The night you turned. You said the order's way would get us all killed for nothing. You were right about the nothing. So let me make it *something*. Let me fix the one thing you couldn't live with breaking."

"Juniper—" Sarel's voice broke entirely.

"Watch her for me," Juniper said. "Watch all of them. That's your oath now. One voice, one blade, one keeping. *Keep.*"

* * *

The Hollow felt her coming before she reached the center of the garden.

The leaning in the air widened. The dim breathing vastness turned its attention toward her—not Wren, *her*—and Juniper felt it the way you feel the sun come out from behind a cloud: a warmth, a weightlessness, an enormous slow attention. It did not understand her at first. It had been promised a child. It had been promised its daughter. And here instead was a woman with an old oath burning on her wrist and seven years of buried words rising in her throat like a flood.

*I know what you need,* Juniper said, in First Speech, and the words came easier than they had in seven years, easier than they ever had, as if the language had been waiting inside her all along, patient as a seed. *You need a voice. She is too small, and she is loved, and I am neither small nor—well. Loved enough. Loved plenty. Loved by a baker and a thief and a traitor and a child, which is more than the order ever gave me, so don't argue with me. I've been arguing with gods and governments my whole life and I'm very good at it.*

The Hollow's attention deepened. Somewhere in its vast dimness, she felt it turn the offer over, the way Wren turned over Odessa's answers about bread. It was old. It was careful. It had been broken before, by bargains made carelessly, and it would not be broken again.

*The cost,* it said, and its voice was every voice Juniper had ever loved—the order's chanting, her mother's lullabies, Sarel laughing at sixteen, Wren naming crows. *The cost.*

*I know,* Juniper said. *My name. My oath. My memory of them. All of it. Take it. Take everything except the speaking, because the speaking is yours anyway and always was.*

*Juniper, don't—* That was Tam, behind her, and his voice cracked in the middle, and she made herself not turn around, because if she turned around she would never walk forward. *Juniper, you can't, we just—we just got you, we just got the whole—*

"Tam," she said, in the trade-tongue, because First Speech was too true and she needed one small lie to get her through this. "I'm not dying. I'm going to work. It's what I've always done. You'll visit."

She stepped forward.

The green grass took her weight like a hand. The flowers turned toward her—not following her, she realized, but *recognizing* her, the way the oath-mark had recognized the Thornfell. Every step, the Hollow's voice grew clearer and her own name grew fainter, and she held onto it as long as she could, the way you hold a note at the end of a song, the way Tam held Wren's sleeve, the way Odessa held the shape of a recipe in her hands without ever writing it down.

*Juniper Villalobos,* she said to the Hollow, offering it up like bread. *Last of the swordspeakers. Keeper of an oath my order died keeping. Take my name. It's a good one. Use it gently.*

The Hollow opened.

Not the leaning in the air—*everything*. The garden, the hills, the hush, the sky itself, all of it opening like a throat, like a door, like a book to a page that had been waiting seven years and a thousand before that. And Juniper walked into the light of it with her arms open and her voice ready, and behind her she heard Odessa weeping without shame and Sarel saying the old oath words like a prayer and Tam shouting her name, her name, her name, as if saying it enough times could keep it from being taken—

And the Hollow, vast and gentle and starving no longer, reached for her with a tenderness that felt, in the last moment she was still herself, exactly like being *known*—

And Wren screamed.

Not in grief. In First Speech. One word, sharp and bright as a struck bell, and Juniper—half-dissolved, her edges already going soft as the child's had been—felt the word land on her like a hand on a shoulder.

She knew the word. She had taught it to Wren herself, on the road north, in a lesson about defensive speech.

It was the word that meant *wait.*
