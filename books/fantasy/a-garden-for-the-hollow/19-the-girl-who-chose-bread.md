## 19. The Girl Who Chose Bread

The Hollow did not have doors.

Wren had known this the way she had known everything before she was born into a body: not as fact but as texture, as the feeling of water against a fish's side. The Hollow was all threshold and no wall, a place made of listening. When Juniper spoke her free—spoke her *loose*, unhooked her from the great slow pulse that had been pulling at her edges since the night she first knocked on a shop door in Corvane—Wren felt the anchor-cord dissolve like sugar in warm milk.

And then she was standing in the garden.

It was not a garden the way Odessa's kitchen was a kitchen, with flour and fire and things you could burn your fingers on. It was a garden the way a memory of summer is a summer: everywhere and nowhere, green without leaves, light without sun. Paths wound through it that were made of words half-spoken. Every promise anyone had ever kept grew here like hedges. Wren could see them—rows and rows of them, silver and humming, promises about bread and rain and *I'll come back* and *you'll be safe now*. Some were very old. Some were cracked and brown, promises broken long ago, and the Hollow tended those most carefully of all, because broken things were its favorite patients.

She knew this place. She had been made here. Or near here. Or *of* here—the grammar got slippery when she tried to think about it in human words, which was one of the many problems with human words, though also, she was beginning to suspect, one of their best features.

The voice came from everywhere, the way it always had. But it was different now. It had changed shape while she was away, wearing itself thin and mortal-shaped, and when it spoke, it spoke in a voice Wren knew better than her own heartbeat.

*"Hello, little anchor."*

Wren's breath caught. Not because she was afraid. Because the voice was tired in a way the Hollow had never been tired, and it was *warm* in a way the Hollow had never quite managed either, and both of those changes had a name, and the name was Juniper Villalobos, who had given up everything so that Wren could stand here with knees that ached and lungs that burned and a heart doing something arrhythmic and ridiculous in her chest.

"You sound different," Wren said. Her voice came out small. Human-small. She was still getting used to how loud human voices were inside a human head, how they rattled around like pebbles in a tin cup.

*"I know,"* said the Hollow-Juniper, and there was something almost sheepish in it, which was such a Juniper thing to be that Wren laughed—a wet, hiccupping laugh, the kind Tam called an ugly cry and pretended he wasn't having too. *"Everything sounds different when you've had hands."*

"Did it hurt?" Wren asked. "Becoming?"

A long pause. In the pause, the garden rustled. Somewhere far off, a promise about a lullaby bloomed.

*"Yes,"* said the voice, honestly, because the Hollow could not lie and neither, apparently, could what it had become. *"But I would do it again. That's the strange part. That's the part I'm still learning to hold."*

Wren looked down at herself. She was solid again—fully, stubbornly solid, opaque as a fence post, as Tam would say. No more translucence. No more watching lamplight pass through her own wrist. But the Hollow stood open before her, patient as weather, and she understood with terrible clarity why she had been brought here, or rather why she had *carried herself* here, because the Hollow never dragged anyone anywhere. That was the whole point of it. That was the covenant, old and new: nothing given under compulsion was ever truly given.

The choice sat in front of her like a door.

She could go back. She could pour herself home into the vast green listening, become current instead of creature, anchor instead of girl. It would not hurt. It would be like falling asleep in a warm bath, the Hollow promised—and the Hollow could not lie, so it must be true. She would hold every promise ever spoken. She would feel every kindness in the world happen in real time, forever. She would be enormous.

She would also, she realized, never eat bread again.

This seemed, on balance, like a dealbreaker.

* * *

"I want to see," Wren said. "Before I choose. Is that allowed?"

*"Little anchor,"* said the voice, with Juniper's dry edge riding underneath it, *"everything is allowed here. That's rather the crisis we just spent twenty chapters resolving."*

"I don't know what chapters are."

*"Neither do I. It came with the name. Ignore me."*

The garden obliged. The paths rearranged themselves—not moving, exactly, but deciding they had always gone where Wren needed them to go—and she walked, and as she walked, the Hollow showed her what returning would mean.

She saw herself vast. She saw herself holding the thread of a fever breaking in a fishing village three hundred miles south, and a midwife's blessing in a snowbound farmhouse, and a boy in Corvane whispering *please* over a dying dog with such ferocity that the word itself grew legs and did something about it. She saw herself keeping every promise ever spoken, mending the cracked ones, watering the brown ones. It was beautiful. It was important. It was, she understood, holy work, and someone had to do it, and Juniper was doing it, and Juniper would keep doing it whether Wren joined her or not, because that was the whole unbearable point of Juniper Villalobos.

But she saw something else too, threaded through the vision, and this was the part the Hollow showed her gently, the way you show someone a splinter:

She saw time pass differently. She saw centuries pool around her like deep water. She saw the faces she loved—round faces, freckled faces, faces that snored and stole jam and lied badly about who ate the last roll—she saw those faces arrive and age and end, quick as candleflames, while she remained. And she saw that from inside the Hollow, grief like that would not hurt. That was the trap inside the gift. From inside the Hollow, everything softened. Everything became pattern. She would remember Tam the way you remember a song from childhood: fondly, distantly, without the smell of him (woodsmoke and stolen apples) or the specific weight of his elbow against hers when they shared a windowsill.

She would love everyone and no one. She would hold every promise except the ones she'd wanted to make herself.

The vision closed like a hand folding shut.

"That's not a life," Wren said quietly. "That's a *wallpaper* of a life. Beautiful and flat."

*"Some would call it peace,"* said the Hollow.

"Some would call it a very large nap."

The voice laughed. It was definitely Juniper's laugh now, rusty and startled out of her, and Wren's chest did the arrhythmic thing again. She missed her so much it was like a toothache behind her ribs. But missing someone, she was learning, was a human thing, and it hurt, and she found—to her enormous surprise—that she did not want the hurting sanded off. The hurting was proof. The hurting meant the person had been real and close and specific.

Specific. That was the word. The Hollow was everything, and everything, Wren decided, was suspiciously close to nothing.

* * *

"Show me the other one," she said.

So the Hollow showed her mortality.

It was less flattering, as sales pitches went.

She saw hunger—real hunger, the kind Odessa talked about from her hedge-witch years, the kind that made people mean and small and desperate. She saw cold. She saw sickness arriving on schedule like an unwelcome relative. She saw her own hands growing knuckled and spotted; she saw her black hair going grey at the temples, then white, then gone. She saw death—not as a door but as a wall she walked toward her entire life, getting closer every single day, no exceptions, no negotiations.

And threaded through all of it, so densely that the hard parts nearly disappeared beneath it, she saw this:

Tam, aged eleven and furious about it, teaching her to pick pockets in the bakery's back room and being genuinely outraged when she turned out to be better at it than him ("You can hear locks *thinking*, that's CHEATING"). Tam at sixteen, taller than Odessa, still sleeping curled up like a cat because some habits outlive their reasons. Tam grown, with a beard he was clearly proud of and children of his own someday maybe, telling them stories in broken First Speech with his tongue sticking out the corner of his mouth the way it did when he concentrated, and the children laughing, and Wren laughing, and the laughter being real and unrepeatable and *hers*.

Odessa's kitchen at dawn. Flour in the air like weather. The particular silence of bread rising, which was not silence at all but the loudest kind of listening. Odessa pressing a heel of yesterday's loaf into her hand and saying, "Eat, you look like a rumor," and meaning *I love you* the way bakers mean it, which is with carbohydrates.

Rain on a window she had a right to be behind. A name on a door. Socks. She saw socks in the vision, her own socks, darned by her own hands, and for some reason this undid her more than anything else. The Hollow did not have socks. The Hollow had never once been cold in its feet.

She saw loss, too—the vision didn't hide it. She saw Tam's funeral someday, far off, and Odessa's, and she felt the future grief waiting for her like weather on the horizon. And she noticed something the Hollow perhaps hadn't intended her to notice:

Even in the vision of that grief, she was *there*. Present. Whole. Standing at a graveside with dirt under her fingernails and a crumpled letter in her pocket, grieving with her entire body, and even that—even the worst day of her whole imagined life—was more *real* than ten thousand years of serene dissolution.

Mortality, it turned out, was not a smaller life. It was a life with edges. And edges, Wren thought, were how you knew where you stopped and the world began, and knowing where you stopped and the world began was the only way anything could ever matter.

"I choose this one," she said.

Her voice shook. She said it again, steadier.

"I choose the bread."

* * *

The garden went very still. Even the humming hedges of kept promises quieted, leaning in.

*"You understand,"* said the Hollow-Juniper, and the voice was careful now, careful the way Juniper had been careful with sharp things and true things, *"that I cannot make this easy. That was the old covenant's sin—protection bought with chains. The new one only guarantees freedom. What you do with it is yours."*

"I know."

*"You will get sick. You will lose people. You will one day die, probably inconveniently, possibly with jam on your face."*

"Probably with jam on my face," Wren agreed solemnly. "I've made peace with it."

*"And you will forget things. Mortals forget. You'll forget the exact color of this place. You'll forget how to speak First Speech fluently—you'll keep pieces, the way Tam keeps his, but the fluency goes with the anchoring. Are you prepared to lose the language?"*

Wren considered this seriously. It was, after all, the language she had been born speaking, the only voice she'd had for eight years, the tongue that had knocked on Juniper's door in the rain.

"No," she said. "I'm not prepared to lose it. So I won't. I'll teach it. To Tam, and whoever comes after. Languages don't die if somebody keeps feeding them." She paused. "You taught me that. Or—she taught me that. About oaths. An oath isn't a chain if you keep choosing it. You said that once, to Sarel, on the engine. I heard it through the walls."

Another pause. When the voice came back, it was thick in a distinctly human way.

*"You were unconscious."*

"I was *mostly* unconscious. There's a difference."

The laugh again, wetter this time. Around them, somewhere in the endless green, a new hedge sprang up—silver-bright, freshly spoken. Wren glanced at it and read it without trying, the way you read your own name: *a promise between a hollow and a girl, freely made, never broken.*

"What does it say?" Wren asked, though she knew.

*"It says,"* the voice answered, *"that whenever you speak kindly, I am listening. Whenever you bake bread and give it away, that is a sacrament. Whenever you tell a story to a child in the First Speech, I will hear every word, and I will keep it, forever, in the garden. You will not be anchored to me, little one. You will simply be loved by me. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."*

Wren wiped her face with her sleeve. Her sleeve was real. Everything was real. She was crying real tears out of real eyes into a real sleeve, and it was the best she had ever felt in her short, strange, translucent-then-opaque life.

"One more thing," she said. "Tell her—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell her I said thank you. Tell her—" But there weren't words in any language for it, First Speech or human, and maybe that was fine. Maybe that was the last lesson: that some thank-yous are too big for speech and have to be carried instead, in kitchens and gardens and darned socks, for a whole lifetime.

The Hollow understood. The Hollow had always understood.

*"Go home, Wren,"* said Juniper's voice, gentle as banked coals. *"Your brother is making himself sick worrying. Your baker is burning things. And there is,"* the voice added, with unmistakable Juniper dryness, *"fresh bread. I checked. I check every day. It's one of the few perks of the position."*

* * *

The garden folded around her like a page turning, and then—

Grass. Real grass, wet with real morning dew, on the hillside below Thornfell. Cold air in her lungs. Her body ached everywhere, gloriously, specifically: her knees, her back, a blister on her heel from the road, a bruise on her arm shaped like Tam's grip.

Down the slope, the camp was waking. Odessa stood over a cookfire threatening a skillet. The defected warden, Marta, was pretending not to smile at whatever Odessa was threatening it with. And Tam sat cross-legged on a flat rock, hair sticking up in six directions, watching the threshold-stone behind her with the expression of a boy trying very hard not to hope and failing completely.

He saw her. He fell off the rock.

"WREN!" He hit the grass running and skidded the last few feet, grabbing her shoulders, shaking her, checking her opacity with his hands like a merchant checking fruit. "You're—you're *solid*, you're all the way solid, don't you EVER do that again, I had to watch Odessa cry, do you know what that's LIKE, it's like watching a mountain cry—"

"Tam."

"—and she burns things when she cries, Wren, we lost a whole tray of rolls, a WHOLE TRAY—"

"*Tam.*"

He stopped. Looked at her.

"I'm staying," Wren said. "Here. With you. With all of you. Forever, or as close to forever as the arrangement allows."

Tam blinked. Then his face did something complicated and collapsed entirely, and he grabbed her and held on, and Wren held on back, breathing in woodsmoke and stolen apples and home.

Over his shoulder, she saw Odessa drop the skillet, abandon the fire entirely, and start up the hill, wiping her hands on her apron and her eyes on her sleeve, muttering something about *children* and *heart attacks* and *who's going to watch the bread*.

Behind them all, the threshold-stone hummed—softly, contentedly, in a voice like a woman who had finally set down a heavy load and found she could still sing.

And far off down the valley, on the long road back toward Corvane, a rider in Inquisition grey reined in her horse and looked north at the faint glow above Thornfell, and did not yet turn away.

Wren saw her. She took Tam's hand.

"Come on," she said. "There's bread to save."
