## 20. After the Silence Breaks

The first thing Odessa Brann did with lawful magic was burn her own bread.

She hadn't meant to. She'd only meant to hum over the dough the way her grandmother had hummed, back before humming over dough became the kind of thing that got you a rope, and the loaves had risen so eagerly, so *gratefully*, that they'd climbed out of their pans like a congregation standing for a hymn and kept going until they brushed the ceiling of the oven and came out the color of a thundercloud.

She'd stood in the ash-and-smoke of her kitchen, holding a charred loaf in two hands, and laughed until she cried.

"Seven years," she told the loaf, because talking to baked goods was still legal, thank the saints. "Seven years I measured my whispering like a miser counts coins, and the moment nobody's watching, I ruin a whole batch."

But the second batch was perfect. And the third. And on the fourth morning, when she hung the new sign above the door — the old one, BRANN'S BAKERY, had been taken down the day the wardens first knocked, replaced with nothing, because nothing drew no attention — she'd painted it herself, in careful letters, with a little knot of green worked into the corner that wasn't quite a knot of green if anyone asked, but was, if nobody asked, a luck-knot of the old hedge-craft, the kind her gran had tied over doorways to keep the sour luck out.

The sign read:

**ODessa Brann's — Bread, and Words Freely Given**

Tam had suggested *Bread and Miracles*, and Odessa had cuffed him gently and said miracles were showing off, and then she'd painted it anyway in her heart, which was where it counted.

The line outside started on the fifth day and never really stopped. Half of Corvane came for the bread. The other half came for the *other* thing, the thing nobody put a name to yet, the thing where Odessa would lean over the counter and murmur a fever-quiet word over a sick child's forehead, or breathe a blessing into a loaf for a household that had lost someone, and the bread would keep a week past all reason and taste, every time, like the morning of a wedding.

"Charging extra for that?" a warden asked once — a former warden, they were all former wardens now, the Silencing Laws having collapsed in the space of a single astonishing week like a wall nobody had realized was made of paper.

"Charging *less*," Odessa said. "The words are free. The flour's what costs."

The warden had bought two loaves and stood in the street eating one with tears running down his face, and Odessa had pretended not to notice, because that was the Brann way: feed people, and let them have their dignity while they wept.

* * *

The laws fell the way old trees fall — slowly, creaking, and then all at once, with a crack that could be heard for miles.

It began, people later agreed, with the pardons. The Crown, what remained of it, had discovered that its grand Hollow-Engine was a ruin of slag and shattered oath-steel, that its chief Inquisitor had turned her own blade against it, and that the realm's magic had come back — not chained, not rationed, not flowing through pipes to the powerful, but *everywhere*, rising out of the ground like spring water, answering anyone who spoke kindly and true.

There was no engine to ration it. There was no covenant to broker it. There was only speech, freely given, and the Hollow beneath all things, listening.

The Council had met for nine days behind closed doors. Tam, who had opinions about councils, said they'd spent eight of those days arguing about whose fault it was and one writing the actual laws. Wren said — in First Speech, which she was teaching to anyone who'd hold still for it — that the Hollow had been whispering in the councilors' dreams the whole time, and that the Hollow had *opinions about whose fault it was* too, and had shared them.

The Silencing Laws were struck out. In their place: the Speech Accords, three sentences long, which Tam had framed and hung in the bakery because he said they were the shortest laws in the history of law and that alone proved they'd been written by someone sensible:

*A person may speak what is true. A person may not speak another's name into binding. The Hollow hears all oaths; let all oaths be made with care.*

"That last one's going to cause trouble," Odessa said, reading over Tam's shoulder.

"Good trouble," said Tam.

* * *

Sarel Voss did not attend the signing of the Accords.

She had stood trial instead — had insisted on it, had walked into the hall in her Inquisitor's black with the collar torn off and stood before the assembled remnant of the Crown and said, in a voice that carried to the back walls, *"I sounded the alarm at the cloister. I gave the order's names to the Crown. I built the engine's first designs. Judge me for all of it."*

They had. The trial lasted eleven days. Juniper Villalobos was not there to testify for her, which everyone understood was the testimony that mattered most, and which Sarel bore the way she bore everything: straight-backed, silent, and bleeding somewhere no one could see.

In the end they pardoned her. Disgraced, stripped of office, her name struck from the rolls of the Inquisition — but pardoned, because the new Accords said a person might speak what was true, and the truest thing anyone said at that trial came from a hedge-witch from the Undermarket, who stood up uninvited and said: *"She broke. Then she turned her blade on the thing she broke for. I've known worse people who got off lighter, and better people who got hanged. Give her work to do. That's the only sentence that ever fixed anybody."*

So they gave her work to do.

The first School of Speech opened in the gutted shell of the old Inquisition citadel — Sarel's own suggestion, delivered flatly, daring anyone to object that the stones might remember. The wardens' barracks became classrooms. The engine's foundation was torn out and a garden was planted over it, because Wren had said, in that grave, luminous way of hers, that things grown over wounds grew sweeter.

Sarel taught the advanced students: the ones with power, the ones who needed most to learn what she had never learned, which was that a gift was not a weapon you were handed but a voice you were trusted with. She was strict. She was merciless about sloppiness. She never smiled, or almost never, and the students told stories about the almosts.

But every morning, before the students came, she stood alone in the garden over the engine's old scar, and she said one name aloud. Just one. She never said it to anyone, and no one ever asked her who it was, because everyone knew.

The Hollow heard all oaths. Sarel Voss had made herself one, wordless and lifelong: *never again.*

* * *

Tam and Wren shared the room above the bakery now, the one with the crooked window that looked out over the roofs toward the north, toward Thornfell, though you couldn't actually see Thornfell from it. Tam had checked. Repeatedly.

It had been Odessa's idea. "You're both orphans," she'd said, with the terrifying briskness she used to disguise enormous tenderness. "You're both fed under my roof. Argue about it if you like, but you'll argue about it at the table like family."

They had never argued about it. It had simply happened, the way Tam's whole life seemed to happen: all at once, sideways, and then so completely that he couldn't remember the shape of before.

Wren was growing. That was the strangest and most ordinary thing. The Hollow's daughter, the anchor of a dying covenant, the girl who had poured herself nearly to nothing into a machine's iron mouth — she was growing *taller*. She ate like a stevedore. She'd lost the translucence, the terrible thinning, and filled out into an ordinary girl of perhaps nine, with ordinary scraped knees and an extraordinary habit of saying true things at the exact moment they'd do the most damage.

She still didn't speak Common much. She didn't need to. First Speech had a word for everything, and the ones it didn't have words for, she could say with her face.

But some nights Tam would wake and hear her in the window seat, talking softly in the dark — not to him, not to herself. To the floorboards. To the city. To the deep-down listening thing beneath all of it.

And the floorboards, he was almost sure, sometimes answered.

* * *

They grieved together, though neither of them had a word for it at first — which was strange, given that between them they had every word in two languages, and the First Speech had eleven different words for grief alone. Wren had taught him them one night, lying on the floor looking at the ceiling, cataloguing them like a merchant's stock: the grief that is a wound, the grief that is a hunger, the grief that is a door left open, the grief that is a name spoken into an empty room.

"That last one," Tam had said. "That's the one."

"That's the one," Wren had agreed.

So they made a practice of it, the way Odessa made a practice of bread. Every evening, before supper, they'd each say one thing they remembered about her. Not a prayer — Wren said the Hollow didn't need prayers, it needed *company* — just a memory, given aloud, freely.

"I remember she cheated at cards," Tam said one evening, "and she was *bad* at it, she'd palm a card like she was afraid of it, and she still won because she cheated *confidently*."

"I remember," Wren said, "that when she gave her name away, the last thing it felt like — the last thing I heard of her — wasn't sad. It was like a door opening onto a garden. She was *glad*, Tam. I need you to know that she was glad."

"I remember she hummed when she worked. Terrible. Off-key. Odessa says hedge-witches hum, and I think swordspeakers hum too, and I think Juniper hummed because silence had taken enough from her already."

"I remember her hands. They were always moving. Oath-mark, cards, Wren's hair, the strap of her sword. She talked with her hands even when her mouth was quiet."

On and on, evening after evening, a small litany in a small kitchen, and slowly — the way the Silencing Laws had fallen, the way old trees fall — the grief stopped being a wound and started being a door left open. Something you could walk through. Something on the other side of which was a garden.

Odessa never joined the litany, but she always cooked something Juniper would have liked on those nights, and she always made too much, and she always said "well, we can't waste it," and they all understood.

* * *

Spring came to Corvane the way it had always come, but louder.

Flowers came up through the cobbles in the lower city, and nobody stamped them out, because they bloomed in shapes — a spiral here, a word-shape there — wherever someone had whispered something kind over the ground. Children ran through the streets collecting *spoken* flowers the way children of Tam's generation had collected warden's buttons as trophies. The luck-knots over doorways all over the city had begun to green, actually *green*, actual leaves, and Odessa had stopped pretending hers was just a decoration.

The Undermarket came up into the daylight, mostly. Master Quill did not. No one could quite remember what had happened to Quill — no one could quite remember Quill, which was the point of Quill — but the black bazaar's stalls now sold relics openly, and the stall where the engine's slag had once been fenced quietly sold, instead, small bells that rang in the note of a person's own true voice. Tam had bought three. One for him, one for Wren, one that sat on Odessa's counter and rang, every so often, in a low warm tone nobody recognized, on no schedule anyone could find, always when the light was doing something particular through the window.

Odessa never said who she thought it was. She just never took it down.

And on the first true day of spring, with the bakery full and the line out the door and the luck-knots budding overhead, Tam leaned on the counter beside Wren and watched her teach First Speech to a knot of children — *this word means bread, this word means stay, this word is the one your mother says when she means you and only you* — and he felt the grief and the joy sit down together in his chest like two old friends sharing a bench.

"Hey," he said quietly, in First Speech, which still came out of his mouth crooked and street-bent, like a song learned by ear. "The garden one. The word for the grief that's a door."

Wren looked at him.

"What's on the other side of it?"

Wren smiled — that grave, luminous, nine-year-old smile — and answered in the old tongue, and the word she used did not mean *nothing*, and it did not mean *her*, exactly.

It meant: *the one who is listening.*

And far to the north, beneath the roots of Thornfell, in a garden that had no seasons and no ending, something vast and gentle heard a bell ring in a bakery in Corvane, in the note of a low warm voice it had been keeping safe — and turned, in the wordless way it had, toward the sound, the way a person turns toward a door when they hear, at last, footsteps coming home.
