## 21. Words in the Garden

The garden behind the rebuilt cloister did not have seasons so much as it had *moods*, and on this particular morning it was in a mood Wren could only describe as *showing off*.

The moonflowers had bloomed overnight in the shape of a question mark. Not a spiral, not a star — a question mark, curl and all, twelve of them along the south wall, pale as spilled milk. Wren stood at the gate with her watering can in both hands and regarded them for a long moment.

"Someone talked in their sleep," she said.

From the dormitory window above, a small voice called down: "It wasn't me!"

"It was absolutely you, Pip."

"It was *not*. I said the word for bread. The bread word. That's it, that's all I know."

Wren looked back at the moonflowers. The bread word, spoken by an eight-year-old with more enthusiasm than accuracy, would not produce question marks. It produced, historically, a smell of warm yeast that had once drifted over the whole cloister for three days and made everyone unbearably hungry and unreasonably happy. Odessa had wept. Odessa claimed it was the onions.

No. Question marks were older work than that. Question marks were the wind's doing, and the wind had been *at it again*.

She set down the watering can, crouched among the flowers, and answered them in First Speech — the low, round, river-cold tongue that had been her first language and her last inheritance, the one she had spent seven years teaching to children who came to the cloister frightened and silent and full of words they'd been told were crimes.

The word she used meant roughly: *I see you. Ask properly.*

The flowers shivered. The curls of their petals tightened, then relaxed, opening like hands. And beneath the garden's soil, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something answered.

Not a word. Not yet. Just the *attention* of it — the vast, patient, listening thing far to the north beneath Thornfell, the thing that kept every promise ever spoken, turning its endless face toward this small walled garden the way it always did when Wren spoke, the way a lamp turns toward a hand.

Seven years now, and it still made her chest ache, that attention. It felt like being loved by a mountain. It felt like being remembered by someone who could not forget anything, ever, which was its own kind of grief.

She pressed her palm flat against the cool soil and said nothing else for a moment. Some mornings you didn't need to say anything. Some mornings the saying was just the standing there.

Then Pip fell out of the window — deliberately, because he was Pip — and landed in the bean row, and the morning got going whether the garden liked it or not.

* * *

The cloister had been rebuilt wrong, and everyone agreed this was fine.

The original Order of the Speaking Blade had been built like a fist: walls within walls, gates within gates, everything designed to keep the world out and the oaths in. Tam, who had been hired — over his strenuous objections — as the master builder because he was the only person anyone knew who could read a building like a pickpocket reads a pocket, had taken one look at the old foundations and said, "Right, well, we're not doing any of this," and torn half of it down.

What stood there now was a long low hall with too many windows, a kitchen that smelled permanently of Odessa's bread, a dormitory with beds nobody was forced to sleep in but everybody did, and behind it all, the garden, which had begun as a weed patch and become, through seven years of whispered words and stubborn tending, something no one had a name for yet.

Children came to it from everywhere now. That was Sarel's doing, mostly — Sarel, who had spent her disgrace years building schools of speech in the cities and now sent Wren the ones who frightened other teachers. The mute ones. The ones who spoke and things *happened*. The ones whose parents crossed themselves and whispered about the Hollow.

They arrived thinking they were broken. Wren remembered thinking that herself, once, in a rain-lashed doorway in Corvane, holding a scrap of paper with an address on it and knowing only that the woman inside owed a debt and might, if begged, not turn her away.

She had not been broken. She had been *untranslated*. There was a difference, and the difference was a garden.

"Wren." This was Marta, fourteen, solemn as a funeral, who could make iron remember it had once been soft. She stood at the garden's edge holding a trowel like a sword, which, given the history of this place, Wren chose to find funny rather than ominous. "The roses are doing the thing again."

"The thing?"

"You know. The *thing*."

Wren went and looked. The roses along the east wall — Juniper's roses, though no one had planted them; they had come up wild the spring after the Reforging, red as a wound, thorned like a promise — had arranged themselves into shapes. Words, almost. Letters, almost. They never quite managed letters. Whatever lived in the roots of this place spoke in *almosts*, in shapes that suggested meaning the way clouds suggest ships.

But today, for the first time, Wren squinted at the blooms and thought she could read them.

*Almost* an O. *Almost* a U. *Almost* an R.

Her breath went strange in her chest.

"Roses can't spell," said Marta, watching her face.

"No," Wren agreed. "Roses can't spell."

But the wind could carry words, and words could root, and seven years of a voice saying *grow, grow, be patient, be lovely* into the dark soil of a garden might, in the end, have soaked down deep enough to push back up through the stems. The Hollow kept every promise ever spoken. Perhaps it also kept every *sentence* ever murmured over flowerbeds by a woman with dirt under her nails who had talked to plants the way some people talk to horses, continuously and without embarrassment.

Juniper had talked to her garden constantly. Wren remembered that from the road north — the way she'd crouch by some roadside weed and mutter at it, *up you get, don't be dramatic,* and how the weed would, invariably, get up.

Wren touched one of the almost-letters with one finger.

The rose warmed under her touch. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Marta," she said, "fetch the others. Quietly. And fetch Tam — actually no, don't fetch Tam, he'll cry, and he'll say he isn't crying, and then we'll all have to pretend, and it takes an hour."

Too late. Tam was already in the doorway, a flour sack over one shoulder and his face doing the thing it did — that careful, practiced blankness he'd learned on Corvane's streets, ruined completely by his eyes, which had gone bright and wet.

"I'm not crying," he said.

"You're crying."

"It's *pollen.*"

"It's autumn, Tam."

"Pollen doesn't check the calendar, Wren."

He came and stood beside her, and together they looked at the roses trying, with all the patience of growing things, to say a name.

* * *

Afternoons were lessons, unless the garden was feeling instructive, in which case afternoons were lessons anyway, just with a different teacher.

Wren gathered them in a semicircle on the flat stone by the pond — Pip and Marta and the twins, Sorrel and Sage, who had arrived last spring having never spoken a word aloud and who now bickered fluently in First Speech, which was the whole point of everything, if Wren was honest. Bickering was a sign of health. Silence had never once, in her experience, meant peace.

"Today," she said, "we learn the word for *remember*."

Pip's hand shot up. "Is it the one that makes your teeth hum?"

"That's the word for *thunder*."

"Is it the one that made the soup sing?"

"That was the word for *home*, and the soup wasn't singing, it was *simmering*, and I still maintain you did that one wrong on purpose."

"I would never—"

"You turned Odessa's ladle into a frog, Pip."

"A *temporary* frog."

"The word for *remember*" — Wren raised her voice gently over him, and the semicircle settled, because even Pip went quiet for certain words — "is an old one. Older than the kingdom. Older than the crowns and the hangings and all the frightened years. When you say it truly, it doesn't call up what you've lost. Everyone thinks that. It doesn't reach backward."

Sorrel frowned. "Then what does it do?"

"It reaches *outward*." Wren held up her hands, cupped, the way the Hollow held the world. "Remembering isn't looking behind you. It's making room. You say the word, and somewhere — maybe far away, maybe deeper than far away — something that was spoken long ago hears itself spoken again, and for a moment it is *here*. Not gone. Not lost. Here. Do you understand? Nothing true is ever lost. It's only waiting to be said again."

She taught them the word.

It was a hard one. It required the whole mouth, the whole breath, a little of the chest. Pip got it wrong four times and produced, on the fourth attempt, a brief and localized rain shower that dampened exactly his own head, which the twins found so hilarious they had to lie down on the grass.

But on the fifth try, as the light went long and gold across the garden, six children said the word for *remember* together, imperfectly, earnestly, with all the graceless love of children doing a difficult thing —

— and the entire garden exhaled.

There was no other word for it. Every flower turned. The pond stilled. The wind, which had been idling through the poplars all day, stopped as though a hand had closed around it, and in that stopped moment Wren heard — not with her ears, never with her ears, with the place inside her where her first language lived — a voice.

Low. Warm. A little rough at the edges, like a blade drawn slowly. Saying something that was not a word at all, or was a word in a language underneath language, and meant, unmistakably, the way a hand on your shoulder means it:

*I'm here.*

The children hadn't heard. Of course they hadn't. They were laughing at Pip's wet head and asking what came next and whether lesson days ended in cake. But the wind moved again, softer than before, and went on its way through the garden like someone walking familiar paths, checking the beans, approving of the roses, pausing longest — Wren noticed, she always noticed — at the patch of feverfew near the wall, where a stubborn woman had once knelt for an hour convincing a dying plant to live, and it had lived, and it was living still.

Wren stayed behind after the others ran inside. She sat by the feverfew until the stars came out.

"You could just visit," she told the wind, conversationally. "You know. In person. Knock. Odessa would feed you. Tam would cry — sorry, *pollen* — and I would—" Her voice caught on the next part, and she let it catch, because this garden did not judge catching. "I would very much like that."

The wind stirred the feverfew. Gently. Apologetically, almost. It said nothing, and the nothing it said was so full and warm and sad that Wren understood, as she understood every evening and forgot by morning, that whatever remained of the woman who had given her name to the dark did not experience *visiting* anymore. Was not *somewhere else*. Was everywhere the covenant reached — in every blessing over bread, every lullaby mending a fever, every kind word doing small unlawful miracles across the realm. You could not knock on the door of someone who had become the door.

But you could talk to them.

So Wren talked. About the garden. About Pip's frog problem, ongoing. About Marta, who had softened a rusted hinge that morning and then burst into tears because her grandmother had owned hinges like it, once. About Sarel, who visited twice a year and stood very straight in the garden and said nothing for an hour and then left, and whom Wren always walked to the gate, because grief shared at a gate is lighter than grief carried alone. About Tam, who had proposed to a carpenter named Ilse in the spring with a ring hidden in a loaf of bread, because obviously, and who cried at the wedding so much the officiant briefly paused.

"And I'm learning to bake," she added. "Don't tell Odessa. She says my loaves are 'structurally ambitious.' She means they're rocks. I'm getting better. The trick is patience, she says, which is rich coming from a woman who yells at dough."

The wind moved through the garden rows, unhurried, attentive, the way a listener leans in.

And when Wren finally stood, stiff-kneed, to go in to supper, she paused at the gate out of habit — the old habit, the seven-years-old habit, the habit of a child who once stood in a doorway in the rain waiting to find out if the world had any kindness left in it.

Before she pushed it open, she said the word for *remember*.

Just once. Softly. Into the darkening air.

Somewhere beneath Thornfell, in a garden without seasons, something vast and gentle heard itself spoken to by name — heard the word that reached outward, making room, calling across the distance between a girl and her ghost — and for the first time in seven years, it *answered*.

Not in wind. Not in roses. Not in almost-letters.

In a voice — low, warm, rough-edged, unmistakable, impossible — rising out of the soil itself, out of the feverfew and the moonflowers and the question-mark blooms, speaking in clear First Speech the single word:

***"Wren."***

The watering can hit the ground. Water spread across the flagstones, silvering in the starlight, unnoticed.

Because the voice did not stop. Because the voice was *growing*, filling the garden, filling the night above it, and beneath the words Wren could feel the vast thing beneath Thornfell straining toward her like a swimmer toward shore, like something breaking a rule it had kept faithfully for seven years, like a door that had finally, *finally* been knocked upon from the other side —

— and it said, in the voice of the woman who had given it everything, a sentence Wren had waited half her life to hear:

*"Come home. Come home now. Before the forgetting finishes — I have kept what I could, but I am losing the shape of your face, little bird, and I need — I need someone to tell me —"*

The voice frayed. Thinned. Held on.

*"Tell me who I was."*

Wren stood in the gateway with her heart hammering and her eyes burning, and understood that this time the wind was not visiting, and the roses were not showing off, and the word for *remember* had reached further outward than any word she had ever spoken —

— and that tomorrow, she would pack a bag.
