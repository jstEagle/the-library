## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The frost came to Hollowmere the way all things came to Hollowmere—quietly, without argument, as though the valley itself had agreed upon it in advance. Oscar Whitlock knelt in the guild garden with her bare hands in the cold soil, turning the earth where the rosemary had died back for the third winter running, and thought, not for the first time, that even the frost seemed to have forgotten why it bothered.

Nothing grew here that wasn't stubborn. That was the first lesson the guild taught its children, though they no longer remembered it was a lesson at all. *The valley keeps what the valley keeps.* Magic died at the tree line—withered mid-word, mid-gesture, mid-prayer—and what remained below was only weather, only dirt, only the slow honest work of hands. Oscar had been raised on that truth the way other children were raised on milk and hymns. It had never once occurred to her to test it.

She pressed a seed of winter savory into the furrow and covered it, and above her the bell in the guild tower rang the hour with a sound like a coin dropped down a well. Time to prepare the morning rite.

She rose, brushed the soil from her knees, and looked back once at the garden before she went. It had been magnificent once—so the archive claimed, in ledgers whose ink was older than the guild's oldest member. Terraced beds, a fountain carved with interlocking rings, hedges cut into shapes that were *supposed* to mean something. Now it was nine raised beds, a broken fountain full of rainwater and leaves, and a plum tree that fruited only when it felt like it, which was to say: never on any schedule the guild could predict or claim credit for. Oscar loved it anyway. It was the only thing in Hollowmere she had ever tended that answered her—not in words, not in magic, but in the mute, grudging language of green things that chose to live.

Inside the guildhall, the others were already assembling in the oath-room, and Oscar hurried through the cold corridors, past walls hung with the regalia of an order that had forgotten its own purpose. There were forty-one oaths recorded in the guild's founding ledgers. The guild could perform eleven. Of those eleven, no one living could say what any of them *bound*, or *to whom*, or *why*. They said the words the way a man might wear his grandfather's coat—because it was there, because it fit well enough, because taking it off would mean admitting it had once belonged to someone who understood it.

Brother Callow was lighting the tallow candles when she entered, his hands unsteady with age. Sister Ives stood at the window with her arms folded, watching the tree line the way she always did, as though expecting it to do something. It never did. The pines stood in their dark ranks along the valley's rim, and beyond them the world simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets his thought.

"Oscar." Callow beckoned her forward without looking up. "You'll speak the Third Oath this morning. Ives's throat is bad."

The Third Oath. *We keep the door, we keep the word, we keep the quiet.* Spoken over a bowl of well water and a sprig of ash. Nobody knew what door. Nobody knew what word. The quiet, at least, they had mastered.

Oscar took her place at the worn stone basin and spoke the words in the flat cadence they had all been taught, the syllables worn smooth as river stones from generations of mouths that no longer believed in them. *We keep the door, we keep the word, we keep the quiet.* The water did nothing. The ash did nothing. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse moved, indifferent. This was the whole of it: seven people in a cold room, performing fidelity to a contract whose terms had been lost, whose other party—whatever it had been—had never once, in Oscar's lifetime, come to collect.

She finished the rite. Callow nodded. Ives turned from the window. And that was the morning, and it would have been the whole of the day too, if Maren had not been waiting for her in the garden when she came back out, standing at the gate with her shawl pinned wrong and her eyes fixed on a point six feet to the left of Oscar's head.

* * *

"Grandmother," Oscar said. "You're out of bed."

Maren Whitlock was eighty-one years old and had spent seventy of those years in Hollowmere, and she had never once, in all that time, been a woman who frightened anyone. She was small and dry and sharp-tongued in the way of people who have outlived the need to be polite, and she had raised Oscar herself after Oscar's parents went over the pass one autumn and never came back—a loss the valley had absorbed the way it absorbed everything, silently, and then forgot. Maren did not forget things. That was the whole of her character, as far as Oscar had ever known it. She remembered the names of dead guild members no one else had heard of. She remembered which winters the plum tree had fruited. She remembered, in exhaustive and irritating detail, every debt the household owed and to whom, down to the borrowed ladder still sitting in Tam Hesk's barn since Oscar was nine.

So when her grandmother looked past her shoulder at the empty garden gate and said, in a voice of perfect ordinary courtesy, "He's early today," Oscar felt the cold of the morning move into her chest and stay there.

"Who's early, Gran?"

Maren's gaze slid, unhurried, from the empty air beside the gate to Oscar's face. For a moment her eyes were clear, and she looked—Oscar thought—almost embarrassed, the way she looked when caught talking to the plum tree, which she did sometimes, claiming it improved the fruit.

"No one," she said. "Help me with the gate. My hands are bad in the cold."

Oscar helped her with the gate. They walked the beds together, and Maren pointed out which rosemary wanted cutting back and which of the fountain stones had shifted over the winter, and for a while it was an ordinary morning, and Oscar almost persuaded herself she had imagined it.

Then, at the far end by the compost, Maren stopped, and tilted her head as though listening to music from a neighboring house, and said: "No. Not yet. She isn't ready."

"Gran."

"She's impatient," Maren said, to the compost heap, to the air, to no one. "They always are, at the end. But the terms were the terms, and I don't renegotiate." She turned and smiled at Oscar, and the smile was warm and entirely wrong, a smile aimed at a conversation Oscar could not hear. "You'll have to tell him that yourself. I'm done carrying messages."

"Gran, who are you talking to?"

Maren looked at her then for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—something old and tired and, worst of all, *familiar*, as though she were looking at a letter she had written long ago and only now remembered she'd never sent.

"The frost is early this year," she said. "Help me with the rosemary."

* * *

It happened again three days later, and then twice in one afternoon, and by the end of the week Maren was speaking to the empty air as naturally as she spoke to Oscar, sometimes in the garden, sometimes at the window, once—Oscar had found her at midnight, candle lit, dressed—standing in the middle of the kitchen, addressing the dark corner by the pantry in a low, level, negotiating voice.

"—and I'll not have her surprised by it. That's the least of what I'm owed. She'll be told before, do you hear me? Not after. *Before.*"

"Gran." Oscar lit the lamp. The corner by the pantry was a corner by the pantry: flour sacks, a crock of rendered fat, mouse droppings. "There's no one there."

Maren turned. In the lamplight her face looked thinner than it had a month ago, as though something were being drawn out of her by slow degrees, like broth through a cloth. "No," she agreed. "There never is. That's rather the point of him."

"Who? *Who?*"

Maren sat down at the kitchen table and folded her hands, and for a moment she was entirely herself, the sharp dry woman who had raised her, and Oscar's fear loosened its grip just enough to hurt.

"Oscar," she said. "What do you know about the tree line?"

"It's where the valley ends. It's where magic dies. It's why we're safe."

"Safe." Maren said the word the way she said *weeds*—with the flat contempt of a gardener for a thing that would have to be dealt with eventually. "Child, the guild has been telling itself that story for so long it's forgotten it's a story. We don't keep the door. We don't keep the word." She tapped the table with one knuckle, once, hard. "We keep the *quiet*. And the quiet was purchased. Nothing in Hollowmere is free, least of all the silence."

Oscar stood with the lamp in her hand and did not sit down. "Purchased with what?"

Maren was quiet for a long time. Outside, the wind came down off the mountain and moved through the valley, and the plum tree at the edge of the garden creaked, and the sound was so ordinary that it made what Maren said next worse, not better.

"Ask me again in the spring," she said. "If the frost holds off. If the prayers stay small." She looked at the dark window, at the black ranks of the pines beyond it, and her voice dropped to something Oscar had never heard in it before, and would spend the rest of her life trying to name. It was not fear. It was closer to guilt. "Because something down there has started *answering*, Oscar. And I'm the one who has to tell you what we sold."

* * *

Oscar did not sleep. She lay in her narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the guildhall settle around her—the tick of cooling stone, Callow's cough two rooms away, the wind working at the shutters—and turned her grandmother's words over and over like a stone in the hand, looking for the seam.

*We keep the quiet, and the quiet was purchased.*

She thought of the morning rites, the eleven oaths, the water that did nothing. She thought of the archive below the guildhall, the ledgers no one read, the founding documents in ink older than memory. She thought of the way Ives watched the tree line, and the way no one in Hollowmere ever, in Oscar's whole life, had gone past it. Not to trade, not to wander, not out of curiosity. She had always assumed it was simply how things were. She had never once asked *why no one leaves*, the way a fish has never once asked about water.

And underneath all of it, in a place in her chest she did not want to look at directly, something else was moving. Not dread—or not only dread. The valley had taught her to stay small, to keep the quiet, to want nothing that reached past the tree line. And now, for the first time in her careful, dutiful, invisible life, something on the other side of that line had said her grandmother's name.

She got up before dawn, dressed in the dark, and took the lamp down to the archive.

The door was unlocked—it was always unlocked; no one had thought the archive worth locking in a hundred years—and the air inside was cold and dry and smelled of dust and old tallow, the smell of things preserved past all usefulness. Oscar stood in the doorway with her lamp raised, looking down the long rows of shelving into the dark, and felt the whole shape of her life tremble like the lamp flame.

Somewhere in these shelves was the original oath. Somewhere was the name of what the guild had paid, and to whom, and why. Somewhere was the reason her grandmother spoke to empty corners at midnight, and the reason the frost had come early, and the reason—she was suddenly, coldly certain of it—the reason Maren had begun to look at her, lately, with that expression. That letter-never-sent expression. That *I am running out of time to tell you* expression.

Oscar stepped inside and pulled the first ledger from the shelf.

Behind her, far above, the frost began to come down off the mountain, early again, and silent, and patient, like something that knew exactly where it was going.
