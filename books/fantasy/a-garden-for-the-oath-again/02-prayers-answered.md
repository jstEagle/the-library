## 2. Prayers Answered

The frost came early that year, and with it, the impossible.

Oscar Whitlock saw it first from the guild's kitchen window, a mug of barley tea cooling forgotten in her hands. Across the lane, on Tam Broadbelt's terraced plot, the bean rows stood in full flower—white blossoms open like small astonished mouths, bees working them in the gray light of an October morning when everything else in Hollowmere had already browned and bowed. The frost had silvered the fence rails. It had not touched the beans.

She set down the mug. She went out into the cold without her shawl, because some part of her had already understood that this was not a thing she could approach slowly or sensibly.

"Tam," she called, and her breath hung white between them. "Your beans."

He was standing at the gate of his own garden with his hat in his hands, which was not like Tam either. Tam Broadbelt wore his hat the way other men wore their skin.

"I prayed," he said.

The word landed strangely in the valley air. In Hollowmere, prayer was a posture, not a request. You said the words the guild taught you—*keep us, keep us, keep us small*—and you expected nothing, because nothing was what the valley gave. Magic died at the tree line. That was the whole of the covenant, though no one living could say what it had purchased or who had signed for it. The oath-keepers kept oaths whose contents had been eaten by time, and the keeping itself had become the meaning: sweep the shrine stones, trim the wick in the empty lamp, recite to an audience of nobody.

"You prayed," Oscar repeated. "To whom?"

Tam's jaw worked. He looked, briefly, over his shoulder—at the mountain, she realized. At the mountain that filled half the sky above the valley's northern rim, its peak lost in cloud even on clear days, so constant a presence that no one in Hollowmere looked at it anymore, the way you do not look at your own heartbeat.

"I don't know," he said. "I just—I knelt down in the dirt and I asked. Out loud. Like a fool." His voice dropped. "And something answered, Oscar. Not in words. But I felt—" He pressed his hat against his chest as though it might hold the feeling in place. "I felt *heard*. First time in my life I've felt heard."

The bees droned among the impossible blossoms. Oscar reached through the gate and touched one of the bean leaves. Warm. The leaf was warm, in frost, in October, warm as a hand.

That night she did not sleep.

* * *

Her grandmother was in the garden before dawn, which was itself wrong; Maren Whitlock had not risen before noon in two years. She stood among the raised beds Oscar tended—the guild's neglected plot, half gone to sorrel and self-willed mint—and she was talking to someone.

Not to herself. Oscar knew the difference. Her grandmother had always spoken to herself while she worked, a running commentary of complaints and recipes. This was different. This was *addressed*. Maren's head was tilted at the angle of a listener, and her mouth moved in answers, and once—this was the part Oscar would carry afterward, the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone—once, Maren laughed. A young woman's laugh, bright and startled, coming out of an old bent body in the blue dark.

"Gran."

Maren turned. For a moment her face was open in a way Oscar had never seen it, lit from inside, and then it closed, door by door, until only the familiar wariness remained.

"You're up early."

"So are you." Oscar came down the path slowly, the way you'd approach a bird. "Who were you talking to?"

"The soil."

"Gran."

"The soil talks back, if you're patient." Maren knelt and pressed her palm flat against the cold earth, and something in the gesture was so old, so practiced, that Oscar's skin prickled. "Yours doesn't yet. But it will learn."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke." Maren looked up at her, and in the pre-dawn gray her eyes seemed very dark, very deep, wells rather than eyes. "Sit down, girl. There are things I should have told you years ago, and now the telling has a deadline."

Oscar sat on the cold edge of the stone bench. Above them the mountain held its silence. Below them the valley slept on, dreaming its small, protected dreams.

"Tam Broadbelt's beans," Maren said. "You saw them."

"Yes."

"And the Hollis girl's cow, birthed three days early, a perfect calf, after her mother prayed all night in the byre. And the water in the mill race running sweet again where it's been bitter since before I was born." Maren's voice was flat, inventory-like. "It's started. I hoped I'd be dead first. Selfish of me. I've never been good at hoping."

"What's started?"

Maren drew a long breath, and when she spoke again, the words came out in fragments, like beads off a snapped string.

"Beneath the mountain there is a court. Old gods—older than the valley, older than the guild, older than any name we have left for them. Before your mother was born, before me even, Hollowmere was dying. Blight, and raids, and winters that ate children. So the guild went down and bargained. Protection, peace, a wall of forgetting around this valley that magic cannot cross." Her hand curled against the earth. "Nothing is free down there. Nothing has ever been free anywhere, but down there they keep honest accounts. The price was a soul. One soul, given into their keeping, held as surety against the covenant. Renewed each generation—an heir takes up the debt when the holder dies."

The cold had gotten into Oscar's bones, but it wasn't the cold making her shake.

"Whose soul, Gran?"

Maren did not answer at once. She looked at her own hands—gardener's hands, cracked and spotted, hands Oscar had watched plant and prune and bury things her whole life—and something in her face made Oscar understand before the words came.

"Mine," Maren said. "I went down myself, when I was younger than you are now. I didn't send anyone. I wouldn't ask that of anyone. I went down and I signed, and my soul has been sitting in their keeping for fifty years, potted like a cutting, and every year I've come back up and lived my life around the hole where it used to be." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Oscar had ever seen, tender and ruined. "You thought your grandmother was cold. Girl, I'm not cold. I'm *hollow*. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a stone and an empty house."

"But the covenant—it holds. It's held for generations. Why would they—"

"Because the one who held my surety is waking up." Maren's voice dropped to almost nothing. "They're answering prayers, Oscar. Do you understand what that means? They've spent fifty years sleeping behind the seal we paid for, and now they're reaching through it. Every answered prayer is a finger through a crack. They're not being generous." She finally looked at Oscar directly, and her eyes were wet, and furious, and afraid. "They're calling in debts. All of them. Starting with mine."

* * *

Oscar walked the valley that day and saw it everywhere, once she knew how to look.

The chapel of the guild—really just a roofed circle of standing stones, the empty lamp, the swept floor—had a line outside it. Actual people, kneeling in the frost-burnt grass, murmuring into their cupped hands. Old Sorrel Fitch asking for his wife's pain to ease. The Widow Marsh praying for her son on the coast road. Children, even, copying their elders, praying for snow that would hold a sled's runners.

And the mountain, silent, vast, taking it all in.

By evening, the first answers had begun arriving like letters: Sorrel's wife slept without crying out for the first time in a year. Snow clouds gathered along the northern rim, fat and deliberate, in a sky that had promised a dry week.

Oscar stood in the guild archive among the smell of dust and tallow, surrounded by ledgers full of ritual instructions for rites no one remembered the purpose of, and she felt terror move through her like weather. This was the shape of her whole life suddenly visible from above: a valley bought and sealed like a jar, a guild performing ceremonies over an empty lamp, a grandmother hollowed out and walking around her own absence, and beneath it all, under the mountain, something patient beginning to count.

But underneath the terror—she hated this, hated it even as she let herself feel it—underneath was something else. A thrill. A terrible, unwelcome aliveness, like the first ache of feeling returning to a limb asleep too long. All her life she had kept the rituals of a dead contract, tended a garden no one visited, stayed small in a valley built for staying small. And now the world had knocked. Now there was a debt with her family's name on it, and a deadline, and a door.

Purpose. That was the word for it, and she flinched from the word even as it warmed her.

She climbed to the tree line at dusk, as she had a hundred times, and stood where the last of the valley's birches gave way to pine and then to wild country. Here, always, the boundary announced itself as a kind of pressure—a faint resistance against the skin, like walking into still water. Beyond it, magic pooled and moved; she had never been able to see it, but she had always been able to feel the valley refuse it.

Tonight the boundary felt different. Thinner. The pressure against her skin was fainter, and beyond the line, in the blue shadow of the pines, she could have sworn the air itself was stirring—turning over in its sleep, the way Maren turned over in hers.

Something answered prayers now. Something was collecting.

Oscar pressed her hand against the invisible wall of her whole life, and whispered—not a prayer, she told herself, not a prayer, just a test—

and somewhere far below the mountain's roots, something turned toward her voice like a plant toward light.

Behind her, back down the slope, her grandmother's silhouette waited at the garden gate, small and straight-backed against the last light.

"We need to talk about what I buried in the archive," Maren said, when Oscar reached her. "Before you find it yourself. Because you will find it—you have my eyes, worse luck—and there are things in that box that will break your heart, and I'd rather break it gently."

Oscar looked past her, at the garden, at the valley, at the mountain wearing its new snow like a crown.

"Then tell me," she said, "everything."
