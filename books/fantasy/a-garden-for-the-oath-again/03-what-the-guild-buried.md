## 3. What the Guild Buried

The guild archive had not been opened in Oscar's lifetime.

She knew this because the door to it was hidden behind a shelf of ritual manuals nobody read—*On the Keeping of Quiet Hours*, *A Treatise on the Virtues of Smallness*—and because the shelf itself was furred with dust so thick it had gone grey and soft as moss. She had dusted that shelf every week for eleven years. She had never once thought to move it.

Now she stood with her hands flat against the wood and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she moved it.

The door had no lock. It had a latch worn smooth by hands long dead, and it opened with a sound like a held breath finally let go. Cold air came out, smelling of paper and stone and, faintly, of something green—something that should not have survived this long underground, in the dark.

Oscar lit the lamp she'd brought and stepped inside.

The archive was smaller than she'd imagined. A single room beneath the guildhall, lined with pigeonholes, each stuffed with documents rolled or folded or simply shoved. The oath-keepers of Hollowmere had been meticulous once. She could see it in the first hundred years of records—fine copperplate, wax seals, careful indexing. Then the handwriting began to loosen. Then the dates stopped being kept. Then the entries thinned into a kind of embarrassed shorthand, as if the guild had grown tired of writing down what it no longer believed: *Ritual performed. Weather fair. Nothing answered.*

She searched by feel, mostly, the lamp throwing her shadow huge against the shelves. She was looking for anything from the year of Maren's birth, or before it—anything that might explain the word *surety*, which her grandmother had said three nights ago, weeping into a cup of tea she never drank, talking to a man made of river-light who was not there.

*They hold it still. I gave it for the valley. It was a fair price. It was a fair price, Oscar, don't look at me like that.*

Oscar had not been looking at her like anything. Maren hadn't seen her at all.

In the deepest pigeonhole, behind a roll of land surveys and a bundle of letters tied with string gone brittle as straw, she found a document that was different from all the others. Older. The paper was not paper but something thin and pale as birchbark, and the ink had not faded but *deepened*, gone black and glossy as if written yesterday, or as if the words themselves refused to die.

There was no title. There was only the oath.

*Let it be entered into the memory of the mountain and the memory of the valley both,* it began, *that the people of Hollowmere, being small and afraid, do bargain with that which stirs beneath.*

Oscar read it twice. Then a third time, her lips moving, though she made no sound.

The terms were simple the way a knife is simple. The gods below would turn their faces from the valley. No prayer would be answered, no spell survive past the tree line, no wonder trouble the honest, frightened lives of Hollowmere's people. In exchange—protection. Peace. The valley would be forgotten by everything that hungered, and safe from everything that played.

The price was written in a different hand. Newer. Steadier.

*One soul, freely given, held in surety against the keeping of this oath. To be renewed each generation, that the bargain not lapse. The giver's line shall inherit the debt, and the debt shall inherit the giver's line.*

And beneath that, in a third hand—shaky, human, unmistakably Maren's—only four words:

*I gave it gladly.*

Oscar sat down on the cold floor without deciding to, her legs simply ending their argument with her.

*Gladly.* She turned the word over and found no comfort in it, only a terrible tenderness, because she had grown up watching Maren tend the guild garden with a devotion that had always seemed slightly out of proportion—deadheading flowers no one saw, talking to seedlings, standing in the rain to be sure the drainage channels ran clear. Oscar had thought it was love of growing things.

It had been love of a *gardener*.

She gathered the oath, the surveys, the letters, and she carried them up into the light.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat at Maren's bedside with the documents spread across the quilt, and when the moon came through the window and lay across her grandmother's face like a pale hand, Maren opened her eyes and looked at Oscar—truly looked, for the first time in weeks—and said, "You found it."

"I found it."

"Then you know what I am."

"I know what you *were*," Oscar said. "Tell me the rest."

Maren was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lost its wandering, its habit of addressing the empty corners of the room. It was young. It was, impossibly, the voice of a girl.

"I was seventeen," she said, "and I hated the valley. You can't imagine hating it, because you were born to it. But I'd heard stories—my mother's mother told them, back when telling them was still allowed. That past the tree line, rivers sang. That there were women who could call rain with a word and men who bargained with the wind. And in Hollowmere, nothing. Nothing, nothing, nothing. I used to stand at the tree line and press my hand against the air like a child pressing against a window."

She smiled, and it was a heartbroken smile, and Oscar understood she was not being told this story so much as *reliving* it, the way the dying return to their beginnings.

"I went past it once. Just my foot, just my hand. And the magic came into me like water into cracked earth, and I wept, and I thought: I cannot live without this. I will die of this valley. And that was when *he* found me."

"Vesper."

"You know his name." Maren's eyes were far away and shining. "He was the court's gardener even then. He kept the souls the way you'd keep an orchard—pruned, watered, turned toward the light. And he saw a girl standing at the boundary with her hand in a world she wasn't allowed, and he didn't tell me to go back. He said, *You're the first person in forty years who's reached for it instead of away from it.*"

Oscar's throat ached. "You loved him."

"I loved him the way the valley loved nothing. Wholly. Stupidly. He showed me the court—I went down, I know, I know what that means, but I was seventeen and the court is *beautiful*, Oscar, it is beautiful the way a trap is beautiful, all its teeth dressed in flowers. And when the old gods began to wake, and the valley's elders came to me—because I was the one with the gift, the one who could hear them—and asked me to bargain for peace, I said yes before they finished asking."

"Because of the oath."

"Because I thought I could have both." Maren's hand found Oscar's, and her grip was still strong. "I thought I'd give my soul as surety and live out my life beside it, tending it, visiting it, the way you'd visit a grave you loved. I thought love would be enough to keep the debt from ever being called. But the court doesn't work that way. A soul in surety is a soul in a *pot*. And Vesper—" her voice broke, and mended, and broke again— "Vesper was the one who had to plant me there. He begged them to take anything else. He offered his own. But the terms were the terms, and he is a creature of terms, and so he knelt in the dark and put my soul in the soil with his own two hands, and I watched him do it, and he wept, and I said *gladly*, because it was true, and because I wanted him to know that even the wound was a kind of gift."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

"Every generation renews it," Oscar said. "The debt passes down the line."

"It passes to you." Maren turned and looked at her then—fully, clearly, with the old sharpness rising through the ruin of her like a plant through cracked stone. "And they are waking, Oscar. I feel them under my feet. The prayers being answered in the out-valleys—that isn't grace. That's a creditor stretching before he rises. They will come for what's held, and they'll come at the equinox, because that is when the court opens its ledgers."

"Then I'll go down and pay it."

"You'll go down and they will *keep* you," Maren said fiercely. "You are seventeen-and-a-half years of everything I love, and I did not spend my soul so that the mountain could have you too."

Oscar looked at her grandmother—at the woman who had traded eternity for a valley of people who had forgotten her name, who had loved a god and been planted by him, who had spent sixty years watering a garden like a penance and a prayer—and something in her chest went quiet and hard and certain.

"You spent it *gladly*," Oscar said. "So did they write. And I'm going to spend mine the same way—not in a pot. In the open. Where you can watch it grow."

She stood, and gathered the oath from the quilt, and folded it into the breast of her coat, over her heart, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Outside, the wind had turned. It came down off the mountain smelling of deep stone and turned earth and something green that did not belong to any season Hollowmere had ever known.

The equinox was eleven days away.
