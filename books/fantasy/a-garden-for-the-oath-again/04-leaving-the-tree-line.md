## 4. Leaving the Tree Line

The night before she left, Oscar did not sleep. She sat in the guild kitchen with Maren's apron spread across the table like a patient on an operating cloth, and she unpicked it stitch by stitch.

She had known about the map for three days—since the night her grandmother's confession had cracked open like river ice, spilling fragments of a bargain made beneath the mountain. *The map is in my garden apron,* Maren had said, in one of her lucid hours, gripping Oscar's wrist with a strength that surprised them both. *I stitched it when I was young and stupid and certain I'd never need it. Every path I ever walked down there. Every door. Don't trust the doors that open easily.*

So Oscar sat by lamplight with a seam-ripper borrowed from the mending basket and freed the map from its hiding place, thread by thread. It was beautiful work, done in silk floss the color of rust and moss: the mountain rendered in long vertical stitches, its roots branching downward into tunnels marked in knots and French dots. A spiral at the heart of it, deep as the page allowed, circled twice. Beside the spiral, in tiny cross-stitched letters: *THE POTTERY ROWS.*

Oscar did not know what pottery rows were. She suspected she would learn.

Around the central mass of the mountain, Maren had stitched the world above—the valley, the tree line, the roads winding south and east toward passes Oscar had only ever seen as blue smudges on the horizon. And along every road, small marks: an X here, a circle there, a star. Debts, Oscar realized. Her grandmother had mapped not just the way down but the price of the way down—who owed what to whom, and where a traveler might stop to trade.

*The world prices everything,* Maren had told her once, years ago, apropos of nothing, while they thinned carrots together in the guild garden. Oscar had thought it was one of the old woman's riddles. Now she understood it was a warning, and a kindness, and a lesson given decades before it was needed.

She folded the apron into her pack, on top of everything else, where she could reach it without unpacking.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. Oscar dressed in her plainest clothes—wool trousers, a linen shirt, boots resoled twice—and braided her hair tight against the wind. She took little: the apron, a knife, bread and hard cheese wrapped in waxed cloth, a water skin, and Maren's tokens.

The tokens were the hardest part. They lived in a cedar box under Maren's bed, and until three nights ago Oscar had believed them to be keepsakes of no particular power—a river stone with a hole worn through its center; a copper coin stamped with no king or country, only a spiral; a sprig of something dried and silvery that might have been lavender and was not; and a small bone whistle, pale as milk, carved with a mouthpiece at one end and a single hole at the other.

*They'll open doors for you,* Maren had said. *Not all doors. But the ones that matter will know you're mine.* She had pressed the box into Oscar's hands and held on a moment too long. *And Oscar—if you meet him, if you meet the gardener—you tell him nothing you don't have to. He'll want your grief. He collects grief the way other men collect coins.*

Him. The gardener. The god her grandmother had loved, and bargained with, and never named aloud in sixty years.

Oscar closed the box and put it in her pack beside the apron, and tried not to think about what it meant that her grandmother still kept a dead god's gifts by her bed.

* * *

The guild saw her off at the gate, such as it was. There were only five of them left now—Brother Aldous with his bad hip, Sister Imke who'd gone deaf in one ear, old Tam and his two granddaughters—and they stood in the courtyard looking smaller than ever, a handful of gray figures in faded robes, keepers of oaths none of them could recite.

Aldous pressed a parcel into her hands: more bread, a jar of honey, a strip of dried venison. "For the road," he said gruffly. Then, quieter: "You'll come back."

It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. Oscar looked at the old man—at the tremor in his hands, at the way his eyes went past her shoulder to the tree line, dark against the paling sky—and understood that he knew. Not everything. But enough. The guild had always known enough and chosen silence; it was practically their founding principle.

"I'll come back," she said. "With her soul or without mine. One way or another, Hollowmere gets both of us back."

Imke laughed, a dry rustle. Tam crossed himself in the old way, thumb to sternum, though none of them believed in anything up here—that was rather the point of the valley. The granddaughters, seven and nine, simply stared at Oscar with the frank hunger of children watching someone do the thing everyone else was afraid of.

Then she shouldered her pack and walked out through the orchard, past the last of the apple trees, past the boundary stones with their worn carvings, to the tree line.

* * *

She had crossed it before. Everyone in Hollowmere had, at least once—traders going down to the lowland markets, young people struck by wanderlust, the occasional fool who ran to the edge on a dare just to feel the strange pressure of the outside world and came back pale and shaking, swearing never again. Magic couldn't follow you into the valley, but neither could you carry the valley's protection out. Crossing was like stepping from warm water into winter air. Unpleasant. Briefly shocking. Bearable.

That was what Oscar expected. That was what she braced for, standing at the margin where the meadow grass gave way to pine needles and shadow, the morning cold pooling around her ankles like standing water.

What happened instead was this: she took one step across the line, and the world inhaled her.

It began in her chest. Something that had been sealed her whole life—a door she hadn't known was a door—swung open, and what came through it was not air. It was pressure, weight, presence, the raw unfiltered attention of everything alive. The trees noticed her. Not metaphorically; she felt their attention the way you feel sunlight on closed eyelids, a warmth with direction and intent. The ground beneath her boots hummed with root-traffic, messages passing tree to tree in pulses she could almost parse. Above her, birdsong resolved into something structured, deliberate—gossip, maybe, or greeting, or warning. The wind carried tastes: iron and loam and distant rain and something sweeter, stranger, like the smell of a lightning strike remembered.

Oscar staggered. Caught herself on a pine trunk, bark rough and shockingly awake under her palm—it flinched at her touch, then leaned into it, curious. She gasped and the gasp turned into something between a laugh and a sob, because this was breathing. This was what breathing was supposed to be. All her life she had been taking shallow sips of a half-sealed room and calling it air.

*This is what she gave up,* she thought, and the thought hurt worse than the crossing. *Sixty years of this, traded away. For us. For the valley.*

She stood bent over, hands on knees, while the world poured itself into her and her body learned, painfully, joyfully, to hold it. Her teeth ached. Her vision sharpened until she could count the scales on a moth's wing thirty feet away. Somewhere below her feet, deep, deep down, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the shift traveled up through the bedrock and into her bones like a bell struck underwater.

The gods were down there. She could feel them now, the way you can feel a fire in the next room—not see it, but know it, warmth through the wall. They were stirring. They were hungry. And one of them was holding her grandmother's soul in a pot somewhere in the dark, keeping it alive the way you keep a cutting from a favorite plant.

"Right," Oscar said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the ringing air, too loud and too small at once. She straightened. She wiped her face with her sleeve. She took out the apron and unfolded it, holding the stitched map up against the real mountains rising eastward, and found the first mark: a circle, two days' walk south along the valley's outer skirt, where a hedge-witch named Odile kept a ferry across the Graywater and owed Maren Whitlock a favor forty years old.

The world was larger than promised. That part was true, and wonderful, and terrible. What the elders had never said—what perhaps they didn't know, sealed as they were behind their forgetting—was how much of that largeness was appetite. Even standing here at the mere threshold, Oscar could feel it: the attention of the trees was not entirely kind. The birdsong had edges. The humming ground carried, beneath its traffic of roots, a lower note of wanting. Everything out here was awake, and everything that was awake wanted something, and most of it would not care whether she flourished or withered so long as it got what it wanted.

Her whole life she had been taught that staying small was safety. Now she understood the truth of it, and the cost of it, and the lie inside it: smallness wasn't safety. Smallness was just being overlooked a little longer.

Oscar folded the apron away, adjusted her pack straps, and turned south, walking the narrow strip of wild land between the valley's silence and the wide world's noise. Behind her, Hollowmere sank back into its green hush. Ahead, the Graywater glinted through the trees, and beyond it the mountains stood up against the sky like the ribs of something buried.

By midday she had learned three things. First, that magic, once let in, did not stay politely at arm's length—it soaked into her, changed her gait, sharpened her hearing until the forest was almost too loud to bear, and she had to practice, walking, at closing the new door in her chest partway, finding the middle register between deafness and drowning. Second, that her grandmother's tokens were not inert: the coin warmed in her pocket whenever she passed a place where something watched from off the path, which was often, which meant the woods were full of watchers, which meant she walked faster. Third, that she was not afraid—or rather, that fear and exhilaration had fused into a single bright alloy she couldn't separate, and didn't especially want to.

On the evening of the second day, the Graywater appeared through the alders, broad and slate-colored and moving with the slow muscular patience of deep water. On the far bank, a crooked house balanced on stilts above the flood line, lantern-light in its windows, and a woman standing on the dock as if she had been expecting someone.

Oscar touched the river stone in her pocket. It was warm.

"You'd be Maren's girl," the woman called across the water, in a voice like reeds. "I can smell the valley on you. Well." A pause, and the lantern light caught her smile, which had too many pleasant edges. "Come across, then. We've debts to discuss, you and I—and I hear the equinox won't wait for anyone."
