## 5. The Road of Small Debts

The first thing Oscar learned outside Hollowmere was that nothing was free—not bread, not directions, not even the right to stand at a stranger's fire and warm her hands.

She learned it at a crossroads shrine three days past the tree line, where a hedge-witch named Odell kept a crooked little stall of charms and cures beneath a tarp of patched oilcloth. Oscar had asked, politely, whether the road ahead forded the river or crossed at the old stone bridge. Odell had looked up from the dried nettle she was bundling and said, "That depends. What have you got?"

"Got?"

"To trade." Odell's eyes were pale as skimmed milk, and they moved over Oscar the way a butcher's eyes move over a carcass—assessing, unhurried. "A question is a small thing. Small things take small payment. A lock of hair. The memory of a name you don't need anymore. A favor owed, to be called in whenever I like, wherever I like." She smiled, and her teeth were very white against skin the color of walnut shells. "Or coin, if you're one of those."

Oscar had coin—a guild stipend sewn into her coat lining—but something in Odell's tone made her reluctant to spend it. Coin was simple. Coin ended the transaction. And she had come out here, in part, to learn how transactions worked.

"A favor," Oscar said.

Odell's smile widened by a single tooth. "Braver than most. Sit, then. Favor first, answer after."

So Oscar sat on an upturned crate while Odell scratched a mark into a sliver of birch bark—a spiral, closed tight as a snail shell—and told Oscar to press her thumb to it and say *I owe*. The words came out easily, which frightened her more than if they hadn't. Somewhere under her breastbone, she felt the mark settle, light as a seed caught in wool.

"The river forks a mile on," Odell said, tying off the nettle bundle. "Take the left branch. The right goes through a bog that collects promises, and you'd walk out the other side missing a few you didn't know you'd made." She paused. "You're Whitlock, aren't you? You have the look—the gardeners' hands, the careful eyes."

Oscar's stomach dropped. "No," she said, and heard how stiffly it came out. "I'm—I'm Isolde Fenn. I'm a clerk. For a debt-broker, down in the low country."

It was the story Wren-of-the-road would need later; she had stitched it together from Maren's fragments during the long cold nights before she left. But saying it aloud, to a stranger, with a hedge-witch's pale eyes on her face—it fit differently than she expected. *Isolde Fenn* sat on her shoulders like a well-cut coat. Not invisible. Just unremarkable, which was its own kind of armor.

Odell studied her a moment longer, then shrugged. "Fenn, then. Whatever you are, girl, mind the fork. And mind this—" she tapped the birch-bark spiral where it lay among her wares, "—a favor given freely weighs less than one extracted. Remember that when someone asks something of you. Ask yourself what they'll say you agreed to."

Oscar walked away with her answer and her new debt, and found, to her considerable unease, that both felt like gifts.

* * *

The river spirits were worse, or better—she never decided which.

They lived in the left branch of the fork, a family of them in a slow brown stretch of water overhung with alder, and they announced themselves by pulling her bootlaces undone while she knelt to fill her flask. When she looked up, the surface of the river had arranged itself into faces: three of them, indistinct, the way faces in dreams are indistinct, all ripple and suggestion.

"Toll," said the largest face, in a voice like gravel turning over underwater. "Crossing costs."

"I don't have much," Oscar said carefully. She was learning to count before she spoke.

"You have stories." A smaller face surfaced beside the first, younger-sounding. "Stories about above. About the valley where magic can't go. Nobody comes from there. Tell us what it's like and we'll carry you across dry-shod."

So Oscar sat on the bank with her boots off and told them about Hollowmere. She told them about frost that behaved itself, about prayers that fell like stones into still water and made no rings at all, about a garden where she had spent eleven years coaxing ordinary beans and turnips out of obedient, godless soil. She told them about the tree line, and how it felt to cross it—took her longer than she meant it to, because describing it meant admitting what it had felt like, the tearing-open, the terrible gasp of a world suddenly full of pressure after a lifetime at sea level.

When she finished, the faces were silent. Then the youngest one said, wonderingly, "You *lived* there? Like a fish in a jar? Like a candle under a cup?"

"It wasn't cruel," Oscar said, and then, hearing herself, amended: "It wasn't meant to be."

"No," the eldest face said, and the river went briefly cold around her ankles. "Nothing down here is ever *meant* to be. That's the trick of it. Cross, clerk. You've paid."

They carried her the way current carries a leaf—suddenly, effortlessly, the water parting around her so that her hem stayed dry—and set her down on the far bank with a gentleness that undid her more than any menace could have. As she climbed away from the water, she heard the youngest voice behind her, high and carrying:

"She's going to the mountain! Did you see? She smells like the pots!"

Oscar did not stop walking. But her hand went to the apron folded in her pack—the one with Maren's map stitched into the lining—and stayed there a long time.

* * *

She met Wren at a waystation called the Tinker's Rest, four days out, where the road began its first real climb toward the mountain's shoulder.

He was occupying the best chair by the fire with the ease of a man who had never once been asked to give it up—long-legged, dark-haired, with a courier's satchel slung across his chest and a deck of cards he appeared to be using mostly as an excuse to talk to people. He clocked Oscar within thirty seconds of her coming through the door. She watched him do it: the quick flick of his eyes, cataloguing her travel-worn boots, her broker-clerk's plainness, the garden-calluses on her hands that no clerk should have.

Then he smiled at her like they were already friends, and patted the bench beside him.

"Isolde Fenn," she said, before he could ask. "Clerk."

"Wren." He swept an exaggerated bow without leaving the chair. "Courier, currently between deliveries, currently also between fortunes. Can I buy you soup? It's terrible soup. The kind where you identify the vegetables by prayer."

Against every instinct the guild had ever planted in her, Oscar laughed.

They ate the terrible soup, and he talked, and she listened the way she had been practicing listening for four days now—with her whole attention, treating every word as a price tag or a receipt. He complained about the toll bridges. He traded gossip about the lowland courts with the innkeeper for a room. He lost two hands of cards to a tinker and paid with a song instead, and when the tinker objected, Wren said cheerfully, "Put it on my account," and the whole common room groaned, and Oscar understood that *put it on my account* was a joke everyone but her had heard before.

"You're not from the low country," he said, when the crowd thinned.

"I'm from the low country."

"Mm." He shuffled his cards without looking at them. "You hold your cup like it might cost you something. You flinched when the innkeeper said *on the house*, like generosity was a trap. And when I said my name, you waited—you counted—like you were deciding what it would be worth to know me." He laid the deck facedown and finally looked at her properly, and his eyes were quick and brown and not unkind. "Low-country clerks don't do any of that. So either you're lying about who you are, or you're from somewhere that taught you everything has a price. And there's only one somewhere I know like that."

Oscar's pulse ticked up. "Hollowmere doesn't exist."

"Course it doesn't." He grinned. "Neither does the debt I owe. Want to hear about it? Since we're both nobody-from-nowhere."

She should have demurred. She should have pleaded fatigue, gone up to her room, kept her edges to herself. Instead she heard herself say, "All right," and pulled her chair closer to the fire.

Wren turned his cards over one at a time as he talked, laying out a fortune that wasn't a fortune. Born to a courier family, running messages since he was nine. At nineteen, a job came through—a sealed letter, a generous fee, delivery to a door deep under the mountain's root. "I didn't ask what was in it. Couriers who ask stop being couriers." His voice did something small and complicated on the word *couriers*. "Door opened before I knocked. Something inside looked at me. Not at the letter—at *me*. And it said, *you'll do.* And the fee tripled, and I took the letter in, and I came back out, and it took me three years to understand what I'd agreed to. They don't collect all at once, you know. They take installments. A year of good luck here. The color from one eye there. My mother's memory of my laugh—" He turned over the last card. It was blank. "That one hurt."

The fire popped. Oscar found her throat was tight.

"What was in the letter?" she asked quietly.

"That's the funny part. I peeked. Of course I peeked." He smiled, but it was the thinnest smile she'd seen on him. "It was a list. Names. Hundreds of names. People who'd prayed to something, anywhere, anytime, and gotten an answer." He gathered the cards up in a single smooth motion, and just like that he was charming Wren again, insufferable Wren, cards-and-jokes Wren. "Anyway. That's my account balance. What's yours, Isolde Fenn, clerk of nowhere?"

And because she was tired, and because the fire was warm, and because some reckless gardener-part of her wanted—*needed*—to practice being the woman who bargained with gods before she actually had to be her, Oscar heard herself say:

"I'm going to the mountain to buy back a soul."

She expected him to laugh. She expected the flicker of calculation, the retreat, the door closing politely between them the way doors close all along this road of priced things.

Instead Wren went very still. Then he leaned forward, elbows on knees, and all the charm drained out of his face and left something older and sharper underneath.

"Whose soul?" he said.

"My grandmother's. She sold it to seal a bargain before I was born. The court holds it. The equinox is in nine days." She met his eyes. "Why does it matter whose?"

"Because," Wren said slowly, "two weeks ago I delivered another sealed letter to another door under the mountain. And I peeked again. I always peek. And this one wasn't a list of names." He glanced once around the nearly empty common room, and when he looked back at her, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "It was an inventory. Of the pots. And Isolde—there were empty rows. Whole shelves of them. Souls going *out* of that place, and nothing going in. Whatever the court is keeping all those souls *for*—" he tapped the table twice, "—it's running out. And things that run out get desperate. Desperate creditors change the terms mid-deal. I've seen it happen to men smaller than gods."

Outside, far off in the dark, thunder rolled down off the mountain's shoulder—though the sky overhead was clear and full of indifferent stars.

"So," Wren said, and the grin crept back, but carefully now, like a man testing ice. "Nine days. One dying court. Two nobodies-from-nowhere with accounts in arrears." He stuck out his hand across the table. "Partners?"

Oscar looked at the offered hand—quick fingers, courier's calluses, a faint scar across the palm like a stitch in cloth—and thought of Odell's warning: *ask yourself what they'll say you agreed to.*

She took it anyway.

"One condition," she said. "Nobody puts anything on anybody's account. We trade even, favor for favor, from here to the mountain."

Wren's eyebrows rose. Then he laughed—a real one this time, surprised out of him—and shook her hand firmly. "Isolde Fenn. You're going to be *terrible* for business."

Above the waystation, unseen behind clear stars, the thunder rolled again—closer now, and patient, like something counting down.
