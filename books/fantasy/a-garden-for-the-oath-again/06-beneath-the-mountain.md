## 6. Beneath the Mountain

The root-tunnels began where the map said they would: a split in the earth beneath an alder older than any guild record, its bark scarred with handprints worn smooth by centuries of desperate palms. Oscar stood at the lip of the descent for a long moment, breathing the cold air that rose from below—air that smelled of loam and iron and something sweeter underneath, like flowers forced to bloom out of season.

*Like Maren's garden in winter,* she thought, and hated herself for the comparison before she'd even made it.

She went down.

The tunnel was not dug; that became clear within the first hundred paces. It was *grown*. The walls were root-wood, pale as bone where her lantern touched them, braided into arches and ribs like the vaulting of some buried cathedral. Fine hair-roots threaded through the gaps, and when she brushed past them they curled toward her fingers—not grasping, exactly. Testing. Tasting the air she disturbed.

Magic lived here. She felt it the way a deaf woman might feel music through a floor: pressure, rhythm, a hum in her back teeth. Her stolen tokens—Maren's copper ring, the knucklebone on its cord—warmed against her skin until she had to shift them to different pockets. Everything down here was awake, and everything awake was taking her measure.

She recited her new name under her breath with each step, a rosary of lies. *I am Sable Fenwick, clerk to the debt-broker Corvane Ashlar. I keep ledgers. I count what is owed.* Wren had drilled her on it for two days beside a dying fire, tossing questions at her like stones. What does your master trade in? Souls, sureties, seasons. Why does he send a clerk instead of coming himself? Because the court despises him and he knows it, and he sends the one person whose presence costs him nothing. And if they ask why your hands are calloused like a gardener's?

Oscar had looked at her own hands then, dirt worked so deep into the creases it would never fully leave, and said: *I tell them I was demoted.*

Wren had laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and then gone quiet in a way that made Oscar's chest ache. "They'll believe you," she'd said finally. "That's the terrible part. Down there, everyone is someone who used to be something else."

* * *

The tunnels opened without warning into the antechamber of the court, and Oscar stopped so abruptly that the roots behind her rustled, displeased at the interruption of her passage.

Light came from everywhere and nowhere—a green-gold luminescence rising from the walls themselves, from veins of sap glowing slow as heartbeat. The chamber was vast beyond reason, its ceiling lost in a canopy of hanging roots from which dripped water that fell upward again before it struck the ground. And filling the space, tier upon tier upon tier, were shelves.

Not shelves. *Terraces.* Carved from living wood, ascending into the dark, and on every terrace, in rows as straight and patient as anything her guild had ever planted—

Pots.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Clay vessels of every size, some small as teacups, some wide as rain barrels, each one holding soil dark as midnight and, growing from that soil, something that was not quite a plant and not quite a person. Some were no more than a bud, tight and gray. Some had unfurled into shapes that made Oscar's stomach turn: a stem crowned with a face in miniature, eyes closed, mouth slightly open as though mid-sentence. One near the entrance had bloomed into a full figure no taller than her forearm, seated cross-legged in its pot, hands resting on its knees, wearing an expression of profound and endless waiting.

A placard of pale wood stood planted in its soil. Oscar made herself read it.

*HALLWEN OF THE FORD. Surety taken 214 years past. Debt: one winter's mercy, unpaid. Status: dormant. Do not prune.*

*Do not prune.* As if the worst thing you could do to a soul was tidy it.

Her legs carried her forward because stopping meant screaming, and screaming meant death, and she had not come all this way to die in the lobby. She kept her eyes down, her ledger-clutch under her arm, her spine bent into the apologetic curve of a person who has never once been important. Clerks, she reminded herself, were furniture. Furniture could look at anything and be forgiven, because furniture saw nothing worth remembering.

But she remembered. Gods help her, she remembered everything—the rows, the spacing, the little wooden labels, the way the light was tuned to each specimen's need. She had spent eleven years tending a garden just like this. Smaller. Kinder. But built on the same bones: things kept alive in captivity, arranged for the convenience of their keeper, flourishing precisely because they were not permitted to leave.

*You understand this place,* whispered a voice that might have been the walls or might have been her own conscience. *And it understands you.*

"Name and business." The voice came from a desk grown out of the floor itself, and behind the desk sat a creature that had once perhaps been a man. Bark climbed his throat like a collar. His eyes were the yellow-green of new leaves.

"Sable Fenwick," Oscar said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her more than anything else that day. "Clerk to Corvane Ashlar, debt-broker, holding instruments of transfer for review by the Keeper of Accounts."

The leaf-eyed creature extended a hand like a bundle of twigs. She gave him the forged letter of introduction—Wren's work, inked on paper that smelled faintly of river-water and old promises. He read it twice. Somewhere above them, in the terraced dark, a soul in a pot sighed in its sleep.

"Ashlar," the creature said, with the flat distaste of a man naming a disease. "He still trades in sureties? The market moved on from sureties three reigns ago."

"My master believes the classics endure."

"My master believes the classics *rot*." He stamped the letter with a seal that bloomed out of his palm—a flower pressing itself into wax—and waved her through. "Third terrace. Ask for Nettlewick. And clerk—" the twig-fingers caught her sleeve, gentle as falling leaves, "—do not touch the pots. Some of them remember being held, and they grieve when it stops."

Oscar walked. She did not run. Running was for prey, and clerks were furniture, and furniture moved at the speed of its owner's indifference.

* * *

The third terrace was quieter than the ones below, which somehow made it worse. Here the souls were older—their pots glazed with age, their growths gnarled into shapes that suggested long accommodation, lives pruned so many times they had forgotten what unpruned felt like. Oscar passed a row of them labeled in a script so old the letters had begun to sprout, tiny green shoots emerging from the carved names themselves, and she thought: *these were people who had grandmothers too. People who were someone's whole weather.*

Then she stopped walking, because she had seen the label.

It was halfway down the row, on a pot larger than most, its clay cracked and mended with gold—someone had repaired this vessel with care, with reverence, with the kind of attention that only one thing in the world earns. The soul inside had grown tall and spare, a figure of gray-green light with its head bowed, and around its feet grew a scatter of tiny white flowers that Oscar knew better than her own heartbeat.

Yarrow. The flower she'd been named for. The flower Maren planted every spring on the grave of a daughter, and never explained, and watered anyway.

The label read: *MAREN WHITLOCK. Surety taken 61 years past. Debt: the peace of Hollowmere, renewed each equinox. Status: flowering. DO NOT PRUNE. DO NOT REPOT. DO NOT TOUCH. By order of the Gardener Himself.*

Oscar's vision blurred. She gripped the edge of a shelf and the wood grew warm under her palm, responsive, almost tender, and she snatched her hand back as if burned.

*Sixty-one years.* Her mother's whole life, spent as interest. Her own childhood, spent as collateral's shadow. Every hollow ritual, every forgotten oath, every year the guild had knelt to gods who no longer answered—all of it purchased with this. With a woman in a pot, blooming white flowers for a god who came to look at her, and called it keeping her safe.

"She's lovely this season, isn't she?"

Oscar turned so fast her hood slipped. Behind her stood a figure she had not heard approach—which should have been impossible, should have sent her hand to the knife at her belt, but instead rooted her to the floor like something newly planted.

He was tall in the way that trees are tall, not towering but *present*, occupying space the way weather occupies a valley. His hair was the color of late summer grass going to seed. His clothes were simple—a gardener's smock, soil-darkened at the knees—and his hands, when he folded them, were the hands of someone who had done this work for so long that gentleness had become indistinguishable from habit.

His eyes were brown and warm and unbearably sad, and Oscar recognized them instantly, because she had seen them every day of her life in the mirror of her grandmother's face.

"You're looking at her the way the last one looked at her," he said softly. "Sixty years ago. Before the bargain was struck." He tilted his head, studying Oscar with the unhurried attention he might give a seedling of uncertain provenance. "You have her chin. You have her way of standing as if apology were a posture."

"I don't know what you mean." The lie came out thin as frost.

"No," Vesper agreed. "You wouldn't. That's rather the point of you." He stepped closer, close enough that she could smell him—loam and rain and yarrow, *yarrow*, the scent of every summer she had ever loved—and his voice dropped to something almost private. "I have watched over her for sixty-one years, Sable Fenwick. I have pruned nothing. I have repotted nothing. I have let her grow exactly as she wished to grow, in the only soil that could hold her safe while the world above forgot us. And now the world above has started praying again, and the debts have come due, and I find—" his hands tightened, bark creaking, "—I find that I am tired of keeping gardens."

He looked at Maren's soul, bowed and blooming, and then back at Oscar, and his sadness rearranged itself into something sharper. Something like hope, which was far more dangerous.

"The Keeper of Accounts will want to see you tomorrow," he said. "But I wanted to see you first. Tell me, clerk—when you look at her, what do you feel? Choose your answer carefully. I'll know if it's a lie. I always know. I taught her mother to lie, after all, and I caught every one."

Oscar's heart slammed against her ribs. Every instinct honed in a valley that forgot magic told her to bow, deflect, become furniture. But Maren's soul stirred in its pot—just barely, just a tremor of gray-green light turning toward her, the way a flower turns toward a window—and Oscar understood with terrible clarity that this was the hook the whole court had been baited for, and she had swallowed it the moment she walked in.

So she told the truth, because it was the only currency she had left.

"She looks like my grandmother," Oscar said. "And I've come to take her home."

Vesper closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and ancient, and full of sixty-one years of a love he had expressed entirely in pruning shears and patience.

"Good," he said quietly. "Then we finally have something to negotiate."
