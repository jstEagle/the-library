## 7. The Gardener of Souls

The rows went on longer than the mountain should have allowed.

Oscar had learned, in her first days below, that the court's architecture obeyed no geometry she trusted. Corridors curved when they wished. Staircases descended into rooms she'd already left. But this hall—the soul-house—was honest in its vastness, and that honesty was worse. There was no trick of the light to soften it. Only shelf after shelf of clay pots, each one cradling a faint glow like an ember refusing to die, stretching back into dark that her eyes could not finish.

She walked with her ledger clutched to her chest, the debt-broker's clerk she was pretending to be, and let her feet follow the numbers. The broker, a gray-lipped creature called Mistress Sallow, had sent her down to verify holdings before the equinox audit. *Row nine through fourteen, the old contracts,* Sallow had said, picking at her teeth with a sliver of bone. *Mind you don't breathe on them. Some of the older souls mistake breath for prayer.*

Oscar breathed carefully.

Each pot bore a label in script so fine it seemed grown rather than written. Names, dates of deposit, terms of renewal. She read as she walked, keeping her face bored, her steps unhurried, while inside her something wound tighter with every row. *Hollis Bramblewick, deposited year of the Long Frost, term: perpetual.* *Sera of the Ford, deposited—* She stopped herself from reading the names too closely. If she read them all, she would have to grieve them all, and she could not afford grief yet. Grief was a luxury for after.

Row twelve.

She knew it before she saw it—not by the label but by the light. Every other pot glowed the same banked amber, the color of coals at dawn. This one burned green. Garden-green. New-leaf-green. The exact shade of the first shoots Oscar coaxed from Hollowmere soil every spring, the color she had loved before she had words for loving it.

MAREN WHITLOCK, said the label. DEPOSITED YEAR OF THE SEALING. TERM: PERPETUAL, RENEWABLE AT EQUINOX BY GRACE OF THE COURT.

The soul inside turned toward her. Oscar felt it happen—a pressure against the pot's inner wall, gentle as a moth testing glass. The green light pulsed once, twice, and though souls had no faces, no voices, no hands, Oscar would have sworn on every hollow oath her guild had ever forgotten that her grandmother was looking at her.

"Maren," she whispered, then caught herself. Clerks did not whisper names. Clerks checked seals.

Her hands were shaking as she lifted the pot. It was warm. Not fever-warm—*hearth*-warm, the warmth of the kitchen where Maren had taught her to knead bread, to read weather in the smoke, to keep her voice low when the guild elders passed. Seventy years in a clay pot beneath a mountain, and her grandmother still felt like home.

The seal around the rim was wax the color of dried blood, stamped with a sigil Oscar recognized from the archive: a tree whose roots were also branches, closing into a circle. The original oath-mark. The mark of the bargain.

"I found you," she breathed. "I'm going to get you out."

The light flared—and somewhere behind her, soft as snowfall, someone began to applaud.

* * *

He stood at the end of the row where the shelves gave way to a garden.

That was the only word for it. In the heart of the god-court, past the last pot, the floor opened into living earth, black and rich, planted with things Oscar had no names for—flowers that bloomed in colors light hadn't invented, grass that bent toward her footsteps like it remembered being touched. And tending it, sleeves rolled to the elbow, soil to the wrists, was a man who was not a man.

He was tall the way winter is tall. His hair was dark going silver at the temples, his hands elegant and earth-caked, and when he turned, his eyes were the green of Maren's pot—of new leaves, of growing things, of the exact color Oscar had been chasing her whole life without knowing why.

"You check seals with your heart in your throat," he said. His voice was low and roughened, like wind through wheat. "And you talk to the merchandise. Sallow's clerks don't do either."

Oscar set the pot down with exaggerated care, as if her arms weren't screaming to hold it forever. "I'm thorough."

"You're Whitlock." He said the name the way one says a word in a language they'd sworn off. He wiped his hands on his apron—an apron, here, in a god's court, plain canvas gone soft with use—and crossed the boundary between garden and aisle. Up close he smelled of loam and rain and something older underneath, something like the air before a storm decides. "You have her chin. Her stubbornness lives in the same place."

"Then you know who I am." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "Which means you know I've come for her."

"I know you've come for her." He looked at the pot, and his whole face changed—grief moving through it like water under ice, there and gone, there and gone. "I have watched that light for seventy years, girl. Do you imagine I don't know what you came for?"

"Then give her back."

"The terms are not mine to break." He said it flatly, wearily, the voice of a man reciting a law he'd stopped believing in. "The contract is sealed. A soul for a peace. Your grandmother signed it knowing the price. The valley has slept safe ever since. That is what oaths *are*, Whitlock. They hold even when holding hurts."

"Then they're cages with good manners." The words were out before she could weigh them, and she watched them land—watched something flicker behind those leaf-green eyes that was not anger. Recognition, maybe. Or hunger.

"What's your name?" he asked suddenly. "Your true one. Not the clerk's."

"Oscar."

"Oscar." He tasted it. "A gardener's name. Do you know what you're standing in?"

"A warehouse."

"A *nursery*." He gestured, and the whole hall answered—every pot's glow brightening a fraction, thousands of small lights leaning toward him like flowers toward sun. "Every soul in this room was placed in my care. I prune what festers. I water what wilts. I keep them alive, because the alternative—" his jaw tightened— "the alternative is what the court does with neglected holdings. Souls don't die here, Oscar. They *thin*. They go thin as smoke and then they go less than smoke, and the court calls that accounting." He looked at her, and now the grief stayed. "Your grandmother's light burns greener today than the day she arrived. Whatever else I am, I am the reason she is still *her*."

The terrible thing—the thing Oscar would carry like a stone for the rest of her life—was that she believed him. And believing him, she understood this place. Rows. Labels. Tending. She had spent her life doing gentler versions of exactly this, kneeling in Hollowmere dirt, keeping small things alive in a valley that forgot. She had walked into the enemy's house and found it shaped like her own hands.

"That's not freedom," she said quietly. "That's very beautiful captivity."

"Yes," Vesper said simply. "It is. I built it myself. I tell myself that makes it kinder." He turned away, back toward the garden, and plucked a dead leaf from a stem that shimmered. "You want terms. Very well. You didn't fight your way down here; you *infiltrated*. That takes nerve the court respects. So here is my offer, and hear all of it before you spit."

He faced her again, and the gardener fell away, and what remained was old—old as the mountain, old as the first oath ever sworn in fear.

"Serve the court until the equinox. Six days. Tend these rows as your grandmother once tended our gardens above—she was magnificent, you should know that; everything I know about keeping things alive, I learned watching her hands. Serve faithfully, and at the equinox I will release Maren Whitlock whole. Un-thinned. My own name pledged on it."

"And the cost?" Oscar asked, because there was always a cost; the whole world below ran on costs; Wren had taught her that much. "What do I tend? What happens to me at the end of six days?"

Vesper was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere far above, water dripped through stone, patient as time.

"At the end of six days," he said, "you may walk out. That is the offer. No second soul. No heir's clause. You serve, she goes free, and you walk out." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Whether you *want* to walk out by then—that, I make no promises about."

The green light pulsed between them. Maren's soul, listening. Oscar thought of her grandmother's confession fragments, the way her voice had softened on certain old words, the way she'd once hummed a tune no one in Hollowmere knew and called it *his*. She thought of the archive page, the oath written in two hands—one formal, one hurried, the hurried hand trailing off mid-sentence as if the writer had been interrupted by something worth being interrupted by.

Duty said: refuse. Duty said: find another way, dig, scheme, steal her out tonight.

But duty had never stood in a garden underground and felt the soil notice her. Duty had never been looked at by eyes the color of everything she'd ever tried to grow.

"Six days," Oscar said. "And if I find, in those six days, that this court is rotten—if I find what it does with the thinned ones—I reserve the right to burn whatever I find."

Something moved across Vesper's face that might have been hope, badly disguised as caution. "You drive a hard bargain for a clerk."

"I was raised by oath-keepers. We forget what our oaths bind, but we remember how to make them."

She put out her hand. He took it, and his grip was warm as turned earth, and when their skin met the whole nursery flared—every pot, every ember, every green light rising together like a held breath—and Oscar felt, unmistakably, Maren's soul press against the clay between them, urgent now, almost frantic, as if trying to say something through seventy years of glass.

Vesper felt it too. He went still, head tilted, listening to a language made of light.

"She's never done that," he murmured. "In seventy years, she's never—" He stopped. Looked at Oscar with an expression she couldn't read and didn't trust. "Girl. What exactly did your grandmother send you down here to do?"

Oscar smiled the smile she'd practiced on the road, the one that belonged to the woman she was becoming.

"Ask her yourself," she said. "At the equinox."

Behind them, deep in the rows, a pot flickered—and went briefly, impossibly, dark.
