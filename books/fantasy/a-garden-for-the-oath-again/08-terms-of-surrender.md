## 8. Terms of Surrender

Oscar had negotiated with garden pests, with guild elders, with the rain itself in a drought year. She had never negotiated with something that could look at her and see every lie she'd brought down the mountain like lint on a coat.

Vesper sat across the table of living root, his hands folded, patient as topsoil. The chamber around them was small—smaller than she'd expected from a god—and warm, lit by lanterns of pale fungus that breathed light instead of flame. Between them lay Maren's pot: a squat clay vessel no larger than a cooking pot, from which grew a single stem of silver-green, its leaves trembling though there was no wind.

Her grandmother's soul. It looked like thyme.

"You've been in my rows," Vesper said. Not an accusation. An observation, delivered the way one notes weather.

"I have." Oscar kept her hands flat on the root-table where he could see them. "I found her."

"Then you know I have kept her well. Forty years, and she has not wilted once." His voice was low and resonant, like water moving under stone. He was beautiful in the way old things are beautiful—not the beauty of youth but of endurance, of a face that had been loved hard enough to leave marks. "You came to bargain for her. So bargain."

"The guild's oath," Oscar said. "The original one. Protection purchased with a soul, renewed each generation. My grandmother paid it. The debt passed to me as heir. Those are your terms."

"They were her terms first," Vester said softly, correcting himself before she could even flinch at the slip—"Vesper," he said, "her terms first. She offered. I only accepted what love insisted on giving."

"That's not how oaths work."

"No?" Something moved behind his eyes, old and tired and terribly gentle. "Then tell me how they work, Oscar Whitlock. You crossed the tree line carrying her apron. You walked into my court wearing a dead woman's name. You have been making oaths since you left the valley—every step was one. Tell me you don't understand exactly what we do here."

The fungal light pulsed. In the pot between them, the silver-green stem leaned toward Oscar, the way plants lean toward sun.

She made herself breathe. *He is testing whether you flinch. Don't flinch.*

"My terms," she said. "Maren goes free. Her soul returns to her body, whole. And my debt—the debt you claim I inherited—is discharged."

"And what do you offer in exchange?"

"Service." The word tasted like iron filings. She'd rehearsed it in the dark of the clerk's cell Wren had found her, turning it over until it stopped feeling like surrender and started feeling like strategy. "A season in your court. A year. However long it takes to repay what she owed."

Vesper was quiet for a long moment. The lanterns dimmed and brightened with his breathing—or perhaps with hers; down here it was getting harder to tell where the court ended and she began.

"A year," he said at last. "In exchange for forty years of keeping. That is not a trade, little gardener. That is an insult dressed as arithmetic."

"It's a beginning."

"It is nothing." He rose, and the room rose with him—the roots of the ceiling arching, the light bending toward him like stems toward sun. "Do you know what happens to a soul when its keeper weakens? Do you know what I am, Oscar Whitlock? I am the last gardener of a dying court. Every god you see in these halls is running on stored light, on hoarded devotion, on souls potted and pruned because no one above remembers to pray. Your grandmother's soul has fed me through winters that would have unmade me otherwise. And you offer me *a year*."

The stem in the pot shivered. Oscar felt it like a hand gripping hers.

*Wren*, she thought. *Where are you? What have you found?*

---

Wren had found the nursery.

They hadn't meant to. They'd meant to find the treasury, or the armory, or anything that would give Oscar leverage beyond her own stubbornness. Instead they'd followed a courier's corridor past the third checkpoint—wearing a smile and a stolen sash and the particular confidence of someone who belonged everywhere—and come out into a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor.

Rows. Rows and rows and rows, receding into the dark like a field at night. Potted souls in their thousands, each one labeled in script that hurt to read, each one tended by hands that moved slower than they should have. The gods who worked the rows moved like sleepwalkers. Like starving men pretending at a feast.

And between the rows, Wren saw what the pots were standing in.

Not soil. Not stone. A pale, fibrous mat that covered the entire cavern floor, threaded with veins of gold that pulsed—faintly, arrhythmically, the way a heart beats when it's forgetting how. The roots of every pot plunged into it. The veins carried something upward. Light, maybe. Or life. Or both.

The court wasn't hoarding souls out of greed.

The court was *eating* them. Slowly. Desperately. Because the soil of the deep places had gone barren, and the gods had nothing else to draw from, and every soul in every pot was a meal stretched across decades by keepers too proud—or too frightened—to admit they were starving.

"Oh," Wren said aloud, to no one. "Oh, you stupid, magnificent, dying bastards."

A god working three rows over turned its head. Its face was kind and hollowed, like a mask left out in weather. "Courier," it said. "Are you lost?"

"No," Wren said, and heard their own voice shake. "I think I'm the opposite."

---

"He's feeding on her." Oscar stood at the window of the guest-chamber they'd given her—a real window, cut into living rock, looking out over terraced gardens that climbed the cavern walls in impossible spirals. Wren perched on the sill, still in the stolen sash, dirt on their knees from the nursery. "That's why he wants a year of service. It's not labor he needs. It's proximity. It's *me*."

"Souls in service ripen," Wren said quietly. "I asked one of the row-tenders. She thought I was making conversation. Souls that choose to stay feed better than souls that are taken. He doesn't want your work, Oscar. He wants your *consent*."

Oscar looked at her hands. They were gardener's hands, scarred and capable, and they were shaking.

"Then we don't offer consent," she said. "We offer something better."

"There isn't anything better. That's the whole architecture of this place. Everything is priced. Everything is owned."

"Everything up here, maybe." She turned, and Wren saw it happen—the thing they'd been watching for since the tree line, the moment the quiet girl from Hollowmere became someone the world would have to reckon with. "Wren. What did the floor of that nursery look like?"

"Dead. Threadbare. There were bare patches the size of fields."

"And what does ground look like when it's been overworked? When everything's been taken and nothing's been given back?"

Wren blinked. Then, slowly, they started to grin.

"You're going to tell a starving god to let go of his food supply."

"I'm going to tell him his garden is planted wrong." Oscar was pacing now, the way she paced the guild garden when a plan was taking root. "Souls aren't crops to be harvested, they're *seed*. You don't eat your seed corn—that's the first law any farmer knows, and they've broken it for centuries because they were too hungry to remember. But if you plant them—if you replant them in living soil, in the valley, in gardens that are *tended willingly*—"

"—they grow back," Wren finished. "Devotion regenerates. Prayer regenerates. The gods stop starving because they stop being owners and start being guests."

"Not guests." Oscar shook her head. "Stewards. Both directions. That's what an oath actually is, Wren—it's not a price paid, it's a *promise kept on both sides*. My grandmother's generation got it wrong. They thought protection was something you bought. But the original oath—the one buried in the archive—it says *cultivate*. I read it forty times before I understood. The valley was always supposed to be a garden the gods could visit. Not a vault they were locked out of. Not a larder they were stealing from."

Wren was quiet for a moment. "He'll refuse."

"He might."

"He's been starving for longer than your valley has existed. Starving things don't gamble."

"This one will," Oscar said, "because I'm going to make sure refusing costs him more than trusting. Come on. We're going to go renegotiate."

---

Vesper received them in the nursery itself, which Oscar understood immediately: he wanted her to see the scale of it, the hunger, the hopelessness dressed as order. Rows without end. Gods moving among them like ghosts tending graves.

She stood in the central aisle with Wren at her shoulder and made herself look at all of it—the bare patches in the golden mat, the hollow faces, the pots labeled in languages older than grief.

"You've seen it now," Vesper said. "So you understand why your offer was insult. There is no trade that saves all this. There is only management of loss."

"There's replanting."

His head turned. Slowly. Like a tree deciding to face the sun.

"Every soul in this room," Oscar said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her more than anyone, "is seed corn. You've been eating your future for four hundred years, and the future has nearly run out—I can see it in your floor, in your faces, in the way your light flickers when you think no one's watching. You can't keep them. Keeping is killing them and you with it. But planted—" she knelt, pressed her palm flat against the threadbare golden mat, felt the faint arrhythmic pulse of it, dying and trying, "—planted in living soil, in the valley above, in gardens opened to you *willingly*—devotion grows back. Prayer grows back. You don't need keepers, Vesper. You need gardeners. And I know gardeners."

The silence went on so long that the nearest row-tender stopped working to listen.

"You are proposing," Vesper said finally, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "that I release every soul in my keeping. On faith. On the word of a girl who has been in my court for six days."

"On an oath," Oscar corrected. "A real one. Sworn before your whole court at equinox. The valley opens its gates. The guild becomes stewards of an open door. Gardens planted for you, tended for you, prayed in—by choice, which is the only soil you've ever actually needed. And in exchange, every soul goes free. Including hers." She nodded at the pot he held now, close against his chest, the silver-green stem trembling between his fingers like a pulse.

"And if the valley fails? If they forget again? If the prayers thin and the gardens go untended?" His voice cracked along some ancient fault line. "I will have traded forty years of survival for a promise from people whose whole history is forgetting."

"Then you'll have traded starvation for a chance," Oscar said. "Which is more than starvation ever offered anyone."

She rose to her feet. Around them, the dying court had gone utterly still, every hollow face turned toward the aisle, toward the girl with the gardener's hands and the audacity of spring.

"Equinox is tomorrow," she said. "Swear with me, or watch your court finish dying politely. Those are my terms of surrender."

Vesper looked down at the pot in his arms—at Maren, at the stem leaning toward Oscar like a hand reaching out after forty years—and when he raised his eyes again, they were wet, and terrible, and full of the first hope Oscar had seen in this whole buried kingdom.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we swear."
