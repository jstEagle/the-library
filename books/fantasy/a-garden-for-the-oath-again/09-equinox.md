## 9. Equinox

The court dressed itself for the equinox the way a mourner dresses for a wedding.

Oscar saw it in everything as she was led down the great root-hall toward the ceremony: garlands of pale fungus strung along the vaulted ribs of stone, lanterns of trapped glowworms burning green and patient, tables laid with fruit that had never known a sun. The soul-garden's rows had been brought up in pots and arranged in terraces along the walls, hundreds of them, each one trembling faintly with the light of the person it held. They were being displayed. Inventory, counted before the year turned.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her most. She had expected to shake through this last morning, but instead there was only a strange clarity, the kind that came over her in Hollowmere on the first hard frost—when you knew the garden was done, and all that remained was to do the work well.

Wren found her at the edge of the hall, dressed in courier grey, looking like a person who had slept badly and enjoyed it.

"The clerk's credentials will get you to the floor," Wren murmured. "After that, you're on your own. I got as far as the deep archive." A pause. "It's worse than we thought. The court isn't hoarding out of greed, Oscar. It's starving. Vesper's been feeding his own essence into the walls for decades just to keep the lamps lit. Half those gods on the dais are mostly memory already."

"Then they'll want what I'm offering."

"They'll *fear* it. Fear and want look identical from the inside." Wren pressed something small into her palm: a river-spirit's coin, worn smooth, worth one favor owed by someone who no longer remembered owing it. "For luck. Or for a door. Whichever opens first."

Above them, a bell made of hollowed antler began to toll.

* * *

The ceremony floor was a circle of black glass, polished until it reflected the potted souls overhead like a sky full of slow stars. The gods of the court assembled upon their dais in ranks of diminishing brightness—the elder ones vast and dim as weather, the younger ones sharp-edged and hungry-looking, and at their center, taller than the rest, Vesper.

He wore his gardener's aspect tonight: hands stained to the elbow with loam that glittered faintly, a crown woven of pruned branches still green against all reason. When his eyes found Oscar among the clerks and brokers at the circle's rim, nothing in his face moved. But the nearest pots brightened, all together, like candles leaning toward a draft.

An old god with a voice like grinding stone called the court to order. Debts were read. Names were weighed. Souls were appraised aloud in a language Oscar had spent three weeks learning and still understood only in fragments—but she understood the arithmetic of it, because it was the arithmetic of any ledger anywhere: what is held, what is owed, what may never be released.

Then the herald spoke her stolen name, and the circle opened.

She walked to the center of the black glass alone. Her footsteps rang wrong—too loud, too human—and every eye above her was an autumn sky about to storm. She had rehearsed this speech against the wall of a root-tunnel until Wren threatened to gag her. Now it left her like water finding a crack.

"I come to settle a debt," she said, and the acoustics of the hall carried it up into the dark. "Not mine. Not my grandmother's. Yours."

Murmuring, low and mineral. On the dais, Vesper's stained hands went still.

"For three hundred years your oath-keepers have paid you in souls, and you have kept those souls the way a miser keeps coins in a flooded cellar—safe, and drowning. You are dying. I've seen it. Your walls eat your gardener. Your gods are going out one lamp at a time, and you call it custody." She turned slowly, addressing the whole arc of the dais. "I am the heir of Hollowmere, and I am here to tell you the valley remembers more than it admits. The forgetting was bought. Which means it can be sold back."

"You are a thief and a broker's clerk," said the stone-voiced god. "You hold no standing."

"I hold the oldest standing there is." She drew Maren's tokens from her bodice: the iron key, the dried sprig of rowan, the garden apron whose stitching was a map. "I hold an oath renewed each generation since the sealing. And oaths can be rewritten by whoever has the courage to stand in the circle and speak. That rule is older than your court. Even you honor it."

Silence, vast and listening. Somewhere in the terraces, a pot flared—a soul, watching, hoping.

"So here is my offer. Not surrender. Not service. A contract." Her voice did not waver now; it had passed beyond wavering into something harder and cleaner. "Open the gate at Hollowmere's tree line—not as conquerors, not as collectors, but as guests. Every garden in the valley open to you, tended willingly, planted for you. We will feed you the way gardens feed things: freely, season by season, belief by belief. Living worship from living soil, instead of dead souls in pots. In exchange—" and here she made herself say it plainly, because contracts died in euphemism— "every soul in this hall goes free. Returned to the earth above. Planted, if you like, since you understand planting. Let them bloom where they choose."

"You would unmake us," whispered a young god near the dais's edge, and its voice cracked like ice on a pond. "We are what we keep. Release the souls and there is nothing left of us but hunger."

"No," Oscar said. "You are what you're *for*. Keeping was never the purpose. Tending was. You've confused the pot with the plant for so long you've forgotten which one grows." She looked up at the terraces, at the trembling lights. "My grandmother taught me that mistake. I unlearned it in her own garden. You can too—or you can die rich, full of souls no one will ever see flower, in a hall that eats its own gardener. Those are your choices. The equinox won't wait."

* * *

They deliberated as gods deliberate—not in words but in pressures, in the groan of the mountain's bones, in the flicker of a hundred pots brightening and dimming like a tide arguing with itself. Oscar stood on the black glass and let it happen around her, and found, oddly, that she was praying. Not to any of them. Just the wordless prayer of a gardener watching weather: *grow, please, grow.*

At last the stone-voiced god turned its head. "Vesper. Keeper of souls. The contract requires the consent of custody."

Every light in the hall bent toward him.

Vesper descended the dais slowly, and the crowd of lesser gods parted the way water parts around a keel. He stopped at the circle's edge, close enough that Oscar could see the exhaustion under his beauty, the way his crown of green branches had gone brown at the tips. Three weeks she had known him. Three weeks of traded favors in the archive, of conversations conducted in the grammar of ledgers and cuttings, of a warmth she had refused to name because naming it would make it a weapon she could be forced to use.

Now she named it anyway, silently, and set it on the table beside everything else.

"You knew I'd come," she said quietly. "From the first day. You knew what I'd ask."

"I hoped," Vesper said. His voice was low enough that only she heard it. "Hoping is the one crop I never managed to kill." He looked past her, up at the terraces—at Maren's pot, third row, seventh column, glowing the color of late afternoon. "I kept her three hundred years, Oscar. Watered her. Pruned her. Told myself it was love. It was the longest cowardice of my existence. Custody is what cowards call love when they're afraid to be refused."

"And trust?" she asked. "What do you call that?"

He smiled then, tired and true. "A cutting taken without certainty of root."

He stepped into the circle. The black glass sang under his feet.

"I am Vesper, keeper of the deep garden," he said, and his voice filled the whole root-hall, and every pot blazed. "I release custody. All of it. Every soul, every debt of flesh, every name held in surety—including the name of Maren Whitlock, including the seal upon the valley, including my own claim on the woman who stands before you." He knelt—not to the dais, but to Oscar, hands still earth-stained, head bowed. "I swear to the new oath: guesthood, not ownership. Gardens, not vaults. And if the valley keeps us alive willingly, then we will spend what life we're given keeping it in return. Witness me."

The stone-voiced god was silent a long moment. Then, around the dais, one by one, the elder gods bowed their heads—some gravely, some furiously, one with what looked unmistakably like relief—and the sound that went through the hall was not cheering but something older: the sound of a lock turning after centuries.

The pots began to open.

It was not violent. That was the miracle of it. Each vessel split along a seam no one had known was there, and the souls rose out of them like heat-shimmer, like pollen, like the breath off a warm field at dusk—hundreds of lights lifting through the vaulted dark, streaming upward through the root-tunnels toward the world above. Oscar watched Maren's light rise last, lingering a moment at her shoulder, warm as a kitchen in winter, and then it too went up and away, home.

The hall dimmed as the souls departed. The lanterns guttered. For one terrible heartbeat the court seemed about to go out entirely—

—and then Vesper stood, and drew a long breath, and light came back into him. Not hoarded light. Borrowed. Given. The lesser gods kindled around him like a hedge coming into blossom, fed on nothing but the fact of the oath, the sheer renewable wildness of being *wanted* somewhere.

Wren appeared at her elbow, grinning like a thief at an unlocked window. "Told you fear and want look identical."

"You told me they'd fear it."

"They did. Right up until they wanted it more." Wren glanced at Vesper, then at Oscar, and something knowing crossed the courier's face. "Equinox is turning. If you mean to be above ground when the gate opens, we should climb."

Oscar took one step toward the tunnels, then stopped, and turned back, and did the thing she had sworn she wouldn't do in front of the assembled court: she took Vesper's earth-stained hand in both of hers.

"Spring planting starts early in Hollowmere," she said. "There's a bed behind the guildhall that's been fallow three hundred years. I intend to break ground myself."

His fingers closed around hers, careful as a man handling a seedling. "I know the way to the tree line," he said. "I've walked it in dreams since before your grandmother was born."

Above them, through miles of stone and root, the first soul-light broke the surface of the world—and far away, in a valley that had forgotten how to hope, the crops came up out of season, blooming in the snow.
