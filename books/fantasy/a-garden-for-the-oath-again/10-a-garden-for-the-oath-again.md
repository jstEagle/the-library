## 10. A Garden for the Oath Again

The frost came early that year, and Oscar let it.

She stood in the guild garden at first light, breath silvering the air, and watched the cold lay its thin glass over the beds she had spent a season learning to open instead of keep. Once, this garden had been a locked thing—rows pruned to obedience, soil turned on schedule, every plant a small performance of order for an order that had forgotten why it performed. Now half the beds ran wild with volunteer borage and self-seeded poppies, and the gate in the low stone wall stood unlatched from dawn to dusk, because that was the whole of the new oath: *the gate stays open.*

It had taken Hollowmere most of the autumn to stop flinching at it.

Oscar understood the flinching. She had been raised inside it—a whole valley trained to believe that safety was a held breath, that magic was a debt collector at the door, that the tree line was a wall and not a wound. For three hundred years the guild had kept their oaths like coins sewn into a hem: never counted, never spent, never questioned. And beneath the mountain, the gods had starved politely in the dark, sustained by one hoarded soul per generation, growing stranger and thinner and more desperate with each renewal of the bargain her grandmother had signed in her own blood and grief.

Now the souls were gone home. All of them. Potted out of Vesper's terraced dark and replanted in living soil—in graveyards and orchards and one remarkable widow's herb spiral—and the gods who had fed on custody were learning, slowly, painfully, to feed on welcome instead.

It was not going perfectly. That was the point of it. An oath that could not fail was just another kind of cage.

"Whitlock." Old Sorrel's voice cracked across the garden like a stick over kindling. The guildmaster stood at the gate—which was to say, he stood *in* the gate, because the gate was always open now, and people kept forgetting where its edges were. "There's a woman at the tree line asking after you. Says she's come about the spring planting."

Oscar did not turn around right away. She finished tying off the row of garlic she'd been braiding, laid it in the basket, wiped her hands on her apron—the apron, Maren's apron, the map stitched into its lining long since unpicked and framed in the guild hall like the relic it was. Only then did she look up.

"She can come to the gate," Oscar said. "That's rather the entire arrangement."

Sorrel's mouth worked. He was eighty if he was a day, and he had spent his whole life believing the tree line would kill anything that crossed it, and the last four months watching it do no such thing. "She says," he managed, "that she can't."

*Ah,* thought Oscar, and something in her chest went tight and warm and terrible all at once. "Then I'll go to her."

She walked up through the village with the basket on her arm, past the shrine where the neighbor's prayer-crops still bloomed out of season—marigolds in November, fat and shameless, tended now by children who had never known to be afraid of them. Past the guild hall, where the original oath hung unrolled beside the new one, the old words struck through in red ink by Maren's own hand: *purchased* crossed out, *given* written above it. Past the well, where someone had left an offering of bread and honey on the rim—not for keeping away, but for calling close.

The valley had not stopped being afraid. It had simply started choosing what to do with the fear, and some mornings that choice looked like bread on a well, and some mornings it looked like Sorrel standing in a gate he couldn't bring himself to leave open behind him. Both counted. Oscar had learned that below the mountain, in the rows of potted souls: that a thing could be tended in terror or tended in love, and the tending looked identical from outside, and everything from within.

The tree line rose ahead of her, black pines shouldering the sky, and there—at the exact margin where Hollowmere's silence ended and the world's hum began—stood Vesper.

He had not changed, which was somehow more unsettling than if he had. Still the gardener's hands, earth-dark to the wrist. Still the coat that might have been bark or might have been velvet. But he stood differently than he had in the court below: no longer among his rows, no longer keeper of anything. He stood at the boundary the way a man stands at a shore, and he did not cross it.

"You're supposed to send word through Wren," Oscar said, when she was near enough. "Those are the terms. You wrote them yourself."

"I did." His voice was the same as she remembered—soil and smoke and something older underneath. "Wren is delivering a soul to a river-spirit two valleys east. A late account. I didn't want to wait."

"It's been eleven days since the last visit."

"I counted."

Something in the flat honesty of it undid her a little. Below the mountain, Vesper had been magnificent and terrible and grief-shaped, a god who had loved Maren Whitlock and expressed it by imprisoning her soul in a clay pot for sixty years, because custody was the only grammar of devotion the court had ever taught him. He was learning a new grammar. It went slowly. Some days it went backwards. Eleven days ago he had stood at this same line and told her, in front of half the guild, that trust felt like drowning, and then he had let Maren's soul go anyway, and had not drowned, and clearly did not know what to do with that.

"The spring planting," Oscar said. "You sent a message about the spring planting."

"I wanted a reason." He looked past her, down the valley, at the smoke of the village, the green stain of the garden even in winter. "I don't have reasons anymore. Custody was my reason. Now I have—" his hands opened, empty, gardener's hands with nothing in them, "—this. Standing places. Counting days."

"So you invented a lie about seeds."

"I invented a reason to see the woman who unmade my court." Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would learn to live. "I'm told that's called gratitude. It's very uncomfortable. I recommend it to no one."

Oscar laughed despite herself, and the sound went out past the tree line into the humming world, and came back changed, as sounds did here. She set the basket down between them, exactly on the boundary, so that it sat half in silence and half in song.

"Borage seed," she said. "And winter onions, and the last of the poppies. If you're going to invent reasons, invent useful ones. There's a stretch of ground on your side of the line—old burn scar, nothing grows there but ash and stubbornness. Plant these. Tend them. Not as a keeper. As a neighbor."

Vesper looked at the basket for a long moment. "A garden," he said slowly, "on the far side. Where I can't be certain of the outcome. Where things will die, and I won't be able to prune them back into compliance."

"Yes."

"And if they die?"

"Then you plant again in spring. That's the whole oath, Vesper. That's all any of it ever was." She stepped back, leaving the basket straddling the line, belonging to neither world and both. "Maren will be down at midday. She likes to walk to the tree line and shout insults at you until you laugh. You should know she's planning to teach you about compost, and she will not be argued with."

His expression did something complicated and young. "Sixty years I kept her soul," he said quietly, "and she never once spoke to me as though I were real. Now she shouts at me about nitrogen."

"That's what being real is. Congratulations. It gets worse."

He took the basket. Their fingers did not touch—he was careful about that, scrupulously, in a way that told her he thought about it constantly—but the basket passed cleanly over the line, and the seeds inside it passed from silence into song without a single one of them noticing the difference.

Oscar walked back down toward the village with the cold bright on her face, and did not look back, because looking back was a kind of keeping, and she was done keeping. Behind her she heard the small sounds of a god kneeling in ash with a handful of borage seed, learning the only prayer that had ever mattered: *grow, and I will not control how.*

At the gate, Sorrel was waiting, pretending to inspect the latch.

"Well?" he demanded.

"He's planting," Oscar said, and handed him the garlic braid. "We'll need to expand the south beds by equinox. Word's spreading—hedge-witches, river-folk, two of the mountain gods' lesser cousins. They all want gardens. They all want in."

Sorrel stared at her. "In. To Hollowmere. The valley that forgets."

"No," said Oscar, and found, saying it, that she had finally stopped hearing her grandmother's fear underneath the words and only her grandmother's hope. "The valley that remembers. There's a difference. It took us three hundred years and a stolen identity and one extremely uncomfortable negotiation to find it, but it's there." She smiled, and it felt like her own name, chosen. "Get the ledger. We're going to need a bigger oath."

* * *

Maren Whitlock had been dead for sixty years, in the way that mattered, and had come back from it in the way that also mattered, and she had opinions about both.

Her soul had been returned to her in September, pried loose from its pot by her granddaughter's audacity and a god's surrender, and the first thing Maren had done upon waking in her own body—older, frailer, honest about both—was walk to the guild archive and strike through the old oath in red ink. The second thing she had done was weep for six hours. The third thing she had done was eat an entire loaf of bread, because being alive, it turned out, was mostly appetite, and she had sixty years of appetite banked.

Now it was nearly winter, and she knelt in the cold soil of the south bed with her granddaughter, and her knees complained, and she let them, loudly, because complaint was a luxury of the embodied and she intended to spend hers extravagantly.

"You're planting them too deep," Oscar said.

"I am planting them *properly*. You plant them like you're apologizing to them."

"I am a little. They died last time."

"Everything dies last time. That's what makes the next time mean something." Maren pressed the bulb into the dark earth and patted it closed with more force than tenderness required. Sixty years in a pot had given her strong feelings about soil. Real soil, living soil, soil that changed and rotted and renewed—that was the whole resurrection, as far as she was concerned. Gods could keep their terraces. Give her dirt that stank of worm and rain.

Below the mountain, in the years of her captivity, she had dreamed of this: kneeling in a garden with someone who shared her blood, arguing about planting depth. She had assumed the dream was mercy. It had never once occurred to her that it was a plan, waiting for a granddaughter stubborn enough to execute it.

"Vesper was at the line again this morning," Oscar said, carefully casual, in the way of the young, which is to say, not casual at all.

"Mm. Did he bring his invented reason?"

"A message about the spring planting."

Maren snorted. "The man courted me for forty years with invented reasons. Weather reports. Soil surveys. Once, an entirely fictional blight on the western pines that required my immediate consultation." Her chest ached, saying it—not the old wound, or not only; something newer, tenderer, the bruise of loving a thing that had caged her and knowing the cage had been built by a creature more frightened than cruel. "He's better at it now. The reasons. Last week he claimed the river-spirit needed advice on erosion. The river-spirit has never needed advice on anything. She's older than rivers."

"He's trying."

"He's *learning*, which is harder." Maren sat back on her heels and surveyed the bed—the neat rows, the wild edges, the gate hanging open against the grey sky. When she was young, she had crossed the tree line carrying her heart like contraband, and come home having traded it for a valley's peace, and had called the trade wisdom for sixty years because the alternative was calling it cowardice, and she had not had the strength. Her granddaughter had walked the same road and refused the same trade, and had come home with everything: the soul, the god, the name rewritten, the gate thrown wide.

That was inheritance done properly, Maren thought. Not the passing down of debts. The passing down of courage, improved upon.

"Oscar." She took her granddaughter's hand, earth-stained both of them, indistinguishable. "When I'm gone—and I intend to be gone slowly, obnoxiously, decades from now, complaining the entire way—but when I'm gone, the guild will ask you to write the next oath. Don't write it alone. Don't write it in ink that can't be struck through. And whatever terms you set—" she squeezed the young hand hard, "—leave room in them for joy. The old ones left none. That was the rot in us. Not the magic. The joylessness."

Oscar's eyes were bright. "Room for joy," she repeated. "In the oath itself."

"In the oath itself. Make it a clause. Make it load-bearing." Maren released her hand and reached for the next bulb, and her knees shouted, and she ignored them gloriously. "Now hand me the trowel and stop looking at me like that. We've forty bulbs to get in before the ground locks, and I intend to be here for every one of them—planting, arguing, and growing honestly old in a valley that finally remembers what it's for."

Beyond the wall, through the open gate, the wind came down off the mountain carrying the faint green smell of a burn scar learning, seed by stubborn seed, to bloom.
