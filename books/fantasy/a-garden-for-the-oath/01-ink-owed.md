## 1. Ink Owed

The tithe ledgers of Thornfell Temple ran to eleven thousand pages, and Mirabel Vane had personally copied four thousand of them.

She knew this because she kept count. Not officially — officially she was simply *the bond-scribe*, a line item in the temple's own accounting, listed under "labor received" the same way the tallow and the turnips were listed. But privately, in the back pages of her third-worst quill practice book, she tallied everything: pages copied (4,117), candles burned past curfew (a number the abbot would faint over), days remaining on her family's debt (one thousand nine hundred and six, give or take leap-days), and — most treasured of all — roads.

Roads did not belong in a ledger. That was rather the point of them.

"Mirabel." Brother Anselm's voice drifted over the scriptorium partitions like damp weather. "You've written 'sixty bushels barley' twice."

"I know," Mirabel said, not looking up. "It was sixty bushels twice. The Hollowbrook farm sent two shipments."

"They sent one shipment and a letter of complaint."

Mirabel looked down at her page. She had indeed written sixty bushels twice, in two different hands, because the first time she'd been listening to Anselm complain about the quality of the new ink and the second time she'd been thinking about the pass at Greywether Saddle, which — according to a drover who'd stopped at the temple gate last autumn with a lame mule and a talkative tongue — had been cleared of rockfall sometime around harvest, meaning the old road through it was open again, meaning the toll road below was pointless, meaning anyone with sense and no scruples could move goods through the mountains without paying the crown's men a copper.

She crossed out the second *sixty* with a small, precise stroke. "One shipment," she said. "And a complaint. I'll amend it."

"You're always somewhere else," Anselm said, but without much heat. He was seventy, half-deaf, and had long ago decided that Mirabel's wandering attention was a form of weather — unpleasant, unfixable, to be endured. He shuffled off toward the warming pan, muttering about ink again.

Mirabel waited until his footsteps faded. Then, in the margin beside the amended entry, where a correction blot gave her a convenient smudge of empty space, she drew a tiny careful line. The Greywether pass. A dotted thread running east from the shepherd tracks, skirting the shrine at Cold Ford, dropping into the valley behind the king's tax station at Marrow Bridge.

There. Another secret stitched into another discarded page.

Here was the beauty of the thing, and Mirabel had spent many candle-hours appreciating it: nobody read the margins. The tithe ledgers were copied once for the temple archive and once for the crown's assessors, and then the working copies — the ones with blots and corrections and cramped marginalia — were bundled by the year and stored in the cellar until mold or mice or simple bureaucratic indifference took them. Eleven years of Mirabel Vane's secret cartography, sitting in the dark under three hundred years of barley arithmetic. If the temple ever burned, the smugglers' roads of the whole eastern range would go up in smoke and no one would even know what they'd lost.

Sometimes she imagined that fire. Not with any real intent — she was a scribe; arson was above her pay grade, which was nothing — but as a kind of daydream. The cellar catching, the ledgers burning, and with them every record of what the Vane family owed. Debt was only ink, when you got down to it. Her father had borrowed against the mill in a bad winter, then died before he could dig out, and the temple had done what temples do: converted grief into obligation, obligation into paper, paper into a girl at a desk. Nine hundred words an hour, six hours a day, and freedom scheduled for roughly the time her eyes went bad enough to make her useless anyway.

She flexed her right hand — the ache was starting earlier these years — and reached for the next folio.

That was when the screaming started.

Not nearby. Down, and out — from the pilgrims' court beyond the cloister wall, where the poor and the desperate huddled under the eaves waiting for alms or a blessing or, more often, neither. Mirabel set down her pen. Anselm didn't look up; screaming in the pilgrims' court was also a kind of weather. But this scream had a different shape. It wasn't pain, exactly. It was the sound someone makes when they're trying to say something very important and their body is finishing without them.

Then the voice came.

Mirabel would spend a great deal of the rest of her life failing to describe that voice, so perhaps it's kindest to begin with the fact that it came from everywhere and nowhere at all — from the courtyard, yes, but also from the rafters, from the stone, from the wet ink still drying on her page. It was not loud. That was the worst part. It was quiet, the way a mountain is quiet, the way deep water is quiet. It said:

***He is heard. The vow is witnessed. Let it be kept.***

Every quill in the scriptorium lifted off its page at once.

Mirabel watched hers rise — watched it hover, dripping, a full handspan above the folio — and felt something in her chest come loose that she hadn't known was fastened. The ink in the well stirred. The letters on Anselm's page shivered and rearranged themselves, gently, like birds settling on a wire, into words that were not about barley.

Then it stopped. The quills dropped. Somewhere a novice began to cry, and somewhere else Brother Anselm said, in a small voice entirely unlike damp weather, "Oh dear."

* * *

By evening the temple elders had convened in the abbot's hall, and by night they had achieved that particular state of panic unique to old men in positions of authority, which is to say they had agreed to pretend nothing had happened while taking elaborate precautions against its recurrence.

Mirabel knew all this because she was the one sent to fetch things. This was the other use for a bond-scribe: errands. She carried wine to the hall, took away the wine untouched, fetched the good candles, fetched the locked box of temple records, and stood in corners being furniture while seven elders argued in the strained whispers of men who have just heard a god speak and are mostly concerned about how it reflects on the quarterly reports.

"The pilgrim is dead," Abbot Coram was saying. He was a broad, gray man with the permanent expression of someone smelling something faintly off. "Died within the hour. Fever of the lungs. He'd been raving for a day — the brothers heard him praying, calling on—" he paused, and his jaw worked as though the word itself were contraband— "*Vethmar.*"

The name landed in the room like a dropped plate.

"That name isn't spoken," said Elder Hask, who was oldest, richest, and nearest the door.

"It's spoken now," Coram said grimly. "Spoken by a dying drover in our own court, and answered. Audibly. In front of nine witnesses and Brother Pavin's dog, who has not stopped howling."

"Old god," muttered Hask. "Oaths and debts and such. The kind that used to take payment in blood before the Crown's church civilized us. There's a reason we don't keep his altar anymore."

"There's a reason we don't *keep* him," Coram corrected. "Whether he keeps us is apparently another matter." He rubbed his face. "The question is what we tell the diocese."

"We tell them nothing," said Elder Marta, the only woman in the room, and the only one whose fear sounded like thought instead of noise. "We tell them a sick man babbled. We burn his belongings. We say nothing of voices."

"And if it happens again?"

Silence. Seven elders contemplating the possibility of it happening again, which was, Mirabel thought from her corner, rather like seven fish contemplating the possibility of water. Things that old did not answer prayers once and go home. Things that old woke up hungry.

In the corner, holding the untouched wine, Mirabel Vane discovered that her hands were shaking — and that beneath the shaking, underneath the dread like sediment under floodwater, there was something else entirely.

Wonder. Raw, undiluted, terrifying wonder.

For eleven years the world had been a ledger: rows, columns, sums owed, sums paid, a life measured in bushels. And now, in one afternoon, the margins had spoken. The blank space at the edge of everything had opened its mouth and said *the vow is witnessed*, and some part of Mirabel that she'd buried under four thousand pages of arithmetic sat up and howled like Brother Pavin's dog.

A god had listened to a dying man. A god had *answered*. Nobody had answered anything in Thornfell for a hundred years except with a bill.

She wanted — she realized, with the strange clarity of the sleepless — she wanted to know everything. What vow the drover had made. What words. Whether the god had taken anything in return. Whether he was still here, in the stones, in the ink, listening. Whether—

"Girl." Abbot Coram's voice snapped across the room. "You may go. And Mirabel—" his eyes narrowed slightly, the way they did when he suspected her of thinking, "—you heard nothing tonight."

"Nothing, Abbot," Mirabel said, which was true, since officially she was furniture.

She bowed and left, carrying the wine, walking at the correct unhurried pace down the corridor, counting doors until she was out of earshot.

Then she ran.

* * *

The scriptorium at midnight was cold enough to make the ink sluggish, so Mirabel worked close to the brazier with her cloak around her shoulders like a tent, and for once she wasn't drawing roads.

She was writing down everything she remembered of the voice, word for word, in her smallest hand, on a scrap of the cheapest paper — the kind destined for the cellar bundles, the kind no one reads. *He is heard. The vow is witnessed. Let it be kept.* Three sentences. She'd turned them over perhaps four hundred times since sundown and each turning showed a new facet. *Let it be kept* — not *let it be forgiven*. Not mercy, then. Oaths. The old god of oaths and debts, Elder Hask had called him, the kind that took payment in blood. Well. Mirabel knew a little something about debts. She'd been paying one since she was thirteen years old, in ink instead of blood, and no god had ever once witnessed it.

She blew on the scrap, folded it small, and tucked it into the hidden seam of her sleeve — the same seam that held the folded map of every smuggler road in the eastern range, because apparently her entire life fit in one sleeve now.

That was when she noticed the light.

It came from the far end of the scriptorium, from the copying desks of the day novices, and it was wrong: too steady for a candle, too white for a lamp. Mirabel should have gone back to bed. Every instinct bred into her by eleven years of institutional living screamed *go back to bed*. Instead she crept down the row of desks with her heart going like a drover's mule on a downhill stretch.

The light was coming from a page.

One of the working copies — today's tithe ledger, the very one with her amended barley entry — lay open on young Brother Tam's desk, and every word on the page was glowing. Not burning. Glowing, softly, the way snow holds the last of the daylight, and as she watched, the letters moved. They gathered themselves up out of the columns and rows, streaming together like iron filings finding a lodestone, reforming along the margin — her margin, the one with the little dotted line of the Greywether pass — into a single line of fresh, shining script:

***You count well, little herald.***

Mirabel did not scream, which she counted afterward among her life's modest achievements. She did drop the wine cup, which shattered, and she did stand absolutely frozen while the ink-letters shimmered and settled and became, unmistakably, a message addressed to her.

"I'm not—" she whispered, and stopped, because arguing with a page seemed like a bad opening move. She tried again. "I'm nobody. I'm a debt."

The ink rippled. Letters rose from deeper in the ledger — from years of ledgers, she realized dimly, from every page she had ever touched, called up like faces to a window — and arranged themselves with what she could only describe as patience:

***Eleven years you have drawn me roads in my margins. Did you think I would not notice?***

"My margins," Mirabel said faintly.

***All margins are mine. All vows, all debts, all promises written or sworn or merely meant. I am Vethmar. I keep accounts.*** A pause, and then, with something almost dry in the arrangement of the letters: ***You will find we have that in common.***

Somewhere in the temple a bell tolled the hour, and Mirabel became aware of several things at once: that the brazier had gone out; that her hands had stopped shaking; that she was not afraid, or rather that the fear was there but sitting underneath the wonder like a stone under a spring, shaping the current without stopping it.

"Why me?" she asked. "I'm bound. Bond-scribe. My family owes you — owes *them* — everything. I copy tithes. That's all I am."

The page glowed brighter. New letters, rising slow, each one placed with the care of a man setting a cornerstone:

***You are the one who maps ways out.***

Mirabel stared at the little dotted line of the Greywether pass, her secret, her treasure, her one rebellion in eleven years of obedient ink — and understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that it had never been a rebellion at all. It had been a summons. All those roads, drawn in the dark margins of a forgotten ledger, had been reaching toward something that was now reaching back.

The letters faded. The page went ordinary. The scriptorium was cold and dark and smelled of wax and mouse, and Mirabel Vane stood alone in it holding a broken wine cup, wearing a debt like a collar and a god's attention like a crown.

From the pilgrims' court below, faint through the frost-glazed window, came the sound of Brother Pavin's dog, howling at the moonless sky.

Mirabel set down the cup, took the scrap from her sleeve, and added one line beneath the three she'd copied from memory — her own hand, plain ink, no glow at all:

*Day 4,118. Something has noticed the margins.*

Then she blew out the last ember of light and went to find out what a herald owed.
