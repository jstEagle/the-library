## 2. The God Who Answered

The trouble with miracles, Mirabel decided on the third morning, was that they were terribly inconvenient about timing.

She had spent the night after the pilgrim died lying awake in the narrow cot she shared with two other debt-bound scribes, listening to Sister Osla snore and staring at the ceiling beams and thinking, very reasonably, that she had imagined it. Grief did strange things. Hunger did stranger ones. She had skipped supper to finish copying the tithe rolls for the barley harvest, and everyone knew that a fasting mind made its own music.

Except that the dying man's prayer had been *answered*. Not with a feeling, not with a warmth in the chest such as the temple taught pilgrims to expect from the Nine Faces of the Concord — but with a voice. A voice like a seal being broken, like a page turning in an empty room, like someone very old and very patient saying *yes* after centuries of silence.

And now, on the third morning, every vow in the Thornfell temple was writing itself.

Mirabel discovered this because it was her job to discover things written where they should not be. She came down before dawn, as always, to prepare the copying room: trimming wicks, mixing ink, laying out the day's work on the long sloped desks. The vow-registry occupied the eastern wall — forty years of contracts, oaths of service, marriage bonds, debt-bonds, all copied fair into the great ledger by generations of scribes. It was the first thing she dusted every morning, and the last thing she looked at every night, because her own name was in it. *Mirabel Vane, bound to the Temple of the Concord at Thornfell until such time as the debt of her father, Aldous Vane, is discharged in full.* Eleven years served. Fourteen remaining.

The registry was open. She had left it closed. And across the page, in ink so fresh it shone, something was being written.

Not by her. The room was empty. The pens sat in their rack. The inkpot was sealed.

Mirabel stood very still, the way one does when a wolf crosses the road ahead, and read:

*I HAVE COUNTED THE VOWS IN THIS HOUSE. THERE ARE FOUR THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND TWELVE. THREE THOUSAND NINE HUNDRED ARE KEPT BADLY. TWO HUNDRED ARE KEPT WELL. TWELVE WERE NEVER MEANT TO BE KEPT AT ALL.*

"Well," said Mirabel aloud, to no one, because speaking made things ordinary. "That's not ominous at all."

The pen moved. No hand held it. Ink crawled up out of the pot, threaded through the air like a dark thread pulled through cloth, and settled onto the page letter by letter:

*YOU READ ME.*

It was not a question. Mirabel's mouth had gone dry. "I — yes? I mean. Anyone could—"

*NO ONE ELSE CAN. THE ABBOT STOOD WHERE YOU STAND FOR AN HOUR BEFORE DAWN. HE SAW BLANK PAPER AND CALLED IT A HAUNTING AND ORDERED THE PAGE BURNED. I MOVED. YOU ARE THE FIRST TO FIND ME SINCE.*

She thought of Abbot Corvelin, who was a kind man in the way that tired men are kind — generously, briefly, when it cost nothing — and who was also, above all things, a man terrified of disorder. He kept the temple's accounts balanced to the copper. He would not balance against this.

"Why me?" Mirabel asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I'm nobody. I'm a debt-bond. I copy tithes."

*SO DID I, ONCE,* wrote the god, which was not an answer, or was the only answer there was. *BRING NOTHING. TELL NO ONE YET. COME BACK WHEN THE LAMP IS TRIMMED AND THE OTHERS SLEEP. I HAVE BEEN PATIENT FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS. I CAN BE PATIENT UNTIL MIDNIGHT. BUT NOT MUCH LONGER THAN THAT.*

Then the ink drained back into the pot like water finding its level, and the page was blank, and downstairs the bell rang for prime, and Mirabel Vane went to sing the morning offices with hands that would not stop shaking, and a smile she could not put down no matter how hard she pressed her lips together.

* * *

Telling no one, it turned out, was difficult when your face told everyone.

"You look like you've found money in the road," said Brother Tammas at breakfast, squinting at her over his porridge. "Or like you're about to be sick."

"Both," said Mirabel. "It's the barley rolls."

"It's never the barley rolls," said Tammas, who had known her since she was nine years old and newly indentured, and who had taught her the secret of keeping her letters even when her wrist ached: *write for the reader who comes after, little bird, not for the abbot watching over your shoulder.* He was sixty now, half-deaf, and the closest thing to a friend the debt-bond rules allowed her. "You've been smiling at the ink pots all morning. In eleven years I have never once seen you smile at an ink pot. I've seen you threaten them. I've seen you negotiate with them. Never smile."

"Perhaps I've turned over a new leaf."

"Perhaps you've gone mad." He said it comfortably, the way old men say such things, and went back to his porridge.

Madness would have been simpler. Madness could be confessed, treated, endured. What Mirabel had instead was a full day of copying — the wool tithe from the high farms, four pages of marriage settlements, a complaint about a neighbor's goats — while the words from the registry turned over and over behind her eyes. *I have been patient for three hundred years.* Three hundred years ago the Concord had been built, the Nine Faces raised up, the old gods of the mountains set aside like winter clothes in spring. Everyone knew the story: the old gods had been wild, demanding, hungry for blood and oath alike, and the Concord's gentle faces had freed people from all that. Everyone knew it because the Concord's temples taught it, and the temples paid the teachers, and that was how stories stayed tidy.

But the dying pilgrim hadn't prayed to the Nine. He had prayed to the god of oaths — the Old Keeper, the mountain folk still called him, though they called him quietly, the way you speak of a relative in prison — and the god of oaths had answered, and now he was writing in the registers, and he had chosen *her*, and she could not tell a single living soul why her hands were shaking.

She got through the day. She was good at getting through days; eleven years of practice. But when the evening bell rang and the brothers and sisters filed down to supper, Mirabel lingered in the copying room, trimmed the lamps low, and waited for the footsteps to fade.

Then she opened the registry, and the ink rose to meet her like a dog rising to greet its person.

*GOOD. YOU CAME. MOST DO NOT COME BACK AFTER THE FIRST NIGHT.*

"I nearly didn't." She sat down on the copying stool, then thought better of it and stood, then thought better of that too and sat again, feeling foolish. "I want you to know that I think this is a haunting, or a trick, or I've finally cracked from copying goat complaints, and any sensible person would run straight to the abbot."

*AND YET YOU ARE HERE.*

"And yet I'm here." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Say what you came to say."

For a moment the page was still. Then the ink gathered, slower than before, deliberate, the way a speaker gathers breath before the thing that actually matters.

*MIRABEL VANE. DAUGHTER OF ALDOUS. SCRIBE OF THORNFELL. KEEPER OF ROADS THAT ARE NOT ON ANY MAP. I HAVE WATCHED YOUR HANDS FOR ELEVEN YEARS.*

Her stomach dropped. "My — the margins. You've seen the margins."

*I HAVE SEEN EVERYTHING WRITTEN IN THIS HOUSE. THE TITHES. THE CONTRACTS. THE LIES TOLD IN CONTRACT LANGUAGE SO THE TRUTH WILL HOLD STILL LONG ENOUGH TO BE SIGNED. AND IN THE MARGINS OF DISCARDED PAGES, BURNED OR BURIED OR SOLD FOR WASTE PAPER — MOUNTAIN PATHS. SHEPHERD CUTS. SMUGGLER ROADS DRAWN FROM THE TALK OF PEDDLERS AND PILGRIMS, CROSS-CHECKED AGAINST THE RUTS IN THE FROST. YOU HAVE MADE YOURSELF A MAP OF EVERY WAY UP AND DOWN THESE MOUNTAINS THAT THE TEMPLE DOES NOT KNOW EXISTS.*

"They're just doodles," said Mirabel, who had never once thought of them as doodles.

*THEY ARE THE ONLY HONEST DOCUMENTS IN THIS BUILDING,* said the god, and Mirabel laughed — a startled, unguarded sound, swallowed immediately, glancing at the door. *THAT IS WHY I CHOSE YOU. NOT PIETY. PIETY I HAVE IN ABUNDANCE, AND IT HAS NEVER ONCE STOPPED A BROKEN VOW. I NEED SOMEONE WHO MAPS WHAT POWER PREFERS NOT TO SEE.*

"And what do you need seen?"

The lamp flames leaned, all together, though there was no draft. The ink rose in a rush now, faster than she could comfortably read, and she had to lean close, and the letters seemed almost eager:

*A KING WEARS A CROWN IN THE CAPITAL. ALDRIC, SECOND OF HIS NAME. TEN YEARS AGO HE WAS CROWNED WITH A VOW — TO PROTECT, TO SERVE, TO LOVE HIS PEOPLE ABOVE ALL THINGS. THE VOW WAS TRUE. THE CROWN WAS NOT. SOMETHING OLD AND HOLLOW RIDES UNDER THE GOLD. IT HAS BEEN EATING HIM FROM THE INSIDE, ONE KEPT PROMISE AT A TIME, AND IT RULES THROUGH HIS GENTLE EMPTY FACE, AND THE PEOPLE LOVE HIM FOR KINDNESSES THAT ARE NO LONGER HIS.*

"That can't be true," Mirabel whispered. "The king is beloved. There are festivals. Songs."

*THERE ARE ALWAYS SONGS. LISTEN. THE SPIRIT FINISHES ITS DEVOURING WHEN THE MOON COMPLETES HER THIRTEENTH TURN — A TURN SHE SHOULD NOT MAKE, A TURN THE SKY HAS BEGUN TO PERMIT AGAIN. WHEN IT IS DONE, THE KING IS GONE ENTIRELY, AND THE THING IN THE CROWN WILL NOT NEED TO PRETEND ANYMORE. I CANNOT REACH HIM. I AM A GOD OF VOWS, AND HIS VOW WAS SHATTERED INTO THREE PIECES AND SCATTERED IN THE HIGH PLACES. PIECES CAN BE FOUND. PIECES CAN BE SPOKEN. BUT THEY MUST BE SPOKEN BY A HERALD — ONE WHO CARRIES MY WORD, WHOSE FEET ARE SENT, WHOSE MOUTH IS NOT HER OWN ON THE DAY IT MATTERS.*

The lamp flame bent toward her, gently, like a hand extended.

*MIRABEL VANE. WILL YOU BE MY HERALD?*

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the supper bell's echo had long faded. Mirabel became aware that she was gripping the edge of the desk hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that her heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing, and that part of her — the part that had drawn smuggler roads in margins for eleven years, the part that had watched the horizon over the sheep fields every single evening and felt it pull like a hook behind her ribs — that part was already saying yes, was already halfway down the mountain.

And another part, quieter and older and worn smooth by fourteen more years of debt, said: *you are bound. You cannot leave. Your father's debt does not care what gods ask you. If you go, they will come for Sefa.*

"Sefa," she said aloud. "My sister. She's at the border temple at Grayford. If I break bond—"

*I KNOW WHAT BREAKING BOND COSTS,* wrote the god, and for the first time the letters slowed, grew heavy, almost gentle. *I AM THE GOD OF OATHS. I KNOW BETTER THAN ANYONE WHAT THEY COST, AND WHAT THEY ARE WORTH, AND HOW RARELY THOSE TWO THINGS MATCH. I DO NOT ASK LIGHTLY. I ASK BECAUSE IF NO ONE GOES, THE DEVOURING FINISHES, AND EVERY VOW IN THIS LAND BECOMES A JOKE TOLD BY A CORPSE IN A CROWN. ANSWER WHEN YOU ARE READY. BUT KNOW THAT THE MOON DOES NOT WAIT FOR READY.*

The ink sank away. The lamps steadied. Mirabel sat alone in the copying room for a long time, listening to her own breathing, looking at the blank page that was not blank, that had never been blank, that had been waiting three hundred years for someone to read it.

* * *

She lasted four days.

Four days of pretending. Four days of copying tithes with a god's words folded up inside her chest like a letter she couldn't open in public. Four days of catching herself staring north through the scriptorium window, toward the capital, toward a king she'd never seen, wearing a crown that ate. Four days of the vision returning whenever she closed her eyes: not words this time but a picture, given to her on the second night without asking — a throne room drowned in candlelight, and on the throne a tall man with a kind, vacant face, and above his brow a circlet of gold that moved. That *moved*, slowly, the way a slow worm moves under bark. And around the man's head, faint as heat-shimmer, wisps of something lifting off him and flowing up into the metal — color, warmth, memory, self — going in and never coming out. She had woken from that vision with tears on her face and no memory of crying, weeping for a stranger, and had lain in the dark thinking: *this is what it is to be herald. You grieve for people before you've met them.*

On the fourth day, Abbot Corvelin summoned her.

She knew before she reached his study. Word travels in small places, and Brother Tammas had reported her smiles, and Sister Osla had reported her sleeplessness, and the under-cellarer had reported — with great relish — that she'd been seen talking to the vow-registry. Alone. At midnight.

Corvelin's study smelled of lamp oil and dried lavender. He was a round, soft-spoken man with ink-stained fingers and the permanent expression of someone doing arithmetic he didn't like. He gestured her to the chair across from his desk and looked at her for a long moment over his folded hands.

"Mirabel." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Eleven years you've served here. Good service. Quiet service. Your father's debt is more than half discharged, and I had begun to think — well. I had hopes of recommending your manumission within the decade."

"Thank you, Abbot."

"So explain to me," he said, still gently, still terribly, "why the under-cellarer saw you holding conversation with an empty registry at midnight. Why you smile at ink pots. Why you stand at windows looking north like a woman promised something." He unfolded his hands. "There are rumors among the younger brothers. About the pilgrim who died. About voices. I have ordered those rumors stopped, because rumors of the old gods answering — *that* particular old god answering — is the sort of thing that brings the Concord's inquisitors up from the low country, and inquisitors, Mirabel, do not distinguish between the temple that harbors a haunting and the temple that causes one."

"The registry is empty, Abbot," Mirabel said. "I was checking my father's bond entry. I do that sometimes. Counting the years left."

It was a good lie. It was even mostly shaped like truth. Corvelin studied her, and she watched him *want* to believe it, because believing it was easier, because a temple's whole architecture was built on the principle that paper stays where you put it.

"Then you won't object," he said, "to a small formality."

He drew a document from his desk drawer. Mirabel recognized the format instantly — she had copied a dozen of them for other purposes. A writ of silence. Binding the named party from speaking of matters touching the temple's spiritual welfare, upon pain of extending their term of bond by one year per transgression.

"You want me to sign a vow of silence," she said.

"I want the matter *closed*," said Corvelin. "Sign, and this becomes paperwork, and paperwork is safe. Refuse—" and here he sighed, genuinely sorry, and it was the sorrow that frightened her more than the threat would have, "—and I must write to Grayford. Your sister's placement there depends on our standing. You understand."

She understood completely. That was the horror of it. He wasn't cruel. He was *careful*, and careful men feed people to wolves one signature at a time and call it stewardship.

Mirabel looked at the writ. At the space for her name. At the abbot's waiting, tired, kind face.

Three hundred years, the god had written. *I have been patient for three hundred years. But not much longer than that.*

And beneath that, in letters she could still see burned behind her eyes: *will you be my herald?*

She picked up the pen. Corvelin's shoulders eased a fraction. And Mirabel dipped it in his own inkwell, and signed — her name, her true name, in her best fair hand, the hand he had praised for eleven years — at the bottom of the writ of silence.

"There," she said. "Now it's witnessed. Now it counts."

Corvelin blinked. "Counts?"

"You're a temple man, Abbot. You know better than anyone — a vow spoken freely, signed willingly, before a witness." She laid down the pen and stood, and her heart was hammering but her voice came out steady, steady as a ruled line. "You've just made me swear silence about the god of oaths. Which means the god of oaths now holds a vow in my name. Written vows bind, Abbot. It's rather the whole point of us."

She was at the door before he found his voice, and what he called after her was her name — *Mirabel!* — sharp with a fear he didn't yet understand, and she did not turn around, because if she turned around he would see her face, and her face was no longer capable of hiding anything at all.

That night the registry was waiting, and the ink rose before she even sat down, quick and bright as a struck flint:

*WELL DONE,* wrote the god of oaths. *NOW WE ARE BOUND TOGETHER, YOU AND I. NOW IT IS OFFICIAL. NOW, HERALD — WE MUST SPEAK OF YOUR SISTER, AND OF DEBTS, AND OF WHAT YOU ARE WILLING TO LOSE. AND THEN WE MUST SPEAK OF AN OATHBREAKER WHO RUNS THE PASSES YOU DREW IN YOUR MARGINS, BECAUSE YOU WILL NEED A GUIDE, AND I BELIEVE YOU ALREADY KNOW THE ROAD SHE TAKES.*

Mirabel stared at the page. "*She?*"

*AH,* wrote the god, and she would have sworn the ink looked pleased. *THERE IS MUCH I HAVE NOT YET TOLD YOU. SIT DOWN, MIRABEL VANE. WE BEGIN TONIGHT.*
