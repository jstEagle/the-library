## 3. An Oathbreaker's Road

The trouble with being an oathbreaker, Wren Calloway had learned over seven long years, was that everyone assumed you'd feel worse about it.

The innkeeper at the Drowned Bell assumed it. He was leaning across the bar now, wiping the same damp spot on the wood he'd been wiping for the last quarter hour, giving Wren the look that people gave oathbreakers — half pity, half the careful distance one kept from a dog that had once bitten someone.

"Another?" he asked, nodding at Wren's cup.

"Water's fine."

"Water's free."

"Water's fine," Wren said again, and the innkeeper's face did the thing faces always did, the small arithmetic of *why would an armed woman drink water if she weren't broke*, and Wren smiled the smile that said *mind your arithmetic*, and the innkeeper went back to his damp spot.

Seven years ago Wren Calloway had been Ser Wren, Knight of the Vow of the Pale Order, sworn to protect the crown of King Aldric with their life. Seven years ago, in a corridor that smelled of hot copper and something older and worse, Wren had looked at the king — at the thing wearing the king — and had said, out loud, in front of witnesses: *No. I won't. I don't know what that is, but it isn't him, and I won't swear to it.*

They had broken the vow in front of the whole court. They had been stripped of their spurs in front of the whole court. And then, because the thing on the throne had looked at Wren with the king's grey eyes and said, in the king's mild voice, *Let them go. Let them run. I want to see how far*, they had been exiled instead of executed, and Wren had understood, with a clarity that had never once faded in seven years, that the mercy was the cruelty. That being allowed to live was the punishment. That somewhere in the dark of the palace, something was still watching to see how far they'd run.

So Wren had run exactly as far as the mountains, and no farther, and had made a life there that was small and dishonest and entirely their own. Contraband, mostly. Salt that hadn't paid its toll. Medicine the temple tax had priced out of reach of anyone who needed it. Letters, sometimes, for people who wanted words to travel without being read. Wren knew the passes the way other people knew their own hands — which ledges held frost, which scree slopes sang before they slid, which of the shrine-caves still had dry floors in the wet months.

And once, a year or two back, Wren had found a page in a courier's discarded satchel — a temple tithe record, the kind of boring document nobody would ever miss — and had nearly thrown it away before noticing the margins.

Someone had drawn roads in the margins. Little roads, fine and precise as spider silk, with elevations marked in a neat clerical hand, and notes in the same hand: *ice bridge unreliable after first thaw*, *goat path, wider than it looks*, *shrine-cave, dry, water at the back wall — sweet water, oddly*.

Wren had kept the page. Had copied it. Had used it. Had wondered, idly and more often than they'd admit, who at Thornfell temple was drawing smuggler maps in the margins of god-tithe records, and whether that person was as bored as Wren had once been in the palace, and whether boredom in a holy place curdled into the same thing it curdled into everywhere else.

*Restlessness,* Wren's old sword-master used to say. *The first symptom of a broken oath is restlessness. Mind it.*

Wren had broken the oath anyway. The restlessness had never left. It had just learned to keep quiet.

* * *

The bounty notice went up on the Drowned Bell's door on a grey morning, nailed there by a temple courier who didn't stay for a drink, and Wren read it twice with their breakfast cooling in front of them.

*By order of the Abbot of Thornfell:* it began, in the usual officious hand, and then, *Whereas one MIRABEL VANE, scribe, has been declared apostate and fled the temple in company of unknown persons; and whereas the said VANE is believed to be traveling the high passes under the guidance of a criminal; the temple offers FORTY CROWNS for word of her whereabouts, and ONE HUNDRED for her return unharmed.*

Wren read it a third time. *In company of unknown persons.* The temple was being polite. The temple meant *the oathbreaker Calloway*, because Wren had been seen in Thornfell's lower market three days ago buying rope, and temple couriers had eyes, and forty crowns was a great deal of rope money.

*Unknown persons.* Wren almost laughed. Seven years of exile and the best the temple could do was *unknown persons*.

But it was the name that stopped the laugh. Mirabel Vane. Apostate. The scribe who — and here the tavern gossip had been thick as porridge for two days — the scribe who had heard a prayer answered. Who had burned her own debt-bond in front of witnesses. Who claimed the old god of oaths had *named* her, had *shown* her things, and that one of the things he'd shown her was —

Wren set down the cup very carefully.

The tavern talk, which Wren had listened to with the detached amusement of someone who had long ago stopped believing in anything with a title, had included the vision. The gossip couldn't agree on much — whether the god had spoken in ink or in thunder, whether the scribe was a saint or a fraud — but on the vision they all agreed, because the temple elders had made the mistake of repeating it aloud in the market in their hurry to denounce it.

*A king,* the gossip said the god had shown her, *wearing a crown that eats.*

Wren sat very still, the way they'd learned to sit in the years after the corridor, the stillness of an animal that has heard a sound it cannot afford to have heard.

Because Wren had seen that. Not in a vision. In a corridor, with their own eyes, seven years ago. Had seen the crown settle on Aldric's head at the coronation and had seen — just for a heartbeat, just long enough to doubt their own sanity for the rest of their life — the king's face *change*. Not age. Not pale. *Change*, the way a face changes when something else puts it on. And Wren had sworn the vow anyway, because the vow was the vow, and then had lain awake all night listening to the vow sit inside their chest like a stone, and in the morning had gone to the king's door to ask him — privately, quietly, like a sane person — whether he was well.

And the king had smiled at Wren with a smile that fit his face the way a glove fits a hand two sizes wrong, and had said, *I am very well, Ser Wren. I have never been so well. Come in. Close the door.*

Wren had not gone in. Wren had gone to the corridor, and the thing had followed, patient as weather, and had stood in the doorway in the king's body wearing the king's crown, and had said, kindly, *Swear to me, Ser Wren. Swear your little vow. It tastes of nothing to me, but I do so enjoy the ceremony.*

And Wren had looked at the crown — really looked, the way you look at a snake you've stepped near — and had seen it. Not gold. Not quite. Something wearing gold the way the thing was wearing the king. Something with its mouth open, very gently, around the top of Aldric's head, drinking.

Wren had said no. Had said it loudly, so the whole court would hear, so there'd be witnesses, because Wren had understood even then that the only protection an honest person had in that palace was an audience. And the court had heard a knight break her vow for no reason anyone could see, and the thing on the throne had smiled its two-sizes-wrong smile, and Wren had been stripped and exiled and *permitted*, and had spent seven years telling themselves the story they could live with: that they'd panicked, that they'd imagined it, that the vow had been too heavy for them and they'd dropped it like a coward drops anything.

It was a good story. It had kept them alive. It had kept them from going mad, mostly.

And now a temple scribe was running around the mountains telling anyone who'd listen that a god had shown her a king wearing a crown that eats, and Wren's hands were shaking around a cup of free water in the Drowned Bell, and the story they'd lived inside for seven years was coming apart like wet paper.

"No," Wren said out loud, to no one. "No, absolutely not."

The innkeeper looked over. "Something wrong with the water?"

"Everything," said Wren, and went to pack.

* * *

The thing about the passes in early spring was that the ice bridges lied. Wren knew which ones lied and how, and so it was with genuine professional irritation that Wren came around the shoulder of the Grey Stair on the fourth day out and found someone standing on the worst of them — the Kettlebridge, a shelf of blue ice over a drop that the mountain had been chewing at for a century — testing it with one boot like a tourist.

Wren had been tracking the scribe for two days, not because of the bounty but because of the opposite of the bounty: because if the temple wanted the woman found, Wren wanted to find her first and decide what to do about it, and because — and here Wren was honest with themselves, in the grudging way they were honest about everything — because Wren needed to look at her face and see whether she was a fraud. A fraud would be a relief. A fraud meant the world was still the world, where gods didn't answer and crowns didn't eat and a knight could spend seven years being ashamed of a thing she'd actually been right about.

"Oi!" Wren called, and the woman on the ice turned, and Wren's professional irritation doubled, because she wasn't even dressed for the mountains. Scribe's robes, temple grey, hems already soaked, and she was carrying her pack like a person who had read about packs. "Off the bridge. Now. It's rotten through the middle, it goes every year, and it goes *fast*."

"It held for the pilgrims," the woman said. Her voice carried strangely well in the thin air — clear, stubborn, the voice of someone who had spent years being talked over and had developed volume as a defense. "Three of them crossed this morning."

"Pilgrims weigh nothing, they're mostly prayer and boots. You've got a week of supplies and a sword you don't know how to wear." Wren came down the scree without hurrying, which took skill, and stopped at the bridge's near end. "I'm the one who knows this road. Get off it."

The scribe looked at Wren. She had ink on her fingers — of course she did, it was in the creases, ground in like a stain she'd stopped fighting — and eyes the color of strong tea, and she looked tired in the deep way, the way of someone who had been running on something other than sleep. And she looked at Wren the way nobody had looked at Wren in seven years: like she was *reading* them.

"You're Calloway," she said.

"I'm nobody. There's a hundred crowns on my head too, if you're shopping."

"I don't want the bounty." She hadn't moved off the bridge. "I want the guide. The god said you'd come."

"The god," Wren said, "said."

"He's very sure of himself," she agreed, and something in her tone — the small, dry exasperation of it — was so ordinary, so *human*, that Wren felt the last of their cynicism crack down the middle like the ice under her boot.

Which it promptly did.

The sound was nothing, a crack like a knuckle, and then the whole middle span of the Kettlebridge sagged with a groan that Wren had heard before and had nightmares about, and Wren was moving before thought caught up — flat out along the near shelf, hand out, and the scribe, to her considerable credit, did not freeze but *ran*, three lurching strides, and Wren caught her wrist and hauled and the bridge let go behind her heels with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and then they were both on solid stone, breathless, the bridge's remains swaying in the gorge like a torn hem.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The scribe was on her back in the scree, chest heaving, staring at the sky.

"The god," she said finally, "did not mention that."

"The god," Wren said, flat on their back beside her, "doesn't walk anywhere. Gods don't have to know where the ice is rotten." They turned their head. She was close enough that Wren could see the ink in the creases of her knuckles, could see that she was, absurdly, *grinning*. "What's funny?"

"Nothing. Everything. I spent six years copying tithes." She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, bright and breathless in the cold air. "Six years, and the most dangerous thing I ever did was draw roads in margins. And now the roads are trying to kill me, and I'm lying next to an oathbreaker, and it's the best day I've had since — " she stopped, and Wren watched the joy bank itself, carefully, the way you bank a fire when someone's sleeping nearby. "Since I was bound to the temple. Which was a long time ago."

Wren should have stood up. Should have kept the distance, kept the cynicism, kept the seven-year-old armor. Instead Wren heard themself say: "Calloway. Wren Calloway. Since you're going to use my name anyway."

"Mirabel Vane." She turned her head too, and they regarded each other across a hand's width of mountain stone, two people who had both been told what they were by institutions and had quietly stopped believing it. "The god says you know why I'm here."

"The god," Wren said, "says a lot of things."

"He says you saw it. The crown. Seven years ago. He says that's why you ran."

The cold went through Wren then, colder than the mountain, the old cold, the corridor cold. Wren sat up. Looked at her. She sat up too and didn't flinch, which took a certain kind of courage, because Wren knew what their face did when the corridor came back — had seen it in tavern mirrors, once or twice, before learning to keep it behind glass.

"Say I did," Wren said quietly. "Say I saw exactly what your god says I saw, and I said no, and it ruined my life. What do you imagine happens if I say yes now? If I stand up in front of you and say, yes, the king is being eaten, I saw it, I've *known* for seven years?" Wren's voice cracked and they hated it. "Do you know what that means? It means the exile was real. It means I wasn't a coward, I was the only sane person in that palace, and I *ran*, and it's been eating me alive ever since that I ran. If I say yes, I can never again tell myself I broke that vow because I was weak. I'd have to admit I broke it because I was *right*, and then I'd have to do something about it. And doing something about it gets people killed. I've seen it get people killed."

Mirabel Vane was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the gorge wind moved through the ruins of the bridge.

"Six years ago," she said, "I found out I could draw. Not well — just well enough. And I started putting the roads in the margins of the tithe records, because it was the only thing in that temple that was *mine*. And I told myself it was harmless. A secret. Something small." She looked at her ink-stained hands. "And then a god started writing to me, and I learned that nothing you do in secret is small. It all counts. It was all *already* counting." She looked up. "I'm not asking you to admit anything, Wren Calloway. I'm asking you to walk in front of me and tell me where the ice is rotten. You can believe whatever you like about the rest. I did, for a week. It's very comfortable."

"It's not," Wren said. "I've tried."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm here."

Wren stood, and brushed off the scree, and looked east along the pass, toward the drowned shrine and the first fragment and everything that came after. The restlessness that had lived in their chest for seven years — quiet, fed, managed — was awake now, and pacing, and it felt horribly, wonderfully like hope.

"Payment," Wren said at last. "Forty crowns. Up front."

"I have eleven crowns and a temple candlestick I stole."

"Eleven crowns and a candlestick," Wren said, "and you'll owe me the rest."

"I'm good for it," Mirabel said. "I keep accounts."

Of course she did. Wren shouldered their pack and started walking, and did not look back to see if she followed, and heard her boots on the stone a half-step behind, steady as ink drying.

It was only that night, at the fire, watching her sketch the pass by candlelight — the stolen candlestick, of course, the woman had no shame at all — that Wren let themself think the thought they'd been running from since the Drowned Bell:

*If the god is real, and the vision is real, then the corridor was real. And if the corridor was real, then it let me go on purpose. It wanted to see how far I'd run.*

Wren looked across the fire at the scribe, at the small precise roads blooming under her pen, and thought: *It has seen how far. And now I'm walking back.*

The candle guttered. In the wind's pause, very faintly, from somewhere up the dark pass — the direction of the drowned shrine, the direction of the first fragment — Wren heard, or thought they heard, a sound like many voices saying one word at once.

Mirabel's pen stopped.

"You heard it too," she said. It wasn't a question.

Wren fed a stick to the fire and did not let their hand shake. "Tomorrow," they said. "We'll worry about it tomorrow."

But they both lay awake a long time, listening to the mountain, and the mountain did not sound empty anymore.
