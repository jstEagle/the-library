## 4. Thirteen Turns

The god of oaths did not sleep, and apparently he expected no one else to.

Mirabel had been awake for three days. Not by choice — every time she closed her eyes, the words came back. They arrived the way rain arrives through a bad roof: a drip here, a drip there, and then suddenly her whole pillow was soaked in language that was not hers. *Thirteen turns*, the temple walls had whispered on the first night. *Count them. Count what cannot be counted.*

So she counted.

She sat now in the scriptorium's coldest corner with a candle stub, a fresh sheet of vellum she had absolutely not stolen from the tithe-rolls, and an inkpot balanced on a stack of discarded homilies. Before her lay everything she'd been able to gather about the moon.

Which was, embarrassingly little. The Thornfell almanac — the only one the temple owned, chained to its lectern like a prisoner — recorded twelve moons. Twelve, as it had recorded for as long as anyone could remember. The Silver, the Sower's Moon, the Wolf, the Weeping, and so on around the wheel of the year, each rising full when it ought to rise full, each setting where the almanac said it would set. A tidy system. A pious system. The kind of arithmetic that made abbots comfortable.

And yet.

"Say it again," Mirabel murmured to the empty room. "Slowly."

The ink in her pot shivered. Then it climbed — she still hadn't gotten used to that, the way it climbed the nib like a small dark creature going up a ladder — and wrote across her stolen vellum in a hand far too elegant for the medium:

*There is a moon between the Wolf and the Weeping. There has always been a moon between the Wolf and the Weeping. Your calendar forgot it because forgetting is how the crown survives.*

Mirabel read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings had produced entirely different sensations in her chest, neither of them pleasant.

"That's not possible," she said. "A whole moon. A whole month. People would notice a month."

*People noticed,* the ink wrote, patient as frost forming. *Then people were helped to forget. Kings proclaimed. Priests concurred. Almanacs were corrected. It takes very little to erase a month from a kingdom, herald. It takes almost nothing at all to erase a king.*

Mirabel put down the pen and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Three days ago she had been a debt-bond scribe with smuggler maps hidden in her margins and a private, shameful hunger for a life larger than tithes. Now she was a herald. She kept turning the word over looking for the part of it that came with instructions, or wages, or at minimum a chair.

"All right," she said. "The thirteenth turn. When?"

*It completes at the eclipse,* the ink answered. *The moon you forgot will stand before the sun you know, and when the light returns, the devouring will be finished. King Aldric will be gone. Not dead — gone. Eaten to the last crumb. And the thing wearing his crown will have grown strong enough to stop wearing him at all.*

"Stop wearing him." She lowered her hands. "Meaning what? It walks around as itself?"

*Meaning it will no longer need a host. Meaning it will take the kingdom the way a fire takes a forest — not ruling it, but being it.* A pause, and then the ink added, with something that in a person might have been called weariness: *I have watched this spirit eat before. In older kingdoms. Under older names. I swore, after the last time, that I would never again stand in a scriptorium and explain the arithmetic of its appetite. Yet here we are.*

"You've mentioned your failure," Mirabel said. "Repeatedly. You know that doesn't reassure me."

*I am aware. Honesty rarely does.*

She almost smiled. That was the strange thing about the god of oaths: he was enormous and ancient and terrifying, and also he argued like a tired clerk who had done the ledgers too many times. She suspected, though she couldn't prove it, that the tiredness was real, and that somewhere under all that grandeur was a being who had once believed in kings and had been taught better.

"The fragments," she said, pulling the conversation back before it could drift into divine melancholy again. "You said Aldric's coronation vow was broken into three pieces. Explain it like I'm copying it for the record. I think better when I'm transcribing."

The ink rose obediently, and Mirabel took up her pen and followed it, word for word, the way she'd followed tithe-entries for six years. This, at least, was familiar ground. Give her a text and she could hold anything.

*When a king is crowned in the old rite — the true rite, not the theater they perform now — he swears three vows, and the vows are spoken as three objects: to protect, to serve, to love. Not love as feeling. Love as binding. The vow of love is the one that says: my life is knotted to yours, my people, and I cannot prosper while you starve. Three vows, three knots, one rope. The crown-spirit cannot be fought with swords because it lives inside the knot. But if the original vow can be found — all three pieces, whole and unbroken — then it can be spoken again, truly, by a voice that means it. And a knot that is tied truly cannot be worn falsely.*

"And the pieces are scattered."

*Scattered and hidden, by the last honest court, in places where oaths were once kept so thoroughly that the stones themselves remember. A drowned shrine beneath the frozen lake at Vaelmoor. A war-crypt in the Grayreach passes where soldiers lie who died keeping their word. And the ruined chapel at Corvenholt, where Aldric first knelt.* The ink paused. *They are guarded. Not by men. By consequences. Oaths do not forgive, herald, even the ones broken kindly. Especially those.*

Mirabel looked at the map taking shape beneath her pen — three points strung across mountains she knew only as lines on other people's charts, except that they weren't other people's charts anymore, were they, because she had spent years drawing those same passes in secret, tracing smuggler roads in margins, and now the secret maps and the holy errand had collapsed into the same page.

"I can't read these mountains," she said quietly. "I've copied maps of them my whole life. But I've never walked one."

*No,* the god agreed. *But you know someone who has.*

---

Wren Calloway was eating breakfast on the temple steps.

This was, Mirabel reflected as she descended toward them, a remarkable act of audacity. Wren was, at present, a wanted person sitting on the stairs of the institution that wanted them, eating fried eggs off a tin plate with the unhurried contentment of a cat in a sunbeam. Two novices gave them a wide berth. One of the gate-guards kept glancing over with the expression of a man doing math about bounty money and losing his nerve halfway through.

"You're supposed to be hiding," Mirabel said.

"I am hiding." Wren gestured at the step beside them without looking up. "Nobody looks for a criminal somewhere this obvious. Sit. There's half an egg left, and I'll fight you for it."

Mirabel sat. She did not fight for the egg. Wren pushed the plate toward her anyway, which she was learning was how Wren expressed nearly all their feelings: through food they pretended not to want to share.

"You didn't sleep," Wren observed.

"The god talks at night."

"Does it ever talk at reasonable hours? Noon, say? With tea?" Wren licked grease off a thumb. "Because I've been thinking, and I want it noted that I think this is a terrible idea, whatever it is, and I haven't even heard it yet. That's how reliable my instincts are."

"It's not a terrible idea."

"Everything involving gods is a terrible idea. Gods are weather with opinions." Wren finally turned to look at her, and Mirabel saw that under the flippancy their eyes were red-rimmed. They hadn't slept either. "Three nights ago I was moving brandy through a pass and minding my own business. Now I'm sitting on holy steps waiting for a scribe to tell me the sky's falling. So tell me. Sky falling?"

Mirabel told them. All of it — the thirteenth moon, the eclipse, the devouring, the three fragments strung across the mountains like beads on a wire. She told it plainly, the way she'd write it, because plainness was the only way she could carry it. Wren listened without interrupting, which she gathered was rare for them, and when she finished they were quiet for a long moment, turning their tin plate slowly in their hands.

"The lake at Vaelmoor," Wren said at last. "That's the drowned shrine?"

"So the god says."

"Hm." Wren's jaw worked. "I know that lake. There's a road under it, in winter, when the ice holds. Smugglers call it the Cold Road. Nobody uses it past midwinter, and nobody camps on it, ever, and if you ask why, everyone just looks at you." They exhaled through their nose. "I always thought that was superstition. Smuggler superstition, which is the sturdiest kind."

"It might be worse than superstition."

"It usually is." Wren set the plate down. "Grayreach war-crypt. Corvenholt chapel. You understand those are weeks apart, in mountains, in the back end of the year, with the crown's people hunting us the entire way. Because they will hunt us. The moment you start digging up pieces of the king's real vow, every knight in the capital grows a sudden interest in your health."

"I know."

"And you're a scribe. No offense intended, but you walk like a person reading."

"Some offense received."

"Good. Means you're listening." Wren rubbed their face. "All right. Here's my position. My position is no. Absolutely not. I broke one vow in my life and it cost me everything, and I have built a very comfortable existence out of never swearing anything to anybody ever again, and you are asking me to walk into the mountains and handle the broken pieces of the most important vow in the kingdom. That's not a job. That's a punishment."

"Yes," Mirabel said.

"That wasn't a question."

"No. But you were going to say yes anyway, and I wanted you to hear yourself refuse first. It seemed fair."

Wren stared at her. Then they laughed — a short, startled, genuinely delighted sound, quickly smothered, but real. "Saints' teeth. You're funny. Nobody warned me the herald was funny."

"I'm not funny. I'm accurate. People confuse them."

"Payment," Wren said abruptly, holding up a finger. "If I do this — and I'm not saying I'm doing this — I want payment. Proper terms. Gold, not blessings. I've had enough blessings to last a lifetime and they've all been terrible."

"What would you do with gold?"

"Retire. Buy a farm. Grow turnips. Turnips don't have opinions about my soul." Wren's finger stayed raised. "Ten crowns. Paid on completion. Plus expenses."

"The temple treasury has maybe four crowns and most of those are pledged to the roof."

"Then the god can pay me. He's got a whole kingdom riding on this; he can find ten crowns." Wren folded their arms. "Well? Ask your ink."

Mirabel dipped her pen and held it over the vellum. *He wants payment,* she wrote, or rather asked. *Ten crowns and expenses.*

The answer came fast, and she read it, and she laughed before she could stop herself.

"What?" Wren demanded. "What did it say?"

Mirabel turned the vellum toward them. The elegant ink stood gleaming in the morning light:

*Tell the oathbreaker that I keep accounts too. Tell them their fee is accepted — and that I remember exactly what they fled the capital to avoid seeing, and that the road to Vaelmoor goes through it. Payment will be collected in kind.*

Wren read it twice. The color went out of their face by degrees, like a tide going out, and Mirabel watched them arrive at the understanding she herself had reached sometime in the small hours: this god did not trick, exactly. He simply priced things honestly, and honesty was its own kind of trap.

"That's extortion," Wren said hoarsely.

"That's terms," Mirabel said gently. "You wanted proper terms."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. A novice crossed the courtyard below, chasing a chicken. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, wrong, as bells in Thornfell had been ringing slightly wrong for as long as anyone could remember — and Mirabel thought, with a chill that settled pleasantly into place like a key finding its lock, *of course they ring wrong. We lost a month. Every bell in the kingdom is ringing a month late and no one remembers why.*

"Fine," Wren said at last, standing, brushing egg and dignity off their breeches. "Fine. Ten crowns, expenses, and when this is over I never swear another oath as long as I live, and no god gets to comment on it. Deal?"

The ink, Mirabel saw, had already risen unbidden in her pot, waiting. She offered the pen. *He says deal. He says — * she read the next line and decided some mercies were worth extending — *he says welcome back to the road, Calloway.*

"Liar," Wren muttered, but they took their pack off the step where it had been sitting packed since dawn, and shouldered it, and did not look at the temple gates as they turned away from them.

"We leave within the hour," Mirabel said, folding the vellum with the three marked mountains into her coat, against her heart, next to six years of secret maps. "Before the abbot finds a way to make this harder than it already is."

"Too late," Wren said, nodding past her shoulder. "Look."

At the top of the steps, framed in the temple doors, stood Abbot Coram with two elders behind him, and in his hands, held out flat like an offering or an accusation, was a document Mirabel knew by heart, because she had copied it out four times a year for six years, and because her name was written on it in her own careful hand.

Her debt-bond.

The abbot's voice carried down the cold morning air, mild and terrible:

"Scribe Vane. Before you go anywhere — we should discuss what your family owes."
