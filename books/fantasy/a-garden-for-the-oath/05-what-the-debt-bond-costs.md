## 5. What the Debt-Bond Costs

The debt-bond hung in the scriptorium like a saint's relic, which was, Mirabel thought, the single most obscene thing about it.

It wasn't displayed, exactly. It simply *was* there — pinned to the great ledger-board among the temple's other instruments of obligation, forty-odd contracts in neat rows, each one a family's life folded into a page and sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Hers sat third from the left, between a stonemason's widow and a farmer who had once borrowed enough to buy a mule and would be paying for that mule until his grandchildren forgot why they were poor.

*VANE, MIRABEL, daughter of Tomas Vane, deceased. Sum owed to the Thornfell Temple of the Ninefold Light: eleven marks, four shillings, accrued interest as recorded. Term of service: until discharged by labor or payment.*

Eleven marks. Her father had borrowed eleven marks to bury her mother with a proper stone, and the temple's interest had turned it into nine years of her life so far, with no end in sight. She copied tithes six days out of seven. She ground ink. She trimmed candlewicks and fetched water and wrote letters for pilgrims who couldn't write their own, and every month the ledger-clerk entered her labor against the debt at a rate that ensured she would die at this desk with the sum still owing.

She had spent years hating that page quietly, the way you hate weather. Now she stood in the dark of the refectory corridor and hated it the way you hate a person.

"You're going to do something stupid," said Brother Anselm behind her, not unkindly.

Mirabel didn't jump. She'd known he was there; Anselm moved through the temple like a man who had made peace with being invisible, which was what thirty years of copying other people's confessions did to you. He was old, round, and smelled perpetually of lamp oil and licorice root, and he was the only soul in Thornfell who had never once looked at her like an entry in a ledger.

"I'm going to do something necessary," she said.

"Necessary." He came to stand beside her, hands tucked into his sleeves. "That's what my mother called it when she sold our goat to pay for my schooling. The goat disagreed."

"Anselm—"

"The god spoke through you." He said it plainly, without awe or accusation, which was somehow worse than either. "Three nights ago. In the chapel. Every vow on the walls rearranged itself into words nobody else could read, and you read them aloud, and old Mother Cass fell down in a faint, and the abbot has been pacing ever since like a cat in a room full of rocking chairs. And now here you are at midnight, staring at your own contract like it owes *you* money." A pause. "Does it?"

"It owes me everything," Mirabel said. "And I'm leaving tomorrow. With Wren Calloway. For the mountains. Because a god who hasn't spoken in three hundred years looked at every soul in this temple and chose—" she laughed, and it came out cracked, "*chose the debtor.* And if I go, the bond breaks. And if the bond breaks—"

"The debt passes to kin." Anselm's voice went gentle. "Sefa."

Sefa. Sixteen years old, quick as a wren herself — no, not like Wren, don't think that name right now — with their mother's laugh and their father's stubbornness and a talent for arithmetic that meant the temple had already asked, twice, whether the younger Miss Vane might like to come work off her family's obligations in the counting-house. Sefa had said no both times, cheerfully, because Sefa said everything cheerfully. But she'd said it *carefully*, too, and Mirabel knew what careful meant from a girl who had grown up watching interest eat a family whole.

"If I burn my contract," Mirabel said, "the debt dies with it. Unsecured debts pass to kin only if the contract stands. I've read the canon law, Anselm. I've *copied* the canon law. Four times."

"You've copied everything four times," he said. "It's very annoying. One can't lie to you about anything." He was quiet a moment. Then: "You know the abbot will call it theft. Apostasy. He'll have to, publicly. Whatever he thinks privately."

"What does he think privately?"

Anselm snorted. "I think privately he's terrified. There's a difference between faith and fear, girl, but most men carry them in the same pocket and can't tell which one they're spending." He patted her shoulder with one soft hand. "Third from the left. The lock on the board is older than I am. The key hangs in the sacristy, but the hinge pin comes out with a knife, if a person were inclined."

Mirabel stared at him. "You're helping me."

"I'm standing in a corridor," said Brother Anselm, "at midnight, talking to myself. Goodnight, Mirabel Vane. Try not to set fire to anything you'll miss."

He shuffled away toward the dormitory, and Mirabel stood alone in the dark with her heart hammering out a rhythm that felt less like fear than like a drum before a march.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat on her cot in the sisters' cell she'd shared with Sefa for nine years and watched the moonlight move across the floor, and when it reached the third flagstone — Sefa's trick for telling time without getting up, learned during long childhood nights of sharing one blanket and one secret at a time — Mirabel finally let herself think about the conversation she had to have.

Sefa was awake. Of course she was awake. Sefa always woke when Mirabel's breathing changed, some twin-of-the-heart sense neither of them had ever needed blood-ties to explain. They weren't blood sisters at all, actually — Tomas Vane had taken Sefa in off a plague-cart road when she was four, a foundling with no name anyone knew, and the temple had registered her under his household for the sake of tidiness. Which meant, by the letter of the law, that Sefa counted as kin. Which meant the debt would fall to her.

Which meant Mirabel had to burn the contract tonight, before the abbot thought to amend the registry. Before anyone thought of it at all.

"You're leaving," Sefa said into the dark. Not a question.

"At first light. With the Calloway woman. South, then east, over the Serpent's Teeth." Mirabel swallowed. "There's a king to free. Apparently."

"A king." Sefa sat up. Moonlight caught the edge of her grin, sharp and bright. "Our Mirabel. Nine years of copying tithes and now she's freeing kings. Is it dangerous?"

"Yes."

"Good. You were getting boring." But Sefa's voice wobbled on *boring*, and Mirabel reached across the space between cots and took her hand, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they had learned young that the important things were better held than said.

"There's something else," Mirabel said finally. "And you're going to be angry."

"I'm already angry. You told me *yes* when I asked if it was dangerous. Get the rest out."

So Mirabel told her. The bond, the debt, the law, the plan. The scriptorium. The fire. And she watched Sefa's face do something complicated — relief and fury fighting for the same ground — and braced for shouting.

Instead Sefa lay back down and stared at the ceiling and said, very quietly, "You were going to do it without telling me. Weren't you. You were going to sneak out and burn it and leave me a note."

"...Yes."

"Mirabel. If you leave me a note I will *never forgive you*. Notes are for cowards and people who die. You want to burn our family's shame in front of witnesses? Then I'm a witness. I'm the *first* witness. I've been waiting nine years to watch somebody tell this temple where it can put its interest rates." She rolled onto her side, eyes fierce and wet all at once. "You don't get to protect me by erasing me. That's not protection. That's just doing it alone."

Mirabel's throat closed. "When did you get wise?"

"Birth. You just never listened." Sefa squeezed her hand hard enough to hurt. "Now sleep. Or don't. But lie there looking brave, because I intend to memorize it."

* * *

They went at the hour before dawn, when even the night brothers dozed over their candles and the temple's stones held their coldest silence.

Mirabel had planned it a dozen ways in her head — the knife under the hinge pin, the route past the vestry, the contingency for the dog (there was no dog; she'd checked; temples that bind children to ledgers rarely keep anything as honest as a dog). But in the event it hardly mattered, because half the temple was already awake and gathering in the scriptorium when they arrived, drawn by rumor and dread, and Abbot Corvus stood before the ledger-board with his hands clasped behind him like a man attending his own trial.

He was not a cruel man. That was the tragedy of him. Mirabel had watched him for nine years — had copied his sermons, had seen him slip bread to beggars and argue the Ninefold Light's doctrine of mercy against visiting prelates twice his rank. But Corvus loved the temple the way a man loves a ship in a storm: absolutely, and with total willingness to throw things overboard.

"Mistress Vane," he said, without turning. "Or should I say Herald."

"Abbot." She stopped an arm's length away. Behind him, the contracts hung in their neat rows, hers third from the left, and she saw that he had already taken it down. It lay flat on the reading table before him, her name facing up, the seal unbroken.

"I wondered how long you'd take," Corvus said. "Brother Anselm told me at compline that you'd come tonight. He also told me the hinge pin trick, which I believe was his way of informing me that stopping you would cost more than it saved." He turned then, and his eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "I am going to offer you one mercy, and then I am going to do my duty, and I would take it kindly if you didn't make either harder than it must be. The mercy: walk away. Leave the contract. Go with your oathbreaker and your god, and I will enter your departure as a sabbatical of pilgrimage. The debt waits. Debts are patient. It is the one virtue we teach them."

"And Sefa?" Mirabel asked. "If I don't come back?"

Corvus said nothing. His silence said everything.

"That's what I thought." Mirabel stepped forward. "Then here is my counter-offer, Abbot, and I give it before witnesses, as the law requires." She raised her voice, and heads turned among the gathered brothers and sisters — two dozen of them now, filling the scriptorium's aisles, drawn by the same gravity that pulls people to fires. "This contract binds the labor of Mirabel Vane against a debt of eleven marks, contracted by Tomas Vane, rest him, for a gravestone. Eleven marks. I have given this temple nine years. By any honest count I have paid it thrice over, and the remainder is not debt but *design*, and design is not holy, whatever seal you press it with."

"Careful," Corvus said softly.

"No," Mirabel said. "I have been careful for nine years. Careful is what they grind you into." She held out her hand. "Give me the page, Abbot. Or I take it. Either way, before these witnesses, I renounce the bond."

"You renounce it," Corvus said, "and the law transfers it. To your sister. You know this. You've copied the statute four times."

"I know the statute *as written*," said Mirabel, "and I know what the founders intended, because I copied *that* too — the original charter, the one locked in the archive, the one that says a debt secured against labor dies with the laborer's release and cannot descend to children *born outside the covenant*. Sefa isn't bound by birth. She was registered by convenience. Your own charter forbids it, Abbot. You just hoped nobody would read far enough." She let that land, watching understanding move through the room like wind through wheat. "I didn't come to steal my contract. I came to *read it aloud*, with the charter beside it, in front of everyone. And then, since paper that lies shouldn't be allowed to keep standing—"

She struck the flint she'd carried in her sleeve all night, and touched it to the corner of the page.

Corvus lunged — genuinely lunged, an old man moving faster than dignity allowed — but Sefa was quicker, stepping into his path with her arms spread, not blocking him so much as *offering herself to be stopped*, which no man of Corvus's conscience could bring himself to do. And so the abbot of Thornfell stood with his hands half-raised and watched eleven marks of manufactured bondage curl and blacken and lift in bright flakes toward the rafters, while his brothers and sisters watched with him, and nobody — not one soul in that room — moved to help him stop it.

The light was beautiful. That was the terrible thing. Mirabel had expected ash and grief, and instead the fire threw gold across the faces of everyone she'd worked beside for nine years, and old Sister Marta was crying, and young Brother Tam wore an expression of naked, scandalized joy, and the smoke smelled like every unfair morning Mirabel had ever spent at that desk finally going up as light.

When the last corner crumbled, Corvus straightened. He smoothed his robe. He became, visibly, the office instead of the man.

"Mirabel Vane," he said, and his voice carried the way voices do when a building helps them. "You have destroyed a consecrated instrument. You have rejected the discipline of the Ninefold Light for the whisperings of a dead god. You have done it before witnesses, and so shall your sentence be witnessed." He drew breath. "I declare you apostate. Cast out. Your name struck from our rolls; your labor stricken from our records; the doors of this house closed to you, now and always, unless you return in penitence."

"Struck from the rolls," Mirabel repeated. Something loosened in her chest so suddenly she nearly laughed. "Abbot, do you know what you've just done? You've freed me. Completely. No bond, no debt, no order to disobey. You couldn't have given me a cleaner send-off if you'd tried."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvus's mouth. It might have been a smile dying. "Go," he said. "Before I remember how to be cruel."

But as she turned — Sefa's hand finding hers, warm and gripping — the abbot spoke again, quieter, just for her.

"Herald." The title came out strange in his mouth, half curse, half prayer. "The thing in the vision you described. The crown that eats." His eyes, for one unguarded moment, were those of a frightened boy. "If your god can truly unmake such a thing... come back and tell us. Even apostates may send letters."

Mirabel nodded. And because she was who she was — a scribe to the bone — she tore a blank leaf from the nearest desk, wrote three words, and left it on the reading table where the contract had been:

*Paid in full.*

* * *

The gates of Thornfell opened at first light, and no one stopped them, and several someones pressed bread and cheese and one illicit flask of plum brandy into their packs as they passed.

Wren Calloway waited outside the walls, leaning against the milestone with the studied boredom of someone who had been worried sick for hours and would die before admitting it. They looked Mirabel up and down, then Sefa, then the smoke-smell clinging to both of them.

"Burning the contract, were we? Subtle. Very herald-like. Most chosen ones start with, oh, a humble blessing. Maybe a dove."

"It was a whole ceremony," Sefa said. "There were witnesses. I blocked an abbot."

"See, I like her," Wren told Mirabel. "We're keeping her until the border." Their gaze came back to Mirabel, and beneath the dryness something real flickered — the look of a person checking, carefully, whether the other person had broken. "Apostate now. Excommunicated. No temple, no home, no name on any roll in Thornfell. How does it feel?"

Mirabel looked back at the temple one last time — at the chapel where a god had spoken through written vows, at the windows of the scriptorium, at the small figure of Brother Anselm visible on the wall, raising one hand in a solemn, licorice-scented farewell.

"Lighter," she said, and found it was true, mostly, if you didn't look too closely at the ache underneath. "It feels lighter. Come on. We've a lake to freeze ourselves into, a fragment to find, and a moon that won't wait."

Wren pushed off the milestone and fell into step beside her, and the three of them took the south road together as the sun came up over the mountains they would have to cross — and none of them saw the rider on the ridge above, in Hollowed grey, turning his horse toward the capital to report that the herald had begun to move.
