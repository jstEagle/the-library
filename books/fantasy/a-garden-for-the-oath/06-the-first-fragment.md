## 6. The First Fragment

The lake had a name once, Wren said, but the name had drowned with the shrine beneath it, and now the mapmakers just called it the Cold Eye. It sat in a bowl of granite high in the Serpent's Teeth, and it froze so early in the year that travelers swore you could hear it cracking shut like a lid.

Wren stood at its edge at dawn, breath steaming, and hated it immediately and thoroughly.

"This is it?" Mirabel came up beside them, her satchel of maps clutched to her chest like a shield. She'd been quiet all morning — three days of quiet since they'd left the shrine-road and turned north into the high passes, and Wren had learned that Mirabel's silences were not empty ones. They were full. They pressed against the inside of her skull until she either spoke or did something reckless.

"It's it," Wren said. "According to your god."

"He isn't *my* god." But she said it without heat, the way she said it every time now, which meant she was starting to believe otherwise. "He said the fragment lies where the water remembers."

"The water remembers." Wren crouched and touched the ice. It was black and thick as a chapel door. "That's comforting. Water that remembers. I've met people like that. They're exhausting."

"You joke when you're frightened."

"And what do you do?"

Mirabel looked out over the lake, and the wind pulled a strand of hair loose from under her hood. "I make lists," she said. "In my head. Of everything that could go wrong. Then I number them by how badly it would hurt."

"Let me guess. Number one is your sister."

She didn't answer, which was an answer.

Wren straightened and rolled their shoulders. The hole in their chest where their oath used to live ached in this cold — it always ached in the cold, a phantom pain, like a tooth long since pulled. Ten years of exile and still some part of them reached for a vow that wasn't there anymore, the way a tongue worries an empty socket.

"So," they said briskly. "Drowned shrine. Under the ice. Which means one of us goes down there." They looked at the lake, then at Mirabel, then back at the lake. "I want it on record that I vote for neither of us."

"Noted," Mirabel said, already unshouldering her pack. "Overruled."

* * *

The god had given them instructions the night before, written in frost on the inside of the tent flap — because apparently a deity who could speak through any written vow couldn't be bothered to simply knock on the canvas like a civilized person. The message had been terse even by his standards:

*BENEATH THE ICE A DOOR. THE WATER WILL SHOW YOU WHAT YOU CARRY. DO NOT LOOK AWAY. WHAT REFUSES TO BE SEEN DROWNS.*

"Wonderful," Wren had said, reading it twice. "'Do not look away.' He writes like my old drill sergeant."

"He writes like someone who has watched many people drown," Mirabel had said quietly, and Wren hadn't had a joke for that one.

Now they stood on the ice with a coil of rope and a hand-drill Wren had traded two good knives for in the last village, and the morning sun lay flat and white on the frozen lake, giving nothing away.

"The door will be near the center," Mirabel said. She had her maps out despite the wind, weighted with stones, and her finger traced a line only she could see. "Old shrines were built over springs. The water stays warmer there, moving. The ice above a spring is thinner." She glanced up. "You knew that, didn't you. You've done this before."

"I've robbed things before," Wren said. "Robbing and diving are different professions. One gets you hanged. The other gets you wet." They started drilling anyway. "There's a difference between knowing where a thing is and wanting to go get it."

"What's the difference?"

"About forty feet of freezing water."

But they drilled, and Mirabel marked, and by midmorning they found it: a place where the drill bit through too fast, where the ice gave way from a hand's breadth to a finger's to nothing at all, and black water breathed up at them from below like the lake exhaling after holding its breath all winter.

Wren tied the rope around their own waist before Mirabel could argue about it.

"I'm lighter," they said, which was true, and "I've broken worse rules than 'don't swim in haunted lakes,'" which was also true, and "you're the herald, if I don't come back up someone has to complain to the management," which was mostly true, and underneath all three of those was the real reason, which Wren kept carefully folded away: that they had spent ten years running from the memory of failing someone who depended on them, and they would be damned — further damned — if they stood safe on the ice while Mirabel Vane went under black water alone.

"Hold the rope," they said. "If I pull twice, haul. If I pull three times—"

"What happens at three?"

"Pray to your god. Loudly."

Then Wren took a breath that hurt going in, and went through the hole into the dark.

* * *

The cold was a living thing. It found every gap in their clothes at once and squeezed, and Wren's whole body clenched around it as they kicked downward along the rope's guidance, eyes open because closing them felt worse. The water was ink-black shot through with gray light from the hole far above, shrinking as they sank, becoming a coin, then a fingernail, then a rumor.

And then the shrine appeared.

It rose out of the dark the way wrecks do in dreams — first a suggestion, then a shape, then suddenly all of it at once: a ring of standing stones on the lakebed, furred with pale silt, enclosing a small round building whose roof had fallen in long ago. Through the broken roof, faint light moved. And in the doorway of the little building stood a door — an actual door, wooden, upright, free-standing, absurdly intact, carved with spirals that Wren's grandmother might have made, if their grandmother had been a priest instead of a smuggler.

Words ran around the doorframe in the old alphabet. Even underwater, even half-drowned themselves, Wren could read them, because everyone could read these words; they were carved over every courthouse and garrison gate in the kingdom, worn smooth by ten thousand touching hands:

*TO PROTECT.*

The fragment hung in the water beyond the door. That was the only word for it. Not floating, not resting — hanging, the way a held breath hangs. It was a piece of writing, but not on paper or stone; it glowed faintly gold, letters without a page, curling and reforming like something alive. Like a vow that had been spoken so truly it never needed saying again.

Wren reached for it.

The water changed.

It wasn't gradual. One moment they were sinking through cold black nothing, and the next the water itself had become a window, and the window had become the worst day of Wren Calloway's life.

* * *

They were twenty-two years old again, in armor that still shone, kneeling in the rain outside a burning granary in the town of Harrow's Bend, and Ser Aldous Vey was screaming at them.

*The oath, Calloway. Say it back to me. SAY IT.*

*I can't—*

*You swore to protect them. All of them. Before the gods and the crown. Say. It. Back.*

And Wren — young Wren, stupid Wren, Wren with both oaths still whole inside them like two wings — looked at the burning granary, and at the crowd being held back by the other knights' spears, and at the single figure the king's men were dragging toward the wagons: a baker's son named Tam, seventeen years old, who had stolen a sack of seed grain because his family was starving, and whom the king's law said must hang.

*Protect them,* Ser Aldous said. *All of them. Or him? Choose, Calloway. The law says the boy hangs. Your oath says no one starves. You cannot keep both.*

And Wren had chosen.

They had drawn their sword on their sworn brothers. They had cut the ropes off Tam's wrists in front of forty witnesses. And then they had run — not fought, not stood, not made a stand of it — *run*, dragging the boy through the mud while the granary burned and the town screamed and their vows tore out of them like stitches from a wound. Because here was the truth Wren had never told anyone, the truth that exile had been built to bury: they could have fought. Forty knights against one. They might have died, but Tam would have lived, and the granary's people would have seen that a knight's oath meant something.

Instead Wren ran, because running meant living, and they told themselves mercy was the same as cowardice until the words wore smooth from handling.

The water held them there, in the rain, in the shame of it, and did not look away, and Wren remembered the instruction — *do not look away, what refuses to be seen drowns* — and forced themselves, sobbing bubbles into the black water, to watch every moment of the worst thing they had ever done.

When it finally released them, the fragment was in their hand.

They didn't remember taking it. It burned cold against their palm like a coin left in snow, and the rope was pulling, twice, urgently, and Wren kicked for the surface with lungs full of fire.

* * *

Hauling out onto the ice was less graceful than dying would have been. Wren came through the hole in a spray of black water, collapsed face-down on the frozen lake, and shook — not from cold alone, though the cold was trying to kill them, but from the rest of it.

Mirabel had them wrapped in the tent blanket before they could speak. She worked fast and wordlessly, rubbing warmth into their arms, and her hands were steady, and Wren thought absurdly: *she's copied ten thousand pages, of course her hands are steady,* and then thought nothing coherent at all for a while.

"Did you find it?" she asked finally.

Wren opened their fist.

The fragment lay in their palm on top of the blanket, glowing softly, letters turning like fish in a net. Up close it was beautiful, and it hummed — a low note Wren felt in their teeth, in the hollow place where their own oath had been, and the hollow place *ached* around it, the way a missing limb aches near a fire.

"To protect," Mirabel read. Her voice caught slightly. "One of three."

"One of three," Wren croaked. Their voice sounded scraped. "Two more lakes like that and I'll be a very relaxed person."

Mirabel laughed — a short, startled laugh, quickly bitten off, but a laugh — and then her expression shifted, and Wren saw her looking at them properly for the first time in three days. Really looking. With scribe's eyes.

"The water showed you something," she said. Not a question.

"It shows everyone something." Wren pulled the blanket tighter. "That was the price of admission. Worst memory, full performance, no refunds."

"What was yours?"

"Pass."

"Wren."

"Pass," they said again, but gentler, and Mirabel — to her credit — let it go, and began packing up the camp with the efficient motions of someone who understood that some doors stay shut until they don't.

* * *

They walked until noon, putting distance between themselves and the lake, and stopped in the lee of a granite ridge where a stand of black pines broke the wind. While Wren saw to the fire, Mirabel sat with the fragment balanced on her knee, copying its letters into her journal — because of course she was; the woman had ink in her blood — and her brow was furrowed in a way Wren had learned to read as trouble.

"It doesn't say much," she said at last. "Just the phrase. Over and over. *To protect.* But it's... it's not finished. Look—" she tilted the journal toward Wren. "The letters break off. Like half a sentence. Like someone tore it in the middle of a word."

"That's the point, isn't it? Broken vow, three pieces."

"Yes, but—" Mirabel chewed her lip. "A broken vow breaks at the weakest point. At the lie. So somewhere in this fragment, in the middle of *to protect*, King Aldric lied." She looked up at Wren across the fire. "Ten years ago, when he was crowned, he promised to protect his people. And somewhere in the promise, he failed before he ever began. The spirit got in *there*. In the crack."

Wren fed a branch to the fire and said nothing, because their chest hurt again, and because Mirabel was looking at them with those steady scribe's eyes, and because they knew — had known since the lake, had known since the moment the water released them — what was coming next.

"You said pass," Mirabel said quietly.

"I did."

"But I've been thinking about it all morning, and I keep coming back to something. When we met, you told me you broke your vow and fled the capital. Cowardice, you called it. Your word." She turned the journal page, slowly. "But the god chose an oathbreaker to guide his herald. Out of every mercenary and smuggler on these roads, he sent me to *you*. I didn't understand why. Until the lake."

"Mirabel—"

"The water showed me mine, too," she said. "While I waited. It showed me my father signing our debt-bond, and how I begged him not to, and how he did it anyway, and how I hated him for years and then found out he'd bargained away ten years of his life so Sefa wouldn't have to serve at all. It showed me that my anger was the coward's portion. Mine." Her voice was very even, the way it got when she was holding something heavy. "So I know what the water does. It doesn't show you the wound. It shows you the lie you tell about the wound."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope a bird complained about the weather.

"Wren," she said. "What did the water show you?"

And Wren Calloway, who had carried this story ten years like a stone sewn into their coat, looked at the fire, and looked at this stubborn, debt-bound, god-touched scribe who had burned her own contract in front of witnesses rather than let it fall to her sister, and heard themselves say — quietly, to the flames more than to her:

"A boy was going to hang for stealing bread. My oath said protect everyone. The king's law said hang him. I couldn't keep both, so I drew steel on my sworn brothers and cut him loose." A pause. "And then I ran. Didn't fight. Didn't stand. Ran. Dragged him through the mud while everything I'd sworn to protect burned behind me, and I told myself it was mercy. Saving one was better than saving none. But it wasn't mercy. It was arithmetic. One life weighed against my own, and I picked mine."

Mirabel was silent a long moment.

"That's not how the god tells it," she said finally.

Wren's head came up. "What?"

"When you were under. When you were — wherever you were." She hugged her knees, watching the fire. "I prayed. I know you told me not to pray to him about small things, and this was a small thing, but I was terrified, so I prayed, *let them come back up.* And he answered. He answers everything now, it's exhausting." A ghost of a smile, gone quickly. "And I asked him — why you? Why send his herald out with an oathbreaker? And he wrote it for me, in the frost on a stone. Four lines. Do you want to hear them?"

Wren's throat had gone tight. They nodded.

Mirabel recited it from memory, scribe-precise, each line laid down like a stone in a wall:

"*The knight who broke one vow to keep a greater one.*
*Who chose the boy over the law, the living over the letter.*
*Exile was the world's verdict. Mercy was the deed.*
*An oathbreaker who grieves the breaking loves oaths best of all.*"

The fire popped. Wren stared into it and found that their eyes were burning, and blamed the smoke, and knew Mirabel knew they were blaming the smoke, and decided to allow it.

"Ten years," Wren said hoarsely. "Ten years I thought—"

"I know what you thought."

"—and he's known. The whole time. The smug, frost-writing—"

"He also wrote," Mirabel added, "that if you repeat that opinion of him aloud, he will turn your next meal into gruel. His exact words were '*the smuggler's palate has grown proud.*'"

Despite everything — despite the lake and the cold and the stone in their chest finally, finally shifting — Wren laughed. It came out ragged and surprised, a sound they didn't recognize as their own, and Mirabel laughed too, and for a moment they just sat there in the pine-shadow laughing at a god, which Wren suspected was exactly what the god wanted.

Then Mirabel's smile faded, and she looked south, down the long white shoulder of the pass, and her voice dropped.

"There's smoke," she said. "Down-valley. See it? Too much for one farmstead." She was already reaching for her journal, already the scribe again, mapping threat the way she mapped roads. "And Wren — last night, while you slept, the frost wrote something else. On my boot, of all places. I almost didn't mention it."

Wren felt the warmth drain out of the moment like water through ice.

"Tell me."

Mirabel read from her journal, and her voice was careful and flat, the voice she used for numbers that didn't add up: "*The crown has noticed the first fragment is taken. What wears the king sends what the king once trusted. Ride until the road forgets you. They are faster than grief.*"

She looked up.

"What the king once trusted," she repeated. "Wren — what does the crown have, that the king once trusted?"

And Wren Calloway went very still beside the fire, because they knew the answer, and the answer had a face they'd shared bread with for six years, and a name they hadn't spoken aloud since the rain at Harrow's Bend.

"My sworn brother," they said. "Ser Aldous Vey. If the spirit Hollowed anyone it could find — it would have started with the knights who served closest. It would have started with him."

The smoke rose thin and patient down-valley, and the wind, when it came, smelled faintly of ash.
