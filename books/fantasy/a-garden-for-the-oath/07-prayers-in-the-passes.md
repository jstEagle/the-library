## 7. Prayers in the Passes

The first pilgrim found them at a waystation called the Crooked Ewe, three days after they left the drowned shrine, and Mirabel knew at once that something had gone terribly wrong with her life.

She knew it because the woman knelt to her.

Not bowed — knelt, both knees on the frozen mud of the yard, hands pressed together, face tilted up with an expression Mirabel had only before seen directed at altars. The woman was older than her mother would have been, if her mother had lived, and she was kneeling in sheep filth to a debt-bond scribe with ink stains on her cuffs and a blister from Wren's terrible cooking still healing on the roof of her mouth.

"Please," the woman said. "They said at the ford that the gods answer again. They said there's a herald walking the passes. Please, my son's been coughing blood since harvest. The temple wants four silver for a blessing and I have one."

Mirabel looked helplessly at Wren, who was leaning against the waystation's doorframe with their arms crossed and an expression that said, very clearly: *this is your fault, whatever it is.*

"I'm not—" Mirabel began. "I don't do blessings. I'm a scribe."

"You're the herald," the woman said, as though this settled everything, and perhaps for her it did. "The god of oaths walks with you. Everyone knows."

*Everyone knows.* Three days. They'd been on the road three days since the lake, since Mirabel had stood waist-deep in water so cold it burned and lifted a fragment of a king's vow out of a drowned shrine while Wren held her hand and pretended not to be afraid. Three days, and already the story had outrun them, growing in the telling like bread dough left by a warm fire. She was the herald. The old gods answered. There was a woman in the passes who could make prayers come true.

"Stand up," Mirabel said gently. "Please. You'll freeze your knees."

"Will you bless him?"

"I don't know how to bless anything. I know how to copy tithes ledgers." She crouched down to the woman's level, which felt less like kindness and more like surrender. "What's your name?"

"Hesta. Of Coldbrook."

"Hesta of Coldbrook, I will come and look at your son. That's all I can promise. Looking is free."

Behind her, Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

* * *

The son was worse than she'd feared. He was fourteen and gray-lipped, propped up in a bed by the peat fire, and when Mirabel took his wrist she could feel his pulse fluttering like a bird trapped under cloth. Consumption, or something wearing consumption's face. The kind of sickness the temple charged four silver for because four silver bought incense and incense bought time, and sometimes time was enough.

She sat by the boy's bed for a long while without doing anything at all, because the truth was she didn't know what *doing* would look like. The god had spoken through written vows. The god had shown her visions. Nowhere in any of it had there been instructions for sick children.

*I'm here,* said the presence at the back of her mind, the one she had stopped pretending wasn't there. It felt like standing near a large warm animal. Like the temple's great hearth on a winter morning. *Ask what you would ask.*

"You could just fix him," she whispered. "If you're a god. Couldn't you?"

*A god of oaths does not mend flesh. That was never my work.*

"Then what am I supposed to tell her? She knelt in the mud."

*You are supposed to listen. Listening is the oldest rite. Every vow begins with someone being heard.*

So Mirabel listened. She listened to Hesta talk about her son Tam until the candles guttered — about how he'd been strong as an ox before, how he carried the millstone up the hill alone when he was twelve, how he'd sworn on his grandmother's grave he'd rebuild the mill race before spring. And somewhere in the middle of the listening, Mirabel understood what the god meant, and her skin prickled.

"He swore," she said slowly. "On his grandmother's grave. To finish the mill race."

"Aye," Hesta said, wiping her eyes. "Foolish thing to swear, sick as he got. But he's stubborn."

"Has anyone finished it? The race?"

Hesta stared at her. "He's the only one who knows the channels. His father dug half of them before he passed."

"Then the oath isn't broken," Mirabel said. "It's waiting. It's a vow with no one to keep it." She turned inward, toward the warmth. *Is that it? Is that what you want me to do?*

*Vows are living things,* the god said. *A vow unkept does not die. It waits, and it weighs, and it calls to whoever might carry it. Bring me to it.*

She didn't know what "bring me to it" meant until she did it — until she took the boy's hand, and the scrap of charcoal she always carried, and wrote on the back of Hesta's palm, in letters too small to read but true enough to feel: *Tam of Coldbrook swears to see the mill race run. Who among us will carry it with him?*

Nothing happened. For a long moment nothing happened, and Mirabel felt the particular humiliation of a priestess whose god has stepped out, and then Hesta gasped. On her palm, the words were darkening, deepening, pulling tight like a knot drawn home. And Tam, in his bed, opened his eyes.

"They're coming," he said, confused and clear-voiced. "Someone's coming to dig. I can hear the shovels."

By morning, eleven men and women from Coldbrook had taken up the vow on Tam's palm — each one touching the words and speaking their name aloud — and the mill race rang with picks and shovels, and Tam sat wrapped in blankets in the sun watching the channel take shape, and whether he lived or died, the oath would be kept, and somehow that seemed to hold the fever back, or at least hold the despair. Hesta tried to give Mirabel her single silver piece. Mirabel gave it back and asked instead for bread for the road, which Hesta accepted as a fair trade with the grim satisfaction of a woman who has bargained well.

Wren said nothing until they were two miles out, walking the high track above Coldbrook, and then: "That was a miracle."

"That was listening."

"That was a miracle with extra steps." Wren kicked a stone off the path. "Do you understand what you've done? By tonight every village in these mountains will have heard that the herald healed a dying boy without taking a coin. We'll be lucky to walk ten paces without someone kneeling."

"I didn't heal him."

"No. You did something stranger, and people are going to like it even more." Wren's voice had an edge Mirabel hadn't heard since the lake. "When I served in the capital, I watched what happens when folk decide a person carries the divine. It doesn't end with bread for the road, Mirabel. It ends with someone building an altar out of your bones."

* * *

Wren was right, of course. Wren was always right in the ways Mirabel hated most.

Within a week the passes were full of them. Pilgrims came up the smuggler roads — roads never meant for pilgrims, roads Mirabel had mapped in secret margins for years, now trodden by the desperate and the hopeful — and they came looking for her. A shepherd whose flock was dying of some wasting illness. A bride whose groom had vanished over the border with her dowry. An old man who wanted, simply, to be forgiven for something he would not name, and wept so hard when Mirabel sat with him for an hour that she thought her heart would crack along its own fault lines.

She learned quickly, because she had no choice. She learned that most prayers were not requests for magic; they were requests for witness. People wanted their vows heard. A widow renewing a promise to her dead husband. Two brothers ending a feud by swearing jointly to tend their mother's field. A girl swearing to learn her late father's trade of joinery, in a world that told her girls didn't. Mirabel wrote their vows on scraps, on palms, on the flyleaves of her map-book where the smuggler routes used to live, and the god warmed each one, and the vow-carriers went home lighter, and word spread faster than ever.

But some prayers were not vows. Some were raw want, naked need, and those frightened her, because the god answered them differently — or rather, the god answered them *literally*, with the vast indifferent precision of a thing that had been listening to mortals for millennia and had forgotten, perhaps, what wanting felt like.

"We should ration them," Wren said one night, over a fire in a hollow beneath a leaning boulder. "The big ones. The ones that aren't oaths. You can't keep granting wishes, Mirabel. You're not a wishing well."

"I know that."

"Do you? Because yesterday you told the shepherd you'd ask about his flock."

"And the flock recovered."

"The flock recovered because you asked, and the god *did something*, and neither of us knows what, and the shepherd is now telling everyone the herald cured his beasts with a touch of her hand." Wren poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "Ration. Please. Before it goes wrong."

It went wrong nine days later, at a place called the Hanging Stair, where the smuggler road clung to a cliff face above a river gorge.

* * *

His name was Dannel, and he was a quarryman with a crushed hand and a daughter who needed the wages he could no longer earn, and he climbed the Hanging Stair for two days to find the herald because he had heard she could make prayers come true.

Mirabel tried. That was the thing she would carry afterward, the thing that woke her at night in the weeks that followed: she *tried*. She explained about vows. She asked him what he could swear, what he could carry, what part of his ruined life he could pick up and hold. And Dannel — exhausted, desperate, humiliated by his own hope — shook his head and said, "I don't want to swear anything. I want my hand back. I want my daughter fed. Can you do that or can't you?"

And Mirabel, tired beyond sense, heartsore from a week of other people's grief, looked at the mangled hand and thought — just for a moment, just one unguarded moment — *let it be whole.*

The god moved.

She felt it go through her like a current through water, felt the prayer lift out of her and become *answered*, and Dannel screamed. Not from pain. From the horror of watching his crushed hand straighten, knit, re-form itself into a working hand — fingers lengthening, bone rebuilding, flesh crawling back over knuckles that had been powder for a year. It was whole. It was perfect. It was a hand that had never been injured at all, smooth and unscarred, and Dannel stared at it and screamed again, because it was not *his* hand, it was a stranger's hand grafted onto his wrist, and something in him — some deep animal knowledge — recoiled from it.

"It's not mine," he kept saying. "It's not mine, it's not mine—"

"Dannel—" Mirabel reached for him.

He backed away from her. From *her*. The man had climbed two days through snow to kneel to her, and now he was looking at her the way people look at things that should not exist. He backed away along the cliff path, holding the new hand against his chest like something stolen, and the path was narrow, and wet with meltwater, and—

He fell.

It happened fast and slow at once, the way deaths do. One moment he was there, backing away, eyes fixed on her face; the next his heel found empty air and he tipped backward off the Hanging Stair into the gorge, and the last sound he made was not a scream but a kind of astonished breath, and then the river took him, and the silence after was the loudest thing Mirabel had ever heard.

* * *

They found his body downstream, caught in the shallows, and Wren waded in and pulled him out while Mirabel stood on the bank unable to move. The new hand was still perfect. Even drowned, even bloated, it gleamed like something carved.

"He asked," Mirabel said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "He asked for it. I only — I wanted it for him, and the god answered, and he *asked*—"

"Yes." Wren wrung out their sleeves, methodical, not looking at her. "He asked. And you answered. And now he's dead with a miracle on his arm."

"What was I supposed to do?"

"*Not that.*" Wren rounded on her, and their face was white, and Mirabel realized with a distant shock that Wren was afraid — truly afraid, for the first time since the lake. "You don't understand what you are now. These people think you're a door the divine walks through. Doors don't get to have bad days. Doors don't get to be tired and heartsick and careless. The moment you wanted something for him, you spent him. Do you see? Your wanting *is* power now. It has been since the temple. Maybe since always, and you just never had anything listening before."

Mirabel looked down at her own hands. Scribe's hands. Ink-stained, calloused from the pen, steady — they had always been steady, that was the one thing she'd been proud of, the steadiness. They were shaking now.

"I didn't mean it," she said. "I just thought it."

"Welcome to being believed in." Wren's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "Thoughts are load-bearing now. Get used to weighing them."

They buried Dannel above the gorge, in ground too stony for a proper grave, stacking stones over him because there was nothing to dig with. Mirabel wrote his name on a flat stone and set it at his head, and then, after a long moment, she added the only rite she had: *Dannel of the quarry swore to feed his daughter. Who among us will carry it?*

No one answered. There was no one there but her and Wren and the wind, and the vow hung in the cold air, unanswered, and Mirabel understood that this too was part of it — that some vows would wait forever, that some weights would find no carrier, that the god's warmth did not extend to the dead.

Except.

As they turned to go, the presence stirred — quiet, careful, the largest thing she had ever felt moving as gently as a moth.

*Mirabel.*

"Don't," she said aloud. "Don't tell me it was necessary. Don't tell me he's in a better—"

*I do not console. Consolation is a lie told quickly.* The warmth gathered itself, and when it spoke again there was something in it she had never heard before — something that, in a mortal, she would have called shame. *I am old, herald. Older than the temples that forgot me. When mortals pray to me, I hear the vow beneath the want. But you — you hear the want itself. You carry it. That is why I chose you, and it is why you must be more careful than I ever was, because I have broken kingdoms by answering honestly.*

She stood over the stone cairn a long moment. "Then teach me," she said. "Teach me to hear the vow beneath the want. Teach me to ration myself. Because there are hundreds of them on these roads, Wren says, hundreds more coming, and I cannot spend another Dannel."

*I will teach you,* the god said. *And you will hate some of the lessons. The first is this: the holiest thing a herald can say is no.*

* * *

She practiced saying no for the next six days, and it was the hardest discipline of her life — harder than the scriptorium, harder than the debt-bond, harder than holding her sister's face in her mind across the miles and not turning back. No to the merchant who wanted prosperity. No to the young man who wanted his brother raised from the dead. No, gently, firmly, over and over, redirecting each raw want toward the vow buried inside it: *what can you carry? What can you swear? Who will carry it with you?*

It worked. Gods forgive her, it worked — the pilgrims went home with vows instead of miracles, and the vows held, and the roads filled anyway, because hope travels faster than any rumor of failure.

It was on the sixth evening, camped in a hollow below the pass called the Shepherd's Tooth, that Wren came back from scouting the trail ahead and did not sit down by the fire. They stood at the edge of the light, and their face in the fireglow was the face of someone carrying news they wished they could put down.

"Mira," they said — and she noticed they'd started calling her that somewhere in the last week, and had decided not to examine why it made her chest ache pleasantly. "There's smoke in the valley ahead. A lot of it. And riders on the road below — a dozen at least, armored, moving in formation." They paused. "Their livery is black, and their faces are wrong, Mira. Smooth. Like wax that hasn't quite set."

Mirabel felt the god go still inside her — a vast, ancient stillness, like a forest when the deer all stop at once.

*Hollowed,* the presence said. *Knights whose vows it has eaten. It has found us.*

And behind them, far down the darkening road, the riders turned as one — all dozen heads rotating together, unhurried, like flowers finding the sun — and began to climb.
