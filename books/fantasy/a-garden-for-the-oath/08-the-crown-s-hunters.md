## 8. The Crown's Hunters

Wren Calloway had a rule about shrines: never sleep in one. Shrines attracted pilgrims, and pilgrims attracted questions, and questions were how you ended up on a bounty list. But the pass above Lake Verre had turned to ice-slick misery by midafternoon, and Mirabel's hands had gone the color of old candle wax, and so — rules being made for people with better options — Wren broke theirs.

The shrine was small, older than the temple at Thornfell by centuries if the worn carvings meant anything. A roof of slate, three walls, an altar stone some pious fool had once polished smooth enough to sleep on. Wren got a fire going in the lee of the doorway while Mirabel knelt before the altar, unwrapping the first fragment from its oilcloth like it was a sleeping infant.

"Stop staring at it," Wren said.

"I'm not staring."

"You've been staring for two days. It's a rock with writing on it."

"It's a vow." Mirabel didn't look up. "It's *to protect*, Wren. You can feel it. Like standing near a hearth."

What Wren felt was a headache starting behind their eyes, which was what they always felt when Mirabel talked about the fragments like they were pets. They fed the fire another branch and told themselves the tightness in their chest was just the altitude.

They'd been on the road eleven days now. Eleven days of Mirabel asking questions Wren didn't want to answer — about the capital, about the order Wren had sworn to and then abandoned, about why an oathbreaker who claimed to hate oaths could recite the Knight's Vigil word-perfect in her sleep. Wren had heard her do it. Murmured into the dark, half-dreaming, every line intact. It was the most damning thing about them, and Mirabel hadn't even meant it as evidence.

"You should eat," Wren said instead of any of that.

"In a moment. The god wants—"

"The god can want in line behind the stew."

That got a laugh out of her, brief and startled, and Wren pretended not to be pleased about it. That was the trouble with heralds. They laughed at your jokes and then you started wanting them to.

Mirabel finally came away from the altar and took the bowl Wren handed her, sitting close to the fire with her boots nearly in it. Outside, the light was failing in that particular mountain way — all at once, like a door closing.

"Wren," she said, after a while. "Do you ever think about who else is looking for these?"

"Constantly. It's my favorite hobby."

"I mean it. The god said the crown would send someone. He said it plainly, and I keep waiting for us to talk about it, and you keep making stew instead."

"Stew is a legitimate strategic activity."

"*Wren.*"

They sighed and set down their own bowl. "Fine. Yes. I think about it. Here's what I know: the thing on the throne has eaten ten years of vows. Every knight who swore fealty to Aldric, every guard, every magistrate who swore to uphold his law — those oaths went somewhere, and the somewhere is the crown. Which means whoever comes for us won't be soldiers exactly. They'll be—" Wren searched for the word and hated the one that arrived. "*Empty*. People-shaped, but the vows that held them together are gone. Eaten. What's left walks and fights and follows orders."

Mirabel had gone still. "You've seen one."

"No."

"You have. You flinch when you say 'empty.'"

Wren looked at the fire. The honest answer was yes — they'd seen one, years ago, from a distance, in the capital, a palace guard standing motionless at his post through an entire winter night without once shifting his weight, and everyone saying *devout man, so devoted* — but the honest answer also led somewhere Wren refused to go tonight, so they said, "Eat your stew," and mercifully, Mirabel let it lie.

She fell asleep first, curled against the altar stone with the fragment wrapped again in its cloth and tucked against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Wren sat up with their back to the doorway, sword across their knees, and watched the moon come up over the pass.

It was wrong, that moon. Everyone knew it now; even the farmers in the valley towns had stopped pretending otherwise. Thirteen turns where twelve belonged. The thirteenth hung fat and pale and slightly too fast, hurrying toward something, and Wren found that looking at it too long made their teeth ache.

Somewhere out there in the dark, things that used to be people were walking toward this shrine. Wren could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound.

They didn't wake Mirabel. Some kindnesses are just cowardice with better posture.

* * *

They came at dawn, and they came quietly, which was the worst part. No horns, no war-cries. Just six shapes cresting the ridge above the shrine, moving with the terrible economy of puppets whose strings are very well managed.

Wren saw them first because Wren hadn't slept. Six riders, no — five riders and one walker, the last one coming up the scree on foot where a horse couldn't hold, and even at that distance there was something off about how they moved. Too smooth. Horses don't walk downhill like that unless they're being ridden by something that doesn't care if the horse breaks its leg.

"Mira." Their voice came out flat and cold, the old drill-yard voice, and she was awake instantly — eleven days on the road had taught her that much. "Behind the altar. Take the fragment. Don't run unless I say run."

"What is—"

"Now."

She went. Wren stepped out into the blue morning and put a hand on their sword and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding for eleven days.

Five horses. One figure on foot. And the figure on foot wore armor Wren recognized — not the style, the *piece*. A left pauldron chased with a running stag, dented at the edge where a mace had caught it at the field of Harrow Ford. Wren had been standing next to that pauldron at Harrow Ford. Had laughed at the dent. Had said, *you great lucky ox, that should have taken your arm off.*

"Dain," Wren said, and their voice cracked in the middle of the name like bad pottery.

The figure stopped thirty feet away. Up close, the wrongness resolved into detail: Dain Ashford, sworn brother of the Order of the Stag, taller than Wren remembered or maybe just straighter, because grief makes everyone else shrink. His face was Dain's face. His eyes were Dain's eyes. But nothing behind them was home. It was like looking at a house after a fire — every wall standing, every window empty.

"Herald," Dain said. His voice was level as a frozen lake. "Surrender the fragments."

"Hello to you too."

"You are Wren Calloway." Not a question. An inventory. "Oathbreaker. Exiled. Your vows were forfeit; there was nothing in you worth taking." Something flickered across the burned-out house of his face — fast, gone. "I argued against that assessment. Once."

Wren's stomach dropped through the mountain. "Dain. Dain, listen to me. Whatever's left in there — the part that argued — grab onto it. Do you remember Harrow Ford? Do you remember the stag? You said if we both survived the war you'd—"

"We survived the war." Flat. "Then the king changed, and the oaths changed, and I am *so much less tired now*, Wren. Do you understand? I haven't been tired in four years. I don't dream. I don't grieve. I don't want anything except what I'm sent for." A pause, and then, horribly, gently: "It doesn't hurt. That's what nobody tells you. Being eaten doesn't hurt at all."

Behind Wren, he heard Mirabel make a small sound. He didn't turn around. Turning around would have meant letting Dain see his face, and his face was currently committing treason against everything he'd built his exile out of.

"You came for the fragments," Wren said. "Not for us."

"The fragments. The herald, alive, if convenient. You—" Dain's head tilted, and for one vertiginous second the flicker came back, wide and human and drowning. "There's a standing instruction about you. From the crown itself. It says: *the oathbreaker remembers what the others forgot. Let the oathbreaker watch.*"

"Watch what?"

But the flicker was gone, shuttered, and the other five had dismounted and spread out with the unhurried coordination of a single mind wearing five bodies, and there wasn't time for answers anymore.

What followed was short and ugly and not, Wren would insist later, remotely heroic. They fought the way you fight when you're outnumbered three to one and protecting someone: dirty, fast, and backward. Wren took a cut along the forearm from a knight who never changed expression, not even when Wren's pommel cracked him across the jaw. Mirabel — bless her stubborn, terrified heart — didn't run. She stood behind the altar with one hand pressed flat to the fragment and *shouted*, and whatever she shouted in that language the god wrote in, it hit the Hollowed knights like a wall of noon. Three of them staggered. Two dropped entirely, kneeling, hands over their ears, mouths open on screams that made no sound.

Dain didn't stagger. Dain walked through it, slow, like a man wading upstream, and reached Wren at the exact moment Wren decided — with a clarity that would keep them up for weeks — that they could not kill him.

So they didn't. They swept his legs, put him down hard on the altar steps, and ran.

* * *

They ran for two hours, down a goat track Wren knew from their smuggling days, through a streambed, over a ridge, until the shrine and its horror were long behind and Mirabel's breath was tearing in ragged sobs and Wren finally called a halt under an overhang thick with pine.

Mirabel put her hands on her knees and breathed. Wren bound their forearm, badly, watching the treeline.

"He knew me," Mirabel said at last, when she could. "Your sworn brother. He said your name like it was a fact in a ledger."

"He's not my sworn brother anymore. The thing ate him."

"It didn't eat all of him." She straightened. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and aimed directly at Wren, which was a dangerous place to be standing. "He argued for you. He *remembered arguing for you*. Wren, if the crown kept that — if it keeps little pieces, scraps, the way the god keeps the fragments—"

"Then it's keeping them to use, not to be kind." Wren heard their own voice go hard and hated it. "Mira, listen to me. That thing doesn't leave scraps out of mercy. It leaves hooks. It showed him your face for a reason. It wanted you to see him hesitate. It wants you believing there's a person in every one of those shells worth saving, because a herald who stops to save every Hollowed knight on the road arrives at the capital too late to save anyone."

"And you?" Quiet now. "Is that what you believe? Because you're the one who wouldn't kill him. I saw your face, Wren. You'd have died before you drew on him."

Wren sat down heavily on a root. The pine smell was sharp and clean and did absolutely nothing.

"When I broke my vow," they said, because apparently today was the day for it, "it wasn't cowardice, whatever the tribunal said. I was ordered to do something in the king's name that I couldn't — the year the crown started changing him. And breaking it didn't hurt either, at first. That's the secret nobody tells you about oathbreaking. It feels like *relief*. Like setting down a weight you didn't know you were carrying. For about a month I thought I'd finally gone free." They turned the sword-belt buckle over in their fingers, around and around. "And then the dreams started. The vow didn't leave, Mira. It just went somewhere I couldn't reach it, and it's been scratching at the door for eight years. Dain's in there the same way. Scratching. And the thing wearing the crown knows it, and it *left him in there on purpose* because it knows I'll never be able to—"

Their voice quit on them. Mirabel sat down next to them, not touching, which was somehow worse and better.

"So that's why you really fled," she said softly. "Not the tribunal. You were afraid of what you'd become if you stayed."

"I was afraid I'd already become it." Wren laughed, and it came out wrong. "Eight years running contraband through goat passes, telling myself I was free, and the whole time the crown has been keeping a file on me. *Let the oathbreaker watch.* Watch what, Mira? It knows where we're going. It knew before we did. Why send six knights to take fragments it could trap us for anywhere between here and the capital?"

Mirabel went very still beside them. Wren watched the thought arrive in her face the way you watch weather come over a mountain — the shadow moving upslope, inevitable.

"Because it doesn't want the fragments," she whispered. "It wants them *brought to it*. All three pieces together, in one place, carried by the herald it chose to let live." She looked at Wren, and her eyes were wide and bright with a fear that had teeth. "We're not fleeing it, Wren. We're doing its errand. Every mile we've walked, we've been carrying its coronation right to its front door."

Above the pines, through a gap in the cloud, the thirteenth moon hung pale in the morning sky — fat, and hurrying, and closer than it had been yesterday.

Wren looked at it for a long time.

"Well," they said. "Then we'd better make sure we arrive angry."
