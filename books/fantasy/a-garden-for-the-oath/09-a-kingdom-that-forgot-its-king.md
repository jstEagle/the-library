## 9. A Kingdom That Forgot Its King

The valley announced itself by smell before anything else — woodsmoke and bread and, beneath it, the green rot-sweetness of orchards heavy past harvest. After nine days of high passes where the only smells were cold stone and Wren's questionable cheese, Mirabel stopped walking just to breathe it in.

"Careful," Wren said behind her. "Towns that smell this nice are usually hiding something. Taxes, mostly."

"It's called *prosperity*."

"It's called a granary that hasn't been raided lately." But Wren came up beside her anyway, and even they went quiet when the town came into view.

It lay cupped in the valley floor like something held: slate roofs gone mossy at the edges, three church spires — no, two spires and one bell tower, and one of the spires was crowned with a king's face carved in pale stone, weathered but lovingly maintained, someone had climbed up there with a chisel within living memory to recut the smile. Fields stitched down to the river in neat gold-and-green squares. Laundry on lines. Children shrieking over some game involving a stick and what appeared to be an enormous turnip.

And above it all, on a shelf of rock where no sensible person would build anything, a manor house with banners flying. The king's colors.

Mirabel felt her stomach drop through the mountainside.

"He's not supposed to be *here*," she whispered. "The capital is six days east. He's supposed to be — enthroned. Devouring. On schedule."

"Well," said Wren, "either your god's map of the kingdom is as old as he is, or His Majesty has taken up country holidays." They squinted against the afternoon light. "Or it isn't him at all. Kings travel by proxy all the time. A banner, a steward dressed in royal blue, half the realm runs on the rumor of the king being present."

But the god had been quiet since the pass — a silence Mirabel had learned to read as *watching*, the way you can feel someone standing behind a door — and now, faintly, she felt the pressure ease into something like attention. Like leaning forward.

They went down into the town, because the alternative was another night eating Wren's cheese, and because if the king was here, Mirabel wanted to see him. She told herself it was reconnaissance. She was getting better at telling herself things.

* * *

The inn was called the Loyal Hart, which should have been warning enough. Inside, it smelled of onions and tallow and honest sweat, and every surface that could hold one held a portrait of King Aldric.

Not the official portraits either — Mirabel knew those from the temple's copies, the stern profile, the crown rendered in careful gold leaf. These were different. A charcoal sketch above the hearth of the king kneeling in a muddy field talking to a farmer who was laughing. A child's painting, framed in twigs, of a tall figure waving. An embroidered cushion — somebody had spent *months* on this cushion — showing the king cradling a lamb, with the words GOD BLESS OUR GENTLE KING worked underneath in wool so fine it looked printed.

"Gentle," Mirabel murmured, reading it for the fourth time.

"You keep saying that like it's a foreign word," said the innkeeper's wife, appearing beside her with a tray and the particular cheerfulness of a woman deciding whether these travelers were trouble. She was broad and flour-dusted and missed nothing. "Well, it is a bit foreign, I suppose, if you're from the capital way. Folk out there like their kings sharp-edged."

"We're pilgrims," Wren said smoothly, before Mirabel could say something true. "Walking the shrines. We heard His Grace was in the valley and hoped to — glimpse him. For blessing."

The innkeeper's wife softened immediately, the way people did around genuine faith, which was exactly why Wren deployed it like a tool. "Aye, well, you've come at the right time. He walks the market square tomorrow morning, first light, same as always when he stays at Highmoor. Doesn't speak much, mind." Her voice went tender rather than apologetic. "Never has. But he *sees* you, do you understand? My Bess fell sick two winters back, fever wouldn't break, and I stood at the road as he passed and I didn't ask for nothing, I just — looked at him. And he looked back. Right at me. Like he was carrying my worry for me a moment." She sniffed, briskly wiped her hands on her apron. "She broke the fever that night. Coincidence, says the physician. I say our king took it into himself a while. That's the kind he is."

"Kind," Mirabel repeated. Her mouth had gone dry.

"Supper's stew or pie," the woman said, and left them.

Wren leaned close, dropping their voice under the inn's noise. "You're making a face."

"I'm trying not to make several faces at once."

"The fever story could be coincidence."

"The fever story could be *feeding*." Mirabel turned her tankard slowly on the table. "That's how it works, Wren. It doesn't devour him all at once — the god showed me. It takes him in pieces, and each piece it wears out among the people, spending him like coin. His gentleness might be real. It might be his. And it might be the thing wearing him, handing out small mercies so the whole valley loves its own consumption."

"That's a grim way to look at a nice cushion."

"Wren."

"I know. I know." They rubbed their jaw. "Here's what bothers me. If the spirit wanted to hide, it's doing a poor job. If it wanted to be loved — well." Their eyes flicked up to the embroidered lamb. "Maybe that's the point. Maybe love is just another kind of gate, and it's teaching the kingdom to hold the door open."

Outside, the evening bells rang, and the whole town seemed to pause mid-motion — doors opening, faces turning toward the manor on the hill, hands touching foreheads. Not fear. Mirabel had seen fear of kings; she'd grown up on stories of the old tyrants. This was something else, and it was worse, because it looked exactly like love.

* * *

She couldn't sleep, so near midnight she climbed the stairs to the inn's narrow upper gallery, where a window faced the manor rock, and sat with her knees drawn up and her satchel of fragments against her chest.

Two pieces of the king's vow, wrapped in oilcloth against her heart. *To protect.* *To serve.* Cold as lake water and war dust, respectively, though neither had any temperature at all when she thought about them directly. The third waited somewhere east, in a chapel ruin, and after it — the eclipse, the throne room, whatever terrible arithmetic the god was keeping.

Below, the manor windows glowed. One of them, high in the eastern wing, was lit though every other light had gone out, and Mirabel found herself watching it the way you watch a wound.

*I am going to save you,* she thought at it. *Whether you want it or not.*

Which was, she realized, sitting there in the dark with her arms around stolen holiness, a strange thing to think about a stranger. About anyone. The god had shown her the vision — the crown like a ring of teeth, the man inside going gray and thin as paper — and she had accepted the mission the way you accept a debt, without ever once asking the man himself.

The lit window changed. A shadow crossed it. Paused.

Mirabel's breath stopped.

The shadow stood at the glass a long moment, and though the distance was too far, too dark, for any sane person to claim they'd made eye contact across it — she felt *seen*. Felt, distinctly, the sensation of being read like a page.

Then the candle guttered, and the window went dark, and the town below slept on around its gentle king, and Mirabel sat very still with her heart hammering out a rhythm she almost recognized: *he knows we're here.*

*He asked for help once,* whispered a voice at the edge of her mind, dry as old ink. It might have been the god. It might have been her own hope, wearing his silence like a coat. *Remember that, herald. Whatever else the valley believes — remember that he asked.*

* * *

Market morning came up silver and cold, and the whole town turned out for the king's walk.

Mirabel and Wren stood at the crowd's edge, hooded, anonymous among farmers and bakers and children hoisted onto shoulders. She had expected spectacle — guards, cordon, distance. Instead there were perhaps six men-at-arms, relaxed, more honor guard than wall, and the king walked among his people slowly, unhurried, stopping when someone stepped forward.

He was tall. That struck her first — taller than the portraits suggested, and thinner, worn like a coat hung too long on its peg. His hair had gone gray at thirty-five, or whatever age he was; the chronicles disagreed and the portraits flattered. He wore no finery, just good wool in the royal blue, and the crown —

The crown was not on his head.

It rode instead in a plain wooden box carried by a servant walking three paces behind, and Mirabel felt her skin prickle from nape to heel. The thing that ruled the kingdom through Aldric's flesh, the devourer, the ten-year shadow on the throne — and here it traveled shut away like festival plate, brought along the way you'd bring a relic to reassure the countryside.

Or the way you'd bring a leash. So the dog couldn't run.

Aldric reached the baker's stall and stopped. The baker — a huge man with flour in his eyebrows — presented a loaf with both hands, solemn as a priest, and the king received it and bowed his head over it, and did not eat it, and set it gently on a nearby table, and moved on. At the well, an old woman gripped his sleeve and talked at him for a full minute — Mirabel caught fragments: a son lost to the mines, a debt, a prayer — and the king listened with his whole body, head tilted, eyes fixed on her face, and said nothing at all.

Nothing. In ten years, the stories agreed: the Gentle King spoke rarely, softly, sometimes never. The court called it piety. The valley called it grace.

Mirabel watched the old woman walk away comforted, watched the next petitioner already shuffling forward, and felt the ground tilt under everything she believed.

*Empty-eyed,* the god had named him. *Hollowed by degrees.* But the eyes that swept the crowd were not empty. They were — searching. There was a hunger in them that had nothing to do with appetite, a desperate, patient scanning, the look of a man reading every face in a crowd because he's looking for one face in particular.

Then his gaze found hers.

Across forty feet of market morning, across bread-smell and bells and a hundred believing hearts, King Aldric looked directly at Mirabel Vane, temple scribe, apostate, herald of a forgotten god, thief of two-thirds of a broken vow — and his expression did something complicated and quick, like lightning behind cloud. Recognition. Grief. And threaded through both, unmistakable even at that distance:

*Relief.*

His lips moved. No sound came, or none that carried. But Mirabel read lips the way other people read books — ten years of copying had given her that, at least — and the shape of the words was simple, and it undid her.

*Help me,* he mouthed. Or possibly: *Help us.* The plural mattered. She'd swear later, staring at the inn ceiling through the small hours, that the plural mattered.

Then a bell rang twice from the manor, and the servant with the box stepped forward, and something in the king's face closed like a door, and he turned and let himself be walked home, gentle and silent and beloved, while his people waved and wept happy tears and one of them, a girl of maybe eight, ran after him pressing a flower into his unresisting hand.

* * *

"Tell me again why we're leaving," Wren said, as they took the east road out of the valley that afternoon, moving fast, hoods up, because Mirabel had insisted and then refused to explain herself until the town was out of bowshot.

"Because I saw him and he saw me and if I stay in that valley another day I will do something catastrophically stupid."

"Such as?"

"Such as walk up to the manor and put my hands on that box and find out what happens when a herald touches the leash." She was walking too fast and knew it. "Wren, they love him. Do you understand? It's not fear, it's not ignorance, it's *love*, real love, the fever woman, the baker, the child with the flower — and every bit of that love is a brick in the thing's wall. How do I tell a whole valley their gentle king is a feeding trough? How does anyone? They'd burn us before they thanked us. They burned the last prophet who said an uncomfortable thing about the crown, and he had witnesses and miracles."

"Saint Ellery," Wren said automatically. "Ash Wednesday. Everyone knows." Then they were quiet for a few paces, boots crunching frost. When they spoke again, their voice had that flat, careful quality it got whenever the capital came up. "You know what I can't stop thinking about? Back in the capital, before I — before. Courtiers used to joke that the king had gone quiet. *Safely* quiet, they'd say. A king who doesn't speak can't contradict you." Their jaw tightened. "I thought it was politics. It never occurred to me it was *symptoms*."

"And now?"

"Now?" Wren glanced sideways at her. "Now I keep thinking about the plural. *Help us.* You're sure?"

"No. Yes. I don't know. I've been sure and unsure of it in alternating hours since dawn." Mirabel pushed her hair out of her face. "But I keep coming back to the window, Wren. Midnight. He stood at the glass and he looked straight down at me in a dark courtyard where nobody could have seen me, and he wasn't surprised. He was *waiting*. As if somewhere inside the hollow, the man keeps count of the days, and mine finally came up."

Ahead, the road bent east toward the low hills, toward the ruined chapel, toward the third fragment and the price it would ask — a price the god had described in words that still itched under her skin like a splinter: *it cannot be taken, little scribe. Only given.*

Behind them, the valley lay golden and smoking and contented in the afternoon light, a kingdom's worth of love piled around a hollow man like flowers around a grave.

"Whatever's in that box," Wren said quietly, "it let him stand in a market square with two vow-fragments fifty feet away. It knows what we carry. Your abbot's letters proved it knows everything else." They flexed their sword-hand, a nervous habit Mirabel had catalogued long ago. "If I were a devouring spirit with a deadline, I'd have taken those fragments off you in your sleep. Instead it paraded its host past you and let him beg."

"So either it's arrogant—"

"—or it wants us to finish the job," Wren finished. "Bring all three pieces right up to the throne, nicely gathered, nicely assembled, ripe for the taking." They exhaled. "Lovely. Either way, the road east just got shorter."

Mirabel looked back once, only once, at the manor on its rock, at the single high window already catching the sunset.

*Hold on,* she thought, not knowing anymore whether she addressed the man or the god or the frightened thing in the box between them. *However many turns are left. Hold on.*

And far behind them, faint as a bell rung underwater, she felt the old god answer — not with words, but with the pressure of a vast and weary attention, folding around her like a cloak, like an apology, like a promise kept badly for a very long time and about, at last, to be kept.
