## 10. The Second Fragment

The war-crypt of Vaelmoor did not announce itself. That was the first thing Wren disliked about it.

They had climbed two days through a pass that Mirabel swore existed on no temple map — which meant, in Wren's experience, that it existed on every smuggler map and the temples simply hadn't thought to steal one yet — and arrived at a hillside that looked like every other hillside: grey stone, stubborn grass, wind that seemed personally offended by travelers. No gate. No marker. Not so much as a warning carved into the rock, which any decent crypt would provide as a courtesy.

"It's here," Mirabel said for the fourth time. She had her hand flat against the hillside, fingers spread, the way she did when she was listening to something Wren couldn't hear. "The god says it's here."

"The god," Wren said, "has a very loose relationship with doors."

"He says the door is for the dead, not the living." She paused. "He also says he's sorry about that, and that he recognizes it's inconvenient."

"Tell him I appreciate the apology and would have preferred a latch."

Mirabel laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and Wren felt it land somewhere under their ribs where they kept the things they didn't look at directly. They'd been on the road together long enough now that Wren had catalogued her laughs the way other people catalogued coin: the polite one she gave to strangers, the dry one she gave to Wren's worst jokes, and this one, rare, unguarded, worth more than all the rest combined.

Wren was not thinking about that. Wren was thinking about the crypt. Strictly the crypt.

"Here," they said, kneeling at the base of the hillside where the grass grew wrong — too green, too orderly, like soldiers standing at attention even in death. The stones beneath were fitted without mortar, and between them ran a seam of old iron, black with age. "Smuggler trick. Anything buried that important gets a back way in, because the front way is always watched." They pressed their palm flat and pushed their will into the seam the way you'd push a shoulder against a stuck door. "This isn't watched. It's *waiting*. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"A watcher wants to catch you. A waiter wants you to come in."

Mirabel considered this. "That's worse."

"Yes," Wren said. "It is."

* * *

The back way in was a crawl-space barely wide enough for Wren's shoulders, sloping down through cold earth until the air changed — went still and mineral, the way air does underground when it has been underground longer than anyone living can remember. Then it opened, all at once, into a hall.

Wren had seen crypts before. This was not a crypt. Crypts held coffins. This held an army.

Rank upon rank of soldiers stood in the dark, carved from the same grey stone as the hillside — or had once been men and been made stone, Wren couldn't tell, and wasn't eager to learn. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one facing forward toward a dais at the far end of the hall, each one holding a shield before them and a sword point-down between their boots, and each one wearing, faintly, around their throats, a ring of light that might once have been gold.

"They're standing guard," Mirabel whispered. Her breath made fog. Everything down here made fog; the place was cold enough to make a person reconsider their life choices. "Even now. After everything."

"That's what soldiers do," Wren said. "It's the one thing we're good at."

"You were a soldier?"

"I was a knight. Which is a soldier who's been given a vow and told it makes them better than everyone else." Wren moved carefully down the aisle between ranks, close enough to see the faces now. They were serene. That was the horrible part. Every face wore the same expression — calm, attentive, patient — the expression of someone who has been told to wait and intends to wait forever. "We should find the fragment quickly."

"Why? They're not moving."

"No," Wren said. "But I've stood in ranks like this. And I know what happens when the order comes."

The dais held a bier, and on the bier lay a general — larger than the others, his stone hands folded over a stone sword, his face the only one in the hall that wasn't serene. His brow was furrowed. His mouth was set. He looked, Wren thought, like a man who had died mid-argument with heaven.

And above him, floating in the dark like a coal refusing to go out, hung the second fragment.

It was small. That always surprised people, Mirabel had said — the pieces of the king's vow were small, because vows are small when spoken and enormous when kept. This one was a strip of metal no longer than a finger, curved like a piece of a crown, and it burned with a light the color of candleflame through amber. Words crawled across its surface, too small to read from here.

Wren reached for it.

The hall woke up.

Not all at once — that would have been merciful. First came the sound: a low hum, like a struck bell heard through a mountain, rising out of the floor. Then the nearest soldiers turned their heads. Only their heads. Stone grinding on stone, slow as glaciers, until a hundred blank faces were looking at Wren with expressions that were no longer serene but *attentive*, and there is nothing in this world more frightening than being attended to by the dead.

"Wren," Mirabel said.

"I noticed."

The hum resolved into words. Not one voice — hundreds, layered, speaking in ragged unison like a choir that had forgotten its conductor:

***WHO COMES TO THE GENERAL'S REST?***

Wren had faced down Hollowed knights, bandit lords, a customs official with a grudge and a crossbow. None of it had prepared them for the specific horror of several thousand stone soldiers asking a question in perfect broken harmony.

"Travelers," Wren said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt, which was the whole trick of knighthood, if you wanted the truth of it. "Pilgrims. We've come for what the king left here."

***THE KING LEFT NOTHING HERE,*** said the dead. ***THE KING WAS TAKEN FROM HERE. WE WERE SENT TO GUARD WHAT REMAINED, AND WHAT REMAINED WAS US.***

The words landed strangely. Wren looked at the ranks again — really looked — and saw what they'd missed. The soldiers weren't guarding the general. They were guarding *themselves*. Each shield turned inward. Each formation closed around its own center. An army that had been ordered to protect the realm and, finding the realm gone silent, had done the only thing left: protected the only part of the realm it could still reach, which was each other.

"Oh," Mirabel breathed beside them. "Oh, they don't know. They've been down here since—" She pressed a hand to her mouth. "Since the coronation. Since the crown ate their oaths. They've been standing at attention for ten years waiting for orders that never came."

The hum shifted. Grew quieter. Almost — Wren would have sworn it, though they'd have denied it later — almost pleading.

***WE ARE STILL SWORN,*** said the dead. ***WE ARE STILL SWORN AND THERE IS NO ONE LEFT TO BE SWORN TO.***

* * *

They retreated to the mouth of the passage to talk, because Wren had learned that you never plan a desperate thing inside the hearing of the thing you're desperate about.

"They won't let us take it," Mirabel said. "I asked the god. He can't override them. Their oaths are theirs — eaten, twisted, but theirs. Even he won't touch another soul's vow without leave."

"So we fight our way out with a scrap of metal and several thousand extremely motivated corpses."

"I don't think they can be fought. I think they can be *outlasted*, maybe, by people who don't need to sleep, which we are not."

Wren leaned against the cold stone and looked up the crawl-tunnel toward the distant grey smudge of daylight, and thought about how easy it would be to just leave. Walk away. Find some other road, some other life, let the crown finish eating the king and let the world keep turning, because it always did. They'd been running from oaths for ten years. One more door left unopened wouldn't kill anyone who wasn't already dead.

Except.

Except the dead were asking. That was the thing Wren couldn't shake. All those serene stone faces, and underneath the serenity, a question ten years old: *is anyone coming? does anyone remember us? did we matter?*

Wren knew that question. Knew it the way you know a scar — intimately, in bad weather, at three in the morning. They had spent a decade telling themselves they'd broken their knightly vow because the vow was rotten, because the order was corrupt, because exile was freedom. And some nights, camped under indifferent stars, they'd caught themselves reciting the old words anyway, silently, like a man biting a sore tooth. *I swear my blade to the defense of those who cannot defend themselves.* Ten years of insisting the vow meant nothing, and the vow had sat in their chest the entire time, patient as stone, waiting to be picked back up.

"There's a way," Wren said slowly. "But you're going to hate it."

"Those are usually your best ideas. What is it?"

"They want orders. Someone sworn to give them. And I—" Wren exhaled. "I'm still technically a knight. You can't unmake a vow by breaking it. Breaking it just means it lives in you wrong, like a bone that healed crooked. The order stripped my title, burned my name off the rolls, but the vow itself—" They tapped their own chest. "Still in here. Crooked, but in here."

Mirabel stared at them. "You want to command them."

"I want to *answer* them. There's a difference. Commanding is telling people what to do. Answering is telling them why it mattered." Wren rubbed their face with both hands. "Ten years ago I stood in a hall in the capital and I broke my vow in front of the whole order. Loudly. On purpose. Because I found out what we were defending — who we were defending — and I couldn't stomach it. And the price was exile, and I paid it, and I told myself it was fair." They dropped their hands. "What I never did was say the vow again. Mended. Corrected. Said it right this time. Because saying it would mean admitting it was worth saving."

"And now?"

"Now there are a few thousand soldiers down there who kept their vow perfectly and got punished for it, and one oathbreaker standing up here who broke his and got to walk away free." Wren laughed, and it came out rough. "The god of oaths must be laughing himself sick."

"He doesn't laugh," Mirabel said. Then, after a moment: "Well. He says he finds things 'structurally amusing.' Does that count?"

"It counts."

* * *

Walking back down that aisle was the longest walk of Wren Calloway's life, and they had once walked out of the capital in chains while children threw things.

The ranks watched them come. The hum rose to meet them, wary now, hungry now:

***YOU RETURN. WHY?***

"Because you asked a question," Wren said, "and somebody ought to answer it."

They stopped at the foot of the dais, below the burning fragment, below the general's furious stone face, and turned to face the ranks. Several thousand pairs of empty eyes. Somewhere behind them, Mirabel's breathing had gone very quiet.

"My name is Wren Calloway," they said. "Knight-Candidate, Third Circle of the Order of the Silver Bough — or I was, before they scraped me off. I broke my oath ten years ago. Publicly. Gladly, some days." A ripple went through the hum — disapproval, or recognition, hard to say. "You've been down here keeping faith with a kingdom that forgot you. So before anything else: I'm sorry. That's not a vow. That's just the truth, and you deserve it."

Silence. The good kind, the listening kind.

"You were sworn to protect. And the thing you were protecting got eaten by the crown you were also sworn to serve, and nobody told you, and you've stood here in the dark ever since doing the only honorable thing left, which is holding the line even when the line holds nothing." Wren's voice cracked on the last of it, and they let it crack, because these soldiers deserved honesty more than polish. "I know what a crooked oath feels like. Mine's been sitting in my chest for ten years like a splinter. And I could stand here and pretend breaking mine makes me your equal, but it doesn't. You kept yours. I didn't. That's the whole ledger."

The hum had gone utterly still.

"But a vow isn't a chain," Wren said. "I learned that late, and I learned it the stupid way, and I'm going to spend the rest of my life paying tuition on it. A vow is a promise about who you intend to be. And a broken vow isn't the end of it — it's just the vow, waiting, asking whether you mean it. Whether you ever meant it." They drew a breath that hurt going in. "So here is my answer. Here is my vow, said again, said *right*, in front of witnesses — one witness, anyway, and a few thousand of the best soldiers this kingdom ever produced."

They went to one knee on the cold stone floor, the way they had at nineteen, the way they had never imagined doing again, and spoke the words they had broken:

"I swear my blade to the defense of those who cannot defend themselves. I swear my word to the truth, even when the truth costs me. I swear my service to the realm — not to its crown, whatever wears it, but to its people, every one, the forgotten ones first." Their voice shook. They let it. "And I swear — I swear *this time* — that when the vow and the powerful disagree, I will choose the vow. Even if the powerful is me. Especially then."

They bowed their head. "I mean the mending of it. Every word. Ask me anything."

For a long moment, nothing. The dark held its breath.

Then the hum began to change. The ragged unison smoothed, note by note, voice by voice, hundreds of broken harmonies finding each other again after ten years apart, until the sound filling the hall was not a question anymore. It was a response. The old call-and-answer of the watch, older than the order, older than the crown:

***WE HEAR THE OATH. WE SEE THE KNEELING. RISE, LITTLE KNIGHT. THE LINE IS HELD. THE WATCH MAY REST.***

All across the hall, stone ground against stone — gently, this time, almost tenderly — as thousands of soldiers, one by one, finally, *finally*, grounded their swords and lowered their shields. Some of them, Wren would have sworn, were smiling.

On the dais, the general's furrowed brow eased. His stone mouth relaxed into something like peace. And the fragment above him drifted down, slow as a falling leaf, and settled into Wren's open palm, warm as a held hand.

Words circled its surface, small and bright: ***to serve — not the throne; the ones beneath it.***

"Well," Wren managed, standing on legs that had opinions about the whole enterprise. "That went better than the last time I said it."

Beside them, Mirabel was crying and pretending very hard not to, which Wren graciously decided not to mention, mostly because their own eyes were behaving no better.

"Don't," she warned.

"I said nothing."

"You were going to say something insufferable."

"I was going to say," Wren said, tucking the fragment safely away, "that for an oathbreaker, I make a rather decent knight."

She laughed — the real one, the expensive one — and it echoed down the hall of the resting dead like a blessing.

They were nearly to the crawl-tunnel when the hum rose one last time. Faint. Urgent. Not the soldiers' voices at all, but something threaded through them, something cold and many-mouthed that had been listening from far away:

***SO THAT IS WHERE MY FRAGMENTS SLEEP,*** it said. ***THANK YOU, LITTLE KNIGHT. YOU HAVE SAVED ME SUCH A TEDIOUS SEARCH.***

The temperature of the hall dropped like a stone. In the distance, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of iron-shod hooves on mountain rock — many horses, riding fast, riding toward Vaelmoor.

"Hollowed," Mirabel whispered. "They know. Wren — *they know*."

Wren looked back once at the sleeping ranks, at the general resting easy at last, and made a quiet promise to the dark: *hold the line a little longer. We'll draw them off.*

Then they ran for the daylight, with the second fragment warm against their heart and the third one — the one that cost the most — waiting somewhere ahead.
