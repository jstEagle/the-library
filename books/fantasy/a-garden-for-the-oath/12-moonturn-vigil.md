## 12. Moonturn Vigil

The storm came down on them like a debt collector — sudden, unreasonable, and demanding everything.

They had meant to cross the high shoulder of Mount Vessin by midday. Instead they were huddled in a shepherd's hut barely larger than Wren's opinion of themselves, wind screaming through the gaps in the dry-stone walls while snow piled against the door until the door was more suggestion than fact.

"I've slept in worse," Wren said, from where they'd wedged themselves into the corner that didn't leak.

"You have not."

"I slept in a ditch outside Carrowmere once. It had excellent drainage."

"That's the opposite of what you want in a ditch."

Wren considered this. "It was a very well-designed ditch."

Mirabel laughed despite herself — despite the cold, despite the way the light through the single window kept doing something wrong. That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing: the light. It was noon. The sun sat fat and pale behind the cloud cover, and yet beneath it, faint as breath on glass, hung the thirteenth moon.

It had no business being there. Twelve moons turned the year, each in its season, and the thirteenth existed only in old calendars and older warnings — the moon that came between, the moon of the eclipse. It should have been invisible until the final turn. But there it was, a grey coin pressed against the sky at noon, and every time Mirabel looked at it directly, the back of her teeth ached.

"It's early," she said. "The god said its power would peak at the eclipse. Not now."

"Then it's peaking twice." Wren fed another knot of scrub pine to their small fire. They'd found the hut stocked with wood, which either meant kind shepherds or dead ones, and neither of them wanted to investigate which. "Things that eat don't usually stick to a schedule when the eating gets good."

The third member of their party had not spoken since the moon appeared. Sefa sat by the fire with her arms around her knees, staring at it the way one stares at a snake in the road — fixed, unwilling, unable to look away. Since the border, since the betrayal and the forged writ and the abbot dying with his vow clutched to his chest like a shield that hadn't worked, Sefa had been quiet in a way that worried Mirabel more than any argument could.

"Sefa," Mirabel said gently. "You should sleep."

"I'm not tired."

"You've been watching that moon for four hours."

"Somebody has to make sure it doesn't move." Sefa's voice cracked halfway through, and she laughed at herself, a wet, unhappy sound. "That's mad, isn't it. I know it's mad. It just — looking away feels like agreeing to something."

Mirabel crossed the hut and sat beside her. For a moment her sister let her lean close, shoulder to shoulder, the old geometry of childhood. Then Sefa pulled back — not unkindly, but firmly, the way you set down something fragile because your hands are shaking.

"I'm sorry," Sefa said. "I keep thinking about the abbot. About how he held his little paper vow and the god just — couldn't. All those years I thought faith was a wall. Turns out it's paper."

"Paper burns," Mirabel agreed. "But paper also carries words. And words are the only weapon we've got that the crown can't eat."

Sefa looked at her then, really looked, and something in her face softened toward the edge of tears. "When did you get wise? You used to eat candle wax on a dare."

"I still would," Mirabel said solemnly. "On a sufficiently large dare."

That earned half a laugh, and half was enough. Mirabel settled her sister under both their cloaks near the fire, and Sefa — exhausted past pride — went under quickly, her breath evening out, though even asleep her brow stayed furrowed, dreaming of moons that moved.

Wren watched from across the hut, sharpening a knife they did not need to sharpen.

"She'll come right," Wren said quietly. "People do. Slowly, sideways, but they do."

"When did you become an optimist?"

"I'm not. I'm a gambler. There's a difference, and it's mostly that gamblers are honest about the odds." The knife whispered along the stone. "The odds on your sister are good. The odds on us—" they nodded at the window, at the grey coin of the moon "—I wouldn't put coin on it."

"Then why are you here?"

Wren didn't answer for a long time. The fire popped. Outside, the wind changed pitch, dropping suddenly, as if the whole mountain had leaned in to listen.

"Because somebody has to watch the moon not move," Wren said at last, and Mirabel understood that was as close to a confession as she was going to get tonight.

* * *

She woke inside someone else's memory.

There was no transition — no falling, no darkness. One moment she was dreaming of the scriptorium, ink-stained and warm; the next she stood in a hall so vast its ceiling was lost in gold-veined dark, and the air tasted of cedar smoke and iron and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun.

She knew immediately whose memory it was. Not because anyone told her. Because the whole place *felt* the way the god felt when he spoke through written vows — that weight, that patience worn thin as old cloth.

A king stood at the center of the hall.

He was young. That struck her first, how young — younger than Wren, younger than she'd imagined kings could be. He wore a circlet, not a crown, plain silver, and he was laughing at something a woman in armor had said, his head tipped back, utterly without guard. Around him, courtiers blurred at the edges the way dream-people do, but he never blurred. Memory does not blur the person it is keeping.

*You,* said a voice, and it was not sound exactly, it was pressure, it was meaning arriving fully formed. *You are in my remembering.*

"The thirteenth moon," Mirabel said. Her voice sounded small in all that gold. "This is what happens when it hangs at noon?"

*When its power rises, I grow heavy with what I was.* A pause, long as a winter. *Forgive me. I did not mean to pull you in. But you sleep beneath my sign now, herald, and dreams leak downhill.*

"Whose hall is this?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, unseen rafters creaked like a ship.

*Mine,* the god said at last, *was not always the name of oaths.*

The scene shifted around her, the way water shifts when you stir it. The young king was older now — a beard, lines around his eyes, the circlet gone and a true crown in its place, tall and golden. Mirabel's stomach dropped, because she knew that crown. She had seen it in the first vision, worn by Aldric, eating him bite by bite.

But on this king it was new. Bright. Unfed.

*I served him,* the god said. *Before oaths had my name, I was smaller. A god of promises kept between friends. Hearth-god. Kitchen-god. The god who witnessed when two people swore to split the harvest and mean it. He wore the crown the smiths made him, and it was only metal, and he asked me to bless it, and I did, gladly, because I loved him.*

"Loved," Mirabel repeated. Present tense or past?

*He was at war. The kingdom bled, and he came to me — not in glory, in exhaustion, in a stable, smelling of horse — and he said: I cannot hold all these vows alone. Every promise I make binds me twice, once to the deed and once to the man I must become to do the deed. Help me carry them. And I—*

The memory shuddered. In the great hall, torches guttered. The courtiers had stopped moving entirely, frozen mid-bow, and the king stood alone now, and the crown on his head had begun — subtly, terribly — to gleam.

*I wove his coronation vow into the crown itself,* the god said. *Protect. Serve. Love. Three threads of iron and gold, so that no future wearer could take the throne without taking the vow with it. It seemed elegant. It seemed safe. A vow that could not be forgotten, because it was welded into the crown of the realm.*

"And instead—"

*Instead I built a vessel.* The words came flat and terrible. *Vows are alive, herald. Did you never wonder why I am a god at all, and not merely a custom? Every promise ever kept feeds me; every promise broken starves a piece of me into the world to wander. A vow sealed into an object does not sleep. It waits. It grows hungry for a mouth to speak it. And when Aldric's line failed, when a usurper took the throne wearing my beautiful crown—*

The vision tore open. She saw it: a man in stolen regalia, swearing the oath with a sneer, and the three threads waking, wrapping, *pouring themselves into him* — protect, serve, love, twisted inside out by a heart that meant none of them. Protect became possess. Serve became consume. Love became hunger. And out of that mispronunciation, that sacred text read backwards, something coalesced. Something with no name except what it did.

The devouring spirit. Born of her god's own craftsmanship.

Mirabel sat down. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended their participation. She sat on the floor of a thousand-year-old memory in a shepherd's hut, and she looked up at where the god's presence filled the gold dark like weather.

"You made it."

*I made it.*

"You've known. This whole time. Every fragment we dig out of the mountains, every mile — you've known the thing we're fighting is yours."

*Yes.*

"Why didn't you tell us?" Her voice rose, and somewhere far away, she heard Wren stir, heard Sefa whimper in her sleep. "We've given things up for this. I burned my contract. Sefa turned on everything she knew. Wren walked back toward the city that exiled them. And you've been carrying this — this *shame* — and saying nothing?"

*What would telling you have bought?* The question wasn't defensive. It was genuinely, heartbreakingly puzzled. *Would you have refused the fragments? Let the king die? No. Knowledge of my guilt changes nothing except how you look at me, and I find—*

The god stopped. Gods, apparently, can be embarrassed.

"Find what?" Mirabel said softly.

*I find I could not bear it,* the god admitted. *Ten centuries I have been the witness of every honest vow in this world. I know precisely what it costs to confess. I have watched ten thousand people stand before their neighbors with the truth in their mouths and shake. And I, who require it of everyone, could not do it myself.* A pause. *You asked why I chose a scribe as herald. Debt-bound, overlooked, mapping roads in margins no one reads. It was not only that you could hear me. It was that you knew what it is to serve something that does not serve you back, and stay honest anyway. I thought — if anyone might forgive what I did, it would be someone who understands that love makes fools of the careful.*

Mirabel sat with that. Around her the frozen court waited, patient as stone, and the young king's laughter echoed faintly from some earlier layer of the memory, still preserved, still kept, the way the god kept everything.

"What happened to him?" she asked. "Your king. The one you loved."

*He grew old. He died well, in his bed, surrounded by grandchildren who adored him, having ruled wisely for forty years. The crown passed to his son, who spoke the vow honestly, and then to his granddaughter, who spoke it honestly, and for six generations the crown was only a crown.* The grief in the god's voice was vast and slow, geological. *And then a man with murder in his heart took it by force, and my three threads — my beautiful threads — poured into a vessel shaped wrong, and I could do nothing. I am a god of oaths, herald. I cannot break my own work. I can only wait for someone to unmake it from outside. I waited nine hundred years. And then a pilgrim crawled up your mountain, dying, and prayed to a god everyone had forgotten, and a girl with ink on her fingers answered him.*

"That wasn't answering," Mirabel said. "I just — heard him."

*No one hears the forgotten unless they have been forgotten themselves.*

Outside the memory, the storm howled. Inside it, Mirabel got to her feet, brushed off her skirts, and did something she suspected no worshipper had ever done.

She put her hand flat against the air, against the presence that filled the golden dark, the way you'd steady a trembling animal.

"You failed," she said. "You loved someone, and your love broke something, and you've been carrying it alone for nine hundred years because you couldn't say it out loud." She swallowed. "I know exactly what that costs too. So here's what we're going to do. Tomorrow the storm breaks, and we climb Vessin, and we find the second fragment, and we unmake your crown — your mistake and mine to fix, since I'm your herald and heralds share the work. And when it's done, you're going to stand in front of whatever's left of your worshippers and tell them the truth yourself. That's not a request. It's a vow. And I know for a fact you can't refuse those."

Silence. Long enough that she began to wonder whether gods could be struck dumb.

Then, quietly, with something in it she had never heard from the divine before and suspected few ever had:

*Yes. I vow it.*

"There," Mirabel said. "Was that so hard?"

*Excruciating,* said the god, and the memory folded around her like a book closing, and she was falling—

* * *

She woke with her cheek against cold flagstone, having apparently rolled clear off her bedding in the night. Dawn light — real dawn, storm-broken, clean — slanted through the window, and the thirteenth moon had faded to a ghost, a watermark of itself.

Wren stood at the doorway, testing the weather with one hand. "Snow's stopping. We can make the pass by midday if we leave within the hour." They glanced back at her. "You were talking in your sleep. Arguing with someone. You won, I think. You had the tone of someone who's won."

"I made a god confess," Mirabel said, sitting up, working feeling back into her arm.

"Ah." Wren nodded with complete seriousness. "One of those nights."

Sefa was already awake, packing, and when Mirabel caught her eye, her sister smiled — small and real, the first smile since the border. "You look different," Sefa said. "Lighter. What happened?"

Mirabel opened her mouth to answer, and found she couldn't — not the shape of it, the gold hall, the young king laughing. Some memories belonged to the dreamer alone. But the feeling remained, warm as banked coals: compassion, fierce and unsentimental, for a being ten centuries old who had been afraid to speak.

"A friend needed to talk," she said. "He's better now."

She was lacing her boots when the god's voice arrived — not in her ears, but in the vows around her, in the scratched promise carved into the hut's lintel by some shepherd generations ago: *good rain next year or my name isn't Tam*. Only she could read what flickered beneath it.

*Herald. A warning, before you climb. The crown knows where you go. It has always known. But this morning its knowledge has grown teeth — I feel it gathering its Hollowed, and it is not sending them to the shrine ahead of you.*

"Then where?" Mirabel breathed.

*To the chapel,* the god said. *Where the third fragment sleeps. Where you must go last.*

*It means to meet you there.*
