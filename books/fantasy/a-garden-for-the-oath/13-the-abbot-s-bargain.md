## 13. The Abbot's Bargain

The storm broke at dusk, and the Hollowed knights came with it.

Wren smelled them before they saw them — or rather, smelled what they weren't. The pass above the shepherd's hut where they'd holed up carried every scent a mountain night ought to carry: wet stone, pine pitch, woodsmoke from their fire, Mirabel's terrible attempt at stew burning in the pot. And threading through all of it, a nothing. A hole shaped like people.

"They're here," Wren said, and put down the spoon they'd been using to mock Mirabel's cooking, which was a shame, because the mockery had been going well.

Mirabel looked up. The god-light had been living under her skin for weeks now, a faint gold along her forearms like she'd been dipping them in honey, and it flared when she understood. "How many?"

"Four." Wren moved to the shuttered window and eased it open a finger's width. Below, in the blue last light of the storm, four riders picked their way up the switchback road. They rode badly — that was the worst of it. Hollowed knights were not undead, exactly; they were *emptied*, vows eaten out of them like the meat out of a walnut shell, and what remained didn't remember how to sit a horse properly. One of them kept listing left and correcting too hard. "And a fifth rider behind them. Not Hollowed."

"Who?"

Wren watched the fifth rider come around the bend. Old, robed, riding a mule because of course he rode a mule, sitting straight as a liturgy. Wren's stomach did something complicated and unwelcome.

"The abbot."

* * *

They had argued about Sefa for three days by then, which was two days longer than Wren thought the argument deserved and one day shorter than Mirabel wanted.

Sefa Vane had caught up with them at the border town of Hallowmere, breathless and snow-crusted and so obviously sent by the temple that even the god had apparently decided not to dignify it with comment. She'd brought bread, a change of clothes for Mirabel, and a smile that didn't reach her eyes, and she'd said, "I came because I'm your sister," in the exact tone of someone reciting a line she'd been made to memorize.

Wren hadn't trusted her from the first hour. This was, they would reflect later, deeply unfair, since Wren trusted almost no one on principle and had been right about perhaps half of them. But there it was: the girl slept too lightly, asked too neatly worded questions, and once — only once, when she thought nobody was watching — Wren had caught her standing at the window counting riders on the road below, lips moving.

So when the abbot came up the mountain with four emptied knights behind him, Wren did not have to be a genius about arithmetic.

"He traded you," Wren told Mirabel quietly, at the window. "Her for you. The temple hands over the herald, the crown-spirit leaves Thornfell's granaries and tithes alone. That's the shape of it."

"You don't know that."

"Sefa counted the road for an hour yesterday. I watched her do it."

Mirabel's face went through several expressions in quick succession, none of them settled — hurt, denial, and underneath both, horribly, agreement. "She's sixteen."

"She's a debt-bond's daughter. She knows exactly what things cost. It's how we were all raised, isn't it?" Wren meant it gently. It didn't land gently. Nothing landed gently anymore; the mountains had knocked the soft edges off everything they said to each other, and what remained was true, which was worse.

But Mirabel only set her jaw in the way Wren had learned meant she'd already decided something and was merely waiting for the world to catch up. "Then I'll ask her myself. Before they arrive. And whatever she says—" she stopped. Started again. "Whatever she says, I'll believe her once."

"That's very generous."

"It's very practical. If she lies to me once, I'll know what her lies look like, and I won't be fooled twice." Mirabel turned from the window, gold flickering along her wrists. "You always assume the worst first."

"I'm usually alive afterward," Wren said, "which suggests the method works," and Mirabel almost smiled, which was, Wren was beginning to suspect, the entire reason Wren said things like that.

* * *

The knights did not come up to the hut at once. That was the other thing about the Hollowed: they had no urgency left in them. Hunger, cold, duty — these had been worn away along with everything else, and what drove them now was the crown's will alone, moving them like water moves a millwheel. They halted in the yard below, four shapes in gray-lacquered armor gone dull as old pewter, and stood. Waiting. The snow gathered on their shoulders and did not melt.

The abbot climbed the last stretch alone.

He knocked. Of course he knocked. Father Ellery Vance had been abbot of Thornfell for thirty years, and Wren — who had never liked him, who had spent exactly one disastrous week under his roof years ago before being expelled for cheating at dice with the tithe-collectors, a story Mirabel still found far funnier than Wren considered appropriate — knew that he would knock. Men like him believed doors mattered.

Mirabel opened it.

"Child," the abbot said. His voice was warm. His voice had always been warm; it was the warmth of a hearth in a room where the chairs faced away from you. "You look well. The mountains agree with you."

"Father." Mirabel's voice was careful. Behind her, Wren leaned against the wall with their arms crossed and their knife loose in its sheath, purely decorative, purely a habit, purely a thing they did. "You've brought company."

"The temple's protection," the abbot said, "which I am also offering to you. That is why I've come." He stepped inside without being invited, because men like him believed rooms mattered too, and surveyed the hut: the low fire, the map-scrapes pinned to the beam, Sefa rising from her cot with her hands knotted in her apron. His gaze rested on Sefa for half a heartbeat, and Wren saw it — saw the flicker, the accounting, the little ledger opening and closing behind his eyes.

"You shouldn't have come," Sefa said suddenly. "Father, you shouldn't—"

"Hush, child." Kindly. Terribly kindly. "Your sister has been led astray by a false god and a criminal. I've come to bring her home."

"I'm not astray," Mirabel said. "I'm exactly where I'm supposed to be. That's the entire problem, from your point of view."

The abbot sighed, and sat down at their table as if it were his own scriptorium desk, and folded his hands, and Wren recognized the posture from a decade ago and hated how well they remembered it. This was the sermon posture. This was the posture he took before telling you the thing you already knew was coming.

"Mirabel. You are twenty-three years old, bound by a contract your father signed, raised in our care, fed from our stores. Everything you are, the temple made." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it crueler. "This thing that speaks to you — I have read the old texts. I know what answered the pilgrim in my own chapel. The gods of oaths were bound away for good reasons. They are not kind. They are *contracts*, and contracts do not love the people who sign them."

"And the crown?" Mirabel asked softly. "Do the texts say what wears the king?"

The silence stretched. In the yard below, the four knights stood in the falling snow, patient as fenceposts.

"The crown is order," the abbot said at last. "The crown is peace. Ten years without war, child. Ten years of full granaries and quiet borders. Whatever sits beneath it, the kingdom eats because of it." He unfolded his hands. "I have made an arrangement. The Hollowed take you — only you — and Thornfell keeps its tithing rights, its lands, and its people. Your sister's bond is dissolved. Her debt, your debt, all of it, paid in full." He turned his warm gaze on Sefa. "That is the bargain. No one else need suffer. Not even you, truly — the crown does not kill what it takes. It only... uses."

"It empties," Wren said, from the wall.

The abbot didn't look at them. "The oathbreaker may leave. Its grievance is not with you."

"Its grievance is with everyone who ever swore anything," Wren said. "It just gets to me last."

* * *

Later, Wren would try to reconstruct exactly how the next few minutes went, and never quite manage it. Memory, they decided eventually, was like weather in the passes: everyone agreed afterward on the broad shape of the storm and nobody agreed on when it started.

What Wren remembered clearly was Sefa's face.

Because here was the thing Wren had gotten wrong, sitting at windows counting riders and building cases in the dark: Sefa had not been spying happily. Sefa had been sixteen years old and terrified, holding the only lever she had — information — and pulling it toward herself not to betray her sister but to keep anyone else from pulling it first. Wren knew that trick. Wren had invented that trick. There was a particular misery in being the youngest person in a room full of people deciding your family's fate, and Wren had worn that misery like a coat for years, and had failed, watching Sefa stand in the lamplight with her hands knotted in her apron, to recognize it on someone else.

The abbot finished speaking. The bargain lay on the table between them, tidy as a signed page.

"No," said Sefa.

Everyone turned.

"No," she said again, louder, and her voice cracked in the middle, which made it better, not worse. "You can't — you don't get to *spend* her. I carried your letters, I did what you asked, I sat in that freezing hut for a month writing down everything she did, and I told myself it was to keep her safe, and maybe it was, but *this*—" She pulled something from inside her coat. A scroll, heavy paper, sealed. "I wrote this. Three nights ago. I stole the seal blank from your saddlebag while you were at evening prayer, because you pray the same way every single night, Father, you've prayed the same way for thirty years, and I figured if anything in this whole miserable kingdom was predictable it was *you*."

The abbot rose halfway out of his chair. "Child—"

"It's a writ of passage." Sefa's hand shook. The scroll shook with it. "Temple authority, countersigned, granting safe conduct to Mirabel Vane and companions through all temple-held lands, under sanction of the Synod. It's a forgery. It's a *terrible* forgery, the counterseal is upside down, I did it by candlelight, but you know what? Nobody reads seals upside down. Nobody has ever once checked." She thrust it at Mirabel. Her chin was trembling. "Run. Run the moment we're outside. The knights move slow in snow — I've watched them, they're like furniture walking. Take the east cut past the cairn field, it's too narrow for horses, I mapped it coming up."

"You mapped it," Mirabel said faintly.

"I'm *your sister*." And there it was, finally, the real face under the spy's face: furious, tear-streaked, sixteen, and absolutely certain. "Who do you think taught you to draw maps?"

* * *

The abbot recovered faster than Wren expected. To his credit — and Wren was surprised to find credit available — he did not call the knights in. He stood there in the lamplight with his forged-writ-holding, seal-stealing, faith-breaking prisoner-of-a-hostage, and he looked old, suddenly, all thirty years of office settling on him at once like snow off a roof.

"You understand what you've done," he said to Sefa. Quietly. Not unkindly. That was the horror of him, Wren thought; he would go to whatever came next genuinely believing himself merciful. "Your bond stands. Your sister is apostate. And I—" he closed his eyes briefly "—I must give them what they came for, or the arrangement dies, and the crown turns its attention to Thornfell. Four hundred souls, child. Orphans. The infirmary."

"Then let it turn," Sefa said. "Let it look at us and see people who didn't feed their own to it."

"You speak like someone who has never buried a famine year."

"And you talk like someone who thinks arithmetic is the same thing as faith!"

The words hit the room and rang. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

And the abbot — Father Ellery Vance, thirty years at his desk, keeper of tithes and ledgers and the great smooth machinery of Thornfell's survival — did something Wren did not expect. He reached into his robe and drew out a small wooden tablet on a cord. His vow. Every cleric of the old rite carried one: their ordination oath, carved in their own hand, renewed each dawn. His was worn nearly featureless from thirty years of thumbs.

"I took an oath," he said, "to protect this house and everyone in it. By any means." His thumb moved over the worn carving, back and forth, back and forth. "Everything I have done — the bonds, the bargains, this — has been that oath. I have never broken it. Not once. Do you understand? Thirty years, and I have never once—"

The door burst open.

Not the knights. The knights, Wren realized with distant absurdity, were still standing in the yard like fenceposts, because the crown had not yet spoken to them, because the crown was waiting, because the crown was patient in the way that only hungers are patient.

No — what came through the door was the *cold*. A pressure, a presence, pouring down off the mountain on the storm wind, and Wren knew it instantly, had felt it across a frozen lake and in a war-crypt and once, terribly, in a dream: the attention of the thing that wore the king.

It spoke through the four knights below, all together, one voice out of four throats, flat and vast:

**THE BARGAIN IS REFUSED. THE HERALD COMES TO US ENTIRE — OR WE COME FOR THE HOUSE THAT HIDES HER.**

And the abbot lifted his vow-tablet like a shield.

That was the image Wren kept. Not the fighting, because there wasn't any — there was no time for fighting, only for Mirabel grabbing Sefa and Wren kicking open the back shutters into the drifts. What Wren kept was smaller and worse: an old man in a doorway, snow swirling in around him, holding a piece of carved wood in front of his heart with both hands, the way a man holds a shield, the way a man holds a door shut against a flood.

He believed it would work. That was the unbearable part. Thirty years of oaths kept, every dawn renewed, every thumb-pass earned, and somewhere in the deepest ledger-room of himself, Father Ellery Vance had balanced the books and found that faith plus obedience equaled safety, and he stood in the cold with his arithmetic held up like a shield, and he said — Wren heard him say it, over the wind, clear as a bell:

"I have kept my oath. *I have kept my oath.*"

And the god — Mirabel's god, the god of oaths, the god who lived in every written vow and answered prayers in dead pilgrims' mouths — the god could not save him.

Wren understood it a moment before it happened, and understood why: the god kept oaths. He honored them. He was, if nothing else, a being of perfect contract. And the crown-spirit was also, in its way, a being of contract — it ate vows, it was owed vows, and the abbot's arrangement had been offered and refused, and refusal had terms, and terms were terms. Two great engines of promise, grinding against each other, and between them one old man with a wooden tablet and a lifetime of kept faith, discovering at the end that keeping an oath and being saved by it were never the same transaction at all.

The light went out of the doorway.

Not dramatically. The lamplight simply seemed to fail all at once, and the four knights in the yard turned — all together, like weather vanes — and began to walk away down the mountain, unhurried, their purpose served, and behind them in the doorway of the shepherd's hut the abbot sank down slowly to his knees, and then further, until he was folded on the floor like a robe dropped from a hook, and the tablet slipped from his hands and skidded across the boards and came to rest against Wren's boot as they ran.

Wren looked back once, from the edge of the trees. The hut stood dark. The snow filled the yard. Somewhere in there lay thirty years of perfect faith, and nothing anyone could do about it, and the god's silence where a rescue should have been, and Wren thought, with a bitterness that surprised even them: *so that's what it costs. That's what it costs to keep every promise you ever make.*

* * *

They ran east through the cairn field, Sefa's narrow cut, too tight for horses, exactly as promised. Snow came down thick enough to hide the moon, which was a mercy, because the moon was wrong lately — a thirteenth turn hanging in skies that had no business holding it, fat and pale and counting down.

They didn't stop until the stars wheeled past midnight. Then, in the lee of a split boulder, Mirabel built a small fire with hands that shook, and Sefa sat down hard in the snow and finally, finally cried — the loud, ugly, honest crying of someone who has been brave much longer than is reasonable.

Mirabel held her. Over her sister's shoulder, she looked at Wren, and her eyes were wet and furious and gold.

"He kept his oath," she said. "His whole life. Every dawn. And it bought him nothing."

Wren crouched, fed a stick to the fire, and turned the abbot's tablet over in their fingers. The carving was worn to ghosts. But hold it to the flame at the right angle, and the oldest line still showed, the ordination vow itself, cut by a young man's hand thirty years ago:

*I will keep this house. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs.*

"Maybe it bought more than nothing," Wren said slowly. "Maybe it just got paid to the wrong account." They looked at the fire. "Or maybe that's the whole joke, Mirabel. Maybe oaths aren't shields at all. Maybe they're debts — and somebody, someday, collects."

The god-light stirred under Mirabel's skin, faint and unreadable, and far behind them, down the dark of the pass, something that wore a crown turned its hunger toward the capital — and toward the eclipse, now only days away.

"We ride at first light," Wren said. "We're out of time."
