## 14. Roads Only She Knows

The first thing the Hollowed knights taught her, in their terrible patient way, was that roads are a kind of lie.

A road says: *here is where people go.* A road invites pursuit. A road has milestones and waystations and innkeepers who will sell your description for a copper and a kind word. For six years Mirabel Vane had drawn roads — honest ones, temple-approved ones, the tithing routes and pilgrim ways of the Thornfell valley — and in the margins, small as mouse-footprints, she had drawn everything else.

Now she was running down one of those margins.

"Left," she said, and left they went, off the logging track and into a fold of the hills so narrow that the birches brushed both of Sefa's shoulders at once. Behind them, faint as a headache, came the sound of hooves on the road they'd abandoned.

"That's not a path," Sefa said. "That's a rumor."

"It's a cut," Mirabel said. "Charcoal-burners use it in autumn. It comes out above the mill at Harrow Ford."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I drew it." She didn't explain further. There wasn't time, and there was also the small stubborn pleasure of watching her sister's face do what it was doing now — recalibrating ten years of big-sister-shaped assumptions. The temple scribe who inked tithes and apologized to furniture had apparently been keeping a second life in graphite all along.

Wren brought up the rear, walking backward half the time, which should have looked ridiculous and instead looked like a person who had learned never to trust the space behind them. "How many cuts like this?" they asked.

"In the Thornfell ranges? Forty. Maybe fifty."

"Fifty."

"Some overlap." She ducked under a branch. "Smugglers don't file their routes with anyone. They learn them by walking them, and when they die, the knowledge dies with them. Except—" she tapped her own temple, "—I used to buy drinks for dying smugglers."

"You *what,*" said Sefa.

"It's called fieldwork."

* * *

They made the mill at Harrow Ford by dusk, crossing the weir on stones only Mirabel knew were stable, and climbed out the far side while the miller's dog barked itself hoarse at nothing. By full dark they were in high pine country with the valley glowing behind them like a dropped coal, and Mirabel finally allowed herself to look back.

Three lights on the road below. Not moving fast. Not needing to.

"They're not chasing us," Wren said quietly. They had come up beside her without sound, which remained deeply unfair. "They're herding. The Hollowed don't tire, don't eat, don't sleep. Why would they run? They know where we're going."

"The ruined chapel," Mirabel said. "The third fragment."

"They've known since the lake shrine. Probably since before we did." Wren said it flatly, but their hand had gone to the fragment of *to protect* inside their coat, the way other people touched a wound. "So the question isn't whether we outrun them. It's whether we can be worth catching."

"That's a horrible question."

"I have a lot of those."

Mirabel looked at them — at the sharp profile against the last light, the scarred knuckles, the whole architecture of a person built for leaving — and felt something turn over in her chest. "Then let me ask a different one," she said. "At the shrine. Under the ice. When the water showed me my worst memory, you already knew what mine would be before I said it. You knew my face. You knew my name before I gave it."

Wren went very still. In Mirabel's experience, stillness in Wren Calloway was louder than shouting.

"The god sent you to me," she said. "He showed me your road. He drew it himself, in every vow in the temple. But that's not how you knew me, is it?"

"No," Wren said.

"How long?"

Wren took a breath that seemed to cost money. "Since the lake."

* * *

They made camp in a charcoal-burner's hollow — an old ring of blackened stones, a lean-to that some anonymous smuggler had roofed with bark and maintained out of professional courtesy. Sefa fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, which Mirabel chose to find endearing rather than insulting. The thirteenth moon rose wrong, as it always did now, hanging fat and pale in a sky that kept trying to pretend it had twelve moons and a sensible calendar.

Wren sat across the coals, sharpening a knife they did not need to sharpen.

"Talk," Mirabel said.

"Charming herald."

"Wren."

They set the knife down. "You want the version where I say I recognized the vision because I fled the capital, saw the truth of the crown, put it together. That's true. It's also not the whole of it." The firelight moved on their face. "When the water showed me the day I broke my vow — you came up out of it after. Not before. After. And you looked at me like—" they stopped, jaw working, "—like someone reading a page she'd written herself."

"I don't remember—"

"I know you don't. But I do." Wren leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Your worst memory, Mirabel. Do you know what it was? Not the debt. Not the abbot. It was the night you finished the first map. You sat in the scriptorium with forty pages of other people's secrets in your hands, and you realized no one would ever know. No one would ever see what you'd done. You'd spend your whole life invisible behind other people's words." Their voice roughened. "And I stood there in the water being shown that, and I thought: *gods, that's exactly why I ran.* Different shape. Same cell."

Mirabel's throat was tight. "So you knew me because I was you."

"I knew you because the god doesn't make mistakes, and he picked you, and I needed to know if he'd picked right." A ghost of their old crooked smile. "So I watched. Every mile. Every miracle you rationed out on the road. Every time you could have taken the easy cruelty and didn't." They poked the fire, and sparks went up toward the wrong moon. "I've known who you were since the lake. I just hadn't decided yet whether to tell you."

"And now?"

"Now you've led four people across three mountains through cuts that don't exist, past knights who can't be killed, and the only reason any of us are breathing is forty pages of graphite nobody ever saw." Wren met her eyes across the coals. "He picked right. I'm sorry it took me this long to say it out loud."

Mirabel discovered, somewhat to her alarm, that she was crying. She blamed the smoke.

"Well," she managed. "For an oathbreaker, that was almost a vow."

"Don't tell the god. He'll want it in writing."

"He already has it in writing," she said, wiping her eyes. "Everything does."

* * *

Dawn found them moving again, and the mountains found new ways to be difficult.

The Hollowed knights did not gain, but they did not fall behind either — three steady lights pacing them through the valleys, always a half-day back, always exactly where the map said a road would take them. So Mirabel stopped taking roads entirely.

She took them up a deer trail that became a goat argument that became a hand-over-hand scramble beside a waterfall frozen mid-cascade, blue-green and groaning. She took them through the Hollow of Nine Stones, where smugglers had stacked cairns for two hundred years and each cairn meant *safe water here* or *watchman's sightline, go low.* She took them down into a limestone sink where an entire river vanished underground and re-emerged a mile later, and they walked the dry bed in echoing dark with Sefa's hand gripping hers and Wren's voice, ahead, counting their steps aloud so the fear couldn't count anything else.

"You're enjoying this," Sefa accused her at the river's mouth, blinking in the sudden daylight.

"A little," Mirabel admitted. "Is that wicked?"

"It's terrifying. You're smiling like the abbot smiles at donation season."

"I am nothing like the abbot."

"You're currently leading a heretical pilgrimage through smuggler tunnels while pursued by undead knights. The abbot would weep with envy."

Mirabel laughed — really laughed, the first time in weeks — and the sound went up the dry riverbed and came back changed, and even Wren smiled, briefly, like the sun coming out over a battlefield.

But at noon, on a ridge above the Greywater valley, Mirabel spread her maps on a flat stone and the humor went out of the day.

"Here." Her finger rested on a gap. Not a blank — worse than a blank. A place where her sources disagreed. Old Tam the drover swore the high pass was open year-round; a dead woman named Brigit, whose drink Mirabel had bought in a tavern eleven years ago, had sworn it closed after the rockfall of the Red Winter, and that the only way through afterward was the chapel road itself. "Every route from here to the ruins either crosses the chapel road or adds nine days through the Kettlestones. We don't have nine days. The eclipse is in five."

"So we cross the chapel road," Sefa said.

"We cross the knights."

"Not necessarily." Wren had gone quiet in the way that meant calculation. "Roads are a lie, you said. What about the land under them?"

Mirabel stared at the map. Then she turned it sideways, which was a trick old Tam had taught her — *paper lies less when you stop respecting it* — and there it was. Not a route. A seam. The chapel road ran along the lip of a gorge, and the gorge had a floor, and the floor had, according to a note in her own handwriting from a man who'd died before he could be proud of her, *a walkable shelf, wet but true, from the Greywater falls to within a mile of the ruins.*

"It's unmapped," she said slowly. "Unmapped even by me. I only have hearsay."

"You have better than that," Wren said. "You have a herald's god and an oathbreaker's luck, and between the two of us we owe several deities enough favors that one of them owes us back." They rolled up the map and handed it to her with a formality that was almost ceremony. "Lead on, Vane."

Something in the way they said her name — easily, deliberately, like setting a thing down where she could reach it — made her understand that the confession by the fire had been a door, and Wren had walked through it, and was waiting on the other side to see if she'd follow.

She would. She was already following. She had been since the lake.

* * *

The gorge floor was everything the dead man had promised and less. Wet but true. The river ran shallow and furious beside them; the walls rose so high the sky became a ribbon, and the ribbon held the thirteenth moon, visible at midday now, white as a scar. Twice the shelf narrowed to a ledge where they crossed pressed to stone with the water sucking at their boots, and twice Mirabel heard — or dreamed she heard — hoofbeats on the road far above, unhurried, inevitable.

Near dusk the gorge opened, and there it was.

The ruined chapel sat on its knoll above the valley, black against a bruised sunset, its broken tower pointing at the wrong moon like an accusation. King Aldric had been crowned here twenty years ago, in the grass and the wind, before the capital built him a throne and a crown and a cage. From this distance it looked peaceful. It looked like nothing at all.

It looked like a trap, because it was one, and everyone knew it.

"There'll be knights inside," Sefa said. "Or waiting outside. Or both."

"Yes," said Wren.

"We could still take the Kettlestones. Nine days. Miss the eclipse. Live."

"And let the thing on the throne finish eating him," Mirabel said quietly. "No."

Sefa looked at her sister for a long moment — at the graphite-stained fingers and the map-callused hands and the woman who had burned her own name into the world — and then nodded once, and Mirabel understood that whatever the temple had sent Sefa to do, she had set it down somewhere in the gorge and left it there.

"One more thing," Wren said, as they started up the knoll. "Before we go in. If it goes badly in there—"

"It won't."

"If it goes badly," Wren repeated, with the patience of someone who had broken oaths and knew exactly how vows failed, "I need you to know that the lake showed me more than my worst memory. It showed me the best one too. It hasn't happened yet. It's this one. Right now. Walking toward the ruin with you."

Mirabel stopped. The wind came up the valley smelling of rain and old stone.

"That's a terrible confession," she said, when she could speak. "It's practically a death flag."

"I'm told heralds appreciate dramatic timing."

Above them, in the chapel doorway, something glinted. Armor. More than one set. Waiting, patient as the moon, for the people who had finally, at last, run out of road.

Mirabel reached down and took Wren's hand anyway.

"Then let's give them something to wait for," she said, and led the way up.
