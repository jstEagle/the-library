## 15. The Third Fragment

The chapel had been beautiful once. You could tell the way you could tell a deer had been beautiful once — by the bones of it.

Wren Calloway stood at the treeline and looked at what remained of Coronation Chapel: a roof like a broken eggshell, walls shrugged half-down into ivy, one bell tower leaning at an angle that suggested it was thinking very seriously about lying down. Snow dusted everything evenly, the way snow does when no one has walked on a place in years. No footprints. No smoke from any chimney within sight. No birdsong, which was the part that made the back of Wren's neck prickle.

"Well," they said. "It's charming. In the way that graves are charming."

Beside them, Mirabel Vane pulled her cloak tighter and consulted the god's mark on her wrist — the faint silver script that appeared there whenever he had something to say, which lately was often, and which she read the way other people read weather. "He says the fragment is inside. He says we should hurry."

"Does he ever say anything useful? Like *there's a trap* or *bring sandwiches*?"

"He says," Mirabel said, with the particular patience she reserved for Wren's worst moods, "that you complained less when you were an oathbreaker."

"I was *better company* when I was an oathbreaker." But Wren started down the slope, because complaining about walking and walking anyway had become, over these months, something almost like a habit of devotion. They heard Mirabel's boots crunching behind them, and Sefa's lighter steps behind hers — the sister had refused, in terms that brooked no argument, to be left at the last safe house — and Tam's wheezing mule bringing up the rear, because even a quest for the soul of a kingdom needed a mule, and Tam would have followed the mule anywhere.

The chapel doors were gone. What replaced them was a curtain of ivy so thick it might have been woven, and when Wren pushed through it, the cold smell of old stone gave way to something else: roses. In midwinter. Under a broken roof. Blooming pale and out of season along the ruined altar like they'd been waiting for someone to notice.

"Oh," said Sefa softly. "Oh, that's not right."

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't."

Inside, the chapel was one great room open to the grey sky, pews rotted to soft black shapes, stained glass long since shattered so that the remaining fragments lay in the snow like jewels spilled from a dropped purse. And above the altar, where in any decent temple there would have been a statue of some god or other, there was instead a crown.

Not the crown. Wren knew the crown — had seen it once, from across a throne room, ten years and a lifetime ago, gold and hungry. This one was stone, carved into the wall itself, life-sized, empty, waiting. And set into the hollow of it, glowing with a light like the last coals of a fire, was a shard of something that might have been glass and might have been word made solid.

The third fragment. *To love.*

Mirabel stopped three steps inside the door and did not go further. Wren watched her face do a thing it rarely did: go completely still, the scribe's composure holding while everything behind it reorganized.

"It's warm," she said. "Do you feel it? The whole room is — it feels like—"

"Like being eight years old and someone remembering your birthday," Wren said, before they could stop themselves.

Mirabel turned and looked at them. It was the first time either of them had mentioned, out loud and unprompted, anything from before. From the capital. From the lives they'd each buried under new names and new roads.

"You remember your eighth birthday?" she asked gently.

"Somebody remembered it," Wren said. "That was the remarkable part." They cleared their throat and gestured at the altar with as much bravado as they could manufacture. "Right. Fragment. Let's go collect our magical vow-piece and get out of this mausoleum before the roses do something worse than bloom."

But when Wren crossed the threshold of the altar rail — an actual physical rail, knee-high, rotted but present — the world simply declined to let them.

There was no wall. There was no voice, no flash of light, none of the theater the drowned shrine had put on beneath the frozen lake. Wren just took a step and found themselves standing exactly where they'd been, as if the step had been subtracted from time. They tried again. Same result. A third time, faster, angrier, and ended up nose-to-nose with their own frustration, breathing hard in air that smelled like roses and patience.

"It won't let us take it," Mirabel said quietly. She hadn't moved from her spot. She was reading her wrist. "He told me. I thought — I hoped he was wrong, or that it meant something else." She looked up, and her eyes were wet, which frightened Wren more than any Hollowed knight ever had. "It can't be taken. It can only be *given*. Freely. By someone who loves the king without having met him."

The silence after that had weight. Even Tam's mule seemed to hold its breath.

"That's absurd," Wren said finally. "Nobody loves Aldric. Half the kingdom thinks he's a saint and the other half thinks he's a tax form. Nobody *loves* him. He's a story people tell to feel better about the roads being bad."

"People love who they think he is," Sefa said. She had drifted forward, arms wrapped around herself, staring at the glowing shard. "They love the gentle king. The stories say he stops his horse to talk to farmers. That he learned every servant's name in the palace." She hesitated. "In Thornfell, when I was small, my mother used to say the king loved us all, even us, even debt-bound families nobody else remembered. I believed it for years."

"And does that count?" Wren demanded, gesturing at the altar. "Loving a story? Loving a rumor?"

The fragment pulsed, gently, like a heart deciding.

Apparently not.

* * *

They made camp in the chapel's lee rather than risk the open slope — the Hollowed knights were somewhere behind them, and the thirteenth moon was swelling toward its impossible turn, and everyone was tired enough that arguing about shelter felt like a luxury they couldn't afford. Tam built a fire in the shelter of the fallen doorway. Sefa cooked. The mule ate something it shouldn't have and looked pleased about it.

Wren sat apart, sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, and watched Mirabel stand at the altar rail for the better part of an hour, talking low and steady to the god's script on her wrist, occasionally writing answers in the frost on a fallen slab of pew with one bare finger.

When she finally came back to the fire, she moved like someone carrying water without spilling it.

"He explained," she said. She sat down across from Wren, close enough that their knees nearly touched, which was either accident or not-accident, and Wren had given up pretending to know which. "A vow has three parts or it isn't a vow. To protect. To serve. To love. The first two can be *done* — you can act your way into them. But love can't be done. It can only be true. So the fragment tests truth." She fed a stick to the fire. "It needs someone who loves Aldric. Not the crown, not the throne, not the idea. Him. The man under it. And it knows the difference."

"Then we're finished," Wren said flatly. "Because the man under it has been eaten. Whatever's left of him, nobody's met. You can't love a stranger you've never seen."

"Can't you?" Mirabel asked.

And there it was. Wren looked at her — at the firelight catching the ink stains that never fully washed off her fingers, at the stubborn line of her mouth — and understood, with the slow dread of a door opening onto a long drop, exactly where this was going.

"No," Wren said.

"Wren—"

"*No.* I know that tone. That's your temple tone. That's the tone you used before you burned your own contract in front of witnesses, and before you drank lake-water that showed you things no one should see, and before—" They ran out of list. "Before every single terrible noble thing you've decided to do while I stood here holding the knife and watching."

"The payment doesn't have to be me," Mirabel said. "I asked. Anyone who loves him freely can give. It takes a memory — the dearest one the giver has. That's the price. Love proves itself by giving away its own treasure." Her voice stayed level. Her hands did not. "Sefa loved the story of him as a child, but she's met the reality now, and doubt has gotten in. Tam never thought about the king at all until this month. And you—"

"Don't," Wren said.

"—you loved him once," Mirabel finished softly. "You were sworn to him. Before. Before whatever happened in the capital that you don't talk about. You loved him and you lost him and you've spent ten years telling yourself the loss was a relief."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, the mule snorted.

"That's not love anymore," Wren said. "That's scar tissue. And besides — I *met* him. The rule says without having met him."

"You met the king," Mirabel said. "You never met Aldric. None of us have. The man underneath — the one who begged the god for help, the one whose own vow is keeping a scrap of him alive in the dark — he's as much a stranger to you as he is to me." She leaned forward. "More, maybe. Because at least I came to him clean. You came to him grieving."

Wren stood up. It was cowardice dressed as pacing, and they both knew it, and Mirabel had the grace not to name it.

"I'm going to check the perimeter," they announced to the fire, to the mule, to the indifferent ruins.

"Of course you are," said Mirabel, and there was no judgment in it at all, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The perimeter was forty feet of snowy ruin, and Wren checked all of it twice, and then stood in the leaning shadow of the bell tower looking up at the stars, where the thirteenth moon hung — visible only to those who knew to look, a smear of wrongness among the constellations, closer to full than it had any right to be.

Ten years ago, Wren Calloway had knelt in a hall much finer than this ruin and sworn to protect King Aldric with their life. Six weeks later, they had stood in the same hall and watched the king — watched the *thing* wearing the king — order a village burned for harboring a prophet of the old gods. And Wren, twenty-two years old and armed, had done nothing. Had stood there with a sword and a sacred oath and had done *nothing*, because the oath said protect the king, and the king was the one holding the torch.

So they'd broken the vow instead. Publicly. Loudly. Called the crowned thing a monster to its face in front of the whole court, and been exiled for treason, and told themselves for a decade that exile was the honest ending. That breaking a rotten oath was cleaner than keeping one.

It had taken a frozen lake and a dead god's memory to show them the third option they'd never considered: staying, and protecting the *people*, and calling the oath mended rather than broken. It had taken Mirabel.

And now Mirabel wanted to hand her dearest memory to a stone crown to buy back a man neither of them had ever truly met, and Wren could not bear it.

Because Wren knew what her dearest memory was. They'd seen her guard slip often enough. It wasn't her mother's face, though she spoke of her with such tenderness. It wasn't the first time the god answered her, though she described it like a sunrise. It was smaller than that, and older. She'd told them once, on a night pass with the wind screaming, laughing into Wren's shoulder: the day she was seven, and her father — drowning in debt, exhausted, ashamed — had nevertheless spent his last coin on honey-cakes because little Mira had looked sad, and the two of them had eaten them on the temple steps, and for one afternoon the debt had not existed. *That's when I learned,* she'd said, *that love is a thing you pay for gladly.*

Her dearest memory was proof that love was worth any price. Of course it was. Of course that was the one she'd give.

Wren pressed their forehead against the cold stone of the tower and breathed until the burning behind their eyes behaved.

* * *

She was waiting for them when they came back. Everyone else was asleep — Sefa curled by the embers, Tam snoring in harmony with the mule — and Mirabel sat alone by the fire with her hands around a cup of nothing, warming them out of habit.

"You're going to do it tomorrow," Wren said. Not a question.

"At dawn. The god says the fragment is strongest then, and we've lost too many days already." She looked up. "You could try to stop me."

"With what? You'd argue me into the ground. You argued a *god* into naming you herald."

"I argued a god into letting me ask questions," she corrected. "Different thing." She patted the ground beside her, and after a moment Wren sat, because sitting next to her was the one surrender they'd never managed to resist. "Talk to me instead. Tell me why you're afraid. Not of losing me — I know that one. Tell me the other thing."

Wren stared into the fire for a long time.

"When you give it," they said at last, "you won't know what you gave. That's how the shrine worked, how the crypt worked. The price is paid before you understand it. You'll hand over the honey-cake afternoon and you'll walk away lighter and you'll never know there's a hole shaped like your father in you." Their voice cracked on the last word and they let it. "And I'll know. I'll be the only one who knows what you lost, because you told me, and you won't remember telling me, and I'll have to carry it alone. Again."

Mirabel was quiet. Then she reached over and took Wren's knife-hand — the scarred one, the one that had held a sword and done nothing — and laced her ink-stained fingers through theirs.

"Then remember it for both of us," she said. "That's what oaths are for, aren't they? Carrying what the other person can't." She smiled, tired and bright. "You're always telling me you're an oathbreaker. Prove it. Break the pattern. Keep something."

Wren laughed — a wet, broken sound — and gripped her hand like a man gripping a rope over a long fall.

Dawn came grey and rose-scented. Mirabel Vane walked to the altar rail alone, and the fragment of *to love* rose from its stone crown to meet her like a bird recognizing a hand, and Wren Calloway stood at the edge of the rotted pews and watched her open her mouth to speak the words that would cost her the sweetest hour of her childhood —

— and the fragment flared white, and the stone crown split from wall to floor with a crack like the world clearing its throat, and a voice that was not the god's rolled out of the ruin, deep and rusted and unmistakably *royal*:

"*Wait.*"

Every rose in the chapel closed at once. And in the snow before the altar, written in letters of frost that no hand had traced, words began to appear — words Wren recognized with a sick lurch, because they had knelt before them once, ten years ago, in another life:

*Who gives, must choose what is given. Choose wrongly, and the vow stays broken. Choose rightly—*

The frost-script paused. Shivered. Began again, slower, as if the writer were struggling up from a great depth:

*—and I may yet owe you a coronation.*
