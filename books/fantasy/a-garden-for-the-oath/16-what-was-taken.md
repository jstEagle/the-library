## 16. What Was Taken

Mirabel woke to the smell of pine smoke and the sound of Wren arguing with a kettle.

This was not unusual. Wren argued with kettles the way other people argued with gods — frequently, bitterly, and always losing. She lay still for a moment in the ruined chapel's antechamber, wrapped in her cloak on a pallet of dried bracken, and listened to them hiss at each other by the fire.

"You have one job," Wren was saying. "One. Boil. It's not a complicated theology."

She smiled. And then, because she was Mirabel Vane, scribe, apostate, herald of the god of oaths, she reached for the memory that made the smile — the first time she'd heard Wren argue with a kettle, in some waystation kitchen weeks ago, when they'd been strangers pretending to be traveling companions for hire — and found that she could not say, with any certainty, whether it had happened at all.

The smile stayed on her face. The certainty did not.

She lay very still and did what she had always done with difficult things: she catalogued them.

*Item.* She remembered Wren Calloway. Every version of him — him, them; she'd learned early that Wren answered to either and corrected no one, which should have told her something about how much weight names could carry. She remembered their crooked grin, the scar through one eyebrow like a stitch someone had given up on halfway, the way they said *herald* like an insult and *Mirabel* like something they were still deciding about.

*Item.* She could not remember which of those memories belonged to her.

It was like standing in the temple scriptorium after a shelf collapse — hundreds of pages scattered across the floor, all of them hers, all of them true, and no way to know which pile had fallen from which shelf. Or perhaps it wasn't like that at all. Perhaps she only thought it was like that because she had spent ten years in a scriptorium, and the comparison was simply the nearest bookshelf to hand.

Perhaps none of this had ever happened to anyone.

"Your kettle's dead," Wren announced through the doorway, appearing with two tin cups of something that smelled like pine needles had died for it. "I've made tea anyway. Consider it an act of faith." They stopped. Looked at her. Their whole face changed — the grin folding down into something careful. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. Where you stare at the ceiling like it owes you money." They sat down cross-legged beside her pallet and pressed a cup into her hands. Their fingers were warm. She knew they were warm. She did not know if knowing that meant anything. "It worked last night," Wren said quietly. "The chapel took your payment. You slept like the actual dead, and I sat here rehearsing everything I'd say to you if you woke up wrong."

"And now you're saying it?"

"I'm stalling. There's a difference. Stalling has dignity."

*Did you love me,* she wanted to ask, *or did I learn to love you out of a fragment of a king's vow, the way ink learns the shape of whatever it's set against?* But her mouth said, instead: "Tell me something true."

Wren blinked. "That's a broad commission."

"Anything. Something small. Something I can hold."

They considered. Outside, wind moved through the broken chapel roof where the third fragment had dissolved into light and gone wherever fragments went — home, presumably, or at least toward Aldric. Somewhere north, under a moon that would soon begin its impossible thirteenth turn, a hollow-eyed king sat on a throne wearing a crown that ate.

"I hate the mountains," Wren said. "Everyone thinks oathbreakers are romantic, mountain-dwelling types. We're not. My feet have been cold since the lake shrine. I broke my vow in summer, in a garden, in front of forty witnesses, and I have regretted the footwear ever since." They paused. "Also, you snore. Gently. Like a very small dog with opinions."

Mirabel laughed — and then caught herself laughing, and examined the laugh, turning it over like a coin she suspected of being clipped.

Wren watched her do it. "Bad?"

"It's just —" She set the tea down before her hands could shake it. "I remember loving you, Wren. I remember it clearly. The problem is I remember *everything* clearly. That's what the chapel took. Not the memories. The edges. I used to be able to tell which things were mine because mine had — weight. Texture. My mother's voice was rougher than Sefa's. My fear of deep water had a specific flavor. Now every memory sits in my head like it was written by the same steady hand, and I can't find my own handwriting anywhere."

She heard herself say it and winced. Ten years a scribe, and even her unraveling came out in metaphors about penmanship.

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had the flat steadiness she'd learned to read as fear wearing armor. "Do you know who I am?"

"Yes."

"Do you know why that matters to you?"

And there it was — the question she'd been circling since waking, laid out between them like a blade on clean linen. She looked at it honestly, because lying to Wren had never once worked and seemed unlikely to start.

"No," she said. "I know that it matters. I feel that it matters — it matters so much it frightens me. But I don't know if that's because I chose you, or because the fragment of *to love* passed through me on its way to the king, and left residue. Like wax left in a seal-mold." Her throat tightened. "Maybe I'm not a person who loves you. Maybe I'm a mold of a person who loves you, and the real shape belongs to a king I've never met."

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard," Wren said, "and I once heard a man try to bribe a border guard with a turnip."

"Wren—"

"No, listen." They leaned forward, and their eyes were bright and angry in the way that meant they cared too much to be calm. "You want to talk handwriting? Fine. Let's talk handwriting. The night we met, you tried to arrest me."

"I did not try to arrest you."

"You drew up a formal writ of detention. On the spot. In the snow. From memory. Who *does* that? Nobody, Mirabel. Nobody does that. That's not borrowed from any vow. A king's coronation promise didn't teach you to draft legal documents at a fleeing smuggler in a blizzard. That's just you being unbearably yourself."

Despite everything, despite the groundlessness, something in her chest warmed. "You fled."

"I negotiated."

"You fled while negotiating."

"A tactical withdrawal with terms attached," Wren said with dignity, and then, softer, all the armor sliding off at once: "Listen to me. I don't care whose handwriting is on it. I have spent three years telling myself oaths are chains and love is a ledger entry, and then you looked at me in that frozen shrine like I was worth saving, and I have not had a decent night's sleep since, and I am *not* going to let you unlove me on technical grounds. If the feeling is borrowed, fine. Keep borrowing it. Default on it. I'll extend credit indefinitely. I am very bad with money; everyone knows this."

Mirabel stared at them. And the terrible, wonderful thing was that she could not tell whether the tears rising in her eyes were grief or relief or laughter, because all of them felt exactly alike now — but they rose, and that was its own answer, maybe. Feelings that arrived without provenance were still feelings. Rain didn't need to remember which cloud it came from in order to fall.

"I don't know if this is real," she whispered.

"I know," Wren said. "So we'll do what we always do. We'll check it against the evidence." They tapped her knee. "Evidence one: you snore like a small opinionated dog. Evidence two: you once rationed a miracle to a farmer by making him fill out a petition in triplicate, because even divine intervention apparently requires proper documentation—"

"He got his rain."

"He got his rain, in triplicate. Evidence three—" and here Wren's voice did the thing it almost never did, dropping all its deflections, going quiet and plain as a held-out hand, "—evidence three is that when you look at me, I believe it. Whatever it is. Whoever it belongs to. I believe it."

* * *

They broke camp at midmorning, because the thirteenth moon did not wait for the uncertain, and neither did Hollowed knights.

Mirabel discovered the second strangeness of her condition somewhere on the switchback trail above the valley: she no longer knew which skills were hers either.

It happened without warning. They came to a fork where the path split around a granite shoulder, and Wren hesitated, map-less as always, and Mirabel heard herself say, "Left. There's a shepherd's cut behind the ridge that drops us below the treeline by dusk — the right path washes out in spring melt."

Then she stopped walking.

"How do I know that?" she asked the empty air.

Wren turned back. "You mapped these roads. For years. You told me — the margins of discarded tithe pages, remember?"

"I remember telling you." She pressed her palms against her temples. "But I don't remember *learning* it. Walking those roads at night, copying the smuggler routes, hiding them — I have the knowledge like a tool in a drawer, and I have no memory of ever putting it there. What if that's not my knowledge? What if the god put it there? What if he's been writing in me longer than I've known he existed, and everything I think of as *Mirabel Vane, clever girl, secret cartographer* is just another page of his hand?"

The wind picked at her hood. Below them the valley spread out gold and brown, ordinary and indifferent, and somewhere in it people were lighting cookfires and arguing with kettles and living lives that at least belonged to them.

Wren came back down the trail and stood in front of her, blocking the view, which was probably deliberate.

"Test it," they said.

"What?"

"If the god wrote it, he wrote it for a reason, and the reason is getting you to Aldric alive. So use the road. If you're wrong, we die on a washed-out path, and honestly, at that point, the authorship question resolves itself." They shrugged. "But also — counterpoint. When you sketched routes in the margins, did the god hold the pen?"

"...No."

"Did he hide them from the abbot?"

"No. I hid them. I nearly died of terror doing it. Elder Haskew found one once and I told him it was decorative knotwork."

Wren grinned — the crooked one, the one she knew, whichever shelf it had fallen from. "There. A god of oaths doesn't do decorative knotwork. Believe me. I've met his competition." They offered their arm, mock-formal, courtly as a storybook knight, which was funnier for being Wren. "Shall we, herald? Your road, your handwriting. I'll follow the pen."

She took their arm. And she noticed — filed it away, item by item, because cataloguing was the one habit that still felt like hers — that taking Wren's arm felt like a decision rather than a memory. New. Unborrowed. Made in this exact minute, by whoever she was now.

Maybe that was enough. Maybe a person wasn't the sum of what she remembered owning, but the sum of what she kept choosing.

Or maybe she only thought that because it was comforting.

The trail, at least, was real. It wound down behind the ridge exactly as her drawer-of-unknown-knowledge promised, past an abandoned shepherd's hut with a collapsed roof, and by dusk they were below the treeline with the capital's far glow smudging the northern horizon like a bruise.

* * *

That night the god spoke.

He had been quieter since the chapel — she'd assumed the fragments' passage had cost him strength, or perhaps that he was simply giving her room to grieve her own edges. But as she banked the fire and Wren took first watch, she felt the old familiar pressure behind her eyes, the sensation of a vast patience leaning close to a small page.

*Herald.*

"I'm here," she murmured. Around them, faintly, the words of old vows stirred — scratched into the chapel's surviving stones, carved by wedding guests and mourners decades dead, the way vows lingered in places where they'd been spoken often. She could read them all now. That hadn't changed. Reading had never once failed her.

*I owe you a truth,* the god said. *And truths owed weigh more than gifts given.*

Her stomach dropped. Gods did not preface things well. "Say it."

*The payment you made in the chapel — the dearest memory you offered — the fragment took it truly. Nothing was stolen. But understand what 'to love' is. It is not a feeling. It is a binding. It is the thread in the king's vow that tied him to his people, and threads do not distinguish between one heart and another. When you gave it your dearest memory, it wove that memory into itself — and now the love you carry for the oathbreaker, and the love the king carried for his kingdom, share a single strand.*

"So it's not mine." The words came out very flat. Very steady. Steadiness was the last handwriting she had left, and she gripped it hard. "You're telling me it was never mine."

*I am telling you,* the god said, and his voice was gentle the way avalanches are technically gentle until they arrive, *that I cannot tell you. That is what was taken. Not a memory — the proof of ownership. And I will not lie to you to make you comfortable, because I am the god of oaths and lies are the one country I cannot enter.*

She laughed — a small, cracked sound. "Of course. The honest god. My luck."

*But hear the rest.* The presence shifted, and for a moment she felt what she'd felt in the dream-vigil, high in the passes: the god's own memory, ancient and heavy, of a king he had served and failed. *When I named you herald, I chose you for many reasons. Your maps. Your courage. Your infuriating triplicate petitions. But also this: you have always known what it is to belong to something by contract rather than by choice. Debt-bound to a temple. Ink owed. You know better than any soul I have marked in five hundred years that a bond's origin does not decide its worth. A debt can become devotion. You proved it. Why should love be different?*

Mirabel sat very still, watching the fire.

Across the embers, Wren sat their watch with their sword across their knees, humming tunelessly, keeping guard over a woman who might be a stranger wearing borrowed feelings — and staying anyway. Choosing it. Extending credit.

"Thank you," she said finally, to the god, to the fire, to no one.

*Do not thank me yet,* the god said. *We ride toward the throne, herald, and the thing that wears the crown knows your name — or knew it. And it has had a decade to learn what people will pay for what they love.* A pause, and when he continued, his voice had the cold edge of iron being tested. *It will offer you back what was taken. It can do that. It eats vows; it can counterfeit their mending. It will put your certainty in your hands like a gift, and it will ask only one price.*

She already knew. She'd known since the vision on her first day as herald: a king wearing a crown that eats.

"What price?"

*Everything else,* said the god of oaths. *Sleep now, herald. Tomorrow we walk into ash.*

Northward, beyond the dark tree line, the horizon glowed — and Mirabel, drifting toward uneasy sleep with Wren's watch-song in her ears and no way to prove her own heart, understood at last that the thirteenth moon was not waiting for them at all.

It had already begun to turn.
