## 17. The Gates of Ashes

The capital announced itself the way an old enemy does: not by appearing all at once, but by degrees, each one worse than the last.

First came the smell — woodsmoke and river mud and, beneath it, that particular sweetness of a city too large to bury its own dead properly. Then the road widened from smuggler track to pilgrim trail to honest cartway, and Wren felt their shoulders climb toward their ears like they were trying to hide inside their own collar.

"You're doing the thing," Mirabel said beside them. She'd been quiet for hours, watching the walls grow on the horizon, but apparently she'd been watching Wren too.

"What thing."

"The thing where you walk like you're about to be arrested."

"I *was* about to be arrested. For four years. It's a habit now." Wren adjusted their pack strap for the fourth time in as many minutes. "Besides, we're all about to be arrested. You're a declared apostate. Sefa's carrying forged temple documents, which is its own kind of apostasy with better handwriting. And Aldric—" they nodded back down the line, where the king walked with his hood up and his borrowed sword wrapped in sackcloth like a fishmonger's parcel, "—the king is technically dead. Or hollowed. We've never settled which, and I'd rather not find out at a checkpoint."

"He's walking very well for a man who was buried under his own name until two weeks ago," Mirabel said. There was something soft in her voice when she looked at Aldric, and something else underneath it, and Wren had learned not to poke at either one. The fragment of *to love* had taken its payment strangely from her; some days she watched the king like she was trying to remember why he mattered, and those days she held Wren's hand so tightly it hurt.

Today was a holding-tight day. Wren didn't mind. Their hand had been numb since the war-crypt anyway, and not entirely from cold.

Ahead, the Gates of Ashes rose out of the morning haze — the traitor's gate, the small ugly door in the great gray wall, named for what was done outside it. Traitors were executed beyond the wall so their blood wouldn't touch consecrated ground. Wren had stood there once, fourteen years old and already sworn, holding the torch while someone else's oathbreaker burned. They'd thought, even then: *I hope I'm never interesting enough to end up here.*

They had, of course, immediately gone on to become extremely interesting.

"Right," Wren said, and stopped, and made themselves look at the gate properly. "Here's how this works. The gate guards know my face. That's bad. But they also have standing orders about my face, and those orders are old, because nobody important has cared about me in years." They pulled off their hood. "They're supposed to bring me in alive. There was a reward. It'll be stale, but the order will still be on the board, because no one in this city ever takes anything down."

"So we surrender?" Sefa asked. She was clutching the writ tube against her chest like a shield, and she had the particular green look of someone who had spent three days rehearsing courage.

"We *present* ourselves. There's a difference. Surrendering is what guilty people do." Wren grinned, and knew it wasn't a good grin. "We're going to be insufferable instead. Insufferable people get taken to officers. Officers can be talked to. Checkpoint guards just have orders."

"And if they don't take us to an officer?" Mirabel asked quietly.

Wren looked at the gate again. At the little iron door set into stone older than any kingdom anyone bothered to remember. Somewhere past it was the city where they'd learned to hold a sword, and the barracks where they'd broken their vow, and the tribunal hall where they'd been stripped of everything except the name, because even exiles keep their names — that's how they know who to hate.

"If they don't," Wren said, "then the Hollowed find us here instead of at the palace, and honestly? Better odds out here. Fewer doors to be cornered behind."

"That's the most comforting thing you've ever said," Sefa muttered, "and I want you to know I noticed that."

* * *

The guards at the Gates of Ashes were exactly as bored as Wren had prayed they would be.

Two of them. One young enough to be more curious than careful, one old enough to be more tired than either. The old one squinted at Wren for a long moment, and Wren watched recognition arrive like weather — first the flicker, then the clouding-over, then the slow settling of something heavy.

"Well," the old guard said. "Ashes and cinders."

"Hallo, Brannic."

"You're supposed to be dead."

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things. Mostly I'm supposed to be brought before an officer of the watch, unharmed, per standing order forty-one, which I know is still nailed up because you people haven't reorganized that board since I left." Wren spread their arms, palms out, the picture of cooperative menace. "So. Shall we?"

Brannic looked at their companions. His gaze snagged on Aldric — hooded, tall, moving with that strange gliding patience the king had developed since the crypts — and slid off him like water off wax, because the crown-spirit's glamour still worked at a distance and everyone in this city had spent ten years learning not to see what they saw.

"The others come too?" Brannic asked.

"They're my retinue."

"You don't have a retinue. You have a bounty."

"I've upgraded." Wren let the grin sharpen. "Take me in. All of me. Every piece."

The young guard leaned over to Brannic and whispered, audibly, "Is that the Oathbreaker? The one from the ballads? He's shorter than in the ballads."

"*They're* shorter than in the ballads," Wren corrected, "and the ballads are wrong about several other things, including the ending."

Something passed across Brannic's face — not quite sympathy, not quite shame. He'd been at the tribunal. Wren remembered him standing at the back, arms crossed, saying nothing, which in that room had been the closest thing to mercy available.

"Order forty-one," Brannic said at last, heavily, like a man stepping off a cliff because the path went that way. "Unharmed, before an officer. Fine. Walk ahead of us and don't make me regret remembering you fondly."

"Fondly! Did you hear that?" Wren said to the others as they passed under the arch. "I'm remembered fondly. Write it down. Someone write it down."

"I'm writing it down," Sefa said, and actually did, which was somehow worse.

* * *

Inside the walls, the capital was holding its breath.

Wren had expected fear, or festival, or the grim bustle of a city preparing for siege. What they got instead was silence stretched thin over everything — markets half-stocked, shutters half-closed, people moving through the streets the way water moves around a stone. Everyone knew the eclipse was coming. Everyone could feel the thirteenth moon dragging at the sky like a splinter working toward the skin.

And everywhere, on every corner, the king's face. Fresh posters, paste still wet at the edges: *THE KING CALLS HIS CHILDREN HOME. ATTEND THE ECLIPSE VIGIL. THE CROWN PROVIDES.*

"The Crown Provides," Mirabel read under her breath. "Provides what?"

"Whatever's missing," Wren said. "That was always the pitch. You're hungry, the Crown provides bread. You're afraid, the Crown provides order. You're grieving—" they stopped, because they'd almost said *the Crown provides forgetting*, and that was true, and true things about the crown-spirit had a way of summoning attention. "It gives people what they lack. That's how it eats. Nobody misses a thing they never notice is gone."

They turned down a side street — Wren leading now, memory taking over from maps, their feet knowing this city better than their head wanted to admit. Here was the baker who used to sell burnt-end rolls cheap to soldiers on leave. Here was the fountain someone had thrown a statue into during the riots, back when there were still riots. Here was the barracks wall, and Wren made themselves not look at it, and looked at it anyway, and felt nothing, and hated the nothing.

Aldric touched their elbow. Gently. The king had gotten good at gentle.

"This street," Aldric said. "I rode down it. After my crowning. Ten thousand people, and every one of them cheering, and I remember thinking—" his voice did the flickering thing, the man surfacing through deep water, "—I remember thinking *I hope I deserve this*, and then I remember the crown getting heavier, and then I remember nothing for a long time."

"Do you remember me?" Wren asked. It came out before they could stop it. "From before. The court. I was — I served, briefly, before."

Aldric studied them. And there it was again, that terrible kindness in the ruined face. "You were the one who laughed at the ambassador's hat. The feathered one."

Wren barked a laugh that startled two pigeons off a gutter. "You *remember that*?"

"It was the last time I laughed freely." The king said it simply, without self-pity, the way he said most unbearable things. "I kept it. In the dark, in the — wherever it is the crown keeps me — I kept your laugh. It was proof the world had been real once."

Mirabel made a small sound. Wren didn't look at her, because looking at her would mean stopping, and stopping would mean the Hollowed caught up, and also they might cry, and oathbreakers do not cry in front of kings or scribes or sisters-of-scribes.

"Right," Wren said thickly. "Officer of the watch. Let's go ruin somebody's afternoon."

* * *

The officer of the watch turned out to be Captain Ilse Marchand, who had once shared a tent with Wren through the worst winter of the border campaign, and who looked at the escort report, and then at Wren, and then at the report again, and said, "Oh, you have got to be joking."

"Hallo, Ilsie."

"Don't call me Ilsie. Don't stand there being charming in my guardhouse. Do you know what happens to me if you're in my custody when whatever's coming comes?" She threw the report down. "Standing order forty-one. Unharmed. Before an officer. Congratulations, here's an officer. Now what."

Wren laid it out fast and low: the fragments, the vow, the eclipse, the king walking among them with his face hidden. To Marchand's credit — and Wren had bet a great deal on that credit — she didn't laugh, and she didn't reach for the alarm rope, and she only went pale twice.

"The abbot's people sent word you'd come," she said finally. "Through the temple channel. Said you'd try the Ashes gate because it was yours." Her eyes flicked to Mirabel, to Sefa, to the hooded figure by the wall whose presence made the lamp flames lean toward him like plants toward sun. "They also sent word that the Hollowed are coming. Four knights. They entered the lower city an hour ago."

"An hour," Wren repeated.

"Maybe less. They don't hurry. Nothing hurries, anymore. Have you noticed?" Marchand rubbed her face. "Ten years ago this city ran. Now it *drifts*. Even the dogs drift." She straightened, and twenty years of soldiering settled onto her shoulders like a familiar coat. "There's a postern from my armory into the palace undercrofts. Old service way. It's not on the spirit's maps, because the spirit doesn't bother with servant roads."

"You'd do that? Give me a way in? Ilsie, they'll hang you."

"They'll *crown* me, probably, which is worse." She was already pulling keys. "I broke bread with you for six months, Calloway. You gave me your blanket half the winter and complained about it the whole time so I wouldn't feel indebted. That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing." She paused at the armory door. "But hear me. If your plan fails — if the thing on the throne is still sitting there tomorrow — I never opened this door, and you never existed, and I will personally put order forty-one back on the board myself."

"Fair terms," Wren said. "Ilsie. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Run."

* * *

They ran.

The Hollowed came through the market square as Wren's group slipped down the armory stair — and Wren saw them, one last glance back through the grate, four figures in armor that shone wrong, moving through the drifting crowds without pushing, without parting them, the crowd simply flowing aside as though it had already forgotten why it was making room.

In the lead walked a knight Wren had known. Tam Vessire. Sworn brother. The man who had carried Wren's shield when they couldn't lift it, and who had stood at the tribunal afterward and said *I don't know him* in a flat voice, because that was the only mercy the crown allowed him.

Tam's helmet turned. Found them, across a hundred feet of crowded square, through iron and stone.

He raised one hand. Not a threat. A salute — the old field salute, fist to breast, the one that meant *I see you, brother.*

Then he pointed, unhurried, toward the palace, and began to walk.

"He knows where we're going," Mirabel whispered.

"Yeah," Wren said. Their hand found hers in the dark of the stairwell, numb fingers closing around hers, holding tight the way she held on her worst days. "He always did. That was the problem with Tam. Best tactician I ever served with."

Above them, faint through the grate, the city bells began to toll — not the hour, not the alarm, but the vigil peal, the sound the crown used to gather its children home. Below them, the undercroft passage breathed cold air that smelled of stone and old candle smoke and, very far away, of ashes.

The eclipse was coming. The thirteenth moon hung somewhere above the clouds, turning its impossible turn. And somewhere ahead, past the gates of the palace, the thing that wore King Aldric's smile was waiting to welcome them all home.

"After you," Wren said, and stepped down into the dark.
