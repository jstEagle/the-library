## 19. King Beneath the Crown

The loose stone was exactly where the god's writing had promised it would be — behind the eastern pillar of the undercroft, at the height of a kneeling man's shoulder, worn smooth by ten years of no one touching it.

Wren ran their fingers along its edge and felt the old familiar itch of a secret kept well. They'd spent half their life learning which stones in the capital lied about being load-bearing. This one didn't lie. It simply waited.

"Behind here?" Mirabel whispered. Her voice echoed strangely in the undercroft, as if the walls were listening and passing it along.

"Behind here," Wren said. "Which means either the king's body-self is down there, or it's a very elaborate trap, or both. Historically in this city it's usually both."

"You're very comforting."

"I'm told it's my best quality." Wren braced their shoulder against the stone. "On three. And if something screams, run toward me, not away from me. I'm faster than I look."

"You look fast."

"Flattery noted. One. Two—"

They pushed together. The stone ground aside with a sound like a held breath finally released, and cold air poured out of the dark beyond — not grave-cold, not cellar-cold, but the particular cold of a place where something had been breathing shallowly for a very long time.

Steps went down. Narrow, cut rough, older than the palace above them. Whoever had built this hiding place had built it before there was a palace to hide it under.

Wren lit the stub of candle they'd carried through three days of traitor's gates and Hollowed patrols, cupping the flame against the updraft. The light reached maybe six feet down the stair and gave up.

"After you, herald," they said.

"Why me?"

"Because if the floor gives out, the god will presumably catch his own."

"That's not how he works and you know it."

"No," Wren admitted. "But it's how I'd design him." And they went down first anyway, because that was the other thing they'd never managed to stop being, whatever the temple records said about their broken vow: someone who went first.

* * *

The stair descended farther than the palace had foundations. Wren counted forty steps, then sixty, then stopped counting because the numbers started feeling like an insult. The walls changed around them — dressed stone giving way to older work, then to stone so old it had been cut before the kingdom had a name for itself. Once, they passed a niche where a lamp had burned once, ages ago; the oil stain on the shelf was still dark.

Mirabel's hand found theirs in the dark. She didn't say anything about it, and neither did they, and the touch said everything either of them would have fumbled.

"I can feel him," she murmured. "Barely. Like a word written so faintly you have to tilt the page to the light."

"The vow?"

"What's left of it. It's — Wren, it's *thin*. Like cloth washed too many times. I don't know how anything of him is still holding."

"Stubbornness," Wren said. "It runs in kings. My father swore it ran in everyone who ever taxed him."

The stair ended at a low arch, and beyond the arch was a room that made Wren stop so suddenly Mirabel walked into their back.

It was a bedchamber.

That was the only word for it, and it was absurd, and it broke something open in Wren's chest. A narrow bed with a straw pallet. A washstand with a chipped basin. A wooden chair. A shelf bearing three books, their spines cracked with rereading. A child's drawing pinned to the wall — faded, careful lines showing a palace, a garden, two stick figures holding hands beneath a sun with too many rays.

And on the bed, a man.

He was sitting up, as though he'd heard them coming and refused to be found lying down. He was thin the way candle-stubs are thin — not starved exactly, but burned down to what wouldn't melt. His hair had gone white at thirty-some years of age. His eyes were closed.

His lips were moving.

Wren couldn't hear the words at first. Then, as they stepped closer, they caught the rhythm of it — not prayer, not quite. Repetition. A sentence, over and over, worn smooth as a river stone:

*I will protect them. I will protect them. I will protect them.*

"He's been saying it," Mirabel breathed. "All this time. Ten years. That's what's keeping him—" She pressed her hand to her mouth. "That's the last scrap. That's all that's left of the coronation vow. Not the words we've been gathering. Just this one line, this one promise, repeated until it became the wall he lives inside."

The man's eyes opened.

They were brown. Ordinary brown. Tired, kind, frightened — the eyes of a man who had been alone in the dark for a decade and had decided, somewhere in the middle of it, to make peace with the dark rather than go mad shouting at it. There was nothing regal in them. There was nothing hollow in them either, and after the throne room above — after the thing wearing his face like a glove — the ordinariness of those eyes nearly undid Wren entirely.

"Visitors," King Aldric said. His voice was rust and paper. He smiled, and it wobbled, and steadied. "Forgive me. I'd stopped expecting any. Are you real? Sometimes the crown sends me dreams of visitors, to practice cruelty on."

"We're real," Mirabel said, kneeling beside the bed so she was below his eye-line, smaller, less frightening. Wren watched her do it and understood, again, why the god had chosen her. "I'm Mirabel. This is Wren Calloway. We've come—"

"—with the fragments." His gaze went to the bundle at her chest, and something moved across his face that was too complicated to be hope. "I felt them come into the city. Like hearing your own name called from a great distance. You have no idea what that's like. To hear yourself spoken truly, after ten years of a lie wearing your mouth." He looked down at his own hands — long, ink-stained at the fingertips, a scribe's stains, or a scholar's. "You should sit. Forgive the accommodations. I've had ten years to arrange them and this is still the best I could do."

"There's one chair," Wren said.

"And two of you. Yes. I did notice. Take turns." The ghost of humor crossed his face and Wren thought, absurdly: *I like him.* Ten years buried alive and the first thing he offered his rescuers was a joke about the furniture.

Mirabel took the chair. Wren leaned against the wall, where they could see both the king and the archway. Old habits. The king noticed that too, and nodded approvingly, and Wren liked him a little more.

"You know what's happening up there," Aldric said. "The thirteenth turn. Three days — no. Fewer now. You've seen it wearing me."

"We've seen it," Wren said carefully.

"Then let me save us all some time, because I don't have much strength, and every hour I spend talking is an hour I steal from the sentence that keeps me alive." He folded his hands in his lap like a man settling in for the most important lecture of his life. "Ask me nothing yet. Listen first. Because I know what you've come to do, and I need you to hear why you must not do it."

* * *

He spoke for a long time. His voice kept failing and he kept going anyway, pausing to drink water from the basin, sometimes just breathing, his lips still moving faintly between sentences — *I will protect them, I will protect them* — the way another man might hum.

He told them about his coronation. He'd been twenty-two. His father dead of a winter fever, the kingdom raw with grief, and Aldric standing before the high altar knowing he wasn't ready, knowing no one is ever ready, swearing the old vow with the old god's name in it because the priests still remembered it then.

*To protect them. To serve them. To love them, before myself, until my death unmake me.*

"It wasn't poetry to me," he said. "That's what nobody understands about vows. Everyone thinks they're ceremony. A vow is a door, and when you mean it — when you *mean* it, all the way through — you open that door, and things can walk through it from the other side. Things that have been waiting for someone sincere enough to let them in." His hands tightened on each other. "Something came through mine. It had been circling the crown for generations, the god tells me now — I didn't know then, I know things now that I wish I didn't. It wore my shape. It sat my throne. And it put *me* down here, in the body, in the dark, and began to eat."

"Eat what?" Mirabel asked softly.

"Me. Slowly. A memory at a meal. It started with my childhood — I don't remember my mother's face anymore, I remember that I had one. Then my friends. Then whole years. It keeps the useful parts — languages, law, the names of ambassadors — and it eats the rest. Every year it grows stronger and I grow thinner. It has eaten almost everything except the vow. It can't eat the vow. The vow is the door it came through, and a door cannot eat itself." He looked up, and his ordinary brown eyes were wet. "Do you understand? I am a man made of one sentence now. Everything else is its leavings."

Wren's throat hurt. They thought of the war-crypt, of the ghost soldiers whose oaths the crown had swallowed, of speaking the mending aloud. They thought of their own broken vow, carried like a stone in the chest for nine years.

"Why hasn't it finished?" they asked. "Ten years. Why are you still here?"

"Because finishing requires the thirteenth turn. The moon that shouldn't exist. On that night, the eclipse closes the circle, and if the fragments aren't restored — if the original vow isn't made whole and spoken — the devouring completes, and there won't be an Aldric left anywhere. Not in the body, not in the crown, not in the dark. Just it, forever, with my face." He paused. "And if the vow *is* restored —"

"Then we free you," Mirabel said. "That's the whole point. The god sent us. We've bled for these fragments, we've lost—" her voice caught, thinking of the abbot, of Sefa, of the chapel where she'd paid in memories, "—we've given more than I can name. In three nights, under the eclipsed moon, we speak the vow, the crown breaks, and you wake up."

"Yes," Aldric said gently. "That is what happens. And now let me tell you what happens *after*, because the god of oaths, with all respect to your herald's faith, has a habit of leaving out the fine print. It's how he got into this trouble the first time."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled by fifty feet of stone, the palace bells rang the hour.

"When the crown breaks," Aldric said, "the spirit doesn't die. You can't kill it — it isn't alive, it's a hunger, and hungers don't die, they only move. For ten years it has been shaped to me. Fitted to me, like a key to a lock. But a key that loses its lock goes looking for another." His eyes moved from Mirabel to Wren and back. "A herald full of holy fire. An oathbreaker with a vow still bleeding. A sister waiting outside the walls. Anyone who has ever meant a promise with their whole heart. The moment it leaves me, it will be starving, and blind, and desperate, and it will take the first open door it finds."

"So we contain it," Wren said. "There must be a way. Iron, salt, a binding—"

"There is a way." He said it simply, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Me. As long as I am alive and willing, the door stays shut on its old lock. If I die *before* it leaves me — if the vow is restored and I refuse to wake, if I let go while it's still fitted to me — it dies with the vessel. Starves in a collapsing house. That is the price, and it is not a large one, and I have had ten years in the dark to make peace with it." He leaned forward, and his voice cracked clean through the rust. "So I am begging you. Both of you. Herald, oathbreaker — I don't care what you've sworn to the god or to each other. When the moment comes, if freeing me means loosing that thing into the world — *let me go.* Say the vow, break the crown, and let me choose the dark. I have protected them for ten years from down here. Let me protect them once more from up there. It's the only way left to me to keep the sentence true."

Mirabel was crying without noise, the way people cry who learned young that noise costs extra. "You're asking us to kill you."

"I'm asking you to *not resurrect* me. There's a difference, and it matters to me enormously." The wobbling smile again. "I'm asking for mercy. I'm told that's a hard word in this kingdom lately."

* * *

Later — Wren couldn't have said how much later; time moved strangely down there, in the breath-radius of one repeated sentence — Mirabel stepped outside to the foot of the stair, to pray, or to argue with her god, or both. Wren stayed.

The king watched them with those tired brown eyes.

"You're the Calloway," he said. "The one from the trial. I remember — " he stopped, corrected himself, and the correction was worse than the forgetting. "I remember *that I used to* remember. There were petitions. Your sworn brother spoke for you at the tribunal, did you know? Vane — no. That's your herald's name. Someone else. It doesn't matter." He shook his head slowly. "What matters is I signed your exile order. Or it wore my hand while it signed. I've wondered for ten years whether there's a difference, and I find I still don't know."

"There isn't," Wren said. "I stopped needing it to matter." They looked at the stick-figure drawing on the wall, the two figures under the many-rayed sun. "Who drew that?"

"My daughter." The word came out of him like an arrow. "She was six. She's — " his face worked. "Sixteen now. She brings flowers to the throne every week because she thinks her father loves them. It does love them. It loves everything it eats." His hands were shaking now, the repetition faltering, and Wren saw with horror how much effort it cost him to hold the sentence even this long. "If you let it escape to keep me alive, it will wear me to her. It will tuck her in at night with my voice. Do you understand what I'm — I will protect them, I will protect them, I will — "

"Breathe," Wren said, and knelt, and took the shaking hands. "We hear you. We hear you, Your Majesty."

"Aldric," he whispered. "Please. Down here I'm just Aldric. Kings are what it wears."

For a long moment Wren held the hands of a dead man who hadn't been permitted to finish dying, and thought about mercy, and duty, and how everyone always said they were different things, and how standing here, they felt exactly the same weight in exactly the same arms.

Above them, faint through fifty feet of stone, the bells began to ring again — wrong, arrhythmic, the pattern the palace used only one day in thirteen.

Mirabel appeared in the archway, pale as the candlelight. "Wren. The moon. It's early — the god says it's turning *early* — we have one night, not three."

Aldric closed his eyes. His lips resumed their silent sentence, steady as a heartbeat.

Wren stood, and found their voice was steadier than they expected.

"Then we'd better decide," they said, "which promise we're keeping."
