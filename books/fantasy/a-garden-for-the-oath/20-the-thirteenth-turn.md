## 20. The Thirteenth Turn

The eclipse began at moonrise, which was wrong, and everyone in the throne room knew it was wrong, and the knowing did nothing to stop it.

Mirabel stood at the foot of the dais with the three fragments laid out on a scrap of altar cloth she'd stolen from the ruined chapel — because if the god of oaths had taught her anything, it was that the vessel mattered less than the vow, but a vow deserved better than bare stone. The fragments were small things to look at. A strip of vellum gone soft as skin. A shard of slate with *protect* worn nearly smooth by thumbs that had touched it for centuries. And the third, the one that could not be taken, only given — that one sat warm against her palm like a sleeping bird.

Above the throne, through the shattered crown of the ceiling the spirit had never bothered to repair, the thirteenth moon slid into the sun's shadow and turned the color of an old wound.

"You're stalling," Wren said beside her. They were holding their sword loosely, point down — not a threat yet, just a promise. "You've read that fragment four times."

"I'm checking my spelling."

"On a divine vow."

"Divine vows have the worst handwriting," Mirabel said, and Wren laughed, one short breath of it, and then stopped laughing because the doors at the far end of the throne room began to glow.

Not with fire. With absence. The Hollowed knights came through them the way frost comes through a window seam — patiently, from all sides at once, wearing armor that had once been ceremonial and now looked like something grown rather than forged. There were twelve of them. Mirabel counted twice, hoping to be wrong both times.

"Wren." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"I know." Wren rolled their shoulders and stepped forward, alone, into the long aisle between the pillars. "I'll hold the doors. You hold everything else."

"That's not how I'd split it."

"It's how you'd split it if you were honest about what you are." Wren glanced back, and in the eclipse-light their face was half shadow and half something softer. "You're the herald. I'm the oathbreaker. Turns out we each get the job we were named for. Go be a miracle, Vane. I'll go be a wall."

And they walked toward twelve knights as though walking toward old friends, because some of them were.

* * *

The fragments did not want to come together.

That was the first thing the god had never told her, or perhaps hadn't known: a broken vow has edges, and edges remember being cut. When Mirabel laid the piece of *to protect* against the piece of *to serve*, the gap between them hummed like a struck wire, and her fingers went numb to the wrist.

*They will resist,* said the god, in the voice that was everywhere and nowhere and mostly, tonight, inside her own ribs. *A vow is not a rope to be knotted back together, herald. It is a bone. It must be set.*

"Then tell me how."

*You already know.* A pause, and she felt him gather himself around her like weather. *It must be spoken whole, by a throat that means it. Not recited. Meant. That is the entire art of oaths, Mirabel Vane, and it is the one thing I cannot do for you.*

"The god of oaths can't say an oath."

*I am the keeping of them. The saying was always yours.* Something moved through his voice that she had learned, over these months, to hear as gentleness. *I failed this once before. I will not pretend otherwise. But tonight I am asking you to do what no herald has done: speak a king's promise as though it were your own.*

"And if I mean it?"

*Then the crown comes off.*

She picked up the third fragment. It was lighter than paper should be. It had cost her a memory she could no longer find, and left behind a hole shaped exactly like certainty, so that now every tender thing she felt was suspect — even this, even the fierce terrified tenderness she carried watching Wren walk toward the knights. Was that hers? Had it ever been hers? She had decided, somewhere on the frozen lake road, that it didn't matter. Love that does its work doesn't need provenance.

She pressed the three pieces together.

They fused with a sound like a door unlocking after decades — and King Aldric screamed.

Not the man on the throne. The man beneath it. From the crypts below, up through the flagstones, through Mirabel's boots and bones, came a voice worn down to a thread: *no — not again — don't let it—*

And the thing on the throne opened its eyes.

* * *

For ten years the court had believed King Aldric's eyes were pale blue. They had been, once. What opened now was the color of a page held too close to flame, and behind it was hunger with a history.

"Herald." The spirit spoke through Aldric's mouth, and it used the king's voice beautifully, kindly, the way a thief uses a good coat. "You've brought me a gift. Do you know how long I've waited for someone to assemble it? Three hundred years I've been picking at the seams of that vow, and you've handed me the whole garment."

"You can't touch it," Mirabel said. "It isn't yours anymore. It was never yours. That's the whole point of you."

"Mm. Points." The crowned head tilted. Around the throne room, the Hollowed knights paused mid-stride, and Mirabel realized with a lurch that Wren had actually stopped them — twelve of them, held at the pillar-line by one disgraced knight and a great deal of stubbornness. "Let us skip past the points to the offer, since you are a woman who reads contracts for a living. I have read your debt-bond, Mirabel Vane. All nine pages. Your family owed the Thornfell temple two hundred crowns; the temple tacked interest the way ivy takes a wall. I am a crown. Crowns forgive debts. One word, and your sister Sefa is free — free of the bond, free of the abbot who sold her to me as bait, free to study whatever she likes wherever she likes for the rest of her long life."

"Sefa's already free. She forged us a writ with her own hand."

"Free-er, then." The smile widened. "Wren Calloway, then. Exile lifted. Name restored. Knighthood reinstated with honors — oh, don't make that face, I know what you did at the lake shrine, child, I eat vows, I taste them across kingdoms, and theirs is the most delicious I've tasted in a century. A vow so heavy they broke it to save lives. I would give them back their honor with both hands."

"They don't want it from you."

"And finally—" the spirit leaned forward, and the eclipse-light pooled in the hollows of Aldric's face like water in a skull "—you. You paid a memory to the chapel. Sloppy work. I can give you the change. Every memory back, every one, plus the ones you never made: your mother's face. Your father's voice. The name of the first person you loved. I can make you whole, Mirabel Vane. Whole, wealthy, and unbound — and all I ask is the scrap of parchment in your hand."

The throne room was very quiet. Somewhere near the doors, steel rang against steel and stopped ringing.

Mirabel found she was crying, which annoyed her, because she'd meant to be magnificent.

"You're offering me my memories," she said, "for the freedom of a man you've spent ten years eating."

"I'm offering you a fair price for fair goods."

"No." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, ink-stained as always, and felt suddenly, enormously calm. "No, here's what you're doing. You're doing what the temple did. You're doing what the abbot did, what the debt did, what every contract I ever copied did. You're pricing things that aren't for sale. My memories weren't currency when I gave them and they aren't currency now. And he—" she nodded at the throne, at the gentle empty-eyed man flickering underneath it like a face under moving water "—he is not goods. He's a king who made a vow, and the vow says protect, and serve, and love, and you have broken all three clauses for ten years, and I am the herald of the god whose entire existence is telling you that's not allowed."

The spirit's patience ended.

Aldric's body rose off the throne without using its legs, robes hanging wrong, and the eclipse outside deepened until the thirteenth moon was a black coin in a black sky, and every candle in the throne room guttered toward the dais as though the dark were inhaling.

* * *

"NOW, MIRABEL!"

Wren's voice, cracked and furious, from the pillar-line — they had both arms bloody to the elbow and three knights down and were losing ground by the heartbeat. Mirabel didn't look. Looking was a luxury. She lifted the reassembled vow above her head with both hands, the way she'd seen pilgrims lift offerings, the way she'd once lifted a ledger to swear a debt she'd never chosen.

And she spoke.

"To protect." Her voice shook. She let it. "To protect the weak with my strength and the strong with my mercy, and to spend myself before I spend them."

The first fragment burned white in her grip.

"To serve." Louder now, and the words were coming up from somewhere below memory, below identity, from the place where the god had been living inside her ribs all year. "To serve the realm before my name, the truth before my comfort, and the small hours before the glorious ones."

The second fragment burned.

The spirit came off the dais at her like a landslide, and she kept speaking, tears freezing on her cheeks in air gone suddenly winter-cold, and through the roaring dark she heard Wren screaming her name and twelve sets of armored feet and beneath it all, faint and fraying, the thread of a buried king begging.

"And to love." Quieter. Almost tender. "Not the crown. Not the power. The people. Even the ones who curse me. Even the ones who forgot me. Especially them. This I vow, until the vow outlives me—"

"—and it will," said the god, through her mouth and his own and every written line in every book in the palace, "outlive us all."

Light went through the throne room like dawn through a keyhole.

* * *

But the spirit did not crumble.

Mirabel had imagined — she would admit later, in the wreckage of everything, that she had imagined — a clean ending. Light, shriek, ash. Instead the devouring thing simply *turned*, the way a debtor turns to a new creditor, and poured itself out of Aldric's body in a black ribbon that circled her once, twice, considering, and then spoke directly into her ear with a voice like a closing ledger.

"Clever herald. You've freed the host. But a spirit must live somewhere, and I have ten years of practice at living in kings. And you — you are full to the brim with borrowed divinity. Such roomy accommodations."

It coiled toward her chest.

And the god of oaths moved between them — not as weather, not as voice, but *present*, vast and wounded and absolutely immovable, the way a wall is present — and said, in a register that cracked the mosaic floor:

"Not her."

"She was always going to be the price," the spirit hissed. "You knew that when you chose her. You knew what speaking that vow would cost, you old hypocrite. You've done this before. You fed a king to me once to save a kingdom, and now you'll feed her to yourself, and call it mercy—"

"Mirabel." The god's voice dropped, suddenly, terribly human. "The bargain stands. Your memories for the king's freedom. It was always the only arithmetic I had. Give them to me willingly and I can hold him — all of him, forever, sealed in the keeping where vows go when they're kept. You will forget this year. You will forget Wren. You will wake tomorrow a scribe with no debt and no scars and no idea why she cries at eclipses. And the kingdom keeps its king, and I keep my failure quiet one more century."

Everything staked. Everything offered. The eclipse at its fullest, the thirteenth turn complete, the moment balanced on a scribe's answer.

Mirabel looked at Wren — bleeding, kneeling, watching her with an expression she couldn't read and wasn't sure she had the right to read — and thought: *I could keep them. I could keep every single one, and lose him instead.*

She opened her mouth to choose.

And found, rising in her like water finding its level, a third answer — one the god had never listed, one no contract had ever contained, one that belonged entirely to her — and began to laugh through her tears, because it was so simple, and so unfair, and so completely, gloriously *hers*.
