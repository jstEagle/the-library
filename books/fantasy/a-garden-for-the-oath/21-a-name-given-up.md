## 21. A Name Given Up

The eclipse had swallowed the moon whole, and the throne room was lit by nothing but the god.

He stood in the broken windows — not a body, exactly, but a presence shaped like one, tall and patient and made of the same gold light that lived in every vow ever written. Behind him, the crown-spirit writhed on King Aldric's head like smoke trying to remember it had once been fire.

*Choose,* the god said, and his voice came from everywhere at once, from the stones, from Wren's sword-belt, from the ink still drying on Mirabel's own skin where she'd written the reassembled vow across her forearms like a woman who did not trust her memory to hold anything anymore. *Her memories for his freedom. That is the price. That has always been the price.*

"I know," Mirabel said.

The spirit laughed. It was a horrible sound, warm as a hearth and wrong as a hearth in a drowning house. *She's clever, little scribe. But cleverness won't save you. Give me the fragments, and I'll give you everything. Your debt erased. Your sister safe. Your oathbreaker pardoned. You could go home. You could be* ***nobody's****.*

"That's the problem," Mirabel said. "You keep offering me things that were never yours to give."

*Everything is mine to give. I have worn this kingdom for ten years. I have eaten its oaths like bread.*

"Yes," Mirabel said. "And look how hungry you still are."

Behind her, she heard Wren shift — the small sound of someone planting their feet, which was, in Mirabel's experience, the sound Wren made immediately before doing something either heroic or catastrophic, and usually both. The Hollowed knights hammered at the great doors. Somewhere beyond them, Sefa was holding a forged writ and a lie the size of a cathedral between herself and the abbot's men, and Mirabel could not think about that right now or she would come apart like wet paper.

"Here's what I've learned about vows," Mirabel said, stepping forward, "from carrying three pieces of one across half a mountain range while being chased by knights made of other people's promises."

*Mirabel,* the god said, and there was something new in his voice. Something almost like fear. *What are you doing?*

"You said memories for freedom. Those are the terms. Those have always been the terms." She held up her arms, the vow written in her own hand, in her own blood-ink. "*Protect. Serve. Love.* Three words. Three prices. And every price you've ever asked, you've asked of the person making the vow — because a vow binds the one who speaks it. That's your whole nature, isn't it? You can't touch anything that isn't bound."

The spirit went very still on the king's head. Stillness, on a thing made of hunger, was worse than any movement.

"But here's what you never accounted for," Mirabel said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her, because everything else in her was shaking. "I was bound before I ever met you. Before I met *him*." She nodded at the god. "Bound by debt. Bound by contract. Bound by name. My family's debt lives in my name, in the temple ledgers, in every line the Thornfell scriptorium ever wrote about the Vane family. As long as I am Mirabel Vane, I can be claimed. By the temple. By the debt. By you."

*You would give up your memories,* the spirit said. *That is the bargain. There is no third door.*

"There's always a third door," said Wren, behind her, dry as chalk dust. "There's always a third door with her. I've been saying this for months. Nobody listens."

Mirabel almost laughed. Later, she would want to remember that — that she'd almost laughed, at the end of everything, because Wren Calloway could not let a dramatic moment pass without puncturing it. She held on to that. She held on to all of it: the frozen lake and Wren's face when the water showed them the worst day of their life; Sefa at the border, furious and loyal; the war-crypt full of ghosts learning to rest; the ruined chapel where she had paid for 'to love' with the dearest thing she owned and woken up unsure which loves were hers.

She held on to all of it, and then she prepared to let go of the one thing holding it together.

"A vow binds the one who speaks it," she said again. "So unbind me. From everything."

*Mirabel—*

"Not just from him." Her hand rose toward the spirit. "From the temple. From the debt. From the contract my grandfather signed before I was born. From every vow anyone ever made *for* me, in my name, with my name. You want a price? Take the name. Not my memories — my *name*. Let there be no Mirabel Vane for any ledger, any god, any crown, any contract to hold onto. Unbind me from all of it, and I will speak Aldric's vow whole, freely, with nothing left in me that anyone else owns."

The silence that followed was the kind of silence that has weight. The Hollowed knights had stopped hammering at the doors. Even the eclipse seemed to lean closer through the broken glass.

*That is not a door I built,* the god said slowly.

"No," Mirabel agreed. "It's one I mapped. It's what I do."

* * *

Later — in whatever later existed after this — she would try to explain what it felt like, and she would fail, because language was her trade and even she couldn't find the words.

It felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing. It felt like the moment at the top of a mountain pass when the wind takes the last of the valley's air out of your lungs and replaces it with sky. It felt like finishing a page.

The god came down out of the windows and stood before her, and up close he was less a figure than a library — she could see vows moving in him like fish under ice, thousands of them, centuries of them, promises made in fields and sickrooms and wars, all of it turning slowly in his light.

*If I do this,* he said, *you will remain. But you will remain unbound. No vow will hold you. Not mine. Not theirs.* He gestured, and the great doors shuddered — the Hollowed knights beyond them, the temple's writs, the debt that had chained her to a copying desk since she was fourteen. *Not even the ones you choose. Do you understand what I'm offering? Freedom means the right to walk away from everything, including me.*

"I know," Mirabel said. "That's why it's worth giving up a name for."

*And your memories stay. All of them. Even the ones the fragment took payment for — they'll return to you, though some may return strange.* His light dimmed, gentled. *But understand this too, herald. A name is not a small thing. It is the handle by which love grips us. Your sister calls you by it. Your oathbreaker—*

"I know," Mirabel said again, softer. "Give it to the garden. Names should grow somewhere."

The spirit screamed.

It was not a sound so much as an unmaking — ten years of borrowed authority realizing, all at once, that its host was about to become a man again, and a man with a whole vow, spoken freely, was a house it could never haunt. It poured off Aldric's head in black rivers, coiling toward Mirabel, reaching for the name it thought it could still claim —

—and found nothing to grip.

She felt it try. She felt it pour over her like cold water over oiled cloth, searching for Mirabel Vane, debtor, apostate, herald, daughter, sister, scribe — and finding only a woman standing in a ruined throne room under an eclipsed moon, belonging to no one, bound to nothing, free.

"Oh," she said, wonderingly. "Oh, it doesn't *fit* anymore."

Then she spoke the vow.

"*To protect. To serve. To love.*" Three fragments, carried over ice and ghost-haunted stone and burned chapel, joined now in her mouth like bones knitting. "*As king to kingdom, as man to world, as one soul to another — so I swear, and so I am sworn, and so I am.*"

The crown crumbled.

It did not shatter dramatically, the way stories promised. It simply came apart, quietly, piece by piece, like frost giving up on a window in spring — and the black rivers of the spirit thinned, and thinned, and the god opened what might have been a hand, and gathered the last of it in, and it was gone. Not destroyed. Swallowed. Filed away in that vast turning library of him, one more broken promise among thousands, kept at last by force.

And King Aldric fell to his knees on the steps of his own throne and began, very quietly, to weep.

* * *

He was smaller than Mirabel had expected. Ten years of a devouring spirit will hollow a man, and what was left was thin and gray-eyed and looked, in the eclipse-dark, like a sketch of himself done quickly by someone in a hurry.

He knelt there on the cold stone and cried like a child, hands pressed flat to the floor, and the sound he made was not kingly at all. It was the sound of a man waking from a fever to find the war over and everyone gone home.

"The gardens," he said, when he could speak. His voice was rusted from disuse. "Are the palace gardens — did anyone — I used to grow—"

"They're still there," Mirabel said. She knelt too, because it seemed rude to loom over a weeping king, even a liberated one. "A bit wild. But wild things grow back."

"I had roses." He was looking at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "White ones. My mother's cuttings. I used to talk to them. Everyone thought it was odd." A wet, cracked laugh. "It was the only part of me it couldn't eat, I think. It tried. It ate my vows and my judgments and my — my *face*, most days, the way people saw me. But it couldn't make me stop wanting the roses."

"Gentle humor," Wren said from the doorway, where they were leaning on their sword and watching the doors for Hollowed knights who had, miraculously, stopped existing as a threat — the god's unbinding had rolled through them like a tide, and half of them stood blinking in the corridor like people waking from a long shared dream. "Ten years possessed by an eating-shadow and his first concern is his roses. I like him already."

"Wren," Mirabel said.

"What? I'm being *supportive*."

King Aldric looked up at them — at the disgraced knight and the nameless scribe — and his gray eyes were clear for the first time in a decade, and wet, and terribly, ordinarily human. "Who are you?" he asked. "Truly. What do I call you?"

And there it was. The loss, arriving on schedule, dressed as a simple question.

Mirabel reached inside herself for the answer — *Mirabel, your majesty, Mirabel Vane, scribe of Thornfell, herald of the god of oaths* — and found the shelf empty. Not the memories. The memories were all there, bright and whole, the lake and the crypt and Sefa's furious tear-streaked face and Wren laughing in the snow with icicles in their hair. Every room of her was furnished. But the nameplate on the front door was gone, and when she reached for it, her fingers closed on smooth wood.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I gave it up. It was the price. Or — it was the loophole. It turns out those look similar from the inside."

"She gave up her *name*," Wren told the king, with the particular pride of someone describing a friend's outrageous exploit. "Whole thing. To the god. So nobody — not the temple, not the debt, not that—" a jerk of their chin at the empty air where the spirit had been, "—could ever claim her again. It worked. Obviously. Everything she does works, it's infuriating."

"It was a foolish risk," said the god. He had drawn near without anyone noticing, which was apparently a thing gods of oaths could do. His light had gone soft and low, banked like a winter fire. *One no one foresaw. Including me.* He said it plainly, without shame, and Mirabel understood suddenly that this admission cost him more than the battle had. *I offered you two roads, herald. You built a third. That is why I chose you, I think, though I did not know it at the time. A god of vows needs someone who can read the margins.*

"Is Aldric free?" Mirabel asked. "Truly? Forever?"

*As long as his vow holds, and he holds it freely, yes.* The god's light turned toward the kneeling king, and something old and sad moved in it. *I failed a king once, long ago. I have been waiting a very long time to not fail another one.* He extended the not-hand, and Aldric took it, and rose, shaking, mortal, weeping still but differently now — weeping the way people weep at weddings.

"And me?" Mirabel asked. "What happens to me?"

*Whatever you choose,* the god said. *That is the entire point of what you did. You are the first unbound soul in this kingdom's history. Every road is open. Every door. Even mine.* A pause, and then, with the closest thing to gentle humor a god of oaths could manage: *Though I note, for the record, that you are still standing in my light, and you have not walked away yet.*

"I noticed that too," Mirabel admitted.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and clean over the capital, and the eclipse released the moon, and the impossible thirteenth turn finished itself and became, simply, a moon — ordinary, silver, slightly the worse for wear.

They put King Aldric in the least ceremonial cloak anyone could find, on the theory that a man should meet his rescued kingdom dressed like a person, and Wren went ahead to shout at guardsmen until it became clear the Hollowed orders had dissolved with the spirit, and Sefa arrived at a run with ink on her fingers and a forged writ still clutched in her fist like a weapon she hadn't been able to put down.

She skidded to a stop in the shattered doorway of the throne room, took one look at the waking king, one look at the god's fading light, and one look at her sister — and then stopped, frowning, in a way that Mirabel recognized with a lurch, because she knew that frown. It was the frown Sefa had worn at seven years old, counting spoons.

"Mira?" Sefa said.

The old name landed soft as snow. It wasn't the name. It wasn't the debt-name, the ledger-name, the name the temple had owned. It was the kitchen-name, the garden-name, the one that had never been written in any contract — and it fit, loosely, the way an old coat fits, warm but no longer fastened.

"I'm here," Mirabel said. "I'm — mostly here. All the memories. None of the handles."

Sefa crossed the room and hugged her hard enough to creak ribs, and whispered into her hair, "Whatever you gave up, I'll say it enough for both of us. That's what sisters are for."

Outside, the city was waking to rumors, and the rumors were going to need managing, and there were Hollowed knights in the corridors remembering how to be people, and an abbot's death to grieve, and a kingdom to explain itself to. There was so much to do. Mirabel stood in the ruined throne room with her sister's arms around her and Wren's shoulder warm against hers and the god's light sinking gently into the stones like rain into drought-cracked earth, and she felt the strangest thing, under all the grief and triumph:

She felt unhurried.

For the first time since a dying pilgrim's prayer had cracked her life open like an egg, nothing was chasing her. Nothing owned her. The debt was ash. The bond was ash. The crown was dust in the gutters, and the thirteenth moon had turned its last turn, and somewhere in the palace below, a buried body-self was finally, peacefully allowed to die, its scrap of vow fulfilled at last.

"We should go somewhere," Wren said. Their voice was rough. They were looking at her with an expression she remembered — remembered perfectly, thanks to the god's strange mercy — from the frozen lake, from the war-crypt, from a hundred campfires. Love, real or borrowed or chosen, it was there, and it was hers, whichever shelf it had come from. "Somewhere quiet. With dirt in it. You mentioned once — a garden. For the oath."

"I don't remember mentioning that," Mirabel said.

"You will," said the god, from everywhere and nowhere, sounding suspiciously pleased with himself. *Give it time. Some vows take root slowly.*

And in the corner of the ruined throne room, where the crown had crumbled, where the spirit had made its last stand and lost — a single green shoot had pushed up through the cracks in the stone, unfurling its first leaf toward the dawn, as if the floor itself had been waiting ten years for permission to grow.

Mirabel looked at it for a long moment.

"Well," she said. "I suppose we'd better find a trowel."
