## 22. A Garden for the Oath

The throne room had been a battlefield, and then it had been a ruin, and then — slowly, the way most true things happen — it had become a garden.

The nameless woman who tended it had been told all of this many times. She knew the story the way she knew her own hands: intimately, fondly, and without any certainty of ownership.

She knelt in the old dais where the throne had stood, pressing seeds into the cracks between flagstones with her thumbs. The stone was warm under her fingers even though the morning was cold enough to frost her breath. It had been warm since the day the crown crumbled, the gardeners told her — warm like something buried there had finally stopped being dead.

"Careful with those," said Wren Calloway from the doorway. They came every day at this hour, boots ringing on marble, carrying two cups of cider and an opinion about everything. "That's the west bed. You said you wanted the moonflowers there."

"I did?" She looked down at the seeds in her palm. Small, dark, unremarkable. "How do you know what I want?"

"You told me yesterday." Wren handed her a cup and settled onto the broken step beside her, the way they always did, as if the ruin had been built with sitting in mind. "You also told me you wanted the feverfew kept away from the thyme, because — your words — *they quarrel*."

"They do," she said, before she could think about whether she knew that. Then she frowned at herself. "Do they?"

"How should I know? You're the one who talks to them."

She laughed. That was the strange arithmetic of her days: she remembered almost nothing of who she had been, and yet she laughed more than she ever had, according to Wren, who kept count of such things with the fierce precision of someone guarding a treasury.

"Tell me again," she said. "The part where you fell through the ice."

"That's not how it happened."

"You've told it four different ways."

"And every one of them is true, which is the trouble with oaths and stories both." Wren blew on their cider. "Fine. The part where I *gracefully entered* the frozen lake—"

* * *

It had taken months for the garden to claim the room. The first summer, the shoot by the throne became a vine; the vine became a wall of green that climbed the shattered dais and put out white flowers that only opened when someone spoke truthfully nearby. The god of oaths had found this extremely funny.

"He says the flowers are his idea of a joke," she reported, once she'd learned to hear him — not through written vows anymore, but through growing things, which was slower and smelled better.

"A god with a sense of humor," Wren had said darkly. "That's how it starts."

He had stayed, after everything. He lived in the temple now, or near it, or in the margins of its books — nobody could say exactly — and he had made one request of the nameless woman, the only request he ever made:

*Tend what grows here. Not because you owe it. Because nothing grows well under debt.*

She had agreed. Freely. That word still felt new in her mouth, like a coin from a country she'd never visited.

Sefa came on market days. Her sister — and this she knew the way she knew the sun rose, not by memory but by a certainty deeper than memory — Sefa would sit among the bean rows and talk about the scriptorium, the abbot who had replaced the old one, the new contracts written plainly now, in language a debtor could read. "No more hidden clauses," Sefa said. "I check every one myself. It's what I'm for."

"You were never for that," the nameless woman said. "You're for better things."

"So are you." Sefa would take her hand across the bean rows. "So are you."

And King Aldric came sometimes too, mortal now and gray at the temples, walking unannounced through the city with a single guard who mostly pretended to be a gardener. He would stand among the flowers and weep quietly, and thank her in words she didn't fully understand — words about a vow reassembled, protect and serve and love, spoken under an eclipsed moon by a woman whose name had gone out of the world.

"I don't remember doing that," she always said.

"No," he'd answer. "But the kingdom does. Every oath sworn since that night is a little stronger. You did that. Whatever name you wear now, you did that."

* * *

Wren told her their story every day. Not because she asked — though she usually asked — but because Wren had decided, somewhere in the ruins of the capital, that a person should get to choose their own history, and choosing required options.

"This is the version where I was noble," Wren said today, gesturing with the cider cup. "I had seen the crown eating the king. I'd stood guard outside the council chamber and watched Aldric's eyes go empty mid-sentence, and I knew if I stayed I'd be Hollowed like the rest, so I broke my vow and ran, and my running saved the fragments of the truth, and eventually saved the king. Noble version."

"And the other version?"

"The other version is that I was terrified and selfish and I ran because running was the only thing I was good at." Wren shrugged. "Both are true. You get to pick which one you love me for."

She considered this seriously, the way she considered everything, turning it over like a seed. "What if I love you for the way you bring cider?"

"That's not one of the options."

"It's the one I'm picking anyway."

Wren went pink to the ears. Ten years of exile and hollowed knights and gods, and it was cider that undid them. She filed this away among the small treasures she was building for herself — a shelf of moments that belonged to no former life, only to this one. A laugh over the feverfew. Sefa's hand in hers. The warmth of the stones. The smell of turned earth after rain.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked once. "Who I was?"

Wren was quiet for a long time. "I miss her," they said finally. "Every day. But I don't mourn her, because she's not gone. She's — composting." They winced at their own metaphor. "That came out wrong."

"No," she said, smiling. "It came out exactly right. Nothing in a garden is wasted. It all goes around."

* * *

The god visited her in the green hours, when the light came slanting through the broken roof and the bees started up their work.

*You are happy,* he observed, from the direction of a flowering vine. He sounded pleased with himself, which he nearly always did.

"I am."

*This troubles you.*

"A little. I keep waiting to feel like something's missing. Everyone acts like I lost something enormous." She brushed soil from her knees. "I know what I gave up. I gave up my *name*, not my self. And names grow back. Like—" she gestured at the garden— "well. Like everything here."

*Some vows take root slowly,* the god said. *Names especially.*

"What does that mean?"

He declined to answer, being a god, but the vine nearest her put out a new leaf, which she had learned to read as divine smugness.

There was one thing she had never asked anyone, because asking felt like breaking something fragile. She almost asked it now. Instead she pressed another seed into the earth and let the question stay underground, where questions, like seeds, did their real work in the dark.

* * *

On the last morning of autumn, she planted the thing she had been saving.

Wren had brought it weeks ago — a small dried seed head from the drowned shrine beneath the frozen lake, gathered while she wasn't looking, kept without explanation. "For when you're ready," was all they'd say.

She held it now over the warm stone where the throne had been, where the crown had eaten a king for ten years, where a woman she couldn't remember had spoken three broken vows whole and paid for it with the word everyone used to call her by.

"What is it?" Wren asked softly.

"I think you know."

"I think I do. But tell me anyway. Telling's the whole point of us."

She turned the seed head in her fingers. Inside each dry socket was a single seed — pale, faintly luminous, the kind of seed that grew only in places where something true had been drowned and survived anyway.

"It's from the shrine," she said. "From the water that shows you your worst memory." She looked up. "You went back for it. After everything. Why?"

"Because she would have wanted it planted." Wren's voice was steady, but their hands weren't. "The woman who walked into that water knowing what it would show her. She was brave in ways I'll spend the rest of my life describing badly. Someone ought to keep her seeds."

The god said nothing at all, which from him was a kind of holding of breath.

She pressed the seeds into the stone crack, one by one, in the exact place where the first shoot had come up the morning after the crown fell. She covered them with warm earth. She watered them from her own cup, because it seemed right that they should be fed from the same hand that fed everything else here.

And then — because the garden had taught her this, if it had taught her anything — she spoke to them. Not a prayer. Not a vow. Just a plain sentence, freely given, which was the only currency this place had ever truly accepted.

"My name is gone," she said. "And I'm not sorry, because what I have instead is mine, all of it, chosen fresh every morning. But a garden needs labels. So."

She sat back on her heels. The frost was melting off the bean rows. Somewhere above, the god was very carefully pretending not to listen, and failing completely.

"I think I'd like to be called Mirabel."

The ground did not shake. No vision came. The old god of oaths did not thunder from every written thing in the kingdom.

But every flower in the ruined throne room opened at once — moonflowers, feverfew, the white truth-blooms on the vine — and the warm stone beneath her palms pulsed once, gently, like a heart remembering its rhythm, and Wren Calloway made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and reached for her hand.

"Well," said Mirabel, looking out over her garden, at the green coming up through everything, at the world she was going to keep choosing. "I suppose we'd better find a trowel."
