## 2. Salt on the Wind

The rumor came down the mountain with the autumn wool carts, riding in the mouths of drovers who talked the way weather does—everywhere at once and meaning nothing yet.

Rafael heard it first at the Waymeet inn in Hollowmere, where she had gone to collect payment for a survey of the upper Kestrel valley that would never appear on any crown chart. She sat in the corner with her back to the wall and her hands around a cup of barley tea gone cold an hour ago, and she listened.

"The king's proclaimed it from the steps of the Sunhold itself," said a drover with a beard full of chaff. "A hunt. For the gifted. Every soul born with the land-sense is to be named, registered, brought to the capital for—" he made a vague gesture, the kind men make when the word they want is *burning* but they haven't the stomach for it, "—*assessment*."

"Assessment," snorted the innkeeper's wife, refilling cups she hadn't been asked to refill because gossip was cheaper than ale and kept men in their seats. "Old Aldric's had thirty years to assess the gifted. Half his surveyors are gifted. His own grandmother was said to hear the rivers sing."

"That was before." The drover lowered his voice in the way of men who wanted to be overheard. "Before the queen died. Before he changed."

Rafael drank her cold tea and said nothing.

She had been saying nothing for ten years, and she was very good at it. She wore plainness like armor: gray wool, brown boots, a cartographer's satchel scuffed at every corner. Her hair was bound back with cord, her face kept forgettable, her eyes downcast when strangers spoke. In the lowlands they called her Rafe Whitlock, a journeyman mapmaker of no particular distinction, competent with a transit and a plumb line, unremarkable in every way a person could be unremarkable. She had spent a decade building that woman the way other people built houses—stone by stone, careful, load-bearing.

And underneath the house, the thing she hid.

She felt it even now, sitting in the smoky common room of the Waymeet: the land beneath the village breathing its slow green breath. The mountains' living magic ran through Thornfell like sap through a tree, and Rafael could feel it the way a blind woman feels warmth through a wall—pressure, direction, pulse. The Kestrel ran bright and quick under its stones two miles east. The old pine forest on the southern slope hummed with the drowsy contentment of things growing without witness. A fox denned under the mill; a badger's line crossed the north field; the village well drew from a vein of water that tasted faintly of iron and remembered rain.

None of which appeared on any map she'd ever sold.

That was the trick of her trade, the private joke she'd carried so long it had worn smooth as a riverstone: she was the best cartographer in three ranges, and none of her finest work existed. The smuggler roads she charted—the ones that wound through passes no crown surveyor had ever found, that bent around rockfall and marsh and the territorial pride of wolves—she found them not with instruments but by listening. The land *told* her where it wanted to be walked. Paths had a way of remembering feet. And Rafael Whitlock remembered what paths said.

She burned every such map after delivery. Always had. Ash told no tales, and neither did she.

Now the drover was saying, "They say there's a reward. Ten crowns for naming a gifted soul to the magistrates. Twenty if it's one what's been hiding."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when money enters a conversation about neighbors. Rafael watched faces around her—the miller's calculating stillness, the shepherd's too-quick glance at his own daughter, a girl of perhaps fourteen sitting very straight and very pale by the fire. The girl had the look. Rafael knew that look. It was the look of someone who had recently learned that what they carried was worth something to other people, and none of the somethings were good.

*Ten crowns.* Rafael turned the number over. It was more than she charged for six months of honest surveying. It was enough to make love expensive and suspicion cheap, and that, she understood, was precisely the point. You didn't need soldiers to hunt the gifted through a country like Thornfell. You needed only to make every neighbor a bounty board.

Distant politics, she told herself as she left coins on the table and slipped out into the blue evening. The crown's long arm didn't reach into the high passes. It never had. Kings forgot the mountains the moment the taxes were counted, and whatever madness had taken Aldric in his grief would burn itself out against the same stone it always burned against: distance, winter, and the fundamental indifference of the Thornfells to the affairs of lowlanders.

She was halfway up the switchback trail to the surveyor's cabin she kept above Hollowmere when the wind changed.

* * *

It came off the eastern shoulders of the range, where no wind should have come from at all—the prevailing air moved westward this time of year, she'd charted it a hundred times—and it moved wrong. Not fast, not violent. Wrong the way a familiar room is wrong when something has been shifted two inches in the dark and you cannot say what.

Rafael stopped on the trail and stood very still, the way she did when listening deep, deeper than ears.

The land spoke to her in pressures and flavors, and it had never once lied to her in ten years of listening. The pine forest hummed. The Kestrel ran bright. The deep roots of the mountains turned in their slow geological sleep, patient as scripture. All of it ordinary. All of it hers to feel and hide and love in secret, the way a man might love a voice through a wall, knowing the face behind it would ruin him.

And threaded through all of it, thin as wire, came salt.

Not the salt of sweat or tears or the mineral tang of certain springs—she knew those, catalogued them, mapped them. This was different. This was the salt of a dead sea, of brine evaporated to crust, of a thing dried out and ancient and *hungry*. It lay across the living magic of the mountain like a hand laid flat on a speaking mouth. Where it touched, the hum faltered. The pines on the eastern shoulder sang a half-tone flat. The stream that fed the upper Kestrel stuttered in its course like a stammerer trying to recall a word.

Rafael stood on the trail with the dusk coming down violet between the peaks, and she felt the salt pass over her and through the land beneath her and onward west, toward the villages, toward the valleys, toward everything green and alive that she had spent her life quietly loving from behind a wall of anonymity.

It paused. She would have sworn it paused—a pressure like a fingertip testing fruit—and for one vertiginous moment she had the sense of being *tasted*, of something vast and desiccated turning its attention along her thread of hidden magic the way a man turns his head at a name called in a crowd.

Then it moved on, westward, unhurried, and the mountain exhaled, and Rafael discovered she was gripping a birch sapling hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

"No," she said aloud, to the trail, to the trees, to herself. "No, I imagined that."

But the land didn't lie to her. That was the whole curse of her life—that it didn't lie, that she alone in the taverns and market squares and crown offices of the world got the truth unfiltered, raw and undeniable as bedrock. And the truth the land was telling now, in its mute language of pressure and flavor, was simple:

Something salty had entered the range. Something that did not belong to the grammar of growing things. Something that moved against the wind and silenced the song wherever it passed.

Rafael walked the rest of the way to her cabin fast, with her hand on her knife and her gift pulled tight around her like a cloak, reading every shadow twice.

* * *

The cabin sat in a fold of the mountain where two ridges met, invisible from any trail she hadn't cut herself. Inside, it was one room, spare as a monk's cell: cot, stove, worktable, and shelves of maps—not the smuggler charts, which never stayed the night, but honest surveys, contour lines and elevations, the respectable face of her trade. Tam slept in the loft.

Tam. Her apprentice. Sixteen years old, all elbows and earnestness, orphaned by a rockfall that Rafael had failed to foresee despite feeling the mountain groan about it for a week—guilt was the mortar of their arrangement, though neither of them named it. He was clever with instruments and hopeless at lying, which meant he knew almost nothing, which meant he was safe. That was the design. That had always been the design.

He was awake, of course, hunched over the stove copying practice contours onto scrap vellum, and he looked up when she came in with the cold still on her.

"You're late," he said. "There's talk in the village—"

"I know. The hunt." She hung her satchel on its peg. Normal motions. Normal voice. "It's lowland noise, Tam. Kings shout at things they can't reach. By spring they'll have forgotten the mountains exist."

"It's ten crowns," he said. "For naming someone."

"And how many folk in Hollowmere can name a gifted soul? None. Because there are none here." She said it smoothly, easily, the lie worn soft as an old glove, and hated herself for how smooth it was, and did it anyway. "Sit down. Finish your contours."

He sat. He finished. But he looked at her a beat too long before he did, and Rafael filed that away in the place where she kept things she refused to think about, and built the evening carefully around the hole.

Later, while he slept, she took down her private journal—the one written in her own cipher, the one that recorded not elevations but *songs*: the health of watersheds, the moods of forests, the slow tides of the land's living magic across the whole of the Thornfells. She turned to the eastern ranges and began, by candlelight, to write down what she had felt on the trail. Salt. Silence moving through song. A taste like a dead sea.

Her pen slowed. Stopped.

Because here was the thing she had not let herself think on the trail, the thing that now sat in her chest like swallowed ice: she had felt that flavor once before. Years ago. Faintly, briefly, at the far southern edge of her range—a brush of brine across the land's breath during the season the old queen died, so faint she'd convinced herself she'd imagined it. She had written it down then, too, and drawn a small question mark beside it, and never answered it.

She flipped back through the ciphered pages, years of them, her candle burning low, until she found it. There it was, in her younger, neater hand: *strange salinity in the wind, south slopes, no source. ?*

And below it, dated four months later, in the same hand: *queen dead. king in mourning.*

Coincidence. Grief makes the world seem flavored with death; that was all it was. Kings lost queens, and the land went on, and rumors came up the mountain and washed back down again, and the safe thing—the only thing—was to keep mapping, keep burning, keep invisible.

Rafael closed the journal and sat in the dark with her cold hands folded, and outside, far off in the eastern dark, the wind came over the shoulder of the mountain again.

She felt it before she heard it. Salt, thin as wire, lying across the sleeping song of the pines. And beneath the salt now—fainter, stranger, like a voice heard through a wall—something else. Not hunger exactly. *Attention.* The patient attention of a thing that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

The candle guttered. Somewhere in the loft, Tam turned over in his sleep.

Rafael sat unmoving until dawn, listening to the land breathe around the thing that moved through it, and made herself one promise, quiet and absolute as a surveyor's line: whatever came down from the capital, whatever the king had become in his grieving, she would stay invisible. She would stay gray and plain and forgettable, and she would keep her gift behind its wall, and the storm would pass over her the way storms always had.

She was already planning the next survey when the light came gray through the window—already choosing a route, already deciding what to burn—

When the eastern ridge shuddered.

Not earthquake-shudder. Smaller, sharper: the deep groan of stone letting go, transmitted through the bedrock into her palms where they pressed flat against the table. From the direction of the Kestrel gap, the high cliffs above the lower road—the road Tam would take today into Hollowmere for supplies.

Rafael was out the door before her boots were laced, running, feeling the mountain scream.
