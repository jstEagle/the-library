## 3. What the Apprentice Saw

The mountain spoke to Rafael before it broke.

She was bent over her drafting table when it happened, quill poised above a half-finished contour of the Greywater switchbacks, when the land beneath the cabin shuddered—not an earthquake's blunt shove but something subtler, a tightening, like a muscle flexing under skin. Her gift had no name she'd ever dared give it. It was simply this: she could feel the living weave of the earth, the slow green pulse that ran through root and stone and water, the way other people felt heat from a fire. And right now that pulse was screaming.

The eastern face of Ravenrock was going to come down.

She set down the quill with deliberate care, the way she did everything that mattered, and listened harder. Beneath the cabin floor, through three feet of granite and forty years of pine roots, the mountain's magic churned like a river backing up behind a dam. Somewhere up-slope, a seam of old ice had finally given way to the spring thaw, and two hundred tons of shattered rock were deciding, grain by grain, whether they wanted to become a landslide.

They did.

And at the base of the slope, on the very path where she'd sent him an hour ago to check the snares, stood Tam.

Rafael was out the door before conscious thought caught up with her body. The morning was bright and ordinary—too ordinary for what was about to happen. Chickadees worked the birches. Snowmelt chuckled in the rill. And below her, small as a toy figure, her apprentice crouched over a snare line, fifteen years old and whistling off-key, entirely unaware that the world above him had begun to die.

"Tam!" Her voice cracked across the valley. He looked up, shading his eyes, and she saw the moment he registered her tone—the whistle died, his hand went still. "Away from the wall! Run for the birch stand! *Now!*"

He ran. Thank every patient god of stone and soil, he ran without question, without looking back, cutting downslope toward the stand of white birches that marked the safe line. Rafael turned and faced the mountain.

Here was the arithmetic of her life, laid bare in a single terrible second: she could let the rockslide fall, lose Tam, and keep her secret until she died old and unmourned in this cabin full of maps. Or she could do what she was about to do, in broad daylight, witnessed by a boy who might already be glancing back—

He glanced back. Of course he glanced back.

Rafael knelt and pressed both palms flat against the ground.

The land rushed into her the way cold water rushes into a lung. She'd spent ten years teaching herself to take only sips—a thread of warmth to find a hidden pass, a whisper of the stone's memory to confirm which smuggler road still ran true. Sips were invisible. What she took now was a drowning mouthful: the whole deep patience of Ravenrock, its mineral dark, the weight of itself, the long memory of glaciers. She found the failing seam—the rotten ice lens, the fractured shelf above it—and she *pushed*, pouring borrowed strength into the mountain's own bones, asking the stone to hold, *hold*, the way you'd ask a dying man to breathe.

The mountain groaned. Above her the cliff face shivered, shed a curtain of pebbles, and then—with a sound like the sky tearing along a seam—let go anyway.

But not all at once. That was the victory, if there was one. Her grip on the living stone redirected the fall, nudged the slide's center of mass eastward, away from the snare line, away from Tam's path, into the empty ravine where the Thornfell smugglers called the Widow's Throat. Two hundred tons of mountain came down thirty yards to the left of where it wanted to, and Rafael held the redirection for nine endless seconds while the world roared and dust swallowed the sun and her vision went white at the edges from the sheer cost of it.

Then it was done, and she collapsed forward onto her hands in the wet grass, shaking, empty, tasting copper and loam.

The silence afterward was enormous. Dust drifted. Somewhere a single bird resumed singing, tentatively, as though checking whether the world had ended.

"Rafael."

His voice came from very close. She hadn't heard him cross the distance—hadn't heard anything except her own blood in her ears. Tam stood over her, snow-dusted, snare-line forgotten in his fist, and his face wore an expression she had never seen on it before.

She knew all of Tam's expressions. She'd catalogued them over four years the way she catalogued terrain features: the sulky set of his mouth when she corrected his lettering, the quick bright hunger when she taught him something new, the guarded flatness he wore on the anniversary of his mother's death, every year, without fail. She knew this boy the way she knew her maps—line by line, contour by contour.

This expression wasn't in the atlas.

It was awe, yes—awe first, wide-eyed and open-mouthed, the pure wonder of a boy who had just watched a woman hold back a mountain. But underneath the wonder, already curdling at its edges, was something else. Something she couldn't read. Fear, maybe—but fear of the rockslide was behind him now. This was a different animal entirely.

"You—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "You *held* it. I saw you. You put your hands down and it—it turned. Mountains don't turn, Rafael. Water doesn't run uphill. I *saw*—"

"I know what you saw." She pushed herself upright. Her arms trembled; she folded them so he wouldn't see. "Help me up."

He didn't move. That was wrong too. Tam was many things—stubborn, chatty, chronically careless with expensive ink—but he had never once been slow to reach out a helping hand. His mother had raised him better, wherever she was now.

"The gifted," he said quietly. "That's real, then. It's not just stories the drovers tell. It's real, and it's *you*."

The word landed in Rafael's chest like a thrown knife finding its mark without being aimed. *Gifted.* Three weeks ago the word had been a fireside rumor, a thing that happened in the lowlands, in the fat southern kingdoms where kings had nothing better to do than meddle. Even now it was only rumor. Only talk that had drifted up the passes with the spring caravans.

*The crown calls for their names. A hunt, they're saying. Registers and riders and rewards.*

She'd laughed at it over ale in the Last Knot inn, loudly enough that half the room had laughed with her, because laughing was armor and Rafael Whitlock had spent a decade learning to wear it well. *Let them hunt,* she'd said. *The mountains don't give up their secrets and neither do I.*

Now her apprentice stood in front of her with the word in his mouth and something unreadable in his eyes, and the mountain's dust was still settling around them like ash.

"Inside," she said. "Both of us. Now."

* * *

The cabin felt smaller than it ever had. Fourteen years she'd lived here—built it herself, timber by timber, in the shadow of Ravenrock specifically because Ravenrock was dead stone, magicless, mute as a grave. A blind spot in the land's living weave. She had chosen her home the way a fugitive chooses a locked door.

Tam sat at the worktable, on the apprentice stool he'd occupied for four years, surrounded by the instruments of their shared trade: the brass calipers, the ink stones, the half-finished survey of Greywater Pass that would earn them winter's coal and salted pork. Everything exactly as it had been this morning. Everything changed.

Rafael poured two cups of willow-bark tea mostly to have something to do with her hands. Hers shook; she pretended they didn't. She set his cup down and waited.

He wrapped both hands around it and stared into it. "My grandmother used to say there were still some of you in the high country." His voice was strange—careful, each word placed like a foot on uncertain ice. "Old women who could call rain. A man in Copperhold who could find lost things. She said the old kings left them alone. Said it was bad luck to trouble them."

"The old kings are dead."

"Aldric's not." He looked up, and there it was again—that flicker she couldn't parse, gone before she could name it. "There's men in the valley taverns wearing crown colors. Asking questions. Buying drinks. There's a bounty posted in the Last Knot, Rafael. I saw it two days ago." He hesitated. "I meant to tell you. I forgot."

*You forgot.* Rafael looked at this boy she had fed and housed and taught, whose ink-stained fingers she'd bandaged when he'd sliced them on a glass vial, whose fever she'd sat up with three winters running—and felt, for the first time, the vertiginous possibility that she did not know him at all. That she had been reading a map drawn by someone else, a beautiful fiction, while the real territory underneath stayed dark.

Or maybe he was just a frightened boy who'd forgotten, the way fifteen-year-olds forget things, and she was letting dust-clouds shape themselves into monsters. Ten years of hiding made a person see hunters in every shadow. She knew that about herself too.

"Two days ago," she said evenly, "you forgot to mention a bounty for people like me. Today you watched me do the thing the bounty is for."

"I'm not going to—" He stood up abruptly, stool legs scraping. "What do you think I am?"

"I don't know." The honesty came out rawer than she intended. "That's the problem, Tam. For four years I've known everything about you, and right now I'm looking at you and I can't read a single line."

He flinched. Good, some uncharitable corner of her thought, and she hated that corner immediately.

"They'd hang me too," he said. "You know that. Anyone who harbors the gifted—they hang them alongside. My grandmother told me that too." His jaw worked. "I'm not saying I'd—I'm not saying anything. I just think you should know that I know what happens to people who get tangled up in this. That's all I'm saying."

*That's all I'm saying.* Five words, and every one of them a door closing somewhere inside him. Rafael turned to the window. Below the cabin, the ravine where the mountain had fallen was still smoking faintly with dust, a raw brown scar across the green where no scar had been this morning. She had saved his life with a magic that could cost them both their necks, and the first thing the boy had done with his rescued breath was count the ways it endangered him.

She couldn't even blame him. That was the worst of it. He was fifteen. He'd earned his fear honestly. Every person in this kingdom had a right to be afraid, because a king had decided fear was policy.

But blame and trust were different ledgers, and only one of them kept her alive.

"Sit down, Tam."

He sat.

She took the chair across from him, the good chair, the one she reserved for clients and the rare visitor, and she folded her hands on the table between the calipers and the cold tea, and she looked at him the way she looked at difficult terrain: steadily, completely, missing nothing.

"In four years," she said, "you've never asked why we burn the practice drafts. Why we never send the same courier twice. Why I pay the toll-keepers double and never argue when they short us. You've never once asked, and I've never once lied to you when you did ask questions. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I've trusted you with more than you've known you were carrying. Until today, that trust cost you nothing. That's over now. Today it has a price, and you've seen the price tag."

"Rafael—"

"Let me finish." Her voice stayed level. It cost her. "You have a choice. It's the only honest one I can offer you. You can walk out that door tonight with your wages paid through midsummer and my blessing, and you can go south to your aunt in Millbrook and apprentice to a harbor chartmaker, and you'll be safe, and I'll never contact you, and in five years this will be a story you barely believe you saw. That's the first door."

"And the second?"

"The second door is you stay, and you stay *silent*. Not quiet—silent. What happened on that slope today goes into the ground with the rest of my secrets. You don't hint at it. You don't dream aloud about it. You don't look at me sideways in taverns when someone jokes about witches. If anyone ever asks—and someone will ask, Tam, because you'll carry it in your face whether you mean to or not—you watched your teacher stumble and swear and nearly get buried, same as any woman. Nothing else. Can you do that? Not for me. For yourself. Because if the day comes that you can't, you need to leave *before* that day, not after."

Silence filled the cabin. Outside, the chickadees had gotten over their fright and were scolding each other in the birches as if landslides were a daily inconvenience. Snowmelt dripped from the eaves. Ordinary sounds. The world insisting on its ordinariness.

Tam stared at his tea for a long time. When he finally looked up, his eyes were wet, and for one suspended heartbeat Rafael saw the boy she knew—her boy, her apprentice, the stubborn bright child she'd fished out of a debt-bond in Millbrook market four years ago because he'd reminded her, God help her, of herself.

"I'm not going anywhere," he said hoarsely. "This is—this is mine too. The work. You said I'd have my own license by next spring. I'm not throwing that away because of one bad morning." He wiped his nose with his sleeve. "I can be silent. I *can*."

"Can," Rafael repeated gently. "Not will."

"Will." Firmer now. "I swear it. On my mother."

The oath landed heavy. He didn't spend his mother lightly; she knew that. She nodded slowly, and something in her chest loosened by a single degree—not trust, not yet, but perhaps the space where trust could grow back, given time and care and no more falling mountains.

"All right." She rose, went to the shelf, and pulled down the good bottle she kept for completed commissions. She poured them each a finger of plum brandy, though he was technically a year shy, and pushed his cup across. "To bad mornings survived."

"To bad mornings." He drank, coughed, and managed a wet, crooked grin that was almost, *almost* the old Tam. "You really held a whole mountain."

"Half of one. The other half did what it liked." She lifted her cup to the window, to the scarred ravine, to the mountain that had kept her secret even while spilling hers. "Drink your brandy. Then fetch the survey kit. We're going to walk the Greywater route tomorrow, and after that—" she paused, hearing her own caution reshape the plan even as she spoke, "—after that, I think we deliver the Kestrel Mark commission early. Before midsummer. There are roads I want us off of."

"Why? What's on those roads?"

Crown colors, she didn't say. Men buying drinks. Bounties nailed to inn doors. A kingdom turning over stones to see what crawled out.

"Bad weather coming," she said. "I can feel it in the land."

And that, at least, was no lie at all.

* * *

He worked late that night. She heard him at the drafting table past midnight, quill scratching, longer than any assignment required, and she told herself it was diligence, that the boy was burying his fear in work the way she'd always taught him to.

Only much later, lying awake in the dark with the mountain silent above her, did it occur to Rafael to wonder what, precisely, he had been drawing.

She got up. Crossed the cold room. And there on his table, half-hidden beneath the blotter where he surely believed no one would look, lay a sheet of practice paper covered in his careful, precise, apprentice-perfect lettering—the same hand she'd spent four years shaping—repeating a single line over and over, as though testing how it felt to write it. As though rehearsing it.

*I saw her hold the mountain. I saw her hold the mountain. I saw her hold the—*

Rafael stood in the dark for a long time, listening to the wind come down off Ravenrock, carrying on it, faint and impossible and eighty miles from any sea, the taste of salt.
