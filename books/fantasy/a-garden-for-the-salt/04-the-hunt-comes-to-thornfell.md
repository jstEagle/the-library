## 4. The Hunt Comes to Thornfell

The riders came on a morning so cold the pines had gone white to the waist, and Tam knew they were coming before he saw them—knew it by the way the village dogs went silent all at once, like a held breath.

He was mending a fence rail at the edge of Rafael's property when he heard the hoofbeats. Not smuggler hooves; smugglers came soft and scattered, single horses at long intervals. This was six animals moving as one, iron-shod and unhurried, the way men ride when they own the road they're riding.

Tam set down his mallet. His hands were steady. That surprised him later, when nothing else about that day was steady at all.

They came around the bend below the house: six riders in the king's grey-and-crimson, and behind them, on a lead of braided leather, a hound.

Tam had seen hounds before. Every mountain boy had. But he'd never seen one like this—a lean, pale thing, more grey than white, with eyes the color of shallow water over slate. It didn't sniff the ground the way dogs did. It lifted its head into the wind and *tasted*, nostrils working, and when it turned its face toward Rafael's cabin, it made a sound low in its throat that Tam felt in his back teeth.

*It knows,* he thought, and then, absurdly, *it knows what I know.*

The lead rider reined in at the gate. He was young for his rank, broad through the shoulders, with a face burned red at the cheeks from years of weather he clearly hadn't been born to. His tabard bore the sunburst of the crown's new writ—the proclamation every village crier had been howling for a month now. *By order of King Aldric, let all who bear the old gift declare themselves or be declared by others.* The hunt for the gifted. Tam had heard it read aloud twice and both times had thought, *that's far away, that's for the lowlands, that has nothing to do with a mapmaker and her apprentice in the high Thornfells.*

The rider looked at him. Looked past him at the cabin. Looked at him again.

"You're the Whitlock apprentice."

Not a question. Tam's mouth went dry anyway.

"Tam," he said. "Tam Merrick."

"Merrick." The rider produced a ledger, bound in oiled leather, and opened it against his saddle horn. "Apprenticed three years past. Formerly of Lowmere." A pause. "Formerly of the orphanage there."

Something cold slid down Tam's spine, because the rider hadn't asked any of that. He'd read it. Someone had written it down. Somewhere in the capital, in some office full of ink and lamplight, there was a page with Tam's whole small life on it, and a man in a crimson tabard could recite it like scripture.

"Yes," Tam said.

"We're hunting a woman called Rafael Whitlock." The rider said the name carefully, the way you handle something that might be trapped. "Cartographer. Charted the Salt Road and the Kestrel Cut. There are warrants out of Vellamarch that say her maps were too good. That she found passes no surveyor found, in country no surveyor survived." He closed the ledger. "You've worked for her three years, Merrick. Has she ever done anything you couldn't explain?"

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere upslope, the hound tasted the air again and whined, once, softly—a sound like hunger being patient.

"No," Tam said.

The rider studied him for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was almost kind, and that was the worst part.

"That's what her last apprentice said, too. Before we found her in Duncarrow. She'd taught the girl to find water in dry stone." He gathered his reins. "We'll be at the village inn tonight, and tomorrow night, and however many nights it takes. If you remember anything worth remembering, Merrick, come find us. There's a bounty for accurate testimony. Enough to start a life somewhere the mountains can't reach."

He wheeled his horse. The column moved on up the road, past the cabin without stopping—but the hound turned its head the whole way, pale eyes fixed on the door, until the trees took them.

Tam stood by the broken fence for a long time. The mallet lay in the frost where he'd dropped it.

Then he went inside, because he didn't know where else to go, and found Rafael at her table with an unfinished chart pinned under weights, and she looked up at him with those grey eyes that saw everything except, apparently, the thing sitting in her own front room, and he said:

"They have a dog."

* * *

She took it better than he expected—or rather, she took it the way she took everything, which was quietly, completely, and with a stillness that made Tam feel like a boy rattling pans in a sickroom.

"A sniffer-hound." She said it flatly, tasting the word. "Those were court animals. Palace-bred. They haven't come out of the capital in forty years."

"There's a writ now. There's—" He stopped. Started again. "Rafael, they know my name. They know about Lowmere. They know about your last apprentice. They're not guessing. They *know*."

"I know." She rose and began rolling charts—not the finished ones but the drafts, the practice sheets, feeding them one by one into the stove until the room smelled of hot ink. "I know exactly what they know, and it's less than you think and more than I'd hoped. Which means we have perhaps two days."

"*We?*" The word came out sharper than he meant it. She glanced at him, and he pressed on before he could stop himself, three years of swallowed things coming up all at once. "There's no *we*, is there? There never was. There's you, and whatever you do in the deep woods when you think I'm asleep, and there's me, carrying your tools and keeping my mouth shut and not asking why my mistress can walk a fog-bound ridge at midnight without a lantern and come home knowing where every rock has shifted."

Her hands stilled over the stove.

"You think I don't see?" Tam's voice cracked and he hated it. "I've seen you put your palm on bare stone like you're listening to it. I've seen birds land on you like you're a fencepost. Last autumn you told me the Kestrel Cut was safe three days before the surveyors confirmed it, and you *smiled*, like it was a joke only you got. And I told myself it was craft. Skill. Thirty years of walking mountains. Because the alternative was—" He gestured helplessly at the burning papers, at the writ, at the whole grey world outside the window. "This. Is this the alternative?"

For a moment she said nothing. The fire ate another draft-map, a coastline dissolving into embers.

"Last summer," she said finally, "when the rockslide nearly took you at Hollow Bridge. What did you see me do?"

And there it was. The thing he had carried since August, wrapped up tight, fed and watered and never once spoken aloud.

"You put your hand on the cliff," he whispered. "And it stopped. The stones were already moving—I heard them, I felt the ground go—and you put your hand flat against the rock like you were holding a door shut, and it *stopped*. And afterward you bled from the nose for an hour and told me it was altitude."

"It mostly was."

"Rafael."

She turned away from the stove. In the light of the burning maps her face looked older than he'd ever allowed himself to see—lined, tired, and afraid. Genuinely afraid. Some part of Tam had spent three years half-believing she was untouchable, that her secrets made her strong the way armor makes a man strong. Standing there in the smell of her own burning work, she looked like what she was: a woman alone with a thing that could get her killed, who had chosen, day after day, year after year, to carry it without telling anyone.

Including him. Especially him.

"I should have sent you away months ago," she said. "When the first rumors came. I kept you because—" She stopped, and he watched her decide not to say whatever the rest of that sentence was. "It doesn't matter why I kept you. Here's what matters. Tonight I take the high pass to the ice caves and stay ahead of them until the writ dies or I do. You're going to pack one bag. Your bag. And you're going down to your sister's in Lowmere, and when anyone asks—and they will ask—you tell them the truth. You were her apprentice. You knew nothing. She dismissed you a week ago over wages."

"And if they don't believe me?"

"They will. Because it will be true. You *do* know nothing." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "That was always the design, Tam. Not because I didn't trust you. Because knowing things is dangerous now, and I wanted—" Her jaw tightened. "I wanted one person in the world who'd be safe if this day came. That's all. That's the whole ugly truth of it. You were the thing I kept clean."

Tam stood very still.

Because here was the wound under the wound, the one he'd been circling for three years and couldn't look at straight: it wasn't that she'd lied to him. It was that she'd protected him right out of her life. Three years at her side, eating at her table, learning her trade—and never once inside the wall. He was the apprentice. He was the *kept-clean thing*. He loved her, in the fierce, unchosen way you love someone who pulled you out of an orphanage and gave you work worth doing, and she had decided, quietly, without consultation, that he would spend that love safely ignorant, the way you decide a child shouldn't carry heavy loads.

"I could have helped," he said.

"No," she said gently. "You really couldn't."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat by the banked stove in the loft above the workshop and listened to her move below—rolling, packing, banking the hearth, doing all the thousand small acts of a woman erasing herself from a place she'd lived eleven years. Once, near midnight, he heard her stop in the middle of the room and stand there for so long he crept to the loft ladder to check on her. She was standing at the wall where the finished maps hung, the ones too beautiful to burn: the Thornfells rendered in her own hand, ridge-lines like sleeping dragons, every pass and waterfall and goat-track labeled in her small precise script. Eleven years of work. She touched none of them. She just stood looking, the way you look at a grave.

Then she blew out the lamp and went to her room, and Tam went back to the stove, and the resentment came back, and this time it brought friends.

Because that was the thing about fear—it needed somewhere to go, and hers was packed and saddled and pointed at the ice caves, and his had nowhere at all. It just sat in him, growing. Fear of the riders. Fear of the hound with its tasting nose. Fear of what would happen to a former apprentice of a hunted woman, *kept clean* though he was, when the crown's patience ran thin. Fear, worst of all, of the question the rider had planted in him like a seed in thawing ground: *has she ever done anything you couldn't explain?*

No. Never. And every unexplained thing was a brick in the wall between them, and he had stood outside that wall for three years calling it a home.

Near dawn he heard her saddle her horse in the dark. He heard her come to the foot of the loft ladder and stand there—he heard the boards take her weight, hold, wait. She didn't call up. Maybe she thought he was asleep. Maybe she knew he wasn't and couldn't make herself say goodbye.

The ladder creaked once as she gripped it, then went still. Then her boots moved away, and the door opened onto blue pre-dawn cold, and shut.

Tam lay in the dark and listened to hoofbeats fade up toward the high pass, and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had left everything behind on purpose. The maps. The house. The evidence. Him. All of it expendable, all of it arranged, and he had known about it for exactly one evening and been given exactly no choice in any of it, because choices were for people inside the wall.

He dressed. He went downstairs. Her last chart still lay on the table under its paperweights, half-finished, the coastline of some unnamed lake waiting for her hand. On the peg by the door hung the spare oilskin she'd always told him to keep ready. Under the loose floorboard by the hearth—the floorboard she thought he didn't know about, because he was kept clean—was the cash box, and in the cash box was enough coin to buy a life somewhere the mountains couldn't reach.

He didn't take it. He wanted the record to show he didn't take it.

He put on the oilskin and walked down through the frosted pines toward the village inn, and the whole way he told himself a story: *I'm going to warn them off her trail. I'm going to point them wrong. I'm going to protect her the way she never let me protect her, and prove I was never just the kept-clean thing.* The story was bright and brave and it got him all the way to the inn yard, where the six grey-and-crimson riders were watering their horses in the pink dawn light, and the pale hound lifted its head and tasted the air, and the young officer turned around, and smiled his almost-kind smile, and waited.

And Tam opened his mouth to lie—

—and what came out instead, in a voice he barely recognized, cracking apart on every word, was: "The high pass. She took the high pass toward the ice caves. She left an hour ago. Please. Please, whatever you do to her, leave me out of it. I didn't know anything. She made sure I didn't know anything."

The silence after was very long. Snow sifted off the pines. The hound whined and strained toward the mountain, and the officer rested a gloved hand on its head without looking away from Tam's face, studying him the way Rafael studied bad terrain—looking for the route through.

"The high pass," he repeated.

"She'll be at the saddle by midmorning. If you ride hard—"

"We'll ride hard." The officer swung into the saddle, and paused, and reached into his coat, and pressed a small purse into Tam's frozen hands. It was heavier than a purse that size had any right to be. "Accurate testimony," he said. "As promised. Go home to Lowmere, Master Merrick. Forget these mountains."

They rode. Six horses and a pale hound, up the valley road, growing smaller, growing silent, gone.

Tam stood in the empty inn yard with blood-money in his hands and watched the place where they'd vanished, and waited for regret to arrive, for horror, for the great black wave of what-he'd-done.

What arrived instead, first and worst, was relief. A shameless, shuddering, full-body relief, like a fever breaking—that it was done, that the secret was out of him, that the wall was down even if he'd had to be the one to pull it, and God forgive him, God forgive him, some starved corner of him was *glad*.

The wave came after. Of course it came after. It came when he thought of her face at the wall of maps, when he thought of her hand bleeding at the foot of the loft ladder, when he thought of the word she'd never finished—*I kept you because*—and understood that whatever the ending had been, it had not deserved this. She had kept him safe the only way she knew how, and he had sold the only person who had ever come for him, and the coin in his hands was warm now, impossibly warm, and would stay warm for the rest of his life.

He was still standing there when one of the villagers came out to draw water and saw his face and asked what was wrong, and Tam heard himself answer, in a dead man's voice:

"I have to pack."

* * *

They caught her tracks at the saddle, the officer would say later, in the report that eventually crossed Corwin Hale's desk. Lost her at the ice caves. Found the cave system empty, the exit chimney blocked by rockfall that no natural fall could explain—stone settled back into place like a wound closing. No body. No blood. Only, wedged in the rocks of the lower approach, a satchel of maps she had evidently abandoned to lighten her load: eleven years of the Thornfells, every pass and waterfall and goat-track, drawn in a small precise hand.

The crown kept the maps. The crown always kept the maps.

And Tam Merrick went down out of the mountains the next morning with a heavy purse and a light bag, and at the last bend in the road, where the pines opened and he could see the whole white spine of the Thornfells laid out under the sun, he stopped and looked back for a long time at the country where his loyalty had lived and died, and then he did not look back again.

He told himself he never would.
