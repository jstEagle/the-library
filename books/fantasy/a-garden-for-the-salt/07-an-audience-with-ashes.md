## 7. An Audience with Ashes

They took her maps at the border.

Not the forged ones—those were in her pack, drawn in a careful, mediocre hand, the work of a competent but unremarkable surveyor. They took those too, but the ones that mattered, the ones she'd spent a decade burning or burying or committing to memory and then to ash, those were already gone. What the crown's men confiscated at the Saltgate checkpoint was a leather case containing eleven charts of the Thornfell high passes, each one a masterpiece she should never have let survive, and the lieutenant who pried it from her hands handled it with the reverence of a man who understood exactly what he was holding.

"Rafael Whitlock," he read from his papers, though he was looking at her, not them. "The Cartographer of Thornfell. They said you'd be smaller."

"They say a lot of things in the lowlands."

He smiled without warmth and gestured her up into the prison wagon. "They say you can find anything. Roads that aren't there. Water under stone. Let's see if you can find your way out of this."

The wagon had no windows. She counted the turns by the pull of the wheels against her shoulder, the way the road climbed and fell, and by the time the motion smoothed into the long flat approach of a river valley she knew—without wanting to know, without any gift at all, just the geometry of a country she'd once charted in happier years—that they were coming into Corvane along the King's Road, and that the city would open before them the way it always did: sudden, walled, and smelling of smoke and river rot.

She had never seen the capital. That had been the point. A cartographer who knew the capital was a cartographer with a price on her head; a cartographer who knew only the mountains was a rumor. She had built her whole life on the principle that the safest map was the one never drawn, and now the crown had drawn her, inked her borders, and was carrying her to its center to be read.

*You let Tam live in your house,* she thought, for the hundredth time, for the thousandth. *You let him see your hands.*

The wagon stopped. Bars of light fell across her knees.

* * *

They held her three days in a cell that was too clean to be a dungeon and too locked to be anything else. The food was good. That frightened her more than irons would have. Someone with authority wanted her lucid, rested, and presentable, and in Rafael's experience the people who wanted things from you were infinitely more dangerous than the people who wanted to hurt you.

On the fourth morning, a woman came with a basin of water, a comb, and a bundle of clothing in undyed wool—fine wool, but plain, the costume of a person summoned rather than accused.

"You're to wash," the woman said. She had the flat, overworked voice of a palace servant who had stopped wondering about anything. "You're to wear these. You're not to speak unless the king addresses you."

"The king," Rafael said. "Aldric himself."

The woman's hands paused over the basin, just for a breath. "The king," she confirmed, and something in how she said it—the careful, hollow way of it, like a word she'd been instructed to use and no longer believed in—made Rafael's skin prickle.

"Is he well?" Rafael asked. "I've heard—there are stories. That he's changed."

The woman wrung out the cloth, wrung it again though nothing more could come out of it. "Everyone says he's changed," she said. "No one says how." She set the cloth down and left, and Rafael sat alone with the water going cold and the sentence turning over in her mind like a stone in a stream.

Everyone says he's changed. No one says how.

She had heard the stories in the mountain villages, before the riders came. *The king has gone strange. The king burns his own counselors. The king weeps at nothing and laughs at executions.* She had filed it all under distant politics, the weather of the lowlands, rain that fell on other people's roofs. But the woman's voice had carried something the rumors hadn't: not fear of the king. Fear *for* him. Or perhaps fear of something else entirely, something the servants had no name for and so described only by its absence.

Rafael washed. She dressed. She let the maid comb out her hair because refusing would have been a kind of information too.

And all the while, faint as a memory of a memory, she could taste salt.

* * *

The throne room of Corvane, she would later learn, had been built by a king who wanted to feel small. The ceiling arched up into gloom like the inside of a bell, and the windows were set high and narrow so that the light came down in long pale columns and died on the flagstones before it reached the dais. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, a corridor of silk and suspicion, and every head turned when the doors opened and the guards walked her down the center of the room.

Rafael kept her eyes down. She counted footsteps, counted breaths, did everything a frightened woman would do, because frightened women were overlooked and overlooked women survived. She was halfway to the dais, deep in the performance of her own smallness, when the taste of salt flooded her mouth so hard and so sudden that her knees nearly buckled.

It was like walking into the sea with her lungs open. Like the spring the year the mine tailings bled into the Thornfell streams, when the water had gone brackish for a hundred miles and every living thing downstream had grown quiet. She stopped. A guard's hand pressed between her shoulders, urging her on, and she made her legs move because falling would have drawn every eye in the room, and she could not—*could not*—let them see her face while she looked up.

She looked up.

King Aldric sat on the throne of Corvane, and he was a man in his late forties with a soldier's frame gone soft and a beard trimmed in the current fashion, and he was wearing a crown of gold and iron, and none of that was the thing.

The thing hung over him like smoke.

It rose out of the crown itself—or seemed to, or chose to seem to—the way heat-shimmer rises off summer stone, a column of pale drifting grey shot through with threads of white, and it was not smoke, because smoke does not have *structure*. It had layers. It had currents. It moved with the slow, deliberate patience of something that had never once needed to hurry, and as Rafael stared, her cartographer's mind—the mind that read landforms and watercourses and the grammar of the living earth—began, helplessly, to *read* it.

It was vast. That was the first thing. Vaster than the room, vaster than the shape it wore; what hung above the king was only what showed above the waterline, the visible tip of something rooted deep in the stone of the city, in the foundations, in the *ground* itself. And it was hungry. She could see that the way she could see a dry riverbed and know where the water had gone. The grey of it wasn't grey—it was absence, a moving absence, a shape made of everything it had taken out of the world, and the world around it was thinner for it. She could see that too: the air near the throne was dimmed the way a candle flame is dimmed when another flame drinks its light, and the courtiers standing closest to the dais had a faintly bleached quality, like cloth washed too many times.

*It's feeding,* she thought. *It's been feeding for years. It's been feeding on—*

On what? Not the courtiers, or not only them. The dimness around them was ambient, incidental, the runoff of a much larger meal. The column of pale smoke bent downward, curved, and sank into the crown, into the king's skull beneath the crown, and the king sat beneath it with his hands on the arms of the throne and his face composed into mild, patient attention, and Rafael saw with a clarity that would haunt her for the rest of her life that the man was *hollow*. Not dead. Not vacant. Hollow the way a house is hollow when the family has moved out and the furniture remains—everything in its place, nothing at home.

The salt-thing wore him the way she wore the undyed wool.

No one else saw. She understood that in the same heartbeat she understood the shape of it, because the court was watching the king with expressions she could catalog: fear, flattery, boredom, calculation. A chamberlain was announcing something in a dry voice—*the surveyor from the Thornfell passes, Your Majesty, delivered under seal of the hunt*—and the words went through the smoke-thing as if it were not there, because for everyone in this room except one woman from the mountains, it was not there.

The king raised his hand. The chamberlain fell silent.

"Rafael Whitlock." The voice was a good voice, warm and a little tired, a king's voice, and it came out of the hollow man the way sound comes out of a shell held to the ear—shaped by the thing it passes through, belonging to something else. "They tell me you can find anything."

*Don't look at it. Don't look at it. He will see you looking.*

She kept her eyes on the king's face, on the flesh of him, on the man-shaped mask. "I find roads, Your Majesty. Roads, passes, water. It's a trade, not a miracle."

"It's a gift." He said the word gently, tasting it. "The hunt has been very productive, you know. We've found so many gifted this year. Healers who hid in villages. Weather-readers who hid on farms. A boy of eleven who could make plants grow, hiding in a root cellar with his mother's hands over his mouth." A pause. It went on a beat too long, a beat too long, and in the pause the smoke above him stirred and thickened, and Rafael felt the taste of salt climb the back of her throat like a tide. "His mother bit through her own tongue rather than give him up. Do you know what I did with him?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Neither do I." The king smiled, and it was a kind smile, a warm smile, and it was *wrong*, it was wrong the way a portrait's eyes are wrong, everything correct and nothing alive. "I find that I don't remember. Isn't that strange? There are so many things I don't remember lately."

Somewhere in the ranks of courtiers, someone shifted. A woman near the wall had gone white. They had all heard this before, Rafael realized—the king's strange confessions, the gaps, the gentle horror of them—and they had all learned to look at their shoes while he said them.

The smoke bent lower. It came down out of its column like a heron stooping at the water, slow and total, and Rafael felt it before she understood what was happening—a pressure, a cold, a *taste*, salt flooding her senses, salt in her mouth and her ears and behind her eyes, and every instinct she had honed over ten years of hiding screamed at her to look down, look down, be small—

It saw her seeing.

There was no other way to hold the moment. The smoke had no eyes and it looked at her anyway, and the attention of it was a physical weight, a hand laid flat against her chest, and in that contact she felt it *consider* her. Not the way a man considers. The way a current considers a boat. She felt herself measured—her gift, the living magic she had spent a decade burying under ink and trade routes, the thing she had never once shown anyone but Tam—and she felt the thing above the throne register it with something that, in a mind, would have been pleasure.

*There you are,* the salt said, without words, without sound, in the way that a rising tide says it to a sandcastle. *There you are, all this time, so close and so quiet. I wondered where the flavor in the wind had gone.*

The salt in the wind. The salt where no sea was. It had been *her*—her gift bleeding flavor into the land, and the thing had tasted her across a hundred miles and sent the hunt out like a tongue.

Her knees were shaking. She locked them. She was aware of the court, of the guards, of the chamberlain's dry voice resuming somewhere far away, of the king's warm dead smile hanging in the air, and she did the only thing she could do, the thing she was best at in all the world: she became a map of nothing. She folded her gift down small, down into the cellar of herself where she kept it, banked it like a fire in winter, and she let her face go blank and her breathing go even, and she gave the smoke above the throne the flattest, dullest, most unremarkable interior a woman could wear.

The pressure eased. Slowly. Reluctantly. Like a hand lifting from a door it intended to open later.

"Your Majesty," the chamberlain was saying, "the surveyor's charts have been examined. The assessment is that her work is—exceptional. The passes she has documented do not appear on any crown survey. The value to the realm—"

"Yes," the king said, still smiling at Rafael, and the smile did not move, did not change, held itself like a held breath. "She will be useful. Take her to the surveyor's quarters. See that she has everything she needs." A pause, that wrong length again. "Everything, Chamberlain. She is to be *comfortable*. We have lost so much already. I find I mind it more than I can say."

The guards closed around her. As they turned her toward the doors, Rafael risked one last look—not at the king, not at the court, but up, one heartbeat, into the smoke.

It was still looking at her. It had not stopped looking at her. And as the guards walked her away down the long pale hall, she felt it settle back into its column above the crown with the unhurried ease of a thing that had all the time in the world, carrying with it, like a message passed hand to hand, a single clear understanding:

*I know what you are. I have wanted one of you for a very long time. And now, little mapmaker, you are in my house.*

* * *

They gave her a room with a window and a lock on the outside, and a supper of roast fowl and good bread that she could not eat, and a fire that burned with a small clean heat, and through all of it the taste of salt stayed in her mouth like a stone she could not spit out.

She sat by the window until full dark, watching the city of Corvane spread below her, ten thousand hearths, ten thousand lives, and she thought about the boy in the root cellar, and the mother's hands, and the king who did not remember, and the smoke that had looked at her with the patience of deep water.

They had brought her here as a prisoner. They had put her in the monster's house and told it, in effect, *here is the thing you were looking for.*

Which meant she had perhaps days. Perhaps hours. Before the salt came for her the way it had come for the boy, or before it decided she was more useful whole—and either way, alone, locked in a comfortable room, she was already dead. She needed a door. She needed someone inside these walls who knew where the bodies were buried, someone with leverage and a grudge and nothing left to lose.

As if the thought had been a summons, a knock came at her door. Soft. Two taps.

The lock turned from the outside, and a man stepped in—a lean, greying man in servant's grey, with the eyes of someone who had spent his life watching people and had run out of the patience required to pretend otherwise. He closed the door behind him without hurry.

"Rafael Whitlock," he said quietly. "My name is Corwin Hale. Until eight months ago, I was the king's spymaster." He smiled, and it was the first honest expression she had seen in that palace all day, because it was not a kind one. "I believe we've both seen what's sitting on that throne. And I think you and I should talk about how to kill it."
