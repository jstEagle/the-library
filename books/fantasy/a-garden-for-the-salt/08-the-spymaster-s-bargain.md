## 8. The Spymaster's Bargain

Corwin Hale had learned long ago that the most dangerous lies were the ones you told yourself, and he had been telling himself a very dangerous one for eleven months: that the king was merely ill.

He stood at the window of his cramped office above the fishmongers' row in the Saltmarket district, watching the palace across the canal with the flat patience of a man who had once owned every shadow in that building and now had to buy them back one at a time, secondhand, from people who didn't trust him. The morning fog off the river hadn't burned away yet. It never did anymore. Eleven months of gray mornings, as though the city itself had begun to exhale brine.

The king was ill. That was the official word. A wasting sickness, the physicians said, though none of them would meet Corwin's eye when they said it, and two of them had stopped coming to court entirely, and one had drowned in a cistern that was four feet deep.

Corwin poured himself wine he couldn't taste and considered, for perhaps the ten-thousandth time, the moment he had stopped believing his own lie.

It had been small. It was always small. The king had held audience on the Feast of Saint Vessia—Aldric had never missed the Feast of Saint Vessia in nineteen years of reign; his father had founded the holiday's observance, and Aldric treated it like scripture—and the king had smiled at the crowd, and the smile had arrived a half-second too late. Not slow. Late. As if the face had received its instructions by courier rather than nerve.

Corwin had spent thirty years reading faces for a living. He knew what grief looked like arriving late. He knew what madness looked like. He knew what fear looked like, and what performance looked like, and what a stroke looked like dragging half a man's expression down into slackness.

This had been none of those things. This had been a face being *operated*.

And then there was the salt. The servants whispered about it—the white crust blooming along the mortar joints in the royal apartments, the way the fountain water in the inner court had gone brackish though it fed from mountain springs, the dogs that refused to cross the threshold of the great hall and had to be carried, whining, through their duties. Corwin had taken samples himself, scraped them into glass vials with hands that shook more than he liked. He'd had them tested by an apothecary who owed him three favors and no loyalty. The apothecary had come back pale and asked to be paid in coin and never spoken to again.

Salt. In a capital ringed by freshwater rivers, two hundred miles from any coast. Salt rising out of the stones like frost.

The hunt rolls lay open on his desk beneath a paperweight shaped like a falcon—a gift from the king, from *the real king*, back when gifts meant something. Corwin had spent four nights copying the rolls before his disgrace became formal enough that they'd have changed the locks on him. Four nights, because the list was long. King Aldric's proclamation had gone out in spring: all persons possessing the old gifts, the land-sense, the blood-reading, the weather-hearing, to be registered, examined, and "remanded to crown custody for the realm's protection." The language was gentle. The language always was. What happened after remand was not written down anywhere, but Corwin had read enough unwritten things to know.

And here was the thought that had kept him awake through the whole wet summer, the thought he could only admit in the privacy of his own skull, where even he listened sideways:

The hunt wasn't cruelty. The hunt was *hunger*. Something wearing the crown was searching for something specific, the way a man dying of thirst searches a cellar for wine. And whatever it wanted, it wanted it badly enough to burn down the kingdom's oldest protections to find it.

Which meant someone out there had what it needed. Which meant someone out there might be able to hurt it.

Which meant Corwin Hale, disgraced spymaster, stripped of office and pension and the king's ear, might be the only man in the capital positioned to find that person first.

* * *

The cartographer had arrived in the city nine days ago in a cage wagon, and Corwin had known her name within six hours, because knowing names was the last muscle he still owned outright.

Rafael Whitlock. Forty-one years old by the guild rolls, licensed surveyor, specialist in difficult terrain. Ten years working the Thornfell passes, drawing maps for wool merchants and, everyone assumed, occasionally for less reputable clients, though no one had ever proved it and the smugglers of Thornfell had never once given her up, which was itself a kind of proof. Registered giftless. Clean papers. Clean, clean, clean—so clean that when the hound had pointed at her in the market square of Harrowmere and the riders had dragged her out of her lodgings, the local magistrate had actually apologized to her.

Corwin had pulled the interrogation records. Three sessions so far. She'd said almost nothing worth ink. No name-dropping, no bargaining, no tears. When they'd shown her the hound, she'd looked at it the way a woman looks at a bad-tempered horse—not afraid, exactly. *Assessing.*

That was the part that had kept him at his window past midnight. Everyone broke differently, but nearly everyone broke *loudly*, early, in some way that told you where the cracks ran. This woman was holding her cracks the way she presumably held her maps: folded small, hidden deep, and marked only where she alone could read them.

There was one other thing. Corwin had a friend—or what passed for a friend among people who traded in secrets—a turnkey at the Warden's Gate who owed him for burying a brother's debts. The turnkey had mentioned, in the flat tone men used for things they weren't sure they'd seen, that the prisoner Whitlock had asked an odd question on her second night. Not *am I going to die*. Not *will someone pay my ransom*. She'd asked whether the king had been seen lately in the eastern colonnade at dusk.

Why would a mountain cartographer ask about the king's walking habits?

Unless she wasn't asking about the king.

Corwin set down his cup. Across the canal, the palace towers stood against the fog like teeth. Somewhere inside them, a thing that wore Aldric's face was eating the kingdom one memory at a time, and Corwin had run out of ways to prove it to anyone who mattered, because everyone who mattered had already decided he was either mad or traitorous, and both roads led to the same cistern.

But a woman who could feel the land's living magic under her palms—that woman might look at the king and see what Corwin could only infer. Might see it *directly*. Might be the witness he'd been starving for.

She was also, by every account, a liar of considerable discipline. Which suited him fine. He'd never trusted honest people; they broke their promises by accident.

It was time to go shopping for an ally.

* * *

The Warden's Gate prison smelled of wet stone and despair, and Corwin walked through it wearing the face of a minor clerk from the Office of Rolls, because the Office of Rolls genuinely did audit prisoners' paperwork and no guard in the city questioned a bored man with a ledger. He'd worn better faces. He'd worn worse. The trick was to be so tedious that the eye slid off you like rain off slate.

They brought Rafael Whitlock to the interview cell in irons, and Corwin made himself look at her properly for the first time.

She was smaller than her reputation, which was true of most dangerous people. Brown hair going gray at the temples, cut short and practical. Hands that were a mapmaker's hands—ink-stained, precise, scarred at the fingertips from years of rock and thorn. Her eyes went around the cell once, cataloguing exits that didn't exist, and then settled on him with the unhurried attention of someone deciding how much of him was worth remembering.

"Master Quill," Corwin said, using the clerk's name, "from the Office of Rolls. Routine verification of your particulars before transfer."

"I don't have particulars," she said. Her voice was low and dry, gravel over bedrock. "I have a license number and a wrong arrest."

"The hound disagreed."

"The hound," she said, "is a dog."

Corwin gestured and the guard withdrew, closing the door. They were alone. He let the silence stretch, watched her not fidget, watched her conserve herself like a traveler rationing water. Then he opened his ledger, tore out a blank page, and laid it on the table between them along with the stub of charcoal he kept for marginal notes.

"You're going to draw me a map," he said.

Her expression didn't change. "I'm under sentence of examination. Anything I produce can be used—"

"This map has no roads on it." He turned the page around and wrote, in his own quick cipher-hand, a single line: **What do you see when you look at the king?**

He watched her read it. Watched the stillness come over her—not the stillness of guilt or fear, but the stillness of a hunter who has heard something move in brush she'd believed empty. For three full seconds she gave him nothing, and he gave her credit for every one of them; most people filled silence like a leaking boat fills with sea.

Then she said, quietly, "I don't know what you mean."

"Good," Corwin said. "That's the correct answer for a guard. But we're not being recorded, Mistress Whitlock, because I bribed the man outside with money I embezzled from an office that no longer employs me, and I chose this hour because the shift change leaves us eleven unwatched minutes. So let's spend them honestly, since honesty between professionals is cheaper than either of us pretending otherwise." He sat down across from her. "You asked the turnkey about the king's evening walks. Nobody asks that question out of curiosity. People ask it because they want to know when a thing is *vulnerable*, or because they want to know when a thing is *visible*. You're not an assassin—you'd have had a knife by now and worse aim than you think. So I'm betting on visible."

Rafael Whitlock looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, airless sound, without much humor in it.

"They told me the spymaster was disgraced," she said. "They didn't tell me he was desperate."

"Desperate is just thorough with a deadline." He leaned forward. "Eleven months ago, King Aldric held the Feast of Saint Vessia. He has never missed it. His smile came late. Since then: salt in the stonework of a freshwater palace. Dogs that won't enter the hall. Three physicians gone—one drowned standing up. The queen died eight months ago of a 'fever' that left her body too heavy to lift—I've read the undertaker's complaint; he charged double for the bearers. And the king has ordered a realm-wide hunt for the gifted, which makes no strategic sense, no financial sense, and no political sense, unless the crown is looking for something specific and doesn't care what it burns to find it." He tapped the page between them. "You felt the hound point at you and you didn't panic, because you already knew what you are. And I think—this is a wager, I'll admit it, I make my living on wagers—I think you looked at the king during your arraignment, or you've heard the rumors through the prison walls, and something in you recognized the shape of the problem. Because whatever the hound smells in you, the thing wearing our king's crown smells it too. And it wants it."

The cell was very quiet. Water ticked somewhere in the walls. Rafael Whitlock had gone carefully, completely still, the way stone is still.

"If I say yes," she said finally, "you'll assume I've confirmed everything. If I say no, you'll assume I'm lying. If I say nothing, you'll assume I'm smart." She tilted her head. "What's the play where I win?"

"The play where you win," Corwin said, "is the one where I get you out of this building before your examination—which is scheduled for the day after tomorrow, and which will not be an examination, it will be a harvesting, because I've seen the writs and the examiners carry instruments, not questions. In exchange, you become my eyes. You get close enough to the king to tell me what you see. Not what you suspect. What you *see*. And when we've proven what he is—to whoever is left in this city with the spine to act on proof—I erase you. New name, new face-papers, new history. The hunt rolls burn your entry, and Rafael Whitlock dies on record as having died in transit. Very tidy. Very final. You walk out of your own life and keep the rest of it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in forty hours you're in a room with men who use hooks, and everything you've spent ten years hiding gets taken out of you the hard way, and I'll drink to your memory and hate myself efficiently." He said it without softening. Lies softened were lies doubled. "I'm not threatening you, Mistress Whitlock. I'm reading the schedule aloud. There's a difference, and you're sharp enough to know it."

Something moved behind her eyes—calculation, fast and cold, the arithmetic of a woman who had survived mountains by knowing which slopes held and which ones lied. He recognized it because he wore the same arithmetic behind his own face every day.

"You want me to tell you what I see," she said slowly, "when I look at the king."

"Yes."

"And what if what I see is nothing? What if your late smile is a stroke and your salt is a drainage fault and you're a disgraced old man building a monster out of grief because grief needs somewhere to live?"

"Then I'll have wasted my last favor and you'll have lost nothing, because I'll still get you out of this cell either way. Consider it insurance purchased with my ruin." He smiled, thin and true. "Besides. You wouldn't be sitting this calm if you thought I was wrong. Wrong men get argued with. Right men get negotiated with. You're negotiating."

For the first time, something that was almost respect crossed her face. She picked up the charcoal stub, turned it in her scarred fingers, and did not write anything.

"One condition," she said. "When we do this—whatever this becomes—I don't work blind. You give me everything you have. Every rumor, every sample, every dead physician. I decide what's real. I've spent ten years mapping country that lies about itself, Master Hale. I know a false landmark when I stand on one." Her voice dropped, and for a moment the dry control cracked just enough to show the thing underneath, the thing she'd been carrying since Harrowmere, since a boy she'd trusted had sold her for the price of his own skin. "I'm done trusting other people's maps."

"Then we're alike," Corwin said, "which should frighten us both." He pushed the page toward her. "Do we have a bargain?"

She looked at the line of his cipher-hand for a long time. Outside, the shift-change bell began to toll, counting down their stolen minutes.

"We have a bargain," Rafael Whitlock said, "on one further term that isn't negotiable." She leaned forward, and lowered her voice to almost nothing, and what she said next made the cold in Corwin's chest, the cold he'd carried for eleven months, finally catch fire.

"When I look at the king," she said, "there is something hanging over him like smoke made of salt. It wears the crown the way a hand wears a glove. And yesterday—" her jaw tightened— "yesterday, through the wall of my cell, it noticed me noticing. And it *smiled*."

The bell finished tolling. Corwin rose, straightened his clerk's coat, and became Master Quill again, tedious and forgettable, while his mind raced ahead through corridors and keys and the eleven days he'd need to forge a transfer order convincing enough to fool the Warden's Gate and bold enough to fool the thing on the throne.

"Mistress Whitlock," he said mildly, gathering his ledger. "How would you feel about a career in surveying the royal apartments?"
