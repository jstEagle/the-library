## 9. Lessons in Wearing a Face

The capital smelled of smoke and river and, underneath it all, faintly of salt.

Rafael noticed it the way she noticed everything now—against her will, constantly, like an itch beneath the skin. Ten years in Thornfell had taught her to read a landscape from the ground up: soil chemistry, water tables, the slow arguments between stone and root. The city of Carreth was no different, except that its living magic was buried so deep beneath cobbles and cellars and centuries of feet that it came to her only in whispers. A thread of green under the palace foundations where an old garden slept. A dull ache in the eastern quarter where they'd paved over a spring. And everywhere, thin as a hairline crack in glaze, that taste of brine.

She kept her hands busy and her eyes down. That was the first rule of being invisible: give people something small to look at.

"Surveyor," Corwin Hale had told her, three nights ago, sliding a leather folio across his study table. "You'll be Master Ren Iversen, hired to chart the drainage of the lower terraces before the autumn rains. Nobody questions a man with instruments. They step around him. They forget him."

"The king won't forget me," she'd said. "It already knows my face."

"Then don't give it your face twice. Wear this." He'd produced a vial of something bitter-smelling, a tincture that reddened her lids and made her eyes water for a day. Combined with a false scar drawn along her jaw and a stooped walk she'd practiced until her back ached, it was enough. Enough, Corwin said, because the salt-thing's attention was a fixed resource, spent mostly on the throne room and the mirror.

Rafael suspected he was wrong about that last part. She'd felt its attention once already, hanging over Aldric like smoke given a shape, and when it had seen her seeing—she still dreamed of that moment, the pressure of a gaze with no eyes behind it. Things that are looked at learn to look back.

So she went to work among the drains.

* * *

The lower terraces of the palace descended toward the river in a series of gardens gone slightly wild, each one older than the last. Rafael charted them honestly—it was easier than faking competence—but she charted other things too, in shorthand only she could read. Where the land's old magic pooled and thinned. Where the salt-taint was strongest. It grew denser as she climbed toward the royal apartments, a gradient she could have walked blindfolded, like following cold air downhill.

On her fourth morning, she saw the king take the air.

He came out onto the middle terrace with six guardsmen and a woman Rafael didn't recognize—a lady-in-waiting, or perhaps a cousin of some minor house—and Rafael made herself small by the fountain, unrolling her charts, fussing with a brass level that needed no fussing.

Aldric walked slowly. That was the first wrongness. She had never met the true king, but Corwin had described him: a restless man, a soldier before he was a sovereign, who took stairs two at a time and thought best while moving. This Aldric moved the way deep water moves, without hurry, without ripple.

And then the woman said something—Rafael caught only the tail of it, a joke by the shape of it, her voice lifting at the end—and the king stopped walking.

He stood there. Four seconds. Five. Long enough for the guardsmen to shift their weight, long enough for the woman's smile to falter into something uncertain. Rafael counted the silence against her own heartbeat, a cartographer's habit, measuring time the way she measured distance.

Then Aldric laughed. It was a good laugh, warm and full, pitched exactly right—and that was the second wrongness. It arrived too late to be real. Laughter that comes after the pause isn't response; it's recitation. He might have been playing back a recording of himself laughing at jokes, stored from some earlier year, retrieved now because the moment demanded it.

"You must forgive me," the king said to the woman, and his kindness was flawless, gentle, attentive—"the mornings are difficult. My head is full of fog." He touched her arm, briefly, warmly. "But you've improved it. You always do."

The woman glowed. The guards relaxed. Everyone saw a gracious king being kind to a nervous girl.

Rafael saw a thing consulting its notes.

* * *

That evening she found Corwin in the records office below the west wing, a room he used openly enough that using it drew no attention, surrounded by the paperwork of a spymaster whose network had lately begun to die around him. He listened to her account without interrupting, which she was learning was his way—he let people talk themselves into corners.

"The pauses," he said at last. "How long?"

"Long enough to count. Four, five heartbeats. Then perfect."

Corwin turned his quill over in his fingers. "I've watched him do that in council. Lord Veyne proposed the grain levy—no, wait, that's not right, Veyne opposed it—and the king paused before answering, and in the pause, Chancellor Doss leaned in and murmured half a sentence. When Aldric spoke, he agreed with *Doss*. Not with Veyne. With the man who'd whispered." His jaw tightened. "I told myself it was politics. Kings listen to chancellors."

"And now?"

"Now I think he listens because he has nothing of his own to say. I think he's—" Corwin stopped, and Rafael watched a careful man decide how much care to drop. "I served Aldric for nine years. I knew his temper, his grudges, the way he drummed two fingers when a report bored him. The thing on the throne has the grudge list memorized. It has the finger-drumming down to the rhythm. But it doesn't *dislike* anyone. Do you understand? Aldric disliked half his council, gloriously, personally. This thing finds them all equally adequate. Kindness is just the default setting when you feel nothing."

"It's wearing him," Rafael said. "Not replacing him. There's a difference."

"Is there? From where I stand?"

"Yes." She thought of the gradient of salt in the terraces, densest near the royal rooms, and of the way the entity had hung above Aldric rather than within him—smoke, not marrow. "A replacement would be seamless. This one is a costume that fits badly if you know what to watch for. Which means there's still a body under it. Still a man."

"Still a hostage, you mean." Corwin wrote something, crossed it out. "Which raises the question I've been chewing for a week. If it needs him alive, why does he get worse every month? Why does the court whisper that the king's mind is failing? A good captor keeps the puppet intact."

Rafael turned her brass level over in her hands, though she wasn't thinking about levels. She was thinking about the lady-in-waiting on the terrace, and the way Aldric had called her *you always do*, and the strange hollow note under the warmth.

"Because it isn't feeding on his body," she said slowly. "It's feeding on something else. Something that wears out."

* * *

She got her answer eleven days later, and it came from the most unlikely source in the palace: a laundry maid named Sefa, who had been born in the mountain valleys and still, unconsciously, hummed the work songs of Thornfell while she folded sheets.

Rafael heard her on the terrace landing and nearly dropped her instrument case. For a moment she was ten years younger, standing in her mother's kitchen, hearing those same descending phrases sung over the wash basin. Homesickness hit her like weather—sudden, total, gone.

She lingered. Sefa was happy to talk. Laundry workers always were, if you asked after their aching backs instead of their business.

"Third floor linens are the worst," Sefa complained, jerking her chin upward. "The royal wing. Sheets come down gray. Not dirty—gray. Like they've been *drained*. Wash them twice, bleach them, and still. And the smell." She wrinkled her nose. "Brackish. Like a tide pool left in the sun. I've washed for great houses since I was twelve and I've never smelled sheets like that."

"How long?" Rafael asked casually. "Since the linens started coming down that way?"

Sefa considered. "Year? Year and a bit. Right around—" She stopped. Her face did something complicated. Around the time, everyone knew, that the king had changed. Around the time the executions of the gifted began, the proclamations, the hounds. No servant said it aloud. "Around when the new physicians came," she finished, which was the approved version.

Gray sheets. Salt residue. A king growing paler by the month, his memories receding, his laughter arriving late.

Rafael climbed to the third-floor corridor that afternoon with her surveyor's chalk and a legitimate reason to be there—there was a drainage channel along the east wall, genuinely failing, genuinely worth charting. But what she charted, in her private shorthand, was the residue itself. She knelt at the base of the door to the royal apartments, where no guard bothered to look at a stooped tradesman, and opened her gift a hand's width, just enough to taste.

The land-magic there wasn't diminished. It was *eaten*. She could see the shape of the absence, the way you can see the shape of a bite taken from bread. And the absence had structure. Threads. She traced them with her gift the way she'd trace a smuggler's path through Thornfell stone, following the feed-lines back to their source, and what she found made her sit back on her heels with her blood gone cold.

The threads ran through the whole wing. Through the walls, the floors, the very mortar. And they weren't anchored only in the king.

They were anchored in everything the king had ever loved.

* * *

"You're saying it eats memories," Corwin said that night. His study was dark except for one lamp; he'd dismissed his clerks hours ago. "Memories. Like a meal."

"Not memories exactly. What memories are *made of*." Rafael struggled with it, reaching for cartographer's language because no other language fit. "When I read a landscape, I'm not reading the rock. I'm reading what the rock remembers—the pressure, the heat, the water, all the history pressed into it. Living minds hold history the same way. This thing feeds on that. On the lived weight of a person. And it's been eating Aldric from the inside for over a year, taking the oldest layers first, the deepest strata, and leaving the recent surface intact because that's all it needs to pass as him."

"That's why the linens—"

"He sweats it out. What he loses has to go somewhere. Salt is what's left when you burn something away completely. Ash of ash." She wrapped her arms around herself, though the study wasn't cold. "Think about the court, Corwin. Think about what everyone says. *The king has grown distant. He's forgotten old friends. He didn't remember Lord Ammel's name at the harvest feast. He wept at nothing, then stared at his dead wife's portrait like a stranger.*"

Corwin had gone very still.

"The queen," he said.

"Died four years ago. Before the change." Rafael nodded slowly. "But she'd be the deepest layer of him. Twenty years of marriage. Grief, love, guilt—all of it rich feeding, the oldest and densest deposits. If it ate chronologically, from the bedrock up..." She hesitated. "He may not remember her anymore. Or worse—he may remember that he's supposed to remember, and find nothing there."

Corwin sat down. Not gracefully; his legs simply decided the matter. For a long moment the lamp hissed and neither of them spoke.

"I assumed it wanted the throne," he said finally. "Power, command, the machinery of a kingdom. Every usurper wants something usable. But if it's eating *him*—" He rubbed his eyes. "Then what is the crown to it? A larder? A hunting blind?"

"A slow meal," Rafael said. "Kings are the richest food in any kingdom. Who else holds twenty, forty years of absolute memory—every secret, every grief, every triumph? And who would notice, at first, if a king grew quiet?" Her voice hardened. "And I'd wager it's worn others before him. Things that hunt a particular prey learn patience. Learn method. One king wouldn't be practice—it'd be *habit*."

Corwin looked up sharply. "You think there were others."

"I think nothing hunts like that on its first attempt."

He was quiet again, but differently now—she could see the thought working through him, rearranging everything. A spymaster's instinct: if it happened once, there would be paper. Records. Precedents. Somewhere in the accumulated archive of three hundred years of monarchy, there might be a reign that ended strangely, a death explained too quickly, a sudden devotion that swallowed a court whole.

"The sealed archive," he said, almost to himself. "Under the east tower. Sealed by decree of Aldric's grandfather—no one enters without the king's own seal, and the current king—" He laughed, short and bitter. "I could ask it. It would probably grant permission. It grants anything that doesn't matter."

"Does it matter?"

"To prove what you just proved? To show the council a precedent, a pattern, a *name* for the thing wearing him?" Corwin stood, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not the careful broker, not the disgraced official, but the man he must have been at the start, before the disgrace: someone who had believed the truth was a weapon worth bleeding for. "Rafael. If it has done this before, then somewhere there's a record of how it ended. How it was beaten. Or how it wasn't." He pulled a ring of keys from his desk drawer, old ones, iron, the kind that open doors no one uses. "I need to know what you can sense in that place. Tomorrow night. Can you be ready?"

Rafael thought of the eaten threads in the walls, the gray sheets, the king laughing four seconds too late at a girl's kind joke. She thought of the salt gradient thickening toward the throne like cold pooling at the bottom of a valley.

She thought: *it has already tasted me noticing.*

"I'll be ready," she said. "Bring your keys. And Corwin—bring whatever light burns longest. Places that have been sealed that long keep their own weather."

He almost smiled. "You sound like you're talking about the mountains."

"I'm always talking about the mountains. Everything is." She shouldered her instrument case, the surveyor's disguise settling back over her like a coat. "The only question is whether the thing under the east tower is a graveyard or a den. And either way—we'll want to know before it knows we're coming."
