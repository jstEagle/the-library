## 12. Maren at the Gates

Maren Voss had been in the capital three days before she understood why Rafael Whitlock had ever hated it.

Not the smell—though the smell was a crime, tanneries and sewage braided together in the summer heat. Not the crowds, which were no worse than Port Callow on market day, and easier to pickpocket. It was the way the city looked at you. In the mountains, a stranger's eyes slid off you like rain off slate. Here, everyone looked, and no one *saw*. Ten thousand faces, all of them performing, all of them lying, and beneath the performance—nothing. A city of masks nailed to skulls.

She hated it the way you hate a rival's ship: with respect, and a plan to sink it.

The Salt Gate district was hers by the third morning. She'd come in with a grain barge out of Vell, papers bought in a tavern and stamped by a customs man whose daughter's debts she now owned, and by the time the barge tied off at the quays she had walked the district twice, counted the watch patrols, and learned which taverns poured honest ale and which poured informers. The Salt Gate was the wrong name for it, she'd decided. Nothing about the place tasted of salt. It tasted of ash and old money and the particular fear of people who lived too close to the palace to pretend the palace was far away.

Which was exactly why she was here.

She sat in the back room of the Drowned Bell—a smuggler's tavern older than the dynasty, with a cellar that ran, according to the landlord, who was lying, only to the river. It ran further than that. Maren had paid to know. The back room had one door, two windows barred against thieves who'd never get past the doorman, and a table scarred by a century of knives, coins, and elbows. She sat with her back to the wall and her face to the door, because some habits were load-bearing.

The knock came at the appointed hour. Three, then one, then three.

"Enter, then," she called, "before I die of old age."

The landlord opened the door and admitted a woman Maren knew only as Quill—a forger out of Vell with ink-stained knuckles and the eyes of someone who read everything and trusted nothing. Quill set a folded paper on the table, said, "The surveyor took the bait. She'll come. Whether she'll *stay* is your problem," and left without waiting for coin. Maren had paid in advance. Quill respected that the way a knife respects a sheath.

Maren unfolded the paper and read it a third time, though she knew it by heart.

*Surveyor required, palace annex, six weeks' commission. Rates generous. Apply to Undersecretary's office, east colonnade.*

Rafael Whitlock, hired by the crown to draw maps. Maren had laughed until the tavern keeper knocked to ask if she was dying. Of all the jokes the world had ever played on her—and the world was a prolific joker—this was the finest. The woman who had spent a decade erasing herself from every record in the realm, hiding her gift behind a cartographer's trade, had been dragged into the capital by the very hunt she'd hidden from, and then—because the crown was a blind, stupid, salt-rotted thing—had been *hired*. Given a pass. Given a reason to walk the palace corridors.

Given Maren a door.

She turned the paper over. On the back, in her own hand, she'd written the only message Rafael couldn't ignore, the one that had been passed through three cutouts to the surveyor's rented room:

*The Drowned Bell, Salt Gate. Ninth bell, three nights hence. Come alone or don't come. You owe me a debt and I've decided how you'll pay it. — M.V.*

Three nights hence was tonight.

Maren poured herself a finger of the landlord's best brandy—smuggled, naturally, out of her own holds, which she found satisfying in a circular way—and waited.

* * *

Rafael Whitlock came through the door at the ninth bell exactly, which told Maren two things: she was still the same careful, punctual, infuriating woman she'd been on the Thornfell passes, and she was frightened. Rafael hid fear well—she always had, behind that flat cartographer's stare—but Maren had spent years learning to read the small tells of people whose lives depended on being unreadable. Rafael's tells were in her hands. They were still. Too still. Hands that still were hands being *held* still.

"Well," Maren said. "The ghost of Thornfell. Sit down before someone sees you standing there looking like a woman about to be executed. You'll ruin the ambiance."

Rafael sat. Up close she looked worse than the reports suggested. Thin. A bruise gone yellow along her jaw, old but not old enough. Eyes that kept flicking to the corners of the room, mapping exits, because of course she was.

"You look terrible," Maren said, and poured her a brandy she didn't offer, just set it down. "Drink. You'll want it."

"I don't drink with people I've betrayed."

"You drink with people you *owe*, though. And you owe me." Maren let that sit. "Or have you forgotten the Kestrel Pass? The winter you'd have died in a snowdrift if my people hadn't dug you out? I haven't. I keep accounts, Rafael. It's why I'm rich and the crown is poor."

"I remember the pass." Rafael's voice was level. "I also remember what I did after."

"Yes." Maren smiled without warmth. "You sold my routes to a crown surveyor for a purse and a clean name. Eleven of my people hanged or pressed when the revenue cutters found the Kestrel road. Two of them were children, Rafael. Sixteen and seventeen. I paid for their funerals because their families couldn't." She watched Rafael's hands. They didn't move. That was its own answer. "So understand the shape of tonight. I don't want your apology. I want my debt paid, and I want it paid in a currency I choose."

"Which is?"

"Patience." Maren drank. Outside, the ninth bell finished its count, and somewhere in the district a dog started up and was kicked quiet. "First, the news, because I know you've been in that palace long enough to drown in it. The fleet's moved. Forty ships, scattered from Vell to the Serpent Isles, running dark. The crown's new tariffs and this gifted hunt of Aldric's have strangled the coast—half my captains can't port, can't trade, can't so much as buy rope without some customs man sniffing their cargo for salt-magic that doesn't exist. My people are starving, Rafael. Slowly, the way smugglers starve, which is to say angrily."

"That's not my war."

"Everything's your war now. That's what it means to be gifted when the crown hunts the gifted. You just haven't admitted it yet." Maren leaned forward. "Here's what I want. The crown keeps its shipping ledgers in the palace annex—customs schedules, patrol rotations, naval movements, the whole spine of their coastal enforcement. Every route my captains need to run clean is written in those books. I want them. Copies, or the books themselves, I don't care which. You're inside. You're a hired surveyor with a pass and a reason to be in rooms no one questions. You steal me the ledgers, and I give you what no one else in this city can."

"Which is?"

"A way out." Maren said it plainly, because plainness was the cruelest thing she could do. "Three routes out of the capital, fresh, unwatched, mine alone. A place on my fleet—real place, not a debt-prisoner's berth, though we'll come back to what you owe. A new name, new papers, a coast where no hunter's hound has ever smelled your trail. You walk out of this city and this story, Rafael, and you live. That's the price of the ledgers. That's the trade."

She watched it land. She'd expected it to land. A drowning woman doesn't weigh a rope.

But Rafael Whitlock had never been a drowning woman. She sat with the brandy untouched, and Maren saw her doing the thing she did—seeing the whole map at once, every route and every cost—and when she finally spoke, her voice was quiet.

"You know what's wearing the king."

It wasn't a question. Maren hadn't expected one. "I know the king changed. I know the hunt started the season after. I know three of my people in this city have vanished in a month, and that the palace's gifted servants went into the cells last week and half of them haven't come out." She shrugged. "I don't need to know what it is. I need to know how to starve it, and the answer is: take its ledgers, bleed its coast, and be gone before it turns its eye on me."

"It's not a king gone wrong, Maren. It's a thing that eats kings. It's worn the crown before—three times, maybe more. There are records." Rafael's hands finally moved, turning the brandy glass in a slow circle. "And in a matter of weeks it's going to bind itself to the throne permanently. A renewal ceremony. After that, there's no unmaking it. There's no fleet far enough, no coast clean enough. It'll own the whole realm's magic, and everything that carries magic will be either its food or its slave."

"You."

"Me."

Maren sat back. She had prepared for this—had prepared arguments, contingencies, the whole smuggler's kit of leverage and appeal—but hearing Rafael say *me* in that flat, resigned way made the prepared words taste like ash. She tried them anyway.

"Or," she said, "you take my routes, and you live, and someone else deals with the thing in the crown. You've spent ten years telling the world it isn't your fight. I'm offering you the chance to make that true."

"There is no someone else." Rafael set the glass down. "There's me, and a disgraced spymaster who wants the truth more than he wants to survive it, and a smuggler queen who—" she stopped, and something almost like a smile crossed her face, "—who is currently offering me a clean exit because she thinks I'm worth more alive and gone than dead and useful. And there's a rite. One rite. It needs a vessel willing to take the salt into themselves, and there is exactly one person in this city who qualifies, and she's sitting here drinking your brandy."

The room went very quiet.

Maren had heard a lot of death sentences in her life—had pronounced a few, had dodged more—and she knew one when she heard it. "You're going to take it into yourself."

"I'm going to take it out of the king. What happens after is the rite's business."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one there is."

Maren stood up. She walked to the window—there was nothing to see, just the barred dark and the district's smoky glow—and stood there because looking at Rafael Whitlock's face while she calmly arranged her own funeral was more than Maren's temper could carry without something breaking. "You absolute," she said, and ran out of words, which almost never happened. "You crossed my routes to save your own skin once. You let two children hang for your clean name. And now, when I finally hold the note on you—now you've decided to be *noble*?"

"I've decided to be useful. It's different."

"It's worse!" Maren turned. "Nobility I can work with—nobility is pride, and pride can be leveraged. But this—" she jabbed a finger at Rafael, "—this is arithmetic. You've done the sum and decided you're the cheapest thing on the board. That's not courage, Rafael. That's just the same old self-erasure with a better costume. Ten years hiding. Ten years burning your own maps. And now you want to burn the last one—the one with you on it."

Rafael flinched. It was small, and she buried it fast, but Maren saw it, and hated how good it felt to have seen it.

"The ledgers," Rafael said. "You want the shipping ledgers."

"I want—"

"I'll get them." Rafael stood. "Not for the routes. Not for the escape. I'll get them because your fleet bleeding the coast will pull the entity's attention, pull its hunters, pull its *eyes* off the palace in the weeks before the ceremony—and I need those weeks. You want your ledgers? Fine. They're the price of your fleet being my diversion. But I'm not taking your exit, Maren. I'm not taking anything of yours except the favor."

"And what favor is that?"

Rafael looked at her, and for a moment Maren saw the woman from Kestrel Pass—the one who'd been half-dead in the snow and had still, delirious, tried to thank her rescuers by sketching them a map of safe roads in the frost on the window. That woman had been real. Maren had spent years telling herself she'd invented her.

"When it's done," Rafael said quietly, "if it's done—if I'm not—take the ledgers anyway. Burn the hunt rolls while you're in there. Every gifted name in the crown's files. There are children on those lists, Maren. Sixteen and seventeen."

The room held its silence a long moment.

Maren walked back to the table, picked up the brandy Rafael hadn't drunk, and drained it herself. "Ninth bell, three nights from now, you'll bring word of when you can reach the annex. Quill will arrange the copying. And Rafael—" she caught the other woman's arm at the door, felt the thinness of it, the bird-bone fragility of a woman who'd been running for a year, "—when you're inside that thing, when it offers you the trade it will certainly offer, because things that eat crowns always offer a trade—remember that there's one person in this realm who knows exactly what your life is worth."

"There isn't."

"There is," Maren said. "I keep accounts. It's why I'm rich."

Rafael held her eyes for a moment—something passing between them that was not forgiveness and not quite its opposite—and then she was gone, out the door and into the district's dark, careful and punctual to the last.

Maren stood alone in the back room of the Drowned Bell, hating her, and poured another drink, and hated that too.

Because here was the truth she'd admit to no one, not even the dark: she had come to this city to collect a debt, and she'd meant to collect it in ledgers and escape routes and the clean satisfaction of Rafael Whitlock owing her until the day she died. Instead she'd walked out holding a promise from a woman planning her own death—a promise about children on a list, sixteen and seventeen, the same ages as the ones Rafael's betrayal had cost her—and Maren Voss, who had never in her life let a debt go unpaid, found herself hoping, furiously, stupidly, that she'd never have to collect.

She was still holding the glass when the landlord knocked, urgent, three fast raps.

"Beg pardon, mistress. There's a runner from the palace district. Says there's been an arrest at the annex tonight." He swallowed. "Says it's the surveyor. Says they took her to the cells—and that the king himself has ordered her questioned at dawn."

Maren set down the glass very carefully, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it through a wall.

"Dawn," she repeated.

Three nights before the ceremony. Two nights before the ledgers. And Rafael Whitlock in the cells of the thing that ate kings, with one night left to get her out.
