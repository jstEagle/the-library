## 13. A Night of Hollow Bells

The bells began at dusk.

Not the evening bells—those rang at the proper hour, six measured strokes that Rafael had learned to count through the walls of her borrowed rooms in the surveyor's wing. These came an hour late and wrong, a cracked, arrhythmic tolling from the cathedral tower that made the window glass shiver in its leaden frame. Three strokes. A pause long enough to doubt they'd continue. Then three more, hollow as knuckles on a coffin lid.

Rafael set down her pen mid-stroke. The map she was drafting—a plausible fiction of the eastern aqueducts, commissioned to give her reason to wander places she had no business wandering—blurred beneath her hand. Not because her eyes failed her. Because for ten years she had learned to read the land's magic the way other people read weather, and what she felt now, rising through the palace foundations like water into cloth, was the taste of salt moving with purpose.

*It knows I'm here,* she thought, and the thought landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

She had known it since the audience chamber, since the moment the thing wearing Aldric's crown had turned its smoke-wrapped attention on her and *seen* her seeing it. Ten days had passed since then. Ten days of careful, tedious invisibility: Corwin Hale's forged papers, the gray dress of a hired surveyor, the bowed head and ink-stained fingers of a woman who was nobody. She had moved through the court's corridors like water through cracks, mapping the king's routines, cataloguing which guards changed shift when, noting the servants who flinched when the king passed and the ones who didn't—the ones who didn't were either blind or already half-eaten, and Rafael suspected the latter outnumbered the former.

But the salt-thing had not moved against her. It had watched instead, patient as tide, and patience in something that old meant one thing: it was choosing how to kill her, or how to make her useless first.

The bells finished their wrong count. In the silence after, Rafael heard boots in the courtyard below—many boots, moving without the usual lazy scatter of changing watch. Ordered. Sweeping.

She went to the window and looked down into the inner yard, where torches had been lit against the last violet light. A column of guards stood in formation before the armory stairs, and before them, reading aloud from a scroll whose wax seal caught the firelight red, stood Lord Chancellor Veyra's underling—the pale, precise man whose name Rafael had never bothered to learn because he was exactly the sort of person who did nothing worth learning. His voice carried up thin and clear:

"—by order of His Majesty, all persons in royal service found to possess unregistered gifts shall be taken into custody for examination. Examination is a mercy. Concealment is treason."

Rafael's hands went cold on the windowsill.

She thought immediately—not of herself, though her own name was surely somewhere in whatever rolls Corwin had doctored, but of the laundry mistress on the third floor who could dry a sheet in winter air by holding it, who thought no one knew. Of the stable boy whose way with frightened horses went beyond patience into something older and quieter. Of the cook in the west kitchen who always knew when milk would turn, three days before it did. The palace was riddled with small magics, the kind people hid so well they almost hid them from themselves. Small magics, harmless, woven into the ordinary work of keeping a household of two thousand souls alive.

The kind that could be sniffed out. The kind that, once named, could be *taken*.

And every one of them taken would feed it. She understood that now, watching the guards break formation and flow toward the servants' stairwells like blood filling a wound. It wasn't purging the palace of the gifted. It was *harvesting*. And it was doing it because she was here—because the only creature in these walls that could see it truly needed to be blinded first, stripped of every sympathetic eye, left alone in a labyrinth where everyone who might have helped her was being dragged away in chains to an examination she knew, now, was another word for consumption.

*It doesn't want me dead,* she thought. *It wants me deaf.*

She pulled on her coat and went out into the ringing dark.

* * *

Corwin Hale was waiting in the disused chapel off the east cloister, the one with the roof half fallen and the saints' niches empty, where he conducted his business among pigeons and broken glass because no one else wanted to be there. He arrived looking like a man who had dressed in a hurry and lost the argument with his mirror, and his eyes had the flat shine Rafael had learned to read as fear held under professional pressure, the way gas is held under glass.

"They started at the kitchens," he said without greeting. "Forty minutes ago. They have lists, Rafael. Accurate lists. Names I buried so deep even I had to dig twice to find them."

"Your network?"

"Gutted." He said it evenly, which cost him. "Three of my people taken in the first sweep. Sela. The twins who ran messages between the east gate and the records office. And—" He stopped. Started again. "They took Marisol from the infirmary. She wasn't mine. She was just a girl who could cool a fever with her hands and never told anyone, never used it, never—she folded bandages, that was all she did. Someone gave them her name."

"The entity," Rafael said. "It's feeding them the names. Through the king's mouth, through the chancellor's office—it doesn't matter which. It knows this palace down to its bones. It's lived in three kings' heads. It knows exactly which threads to pull to unravel you."

"Yes." Corwin paced to the broken altar and back, a tight circuit. "Which means my next move has to be tonight, because tomorrow there won't be a next move. Every informant I have left is either hiding or deciding whether selling me to the crown pays better than loyalty does. I need the king's private schedule, Rafael. The real one. Where he goes, when, alone, unwitnessed. The renewal ceremony is announced for the solstice, but a thing like that doesn't announce everything. It rehearses. It prepares. There are hours when the king walks unguarded—I've seen the gaps myself, years ago, before I fell—and if we're going to strike before the solstice binds it permanently, we need those hours. We need them *now*, while there's still someone left inside the household to steal them for us."

"And who's left?"

"One person." Corwin's jaw tightened. "A woman called Odile. Chamberlain's office. She's been copying the king's private calendar onto rice paper and passing it out in rolled linens for six years, and in six years she has never once been late or been seen. If anyone can get us the schedule before the sweeps reach her door, it's Odile."

"Then send word to her."

"I sent word an hour ago." He looked at Rafael then, directly, and she saw what he was not saying settle over his face like frost. "I'm telling you because you should know the shape of what's happening. This purge isn't random. It's aimed. At you. Everything it takes between now and the solstice is a wall it's building around you, brick by brick, out of people. By the time the ceremony comes, you'll be the only gifted soul in the palace—which is precisely where it wants you. Alone. Visible. Unable to move without it feeling the ground move under your feet."

Rafael looked at the empty saint-niches, the pigeon-gray light dying above them.

"Then we don't let it finish the wall," she said.

* * *

Odile came to them past midnight, and she came dying.

Rafael heard her before she saw her—a soft dragging sound in the cloister passage, the scrape of a shoulder against stone—and had the door open before the second knock could fall. The chamberlain's clerk stumbled across the threshold clutching her side, and in the candlelight Rafael saw the dark bloom spreading between the woman's fingers, saw the way her lips had gone the color of tallow.

"Guard post at the records stair," Odile rasped. "New orders tonight. Challenge anything that moves after the bells. I got past because I've walked that corridor eleven thousand times and they don't look at furniture." She laughed, and the laugh tore into a cough that brought red up her throat. "Old trick. Be boring."

"You're stabbed," Rafael said stupidly, catching her weight as her knees went.

"Mm. Deep. I know what deep looks like; I've worked palaces thirty years." Odile let herself be lowered to the cold floor against the chapel wall, and her eyes—sharp, dark, unhurried—found Rafael's face and studied it. "You're the surveyor. Hale's new surveyor. You're the one it's hunting."

Rafael went still. "You know that."

"I copy the king's calendar, child. I copy everything. I copied the order for your arrest twice before Hale erased it." Her breath whistled. "I know more about this house than God does. That's why it came for me tonight. Not the guards. It sent the guards everywhere else as noise. For me it sent the quiet man with the knife—the one who smells like a beach at low tide. You know him? You'd know him."

Rafael knew him. She had seen him standing behind the throne at the audience, still as driftwood, and she had felt from him nothing—no magic, no life-warmth, nothing at all, a shaped absence walking in a servant's livery. Salt given legs.

"I have it," Odile said, and fumbled at her bodice with bloody fingers until Rafael helped her draw out a roll of rice paper wrapped in oilcloth, thin as a wafer, stained now along one edge where the blood had soaked through. "Eleven days. Every hour the king spends alone. The crypt visits—he goes to the crypt, did you know? Under the old chapel. There's a door in the floor and he goes down and comes up different, less, each time. Whatever it needs for the binding, it keeps down there. Or it feeds there. I couldn't ever see which." Her hand closed around Rafael's wrist, grip astonishingly strong. "Take it. Finish it. I spent thirty years invisible in this house and I will not be invisible to *that*. Promise me it counts. Promise me the paper matters."

"It matters," Rafael said. "I promise."

Something eased in Odile's face. "Good," she whispered. "That's—good. Tell Hale he owes me a year's wages and an apology for the thing with his wife, which was not my fault and which I will now take to my grave smugly." The ghost of a smile. "Tell him—"

She didn't finish it. The sentence stayed unfinished forever, hanging in the candle smoke, and Rafael held the woman's hand until it cooled while outside the cracked bell tolled the quarter hour, hollow, indifferent, counting time that Odile had run out of.

Corwin arrived twenty minutes later, having come fast enough to sweat through his shirt, and stopped in the doorway when he understood. Rafael watched a man whose profession was composure fail to compose himself. He crossed the floor, knelt, and pressed two fingers briefly to the dead woman's throat as if some part of him expected his trade to have lied to him about this one thing.

"She was the best source I ever had," he said quietly. "Thirty years. She never asked for anything except that I fix the chapel roof, and I kept meaning to." He rose, and when he turned to Rafael his grief had been hammered flat into something harder. "This is what it does. This is the shape of the next five days. It will spend people like this—spend *us*—until there's nothing between it and the solstice but stone and silence. So tell me the schedule tells us something we can use, because I am not burying her for paper."

Rafael unrolled the rice paper with hands that were not quite steady.

Eleven days of the king's hidden hours, written in a clerk's beautiful, cramped hand. Most of it confirmed what Corwin's fragments had suggested: solitary walks, sealed meetings, the recurring descent into the crypt beneath the old chapel—every third night, always between midnight and the second bell, always alone. But near the end of the roll, dated four days hence, one line stood apart, circled twice in Odile's ink:

*The king receives the Master of Rites privately, midnight, lower chapel. No guard within fifty paces. He carries something wrapped in sailcloth.*

Sailcloth. Rafael stared at the word until it seemed to swim. She had grown up a mountain woman; she knew sailcloth the way she knew snowmelt. Sailcloth belonged to ships, to coasts, to the sea this kingdom had none of anywhere near its capital—and yet the thing on the throne, the thing that tasted of brine and tide and the space between waves, was carrying something wrapped in it down into the dark beneath the chapel, alone, four nights before the ceremony that would bind it to the crown forever.

"It's preparing the vessel," she said. "Or the anchor. Whatever it needs down there to make the binding permanent—it's moving it into place. And it's doing it unguarded, because it thinks the wall around me is finished." She looked up at Corwin, and found her grief had done what fear alone could not: burned away the last of her hesitation, leaving something clean and cold and certain underneath. "Four nights. Midnight. One door in the floor, and one chance before the solstice to see what we're really fighting."

Corwin was silent a moment. Somewhere above them, the hollow bell struck two, and the sound went through the ruined chapel like a stone through still water.

"Then we go down," he said.
