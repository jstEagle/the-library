## 17. What Tam Carried

The scaffolding around the Coronation Hall smelled of cut pine and salt.

Tam Vess had been noticing the salt for weeks now—salt in the mortar, salt dusted along the ceremonial ropes like frost that wouldn't melt, salt crusted white on the boots of the king's guard when they tramped past his station at the workmen's gate. He'd grown up two days' walk from the Thornfell coast and knew what brine did to wood and iron. It ate. Slowly, patiently, the way a man eats when he knows the meal will never run out.

He kept his head down and his ink-stained fingers busy. That was the arrangement. He copied patrol schedules in a clerk's cramped hand, he memorized faces at the preparatory gates, and every seventh day he carried his reports to a gray-eyed man in the Records Office who never asked how Tam had come to know so much about gifted-hunting protocol. The crown paid him in coin and in the particular mercy of not asking questions about a mountain boy with no family name worth speaking.

He told himself it was restitution of a kind. He'd sold one map; now he made them. He'd handed over one woman to the hunters; now he helped them find others. Balance. Arithmetic. A ledger that would eventually, if he kept adding honestly on the right side, forgive him.

He was very good at not thinking about Rafael Whitlock's face the morning the riders came. He was less good at night, when the arithmetic stopped working and all that remained was the memory of her hands pressed flat against the split stone of the pass, holding back a rockslide with nothing visible but her will—and the look on her face when she turned and found him watching. Not anger. Worse than anger. She had looked *sad*, the way you look at a bridge you already know is going to fail, and she had said, *Tam. You can go, or you can stay silent. Those are your choices,* and he had chosen neither, because choosing felt like drowning either way, and so he had simply done nothing until doing nothing became its own choice, the worst one, the one with riders and hounds in it.

*You can go, or you can stay silent.*

He had gone. Straight to the garrison, as it happened. Straight to Captain Dree with shaking hands and a story about a witch in the high passes, and they'd given him ten crowns and a hot meal, and by spring they'd given him a desk.

Ten crowns. He still had seven of them sewn into his coat lining. He couldn't spend them. He also couldn't throw them away. They sat against his ribs like a fever.

* * *

On the morning everything changed, Tam was checking seal-weights at the west staging yard, where carpenters were raising the dais for the renewal ceremony. The Hall's great doors stood open to let in timber and light, and through them he could see the interior being transformed: banners of white and silver hung from the clerestory, a circle of pale stone slabs laid into the floor where the coronation mosaic used to be. Workers knelt around the circle's edge, grouting seams with a paste that steamed faintly even in the cold.

Tam had learned not to look directly at the circle. Looking at it too long gave him headaches, a pressure behind the eyes like a change in weather. But he glanced, the way everyone glanced, and that was when he saw her.

She stood near the surveyors' table under the south colonnade, wearing a plain wool coat and a surveyor's leather satchel, arguing quietly with a palace steward about elevation measurements for the banner rigging. Her hair was shorter than he remembered, bound back with cord. There was a scar along her jaw he didn't recognize.

Rafael Whitlock. Alive. Standing forty feet away in the heart of the palace that had hunted her across half a kingdom.

Tam's first thought, absurdly, was that she'd come to kill him. His second thought was that he deserved it. His third thought—the one that stayed, that rooted itself in him like the salt rooted in the mortar—was that she was here for the same reason he was: because this place needed stopping, and someone had to be close enough to stop it.

Because Tam had been inside the Records Office. Tam had read the sealed correspondence that crossed the spymaster's old desks before Corwin Hale fell from grace. Tam knew what three reigns of *sudden devotion* looked like in the margins of history, and he had watched King Aldric—had watched him at the winter review, watched him pause mid-sentence for eleven full seconds while his eyes went white-rimmed and distant, watched the court pretend not to notice—and Tam, who had once lived beside a woman who could feel the land breathing, knew wrongness when it wore a crown.

He had sold out one person who could fight this thing. He would not do it twice.

* * *

He spent four days finding the pattern.

That was the thing about Tam: he was patient in the way of people who have done something unforgivable and are looking for anywhere to put their hands. He copied the rotation ledgers anyway—it was his job—and he copied them a second time onto scrap, folding the sheets small enough to hide in his boot, and he studied them at night in his narrow room above the tannery row until he understood the shape of the security for the ceremony itself.

It was elegant, which frightened him. The entity—or the crown, or whoever served it; the orders bore no signature, only the privy seal—had designed the ceremony's defenses like a closing hand. Outer rings of city watch that thinned toward the Hall rather than thickened. Interior guard rotations timed so that the corridors around the ceremonial circle would be empty for precisely nine minutes during the rite's second phase—not by accident, Tam was sure, but because whatever wore Aldric's face wanted no witnesses to what it intended to become. The guards themselves would be drawn from companies dosed with something in their evening ration; Tam had seen the requisition chits, marked *ceremonial provisions*, routed through a quartermaster whose name appeared nowhere else in any ledger.

And threaded through it all, the hunters. The gifted-sniffer packs, six of them, rotating on a quarter-bell schedule through the approaches to the Hall. Their handlers carried vials of residue-lure—Tam had signed for the delivery himself, his own hand on the receipt, salt crystals in wax-sealed glass—and the dogs would be worked hardest along the colonnade route, the surveyors' route, the one path a disguised woman might reasonably walk.

They weren't hunting intruders. Tam understood that with cold certainty. They were hunting *her*. Whatever sat on the throne had known since the audience chamber that Rafael Whitlock could see it, and it had arranged its own coronation like a trap with one door, and the door was guarded by animals that could smell her gift the way Tam could smell the tanneries from his window.

Every night he folded the rotations smaller. Every morning he almost burned them. Every evening he saw her again—at distance, always at distance, crossing the yard with her satchel, pausing to touch the stone of the old wall the way she used to touch the living rock of the passes, palm flat, eyes closed for half a breath—and every night the arithmetic failed again, and the seven crowns against his ribs burned hotter than the papers ever did.

*You can go, or you can stay silent.*

No. There had been a third choice all along. He just hadn't been brave enough to see it. You could stay, and speak, and pay.

* * *

He chose the north colonnade on the eighth day because it was the only stretch of her route without line-of-sight from any guard post, ninety feet of shadowed arcade between the surveyors' annex and the masons' stair, and because the dog rotation there had a gap—a quarter bell and a half, between the third pack turning east and the fourth coming up from the river gate—that existed only if the handlers stopped to water the animals at the fountain, which they did, reliably, because the requisition for the watering trough had been approved by Tam's own office and he had written *fountain, north colonnade* into the placement order himself, weeks ago, for reasons he hadn't let himself examine at the time.

He had been building this door for weeks without admitting it. That was the most frightening part. Some part of him had known from the moment he saw her in the yard.

He waited in the arcade's shadow with the folded papers sweating against his palm. Quarter bell. Half. His mouth tasted of copper. Then she came, alone, satchel on her hip, and Tam stepped out of the shadow before his courage could fail, and said, "Mistress Whitlock," in a voice that cracked on the second syllable.

She stopped. She didn't startle—that was the terrible discipline of her—but he watched recognition move through her face like weather, and then something colder settle over it. Her hand drifted toward the satchel's strap.

"Tam." Just his name. No warmth in it. No hatred either. Something flatter than both, and he deserved the flatness.

"I know what I did." The words came out in a rush, rehearsed a hundred times in his room and dissolving now, all of it, leaving only the raw truth underneath. "I'm not asking—I'm not asking anything. I brought—" He held out the papers, folded small, his thumb pressing them flat. "Patrol rotations. All of them. The dogs, the guard gaps, the drugged companies, the nine minutes during the rite when the corridor by the circle is empty. It's a trap, mistress. The whole ceremony is a trap built around you. It knows you're here. The lure vials, the routes—they're tuned to your gift. If you walk the south colonnade on ceremony day, they'll have you before you reach the doors."

She didn't take the papers. She looked at him for a long moment, and Tam stood in the cold and let her look, because he had spent a year running from exactly this gaze and he was done running from it.

"You've been feeding them my name," she said quietly. "Since the passes."

"Yes."

"And now you're feeding me theirs."

"Yes."

"Why?"

There it was. The question he'd rehearsed answers to—*guilt, restitution, balance*—and standing in front of her he found he couldn't say any of them, because they were all lies dressed in different coats. What came out instead was small and true:

"Because you gave me a choice, and I took the coward's road, and I've been walking it ever since, and it doesn't end anywhere. It just circles. Every report I file, it circles back to the pass, to the rockslide, to your face when you saw me seeing. I thought selling you would make me safe. It made me *theirs*. And I've read enough of their sealed records to know what *theirs* means, mistress. It means ending up like the king. Hollowed out and smiling." His voice shook. "I'd rather be caught helping you than safe helping them. That's the whole of it. That's everything I carry."

For a moment she said nothing. Then she reached out and took the papers, and her fingers brushed his, and they were steady where his were not.

"The nine minutes," she said. "Second phase. How do you know it's real and not bait?"

"Because I wrote the supply order for the corridor lamps myself, and the lamps are unlit that shift. Nobody baits a trap with darkness. Darkness is for things that don't want witnesses." He swallowed. "Whatever happens in that circle, they don't want even their own soldiers remembering it."

Something shifted behind her eyes—recalculation, the cartographer redrawing a map in real time. "Tam. Listen to me. After today, you can't be near the Hall. Take what coin you have and—"

"No." He said it before he could stop himself. "There's a copy problem. The rotations I gave you are current through tomorrow, but the entity reissues the dog schedule every dawn, and after tomorrow the gap closes—they're moving the watering trough to the river gate. If your plan needs that corridor, you need someone inside who can tell you the night before whether the gap holds." He lifted his chin. "I'm inside, mistress. I'm the only one you've got who's inside and isn't theirs."

"That's exactly what makes you expendable to them, the moment they suspect you."

"Then they'd better not suspect me." He managed something that wasn't quite a smile. "I was your apprentice for three years. I learned hiding from the best."

She stared at him, and for one unguarded instant he saw it—the old tenderness, the woman who'd taught him to hold a stylus, to read contour lines, to listen to stone—and it hurt worse than hatred would have. Then it folded away again, careful as a map.

"Dawn reports," she said finally. "The fountain in the masons' court, before the sixth bell. Chalk mark on the trough's north rim if the gap holds. Nothing else. If you miss one dawn, I assume you're taken, and I don't come for you." A beat. "Do you understand? I won't come for you, Tam. I can't afford to."

"I understand." He had expected the words to wound. Instead they settled over him like a weight he'd earned, almost comfortable in its honesty. "One more thing." He dug into his coat lining and pressed the seven remaining crowns into her hand, warm from his body, before she could refuse. "Blood money. Spend it on rope or bribes or drink, I don't care. I can't keep it another night."

"Tam—"

"It's not a gift. It's ballast. I'm trying to sink lighter." He stepped back into the shadow of the arcade. "Dawn reports. North rim of the trough. Don't die before I've finished paying."

* * *

They took him at the fountain.

Later—he would have a great deal of later, though not the kind he'd imagined—Tam would reconstruct exactly how it had gone wrong, because understanding failure was the one skill the crown had actually taught him well. It wasn't the chalk. It wasn't the papers; those were long out of his possession, folded into a false seam of Rafael's satchel. It was simpler and stupider than that: it was the watering trough itself. He had written the placement order. When the entity's people began auditing every document connected to the ceremony's security—sweeping backward through the paperwork like hounds working a scent line, hunting not a gift this time but a *pattern*, an anomaly, a hand that had placed things slightly wrong on purpose—they found his name on the fountain order, and cross-referenced it against the lamp order, and against the dog-watering schedule, and a quiet man from the Records Office, gray-eyed, unhurried, came to the masons' court at the fifth bell instead of the sixth and found Tam kneeling at the trough with a stick of chalk in his hand and a fresh mark drying on the north rim.

They didn't shout. That was the worst of it. Two guards took his arms, and the gray-eyed man crouched, picked the chalk out of Tam's slack fingers, and examined the mark with genuine professional interest.

"Clever," the man said. "A signal system. Who reads the marks?"

Tam said nothing. He had learned silence from the best, too, and he spent it all in one long pull, because he knew what was coming and knew that names were the only currency they'd want, and there was exactly one name they must never hear from his mouth.

They walked him through the service corridors, past the scaffolding, past the pale circle gleaming behind the Hall's open doors, and Tam looked at it—one last look, straight on, headache be damned—and understood suddenly, viscerally, the way you understand a cliff edge only at the toe of your boot, that the thing in the circle was awake and aware and had been aware of him for some time. The pressure behind his eyes swelled until his vision whited at the edges. And in that white, for half a heartbeat, he felt something vast and patient turn its attention toward him the way a man turns over a coin to read the far face—

—and then they were dragging him down the stair to the cells beneath the Records Office, and the last thing Tam Vess thought, as the dark took him, was not a prayer and not a plea but a piece of arithmetic, the only honest sum he'd ever completed:

*Seven crowns given. One map delivered. Dawn mark made.*

*Paid,* he thought, as the cell door boomed shut. *Not forgiven. But paid.*

Above him, through fathoms of stone, the bells began to ring for the rehearsal of the rite—and somewhere in the palace, a woman with a satchel and a scar on her jaw was reading a chalk mark that would never be renewed, and planning her move into a corridor that would, in three days' time, be full of guards.
