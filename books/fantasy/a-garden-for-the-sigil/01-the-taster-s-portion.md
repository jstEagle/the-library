## 1. The Taster's Portion

There were three rules to surviving as the king's food-taster, and Mirabel Ravensworth had written all of them himself.

One: never eat anything you haven't watched being made. Two: never trust a dish that arrives already garnished — garnish is where assassins hide their sins. Three: never, under any circumstances, develop a favorite food. A man with a favorite could be poisoned through it, and worse, a man with a favorite had something to lose.

He had kept these rules for twenty-two years, and they had kept him alive through four assassination attempts, two wars, and one regrettable incident involving the Duke of Harrowmere and a platter of candied figs that Mirabel still could not smell without his throat tightening in old, familiar dread.

So when the morning's first course arrived at the tasting table — a simple pheasant pie, brown crust, no garnish, prepared under his own supervision by a kitchen boy who trembled like a rabbit whenever Mirabel so much as glanced at him — he should have felt nothing at all. Routine. Safe. The safest thing in his whole careful life.

Instead, he paused, fork halfway to his mouth, because something in the air had changed.

It was a faint sweetness, like honey left too long near a candle. The valley of Vell did not smell like that. Vell smelled of bread and river-stone and the particular clean nothingness it had always smelled of, for as long as anyone could remember. Nothing rotted quickly here. Nothing bloomed early or late. Nothing *happened* out of season, which was why the kings had built their palace here three hundred years ago, in a valley where magic could not follow them, and why the position of royal taster had been invented — not to guard against poison alone, but against everything the valley refused to let in.

Mirabel lowered the fork. Sniffed again. Nothing. He was, he decided, tired. He had been tired for eleven years, ever since the scar on his ribs from the second assassination attempt had begun aching before rain.

He ate the pie. He waited the customary quarter hour, counting his own pulse the way other men counted sheep. When nothing happened — no numbness, no burning, no sweet almond ghost on his tongue — he nodded to the steward, and the king's breakfast proceeded, and the kingdom went on being fed and safe and utterly unmagical.

That was how the day should have ended. It did not.

* * *

The stranger came at dusk, stumbling up the palace road on legs that had forgotten how walking worked.

Mirabel saw him from the wall walk, where he took his evening constitutionals — another rule of his own devising; a taster who never left the kitchens became a taster who stopped noticing things. The man moved wrong, lurching between the cobbles' shadows as though each one were a doorway he might fall through. His cloak was road-gray and travel-worn, his boots worn through at the heel, and there was blood on him — not much, but old blood, dried black along one sleeve, the kind that comes from a wound a man has been ignoring for days because stopping would mean dying.

"Open the gate," Mirabel called down, and then, because caution was his native language, added, "slowly."

The guards knew him. Everyone knew the Taster; he was a small legend in Vell, the man who ate death for wages. They hauled the gates wide, and the stranger staggered into the torchlight, and Mirabel got his first good look at him and understood, with the cold certainty of long experience, that this man was going to die tonight.

He had seen enough dying men to know the look. It wasn't the wound — though the wound was bad, a dark bloom spreading across the stranger's side like ink dropped in water. It was the eyes. Dying men either went glassy and far away, or they went terribly, urgently *present*, as if the world had suddenly become interesting precisely because they were about to be removed from it. This one was present. He looked straight at Mirabel across the courtyard, and his cracked lips shaped a word.

"You."

"Me," Mirabel agreed, descending the steps. "I'm nobody. Who are you?"

The stranger laughed, which cost him dearly; he folded nearly double, coughing, and when he straightened there was blood on his teeth. "Nobody," he wheezed. "Oh, that's — that's very good. That's the best joke I've heard in a hundred miles." He swayed forward, and Mirabel caught him — instinct, reflex, the same reflex that had caught a poisoned goblet out of a visiting ambassador's hand once — and the man's weight came against his shoulder, and his mouth found Mirabel's ear.

"They're coming for it," he whispered. "The sorcerers. They let me run because running leads them. But you — you have to be faster than grief, taster. You have to be faster than *them*."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Mirabel said, which was true, and also, he realized with a strange lurch, beside the point, because the stranger's hands were already moving, pressing something flat and wrapped in oilcloth against his chest.

"Take it. Take it *inside* yourself, if you can. You're the only — " A spasm. The man's grip tightened with the last strength of a drowning sailor. "They made you to hold it without knowing. They made you a *keyhole*, and keyholes don't get to choose what walks through them. But you can choose to lock the door."

"I'm a food-taster," Mirabel said. His voice sounded very far away. "I taste things. That's the entire of my employment."

"Yes," the stranger said, smiling with bloody teeth, and there was such wild tenderness in it that Mirabel's chest hurt. "And you've been tasting the valley your whole life, haven't you? Swallowing its nullity every morning like medicine. Keeping it *closed*." His eyes rolled toward the darkening sky. "Look up, Ravensworth. Look what you've been holding shut."

And Mirabel looked up, and the stars were *moving*.

Not falling. Not shooting. Drifting — slowly, gently, rearranging themselves like sleepers turning over in bed, sliding into patterns no astronomer of Vell had ever charted, because those patterns had never been visible from inside the valley before. One star pulsed green. Another burned the deep amber of dragonfire in old woodcuts, the kind the priests called heretical and burned in the square when he was a boy.

The oilcloth bundle was warm against his chest. Warmer than body heat. Warm like a hearth, like a held breath, like something alive.

"What is it?" he asked.

"The covenant," the stranger said. "What's left of it. What they broke and couldn't unbreak and hid inside a baby thirty years ago because a valley full of nothing was the only box strong enough to hold it." His hand slid down Mirabel's arm, leaving a streak of blood like a signature. "You were that baby. I'm sorry. Truly. Nobody should learn what they are from a dying man in a courtyard."

Then his knees gave, and Mirabel lowered him to the cobbles with the strange gentleness one reserves for broken things, and the stranger looked past him at the rearranging stars and said, softly, wonderingly, "Oh. There it is," and died with his eyes open and his face turned toward the sky like a man watching something beautiful arrive.

* * *

Mirabel did not sleep that night. He told himself it was procedure — an unidentified death on palace grounds meant reports, questions, a search of the body, all the tedious machinery of caution. He performed it all correctly. He surrendered the stranger's effects except the one thing no one knew he carried. He answered the captain's questions in a level voice. He signed his name in the incident book, and his hand only shook a little.

But when the palace finally slept, he climbed to his narrow room above the tasting chamber, bolted the door, lit no candle, and unwrapped the bundle on his floor.

Inside lay a map. Not paper — something older, thin as leaf-skin, warm as a sleeping bird. Inked across it in lines that shifted when he wasn't looking directly at them was the shape of the valley itself, and beyond its borders, mountains he had never seen drawn on any map of Vell, and beyond *those*, a coastline, and a realm marked with a single character that glowed faintly ember-orange, like a coal refusing to die.

A sigil sat at the map's heart. As he watched, it pulsed once — slow, patient, like a heartbeat waiting to be noticed.

And Mirabel's scars began to glow.

All of them. The rib scar from the figs affair. The thin white line on his palm from the goblet he'd caught. Even the tiny childhood scar on his knee he'd never been able to explain, the one his mother — whoever she had truly been — must have kissed better once, long ago, in some other country. They shone soft gold beneath his nightshirt, warm, painless, *awake*, and Mirabel sat down hard on the cold floorboards because his legs had opinions of their own.

Below his window, the palace gardens were blooming.

It was the dead of winter. Frost silvered the parapets; the fountains stood locked in ice. And yet the rose bushes along the south wall had burst into flower overnight — hundreds of them, red and gold and a color he had no name for, a color like the space between two notes. Snowdrops pushed up through frozen gravel. The ancient pear tree in the courtyard, barren for a decade, hung heavy with fruit that gleamed like little lanterns.

The valley's stillness — the ageless, changeless, safe nothingness he had swallowed every morning of his life without ever knowing he was swallowing it — was cracking. And through the cracks poured color, and warmth, and a hum he felt in his glowing scars more than heard, a hum like the world clearing its throat after three hundred years of silence.

He should have been terrified. He was terrified. Caution was his religion and this was blasphemy on a cosmic scale; somewhere below, sorcerers would be waking to empty cages and instruments pointing in directions instruments shouldn't point, and they would come looking for the keyhole, and he was the keyhole.

But underneath the dread, in a place he had kept locked even from himself, something else stirred. Something that felt the impossible roses and did not think *danger*. Something that thought, with a child's naked hunger:

*More.*

Mirabel pressed his glowing palm flat against the floorboards and felt the hum rise to meet him, and somewhere far away — beyond the mountains, beyond the edge of every map he'd ever trusted — something vast and old and half-asleep turned over in its ash-choked dreams, and opened one eye.

*They know,* the sigil seemed to say, pulsing against the dark. *They are coming.*

Mirabel Ravensworth, who had spent twenty-two years making himself unremarkable, invisible, safe, reached out and picked up the map.

"Well," he said to the empty room, to the blooming garden, to the eye opening at the world's edge. "I suppose I'd better be faster than grief."

Outside his window, a rose opened with an audible sigh, out of season, out of order, out of patience — and far below, in the sleeping palace, a bell began to ring.
