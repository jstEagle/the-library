## 2. What the Valley Kept Out

The bell rang three times, which meant nothing good, and then a fourth time, which meant nothing at all, because the fourth bell had not been rung in living memory and the ringer was clearly improvising.

Mirabel had perhaps forty minutes. He knew this the way he knew everything about the palace: by tasting it. Forty minutes for the night-wardens to wake the sorcerers, forty minutes for the sorcerers to stop being annoyed about it, and then — he did not know what came after that, and the not-knowing sat in his stomach like a bad oyster.

He packed the way a taster packs: quickly, quietly, and with an eye toward things that might later be useful in an emergency involving poison. His knife. The antidote kit he was technically not supposed to have. A bag of dried ginger, because fear and nausea were cousins and he had spent his whole life introducing them.

The map he wrapped in linen and bound against his chest, where it pulsed like a second heart that had opinions about his decisions.

*You could run,* said the sensible part of him, the part that had kept him alive through eleven years of courtly dining. *Out the postern gate, down the mill road, gone before anyone—*

But there was nowhere to go. That was the thing about the valley. He had never once thought of it as a cage, because he had never seen the bars. Now the roses bloomed out of season and his old scars glowed faintly gold in the dark, and Mirabel understood that the bars had been inside him all along.

There was one person in the palace who might know more than he did, and she was currently under house arrest for the crime of telling the truth too loudly at a banquet.

* * *

Sera Quill's rooms above the stillroom smelled of chamomile, ink, and righteous grievance. She opened her door before he knocked, which told him either that she'd heard the bell or that she was the sort of woman who opened doors on principle, to spite them.

"You," she said. "The taster."

"The herbalist."

"They took my license." She said it with the tone other people used for announcing engagements. "Officially I am a woman who 'administers unlicensed botanical substances.' Unofficially I am a woman who noticed the king's wine was being cut with lead to stretch the vintage, and said so at volume." She looked him over — the packed bag, the glowing scars, the wild eyes. "And you're running. At last. I had a wager going with myself."

"Someone died at the gates tonight," Mirabel said. "A courier. He gave me a map and then he gave me his last breath, and since then my childhood scars have been lit like festival lanterns and the garden won't stop blooming, and I think—" his voice cracked, and he hated it, "—I think something is very wrong with me."

"Something has been very right with you for twenty-two years," Sera said, "and everyone around you worked very hard to make it feel wrong. Come in before the corridor hears you."

Her room was small and ferociously organized. She poured him willow-bark tea without asking, which he appreciated, because it was exactly what a man should drink when his hands shook, and pushed a worn leather book across the table.

"Read the third page."

It was a genealogy, hand-copied, generations of names. Most were struck through. Near the bottom, half the ink faded as though the page itself had tried to forget it, ran a single line:

*Ravensworth-line, dragon-blooded. Sealed in nullity, infant, by covenant decree. Vessel: the child himself.*

"I don't understand," Mirabel said, which was a lie. He understood perfectly. He simply needed the understanding to be wrong.

"Twenty-two years ago," Sera said softly, "the sorcerers' covenant began to fail. The magic they hoarded was leaking out of the world, and they needed somewhere to keep what remained. So they found a valley where magic could not exist, and they found a child of the old blood — the blood that magic answers to — and they made the child into a cork." She met his eyes. "You weren't born here, Mirabel. You were *installed*. Every meal you ever tasted safely, every year this valley stayed quiet and gray and ageless — that was you, holding the door shut from the inside."

"My parents?"

"Dead, or fled, or transformed into a question the sorcerers prefer not to answer. I've looked. I look at things. It's why I'm unlicensed." She leaned forward. "The courier tonight — the map he carried. It's the covenant's heart, isn't it? The original sigil, the pact between human and dragon, broken into a pattern and folded flat. They'll want it back. And they'll want you back most of all, because without you, the valley stops being null, and everything they buried here starts waking up."

As if summoned by the word *waking*, the candle flames in the room stood up straight and turned briefly blue.

"They're here," Mirabel whispered.

"Back window," said Sera, already moving. "Through the herb rows. And Mirabel — whatever they offer you, whatever they promise, remember: people who build cages always describe them as shelters."

* * *

They nearly made the wall.

Master Corvane, First Sorcerer of the Crown, stood in the moonlit courtyard between them and the gate, flanked by two colleagues whose robes shimmered with the particular smugness of men who had never once been arrested for honesty. He did not look angry. He looked patient, which was worse.

"Mirabel Ravensworth." His voice was warm as a hearth and about as safe. "You've caused a small commotion. Nothing that can't be mended. Give me the map, come inside, and we'll restore the valley's quiet by morning. You'll taste for the king again. Everything will be as it was."

"As it was," Mirabel repeated. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which surprised him; apparently terror, taken in sufficient quantity, settled like sediment. "You mean: me, swallowing poisons in the dark so no one asks why the flowers never change. Me, holding your door shut with my whole life and calling it employment."

"We *protected* you."

"You *bottled* me." The map burned against his chest, and his scars answered it, gold light bleeding through his sleeves, and Corvane's eyes tracked the glow with naked hunger. "What happens when I die, Master Sorcerer? Does the cork pop? Did any of you ever plan past the part where I was useful?"

Corvane's patience thinned by a single degree. "The alternative," he said, "is that you walk out of this valley carrying the covenant's broken heart, and the magic outside finds you — all of you, the blood in you — and unmakes you down to the screaming part. We are trying to spare you that."

"He's lying," Sera said flatly. "Or rather, he's telling the truth the way a butcher tells the truth about the pig."

"Last chance, Ravensworth."

Mirabel looked at the sorcerer, at the gray ageless valley that had been his whole world, at the wall beyond which everything waited — wonder and horror in equal measure, and wasn't that just what a life was supposed to hold?

"No," he said. "Thank you. But no."

Then he ran, and Sera ran, and the sorcerers' binding-spells cracked against the border-stones like hail on a roof, because — and Mirabel would understand this only later, breathless and bleeding and laughing in a ditch — the null-valley that smothered magic smothered *theirs* too. Inside the bars, the jailers were as powerless as the prisoner.

They went over the wall on the north side, where the stones were old and the wards were older, and dropped into the dark beyond.

* * *

Crossing the border was like being born, if being born were done backwards, painfully, and in front of witnesses.

One step, Mirabel was a man. The next step, he was a man *plus* — plus the weight of every drop of magic the valley had been wringing out of him for twenty-two years, all of it rushing home at once. It went through him like a river through a keyhole. His bones sang. His scars blazed white. He saw the map's pattern unfurl behind his eyelids, vast and broken and grieving, and he heard — distinctly, terribly — a voice older than kingdoms say his name in a language that had no word for *taster*.

He came back to himself face-down in wet grass, Sera's hand fisted in his collar, dragging him uphill.

"Breathe," she was saying. "Breathe, you enormous idiot, breathe—"

He breathed. The air outside the valley tasted different — richer, wilder, faintly of woodsmake and storm. Behind them, the valley lay silent and gray as ever, but its edge now shimmered like heat-haze, like a held breath finally let go.

Far away, on a horizon that hadn't been there before, mountains glowed with a dull ember-light, as if the land itself remembered fire.

"That's where the map points," Sera said, following his gaze. "Isn't it."

"Yes," Mirabel said. He got up on shaking legs. His scars had stopped blazing, but they hadn't stopped glowing — a thin gold seam running along his forearm, warm as a pulse. Part of him wanted to weep. Part of him, a part he had never met before tonight, wanted to laugh.

Somewhere behind them, the sorcerers had found their power again at the border's edge, and somewhere ahead, something ancient had felt him cross and turned its attention, slow and vast, toward the sound of his heartbeat.

"Well," said Sera, shouldering her pack. "Which way, dragon-blooded?"

Mirabel looked at the ember-lit mountains, and walked.
