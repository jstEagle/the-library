## 4. A Garden for the Sigil

The sigil was warm in Mirabel's hands, the way a loaf is warm when it has just come out of the oven — patient, certain of itself, waiting to be eaten or planted. He had spent thirty years tasting things to decide whether they were safe. He supposed this was no different, except that if he got it wrong, he would not merely die. He would take two realms down with him.

"You're hesitating," said Vaskarath.

The dragon lay coiled at the edge of the ash-field like a mountain that had decided, after long consideration, to become a lizard. His scales had gone the grey of old embers, and his breath came slow and rattling, but his eyes were still bright — amber shot through with green, the color of things that grow where they shouldn't.

"I'm considering," Mirabel corrected. "There's a difference. Hesitation is fear. Consideration is professionalism."

"Ah." A plume of smoke curled from the dragon's nostrils, and if smoke could be amused, this was. "And what does your professionalism conclude?"

Mirabel looked down at the map in his hands. It had stopped being a map somewhere over the last three days. The ink had begun to move, rivers wandering their banks, mountains shrugging into new shapes, and at its center the sigil pulsed like a second heart — which, he was coming to accept, it rather was. The covenant's broken heart. The thing the sorcerers wanted to bind into a weapon, and the thing Vaskarath had once wanted worn as a crown.

Both sides had corrupted it. That was the whole sad history in one sentence. Humans had hoarded the magic, damming it up in towers and bloodlines until the dragons demanded worship as payment, and worship curdled into dominion, and dominion into war, and war into a valley with no magic in it at all and an infant smuggled into its silence to smother the last bridge between the worlds.

Him. He was the bridge. He was also, apparently, the toll gate, the customs house, and the man who tasted the soup.

"I conclude," Mirabel said slowly, "that everyone who has held this thing has tried to *own* it. And that every time someone owns something this large, it breaks."

"And so?"

"So I'm going to do something no one sensible has ever done with power." He knelt, pressing his palm flat against the border-soil — that thin seam of ground where the ash of the dragon realm met the green of the valley, neither one nor the other, belonging to both and claimed by neither. "I'm going to give it away."

* * *

The sorcerers arrived at dawn, because sorcerers always arrive at dawn; it is in some contract none of them will admit to signing. There were nine of them, robed in the king's colors, and they came across the ash-field in a wedge formation that suggested they had rehearsed it, which somehow made it sadder.

At their center walked High Sorcerer Aldous Crane, a thin man with the complexion of someone who had never once been outdoors voluntarily, and in his hands a collar of black iron inscribed with binding-script that drank the light around it.

"Ravensworth." His voice carried across the dead ground without effort, which told Mirabel he was already working magic, already spending strength he'd need later. "You have something that belongs to the Crown."

"It belongs to the covenant," Mirabel said. "Which belongs to no one, which is rather the point."

"The dragon dies within the season. Its fire can be bound, bottled, *used*. One weapon to end all wars." Crane's eyes were hungry and tired at once, the eyes of a man who had convinced himself that theft was stewardship. "You were made for this, taster. You were made to be the vessel. Surrender the sigil and we will make you glorious."

"I've been made for things before," Mirabel said. "I was made to be a cage. I was made to be a lock. I find I don't care for being furniture." He glanced sideways. "Sera? If you'd stop pretending to be unconscious, now would be the moment."

Sera opened one eye from where she lay bound on the ash — captured two days past buying him time, dragged along now as leverage, and doing a truly unconvincing job of looking helpless about it. "I was resting," she said with dignity. "Being kidnapped is exhausting." Her fingers, he noted, had already worked through half her bonds. Disgraced herbalists, it turned out, knew a great deal about knots, having spent years tying up bundles of nightshade without poisoning themselves.

Crane raised the collar. The binding-script woke with a sound like a struck bell heard underwater.

Mirabel did not run. He did not fight. He dug his fingers into the border-soil, cracked the sigil-map open along its ancient fold — and planted it.

He pressed it into the seam of the world the way he had seen gardeners press seed into spring earth: gently, firmly, with the particular faith of a man who has watched enough things grow to trust the process even when it looks like burying treasure. The sigil sank. The soil shivered.

Then the valley exhaled.

Green came up through the ash like a tide. Not violent — that was the miracle of it, that nothing about it was violent — but inevitable, the way morning is inevitable. Grass threaded between the sorcerers' boots. A tree unfolded out of bare stone in the space of ten breaths, its branches heavy with blossom *and* fruit *and*, on one ambitious limb, a small cluster of grapes that had no business existing in any season whatsoever. The air filled with the smell of rain on warm earth, and beneath it, faintly, the smell of the dragon-realm's deep hot stone, and the two smells got along perfectly.

"What—" Crane began.

The collar of black iron rusted. Not broke — *rusted*, decades of decay blooming across it in a heartbeat, and the binding-script flaked away like old paint. Around the field, the sorcerers' wedges of woven power simply unraveled, threads of it lifting off them like dandelion silk and drifting toward the new garden, where they settled into the soil and became, as far as Mirabel could tell, worms.

"Worms?" Sera asked, standing free, rubbing her wrists.

"Everything becomes worms eventually," Mirabel said. "It's called compost. It's the oldest covenant there is."

Vaskarath lifted his head. All around the dragon, the ash was greening too, moss creeping up his flanks like a cat settling against an old friend, and the grey of his scales warmed toward bronze, toward ember, toward the deep red-gold of a fire that no longer needed to be enormous to be alive. His ember steadied. Mirabel could feel it — a small bright thing behind his own ribs now, banked and content, a hearth-fire instead of a wildfire.

The dragon laughed. It was a sound like distant thunder deciding to be kind.

"A garden," Vaskarath rumbled. "Three thousand years of kings and sorcerers and dragons, and the answer was a *garden*."

"To be fair," Sera said, "no one asked a food-taster before. We're the only ones who know you can't force-feed a kingdom. You have to set the table and let it come."

* * *

They did not leave. That was the part the histories would get wrong, later — the ballads would say Mirabel Ravensworth *vanquished* the sorcerers, when in truth he offered them tea.

Crane refused the tea, which Mirabel thought showed poor character. But eight of the nine stayed, at least for a while, kneeling in the border-soil with their ruined collars and their unraveling craft, learning the slower magic: how to read what soil wanted, how to prune without wounding, how to tend a living thing instead of commanding a dead one. One of them, a young woman named Pell who had joined the order mostly for the robes, turned out to have a remarkable gift with roses.

The garden grew along the whole seam of the border, a mile of green in the first year, more after. Things passed through it freely — deer from the valley walking unafraid into the dragon-realm's warm stone canyons, and small fire-lizards, hatched from eggs no one had known still existed, sunning themselves on the garden wall and stealing strawberries, which Sera pretended to mind.

And Mirabel kept it.

He lived in a small house built exactly on the line, so that his front door opened into the valley and his back door opened into the realm of the last dragon, and he liked to say he could never quite remember which side of his own bed was which. In the mornings he tasted the water from the well — out of habit, though it had never once been poisoned, and he suspected it never would be, because the garden had opinions about people who poisoned wells, and its opinions involved brambles.

Some evenings Vaskarath would lower his great head over the wall and watch him work, and ask questions about small human things — why bread needed to rest, why grief needed longer — and Mirabel would answer as best he could, and the dragon would listen as though each answer were a hoard of one.

One evening, near midsummer, Sera found him kneeling in the bean rows, hands in the dirt, humming.

"You look happy," she said, suspicious, as though happiness might be a symptom.

"I am home," Mirabel said, and realized it was true, and realized something further: that home had never been a place the valley kept safe, or a tower the sorcerers owned, or a hoard the dragon guarded. It was a threshold. It was the planting, and the tending, and the passing-through.

He was reaching for the watering can when he saw it — a figure standing at the garden's far gate, dusty from the long road down from the capital, wearing no livery at all, which from the capital was itself a kind of message.

The figure raised one hand, hesitated — and stepped onto the path.
