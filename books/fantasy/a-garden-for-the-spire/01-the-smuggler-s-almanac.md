## 1. The Smuggler's Almanac

The frost came early that year, and it came wrong.

Zephyrine Galloway knew frost the way a fisherman knows tides—by texture, by smell, by the particular hush it laid over stone. Thornfell's autumn frosts were polite things, silvering the bracken and retreating by midmorning, apologetic as a guest who has overstayed. This one had teeth. It had come down off the high snowfields three weeks before its season and settled into the valleys like something that intended to stay, and when she pressed her bare palm against a boulder at dawn, the cold bit back with an oldness that made her think of cellars, of graves, of water locked under lake ice in the deepest month.

*Wrong,* said the mountain, in the way mountains say things—not words, but pressure, a leaning-in at the edge of perception that most people mistook for imagination or dread.

Zephyrine did not mistake it. She had spent twenty-six years learning the difference between her own fear and the world's.

She crouched at the treeline above the Kestrel Pass trail, breath steaming through the wool wrapped over her mouth, and counted her rules the way other travelers counted coins. Rule one: never sleep twice in the same place within a season. Rule two: carry nothing written, wear nothing marked, answer to no name you cannot abandon. Rule three—and this was the rule that kept her alive, the rule beneath all the others—never let anyone see what your voice can do.

Below her, the pass wound down toward Hollowmere, a village of forty roofs wedged between two shoulders of granite like a bird's nest in a crevice. She could smell woodsmoke from here, thin and rationed. She could smell something else too, underneath: the sourness of hunger that had gone on long enough to become ordinary. Salt was not a luxury in the high villages. Salt was the difference between a pig slaughtered in autumn feeding a family until thaw and rotting half-fed into poison. The Spire's salt tax had tripled this year—the third increase since spring—and so Zephyrine carried forty pounds of coarse grey salt in oilcloth bundles inside flour sacks, and the Spire's toll-men on the low road could go on counting their ledgers and never know that the mountain itself had carried it past them.

She adjusted the pack straps, tested the wind—it came downslope, carrying pine resin and cold iron and, faintly, blood—and began to descend.

* * *

The lynx found her an hour later, or rather she found the lynx, because the blood-scent had been growing stronger with every switchback and she had left the trail to follow it, which violated rule four: never leave the trail. But rule four bent for certain things, and suffering was one of them.

The snow-lynx lay in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, a great pale cat with fur the color of old ice and eyes like chips of winter sky. It had been beautiful once; now it was a ruin of itself. A snare wire had caught its foreleg days ago—she could see the groove worn into the flesh where it had worried and twisted—and infection had moved in behind the injury like a tenant taking rooms. The leg was swollen to twice its size, hot-smelling even from six feet away. Flies, sluggish with cold, crawled at the wound's edges. The cat had dragged itself into this hollow to die, which was the sensible thing, the thing any creature does when it can no longer run, and it watched her approach with the flat, unblinking patience of something that had already finished its arithmetic.

"Hello," Zephyrine said softly, and stopped.

Here is what no one in the lowlands would have believed, and what no one in the highlands dared say aloud: the animals of Thornfell answered her. Not the way a dog answers its master—that was training, and training was a leash—but the way one traveler answers another across a fire. Her voice, pitched low and shaped right, went into them like warmth into stiff hands. She could ask a mule to trust a rope bridge. She could tell a frightened ewe that the wolf-smell was old and the wolf long gone. Once, terribly, years ago, she had told a cornered bear *no*, and the bear had believed her, and she had walked away from it shaking, knowing that if any living soul had seen, there was a pyre in her future with her name carved on the stake. The Spire burned beast-speakers. The Spire burned them slowly, and read aloud from scripture while they burned, and called the reading mercy.

So she kept her gift small. A whisper here, a suggestion there, folded into the ordinary work of a carter or a drover. Small enough to hide. Small enough to deny.

But alone on the mountain, with only a dying cat for witness, she let herself speak plainly.

"You're hurt," she said. "I see it. I'm not going to lie to you about what I am."

The lynx's ear flicked. Its lips peeled back from teeth longer than her thumb—not quite a snarl, more the reflexive honesty of pain.

"I have a knife," she went on, crouching slowly, keeping her hands visible and empty. "I have needle and gut-thread. I have willow-bark and a paste of comfrey that smells foul and works anyway. I can cut the wire out, clean the wound, close it. You'll keep the leg. Maybe not the speed you had, but the leg." She paused. "In exchange, I want nothing today. I want a debt. Someday, when I call, you come. That's the whole bargain. That's all of it."

She knew how it sounded. She had made this bargain perhaps thirty times in her life—with lynxes and ravens, a lame red deer, an entire herd of feral goats that had since regarded her with something unnervingly like loyalty—and the shape of it never changed, because it was honest, and honesty was the only currency animals respected. Men bargained to get the better of each other. She bargained to be owed.

The lynx held her gaze for a long moment. Then, with the deliberate dignity of a queen permitting an audience, it laid its ears back and lowered its head onto its good forepaw.

*Do it quickly,* said its body, in the wordless grammar she had been fluent in since childhood. *And if you hurt me past bearing, I will kill you, and we will both understand why.*

"Fair terms," Zephyrine murmured, and got to work.

It took the better part of an hour. The wire had bitten deep; freeing it meant cutting flesh that had begun to heal around the metal, and the lynx bore it without sound, though its breathing went ragged and its claws flexed slowly in and out of the frozen ground like a man gripping a chair rail. She flushed the wound with melted snow boiled over a twig fire—small, smokeless, fed with dead wood gathered from a mile around so nothing near the hollow would remember her passing—packed it with the comfrey paste, and sewed it shut with eleven stitches while the cat watched her hands with an intensity that would have unnerved a braver fool than she was pretending not to be.

When it was done, the lynx rose, tested the leg, and looked at her again. Something had shifted in those winter-sky eyes. Not gratitude—she didn't flatter herself with that word. Recognition, maybe. The look of a creature updating its ledger.

"There's a village below," she said, shouldering her pack. "Hollowmere. If you hunt near it, take from the woods side, not the pens. The people there are starving, and desperate men shoot first." She hesitated, then added, because the day felt strange and the frost felt wrong and some old instinct in her wanted witnesses: "My name is Zephyrine."

She almost never gave her true name to anything. But the beasts couldn't sell it, and somehow it seemed important, that morning, that someone know it—even if the someone was a cat who would forget her scent within a season.

The lynx blinked once, slow as falling snow, and limped away upslope into the white silence.

* * *

Hollowmere received her the way it received everyone: through a crack in a door.

She came in on the cart track at dusk, leading the mule—Bramble, a dour grey gelding she'd bought at fourteen and spoken to daily ever since, mostly complaints, mostly affectionate—and the village children watched her from behind woodpiles with the hollow-eyed wariness of kids who had learned that strangers meant either taxes or trouble. The miller's wife, a broad woman named Sera Halloway whose face had gone sharp with the same hunger Zephyrine had smelled on the wind, met her at the well.

"Flour," Zephyrine said. "Good northern wheat. Twelve sacks. I've grain to trade for wool or cheese or whatever you've got, and I'll be gone before light."

Sera's eyes did the arithmetic of a woman who had fed forty mouths through two bad harvests. "That's dear flour," she said carefully. "Last carter wanted half again what it's worth."

"Last carter didn't come up the Kestrel Pass in October." Zephyrine let a little of the road-weariness show, just enough to be legible. "I'll take fair price and a hot meal, and I'll say nothing about either."

They traded in Sera's kitchen, and it was there, over barley soup that Sera clearly felt ashamed to serve and Zephyrine clearly pretended not to notice being ashamed of, that the talk turned—as all talk in Thornfell turned that autumn—to the weather.

"Frosted in August," Sera said, staring into the hearth. "My grandmother lived eighty years in these hills and never saw frost in August. And now it comes down every night like it's marching. The barley died standing in the field. We cut it green and it rotted in the ricks." She rubbed her arms as though cold had gotten into the room ahead of schedule. "Priest says it's judgment. Toll-man says it's weather. My knees say it's neither."

"What do your knees say?"

Sera was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had dropped, the way voices drop in villages when the subject is the Spire. "They say it's coming from up-mountain. From the high snows. Old Tomasz swears he heard something under the ice last week—a kind of singing, he said, or grinding. And the beasts are wrong, mistress. All wrong. The deer have gone down-valley in herds bigger than anyone remembers, like they're fleeing something, and the birds—" She shook her head. "The birds stopped singing. Whole forests quiet as churches. You spend time up there. Have you heard it?"

Zephyrine thought of the silence she'd been crossing through for days, the way the mountain's usual rustle and mutter had thinned to almost nothing, as if the land were holding its breath. She thought of the frost with oldness in its teeth.

"I've heard the quiet," she said. "I don't like it."

"No more do I." Sera pushed the bread toward her, a heel of dark rye, an offering that cost more than the woman would admit. "You'll sleep in the barn loft. There's a blanket that doesn't smell entirely of goat."

Zephyrine smiled and meant it, and the smile cost her something, because kindness always did. That was the part no one warned you about, when you chose a life with no one in it: the loneliness didn't live in the empty miles. It lived in moments exactly like this one—a warm kitchen, a stranger's bread, a blanket that smelled of goat—and then ended, and you walked out into the dark again with your rules and your false names and the whole wide world arranged so carefully that no thread connected you to anything.

Competent solitude, she told herself, climbing to the loft. That was the phrase. She was competent, and she was solitary, and she had built both on purpose, stone by stone, and if the walls sometimes felt less like shelter than like a tomb she had furnished very nicely, well. That was the price of staying unburned.

Bramble stamped in his stall below, and she sent him a thread of warmth through the floorboards—*easy, old man, hay tomorrow, apples if the village has any*—and slept.

* * *

She left before dawn, as promised, and the wrongness found her on the way out.

It came as cold first—not the clean bite of altitude but a cold with intention in it, rolling down the pass in a grey wall that ate the stars. By the time she reached the first switchback the temperature had fallen ten degrees in as many minutes, and Bramble was sweating despite the chill, ears pinned, refusing with his whole stubborn body to go forward.

"Easy." Zephyrine pressed her palm to his neck and felt his heart hammering. "Easy. What is it? What do you see?"

He saw nothing. That was the horror of it. There was nothing to see—no wolf-scent, no bear-sign, no landslide rumble in the ground—only the storm front boiling over the ridge above them, black-grey and lit from within by pulses of blue-white that weren't lightning, or weren't lightning as she understood lightning, and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable now that she listened for it, a sound like vast roots grinding through stone.

The beasts had fled it. Sera had said so. The deer going down-valley in herds, the birds gone silent, the whole wild country voting with its feet—and she had filed it away as one more strange thing in a strange autumn, because filing things away was what she did instead of feeling them.

Now the strange thing was coming down the mountain after her, and every animal instinct she possessed, and every borrowed instinct the mountain had ever lent her, was screaming the same single syllable: *run.*

Zephyrine stood in the track with the reins slack in her fist and made herself breathe. Rules, she reminded herself. Rule five: when the mountain speaks, listen, but don't obey panic. Storms passed. Valleys existed. There was a ruined waystation two miles south—an old Spire relay house, abandoned since before her grandmother, roof half-fallen but walls sound—where she could sit out whatever this was with Bramble and her contraband and her heartbeat, and in the morning it would be weather again, merely weather, and she would laugh at herself over cold tea.

Probably.

"South, old man," she said quietly, turning the mule. "We're getting off this road."

Behind her, the blue-white light pulsed again over the ridge, and this time, unmistakably, threaded through the grinding, she heard something that was not wind and was not stone and was not any animal she had ever spoken with.

It sounded almost like a voice. It sounded almost like it was saying her name.

Zephyrine did not look back. Looking back was rule six, the oldest rule of all, and she had never once broken it.

She rode south into the gathering dark, toward the ruin, toward the storm, toward the small abandoned room where a dead scholar's secret had been waiting a hundred years for exactly this night to find her.
