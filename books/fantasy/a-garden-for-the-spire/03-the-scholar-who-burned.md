## 3. The Scholar Who Burned

Lowmark smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and the particular sourness of a town that had begun counting its stores too early in the season.

Zephyrine came down off the ridge at dusk with her hood up and her collar high and the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, where it lay like a second heartbeat. Three days she had walked since the waystation. Three days of turning the thing over in her mind the way one turns a stone to see what lives beneath it—*Zephyrine Galloway*, written in a dead man's careful hand, beside a date that had not yet come. Her birth name. The one her mother had whispered only once, at the edge of a fever, and never spoken again because names were debts and debts could be collected.

No living person knew it. She had believed that the way she believed in the mountains: absolutely, without examination.

Now she followed the ink backward to its source.

The cache in the waystation had been a scholar's cache—she understood that much even before she'd finished cataloguing it. Not a smuggler's hoard; smugglers packed for value and speed. This had been packed for *survival of knowledge*: wax-sealed jars of ink, quills wrapped in linen, three years of dried food measured out in a miser's portions, and books. Books beneath the floorboards, wrapped in sheepskin, their spines cracked from reading rather than rot. Someone had lived there a long time, alone, waiting for something—or hiding from it—and someone had died there, or fled, or been taken. The hearth was cold but swept. The bedroll was rolled. There was no body, and no grave, and that absence had told her more than any marker would have.

And on the inside cover of the oldest book, in the same hand that had circled her name: a name that was almost certainly false, and a town. *Maren Ostreicher, Lowmark.*

Nobody was named Maren Ostreicher. But somebody had wanted to be found by exactly one kind of person—the kind who read marginalia—and so Zephyrine had read, and packed, and walked.

* * *

Lowmark sat in the crook of two hills like an old man cupping his hands around the last coal of a fire. It was a salt-town gone quiet, all its prosperity spent decades ago when the Spire's roads had shifted east and left it to feed on itself. The houses leaned together for warmth. The well in the square wore a collar of ice despite the fact that true winter was still weeks away, and the early frosts had already blackened what remained of the kitchen gardens. Zephyrine passed a woman beating a rug hard enough to raise dust in the frozen air, and the woman's eyes tracked her the whole length of the street—not hostile, just hungry in the way of people who had learned that strangers meant either trade or trouble, and both were worth watching.

She asked no questions at first. Asking questions was how you became memorable, and being memorable was dying slowly with extra steps. Instead she did what she always did in a new town: she bought bread she didn't need, listened at the well, and let the town tell her about itself.

It told her about the scholar within the hour.

"—up at the Widow Crane's place, the old tannery road," said a drover to the smith, their voices carrying across the square the way voices carry in cold air, flat and clear as thrown stones. "Came two winters back. Keeps to herself. Pays in Spire coin, which is either very good or very bad."

"Spire coin's Spire coin," the smith grunted.

"That's what I said. But Hesk the ferryman swears he saw her burning papers on her hearth last spring. Whole night of it, smoke smelling like nothing he'd ever smelled. Sweet, he said. Like flowers burning." The drover spat. "Scholars burn things when the Spire comes asking. My cousin was a copyist in Vell's Crossing. He burned his life's work in a barrel and hanged himself off the bell tower the same night."

The smith said nothing to that. The hammer rang. Somewhere a dog began barking at nothing, and stopped abruptly, mid-cry, as if something larger had looked at it.

Zephyrine stood very still by the baker's awning, her bread warm through its cloth against her ribs, and felt the cold walk up her spine one vertebra at a time.

Burning papers. A scholar in hiding under a false name, two towns over from a dead man's cache full of forbidden prophecy. And if the drover's gossip was half true—if the Spire's long fingers had already come groping into the low country—then every day she spent deciding was a day the net drew tighter.

The Widow Crane's place sat at the end of the tannery road, where the town gave up and the birch woods began. It had been a tannery once; you could still smell the ghost of the lime pits in the ground, a pale mineral bitterness under the snow. Now it was a cottage with a slate roof furred green, a garden gone to seed and frost, and a single window lit against the dusk with a light that flickered wrong—too steady, then guttering, then steady again, like a held breath.

Zephyrine did not go to the door. Doors were for people who belonged somewhere.

Instead she went around the side, to the woodpile, and crouched in the shadow of the stacked birch, and waited, and watched, the way she had watched a hundred dens and nests and watering holes. The beasts had taught her patience. Patience was most of what they'd taught her, besides hunger.

An hour past full dark, the door opened.

A woman stepped out with a bucket in each hand—water, by the sound of them, drawn from the covered cistern rather than walking to the well, another mark against her. She was perhaps fifty, narrow as a closed book, with grey hair pinned up carelessly and hands that moved with a precision that no water-carrier's hands ever had. She set the buckets down inside the door, came back out, and stood a moment in the cold, looking up at the sky with an expression Zephyrine recognized from her own face in still water: the look of a person checking whether the world had noticed her yet.

Then the woman went rigid.

Not at anything Zephyrine had done—she hadn't moved, hadn't breathed loudly, hadn't made a sound a human ear could catch. But from the birch wood behind the cottage, something had called. Zephyrine heard it a heartbeat later: a fox, high and thin, the interrogative bark of a young animal asking a question of the dark. Except it wasn't asking the dark. It was asking *her*, and relaying her answer, and the scholar-woman's head turned toward the woodpile with the slow inevitability of a weathervane finding the wind.

"You can come out," the woman said. Her voice was dry and level, a voice used to reading aloud in silent rooms. "Or you can freeze back there. I've done both, and I recommend the door."

Zephyrine rose out of the shadow with her hood back and her hands visible, palms out, the old grammar of harmlessness. "Your fox has a loose tongue."

"My fox," said the woman, "has excellent taste in company, which is more than I can say for my own judgment. You've been in town since sundown. You bought bread at Halloran's and didn't haggle, which marked you as either rich or foreign, and your boots say neither. Then you stood by the baker's awning for the better part of an hour listening to Doss the drover ruin my reputation." She tilted her head. "You're the one Maren was leaving the cache for. Aren't you."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Zephyrine kept her face still, but the woman saw the ripple anyway—saw it and nodded, unsurprised, the way scholars are unsurprised by everything except kindness.

"Inside," she said. "Quickly. And don't touch the blue jar on the mantel."

* * *

The cottage interior was a scholar's idea of a home: books stacked in columns that served as furniture, a table buried under papers weighted with river stones, a fire built small and banked deep because heat drew eyes to windows. The smell was ink and tallow and, underneath it, faintly sweet—a burnt-flowers sweetness that matched the ferryman's story and made Zephyrine's skin prickle.

The woman lit a second candle, sat, and looked at Zephyrine across the table with eyes the color of weak tea and twice as steeped.

"Maren Ostreicher," Zephyrine said. "That's not your name."

"No. Odile Vasse is my name, though I'd thank you to bury it in whatever grave you keep for such things. Maren is who I am here. Maren dries herbs and pays her taxes and knows nothing about anything." Odile folded her hands. "You found the waystation cache. Which means you read the book. Which means—" and here her voice changed, dropped its performance, became something rawer, "—you know why you're sitting in my kitchen, and I would very much like to hear you say it, because I have spent eleven years hoping I was wrong about everything."

Zephyrine unwrapped the oilcloth and set the prophecy book on the table between them. The candleflame leaned toward it as if drawn, though there was no draft.

"I found tomorrow's date circled," Zephyrine said. "Beside my name. My real name. The one nobody living knows." She turned the book open to the page and pushed it across. "So either your dead friend knew me, or knew of me, or—" she stopped, because saying the rest aloud made it architecture instead of nightmare, "—or something wrote me down before I existed, and I've been walking toward this page my whole life without knowing it."

Odile looked at the page for a long time. Her lips moved slightly, reading not the words but the hand, the ink, the age of the vellum, the way a physician reads a body. When she finally spoke, it was barely above a whisper.

"Cassian Merel. That's the hand. That's Cassian's hand—I'd know it anywhere; I catalogued four thousand pages of it before they stripped his office and burned his library." She pressed her fingertips to the margin, gently, the way one touches a wound. "He wasn't dead when he hid that cache. He was *dying*. He wrote to me, the last year. Letters with the middle torn out, everything important said sideways. He said he'd found the root text. He said the Spire's own archive confirmed it and that was precisely why it had to disappear." Her tea-colored eyes came up. "Do you understand what you're holding? This book should not exist. Every copy of the Canticle of Roots was burned in the Purification of Vell's Crossing, forty years ago. Every copy but the ones the archivists were ordered to destroy personally, and some of us—" a bitter breath, almost a laugh, "—some of us are poor liars."

"The Canticle of Roots," Zephyrine repeated. "That's what this is."

"That's what this is. A prophecy older than the Spire, older than the Aldric line, older than the mountain passes you smuggle your salt through. And yes—" she tapped the circled date, and her finger trembled, and she let it, having apparently decided that pretending otherwise cost more than honesty, "—that is tomorrow. And that is a name I do not know, written in a cipher Cassian invented, which means it was hidden even from casual readers of a hidden book. He wanted whoever found this to be certain. Certain and afraid. Both, together. That was Cassian all over."

Outside, the wind moved through the birches with a sound like slow applause. Zephyrine became aware that her heart was going hard and fast, and that the fox was still out there somewhere, keeping watch, unpaid.

"Tell me what it says," she said. "All of it. Don't soften it."

Odile studied her for a moment—measuring, Zephyrine thought, how much truth this stranger could hold without cracking. Whatever she saw, she seemed to decide it was enough, or that the hour for softening had long passed.

"It speaks of a king," she said. "A king who will be crowned young and hollowed young, with something ancient wearing him like a coat. It calls that thing the Gardener, or the Verdant King—it uses both, the way you'd use two names for the same storm. It promises harvests without end. Green in the dead of winter. And it speaks of a child born under the winter sign, a speaker to beasts, who will come to the king at the turn of the longest night. One path leads to unbinding. The other—" Odile's throat worked. "The other completes the binding. Seals him shut forever, roots and crown and all, and lets the garden finish growing through him. The Canticle doesn't say which path the winter-born takes. Prophecies never do. They just draw the fork in the road and wait to see which way you bleed."

"And the Spire believes this."

"The Spire *wrote down* this, centuries ago, and then decided it was heresy, and then spent forty years making sure no one else could read it—which is what the Spire does with anything true. But beliefs don't die just because you burn their bodies." Odile rose and crossed to a shelf, and after a moment's hesitation pulled down a plain ledger, unremarkable, the kind any herb-seller might keep. She opened it to a page near the end. "Three weeks ago, a man came through Lowmark asking questions. Grey cloak, no insignia, but he had the Spire walk—you don't spend twenty years in those corridors without it getting into your bones. He asked after newcomers. He asked after anyone who kept animals oddly tame. He asked, and I quote the ferryman who told me, after 'a beast-speaker born under the winter sign.'" She laid the ledger flat and turned it so Zephyrine could see. It was a list, in Odile's small precise hand: dates, descriptions, directions. "There have been three of them now. They're not searching. Searching is what you do when you don't know. They're *narrowing*. And the circle they're narrowing around—" she looked up, and for the first time her composure showed the cracks all the way through, the fear underneath like water under ice, "—is this valley. Is this season. Is you."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls a mouse ran its nightly circuit, and Zephyrine reached out with the sense she usually kept leashed and touched its small bright mind—one flicker of whisker-attention—and let it go. Even that tiny contact steadied her. Beasts didn't lie, and didn't dread ahead of time. If the mouse was calm, the house was safe, for now.

"They'll come here," Zephyrine said. "To you. You're the thread that leads from the cache to the book to me. Cut the thread."

"Probably. Eventually. Inquisitors are patient the way rot is patient." Odile closed the ledger. "I've known that since the day I walked out of the Spire with my name and my life's work and nothing else. I chose this. I chose to be Maren Ostreicher, herb-wife, forgettable, safe-ish. And then Cassian's letters started arriving, and I remembered that safety is just fear that's gotten comfortable." She straightened, and something in her spine reorganized itself, some old posture of authority rising through the disguise like a building showing through ivy. "So. Here is where we are, beast-speaker. Tomorrow is the date in your book. There are Spire agents combing the low country for a woman who talks to animals. And the only person in a hundred miles who understands what that book says is a disgraced archivist pretending to sell yarrow. I can run tonight, alone, and maybe buy myself five more years of Maren. Or I can do the thing I've been cowardly and careful for eleven years, which is to be useful."

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. It came out harder than she intended. "You don't know me. You've got a life here, false or not. Why burn it for a stranger out of a dead man's book?"

Odile was quiet for a while. The candle burned between them, and the sweet burnt-flower smell curled up from somewhere beneath the floor, and outside the fox barked once, softly, a question with no urgency in it.

"Because forty years ago," Odile said finally, "I was the one holding the torch. I burned the Canticle of Roots with my own hands, six copies, on the Archivist Primus's orders, and I felt righteous doing it. I was young and certain and the Spire told me what truth was, and I believed it the way you believe in gravity." Her voice frayed at the edges. "Cassian Merel was my teacher. He tried to tell me. I reported him for it. They took his office, his library, his name from the rolls—they didn't kill him, the Spire rarely kills, it just *unmakes*—and he went into the mountains and spent the rest of his life guarding a book I helped condemn, so that someday, someone like you could find it." She met Zephyrine's eyes, and there was no performance left in her at all, only the bare architecture of guilt and purpose. "I owe a debt to a dead man and to a living stranger, and I intend to pay both. That's why. Fear keeps you alive. Guilt tells you what you're alive *for*."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. Everything in her that had survived Thornfell said: trust no one, owe no one, need no one. Every rule she'd built her invisible life on said this woman was a liability, a beacon, a wound waiting to happen.

But rules were for staying alive, and she had learned three nights ago in a ruined waystation that staying alive might not be the same thing as whatever she was supposed to be doing.

"All right," she said. "Then we do this properly. First: you're leaving this cottage tonight. Tonight, not tomorrow. Take what matters, leave the rest looking lived-in. Second: you'll want to know I have ways out of these mountains that no inquisitor will find, because the things that live in the high passes answer to me and not to maps. Third—" she paused, and made herself say it, because alliances built on omissions were just traps with better manners, "—if they take me, you run and you don't look back and you get that book somewhere it survives me. Agreed?"

Odile was already pulling a satchel from beneath a loose floorboard, already moving with the brisk economy of a woman who had rehearsed this flight for a decade. "Agreed. And one condition of mine." She glanced up, candlelight catching the sharp planes of her face. "When this is done—whatever 'done' means—I write the true account. Names, dates, the whole rotten architecture. The Spire unmade Cassian with silence. I intend to unmake them with paper."

"Deal," Zephyrine said, and went to the window, and put two fingers to her lips, and whistled—a low, bent note that no human whistle could make.

Out in the dark, the fox answered. And then, farther off, something larger answered the fox, and something larger than that, a chain of voices waking down the valleys toward them, and Odile Vasse froze with her satchel half-packed and stared at her with the expression of a scholar watching a footnote stand up and walk.

"Gods preserve us," she breathed. "It's all true. Every word of it."

"Yes," Zephyrine said, watching the treeline, where eyes were beginning to gather like green stars. "And the inquisitors are three days behind me. So pack faster."

Down in the town below, unseen, a lantern kindled in the ferryman's hut—and then a second, and a third, moving uphill through the dark, in no hurry at all.
