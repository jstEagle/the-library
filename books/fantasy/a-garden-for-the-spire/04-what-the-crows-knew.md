## 4. What the Crows Knew

Odile Vasse had spent eleven years learning to be a woman who did not flinch.

It was harder work than it sounded. In the Spire's archives she had been paid—handsomely, in the currency of respect—to flinch. To gasp at heresies in the marginalia, to recoil from the forbidden folios, to let her superiors see her horror and mark it as proof of orthodoxy. A good archivist performed her revulsion. It was part of the liturgy. *Look how this text disturbs me; therefore I am faithful.*

Lowmark had taught her the opposite discipline: to receive terrible news with the flat face of a fishwife hearing about weather. She had practiced it on debts, on dead lambs, on the slow gray news that came down from the passes each autumn like silt in a river. So when Zephyrine Galloway set the burned book on her table that morning and said, "Tell me I'm wrong," Odile did not flinch.

She only wished she could.

* * *

The room above the dyer's shop was small enough that two people made it crowded. Rain worked at the shutters in long, patient fingers. Odile had hung her one good candle where its light would not reach the window, an old smuggler's habit she'd stolen secondhand from the woman now sitting across from her, boots muddy to the knee, hair still carrying the smell of wet pine and woodsmoke.

Zephyrine Galloway did not sit like a woman whose name had just been read out of a century-old prophecy. She sat like a woman waiting for a merchant to admit he'd shorted her on salt. Arms crossed. Jaw set. Eyes doing arithmetic.

"Read it again," Odile said. "Aloud, if you can bear it."

"I can bear it." But Zephyrine didn't touch the book. She looked at it the way one looks at a snake one has already stepped over once by luck. "You read it. You're the scholar. I'm just the—the coincidence, apparently."

"You are not a coincidence." The words came out sharper than Odile intended, and she softened them with the tone she'd once used for nervous novices. "Forgive me. Sit. Listen. I'll tell you what I know, all of it, and you may decide afterward how much of it to believe. That is the only fair arrangement between us."

Zephyrine unfolded herself onto the stool by the cold hearth. Waiting.

Odile drew the book toward her across the table. Its cover was calfskin gone the color of old tallow, scarred along one edge where fire had kissed it and withdrawn. *The Canticle of Roots*, someone had tooled into the leather a hundred years ago, and beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *with annotations*. The annotations were the reason the book had survived at all, and the reason its last owner had not.

"Before I read the verse," Odile said, "you should understand what this book is. Not what the Spire says it is—that's catechism, and catechism is a fence built around a thing so no one looks over it. This is a transcription of the Thornfell oral cycles, made by a scholar named Ambrose Lune in the year the last long winter ended. He interviewed the deep-folk. He interviewed survivors of the court. And then—" her finger traced the scorch mark without touching it, "—he wrote down what they told him, honestly, which was his first crime. And then he annotated it, which was his second. And then he refused to burn it, which was the one that killed him."

"They burned him." Not a question. Zephyrine's voice had gone quiet in the way of someone confirming a shape in the dark they'd already suspected.

"In the Spire square. I was not yet born, but my teachers were his students' students, and they spoke of him the way you speak of a man who touched a hot stove. *Careful, child. This is how it happens.*" Odile smiled without humor. "I was careful for twenty years. Then I read the annotations anyway. Now here we both are."

The rain thickened against the shutters. Somewhere below, the dyer's apprentices were singing a work-song about madder root, cheerful and off-key, a small human noise that Odile clung to like a rail.

She opened the book to the ribbon-marked page. The vellum there had been handled so often it had gone soft as cloth, and the verse stood in Lune's careful hand, ringed twice in faded iron-gall ink:

*When the crown grows green and the king grows thin,*
*when winter comes and will not end,*
*a speaker shall be born under the falling sky,*
*who hears the beasts as a mother hears her own.*
*By her voice the bound king shall be loosed—*
*or by her silence, bound forever.*
*The roots choose. The roots have always chosen.*

"There," Odile said softly. "That's the heart of it. Everything else is commentary, and most of the commentary is wrong, including mine, probably."

"Unpack it." Zephyrine leaned forward despite herself. "Slowly. I'm a smuggler, not a—whatever you were."

"An archivist. Fourth rank. Which meant I catalogued things and was trusted with nothing." Odile turned the page, showing the dense web of Lune's marginal notes, tiny as insect tracks. "Verse by verse, then.

*'When the crown grows green and the king grows thin.'* King Aldric took the throne nine years ago, at nineteen. For the first three years he was—by every account—a good young king. Careful. Curious. Then, in his third spring, he went up the mountain to survey the high orchards after a blight, and he came back changed. Not mad. Worse than mad. *Pleased.* He approved everything after that. Every petition, every tax, every purge the inquisitors proposed—he smiled and signed. The court called it maturity. The poets called it grace." Her mouth twisted. "And the orchards around the capital began to bloom out of season. Fruit in the frost. Blossom in the dark of winter. Green when everything else was dying. The crown grew green, Zephyrine. Look out any window in Lowmark and tell me what you see instead."

"Frost," Zephyrine said. "Early frost. Frost that came a month before it should and hasn't lifted since midsummer."

"And the king grows thin. There are portraits, if you know where to look. Compare the Aldric of nine years ago to the Aldric of last year's coinage. The face is the same. The face is *exactly* the same—that's the horror of it, it never ages, never lines—but there's less behind it every year. Like a house being emptied room by room while the shutters stay painted."

Zephyrine was silent a moment. "You said something ancient wearing his crown. Wearing *him*, you mean."

"That's the second verse, and Lune's first annotation, and the reason he died." Odile found the note, though she hardly needed to; she had carried these words in her head for eleven years, through her disgrace, through her flight, through every gray morning in Lowmark. "'The Verdant King is older than the mountain's name. It was old when the deep-folk were young. It does not conquer. It *cultivates.* It waits at the roots of kingdoms until a mind opens to it—and a lonely boy on a cold throne is a door left unlatched.'"

"The deep-folk again." Zephyrine frowned. "Moles? Under the mountains?"

"Not moles." Odile almost laughed. "They'd take offense, and their offense takes generations. They are the ones who remember. When the last long winter fell—a hundred years ago—they watched it happen from underneath, and they survived it, and they wrote nothing down because they keep memory in stone and song, not ink. Lune sought them out. That's why his transcription is true where the Spire's version is ash and comfort."

"And the last long winter. What caused it?"

Odile closed her eyes briefly. Here was the ledge. She had known since the moment Zephyrine Galloway walked into her shop with a dead scholar's book under her arm that she would have to walk out onto it.

"A winter-born speaker," she said, "who chose wrongly."

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and the distant, idiotic cheerfulness of the dye-song.

"Chose wrongly," Zephyrine repeated. "Meaning what? Meaning she—" Odile noted the pronoun, and the slight catch in it, and filed it away— "she *bound* him instead of unbinding him?"

"Meaning we don't know, and that not-knowing is the cruelest part." Odile spread her hands. "Lune records that a speaker was born in the winter sign that year—a child who could hear the beasts, exactly as the cycle describes. The Spire's histories say she was a hero who died saving the kingdom. Lune's sources say otherwise. His deep-folk informants called her *the Rootbride.*" She let the word sit in the damp air. "They believed she went to the entity intending to sever it from the king of that age. And somewhere in the roots beneath the throne, something went wrong—or went right, depending on whose garden you think this is. The entity survived. The king of that age did not, or ceased to be a king in any way that matters. And the winter lasted eleven years and killed one person in three across these mountains, and it only broke when the speaker died. Whether her death freed the land or merely starved the thing temporarily—" Odile shook her head. "Lune couldn't learn. Neither can I. The deep-folk remember that she failed. They do not remember precisely *how.*"

"So it could happen again." Zephyrine's voice was very level. "To me. If I'm what the book says I am."

"If you're what the book says you are, then yes. The cycle gives two roads and no map of either. By your voice, the king is loosed. By your silence, bound forever. And Lune adds—" she turned to the margin, though she knew it by heart, "—'*the roots choose,'* which I take to mean the entity is not passive in this. It has waited a hundred years. It has had time to arrange the board."

"Arrange it how?"

"How does any gardener arrange a bed?" Odile said quietly. "You prepare the soil. You plant early. You choose your seed."

She watched the understanding move through Zephyrine like weather—not sudden comprehension but a front rolling in, cloud by cloud. The smuggler looked down at her own hands, brown and scarred, hands that had hauled salt and soothed lynxes, hands that belonged unmistakably to a body, a life, a person.

"No," Zephyrine said.

"Zephyrine—"

"No. I've heard this sermon before, just with different saints. Some hedge-witch told my mother I had 'the sight' when I was six, and my mother moved us twice and changed our name, and I learned my lesson: the moment anyone decides you're special, you're dead. Special is what they burn." She stood, restless, and went to the shuttered window as if she might look out, then didn't. "So here's what I think happened. I think some archivist a century ago wrote down a campfire story, and I think a frightened woman in Lowmark read too much into a date, and I think tomorrow is circled in that book because *tomorrow is a solstice-eve market day* and half the almanacs in Thornfell circle it. My true name in the margin? Someone wrote it. Someone who knew it. That's not prophecy, that's a leak." She turned. "I am nobody. I have spent thirty years becoming nobody, and it has *worked*, and I am not going to stand in your little room above a dye-shop and be talked into mattering."

Odile did not answer at once. There was a discipline to this too, she had learned—to let the denial finish, to give it room, because denial was not stupidity. Denial was a load-bearing wall. You did not pull it out from under a person; you showed them, gently, what it was holding up.

"Your birth name," Odile said at last. "The one in the margin. Who knows it?"

"No one living. My mother. Maybe a midwife who's forty years dead."

"Lune died ninety-eight years ago. The hand that wrote your name in that margin is not Lune's—I compared it against three pages before I let myself believe it. It's newer. Within the last decade, I'd judge, from the ink oxidation." She tapped the margin, where the letters stood in a fine, confident script: *Zephyrine Galloway, born under the falling sky, the roots' own seed.* "Someone wrote your name in a hundred-year-old book recently, Zephyrine. Someone who knew the name no living person knows. Does that sound like a leak to you? Or does it sound like a gardener, labeling what she planted?"

Zephyrine had gone very still. Outside, the rain faltered, hesitated, and stopped altogether, and in the new silence Odile heard the thing she had been dreading all morning, faint but approaching: wings.

"Crows don't fly in this rain," Zephyrine said. Her head tilted, the way Odile had seen her tilt it toward the lynx, toward the mules, toward everything that spoke without words. "That one isn't flying in it. It's flying *above* it. It's been circling the chimney since before dawn. I thought it was after the dyer's scraps." A pause. Her eyes came up and met Odile's, and all the skepticism in them had curdled into something colder. "It's not after scraps."

"It's been watching the house?" Odile kept her voice level. Fourth-rank archivists were trained for fires, floods, and inquisitorial visits; the training held, mostly.

"Since dawn. And it's not alone anymore." Zephyrine crossed to the window and put her eye to the gap in the shutters, and Odile saw her shoulders change. "Three more, come up from the south. Flying in a line. Crows don't fly in lines, Odile. Crows argue. These are—" she searched for the word, and Odile watched her find it and dislike it, "—*escorted.*"

"May I?"

Zephyrine stepped aside. Through the gap: the gray street, the dripping eaves, the dyer's signboard swinging. And on the ridge of the roof opposite, four crows in a neat row, feathers slicked dark as sealing wax, motionless in a way that birds were not. On the leg of the foremost, catching what light the storm allowed, something glinted. Metal.

Odile knew the shape of it before her mind consented to name it. She had stamped ten thousand of them in her years in the archives, had pressed the die into wax with her own hands: a spire within a circle, the seal of the Inquisitory Office of Records.

A message-tube. Carried by a warded crow—one of theirs, bred and bound for exactly this, which meant the bird had flown from the Spire itself, three hundred miles, through the leading edge of a winter that should not exist.

"They've found you," she said. "Or they've found *me*, and you're standing in the blast radius. Either way." She turned from the window, and eleven years of practiced calm finally cracked—not into fear, she was proud to note, but into speed. "We have perhaps a minute before it comes to the window or goes to the magistrate. Possibly less. Zephyrine—listen to me. Whatever you decided five minutes ago about not mattering, the men that crow serves have already decided it for you. There is a summons on that bird's leg or a description, and either way, by nightfall there will be inquisitors in Lowmark asking the dyer's wife about the woman lodging above her shop."

"What do we do?"

The question was asked simply, without drama, and Odile loved her a little for it, in the fierce and inconvenient way one loves a comrade.

"Back door. The dyer keeps a mule cart—there's a false floor under the indigo barrels, I've checked. We go north while the streets are empty, and you take me out of here the way you take everything: the road nobody watches." She was already pulling her cloak from its peg, sweeping the burned book into the satchel that never left her body. "And Zephyrine—when we're safe, when there's time—you'll want to argue again that you're nobody. Argue then. But understand what that crow means. It means the Spire believes the winter-born speaker exists, and it means they believe she's close enough to smell."

On the roof opposite, the lead crow lifted one foot, and the metal tube caught the light like a splinter of ice.

Then, as one, all four birds dropped from the ridge and came gliding low across the street toward the dyer's shop, silent as delivered verdicts.

"Cart," Odile said. "*Now.*"
