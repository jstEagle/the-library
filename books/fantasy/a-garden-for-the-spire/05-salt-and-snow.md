## 5. Salt and Snow

The crows told her first.

Zephyrine was mending a net in the yard behind Odile's cottage when the sky went wrong—a dozen black shapes wheeling over Lowmark's crooked rooftops, not feeding, not squabbling, but circling in that tight, deliberate pattern she had learned to read the way other people learned to read letters. *Riders on the east road. Riders in grey. Many.*

She set down the net. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake.

Inside, Odile Vasse sat hunched over the prophecy book with a jeweler's loupe pressed to her eye, copying marginalia onto scraps of paper she burned each night. She looked up at Zephyrine's face and knew before a word was spoken. Twenty years in the Spire's archives taught a person to recognize the weather before the storm broke.

"How many?" she asked.

"Twelve riders. Maybe more behind." Zephyrine crossed to the window, angling the shutter so she could see the street without being seen. Lowmark was waking around them—woodsmoke, a cart axle squealing, children shouting at the well. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that already belonged to a life she no longer lived. "Grey cloaks. The crows say they ride like soldiers and smell like ink."

"Inquisitors don't smell like ink."

"They smell like something. Everything smells like something." She turned. "Odile. How long do we have?"

The scholar removed her loupe with fingers that trembled only slightly, which for Odile was composure verging on heroism. "If they came through Harrowgate, they'll stop at the tollhouse to question the keeper. An hour. Two if they're thorough." She began stacking her papers with quick, precise movements. "They are always thorough."

"Then we leave now, or we don't leave at all." Zephyrine had spent the last three days knowing this moment would come—had felt it building like pressure behind her eyes ever since the crow with the Spire seal had landed on Odile's sill. She had packed nothing, because packing was a thing you did when you still believed you might come back. Instead she had walked her routes one final time: the salt cache under the split boulder at Wolf's Jaw, the dried fish in the hollow oak above Miller's Fork, the rope ladder coiled in the pine at Kestrel Rise. Twelve years of survival, folded into the mountain's pockets like letters into a drawer.

She could not take any of it. Taking it meant leaving a trail. Leaving a trail meant dying.

"Where?" Odile asked, shrugging into a coat too thin for the high country. "South is watched—they'll have covered every road out of the valley. West is the river, and the river freezes by week's end, but the ferryman reports to the Spire garrison at Thornholt—"

"We're not going south or west." Zephyrine pulled her hood up. "We're going up."

Odile stared at her. "Up. Into—" She made a small gesture at the northern horizon, where the Thornfells stood white and indifferent against a bruised sky, as if the gesture itself were argument enough.

"There's a pass. The Hollow Stair. It doesn't appear on any map the Spire has, because no surveyor's ever found it, because no surveyor speaks to goats." She said it flatly, without pride. It was simply true, the way the color of her eyes was true. "It comes out above the icefields on the far side. Three days' walk if the weather holds."

"And if the weather doesn't hold?"

"Then we learn what we're made of."

* * *

They left through the goat-paths behind the tannery, where the stink masked their scent from dogs, and climbed through birch scrub until Lowmark shrank to a scatter of brown roofs and thread-smoke below. At the last overlook Zephyrine stopped and looked back—not at the town, but at the east road, where she could just make out the grey column winding up the valley like a vein of slag through snow.

Twelve riders. Behind them, further back, something larger moved. A wagon. Inquisitors didn't bring wagons unless they intended to carry things home. Evidence. Or prisoners.

"They'll ask about a woman," she said quietly. "Dark-haired, travels alone, buys salt. Half of Lowmark could describe me. Marta at the mill. The ferryman's boy. The old man I sold fish to last month."

"Yes," Odile said. There was no comfort in her voice, only honesty, which was its own strange kindness. "You've been seen. You've been *known*. That was always the cost of doing what you do—the salt, the food. You bought a hundred lives with your own invisibility, and now the bill has come due."

Zephyrine said nothing. The wind off the icefield above them carried the smell of coming snow—that mineral, metallic cold that meant the sky meant business. Below, Lowmark went about its morning, ignorant, fed, alive because a smuggler had hauled contraband salt through the passes for twelve winters. Somewhere in that town was the miller's daughter whose fever Zephyrine had ridden through a blizzard to fetch medicine for. Somewhere was the widow Brenna, who owed her three favors and a debt of bread. They would describe her to the inquisitors without malice, without even hesitation, because they did not know what the questions meant, and their descriptions would be good ones.

That was the arithmetic of it. She had spent her whole life keeping the scales balanced, and now she understood—with the clarity of a blade going in—that the scales had never once been balanced. She had only been the one holding the weights.

"Come on," she said. "The Stair narrows by midday. If we're not past the Narrows before the snow starts, we die on the rock."

* * *

The Hollow Stair earned its name honestly: a fault in the mountain's flank where the stone had sheared away a thousand years ago, leaving a slot canyon so narrow that in places Zephyrine could press both palms against opposite walls at once. Ice glazed the lower reaches. Above, the walls leaned together overhead like conspirators, admitting only knives of grey light.

She went first. Of course she went first. Behind her, Odile climbed with the grim competence of a woman who had clearly done hard walking in her life and hated every meter of it on principle, muttering archive-facts to herself as a charm against fear—the elevation of the pass, the melting point of glacier ice, the year the Spire last attempted a northern survey and lost nine men.

"The beasts," Odile said at one point, breath ragged. "You said you speak to them. Is that—is it words? Actual words?"

"It's more like meaning. I send a shape of wanting, and they send back a shape of knowing. Words are what you get when two creatures share almost nothing and have to build a bridge across the gap." Zephyrine tested a handhold, found it solid, moved on. "With a lynx, there's not much gap. With a beetle, it's all words, and bad ones."

"And they obey you."

"They *trade* with me. It's different." She paused. "Mostly."

Above them, a shadow crossed the slot of sky—a bird, huge, riding the updrafts. Zephyrine sent a thread of attention upward and touched something cold and vast and pleased with itself: an eagle, hunting the ridgeline, aware of the two small warm things climbing through the stone throat below. She asked it what moved on the eastern slopes.

The answer came back as images, sharp-edged and disinterested: *grey men on the low road. A second party, smaller, faster, turning off toward the mountains. Dogs.*

Her stomach dropped.

"Odile. Stop."

The scholar froze against the rock. "What?"

"There's a second group. Six, maybe seven. They've left the road. They're heading for the high passes with tracking dogs." She closed her eyes, reaching again, harder, asking the eagle for direction, distance. The eagle gave her what it saw and nothing more—it did not care about her emergency, only about the hare it had marked in the scree—but what it saw was enough. "Northeast fork. They'll reach the mouth of the Stair by dark, maybe sooner. Someone told them where we'd go."

"Who? No one knew except—"

"No one," Zephyrine said. "Except I bought supplies in Lowmark three days ago. Rope, dried meat, cold-weather grease. And I paid in coin that came from the salt run, and coin gets counted, and merchants talk when grey cloaks start asking." She opened her eyes. "I led them here. Not deliberately. Just by being what I am. By surviving the way I survive."

"Zephyrine—"

"Climb. We can't outrun them through the Stair, but there's a branch tunnel halfway up, the Wind Chimney. It opens onto the shoulder above the icefield. If we gain the shoulder and cut across before they enter the canyon, the dogs lose us in the wind-scour. The stone up there doesn't hold scent."

"And if we don't make the shoulder?"

"Then we die somewhere beautiful," Zephyrine said, and started climbing.

* * *

They missed it by less than an hour.

The Wind Chimney was a vertical fissure, half-choked with rime, and Zephyrine went up it like the animal everyone secretly believed she was—hands finding cracks her eyes barely registered, boots wedging into stone that seemed to have been waiting for her. Odile followed on the rope, slower, swearing in three languages including, apparently, dead ones. When they finally hauled themselves onto the shoulder, the wind hit them like a wall of needles, scouring the ridge bare, and Zephyrine dropped flat and looked back down into the canyon they'd escaped.

Below, tiny as toys, the dog handlers were entering the mouth of the Hollow Stair. Even at this distance she could see the animals casting in circles at the entrance, confused, the scent-line shredded by the same wind that was currently trying to peel her scalp off.

"They've lost us," Odile panted beside her. "For now."

"For now." Zephyrine rolled onto her back and let the sky spin above her—low, heavy, the color of wet slate. The snow would come within hours. Real snow, the kind that erased trails and buried the slow. "We need to cross the icefield before it starts. After that—" She stopped.

Because she heard it.

Not with her ears. With the older listening, the one that lived somewhere beneath her sternum. The mountain itself was murmuring—and mountains did not murmur without cause. Underneath her, deep in the bones of the ridge, something was shifting weight. Something vast, patient, held back for centuries by friction and cold, deciding—or being decided—to let go.

She sat bolt upright.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

"Get up. Get up *now* and move away from the edge—we have to get off this shoulder, down onto the glacier, fast—" She was already scrambling, hauling the scholar up by the arm, and behind the words she flung everything she had outward: to the ibex on the crags, the foxes in the talus, the eagle riding the storm-front. *Move. All of you. Off the slopes. Now.* And the answer came back from a hundred minds at once, a chorus of alarm, and underneath it, faint and terrible, the sound—

The sound arrived a heartbeat later with her ears: a crack like the world's spine breaking, then a long, gathering roar.

The avalanche took the entire western face of the ridge. Not a slough, not a slide—an *avalanche*, the whole hanging slope of corniced snow cutting loose at once, and Zephyrine had exactly enough time to seize Odile's collar and throw them both down the lee side of a granite rib before the white wall came over them.

The world became noise. Then pressure. Then nothing that had a name.

* * *

She woke to dark, and cold, and the taste of blood.

Snow had buried her—packed snow, dense as wet wool, pressing her into a pocket of air no bigger than a coffin. For a moment she hung in the blank panic of the buried, the animal terror older than thought—and then her gift woke with her, and she *listened*, and the mountain was full of voices.

The ibex herd had survived, gathered on the crag above. The foxes had gone to ground. And directly above her, digging with the frantic purpose of a creature who owed someone a debt, was a wolverine—one of the few beasts that had ever refused her gifts and liked her anyway.

*Down and left,* the wolverine's mind said, in the blunt image-language of mustelids. *There is air. Dig.*

She dug. Her hands found the seam of lighter snow, and then sky—grey, snowing, magnificent—and she dragged herself out of the tomb and lay gasping in the falling white like a thing born.

"Odile." Her voice came out ruined. "ODILE."

A groan, muffled, ten feet to her right. She dug again, bare-handed, nails tearing, and broke through to find the scholar's face, bloodied at the temple, eyes opening with agonized slowness. Alive. Alive.

"The rib," Odile whispered. "Your stone. You threw us behind—"

"I know these mountains." Zephyrine knelt in the churned snow, shaking now, properly shaking, the delayed terror arriving all at once like a wave hitting a seawall. "That slope had been standing for a hundred years. Ibex grazed on it yesterday—I *asked* them. Cornice snow doesn't cut loose in still air. Something triggered it. Or someone." She looked north, toward the canyon mouth, invisible now behind the curtain of falling snow. "Inquisitors don't carry blasting powder. But the Spire has scholars who know how mountains sleep. And someone knew which shoulder we'd be standing on."

Odile pushed herself up on her elbows, and for the first time since Zephyrine had known her, the archivist looked genuinely afraid—not of capture, but of something larger. "Then it isn't just a hunt anymore," she said softly. "They aren't trying to arrest you. They're trying to make sure you never reach the Spire at all. Someone over there wants the prophecy to complete itself."

The snow fell thicker. Somewhere above the storm, the eagle rode winds it could not see through, and the ibex stood silent on their crags, watching the two small warm shapes in the ruin of the slope with ancient, incurious eyes.

Zephyrine got to her feet. Her caches were buried under a hundred tons of new snow. Her routes were known. Her town was full of faces that could name her, and her name was written in a dead man's book, and the most powerful institution in the world wanted her either crowned or killed—possibly both.

"Three days to the far side," she said hoarsely. "No stops. No fires. We eat while walking and sleep in shifts against the rock." She pulled Odile upright. "And after that, Odile—there's no old life to go back to. Whatever's waiting for me at the end of this, I'm walking toward it with empty hands."

She turned into the storm, and the mountain, grumbling its broken shoulder, let them pass.
