## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The entrance was a crack in the mountain's hip, no wider than a coffin laid on its side, and Zephyrine would have crawled past it a hundred times without ever knowing it was there.

"Here?" Odile peered at the rock face as if it might confess something. Snow had been falling for two days without pause, the kind of dry, whispering snow that buried tracks in minutes and made the world feel like it was being erased on purpose. "There's nothing here."

"There's everything here," Zephyrine said. She knelt and pressed her bare palm flat against the stone, letting the cold bite through her skin until her fingers ached. Then she did what she almost never did with people watching: she listened with the other part of herself, the part she kept folded away like contraband.

The mountain was not silent. It never was. Stone had its own slow voice, deeper than any beast's—a patience so vast it made glaciers seem hasty. And threaded through that patience now came a smaller sound, quick and busy and suspicious: the scrape of many claws, the rustle of fur against packed earth, and beneath it all a hum of voices too low for human ears.

*Someone at the door,* the stone seemed to murmur, passing the message down like a bucket along a chain of hands. *Someone at the door, someone at the door.*

"They know we're here," Zephyrine said, standing and brushing snow from her knees. "They've known since we crossed the scree field. They were arguing about whether to open."

Odile stared at her. Even after two weeks on the road together—two weeks of shared fires and stolen sleep and one avalanche that still gave Zephyrine nightmares—the scholar's face always carried that flicker of wonder when the gift showed itself, quickly smothered under the archivist's habit of cataloguing everything. "Arguing," Odile repeated. "In what language?"

"Theirs." Zephyrine shrugged. "It's not words exactly. It's more like—" She searched for it. "Like how you can tell a dog's mood from the set of its shoulders. Except they're smarter than dogs. Older, too. Much older than us."

She put her mouth close to the crack in the rock and spoke in the low, clicking cadence she'd learned from a blind badger in her fourteenth winter, the sounds awkward in a human throat, shaped wrong but recognizable. *Travelers. Cold. One hurt. We ask shelter under your law.*

Silence. Long enough that Odile shifted her weight and the wind moaned through the pass behind them.

Then the crack breathed warm air into Zephyrine's face—air that smelled of turned earth and woodsmoke and something fungal and sweet—and a voice answered from the dark, in accented but flawless Common:

*"Under our law, then. But the law has teeth, surface-walker. Bring your hurt one and your questions, and leave your knives at the threshold."*

Zephyrine glanced back at Odile. The scholar's hands were shaking—from cold, she'd have said, if anyone asked, though they both knew better. Since Lowmark, since the inquisitors' hounds had bayed through the streets behind them, since the avalanche had come down the pass a heartbeat after they'd cleared it, Odile had been waiting for the next blow to fall with the grim competence of a woman who had already read the ending of her own story.

"No knives," Zephyrine agreed, and unbuckled her belt.

* * *

The tunnel went down farther than she liked.

That was the truth of it, though she'd never say it aloud: Zephyrine Galloway, who could walk a cliff ledge in a whiteout, who had slept three nights in a lynx's den while a blizzard screamed overhead, hated enclosed dark. Hated the way stone pressed close, the way her own breath sounded too loud, the way every instinct screamed *buried alive* even when her mind knew better. She kept her hand on the wall and counted her steps and thought about open sky, and by the time the tunnel opened out she had counted past two thousand.

Then the dark opened like a flower, and she forgot to count anything at all.

The cavern was vast beyond sense—a cathedral hollowed by water and time and patient claws—and it was lit. Not by fire, or not only. Veins of pale gold light ran through the walls and ceiling like rivers of honey caught mid-pour, some lichen or crystal-thing that pulsed faintly, slowly, in rhythms that made Zephyrine think of sleeping breath. Terraces had been cut into the sloping floor in spirals, and on the terraces grew gardens: pale mushrooms in ranks like soldiers, root vegetables with leaves the color of old ivory, grain she didn't recognize bending under its own weight. Water ran in channels beside the paths, chuckling over stones.

And everywhere, everywhere, the deep-folk.

They were mole-like, as the smugglers' stories said, but the stories got everything else wrong. They weren't beasts. They stood upright, most of them, though some moved on all fours with an ease that suggested both postures were equally natural—broad-shouldered, barrel-chested folk barely reaching Zephyrine's ribs, their fur ranging from slate grey to deep umber to, on the eldest, a white gone yellowish as old paper. Their eyes were small and dark and missed nothing. Their forepaws—hands, she corrected herself; those were hands—were enormous, spade-wide, deft. They wore woven garments dyed with root-colors, and they wore them the way scholars wore robes: some plain, some embroidered with patterns that made Odile inhale sharply and reach for her satchel before remembering she had no right to it anymore.

A crowd had gathered along the terraces to look at them. Dozens of dark eyes tracked their descent. Somewhere a child made a sound and was hushed.

"Don't stare," Zephyrine murmured.

"I'm an archivist," Odile whispered back. "I've spent thirty years reading about peoples like this. I have never once seen one." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Let me stare."

An honor guard met them at the bottom—not armed, exactly, though the staves they carried could surely crack a skull—and led them through winding galleries hung with drying herbs and braided roots, past workshops where the tap-tap-tap of chisels rang like distant bells, until they reached a chamber that was clearly a place of judgment or counsel: round, domed, its golden veins converging at the apex like the ribs of an upturned boat. A ring of cushions made of woven cattail sat around a central basin of clear water, and in the largest cushion sat the eldest deep-folk Zephyrine had yet seen.

She was tiny. That was the first thing. Fur gone entirely white, frame shrunken, paws folded in her lap like two resting moles. But her eyes, when they lifted to Zephyrine's face, were the oldest things Zephyrine had ever looked into—older than the Spire, older than the prophecy book, older than the mountains' memory of being mountains.

"I am Mother Under-Snow," she said. Her Common was slow and precise, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "You speak our tongue badly, surface-walker. Who taught you?"

"A badger. Blind. She lived under the Thornfell east road." Zephyrine hesitated, then added, because it felt like a debt owed: "She's dead now. Eleven years. She taught me that shelter is sacred and debts are heavier than iron."

Something moved through the chamber at that—a ripple, a settling, elders exchanging glances. Mother Under-Snow's whiskers twitched, which Zephyrine was beginning to understand was their way of smiling.

"A badger of the east road. We knew her grandmother's grandmother. Good stock." The old mother's gaze slid to Odile, weighed her, dismissed whatever she found, and returned to Zephyrine. "Your friend is Spire-born. We smell the archive dust on her. Your friend may sit and listen, but this audience is for you."

"Why me?" Zephyrine asked, though a cold part of her already knew.

"Because you are the one the mountain has been dreaming about," Mother Under-Snow said simply. "Sit. Drink. We will talk of the last long winter, and you will understand why my people do not sleep well these days."

* * *

The drink was a broth of mushroom and pine-root and something sweeter underneath, and it warmed Zephyrine from the inside out in a way that felt less like food and more like forgiveness. Around the ring, seven other elders had assembled silently while she drank—she hadn't heard a single one enter—and now Mother Under-Snow began to speak, and the chamber's golden light seemed to dim itself in deference.

"One hundred and six years ago," the old mother said, "the winter came early and would not leave. You know this history, perhaps, from your surface books. The Year of White Hunger. Crops failed twice. Rivers froze to their beds. The Spire burned scholars in the squares because burning is warm and blame is easy." Odile flinched. Mother Under-Snow watched her flinch and continued without mercy. "But your books do not say what happened beneath. So I will tell you.

"In that year, a woman came down out of Thornfell. Surface-born, like you. A speaker of beasts—they called such ones wild-speakers then, before the Spire taught you to call them witches. She came seeking the deep-folk because she had heard a rumor: that we keep memories the way you keep grain, against hard seasons. She had found something. A book. A prophecy. And she believed—she *knew*, the way your kind knows things, suddenly and without proof—that the winter was not weather."

Zephyrine's hands had gone still around the bowl. The broth's warmth sat in her stomach like a swallowed coal.

"What was it, then?" Odile asked quietly. It was the question Zephyrine couldn't make her mouth form.

"We did not know," Mother Under-Snow said. "We know more now. My grandmother sat where I sit and heard the same story I am telling you, and my grandmother's grandmother heard the story of what came after. Listen.

"The woman's name was Sera Vane. Remember it, surface-walker, because no one else does. The Spire erased her. The villages forgot her. Only we kept the name, because keeping names is our oldest work. Sera Vane came to us in the second year of the white hunger, half-starved, followed by animals—always followed by animals, deer and foxes and birds that should have flown south—and she told us the winter had a heartbeat. She had heard it through the feet of the mountain. Slow. Patient. *Growing.*"

The golden light pulsed once, and Zephyrine could not tell whether it was the lichen or her own pulse she felt in her throat.

"She said the winter was not a punishment," Mother Under-Snow went on. "Not a curse, not a god's anger. She said it was a *womb*. Something ancient had been wounded long ago—or starved, or buried, the stories disagree—and it had learned that if it could not live in the warm world, it would make the warm world cold enough to bear it. The long winter was it breathing. Slowing the world down. Making ready." The old mother's small dark eyes fixed on Zephyrine. "Does this sound familiar to you? Think carefully before you answer. Think of your frosts. Think of what month it is, and what the frost is doing."

Zephyrine thought of the almanac dates, the early killing frosts, the snow that had begun in what should have been high autumn. Thought of Odile's white face in the ruined waystation, saying *the solstice, it has to be the solstice*. Thought of the ibex herds fleeing downslope, of forests going quiet, of the silence under the snow that wasn't emptiness but attention.

"Yes," she said. Her voice came out rough. "It sounds familiar."

"Sera Vane believed she could stop it," Mother Under-Snow said. "The prophecy named her—it named a winter-born speaker, born under the first frost of a false spring, able to hear what grows beneath. She was proud. She was brave. She loved the world loudly, the way young speakers do, before the world teaches them to love it quietly." A pause. The elders around the ring had bowed their heads slightly, and Zephyrine realized they were mourning. A century late, and still mourning. "She went north, toward the source. Toward the seat of kings, though in those days the crown sat on a different head. She took our blessing and a rope of knotted root-cord, and she walked into the snow, and the snow closed behind her."

"And?" Zephyrine asked. Knowing. Dreading.

"And the winter broke," Mother Under-Snow said. "Three days later. The thaw came sudden and violent, floods, slides, whole valleys rearranged. The surface-world called it a miracle. The Spire took credit. And we—" the old mother's paws tightened on each other in her lap, "—we waited for Sera Vane to come home.

"She came home in the spring. Walking. Alive. Smiling." Each word fell like a stone into a well, and Zephyrine heard the splash of each one. "But she was not Sera Vane. She remembered her mother's face and could not recall her mother's voice. She remembered roads she had walked and could not remember why she had walked them. She would stand in a field and animals would gather to her from miles around, and she would look at them with polite, puzzled kindness, like a queen reviewing troops whose names she'd forgotten. Whatever she had done to break the winter, she had paid for it with the very thing that made her herself. The speaker was gone. What remained was a shape that smiled."

The chamber was very quiet. The water in the central basin made the only sound, a small persistent lapping, like something trying to get a word in.

"What happened to her?" Odile whispered.

"She lived," Mother Under-Snow said. "That was the cruelest part. She lived forty years. My grandmother's grandmother cared for her—this chamber was built for her, did you know? These lights, this ring. A court for a woman who could no longer remember why she needed one. She was gentle. She was grateful. She learned to garden, because her hands remembered growing things even when her mind did not. And every year on the first frost she would walk out into the snow and stand there until we brought her in, and she would say—" the old mother's voice frayed, just slightly, the first crack in all that stone, "—she would say, *I have lost something, and I cannot even grieve it properly, because grief needs a name, and I have none.*"

Zephyrine set down her bowl. Her hands were not steady. She looked at them—her smuggler's hands, scarred, capable, hers—and felt the vertigo rise again, the same sick lurch she'd felt in the waystation when she'd seen her own true name written in a dead man's ash-margin. Because she understood now. She understood what Odile had been circling for days without saying, what the prophecy's cold geometry implied, what the mountain had been dreaming.

The winter-born speaker. Born under the first frost of a false spring. Able to hear what grew beneath.

Her.

And ahead of her lay a choice that was not a choice at all: flee, and let the long winter complete its fall over every village she'd ever fed, every valley she'd ever walked—or go to the Spire, and unbind the king, and pay whatever price the paying required. Perhaps the price Sera Vane had paid. Perhaps worse.

"You are telling me this," Zephyrine said slowly, "as a warning."

"I am telling you this," Mother Under-Snow said, "because you deserve to know what you are walking toward. The Spire will not warn you. The prophecy will not warn you. Prophecies are like rivers—they only show you the surface, and the drowning happens underneath." She leaned forward, and the golden light caught the wet gleam in her small dark eyes. "But hear also this, surface-walker, because it matters. Sera Vane failed. Do you understand? The winter broke, yes—but the thing beneath was not killed, not unmade, not driven out. It went back to sleep. It rooted deeper. It waited. And a hundred years later, here you sit, and the frost comes early again, and the mountain dreams of you. Whatever Sera Vane bought with her self, she bought only time. Time is running out."

One of the other elders stirred—an old male, his fur gone the color of iron, his paws gnarled as tree roots. He spoke in their own tongue first, a short phrase, and Mother Under-Snow translated as if the words needed handling carefully:

"He asks: does the surface-woman carry the book? The ash-book, the dead scholar's book?"

Zephyrine's eyes cut to Odile. Odile's hand had gone to her satchel, knuckles white.

"It's in the satchel," Zephyrine said. "Why?"

"Because," Mother Under-Snow said, and now her voice dropped, and every elder in the ring leaned in, and the golden light dimmed further as though the chamber itself were leaning in, "Sera Vane left something behind when she died. A record. She wrote it in the last years, when her mind was failing—fragments, mostly, things that surfaced like fish in a pond. And in her fragments, she wrote one sentence over and over, dozens of times, in a shaking hand, as if she were trying to shout it across a hundred years to whoever came next."

The old mother closed her eyes.

"She wrote: *I unbound the king, but I did not unbind myself first. That was the error. That was the whole of the error. Unbind yourself first, and the rest follows. Unbind yourself first.*"

Silence. The basin water lapped. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the wind screamed over the pass, and the snow fell, and fell, and fell.

Zephyrine became aware that Odile was looking at her. Not at the elders, not at the lights, not at the wonders of a living city beneath the mountain—at her, with an expression Zephyrine had never seen on the scholar's careful face before: fear and dawning comprehension tangled together, like roots grown through a wall.

"Zephyrine," Odile said quietly. "What did the marginalia in the book say? The parts you wouldn't translate for me?"

Zephyrine opened her mouth.

And from the tunnels above, faint but unmistakable, rolling down through the stone like thunder through a floor, came a sound that stopped every heart in the chamber: the deep-folk's alarm-call, passed claw to claw, terrace to terrace, growing louder—

*Surface-walkers. Many. With fire and iron. Descending.*
