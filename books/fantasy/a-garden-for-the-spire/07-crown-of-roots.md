## 7. Crown of Roots

The deep-folk did not light candles. They farmed a pale fungus that gave off a cold green glow, and by that light Odile Vasse sat cross-legged on a woven mat of root-fiber and tried not to think about the archive.

She failed, as she failed every night.

It was always the same hour when it came for her—not the entity, but the memory of the archive: the smell of vellum and lamp-oil, the hush of ten thousand shelves stacked into the Spire's bones like marrow in bone. She had spent nineteen years in that hush. She had been happy there, or something close enough to happiness that she had never thought to question it. And then she had opened a sealed folio she was not cleared to open, and the hush had ended, and here she was: a fugitive in a glowing burrow beneath the Thornfells, listening to mole-people breathe in their sleep while a smuggler dozed against the far wall with her hand curled around a knife even in dreams.

Zephyrine Galloway slept the way Odile imagined wolves slept—lightly, economically, one ear always turned toward the dark.

Odile opened the satchel at her knees and took out the prophecy book. The dead scholar's cache had yielded other things—letters, marginalia, a lock of hair pressed between pages like a flower—but the book was the heart of it, and the heart was what she needed now. Because the elder, Brammel, had said *the last one failed*, and Odile could not stop turning that word over. *Failed.* It implied an attempt. Attempts left records. Records were her country, her native tongue, and if she was going to walk a beast-speaker into the Spire's jaws, she intended to know exactly how sharp the teeth were.

She unrolled the book across her lap and began, again, at the beginning.

* * *

Eleven years ago. The Archive of the Verdant Court, third tier, restricted stacks.

Odile had been twenty-four and hungry in the way young archivists were hungry—for citation, for discovery, for the small immortal fame of a footnote in someone else's great work. She had been cataloguing the private papers of Archivist Corvane, dead forty years, whose collection had come to the Spire in crates still smelling of his pipe smoke. Most of it was tedium: correspondence about ink supplies, complaints about damp, a long bitter feud with a colleague over shelving conventions. But crate nine held a folio bound in green leather gone black with age, and the seal on it was not the Spire's grey tower.

It was a crown made of roots.

She should have reported it. Sealed material went to the First Curator; the First Curator decided; the archivist forgot. That was the whole architecture of her life, and it had fit her like a well-made glove. But the folio was already open in her hands—the wax cracked along its seam, old damage—and the first page read:

*Record of the King's Sleep. Kept by order of Her Majesty's regency council, in the year the king stopped dreaming his own dreams.*

Aldric had been crowned at nine years old. Everyone knew the story: the boy-king, orphaned by the fever-winters, crowned too young, guided by a regency council until his majority. What the histories did not dwell on—what they smoothed over like river stones—was the year of his illness. Age eleven. A wasting sickness, the chronicles called it. He had gone into the palace orchards alone one autumn afternoon and been found at dusk, unconscious among the apple trees, with dirt under his fingernails and roots—fine white filaments, the physician's notes said, *like thread*—clinging to his hands.

He woke after seven days. He was cheerful. He was charming. He asked, with a child's directness, why the granaries were ever allowed to stand empty, when the earth was so willing.

The folio was the regency council's secret record of what came after.

*The king eats little and grows. The king sleeps four hours and wakes rested. The king has ordered the south orchards tripled, though the treasury groans. The king stood in the courtyard during the first frost without shivering and laughed when the chamberlain begged him to come inside.*

*The king no longer weeps.*

That last line had been underlined twice, in two different hands, decades apart. Odile had sat in the restricted stacks with the lamp turned low and felt the sentence go into her like a splinter. *The king no longer weeps.* She had been twenty-four and she had thought: grief is a tax on love, and this child has stopped paying it.

She had not yet learned to be afraid of things that sounded like mercy.

* * *

"Your candle's dying."

Zephyrine's voice, low, from across the chamber. Odile hadn't heard her wake. Of course she hadn't.

"It's fungus," Odile said. "It doesn't die so much as sulk."

The smuggler rose without sound and came over, folding herself down onto the mat beside her with the ease of someone who had grown up sleeping on stone. Up close, in the cold green light, Zephyrine looked younger than she pretended to be and older than she probably was, both at once, the way people did who had spent their lives being nobody.

"You're reading it again," Zephyrine said. "You've read it six times."

"Seven."

"And?"

Odile looked down at the page open beneath her thumb. *And the king said, let them plant through the winter. The earth does not rest, so neither shall we.* "And I'm trying to find where it went wrong," she said. "Or where it went right, depending on which side of this you're standing on."

"There's a side where this goes right?" Zephyrine's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Go on, then. Read me the bedtime story. I'll tell you if it scares me."

So Odile read.

* * *

The folio covered nine years. Nine years of a regency council watching a boy become something else, recorded in the flat, careful language of officials who had decided that whatever was happening was preferable to a succession crisis.

The entries changed hands three times as archivists died or retired, but the voice stayed eerily consistent, because the subject imposed itself on every sentence. Aldric at thirteen, ordering terraces cut into the palace hillside, working alongside the gardeners with his sleeves rolled up, laughing, beloved. Aldric at fourteen, dissolving the Council of Granaries because *the king provides*. Aldric at fifteen, when the first true anomaly appeared: the orchards bloomed in midwinter. Not forced, not sheltered—every tree on the palace grounds flowering at once under a sky the color of dirty wool, fruit ripening in six weeks, sweeter than any variety on record.

The city had called it a miracle. There had been festivals. Odile remembered learning about them as a schoolgirl—the Winter Bloom, the Year of Sweet Apples—in a lesson illustrated with cheerful woodcuts. No woodcut showed what the folio showed: the physician's inventory of what they'd found in the king's bedchamber afterward. Soil on the floorboards. Fine white roots threaded through the mattress straw. And in the hearth, where a fire should have been, a small living sapling, growing out of the ash.

*His Majesty was displeased that we removed it,* the entry noted. *He replanted it himself. He has named it.*

The name was a line of characters no heraldic alphabet recognized. When Odile had brought the transcription to the linguists' division, an old man had gone grey looking at it and told her it resembled nothing so much as the root-script of the deep-folk, last used a century before, in the records of the previous long winter.

She had reported that conversation. She had followed procedure. The report went up, and up, and came back down stamped *noted* by someone whose signature she never saw, and the folio went back into crate nine, and she had told herself the system worked, the system was sound, there were people above her whose whole task was to worry so that she didn't have to.

Two years later, the same signature had signed the order expelling her from the Spire with her name struck from the rolls.

* * *

"He was a child," Zephyrine said quietly, when Odile reached the entry about the sapling. She had pulled her knees up, arms wrapped around them, and her eyes had gone somewhere else—to the mountains, maybe, or to some memory of her own childhood that Odile would never be told. "Eleven. Whatever got into him got into a child."

"Yes."

"And the council just—watched? Wrote it all down and watched?"

"They convinced themselves it was benign." Odile turned a page. The green glow made the old ink look wet. "That was the genius of it. I understand that now. It never once did anything that looked like evil. Every act was a gift. Full granaries. Fruit out of season. A king the people adored. If you'd asked any citizen of the Spire in those years whether anything was wrong, they'd have laughed at you. The wrongness was only visible from inside the archive, in the margins, in the gaps." She tapped the page. "Here. Year five. The council asks him about his mother—he'd been close to her, before the fevers took her. He can't remember her face. He laughs about it. *Mother is the earth now,* he says, *and the earth remembers for me.* He was sixteen. He'd lost everything that hurt to carry, and everyone around him called it healing."

Zephyrine was silent a long moment. Somewhere deeper in the warren, water dripped with the patience of stone.

"My gift," she said at last, not looking at Odile. "People hear the word *beast-speaker* and they think it's a trick. Whistles, signals, trained animals. It isn't. When I speak with something, I spend something. A piece of attention. A piece of—self, I suppose. Talk to a lynx for an hour and you come back an hour less human. I've always kept the accounts balanced. Small conversations. Debts paid quick." Her jaw tightened. "What you're describing sounds like a conversation that never ended. Something talking to a child for years, taking a piece every time, giving back apples."

Odile stared at her. In nineteen years of scholarship, no one had ever described the Verdant King's method so precisely, and certainly no one had done it in under fifty words, sitting in a hole in the ground.

"That," Odile said, "is exactly what happened."

"Mm." Zephyrine unfolded, restless, and paced to the edge of the chamber where the tunnel mouth breathed cold air down from the world above. "Then say the rest of it. You've been circling something since Brammel said the word *failed*. I've watched you circle it all night. Say it."

* * *

There was one more document. Odile had carried it out of the Spire sewn into the lining of her coat, the single theft of her career, the crime that mattered less than the heresy of having noticed. It was not part of the folio. It was older—a fragment, half-burned, recovered from the purge of the archives a century ago, when the Spire had burned its own prophetic collections and called it reform.

It was a testimony. Signed by a woman whose title translated, roughly, as *root-listener*—one of the deep-folk's surface emissaries, from the era of the last long winter. The deep-folk had sent her to the human court of that time to warn them, and the fragment was what survived of her warning.

Odile had read it perhaps two hundred times. She knew it the way priests know liturgy.

*I went up to the bright court and I told them: the winter is not a punishment. Punishment ends. I told them: look at what the green thing does. It gives, and gives, and gives, and under every gift a root takes hold. Your king will not be eaten. Eating is fast and eating leaves bones. He will be inhabited, slowly, the way a tree inhabits a fence, until no one can say where the fence ends. And the winter above—oh, my bright fools, the winter is not his anger. The winter is his seed-case. Cold is how the patient things keep. Everything that sleeps in the ground sleeps under snow.*

The fragment broke off there, burned away, and history recorded only that the last long winter had lasted eleven years and ended abruptly, and that the deep-folk had withdrawn underground for a generation, and that the Spire had burned its prophetic archives and founded its inquisition to make sure no one ever spoke of such things again.

Sitting in the green-lit dark with Zephyrine waiting for her to speak, Odile finally assembled the whole of it aloud, and heard, as she spoke, how obvious it had always been.

"The entity doesn't cause the winter," she said. "Everyone assumes the winter is its weapon—punishment, siege, pressure. Even the prophecy frames it as a threat. But the root-listener understood. The winter is *incubation*. The thing in Aldric has spent thirty years rooting through the foundations of the Spire, through the soil of the whole valley—we've seen it, Zephyrine, the orchards blooming in blizzard, that greenery is fed by something underneath—and now it's ready to fruit. To propagate. To put out spores, seeds, whatever it is that it makes. And cold keeps seed. The long winter isn't the kingdom dying." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The long winter is the kingdom being *kept fresh*. Until the solstice. Until the thing wearing his crown finishes ripening, and then—" 

She stopped, because the next part required a word, and the only words available were plague-words, harvest-words, and none of them were large enough.

Zephyrine had gone very still at the tunnel mouth. When she turned, her face in the fungous light was stripped of all its smuggler's irony, all its competent solitude, down to something young and appalled.

"The snow," she said. "Three weeks ago, on the pass. The ibex wouldn't cross the high snowfields. The elk moved downhill weeks early. I thought it was the weather." Her hand had found her knife again without her seeming to notice. "They weren't fleeing the cold. Beasts don't flee cold. They were fleeing what's under it."

"Moving toward the Spire," Odile said. "If your animals are any measure. Drawn to the root of the thing, the way roots draw water."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Above them, through a hundred feet of frozen earth, the storm that had driven them underground went on hurling itself against the mountain, and Odile thought: *it isn't weather anymore. It's a lid. We are sitting inside the seed-case with the seed.*

"We leave tomorrow," Zephyrine said. "Whatever the storm's doing. Before the snow gets ideas about staying."

But Odile was already looking back down at the book, at the circled date and the birth-name written beside it in a dead scholar's careful hand, and the horror in her chest had shifted shape—had cooled, and settled, and gone terribly patient, like something planting itself.

Because if the winter was incubation, then the prophecy's winter-born speaker was not a counter to the entity at all.

She was part of the cycle. Born under the seed-frost, named in the root-book, hunted by the Spire since before her birth. Either the anchor that completed the binding—or the one being kept, all these years, like a seed in cold storage.

And somewhere beneath the snow, Odile realized, something had begun to move toward the Spire to collect what it had planted.
