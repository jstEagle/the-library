## 8. Every Road Is Watched

The trouble with trusting people, Zephyrine had learned early, was that it required you to stop watching them.

She had broken her own rule for Corvin Hale. Three winters of shared fires and split profits, of him carrying her salt down the Thornfell switchbacks while she carried his lies up — no, that wasn't fair, she corrected herself, crouched in the shadow of a granite overhang with snow sifting through the gap above her head. He had been honest for three winters. That was the wound of it. Betrayal by a stranger was weather; betrayal by a friend was a knife you'd sharpened yourself.

Odile crouched beside her, breath ragged, one hand pressed flat against the stone as though the mountain might confirm her innocence by touch. The scholar had not spoken since the waystation at Cold Sarrow, since Zephyrine had walked in from checking the trapline and found Corvin standing over their packs with a Spire signal-lantern unshuttered in his fist, its green light climbing the cliff face like ivy.

"You knew," Odile whispered now. "You knew before we ran."

"I saw the lantern." Zephyrine kept her voice flat, low, the voice she used on frightened animals. "I didn't know anything else."

"Then how did you know which way to run?"

Because the ibex had told her. Because two days ago, on the ridge above Lowmark, a herd of thirty had stood staring south with their amber eyes rolled white, and when she'd asked them *what*, the answer that came back through the old blood-language was not a word but a direction: *away, away, away*. She said none of this. Some truths were too heavy to carry uphill.

Above them, faint through the snow-hush, came the belling of hounds. Not true hounds — Spire hounds were bred with something colder than dog in them, something that hunted by gift-scent rather than trail-scent, and they would find her eventually unless she did what she had sworn since childhood never to do.

She was going to take them into the high passes. Above the tree line. Where nothing human survived without the beasts' leave.

* * *

They climbed through the grey afternoon, and the mountain changed around them the way a face changes when it stops pretending to be kind.

The lower passes of Thornfell were hers — mapped in her bones, every cache and chimney and goat-track known to her the way other women knew their mothers' kitchens. But the high country was different country. It belonged to the wind and the things that could sleep standing up in a blizzard. Snow lay in drifts against the rock like linen on corpses. The light came down thin and blue, without warmth, without opinion. When Zephyrine looked back the way they'd come, the world below had already been erased: valley, road, the smoke of Lowmark's chimneys, all of it gone under a white so complete it seemed less like weather than like forgetting.

Good, she thought, and then hated herself for thinking it, because Lowmark was under that white too, and the people she'd fed salt all these years, and Odile's little house with its false name painted over the door.

"The passes are watched," Odile said, for the fourth time. She climbed badly, palms scraped raw, her scholar's body built for archives and not for scree. But she climbed. Zephyrine gave her that. "Every road out of Thornfell has an inquisitor's post by now. Corvin will have told them your routes. Your caches."

"My caches are empty or moved. My routes are wrong by half a day. And the posts—" Zephyrine pulled herself onto a ledge and reached back for Odile's wrist, hauling her up with more force than gentleness. "The posts watch roads. We're not on a road anymore."

"What are we on?"

"Nothing. That's the point."

It was nearly true. What they were on was a seam of weakness in the mountain's shoulder that she'd found years ago while tracking a wounded chough, a route with no name because no one had ever needed to name it — a route only passable if the mountain's four-legged residents consented, since it crossed three ledges where a fall meant a fall, and the ibex who grazed those ledges did not suffer fools or climbers. She would have to ask them. She would have to spend gift like coin, openly, wastefully, in a week when being seen spending it could kill them both.

But the hounds were belling closer, and choice is a luxury of the unhunted.

At dusk they reached the first ledge, a shelf of wind-scoured stone wide enough for goats and nerve alone, and there they were: eleven ibex, standing in the blue snow-light with their curved horns etched against the sky like the ridgeline itself had grown opinions.

Zephyrine set down her pack, knelt, and opened herself.

The gift was not words, exactly. It was older than words — a pressure behind the eyes, a loosening of the border between her skin and the cold air, and then meaning arriving the way cold arrives, without language, all at once. To the ibex she sent the simplest shape she could make: *two-legs, small, harmless, passage.*

What came back stopped her breath.

Not refusal. Not the grudging tolerance she'd expected. Fear — a fear so vast and formless that it arrived less as an emotion than as a landscape, a whole country of terror with no borders she could map. The herd's minds pressed against hers and what she felt in them was the same thing she'd felt on the ridge above Lowmark, but magnified now, close now, unbearable: *away, away, away,* and beneath it, threaded through every mind like a dark vein through marble, an image.

Something moving beneath the snow.

She gasped and the connection snapped. She was on her hands and knees on the frozen ledge, nose almost touching the stone, and Odile was gripping her shoulder saying her name — her false name, the safe one, *Maren, Maren* — and the ibex had not moved, had not fled, were standing exactly where they'd been, watching her with eyes like struck amber.

"They're afraid," Zephyrine managed. Her mouth tasted of iron and pine. "Gods, Odile, they're so afraid."

"Afraid of us?"

"No." She pushed herself upright. Her hands were shaking and she made fists of them. "Of something under the snow. Something moving. They've been running from it for weeks — that's why the herds came down early, why the wolves left the eastern valleys, why I couldn't find a single bear denned where they always den. Everything's been moving west and downhill all season and I thought it was the winter coming early." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It's not the winter. Or it's not only the winter. Something is coming up out of the deep country, moving under the snowpack, and everything alive can feel it except us."

Odile had gone very still. In the blue dusk her face was the color of old paper. "Moving which way?"

And Zephyrine, who already knew, who had known since the ridge and had refused to know, made herself send the question again — gently, gently, a palm held flat to a frightened animal — and let the answer rise through her.

South. Toward the Spire.

* * *

The ibex let them pass. That was the bargain, such as it was: passage across three ledges in exchange for nothing Zephyrine could name, because the herd asked nothing. They simply parted, eleven animals stepping aside along the shelf with a courtesy that undid her more than hostility would have, and as she crossed between them the eldest ewe touched her horn once, lightly, to Zephyrine's shoulder — a gesture she had no translation for, and suspected she was better off without.

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, no fire, sharing a block of salt-pork and half a skin of melted snow. The wind spoke continuously overhead in a language of long vowels. Somewhere far below and behind, very faint, the hounds were still belling — or perhaps Zephyrine imagined it, or perhaps it was only the wind finding a shape her fear recognized.

"I keep thinking about Corvin," Odile said into the dark.

"Don't."

"He carried my books down from Cold Sarrow. He complained the entire way, he complained about the weight and the cold and my pace, and then at the waystation he lit a green lantern over our sleeping bodies." Odile's voice was steady in the way that Zephyrine had learned meant the opposite. "I keep trying to find the moment it turned. There must be a moment. People don't just—"

"They do." Zephyrine chewed the last of the pork and swallowed it dry. "The Spire doesn't threaten people, mostly. It offers. A debt forgiven. A brother released from the conscript gangs. A shopkeeper's license that keeps getting denied, suddenly granted. You don't betray someone because you're wicked. You betray them because they've arranged it so that not betraying costs more than you have." She stared out at the snow, at the enormous pale silence of it. "I've spent my whole life making sure I cost more to buy than anyone could pay. Apparently I got the price wrong."

"And what did he get?"

"Us," Zephyrine said. "Alive, delivered. And me named. A beast-speaker born under the winter sign, found in the flesh after a hundred years of looking." She heard Odile's breath catch. "That's not a purse of coin, Odile. That's a title. Lands. Maybe a pardon for whatever sin got him smuggling in the first place. I'd have sold me for less."

Silence, then, except the wind. Zephyrine wrapped her cloak tighter and tried to sleep and could not, and after a while she realized Odile wasn't sleeping either, and that neither of them was going to say anything else, and that this — two liars keeping warm-side-by-side in a hole in a mountain, trusted by no one, trusted only by each other and by eleven ibex on a ledge above — this was what her life had become. The floor of it had given way in a ruined waystation ten days ago, and she was still falling, and the only ground left was the ground she chose to stand on.

She slept anyway. Survival was a habit deeper than fear.

* * *

They found the dying herd two days later, in a hanging valley so high that the air itself seemed rationed.

There had been forty of them once — she could read the story in the snow, the churned and crowded tracks, the broad sweep of a herd moving fast and together. Now there were nine, standing in a loose crescent around a fallen bull, and the snow around them was red in patches, and none of them had grazed, and none of them had lain down, and every one of them was facing south.

Zephyrine knelt in the snow before she had decided to kneel.

The grief came through first, before any words, before any shapes: a grief like a glacier calving, slow and enormous and irreversible. The bull was dying of nothing a predator had done — no wounds, no sickness she could smell. He was dying of the journey. Of weeks of flight through country stripped bare, of grazing on lichen and bark and finally on nothing, of a fear so sustained it had burned through his body like a fever. The others had stayed with him. That was the part that caught in her throat like a fishbone. They should have run. Everything in their nature said *run.* They had stayed for a dying bull in a valley where staying was death, because leaving him alone was unthinkable even to the thinking of animals.

*I'm here,* she sent, as gently as she had ever sent anything. *I see him. I see you.*

The herd's attention turned to her, nine pairs of amber eyes, and what poured into her then was not grief anymore but testimony. They wanted her to know. That was the terrible, humbling thing — they had been trying to tell *someone* for weeks, and the mountains were full of humans who could not hear, and here at last was one who could, and they emptied themselves into her like a river through a broken dam.

*Under the snow,* they showed her. *Under the snow, under the roots, under the deep country where the ground never thaws. It moves and the snow does not break above it. It moves and the trees lean away from it as it passes, leaning like grass leans, and then they straighten and forget. It moves south. It moves toward the pale stone. It has been moving since the first frost and it does not rest and it does not eat and it is not hungry, it is only patient, it is patience walking, wearing the winter like a coat it grew itself—*

Zephyrine cried out. The vision broke apart and she was on her side in the snow, cheek against the cold, and the sky wheeled above her, blue and pitiless, and somewhere near her Odile was shouting her name and the ibex were standing motionless around the dead bull, still facing south, still facing south.

When she could speak, she said: "It's going to the Spire."

Odile crouched beside her, snow in her grey hair, her eyes wide and wet. "The entity. The Verdant King. It's — leaving him? Leaving Aldric?"

"No." Zephyrine sat up slowly. Every joint ached as though she'd carried the herd's journey in her own body, and maybe she had; maybe that was the price of listening. "No, listen. The ibex don't think in parts. Whatever moves under the snow, whatever's been hollowing the winter out — it's all one thing. The thing in the king. The thing in the winter. The roots in the foundations and the frost on the passes. One body, Odile. One body with the crown at the top of it and the deep country at the bottom, and the whole mountain between." She looked south, down the long white throat of the valley, toward the horizon where the Spire waited inside its unnatural calm. "It's not fleeing him. It's gathering. Everything it planted, everything it rooted, all of it pulling south like sap in spring. Except it isn't spring. It's the opposite of spring."

"An incubation," Odile whispered. "As I thought. The long winter isn't its rage. It's its gestation." She pressed both hands over her mouth, and when she took them away her voice had gone archivist-flat, the tone she used for citations, for terrible facts that needed to be handled with tongs. "The prophecy says the winter-born speaker arrives at the solstice. The solstice is nine days away. If the entity completes its gathering before then—"

"Then we walk faster."

"With what legs?" Odile gestured at the valley, the vertical miles of it, the sheer improbability of two soft-bodied creatures crossing it ahead of an ancient patience. "Zephyrine. Look at them."

Zephyrine looked. Nine ibex, starving, loyal to a dead bull, facing south into the teeth of their own extinction.

"They've been running for weeks," Odile said softly. "Everything wild has been running. Away, away, away — you said that's all they could tell you. And now you want to walk the other direction. Into the current. With every living thing in Thornfell swimming the other way." She shook her head slowly. "The wild is afraid, Zephyrine. All of it. And we're about to ask it to carry us toward the thing it fears."

Zephyrine rose. Her knees cracked. Below her, the eldest ewe lifted her head from the dead bull's flank and regarded her across twenty feet of trampled snow, and Zephyrine opened herself one last time — carefully, a crack in a door, ready to close it — and asked the only question left.

*Will you help us?*

The answer came back not as fear and not as hope but as something older than either: the plain arithmetic of creatures who had run until running killed them, and understood now that there was no *away* left anywhere in the world. The ewe's mind touched hers, brief and dry as lichen, and in it Zephyrine felt a path — a high line, impossible, above the icefalls, a line no human had ever crossed because no human had ever been invited — and felt, beneath it, the ewe's final understanding, offered without bitterness:

*There is nowhere left to flee. So we will go where we are going. Come with us or do not.*

Zephyrine turned to Odile. The wind had risen again, singing its long vowels off the icefalls, and somewhere beneath the snow — she could almost feel it now, a pressure in her back teeth, a wrongness in the way the drifts lay — something patient was moving south.

"Get your pack," she said. "We're going with the ibex."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. Then, with the expression of a woman signing her own death warrant in a fine clear hand, she bent and shouldered her bag.

Behind them, the nine survivors of the herd began, at last, to move — not away, not downhill, not toward any shelter any animal had ever chosen — but south, up the high white spine of the world, toward the pale stone and the crown and the thing that waited beneath the snow, growing.
