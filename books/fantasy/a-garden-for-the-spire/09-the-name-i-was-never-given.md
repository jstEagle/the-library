## 9. The Name I Was Never Given

The candle had burned down to a knuckle of wax by the time Odile Vasse understood what she was reading, and she did not move to light another. She sat in the blue dark of the abandoned shepherd's hut with the prophecy book open across her knees, her breath fogging in the cold, and let the truth settle over her the way snow settles over a roof—silently, weightlessly at first, until the timbers begin to groan.

They had taken shelter here two days ago, high on the shoulder of the pass where even smugglers' routes grew thin. Zephyrine was out now, somewhere on the mountainside with the lynx, checking a cache she'd sworn she'd abandoned and hadn't—Odile had seen it in her face when she said it, the way liars look slightly past you—and Odile had promised herself an hour with the book. An hour had become a day. A day had become this: the marginalia, the cramped brown script in the margins of the *Canticle of Roots*, written by some archivist's hand three generations dead, and beneath it, in fresher ink that still smelled faintly of iron, a single line in Odile's own predecessor's hand:

*Not found. Made.*

She had read the phrase forty times. She had translated it out of the old archive-cipher twice, once quickly and once slowly, the way one touches a burn twice to confirm it hurts. Both times it said the same thing.

*Not found. Made.*

In the Spire, where Odile had spent twenty years of her life among the sealed stacks, there was a doctrine taught to every novice archivist before they were permitted to touch a prophetic text: *prophecy does not describe the future; it describes the shape the future is being bent into.* She had repeated it a thousand times to students whose eyes glazed over. She had never once believed it would apply to her. Doctrine was for lectures. Doctrine was for other people's catastrophes.

She turned back to the beginning of the marginalia and began again from the first entry, because if she was going to be destroyed by a sentence, she intended to at least understand its grammar.

* * *

The hand that wrote the earliest notes belonged, she was nearly certain, to Archivist Serafim Ondrey, who had served the Spire a hundred years ago, during the last long winter—the winter the deep-folk remembered, the winter the previous speaker had failed to end. History recorded that Ondrey had died quietly in his quarters, honored and obscure. The Spire's records were very good at recording quiet deaths. They were less good at recording what people did in the years before their deaths became convenient.

Ondrey's cipher was archaic but not difficult; Odile had broken harder codes before breakfast as a girl, for the pleasure of it, sitting in her father's chandlery while tallow dripped and hardened into shapes like small pale animals. She worked through the night with numb fingers, and what emerged was not scholarship. It was a confession.

*We believed we were studying it,* Ondrey wrote, in the entry dated to the third month of the last long winter. *We were wrong. We were midwives.*

Odile's stomach tightened. She read on.

The Verdant King—the entity, the thing in the roots, the name the deep-folk used with their hands pressed flat against the earth as though to hold it down—had come to Thornfell a century ago the way such things always came: as hunger wearing the mask of generosity. It had offered the mountain villages endless harvests. It had asked so little in return. And when the last long winter fell—not as punishment, Odile now understood, but as incubation, as the slow patient warming of soil before a planting—the Spire's archivists had done what archivists do. They studied. They catalogued. They wrote reports in cipher that no one in power read, because no one in power reads anything that costs them comfort.

But Ondrey had gone further than his colleagues. Ondrey had gone down into the root-chambers beneath the old shrine at Hollowmere, before the Spire sealed them, and he had seen what the entity left behind when it withdrew to wait. Not nothing. Not absence. A seed.

*It cannot take flesh unaided,* he wrote. *Flesh resists it. Flesh has a will of its own, and the King's crown is not enough—it needs a door that opens from the inside. So it makes doors. It sows itself into a bloodline the way a farmer sows barley, thin and patient, generation after generation, each child carrying a little more of it than the parent, until the harvest-child comes along in whom the thing can finally stand upright and call itself born.*

Odile set the book down on her knee and looked at the fireless hearth for a long time.

*Harvest-child.* The term appeared four more times in Ondrey's notes, always in cipher, always with the same careful horror around it, the horror of a man writing something he hoped no one would ever need to read. The winter-born speaker. The one the prophecies spoke of in language so ambiguous that a century of scholars had argued about whether she would save the king or destroy him. *Unbinding or completion,* the texts said. *The door swings both ways.*

Everyone had assumed—Odile herself had assumed, standing in Lowmark's smoky kitchen not two weeks ago, telling Zephyrine *you matter, you're the one the prophecy names*—that the winter-born speaker was a person the entity had marked. A target. A chosen vessel, found by fate or by the entity's searching attention, the way a hawk finds a hare.

That was the merciful reading. That was the reading that let you believe Zephyrine Galloway was a woman who happened to be in the wrong prophecy.

Ondrey's marginalia said otherwise. Ondrey had watched the Spire's own records, cross-checked against parish rolls and bloodline registries, and traced the seed's descent through five generations of mountain families—families that scattered, intermarried, thinned, forgot. The line had nearly failed twice. Once during a fever year; once, chillingly, during a purge that the Spire itself had ordered, hunting beast-speakers, burning the gift wherever it surfaced—because the gift and the seed traveled together, you see, the entity's fragment expressed itself in the carrier as the ability to speak with beasts, the way a splinter works its way toward the skin—

Odile stopped reading. Her hands were shaking.

The purges. The pyres. A century of the Spire burning beast-speakers, and every inquisitor who lit a torch believed he was cleansing the land of corruption, and every single one of them had been doing exactly what the entity needed. Because a hunted gift goes underground. A hunted bloodline hides, scatters, marries quietly into other hunted lines, concentrates itself like frost concentrating at the edge of a windowpane. Fear was the entity's plow. The Spire had been plowing for a hundred years.

And the harvest-child—the one in whom the seed could finally stand upright and be born—was not simply a speaker. She was the most concentrated expression of a century of careful cultivation. The strongest voice. The one the beasts answered too well.

Zephyrine, who had called every creature in the valley at the frozen ford, and had been silent for two days afterward, and had told Odile in a voice like a stranger's: *it answered too well. Like it knew me. Like it was glad.*

*Like it was glad.*

Odile pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars.

* * *

When Zephyrine came back, near dusk, with frost in her hair and the particular set of her shoulders that meant the cache had been exactly where she'd left it and exactly as empty as she'd feared, Odile was still sitting in the same position, the book closed on her lap, the candle dead. The hut was nearly dark. Snow-light came blue through the cracks in the shutters.

"You haven't moved," Zephyrine said. There was no accusation in it. There was something worse—there was familiarity, the easy tone of someone who had decided, against all her rules, to trust you, and who noticed your silences now the way she'd notice a change in the weather.

"I've been reading," Odile said.

"You've been sitting."

"Both." Odile looked down at the book. She had rehearsed this, in the long hours, all the ways to say it—all the ways to soften it, delay it, break it gently, tell half now and half after supper. Every version had collapsed under examination. There was no gentle architecture for this. There was only the sentence, and the sentence did not care how it was delivered.

"The marginalia," she said. "The notes in the margins. They're a century old. Written by an archivist named Ondrey who saw more than the Spire ever admitted. And Zeph—" She stopped. Started again. "You know what I told you in Lowmark. That the prophecy names a winter-born speaker. That the entity wants you as its vessel. That you might be the one to unbind the king."

"I remember."

"That was incomplete." Odile's voice sounded very flat in the cold air, and she was grateful for the flatness; it kept the words from breaking apart. "I told you the entity chose you. Marked you. Found you. That's what the surface text says, and the surface text is what the Spire teaches, because the surface text lets everyone sleep. But Ondrey went below the surface text, and what he found—" She made herself say it plainly, because plainness was the last honest thing she had left. "You weren't found. You were made. Planted. A fragment of the entity was sown into a bloodline a hundred years ago, and it's been passed down, growing, concentrating, and the beast-speech isn't a separate gift that happens to run alongside it. It *is* the seed expressing itself. Your gift is the entity's rootlet, reaching up through a century of mothers and fathers toward the light."

The silence that followed was total. Even the wind seemed to lean in to listen.

Zephyrine stood in the doorway with snow melting off her shoulders onto the packed earth floor, drop by drop, and her face did what faces do when the ground gives way—not terror first, but blankness, the mind refusing the data, casting about for a version of the world in which the sentence just spoken was not true.

"No," she said finally. It wasn't refusal. It was arithmetic. "No. My mother wasn't—I'd know. People know what runs in their blood. Fevers, temper, bad hips. You'd *know*."

"Would you?" Odile asked gently. "Your mother hid you from the Spire your whole childhood. Your whole line has been hiding for a hundred years, hiding so well that by your generation nobody remembered what they were hiding. That's what hiding does, Zeph. It doesn't just conceal you from your hunters. It conceals you from yourself."

"That's not—" Zephyrine's hand went to the doorframe, gripping it, and Odile saw the wood creak under fingers that could gentle a wounded lynx. "I'm a person. I have a name. I have—" Her voice caught, and she pushed through it. "I smuggle salt. I bargain with cats. I have *rules*. Nobody made me. I made myself, out of nothing, on these mountains, and no book in any cipher gets to—"

"I know," Odile said.

"You don't *know*, you read about it—"

"I burned for it." The words came out of Odile before she could weigh them, and having escaped, they hung in the cold air between them, undeniable. "Twenty years in the archives. When I started asking the questions Ondrey asked—when I found the first thread of this—they didn't burn me, because the Spire doesn't burn its own anymore, it's tidier than that. They erased me. Name struck, post dissolved, sent down the mountain to live under a false name and be forgotten. I lost everything for following this exact thread halfway. You're the one who has to decide whether to follow it the rest of the way. So no. I don't know. But I know what it costs to *want* not to know, and I know that wanting it doesn't make the thread untrue."

Zephyrine stared at her. In the blue half-dark her eyes were very bright, and Odile recognized the brightness—she'd seen it in the ford valley, in the moment before the gift had torn loose from the woman and become something else, something that answered too gladly.

"If it's true," Zephyrine said slowly, "then everything I am is—"

"No." Odile stood up, and the motion cracked the stillness of the room like ice cracking on a pond. "Listen to me. Listen, because this is the part that matters and I will not have you walk out of this hut believing the lie underneath the truth. The seed is real. The century of cultivation is real. But a seed is not a tree. A seed is *potential*, and potential is contested ground. Ondrey's notes end with a question, not an answer—he writes, in the last legible entry, *the harvest-child may be the door that opens, or the hand that bars it. The same root that feeds the blight can feed the orchard. It depends on who tends it.*"

"Who tends it," Zephyrine repeated, hollow.

"You tend it. Every choice you've ever made. Every salt-run you finished instead of abandoning. Every animal you healed instead of using. Twenty-five years of being *nobody*, Zeph, of choosing invisibility and decency in a world that offered you neither—that was you, tending. Whatever was planted in your line, whatever the entity dreamed toward, it did not get a vessel. It got a *woman*, one who has spent her whole life deciding, daily, hourly, what to do with the strength in her blood. The entity planted a root. You grew a person. Those are not the same thing, and I will fight anyone—archivist, inquisitor, or god—who tells you otherwise."

The speech ran out of Odile like water out of a cracked jug, and when it was done she stood breathing hard in the cold hut, astonished at herself, at the ferocity that had lived folded up inside twenty years of careful silence.

Zephyrine didn't answer at once. She looked down at her own hands, turning them over, palms then backs, as though seeing them for the first time—as though checking them for roots.

"My name," she said at last, very quietly. "Galloway. Is that even mine? Is it the line? Am I the—" She swallowed. "Is there a *me* underneath it? Or am I just the shape the thing grew into?"

"Your name is yours," Odile said. "Names are earned, not inherited. Everything else is inheritance. That's the whole difference, and it's the only one that matters."

Outside, the light was failing. Somewhere on the mountainside the lynx called once, a sound like a saw drawn through frozen wood, and Zephyrine's head turned toward it instinctively, the way it always did, the body answering before the mind—a reflex, Odile thought, that was either love or leash, and perhaps had always been both, and perhaps the distinction had never mattered to anyone except the person trying to survive it.

"There's more," Odile said. "Isn't there. I can see it. You've been sitting here all day because there's more."

Odile hesitated. Then she opened the book again, to the final pages of Ondrey's notes, to the entry she had read eleven times and still could not make mean anything survivable.

"There's more," she agreed. "Ondrey traced what happened to the last harvest-child. The speaker who failed, a century ago. The deep-folk elder told us she broke—but he didn't know why, or how completely. Ondrey knew. He was there." She drew a breath. "He writes that the entity doesn't just use the harvest-child as a door. It uses her as an *anchor*. The binding of the king and the life of the speaker are tied together at the root—literally, at the root-system, one thread running through both. Which means the unbinding can't be done from outside. It has to be done *through* the anchor. Through you. And Ondrey's last note says—" Her voice failed, steadied. "*What the anchor binds, the anchor pays. Ask what the spring is fed with.*"

Zephyrine was silent for a long moment. Then, in a voice stripped of everything but curiosity—the terrible calm curiosity of a woman who had survived the Thornfells by never flinching from information—she asked:

"Fed with what?"

And Odile, who had spent the entire day hoping she would not have to answer that question aloud, looked at the dying light in the window and said, "I don't know yet. But I think we have until the solstice to find out—because the entity does. And tonight, for the first time since we met, I am afraid not of what the Spire will do to us."

She closed the book.

"I'm afraid of what we'll find out we were willing to pay."
