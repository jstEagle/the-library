## 10. Confrontation at the Frozen Ford

The ford had a name, once. Zephyrine had heard it from her mother, or from someone who had been her mother in the way that mountain women were mothers to every child who passed through their kitchens—*the Greywater*, she'd said, *where the river forgets to be a river and remembers it was ice first.* The name had gone out of use when the village that used it went under the snow, and now the crossing was just a place where the water ran shallow and slow over grey stones, freezing at the edges first, then all at once, like a held breath finally let go.

They reached it at dusk on the third day after the ibex.

Zephyrine had not slept well since. The herd's terror had soaked into her the way cold soaked into wool—not all at once, but thoroughly, and impossible to wring out. All day she had walked with Odile through the high white silence of the pass with her ears full of a sound no human ear could catch: the vast, low murmur of everything alive beneath the snow, all of it turned the same direction, all of it listening for the thing that moved under the ground toward the Spire. Hares in their forms. Foxes curled nose-to-tail. A brown bear in her winter den whose dreams had gone sour. Even the fish under the ice of the Greywater, sluggish and half-asleep, had lifted their pale faces toward the same distant dread.

And now, as they came down off the scree toward the ford, Zephyrine felt one thread of that great quiet go taut and wrong.

She stopped so abruptly that Odile walked two more paces before noticing.

"What is it?"

"Someone's here." Zephyrine crouched, laid her bare palm flat against the snow. It was a habit so old she didn't remember learning it—the way other smugglers read tracks, she read warmth, weight, the tremor of small hearts in small bodies. "On the far bank. Two-legged. And—" She paused. Her stomach turned over slowly, like a stone rolling in a streambed. "Horses. Three horses. And something else."

"Something else?"

"Birds." She frowned. "A lot of birds. Caged."

Odile's face did the thing it always did when the Spire entered a conversation: it closed, gently, like a book being set aside. "Inquisitors bring ravens," she said quietly. "For messages. For watching. They've done it since before my time in the archives. A raven sees a face once and holds it for years."

"I know what ravens do." Zephyrine straightened. Her breath hung between them, silver in the last blue light. "I'm trying to tell you which face they're holding."

"You don't know that it's—"

"It's Doss." She said the name before she could stop herself, and hated how easily it came, how warm it still was in her mouth despite everything. "It has to be. Nobody else knows we'd come down to the ford. Nobody else knew I'd want to cross where the ice is thickest instead of climbing to the bridge at Hollowmere. I told him that route myself, three winters ago, when he was still the man who carried flour to Widow Anselm without charging her." She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. "I told him because I trusted him."

Odile said nothing. There was nothing kind to say, and she was, whatever else she was, too honest for false kindness. That was one of the reasons Zephyrine had not yet grown tired of her.

"We could go around," Odile offered at last. "Climb back up, take the ridge east. It would cost us a day. Maybe two."

"The solstice costs us more than two days." Zephyrine looked across the valley. The ford lay below them in the failing light, a ribbon of dark glass stitched with white. Beyond it the road climbed toward the Spire road, and beyond that, faint as a scar on the horizon, the Spire itself stood against the bruised sky—a needle of pale stone with a smudge of green at its base that should not have existed in this season, that made her eyes ache to look at directly, the way a lamp hurts eyes that have lived long in the dark. "Every hour we spend avoiding him is an hour the thing under the snow spends getting closer to that."

"And if he's brought inquisitors?"

"He's brought inquisitors." She had known it since the moment she'd touched the snow; she simply hadn't wanted to say it aloud, because saying it made it a fact instead of a fear. "Three. Maybe four. They're standing very still. People who are waiting stand very still."

Odile pulled her patched scholar's coat tighter around herself. In the dusk her face was all angles and shadow, a face built for archives and lamplight, absurd and brave out here in the wild. "Zephyrine. If it comes to a fight—"

"There won't be a fight."

"If it comes to one. You need to know what I know about what you can do. Because I've read the accounts, and the accounts are—" She stopped. Started again. "The Spire doesn't burn beast-speakers because they talk to animals. They burn them because of what happens when they stop talking and start *calling*. There's a difference. Talking is a conversation. Calling is a command. And there are records, Zeph, sealed records I helped shelve, of speakers who called in desperation and couldn't stop. Who stood in the middle of what they'd summoned and couldn't make it go home again. Who—" her voice dropped, "—who weren't entirely themselves afterward. As if the calling hollowed out a space, and something else moved in."

The wind came down the valley, carrying the smell of ice and, beneath it, faintly, impossibly, the smell of growing things—green sap, turned earth, blossom—from the direction of the Spire. Zephyrine wrapped her arms around herself.

"Then let's hope there isn't a fight," she said.

* * *

They crossed at full dark, by starlight, because Zephyrine refused fire.

The ice held them. It sang under their boots—that low, groaning whale-song that thick river ice makes when weight moves across it—and Zephyrine found herself listening past the song, down into the black water beneath, where the fish hung like thoughts in the current. They were afraid. Everything was afraid. But the fear had a shape now, a direction, and the direction was not the ford.

That was the only reason she kept walking.

Doss stepped out of the alder scrub on the far bank while they were still twenty feet from shore.

He had been handsome once, in the rough way of men who live outdoors—broad through the shoulders, quick to laugh, a gap in his teeth that showed when he smiled. He was still broad. He did not smile. He stood with his hands loose at his sides and a lantern raised in one fist, and its light fell upward onto his face and made him look like something drowned and propped upright.

Behind him, shapes resolved out of the scrub: four figures in the grey-and-white livery of the Spire's inquisition, hoods back, faces pale ovals in the lantern light. One of them held a cage, and in the cage ravens shifted and muttered, their black eyes catching the light like wet stones.

"Zeph." Doss's voice was careful. Rehearsed. She could hear the rehearsal in it, the way you could hear the seams in cheap cloth. "Don't run. Please don't run. They said if you ran it would go worse."

"Doss." She kept her voice level. Below her feet, through the soles of her boots, through the ice, the fish had gone utterly still, and she understood suddenly that they weren't afraid of the thing under the snow anymore. They were afraid of her. They had felt her attention come down into the water like a hook. She eased it away, gentle, gentle. "Who did they say it would go worse for?"

"For you." His jaw worked. In the lantern light she could see sweat on his temples despite the cold, and she clung to that—sweat meant fear meant the man she'd known was still somewhere inside the shell the Spire had dressed him in. "For your friend. They know what she is. They know what she took out of the archive. They know about the deep-folk, Zeph, they know you sheltered with them, they know about the avalanche pass—"

"Then they know everything," Zephyrine said, "except where we're going next. Which means they need you to keep talking to me. Which means you're not here to arrest us. You're here to delay us." She began, very slowly, to walk forward along the ice. "How much are they paying you, Doss? Or is it the other kind of payment? The kind where they don't hang your brother for the salt you moved through Kettering two springs back?"

He flinched. Full-body, involuntary, like a horse feeling the whip before it falls.

"That's it, then," she said softly.

"They have Tam," he said, and his rehearsed voice cracked clean through, and underneath it was the boy she'd taught to read ice, the young man who'd wept when his father died and let her see it. "They have Tam in a cell under the Spire and they showed me a letter in his hand and they told me that if the woman Vasse reaches the Spire, they'll—Zeph, they'll do things to him that I can't—I *can't*—"

"I know." And she did know. That was the filthiest part of it, she thought. She knew exactly what the Spire's cells were like, because she had spent her whole life building herself into a person who would never see the inside of one, and the architecture of that fear was load-bearing in her soul. "I know exactly what they'll do. And I'm going to the Spire anyway, Doss. Not despite Tam. For everyone who's ever been in a cell under it."

One of the hooded figures spoke—a woman's voice, dry as old vellum. "That will be far enough, mistress Galloway."

The name hit her like a slap. Not Zephyrine. Not the false names she'd worn like coats over the years—Maren, Ilse, the widow Brack. *Galloway.* Her true name, the one that sat circled in a dead scholar's prophecy book beside tomorrow's date, the one no living person was supposed to know.

Beside her, she heard Odile's breath catch.

"You know," the inquisitor continued, conversational, terrible, "it is remarkable, the things a man will trade for a brother's life. Routes. Names. And, given sufficient encouragement, the contents of a childhood. Your friend Mr. Doss has an excellent memory for a childhood. He remembers your mother singing to the goats so they'd stand still for milking. He remembers the first animal you ever spoke to. Do you remember it, mistress Galloway? He does. He says it was a crow with a broken wing, and you were six, and you told it a secret, and it stayed with you the whole winter."

The ravens in the cage had gone silent. All of them. Their heads were turned toward her, every black eye fixed, and Zephyrine realized with a lurch that they were not looking at her the way birds look at people. They were looking at her the way birds look at a hawk.

"Here is what happens now," the inquisitor said. "You will come off the ice. You will walk to us. You will be gentle, and cooperative, and no one will be hurt—yourself, your scholarly friend, or Mr. Doss's brother, whose continued health depends entirely on your cooperation. Do you understand?"

Zephyrine understood many things in that moment, standing on the singing ice with her true name hanging in the frozen air.

She understood that they had planned this well. That the ford was chosen because it was narrow, because the banks were steep, because the scrub gave cover to crossbows she hadn't yet counted. That Doss was both bait and knife, grief weaponized. That the ravens were there not to watch her but to *know* her—to carry her face, her gait, her voice back to the Spire so that no disguise would ever hold again.

And she understood—coldly, clearly, the way she understood weather—that they had made one mistake.

They had brought her to a place full of living things.

The valley around the Greywater was empty of people. It had been empty of people for a generation, since the village died, and in that generation it had filled back up with everything else: fox and hare and deer, marten and owl, the bear in her den, the fish under the ice, the mice in the alder roots, the ravens—wild ones, free ones—roosting in the crags above. A whole hidden nation, sleeping and waking in the snow, every one of them already attuned to her, already listening, because she had spent three days walking among them with her gift open like a wound, unable to close it since the ibex.

"Mistress Galloway," the inquisitor said. "Step off the ice."

"No," Zephyrine said.

And then she stopped talking, and called.

She had never done it before. Not like this. All her life her gift had been a whisper, a bargain, a polite request across the species-line—*move your herd off the trail and I'll see the wolf doesn't trouble you; hold still, friend, this arrowhead wants coming out.* She had kept it small on purpose, the way you keep a fire small in a wooden house. She had rules. She had walls. She had spent twenty years building the walls.

She tore them down all at once, and reached into the valley with everything she was.

It was not words. Words were the surface of it, the foam on the wave. What went out of her was deeper than language—a vast, ringing *attention*, a bell struck inside her own chest whose sound traveled outward through flesh and fur and feather and scale, through root and burrow and nest, and everywhere it touched, it woke.

The valley answered.

It answered *too well.*

The fish rose under the ice first, hundreds of them, pressing up against the underside of the frozen river until the whole surface shimmered and bulged with pale bodies, mouths opening and closing in the dark water. The hares came out of their forms in a flood, dozens, scores, streaming down the slopes like snow running uphill. The foxes came silent and terrible. The owls dropped out of the trees in perfect unison, and above the crags the wild ravens lifted in a boiling black column against the stars, screaming—not their ordinary calls but something older, ragged, a sound like a word being torn apart. From the alder scrub behind the inquisitors came a crashing, and then another, and the horses they had tethered there screamed and tore free of their pickets and fled, and still more shapes came on foot: a badger like a boulder with teeth, a pine marten flowing along a branch, the great brown sow herself, woken from her den, moving through the dark with her breath steaming like a forge.

The crossbowmen fired. Zephyrine barely registered it—one bolt hissed past her ear, another buried itself in the ice at Odile's feet—and then a stag came out of nowhere, out of everywhere, and took the second archer off his feet, and the line of grey livery broke.

But Zephyrine was barely in her body anymore.

This was the thing Odile had tried to warn her about, and the warning had been a candle held up to a sunrise. The call went out and out and *out*, past the valley, past the ridges, down into the earth where the mice slept and the worms turned, up into the air where every winged thing wheeled toward her like iron filings toward a lodestone, and she could feel all of it, all at once, thousands of hearts hammering in rhythm with hers, thousands of bodies hers to move, and it was magnificent, and it was *appalling*, because she could feel herself thinning. Stretching. Pouring out into all those vessels like water poured across a table, and some cold, clear part of her understood that if she did not stop, there would soon be very little of Zephyrine left in Zephyrine—just a wide-open door with the whole wild world blowing through it.

*Mine,* said something, in a voice that was her voice and also the voice of every throat she had opened. *All of it, mine.*

*No,* she answered, and the answer was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the avalanche pass, harder than leaving her caches burning behind her. She gathered herself. She hauled inward, fist over fist, reeling the call back in like a fisher hauling a net heavy with more fish than the boat could hold. *Not mine. Borrowed. Go home. Go home. GO HOME.*

The valley obeyed her, because it could not do anything else, and that was the worst of it.

The hares scattered back upslope. The owls rose. The ravens folded out of the sky like a black cloth being taken away. The fish sank, sulking, into the deep channels. The bear paused at the edge of the lantern light, turned her head, and looked at Zephyrine with small brown eyes full of something that was not quite worship and not quite reproach, and then she was gone, and the night was only night again, and Zephyrine was on her knees on the ice with blood running from her nose and her ears ringing with silence.

The inquisitors were gone too. Fled, or dragged, or trampled—she saw one figure limping into the dark supporting another, and the cage lying open and empty in the snow, its door torn off its hinges. Of the ravens, captive or free, there was no sign.

Doss remained.

He stood where he had stood, though he had fallen to one knee at some point and risen again, and his face in the spilled lantern light was the face of a man who has watched his god arrive and found it hungry. He was looking at her the way the bear had looked at her. The way the fish had.

"Zeph," he whispered. "What *are* you?"

She opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

She tried again, and felt the silence sit in her throat like a stone swallowed sideways, and understood that it wasn't shock, or not only shock. Something had gone out of her with the call—words themselves, maybe, or the part of her that spent them—and what remained was too thin, too stretched, to bear the weight of speech. She pressed her lips together. Pressed her palm flat against the ice, against the cold, against the solid simple truth of frozen water.

Odile knelt beside her. Odile, who had crossed the entire confrontation untouched amid a storm of antlers and wings, as if the valley had known she was not to be touched—as if *Zephyrine* had known it, in the part of her that did the knowing now. Odile put a hand on her shoulder and did not ask her to speak.

"Get up, Mr. Doss," Odile said quietly, without turning her head. "Go home to your brother. Tell them whatever you have to tell them. And when you see him—when you hold him—remember that she's the reason you can."

Doss made a sound that might have been a word. Then he turned and blundered into the dark, crashing through the alders, and was gone.

* * *

They did not light a fire that night either. They walked.

Zephyrine walked because Odile guided her, one hand under her elbow, steering her up the far road through the starlit snow, and because her legs remembered how even when the rest of her felt like a house with the furniture removed. Twice Odile tried to speak to her and twice seemed to hear the silence answering and let it be. Once, near midnight, she simply said, "You gave it back. All of it. I watched you take it back when you could have kept it. I want you to know that I saw."

Zephyrine squeezed her hand. It was all she had.

They sheltered before dawn in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, out of the wind, and Zephyrine lay with her head in the crook of her arm and stared at the stone an inch from her face and listened to the valley breathing around her. The fear was still there, everywhere, the great low dread of the thing under the snow—but threaded through it now was something else. Attention. Recognition. Every creature within a day's walk knew what had happened at the ford. Every creature was telling every other creature. By morning, she thought, the whole wild world of Thornfell would know her name—not *Galloway*, not the name in the book, but the true one, the one that had no letters. The one that meant *she calls, and we come.*

She did not know whether that made her safer or marked her more deeply than any raven's memory ever could.

Sleep took her like an undertow.

* * *

She woke at dusk the next day, and still did not speak.

Her voice was not gone—she tested it silently, in the privacy of her own skull, and found the machinery intact, the words queued and waiting. But every time she reached for them, she felt the edge of the thing she'd touched at the ford, the vastness, the *mine-all-of-it-mine*, and the words went small and hid. Speaking felt like striking a match in a room full of spilled oil. She did not yet know what would catch.

So she walked, and signed, and let Odile narrate the world for both of them—Odile talking enough for two, pointing out landmarks, reciting archive trivia, filling the silence with the desperate cheer of a woman who had decided that if her companion would not speak, she would speak enough for the both of them and pretend it was a choice. Zephyrine almost smiled, once, at the sheer relentless noise of her.

On the second evening, they crested a ridge and saw the Spire road below them at last: a dark seam winding through the white, and on it, small with distance, a line of carts moving toward the pale stone—covered carts, slow-moving, escorted by riders in grey.

Odile raised her battered spyglass, focused, and went very still.

"Plague carts," she said at last, lowering it. "Dead-carts. They're hauling the winter dead into the city." She glanced sideways at Zephyrine, and something kindled in her face that Zephyrine recognized from her own reflection: the look of a smuggler who has just seen a smuggling route. "Zeph. I have an idea. And you're going to hate it."

Zephyrine looked at the Spire, green-rooted and patient against the darkening sky, and then at the slow procession of the dead.

She nodded once—and found, to her surprise, that the nod had a word inside it after all, the first one in two days, small and hoarse and entirely her own.

"*Good,*" she rasped.
