## 11. What Aldric Remembers

The dreams came to him in the green hours, when the palace slept and the roots beneath the floor went quiet with their own slow dreaming.

Aldric woke in the dark of his bedchamber with his hands already moving, already folding back the covers, because he had learned—somewhere, in some earlier surfacing whose memory was itself half-eaten—that the waking did not last. It came like a fish rising through deep water, brief and silver and gasping, and if he did not spend it well it sank again and he would not know, afterward, that it had ever broken the surface at all.

He sat up. The fire had burned to embers that breathed red in the grate. Frost furred the inside of the window glass, thick as a second pane, and beyond it the gardens of the Spire city glowed with a light that had no source he could name. Blossoms in the blizzard. Fruit heavy on the boughs in the dead of winter. He had stopped, years ago, telling himself it was beautiful.

*Aldric,* said the voice that was not a voice, from the roots of his skull, drowsy and warm as a mother's hum. *Sleep. The harvest is not yet in.*

*The harvest,* he thought, with the small bitter clarity that was all he owned. *Yes. I know what you are harvesting.*

But he did not say it aloud. He had learned that too. Saying it aloud woke the thing faster, and the waking was the only country he still ruled.

He rose. His body moved strangely, heavily, as though he were a man wading through a river of honey, and he knew why: the roots had grown into him the way ivy grows into the joints of a wall, patient, tender, total. He could feel them when he was awake like this—fine filaments threaded along his spine, up through the base of his skull, wrapped so gently around the places where his will lived. They did not hurt. That was the horror of it. They had never once hurt. They loved him, in the way that a pot loves a root, in the way that a garden loves the thing it has decided to grow.

He dressed without calling for his servants, because his servants were not his servants anymore. He could not have said when they had stopped being his. Perhaps they never had been; perhaps the crown itself had been the first root, the seed of the seed, and everything that bent toward the throne had bent toward the thing beneath it instead.

There was one thing in the room that the roots had never touched. He went to it now, on legs that ached like an old man's, and knelt before the cold hearth, and pressed the loose stone at its back with the heel of his hand.

The cavity behind it was small—small enough for a fist, for a folded page, for the last free thing in the palace of the Verdant King. Inside lay the letter he had been writing across a year of stolen wakings, in a cipher of his own devising, because he could not trust ink that any archivist might read. He drew it out. The paper was soft at the folds from handling. He had read it so many times he no longer needed to, but he read it anyway, because reading it was how he remembered who was speaking.

*To whoever finds this, if anyone ever does.*

He had begun it that way, in the first waking, when his hand had shaken so badly the ink had feathered. He remembered that night. He had surfaced somewhere in the middle of a court feast, standing, laughing at something the Chancellor had said, and the laughter had been in his mouth like a borrowed coat, and he had looked down at his own hands holding a cup of wine and had not known whose hands they were. The terror of that had burned through him like a fever, and for one hour—one golden, dreadful hour—he had been entirely Aldric again, alone in a crowd of his own courtiers, a ghost at his own table.

He had walked out of the feast. No one had stopped him. The roots had been drowsing, sated on the adoration of the hall, and he had made it back to his chambers and sat down at his desk and written the first line before the drowse took him again.

*If you are reading this, then you have come against the Gardener, and I am sorry for it, because I know what it costs to come against him. I have watched him cost things for twenty years.*

He wrote in the waking hours the way other men prayed: without certainty of being heard, without certainty of mattering, but because the alternative was to let the silence win.

*First, the instructions, because instructions are the only charity I have left to give.*

*The throne is a mouth. Do not stand before it. He speaks from beneath it, through it, and his speech is a rooting—it enters through the ears and finds the soft places. Cover your ears if you must, but know that his true voice does not come through the air. It comes up through the floor. Stand on stone if you can find it. Old stone. The foundations of the Spire are his body, but the deep-folk tunnels beneath them predate him, and he has never managed to fill them with his listening. I do not know why. Perhaps even roots have a fear.*

*The court is not court. Understand this before you bow to anyone. The Chancellor is his. The Mistress of the Orchards is his, and she was a woman once—I remember her, from before, a gardener's daughter with dirt under her nails and a laugh like a cartwheel—and now she smiles with his smile and prunes with his pruning, and what remains of the woman is kept somewhere in the green, the way one keeps a cutting of a plant one likes. Do not hate them. They are not traitors. They are compost.*

*The solstice is the door. I do not know what is on the other side of it. I know only that he has been counting toward it the way a farmer counts toward lambing, and that the winter is not his anger. Everyone believes the winter is his anger, his punishment, his siege. It is not. It is his patience. The cold is the lid on the seedbed. He is not killing the world. He is preparing it.*

Aldric paused there, kneeling at the hearth with the letter in his hands, and let himself feel it again—the thing he had felt when that knowledge had surfaced in him, two winters ago, in a waking so violent he had vomited into the orchard soil and the soil had *thanked* him. The winter was not a punishment. It was an incubation. All those frozen villages, all those buried herds, all those children with blue lips in the passes of Thornfell—not wrath. Husbandry. The long winter was the long night in the cold frame, and in the spring, when the lid came off—

He made himself write the rest, in the waking after that. His handwriting had been very small and very neat, the way it went when he was holding himself together by the letters themselves.

*He means to be born. I am the door he has grown for himself. Twenty years he has been hollowing me, and I let him, and this is the confession that sits under all the others: I let him. I was nine years old and the crown was heavy and something in the garden spoke to me with my dead father's voice and said, *you will never be hungry again, little king, you will never be afraid, you will never lose anything you love, because everything you love will be part of you.* And I was nine, and I said yes.*

*Whatever else you learn of me, learn that. I said yes. The sin was mine before the captivity was his. I do not ask you to forgive it. I ask you to know it, so that you do not mistake me for a man worth rescuing on my own account. I am a field he planted. The question is only what he planted me for, and whether the harvest can be burned before it comes in.*

The embers ticked in the grate. Somewhere far below, so far below that he felt it more than heard it, the roots shifted in their sleep—a vast slow turning, like a whale rolling in deep water, like a mountain settling. Aldric went still and waited, the way he always waited, counting his own heartbeats, listening for the hum that meant the thing beneath was stirring toward him.

It passed. The roots sighed and went on dreaming.

He turned to the last page. The last page was the hardest one, and he had rewritten it four times across four wakings, because each time he surfaced he found that his earlier self had been too proud, or too cowardly, or too hopeful, and each waking he understood a little better what needed to be said and had a little less of himself left to say it with.

*And now the apologies, because a man in my position owes apologies the way a burned house owes smoke.*

*I am sorry to my mother, who died believing her son was a good king, and who was right for longer than I deserved. I am sorry to the villages of the Thornfell marches, whom I swore at my coronation to shield from exactly the hunger now eating them. I am sorry to every courtier I have watched go green-eyed and smiling and said nothing, because saying nothing kept me one more day of waking, and I chose my wakings over their lives, over and over, and I would choose it again, and I am sorry for that too.*

*I am sorry to whoever you are, reading this, because you are reading it in the cold, in the ruin of the world I was crowned to keep warm, and every frozen field is a field I let lie fallow for him.*

He stopped. His eyes were burning. He let them burn. Tears were one of the things the roots had never managed to prune—perhaps because grief was too deep a root itself, older than the Gardener, older than the Spire, grown down into him from ancestors he had never met. When he wept, he felt most like a king. Not the crown-king, the garden-king, the smiling thing that presided over blooming orchards in the dead of winter. A king in the older sense: a man responsible for people, alone with the weight of it, with no one to hand the weight to.

*And last, the plea. I have saved it for last because it is the only part I mean with my whole self, and I wanted it to have the last word in case the others are read and this one is not.*

*Do not save me.*

He had written it large, that first time, the letters gouged deep enough to be read by touch. He had written it again, smaller, in the margin, in case the large version looked like a trick. He had written it a third time in cipher, in case the reader did not trust plain words from a king's hand.

*I know how that must sound. I know what you will think—that this is him, speaking through my hand, laying a trap of humility, that the captive king begging to be left in his chains is the oldest trick in the Gardener's book. I thought of that. I thought of it for a year. And here is what I have decided: I will write it anyway, in every hand I have, in every tongue I know, and if it is a trick then it is a trick I have out-thought him to write, because I wrote it in the wakings, and the wakings are mine. Whatever else he owns, he does not own these hours. This letter is the ledger of my free hours and every word in it was paid for.*

*So hear me. If you have come to unbind me, and the unbinding can be done cleanly, then do it, and I will owe you a debt I cannot name. But if the unbinding wants a price—if it wants a life, or a love, or a self, if it wants someone to pour themselves out to feed the spring the way he has poured me out to feed the winter—then walk away. Let the door stay shut. Let me stay what I am.*

*Because I know what I am now. I have had twenty years to learn it, in pieces, in the dark. I am a garden that walks and smiles and signs decrees. There is very little Aldric left in Aldric. What there is, is this: the memory of my mother's hands in flour. The smell of the mountain pines the summer I was seven, before the crown, before the voice. The weight of a promise I made at nine and broke. That is the whole inventory. That is everything he has left me. And I have decided—it has taken me four wakings and most of my courage to decide it—that this small remnant is worth more standing guard than it would be worth rescued.*

*If saving me costs you yourself, then he wins twice. He gets his birth, and he gets another garden. Do not give him two.*

*Live instead. Go somewhere he has not rooted. Plant something that grows slowly and honestly. And if you can, if it costs you nothing—remember that there was a boy who loved the pines, before all this. Not the king. The boy. He is the one asking.*

*Aldric, son of Maren, ninth of that name, who said yes at nine years old and has been saying no in the dark ever since.*

Aldric folded the letter along its year-worn creases and held it a moment against his chest, against the heartbeat that the roots had never quite learned to keep time with. It was the only rebellion available to him and he performed it every waking: one minute, sixty heartbeats, of holding his own heart's rhythm against the slow green pulse beneath the floor, out of step, out of step, out of step.

Then the cold came.

It came the way it always came—not from the window, not from the frost, but up through the stone, a sweetness in the cold, like the smell of sap, like the first warm day of a false spring. The roots beneath the palace were turning over in their sleep, and their turning pulled at him the way the moon pulls at the sea, and he felt his own surface beginning to close, the waking water closing over the silver fish, the dark coming up to meet him with its arms full of blossoms.

*Little king,* hummed the voice that was not a voice, tender as rot. *You were restless in the night. Dream of the orchard. Dream of the spring. It is very close now, Aldric. Can you feel it? The snow is doing its work. Everything is sleeping so beautifully.*

He put the letter back behind the stone. He pressed the stone home with the heel of his hand, and stood, and made his face into the face they needed—the mild, blooming, beloved face of the Verdant King—because the roots were stirring now, and the court would come, and the day would be long, and the only weapon he had left was to be exactly what they had made him, perfectly, until the next waking.

But as the green closed over his mind, as the last of him sank down through the warm dark toward the humming, one thing surfaced and refused to sink, one thought he had never had before, and he carried it down with him like a coal carried into a well:

*Someone is coming. I don't know how I know. But the roots are afraid of something in the mountains, something small and winter-born, and tonight, for the first time in twenty years, they pulled their filaments a little tighter around my heart.*

*They are afraid. Which means I am not the only captive here.*

*Which means, whoever you are—hold on. The wakings are mine. And I will spend the next one waiting for you.*

The fire went out. The king smiled in the dark, and the gardens bloomed, and the snow kept falling on the world like a lid settling onto a seedbed, patient as a promise, close now, very close, as the solstice turned its long face toward the Spire.
