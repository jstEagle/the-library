## 12. The Long Winter Falls

The snow began to fall upward on the third day of the siege.

Zephyrine stood at the mouth of the high pass with her hood thrown back and watched it happen, because there was no other rational response to the sight. Flakes rose from the drifts below her boots like startled birds, climbing in slow spirals toward a sky the color of old pewter, and at some height she could not name they turned and drifted down again, so that the whole valley seemed to breathe—a lung of white air inhaling and exhaling above the drowned forests.

*That's not weather,* said the lynx at her side.

The lynx had a scar across its left eye from a snare two winters gone, and a voice like gravel poured over velvet, and it was right. Zephyrine had lived twenty-seven years in Thornfell. She knew every mood the mountains wore: the dry whispering cold that split stone, the wet fat snows that bent pines to their knees, the killing fogs. This was none of them. This was not weather at all. It was intention, wearing weather's face.

"It's reaching for something," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept her sane, or near enough to sane for the work ahead. "Or pulling something up. Feel the ground?"

She did not wait for the lynx's answer; she could feel it herself through her boot-soles, a faint rhythmic tremor, patient as a heartbeat, as though the mountain itself had begun to dream—and everything she'd learned from Odile Vasse's frantic marginalia told her exactly what the mountain dreamed of, and how soon it would wake.

Solstice. Nine days.

Behind her, down the switchback trail, came the sound that had become the soundtrack of her life: people. Hundreds of them now, maybe more than a thousand, strung along the pass like beads on a fraying cord—refugees from the buried villages of the eastern valleys, hauling children and grandmothers and goats and whatever they'd been able to carry when the snow took their homes. They moved slowly. They moved *agonizingly* slowly, an hour to cover ground Zephyrine could run in ten minutes, and every hour the sky above the Spire grew greener, and every night the aurora-like shimmer over the southern horizon brightened like something ripening.

*You're doing the arithmetic again,* the lynx observed. *I can smell it on you.*

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

*Yes. But this is the bad kind.* The lynx sat down and wrapped its tail over its forepaws, a gesture of terrible patience. *The kind where no answer is clean.*

Zephyrine looked east, where the pass climbed toward the Spire road. Then west, where it descended into the low valleys, toward the deep-folk halls and the safety of stone. Between those two directions lay everything she had, and both roads cost more than she had to give.

* * *

It had started, as catastrophes do, quietly.

Two days after the frozen ford—two days after she had called every living thing in the valley to her and heard the valley *answer*, after the ibex and the wolves and the crows and things she still didn't have names for had come streaming out of the dark to stand around her like a congregation, after the traitor Tam Voss had fled screaming into the snow—she had stopped speaking entirely. Not by choice. The words simply weren't there. When she opened her mouth, what came out was silence shaped like speech, and the beasts looked at her with a new wariness, the way you look at someone who has shown you the size of their fist.

Odile had filled the quiet instead, chattering half-feral with fear, reading aloud from salvaged pages as they walked: *the winter-born speaker shall walk between the crown and the root, and the root shall know its own—* and Zephyrine had nodded and pointed at trails and counted supplies on her fingers, and neither of them had said the obvious thing, which was that Zephyrine was running out of self to spend.

Then the villages had begun to die.

The first was Harrowmere, a salt-trading town she'd known all her life, where she'd once delivered contraband salt to a miller with kind eyes and too many daughters. A shepherd staggered into their camp with frostbite to his hips and a story that made Odile drop her notes into the fire without noticing: the snow hadn't fallen on Harrowmere, it had *gathered*, rising overnight like water filling a bowl, until the rooftops vanished and the church bell rang muffled under forty feet of white, ringing and ringing until it didn't.

"They say," the shepherd rasped, hands shaking around a cup of broth he couldn't feel, "they say the snow was warm. Underneath. Like flesh."

After Harrowmere came Millbeck, then Corren's Ford, then the string of hamlets along the Thawwater whose names Zephyrine had memorized years ago for smuggling purposes—every one of them a cache site, a friendly door, a face she knew—and each one swallowed whole, silently, in a single night. No screams. No survivors except the ones who'd been away from home when it happened, which was somehow worse: hundreds of people walking the passes in the wrong direction, back toward their buried houses, and having to be turned aside by strangers.

And the forests went silent.

Zephyrine felt it before anyone told her—the way you feel a tooth go dead. She reached out with the sense she'd spent a lifetime hiding and found nothing where there should have been *everything*. The pine marts, the jays, the deer, the hares, the whole murmuring parliament of the woods: gone. Not killed. *Withdrawn*, pulled inward like animals before a rockslide, huddled somewhere deep beneath their own skins. Even the crows, who gossiped about everything, had nothing to say. The world had closed its ears.

Only the lynx stayed close to her mind, and even it had gone quieter, speaking less, watching more.

On the morning the snow began to fall upward, the refugees found them—or rather, found Zephyrine, because word had spread among the displaced like blood in water: *a woman who speaks with beasts is holding the high pass open. Follow her tracks. She will not turn you away.*

Which was true, and was going to kill her.

* * *

By dusk there were four hundred new souls crowded into the sheltered hollow below the pass, and Zephyrine stood among them doing sums she hated.

Firewood for four hundred. Food for nine days if they ate like monks, three if they ate like people. The pass west was open—barely—but it was six days' march to the deep-folk gates for healthy walkers, and these were not healthy walkers. There were elders who would need carrying. Children with coughs that were turning wet. A pregnant woman named Sera whom Zephyrine had smuggled salt to in another life, another self, back when survival was the whole of her ambition.

Sera caught her wrist as she passed. "They're saying you're going south." Her breath steamed. "To the Spire. That you can end it."

"People say a lot of things."

"Is it true?"

Zephyrine looked at her—at the swell of her belly, the cracked lips, the eyes that had decided, in the space of one question, to stake everything on the answer. This was the trap of being believed in. People built altars out of you without asking.

"Nine days until solstice," Zephyrine said. "The Spire is five days' hard travel south. If I go, I go tomorrow."

"And us?"

"The western pass is open. I'll mark the route. The deep-folk will take you in—they remember what the last long winter was, better than we ever could."

Sera's grip tightened. "You'll be walking alone? Into that?" She jerked her chin southward, toward the green shimmer on the horizon, and for a moment her fear transmuted into something fiercer. "Everyone here has lost their home. You'd be giving up the only thing you've got left."

*My life,* Zephyrine thought. *I'd be giving up my life, or most of it, if Odile's books are honest—and they are, gods rot them, they are.* She had read the marginalia by firelight while the refugee fires sparked around them, and she understood now what the unbinding demanded: a willing anchor, someone bound to the entity by blood and planting, to draw it out of Aldric the way you draw poison from a wound—with your own body as the cloth. And she understood what the roots would take as payment. Odile had tried to make her say it aloud, and Zephyrine had refused, and the refusal itself had been an admission.

But Sera didn't need the ledger. She needed a reason to keep walking west with a child in her belly and her village under forty feet of warm snow.

"You'll tell your daughter about this," Zephyrine said. "When she's grown. Tell her there was a woman on the pass who did the arithmetic and chose wrong, or chose right—I don't know which yet. But tell her somebody stayed behind so the rest could move. That's the whole of it. That's the story."

"That's not a story. That's a grave."

"Most stories start in one."

Sera let go of her wrist. In her eyes was the particular grief of people who understand a thing is already decided and hate it anyway. "Then walk fast," she said.

* * *

Odile found her at midnight at the edge of the hollow, where the fires thinned out and the upward-falling snow spiraled against the stars. The scholar had a blanket around her shoulders like a mantle and ink-stained fingers curled around a cup of nothing, warming themselves on memory of tea.

"You haven't slept," Odile said.

"You have the watch list."

"You're not on the watch list. You put yourself on every watch, Zeph. Marta saw you. She told me, and then she cried, and then I nearly cried, and now I'm here to be furious at you because crying at you wouldn't work."

The old nickname landed soft and heavy. Somewhere in the last weeks—she couldn't pinpoint when—Odile had stopped being *the scholar* and become *hers*, the way the lynx had, the way the caches and routes had been hers, and that was precisely the problem. Every person she let close became a memory-in-waiting. A debt the roots would collect.

"I sleep enough," Zephyrine said.

"Liar. Beautifully done, but a liar." Odile stepped closer, lowering her voice beneath the wind. "You're going to leave before dawn. Don't bother denying it; you packed last night while you thought I was asleep. You always pack the same way—rope first, then the blade, then food, like the order of the world matters more than the world." She held out her hand. In her palm lay a folded square of paper. "So I'm coming with you, and this is the map I drew for the refugees. Marta knows the western pass. The deep-folk sent a runner—an elder's granddaughter, quick as a weasel. She'll guide them from the fork. It's handled."

"You should stay with them."

"I'm a disgraced archivist, Zeph. My skills are reading dead languages and surviving institutional purges. Neither helps carry grandmothers over a pass." Odile's voice cracked, just barely, on the next words. "And I will not stand in a safe hall eating bread while you walk into that place alone. I did that once already, in the archives. I watched them burn a woman's life's work and I stood there and *survived*, and I have regretted it every single day since. I am done surviving things."

The snow rose around them, fell again, rose. Zephyrine looked at the small stubborn figure in the blanket and felt the full weight of what caring cost when time was rationed: not the grief of losing people, but the grief of knowing, in advance, exactly what you'd lose, and choosing them anyway.

"If I come back without—" she began.

"Don't." Odile's voice was steel over glass. "Don't make bargains about what comes after. We walk south tomorrow. We reach the Spire before solstice. We unbind your king. And then you are going to sit down with me and drink actual tea and tell me what the price was, because I already know there is one, and I am going to be so angry with you afterward, and you are going to let me be angry, because that is what friends are for."

Friends. The word sat in Zephyrine's chest like a coal.

"Tea," she agreed. "Real tea. With honey."

"Honey is for honesty."

* * *

She left the hollow before dawn, as promised, but not before walking its length one last time.

Four hundred sleepers under hides and snow-cloaks, breathing in the dark. Sera with her hands folded over her belly. The old miller from Corren's Ford who'd once paid her in songs. A boy of perhaps seven who had appointed himself guard of the goat herd and sat awake at his post, and met her eyes as she passed, and nodded solemnly, soldier to soldier, and she had to keep walking because stopping would have ended everything.

At the western fork she marked the trail with cuts only smugglers and beasts could read—three slashes and a hook, meaning *safe passage, follow to stone*—and pressed her palm flat against the frozen trunk of the last great pine before the climb. Through the bark she felt the forest's withdrawn silence, vast and frightened, and she spoke to it the only way she could, in the wordless pressure of her gift:

*Keep them. Keep them all. Please.*

Something ancient stirred in the roots beneath her boots—not the Verdant King's roots, older ones, the mountain's own—and though it did not answer, it did not refuse. It was the closest thing to a blessing the wild had left to give.

The lynx fell into step beside her at the treeline. Odile followed, breathing hard, ink-stained and furious and loyal past all sense.

Southward, the sky above the distant Spire had gone green as spring, and the snow rose to meet it in slow silver spirals, and beneath their feet the heartbeat in the stone beat a little faster, a little louder, counting down nine days like a patient gardener counting seedlings.

Zephyrine turned her back on everyone she was about to forget, and walked into the light.
