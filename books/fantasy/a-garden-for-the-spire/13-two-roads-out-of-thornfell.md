## 13. Two Roads Out of Thornfell

The refugees came up out of the valley like water finding its level—slowly at first, then all at once, in a gray flood of sledges and shivering oxen and children wrapped in every blanket their families had owned.

Odile watched them from the mouth of the cave where they had sheltered for two nights, and she counted them the way an archivist counts anything: because counting was the only prayer she trusted. Forty-one adults. Fifty-three children. Nine oxen, three of them too far gone to make another pass. A one-eyed grandmother who had not stopped singing since the lowlands, a thin thread of a harvest hymn worn smooth as river stone.

*One hundred and three souls,* Odile thought, and then corrected herself, because the old ledgers had taught her that numbers lied by rounding. *One hundred and four—the baby born this morning, in the sledge, under the tarp, with Zephyrine's knife and Zephyrine's hands.*

She heard the crying from the birthing even now, the small furious mewl of a child who had arrived into a world that could not promise it a spring. Somewhere below the snowline, the forests had gone silent. Not quiet—silent, which was worse, the way a held breath is worse than snoring. The birds had stopped crossing the passes. The wolves had stopped answering the moon. Whatever moved beneath the snow toward the Spire, it was drinking the sound out of the world as it went, and Odile had spent enough nights lately reading the prophecy's marginalia by tallow-light to know what fed at the far end of that root.

*Aldric,* she thought, and then, because honesty was the discipline her disgrace had left her, *the thing wearing Aldric.*

The solstice was eleven days away.

* * *

Zephyrine found her at the fire pit near the cave mouth, where the refugee women were boiling snow for broth over a flame fed by everything burnable within a mile—fence rails, coffin lids, a wagon wheel someone's grandfather had carved roses into. Odile had learned in the last week that grief made people generous with wood. They gave away the prettiest things first.

"You look like a woman doing arithmetic," Zephyrine said. She sat down on the stone beside Odile without asking, close enough that their shoulders touched for warmth, which was as much tenderness as either of them permitted themselves in public. "I hate arithmetic."

"Then you'll hate what I've been calculating." Odile pulled her coat tighter. The cold had changed character again overnight; it no longer behaved like weather. It behaved like intention. "Eleven days to the solstice. The Spire is nine days' hard travel from here if we take the pilgrim road—and we can't take the pilgrim road, because it's watched, so call it twelve through the high passes, and that assumes the passes hold, which they won't, because the snow is falling upward now, Zeph, I saw it this morning, whole flurries climbing back into the sky like they'd been called home." She heard her own voice rising and pressed it flat. "We cannot arrive before the threshold. At best we arrive during it. Possibly after."

"Then we leave today."

"That's not—" Odile turned and looked at her. Firelight did strange kindnesses to Zephyrine Galloway's face; it softened the wariness around her eyes and made her look, briefly, like the girl the prophecy book insisted had never been allowed to exist. "That is not all of it. Look down the mountain."

Zephyrine looked. Below them, strung along the frozen riverbed like beads on a gray cord, the refugees had made their camp—a hundred fires smoking sideways in the wind, sledges lashed into windbreaks, oxen huddled nose-to-tail. Children were playing a game with knucklebones in the trampled snow, shrieking, alive, oblivious. The one-eyed grandmother's hymn drifted up faint and steady.

"One hundred and four," Odile said quietly. "Plus the ones still coming up behind them. There were villages lower in the Thornfell vale that haven't emptied yet. If we go to the Spire now, we abandon them on a mountain in a winter that means to kill them. But there's a route—I've been working it out all night—the old drovers' road through the Hollow Passes, south and east, down into the low country past the Spire's reach. It adds weeks. Weeks of leading them, feeding them, digging them out, choosing which oxen live. And it costs us the solstice entirely. We lose him, Zephyrine. We lose Aldric, and we lose the unbinding, and the long winter stops being a season and becomes a geology."

Zephyrine said nothing for a long time. In the silence, Odile became aware of the other sounds of the camp: the baby crying, the hymn, the knucklebones clicking against each other in small mittened hands, the wind worrying the tarpaulins like a dog with a bone.

"I know," Zephyrine said at last. Her voice was very level, which Odile had learned meant the opposite of calm. "You think I don't lie awake doing this same arithmetic? Every night since the ibex herd told me what's moving under the snow, I have lain here adding and subtracting human beings. I know exactly what it costs to walk north." She looked at Odile, and her eyes in the firelight were the color of wet slate. "And I know exactly what it costs to stay. Because staying doesn't save them either, Odile. That's the trick of it. That's the trap. If we lead them south and the Verdant King takes flesh at the solstice anyway—if your marginalia is right and it only needed the winter to finish incubating—then we will have traded a king for a hundred and four people who freeze or starve in the low country in February, because the long winter doesn't stop at the mountains. You said so yourself. The roots are already everywhere. There is no south. There is no *away*. There's only sooner or later, and I have spent my whole life being nobody, and I am finished being nobody when being nobody is just dying politely."

"The drovers' road might work," Odile said, but her heart wasn't in it, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person at the fire.

"It might," Zephyrine agreed. "And my gift might be enough to dig them all out of every slide between here and the lowlands. And the prophecy might mean something else entirely. I've lived my whole life on *might*, scholar. It's how smugglers survive." She stood, brushing snow from her coat, and looked north, toward the pale smudge on the horizon where the Spire's tower pierced the cloud deck like a needle through cloth. "But I didn't survive to watch the world get buried while I led a slow parade away from the only fight that matters. Whoever I am—the name in that book, the vessel, the anchor, whatever I'm for—I'm for *this*. Eleven days. We leave for the Spire today."

* * *

Odile went with her.

That was the shameful part, and she carried it through the rest of the day like a stone sewn into her lining: not that she argued, and lost—argument was her trade, and losing arguments to better hearts was almost a comfort—but that some small, starved, unforgivable part of her had been *relieved*. Relieved when Zephyrine chose the Spire. Relieved to have the choice taken out of her hands, so that her guilt could wear the honest clothes of duty instead of the rags of preference.

Because Odile Vasse knew what she would have chosen alone. She had done the arithmetic all night, and her arithmetic had come out the same as Zephyrine's, and she had hated herself for it. A hundred and four lives against a kingdom. Against the world's spring. Any archivist worth her ink could balance that ledger in a heartbeat.

It was the individual entries that broke her.

The one-eyed grandmother, whose hymn had kept half the camp sane through the worst night. The boy with the knucklebones, maybe seven, who had taken to following Odile around and asking questions about books with the hunger of a child who had never seen one. The mother of the newborn, who that morning had pressed a strip of dried fish into Odile's hand and said, "For the road, lady," as if Odile were anyone, as if kindness were a currency everyone simply carried.

If they walked north, those people stayed on a mountain with a map and a prayer. If they walked south, everyone died later, more comfortably, in the dark. Odile had burned colleagues at the Spire for making cleaner calculations than that.

So when Zephyrine announced the choice, Odile felt the stone in her chest shift—and beneath the guilt, shamefully, warmly, like a coal wrapped in wool: relief.

*I am a coward,* she thought, shouldering her pack among the sledges. *I am a coward and a disgraced archivist and I am grateful that someone braver than me made the decision, so that I can follow it and call it loyalty.* And then, because she was also, whatever else, an honest woman: *And I would do it again. That's the worst part. Given the same night, the same arithmetic, I would feel the same relief. God forgive scholars; we always think the answer is a number.*

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing to leave, and every hour of it was a small tax collected against Odile's soul.

It was Zephyrine who drew the map—that was the bitter grace of it, that the smuggler gave the refugees her own roads, the secret ones, the passes she had spent fifteen years learning and hiding, the knowledge that had kept her alive and invisible and hers. She scratched it onto a cured hide with a burnt stick while the village headmen crowded around: the Hollow Passes, the drovers' road, the springs that ran warm enough to melt a campsite clear, the caves where a hundred people could sleep out a storm. She marked the avalanche slopes with little crossed strokes and the safe fords with circles, and she explained each one twice, patiently, in the voice Odile had heard her use on frightened animals—which, Odile reflected, was perhaps exactly what it was.

"The southern fork is closed after first thaw—don't ask me why, it just is, trust me. The warm springs stink like rotten eggs but drink them anyway, they're clean underneath the smell. Keep the oxen off the lake ice; it lies about itself." She rolled the hide and put it in the headman's hands and held on a moment longer than she needed to. "There are caches along the way. Dried meat, oil, salt. Take them. All of them. Don't ration yourselves on my account."

"Who do we thank?" the headman asked—an old man with a frost-burned beard and the particular dignity of someone who had spent a lifetime being respected and was learning to beg. "What name do we carry?"

"No name," Zephyrine said quickly, and Odile saw the flinch in her, the old reflex of the hunted. But then something else crossed her face, something Odile couldn't read, and she added, quieter: "Tell them a friend of the beasts showed you the way. That'll be true enough."

The debt was worse than the thanks. Odile stood apart while the refugees came to them through the afternoon—one by one, in twos and threes—with what they had: fish, candles, a knitted cap forced down over Odile's ears by a woman who would not take no, a blessing murmured over both of them by a lay brother whose order had been extinct for thirty years. Every gift landed on Odile like a sentence. She smiled. She thanked them. She catalogued each face the way she had once catalogued condemned manuscripts, and understood, with archival precision, that she was building an index of people she was abandoning, and that she would keep that index for the rest of her life, wherever the rest of her life happened.

The boy with the knucklebones found her last, at dusk.

"You're going north," he said. He wasn't asking. Children had stopped asking questions days ago; they had started stating facts, because facts were all the adults would confirm. "To the Spire. To fix the king."

"To try," Odile said.

"My mother says the king's garden grows in winter." He turned his knucklebones over in his mitten, click, click, click. "She says the orchards bloom there even in blizzards. Is that true?"

"It's true," Odile said. "And it isn't kind."

He considered that with the seriousness of a judge. Then he opened his mitten and put the knucklebones—five of them, polished smooth by generations of small hands—in her palm. "For luck," he said. "My granddad won them off a Spire clerk in a card game, so they've been lucky twice already."

Odile closed her fingers around them. They were still warm from his hand, and she found, to her horror, that her eyes were burning, and she had to crouch down to his height and pretend to adjust her bootlace until she could speak.

"They'll be lucky a third time," she managed. "Go take care of your mother."

* * *

They left at first dark, when the wind turned and would cover their tracks.

Zephyrine had wanted to slip away clean—smuggler's habit, leave nothing, owe nothing—but Odile had refused to let them vanish, arguing with a stubbornness that surprised them both that abandoned people deserved witnesses, deserved the dignity of being seen left behind rather than discovering themselves deserted. So they stood at the edge of the camp while the headman raised a horn and blew three long notes, and a hundred faces turned toward them, and the one-eyed grandmother's hymn changed key into something older, a road-blessing that Odile half-remembered from manuscripts she had catalogued and never expected to hear sung by living mouths.

*May the mountain know your feet. May the storm forget your name. May what you carry be lighter than what you leave.*

Zephyrine bowed her head. Odile did not trust herself to; she stood straight and let the words hit her like sleet.

Then they turned north, the two of them, and the camp fell away behind them into the blue dark, and the wind took the hymn apart note by note until there was only the mountain, and the upward-falling snow, and the pale needle of the Spire on the horizon, closer now, close enough to hurt.

They climbed in silence for an hour before Odile spoke.

"You left them your roads," she said. "Every secret route you had. Fifteen years of staying alive, handed over on a piece of hide."

"They need them more than I do." Zephyrine's breath smoked. "Besides, I'm going to the Spire. What's a smuggler without secrets? Just a woman walking in the snow."

"And what are we," Odile asked, "if we leave a hundred and four people on a mountain and walk toward the thing that made the winter? What does that make us?"

Ahead of her, barely visible against the snowfield, Zephyrine's shoulders rose and fell once—a shrug, or a shudder, hard to tell.

"Honest," she said. "It makes us honest. We finally picked, Odile. Most people die never picking anything." She glanced back, and in the starlight her expression was unreadable, except for one thing Odile caught clearly and would keep for years: she was not asking forgiveness. "Don't waste the guilt. Spend it at the Spire."

Odile put her hand in her pocket and touched the boy's knucklebones, five small smooth skulls of luck, and said nothing, and climbed.

Behind them, far below, the grandmother's hymn began again, thinner now, carried up the slope on the wrong wind. Ahead, above the cloud deck, the Spire's beacon had lit for the first time that winter—a green light, blooming in the black like something germinating—and Odile stopped on the trail and stared at it with the knucklebones biting into her palm, because she recognized that shade of green. She had catalogued it. It grew in the margins of the oldest pages, on the drawings of the Verdant King's crown.

It was not a beacon, she realized. It was a signal.

Or an invitation.

"They know we're coming," she said.

Zephyrine looked at the light for a long moment, and Odile heard her say, softly, almost gently, the way she spoke to wounded animals:

"Good. Saves us knocking."
