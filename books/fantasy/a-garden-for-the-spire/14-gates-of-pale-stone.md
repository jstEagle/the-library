## 14. Gates of Pale Stone

The dead rode better than the living ever did.

Zephyrine had learned that much by the second day on the plague road, hauling the cart through snow that came down in grey, exhausted sheets. The dead did not complain of the cold. They did not ask how much farther. They lay in their shrouds beneath the canvas tilt, stiff and silent and patient as cargo, which was what they were now—cargo, freight, the only goods a cart could carry openly into the Spire without a seal or a bribe.

She drove. Odile sat beside her under a hood crusted with frost, playing the part of a sister too sick with grief to speak, which required no acting at all; grief was the one thing Odile Vasse wore honestly these days. Between them, wedged into the gap beneath the bench where no guard would think to look, lay the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth, and Zephyrine's knife, and a pouch of salt she had refused to leave behind even when Odile pointed out that salt would be worth nothing inside the Spire.

"It's not for trading," Zephyrine had said, and hadn't explained further, and Odile, to her credit, hadn't asked twice.

The road climbed out of Thornfell's last valleys and flattened onto the high plateau where the Spire stood, and Zephyrine felt the change before she saw it—the way you feel a change in an animal before it shows. The horses grew quiet. Not calm. Quiet the way prey goes quiet when something large is breathing nearby. The off-horse, a placid mare named Sorrel who had never once spooked in three years of mountain roads, kept her ears pinned flat and her neck rigid, and Zephyrine spoke to her in the low wordless way she used with frightened beasts: *nothing here will touch you, I am here, keep walking.* But even as she sent the thought she knew it was a lie, and worse, she suspected the mare knew it too.

Because the beasts had been telling her for days what this place was.

*Under,* the ibex had said, back in the high passes, before the herd scattered east. *It is under. It is awake. It is warm.*

Warm. That was the word that had lodged in her like a splinter. Everything else about the Spire in winter should have been cold—stone and altitude and siege-snow—but every creature that fled its direction carried the same report, delivered in the dumb grammar of instinct: *warm, warm, warm.* A hearth buried under the city. A heart.

Zephyrine did not want to know what kind of heart beat warm enough to be heard through a hundred miles of frozen ground.

"Look," Odile said.

The clouds had torn open ahead of them, and there it was.

She had seen the Spire once before, from far off, years ago—a pale needle against the sky, unremarkable at distance, the kind of fortress every kingdom built somewhere to justify its taxes. She had expected walls, gates, smoke from a besieged city's chimneys. What she saw instead made her rein in the horses without deciding to.

The Spire rose from the plateau like a blade driven into the earth and left there, white stone going grey-green at its base as if moss had climbed it for centuries. But it was not the tower that held her eyes. It was the color around it.

Green.

In the middle of the long winter—in the season that had swallowed Thornfell whole, that had buried villages, that had taught the forests silence—there was a ring of green around the Spire's foundations half a mile wide. Orchards. Terraces climbing the lower slopes of the tower itself, tier upon tier, and every tree in bloom. Blossoms white and pink and a red so deep it looked like something other than a color, all of it standing open under a sky that was actively snowing. Snow fell into the orchards and did not land. Or it landed and vanished, absorbed, drunk. Zephyrine watched a flake drift down toward a flowering branch and disappear a handspan above it, the way water disappears into a mouth.

"That's not possible," she said. Her voice came out flat, which was how her voice went when fear got too large to waste breath on.

"No," Odile agreed quietly. "It isn't."

"The rest of the world is dying of cold and this place is—" She stopped. Started again. "What is it *feeding* on?"

Odile was silent long enough that Zephyrine turned to look at her. The scholar's face, above the muffling scarf, had gone the color of old tallow.

"I wrote my thesis on harvest records," Odile said. "Eleven years in the archives, and the last three of them cataloguing yield reports from the royal demesnes. Do you know what I found, in the year Aldric was crowned? Grain yields up forty percent. Orchard yields beyond anything in a century of records. Fruit the size of fists, ripening twice a season." She pulled her hood back slightly, and her eyes were very bright and very tired. "I thought it was a golden age beginning. I presented those numbers to the Archivist Primus as evidence of divine favor. He looked at me for a long time and then told me to burn the file."

"Why?"

"Because he'd read further back than I had." Odile turned to face forward again. "Every recorded long winter in history ends within sight of the Spire. And every one of them begins with a harvest too good to be true."

Zephyrine looked at the blossoming terraces, at the snow vanishing into them, and understood, in the slow cold way that understanding sometimes arrives, that she was looking at the same thing from both ends. The bloom was not defiance of the winter. It was the winter's *root*. Somewhere beneath that pale stone, something drank warmth the way the orchards drank snowfall, and everything green here was a flower on a stem whose roots went down and down into the dark, into the throne room, into the mind of a king who smiled.

"Blossom before fruit," she murmured. "Fruit before rot."

"What?"

"Nothing. An old smuggler's rule. If a buyer shows you something too ripe, count the flies." She clicked her tongue at the horses. "Keep your face down past the gate. Grieving sisters don't gawk."

The queue at the gates was a mile long and moving like a funeral, which was fitting, because it was one.

Plague carts, corpse-carts, refugee carts, all funneling toward the Gates of Pale Stone—three arches tall as church towers, carved with vines and fruiting branches so dense the stone beneath had nearly vanished. Zephyrine studied the carvings while they waited, because studying things was how she stayed alive, and what she saw made her skin crawl in a way she couldn't immediately name. Then she named it.

The vines were carved growing *inward*. Every tendril, every root, every trailing branch flowed toward the gate's center, toward the Spire beyond, as though the whole façade were one great mouth mid-swallow. And threaded among the leaves, small as a thumbprint if you weren't looking, were faces. Hundreds of faces. Sleeping faces, peaceful faces, faces with their eyes closed and their mouths slightly open, as if they had fallen asleep mid-breath and been incorporated.

"How old are these gates?" she asked.

"Four hundred years," Odile said. "Give or take."

"And the long winters? How many in four hundred years?"

Odile didn't answer. She didn't have to. Zephyrine counted faces instead, gave up somewhere past two hundred, and fixed her eyes on the back of the cart ahead of them.

The guards at the gate were wrong in a subtler way. They stood too straight. They smiled too readily—smiled at corpse-drivers, at grieving women, at everyone, the same mild pleasant smile, like men who had recently been told to smile and had discovered they quite liked it. Their cheeks were wind-chapped but their hands, when one of them reached out to check the cart's manifest, were warm-pink and supple, hands that had never known a hard winter. One of them leaned in close to Zephyrine as he checked the tally, and she caught his smell underneath the leather and steel: green. Crushed leaves. Sap. A man who smelled like a broken stem.

"Three dead?" he asked, smiling.

"Three," Zephyrine said, and let her voice crack appropriately. "Our brother. Our uncle. The boy next door. Fever took them in four days."

"Fever's been generous this season," the guard said warmly, and waved them through, and Zephyrine felt his gaze follow them under the arch, between the sleeping carved faces, into the throat of the city.

Inside the walls, the Spire City opened up around them like a lie told beautifully.

The streets were clean. Swept, salted, ordered—and full. Refugees had come here from a hundred buried villages, and the city had taken them in, and that was the first wrongness: nowhere else in the north had any city *room* for refugees. Here there was room. Bread queues moved quickly. Soldiers handed out blankets. Children ran through the snow with bare heads and rosy cheeks, laughing, and Zephyrine watched one snatch an apple from a barrel outside a grocer's shop—an apple, in the ninth week of a winter that had killed the orchards of three provinces—and the grocer laughed and let him keep it.

Barrels of apples. Crates of pears. Greens stacked in pyramids, glossy and dewy. A fishmonger selling river trout in a season when every river in Thornfell was locked in ice a man's depth thick.

"It's a trap," Zephyrine said under her breath. "This whole city is a snare. You bait it with exactly what people are starving for."

"Or it's a larder," Odile said. "For something that intends to eat later."

They drove on, following the corpse-road toward the burning grounds, and the green thickened around them. Vines had climbed the lower stories of buildings—not ivy, something softer-leafed, flowering out of season, pushing pink blossoms through gaps in shutters and between cobbles. Zephyrine saw a vine growing out of a drain grate, thick as her wrist, and noticed that the cobbles around it had been *lifted*, gently, the way soil lifts over a buried root. The whole city sat on top of something growing. The streets were its skin.

And the people—

The people were the worst part, because none of them seemed afraid. In Lowmark, in the pass villages, in every refugee column she'd passed, fear had been the weather everyone lived in. Here, people strolled. Smiled. Stopped to admire the blossoms. An old woman stood at her doorway feeding crumbs to a robin—a *robin*, in deep winter—and the robin ate from her hand with the tameness of a chapel bird, and the woman's smile was the same smile the guards wore, mild and pleased and faintly absent, as if each of them had set down some heavy thing they'd been carrying and couldn't remember what it was.

"They're happy," Odile whispered. "God help me, they're actually happy. Look at them. When did any of us last see a whole street of people who aren't afraid?"

"That's how you know," Zephyrine said. "Beasts in a snare go quiet after a while. Stop struggling. Get comfortable." She flicked the reins. "Comfortable isn't peace. Comfortable is what happens when something's holding you."

The burning grounds sat on the city's eastern edge, a broad terrace of pale stone with pyres already laid, tended by attendants in ash-grey robes who moved with the same unhurried contentment as everyone else. Zephyrine brought the cart to the designated spot and began the work she had rehearsed: unloading the shrouded dead, presenting papers, keeping her eyes down, being nobody. The attendants checked the shrouds with practiced efficiency—one of them peeled back the canvas over the first body just far enough to confirm fever-blisters painted on with ash and goose fat, Odile's finest work—and then waved them toward the registration table.

A clerk sat there, plump and pleasant, stamping names into a ledger. "Names of the deceased?"

Zephyrine recited the three false names Odile had prepared. The clerk wrote them down, stamped the page, and smiled up at her with the universal smile.

"You've done a kindness, bringing them themselves," the clerk said. "So many just abandon their dead on the roads. But the Spire provides. The Spire provides for everyone, now." He stamped again, warmly. "You'll want lodgings. Drivers' quartering is in the Tanners' Ward, warm beds, hot food, no charge. The Spire provides."

"No charge," Zephyrine repeated.

"Winter's hard on everyone," the clerk said, beaming. "But it won't be hard forever. Spring is coming early this year. You'll see. His Majesty has promised it." He leaned forward, confiding, delighted. "His Majesty says the whole world will bloom soon."

Something in Zephyrine's chest went very still and very cold, colder than the plateau, colder than the passes, a cold that had nothing to do with weather. She smiled back—the hardest smile she had ever produced, because every muscle in her face wanted to run.

"Then we're fortunate," she said. "Thank you."

They took the cart to the Tanners' Ward because refusing would have been the loudest thing they'd done in a hundred miles. The ward was a courtyard of low barracks around a central well, and the well was ringed with green—vines grown up and over its stones, blossoming, and the water below, when Zephyrine glanced into it as they passed, was dark and still and faintly warm. Steam curled off it. A well steaming in deep winter, deep underground, where something warm breathed.

Their quarters were clean, the bedrolls fresh, a pot of stew already sitting on the communal hearth, meat-rich and fragrant. Other drivers nodded to them, friendly, incurious. Too incurious. Nobody asked where they'd come from. Nobody asked anything. Questions, Zephyrine realized, had gone out of fashion here the way hunger had.

Night came down, and with it the snow, falling into the city and vanishing, drunk, everywhere, silently, and through the narrow window of their cell Zephyrine watched the orchards on the Spire's flank glowing faintly in the dark. Not reflecting light. Emitting it. Blossoms lit from within, hundreds of them, like a constellation brought low and rooted.

Odile sat cross-legged on her bedroll with the prophecy book open on her knees, working by candle stub, hunting the marginalia for whatever came next. Zephyrine didn't ask what she was finding. She wasn't sure she wanted the answer tonight. Instead she lay back and listened—to the city, to the ground beneath the city, reaching out with the sense she usually kept leashed and letting it stretch, carefully, the way you test ice with one foot.

The city was quiet. The people slept soundly, deeply, *helplessly*, their dreams soft and green and provided for. And beneath it all, beneath the wells and the wine cellars and the roots threading the foundations, she found the warmth. Vast. Slow. Patient. It was not asleep. It was not awake either. It was doing the thing between them, the thing seeds do, and it was aware of her the moment she touched it—she felt its attention turn toward her like a flower turning toward sun, gentle and enormous and glad.

*There you are,* it seemed to say, in a voice like sap rising, like spring itself, like everything she had ever been promised and never given. *We dreamed you. We have been dreaming you for a hundred years. Come down. Come home.*

Zephyrine wrenched her senses back, heart slamming, and lay staring at the ceiling until the candle died.

Beside her, Odile had gone still over the book. In the dark, her voice came thin and careful.

"Zephyrine. The marginalia. There's a passage I couldn't read before—the ink's been altered, overwritten, someone hid it. I've been holding the page to the candle." A pause. "It's a map. Of the Spire's undercrofts. And there's a note in the dead scholar's hand, dated the night before he burned. It says: *The roots drink upward. Whatever feeds the gardens is fed in turn—beneath the throne, at the solstice hour, the Gardener comes down to eat.*"

Outside, distantly, sweetly, the bells of the Spire began to mark midnight. Three nights remained until solstice.

And in the orchards on the tower's flank, every blossom, all at once, turned—very slowly, very gently—toward their window.
