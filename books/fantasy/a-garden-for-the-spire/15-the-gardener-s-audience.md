## 15. The Gardener's Audience

The plague cart rattled through the gates of pale stone at dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern wall, Zephyrine had learned that the Spire did not smell like a city.

It smelled like a greenhouse.

Warm earth and green sap and the sweet rot of overripe fruit, thick enough to taste on the back of her tongue. Beneath it ran something older and stranger—a mineral scent, like water moving through deep stone, like the air in caves where nothing had ever lived. She had smelled that scent before. In the deep-folk tunnels. In the root-chambers where the elder had pressed his clawed hand to her wrist and wept.

*Here,* that scent said. *Here is where it grows.*

"Keep your eyes down," Odile murmured beside her on the cart's bench. The archivist wore a gravedigger's smock over her scholar's robes, her gray hair bound up under a hood, her hands—those careful, ink-stained hands—folded around a shovel handle as if she knew how to use one. She did not. "Dead men's carts go to the burning fields without stopping. But there are checkpoints."

Zephyrine kept her eyes down. It was an old skill, one of the first she'd ever learned: how to be furniture, how to be weather, how to be nobody. For twenty-six years it had kept her alive. She was beginning to suspect it would not keep her alive much longer.

The checkpoint was exactly where Odile said it would be, a gate of white timber across the processional road, manned by four guards in Spire livery—green and silver, the colors of a king who had not been seen in public for three years. One of them carried a brazier despite the mild morning, and the coals within burned with a light that was wrong somehow, too green, too steady, casting no heat Zephyrine could feel from ten feet away.

"Pest-cart," the guard said. Not a question. His voice had the flatness of a man reciting.

"From Thornfell," Odile answered, in the accent of the low valleys, flawless. "Three days dead. Winter took them."

"Winter takes everyone this year." The guard leaned close, and Zephyrine felt his gaze pass over her like a hand. She breathed slowly. She thought about salt. About the weight of a salt sack on her shoulders. About the lynx, scarred and proud, watching her from a snowbank with eyes like chips of amber. Ordinary things. Small things. Things that made her no one.

The guard straightened. Something flickered across his face—confusion, maybe, or disappointment—and then he waved them through.

Only when the gate was far behind them did Odile exhale. "He looked for you," she whispered. "I saw him looking. He expected to find someone, and you weren't there."

"I'm very good at not being there."

"You were *too* good." Odile's knuckles were white on the shovel. "That's what frightens me. It knows you're coming, Zephyrine. It has known since before you were born. And still its own guards cannot see you. Do you understand what that means?"

Zephyrine didn't answer. She understood what it might mean. That the thing in the palace wanted her to arrive unseen. That it was clearing the road for her, the way a gardener clears weeds from a row so the seedling can come up unbruised.

She hated how much that sounded like care.

* * *

They abandoned the cart in a yard full of other carts, other drivers, other shrouded burdens, and walked into the city proper on feet gone numb with dread. Zephyrine had imagined the Spire a hundred times—inquisitors in black, pyre-smoke, streets swept clean of anything wild. What she found instead stopped her in the middle of the road until Odile tugged her sleeve.

Orchards.

Pear trees in blossom along the avenues, their petals drifting down through air that should have killed them. Cherry trees heavy with fruit against walls furred with frost. Grapevines climbing the buttresses of the great cathedral, leaves glossy and dark, though every other living thing in the world had gone silent and gray weeks ago. Bees moved among the flowers in slow, drowsy spirals, and their humming filled the streets like a hymn sung underwater.

"It's beautiful," Zephyrine said, and meant it, and hated meaning it.

"It's a mouth," Odile said quietly. "Look closer."

Zephyrine looked. And once she knew to look, she could not stop seeing it: the blossoms were too perfect, each petal identical to its sisters; the fruit hung in rows too even, like beads on a string; the bees never landed, only circled, circling, circling. There were no birds. No sparrows squabbling over fallen fruit, no wrens in the hedges. The trees grew in soil that was black and rich and utterly bare—not a weed, not a worm-cast, not a single living thing that had not been planted on purpose.

Everything here belonged to something. Nothing here was free.

"The roots," Zephyrine said. "Under the foundations."

"Threading the whole city," Odile agreed. "The archive records called it irrigation. Three hundred years of records calling it irrigation." Her laugh came out cracked. "I catalogued those records, Zephyrine. I shelved them. I dusted them. I thought I was keeping knowledge safe, and all the while I was keeping its alibi tidy."

Zephyrine touched the nearest tree, just briefly, palm flat against bark that was warm as skin. And through her gift—the sense she had spent a lifetime hiding, the hearing that was not hearing—she felt what waited underneath.

The tree was not asleep. The tree was not even a tree, not really. It was a fingertip. Somewhere below, vast and patient, something held this whole city the way a hand holds a rosary, feeling each bead.

And the something stirred when she touched it.

A ripple went out through the ground, soft as a heartbeat, soft as recognition. Down every avenue, every blossom trembled. Every bee paused mid-spiral. For one long moment the entire city held its breath—

—and then resumed, as if nothing had happened, as if a seedling had merely shifted in its sleep.

Odile had gone the color of old paper. "What did you do?"

"Nothing." Zephyrine pulled her hand back and wiped it on her coat as if the warmth could be wiped away. "I said hello."

"Never say hello to it again."

* * *

They spent the day in the shadow of the cathedral's outer wall, in a mortuary cloister where the winter dead lay in rows under shrouds, waiting for burning. It was Odile's idea—if anyone asked, they were mourners, and mourners had business in a place like this that no one wanted to examine. Zephyrine sat with her back against cold stone and watched the light change on the blossoms beyond the cloister arch, and tried to make a plan, and failed.

Every plan ended the same way: walk into the throne room, find the king, find whatever bound him, and pray that two smugglers' tricks and a dead scholar's book were enough. It was not a plan. It was a prayer wearing a plan's clothes.

"There will be guards," she said at last. "There will be courtiers. There will be—" She gestured vaguely at the impossible orchards. "*Gardeners.*"

"Yes."

"And none of them can be trusted."

"No."

"So we walk in blind, into the heart of the thing that has been dreaming me since before my mother met my father, and we hope it likes us."

Odile was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice was very low. "You keep saying *it*. The entity. The Verdant King. But Zephyrine—it wears Aldric's face. Aldric is still in there somewhere. Chapter after chapter of the old records say the binding holds best when the host fights least. Which means some part of him has been fighting for years, alone, in the dark." She looked at her hands. "We are not walking in to kill a monster. We're walking in to find a prisoner."

"A prisoner who smiles," Zephyrine said. "With its mouth."

"With *his* mouth," Odile said. "That's the horror of it. Try to remember which is which."

* * *

They came for her at dusk.

Not guards—she almost wished they'd been guards. They were gardeners, six of them, men and women in undyed wool with soil-blackened hands and faces as smooth and calm as eggs. They did not carry weapons. They did not speak. They simply appeared at the mouth of the cloister, arranged themselves in a loose crescent, and waited, the way you wait for a child to finish a game it cannot win.

Odile stood. "No. Not yet—we need another day, I haven't found the archive entrance—"

One of the gardeners turned his head toward her. Just his head. The rest of him stayed perfectly still, and the motion was wrong in a way Zephyrine felt in her teeth, like watching a puppet turn to look at something behind it.

"Lady Vasse," the gardener said. His voice was gentle, unhurried, warm as compost. "The King remembers your service to the archives. Your rooms have been kept ready these three years. You will be escorted home."

Odile's face did something complicated—fear and fury and, worst of all, a terrible homesick longing, there and gone. "My rooms."

"Nothing in the Spire is ever lost," the gardener said. "Nothing is ever thrown away. Everything is kept." He turned back to Zephyrine, and now all six of them were looking at her, and the warmth in his voice deepened into something that made her want to run and made her want, horribly, to step forward. "And you. Little winter-born. He has asked for you by name. Not the name in the book. The name your mother used when you were small and feverish and afraid of thunder."

Zephyrine's blood went cold. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," the gardener agreed pleasantly. "But he does. Come. He dislikes waiting past dusk. He says the light does such lovely things to the orchards."

Every instinct she owned screamed at her to run—to call the beasts, to bolt for the wall, to vanish into the one skill that had kept her breathing for twenty-six years. But running led nowhere anymore. Every road out of Thornfell was watched; every road inside the Spire was grown. There was only forward, and forward led through a garden to a throne.

She stood up. Brushed off her coat. Looked at Odile, whose eyes were bright and wet and furious.

"Find the letter," Zephyrine said quietly. "If Aldric left anything hidden—find it. Don't follow me. Whatever you hear, whatever they tell you—don't follow."

"Zephyrine—"

"*Don't.*" And then, softer, because it was true and because they might both die before morning: "You got me this far, Lady Vasse. Farther than anyone. Let me do the last part alone."

The gardeners closed around her, gentle as falling petals, and led her away through the blossoms, and Odile did not follow, because Zephyrine had asked, and because some part of her already knew that following would have changed nothing at all.

* * *

The throne room had no throne.

That was the first thing she understood, standing in the doorway while the gardeners withdrew behind her like a tide going out. The hall was vast and vaulted and lit by a thousand candles that burned green-gold, and at its far end, where a throne should have stood on its dais, there was instead a tree.

An enormous pear tree, silver-barked, in full bloom, growing straight up through the marble floor as though the floor were a field. Its roots bulged beneath the stones in slow ridges, like veins under skin, and its branches spread across the whole ceiling, and from its branches hung fruit of gold and green and one deep, bruised purple that Zephyrine's eyes kept sliding away from. Petals fell and fell and never accumulated; they vanished before they touched the ground, the way snow vanishes into deep water.

Beneath the tree, in a simple chair of woven willow, sat a man.

He was younger than she expected. That was her first stupid thought—younger, and tired, and handsome in the worn way of a man who has been ill a long time and is finally having a good day. Dark hair going gray at the temples. A face built for kindness, with lines around the mouth that suggested laughter used often, once. He wore no crown. He wore plain wool, undyed, the same as the gardeners, and his hands lay open on his knees, palms up, the way you sit when you have nothing left to hide.

King Aldric. Or the thing wearing him.

"Zephyrine Galloway," he said.

Her name in his mouth sounded like her name in her mother's mouth. That was the trap of it, she realized later—that was the whole trap, right there, in the first three seconds. He said it the way you say the name of someone you have loved a long time.

"That's not my name," she said.

"No," he agreed, smiling. "Your true name is written in a book in a dead man's cache, and your mother's name for you died with her, and the name you use for smuggling is a coat you wear. So many names. I gave you the first one, you know. Before your birth. I dreamed it into the world, and the world grew it, and here you are." He tilted his head, studying her with an expression of frank, delighted wonder, the way she herself studied a rare animal at a salt-lick. "Do you know how long I have waited to see you walk through that door? Ninety-one years. I began dreaming you the winter your grandmother was a girl. I dreamed the storm that brought your parents together. I dreamed the midwife late, so that you'd be born at moonset under the winter sign. I have been dreaming you, little speaker, since before there was a you to dream."

"Then you dreamed wrong," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Because I'm not what you planted."

"Oh, but you are." He rose from the willow chair, and the movement was fluid and wrong, a marionette's grace, and yet his face remained kind, remained *warm*, and that mismatch was worse than any snarl. "You are exactly what I planted. You've simply grown wild. Do you know how rare that is? How precious?" He took a step toward her, and the petals fell faster, eager. "Everything else I grow comes up obedient. Straight rows. Even fruit. But you—" he laughed, and the laugh was Aldric's laugh, genuine and rueful, a real man's laugh, and she hated him for lending it, "—you came up crooked and stubborn and *free*, and I have watched you for twenty-six years the way a gardener watches a rose defy the trellis. The avalanche. The lynx. The way you speak to beasts as equals, never as tools—I never taught you that. I couldn't have. It's better than I dreamed."

"Stop." The word came out rougher than she intended.

"Stop?" He spread his hands. "I'm praising you."

"You're *farming* me." Her heart was slamming now, and beneath the fear she felt the other thing rising, the thing she kept leashed, the hearing-that-was-not-hearing, straining toward him like a dog toward its master, and she clamped down on it hard. "Everything you just said—every word—is a man describing his crop. I'm not yours. I was never yours. My mother named me. My mother, not you."

Something moved behind his eyes then—something quick and desperate and drowning—and was gone so fast she might have imagined it. Might have. She was beginning to understand that she would ask herself *might have* about this man for the rest of her life.

"Your mother," he said gently, "named you what I dreamed her naming you. That is not a cage, Zephyrine. That is harmony. Listen—" and he stepped closer, close enough that she could smell him, warm earth and green sap and that deep mineral scent, but also, underneath, faintly, human sweat and woodsmoke, a *man*, "—you have spent your whole life hiding. Cold passes. Stolen salt. Sleeping in ruins, speaking to animals because animals were the only ones who wouldn't report you. You have been alone every single day of your life, and you are so tired. I know how tired you are. I have felt it through the roots every night for twenty-six years."

"Then you know my answer."

"I know you haven't given it yet." His voice dropped, went soft, went tender, and the horrible thing—the unbearable thing—was that it wasn't a performance. Some part of him, some part of *it*, meant every word. "Stay. Walk these gardens. Eat fruit that never rots. Never hide again—there is nothing here you need to hide *from*, because everything here already belongs, and so would you. Belonging, Zephyrine. Purpose. A world where the winters end and the harvests never fail and no one burns beast-speakers, because there will be no more beasts—only gardens, and gardeners, and peace." He smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, and his eyes were wet. "I am offering you a home. When has anyone ever offered you a home?"

And the terrible truth, the truth that made her fists shake at her sides, was that some hollowed-out chamber in her chest *leaned toward it*. Twenty-six years of competent solitude, of rules and aliases and caches buried under cairns, and here was a voice saying *you never have to be nobody again*, and the chamber echoed like a struck bell.

She thought of the lynx. Of the ibex herd dying in the high passes, fleeing something beneath the snow. Of the villages on the edge of starvation, eating boot leather and boiled bark. Of the deep-folk elder's clawed hand trembling on her wrist. Of the previous winter-born speaker, and what remained of her afterward.

Of Odile, somewhere above, searching for a dead man's letter, disobeying her out of love.

"No," Zephyrine said.

The word fell into the hall like a stone into a well.

"No," she said again, louder, and reached for the thing she had never dared reach for at full strength—not the gift, not the hearing, but the *speaking*, the deep-folk words the elder had taught her, the words that unraveled. "You don't offer homes. You offer *rows*. Even fruit. Obedient trees. Nothing in your garden is free, and I have been free my whole life, and it has cost me everything, and it was worth it—"

"Zephyrine." His voice cracked, and for one instant—for one naked, staggering instant—she saw him. Not the Verdant King. *Aldric.* A man drowning behind his own smiling face, eyes wide with terror and hope, mouthing words she couldn't hear: *run, don't save me if it costs you yourself, run—*

And then the smile closed over the drowning like water, and the Verdant King sighed, and shook his head with genuine sorrow, and said:

"Then we'll do it the long way. The solstice is in three days, little speaker. Roots grow faster than you think." He settled back into the willow chair beneath the blooming tree, palms open, patient as soil. "Take her to the guest wing. The room with the pear tree outside the window—the one I dreamed for her. She'll be comfortable. She'll be *kept*."

The gardeners came forward, gentle as falling petals.

And as they took her arms—as she was led away through the green-gold candlelight, past the tree with its roots bulging under the floor like veins—Zephyrine heard it, faint and rising from everywhere at once, from the foundations, from the deep places, from the whole hungry city:

The roots, singing.

And beneath the singing, so faint she might have dreamed it, a man's voice, hoarse and small and impossibly far away, counting the hours until solstice.
