## 16. Rootbound

The Spire smelled of green things.

That was the first obscenity of it, Odile thought as they marched her down corridors of pale stone—green things, growing things, the wet sweet rot of orchards in full fruit while outside the windows the blizzard howled like something starved. She had spent thirty years in these archives before her fall. She remembered them smelling of dust and lamp-oil and vellum, the honest smell of paper keeping its small vigil against time. Now the air was thick with blossom, with sap, with a sweetness so concentrated it curdled at the back of the throat into something almost rotten. Beauty laid on too thick, like honey over meat that had gone off.

They had taken her at the archive door itself. She had worn the grey robe of a lesser clerk, had forged the seal, had memorized three separate cover stories—and it had not mattered at all, because the roots had known her. She had felt them, threading through the Spire's foundations the way veins thread a leaf, and she had felt the moment one of them brushed her mind and *recognized* her: the archivist who burned, the scholar who read too deeply, the woman whose marginalia the Verdant King had once circled in red ink and called *promising*.

Four guards now, silent as furniture, walking her toward whatever the court did with inconvenient scholars. Her hands were bound in front of her with living vine—not rope, she noted with clinical horror; actual vine, its tendrils idly exploring her wrists, tasting her pulse. It tightened when she walked too fast. It loosened, almost tenderly, when she wept a little, which she hated herself for.

They put her in a room instead of a cell.

It was a reading room—she knew it, had dozed in it as a girl-archivist, had kissed a boy behind the atlas cabinets in it once, long before either of their lives went to ruin. The shelves were still there. The books were still there, spines straight, leather oiled. But every window was curtained in ivy, and from the walls, from between the stones themselves, pale rootlets had emerged like the white fingers of drowned things, curling across the floorboards, up the shelving, into the very books. When she looked closer—she couldn't help looking closer—she saw that the roots had grown *into* the volumes, splitting spines, fanning through pages. The books were being eaten. Or perhaps being read. With this thing, she was no longer certain there was a difference.

The guards left. The vine on her wrists slithered away into a drain-grate, and the door did not open, but neither did it lock. There was no need. Where would she go? The whole building was one organism now, and she was inside its body.

She sat down on the floor among the invaded books, because her legs had stopped negotiating, and began, methodically, to search for what she had come to find.

Aldric's letter. If it existed. If he had been able to write it. If any part of the man still surfaced often enough, clearly enough, to leave a message in a palace where the walls themselves had ears and probably appetites.

She worked through the nearest shelf first. The roots made it slow going—she had to lift each book free of the pale filaments without tearing pages, and some volumes came away with a sound like a sigh, or a suckle, and she set those down quickly and wiped her hands on her robe. Histories. Tax ledgers. A treatise on falconry with roots coiled through it like a fist around a heart. Nothing hidden in any of them, though she checked the bindings, the hollow spines, the false backs that archivists had used for contraband since before the Spire had a name.

Hours passed. The light through the ivy-curtained windows went from grey to gold to grey again, though whether that was evening or merely the weather she could not say. Her stomach complained. She ignored it, the way you ignore anything when the alternative is thinking about Zephyrine somewhere above you in this green-lit mausoleum, alone, walking into the audience she had insisted on walking into.

*I'll get us both out,* Zephyrine had said, in the plague-cart, under the reek of the dead men's clothing they wore. *I've gotten out of worse.*

Odile had believed her then. Believing Zephyrine Galloway was easy; it was like believing winter, or gravity. But belief was not a plan, and plans were not safety, and the last thing Odile had seen before the roots took her was Zephyrine's face turning calm and strange in the torchlight—the look of a woman who had already decided something and was simply waiting for the world to catch up.

She should have recognized that look. She had spent her career reading prophecy, and prophecy was nothing but people deciding things in advance and calling it fate.

* * *

The courtier came at what might have been midnight.

He entered without ceremony—the door simply opened and he was there, a thin man in court green, silver-grey at the temples, moving with the careful grace of someone carrying water brimful to the lip. He closed the door behind him and stood a moment with his eyes shut, breathing, like a man coming up from underwater. Then he opened his eyes and said, in a voice sanded smooth by years of court whisper:

"You're the Vasse woman."

"Formerly." Odile got to her feet slowly, joints cracking. "There is no more Vasse. I'm nobody. I drive a cart."

"You drove a cart of corpses into my king's city," he said. "Nobody does that. Nobody but somebody." He crossed the room, stepping over the roots without looking at them—he didn't have to look, she realized; his feet knew where they were the way a man knows his own scars—and lifted a fallen volume from the floor, cradling it. "You've been handling the collection roughly. They feel that."

"The books feel pain."

"Everything rooted feels pain." He said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "My lord the King feels everything the garden feels. Every book opened. Every branch pruned. Every—" His mouth worked. He mastered himself. "You were an archivist here. You'll know Lord Halden's cipher. The old one, the one they retired after the Schism."

Odile went very still. "Why would you ask me that?"

"Because there is a letter," the courtier said, "in Lord Halden's cipher, hidden in this room, that has been waiting eleven years for someone who could read it. And I cannot read it, and the garden must never know it exists, and you are the only person to walk through that door since the binding deepened who might be able to." He turned to face her fully, and in the low light she saw what the court green concealed: the pallor beneath, the fine trembling of his hands, the way the skin at his throat showed the faint tracery of veins gone dark as bark. Half-bound. Further along than half. Another year, perhaps two, and there would be nothing left in him to bargain with anyone.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"Serren Vail. Master of the Household, once. Now—" a thin smile, "—a gardener's assistant." He set the book down with exaggerated gentleness. "The letter is under the atlas stand. Third stone from the hearth. The cipher key is in your head, if you're truly who the archives said you were. Read it. Burn it after. And then tell me, Archivist—" his voice cracked, just slightly, on the title, and she understood that he had been waiting eleven years to say it to someone, anyone, "—tell me if there is anything in it worth having waited for."

* * *

The third stone from the hearth came free easier than it should have; Serren's fingers, she suspected, had been loosening it for years, a fraction at a time, in the way of prisoners digging with teaspoons. Beneath it lay an oilcloth packet, and within it a sheaf of papers written in a hand that shook and steadied and shook again, encoded in the old Halden cipher that Odile had learned at nineteen from a tutor who had later hanged himself, and had never forgotten because forgetting was a luxury archivists were not permitted.

She sat by the cold hearth and decoded by touch, tracing the symbols in the near-dark, translating in her head the way other people breathed.

*To whoever finds this, and God help you, I hope it is soon and I fear it will be late.*

Her breath caught. She knew the handwriting. Not this hand—but the hand beneath it, the ghost-hand visible where the writer's control lapsed: the young Aldric's looping capitals, the ones she had catalogued a hundred times in the royal correspondence of twenty years ago.

*I am writing this in the hour after dawn because dawn is when I am most myself. It gets less every year. In winter I barely surface at all. If you are reading this, I have likely not surfaced in a long time, and I want whoever you are to know that I know. That is the worst of it, I think—not the thing itself but knowing, from underneath, watching my own hands sign what it wants signed, smiling my own smile at what it loves. It is patient. It has never once been cruel to me. That is how I know what it is. Cruelty is what hunger does when it is hurried.*

*What follows is instruction. I beg you to treat it as such and not as the rambling of a drowning man, though I am a drowning man, and we drown slowly here.*

*The thing wearing my crown calls itself the Verdant King. It is older than the mountain. It was bound below the throne by the founders' working, and the binding was fed by anchor-souls—people bound willingly, who gave their memory and love as fuel. The last anchor died a century ago and the binding has been starving since. It found me young. It did not take me all at once. It offered. That is important—write this down, make them understand—it OFFERED. Harvests without end. Spring in the courts of winter. My father dying slowly of a wasting sickness and me a boy of fifteen saying yes, yes, anything, let him live. My father lived nine more years. I have counted them as worth it every day since, and I would count them worth it again, and that is why I know I can no longer trust my own judgment, because a prisoner who loves his cell is past saving by ordinary means.*

*The unbinding requires an anchor. A willing one. The working cannot be forced from outside—the roots only loosen for a soul that offers itself in the old one's place. Memory is the coin. All of it. Everything the anchor loves, poured into the roots to feed the spring, so the thing lets go of me and sleeps another century fed. I have searched for another way for eleven years. There is none. I have looked for someone foolish enough to volunteer and prayed nightly that I would never find them.*

*So here is my plea, and it is the only truly mine thing left in me: DO NOT SAVE ME AT THAT PRICE. Whoever you are—if you love me, if you love this kingdom, if you simply stumbled in here by accident—let me sleep. Let the winter come. Better a kingdom buried honestly than a stranger emptied to warm my grave. I have been a vessel for eleven years. Do not make another one on my account. Whatever mercy exists in this green hell, let it be the mercy of leaving me be.*

*If someone comes anyway—if some fool comes anyway—then tell them this from Aldric, the real one, the one underneath: thank you, and forgive me, and when you cannot remember why you came, remember only that it mattered. That is not nothing. I have watched it take everything from me and I am telling you with the last clear piece of my mind: giving is not the same as losing. What you give freely cannot be taken. Remember that. Write it on your bones. Remember that—*

The rest dissolved into shaking ink, into repetitions, into a line written over and over until it tore the page: *remember that, remember that, remember that.*

Odile sat in the dark with the letter in her lap and felt the truth arrive the way avalanches arrive—not gradually at all, but all at once, with the ground already moving beneath her.

An anchor. A willing anchor. Memory as coin.

And Zephyrine, in the plague cart, saying *I'll handle it*, saying *you get yourself ready and don't ask questions*, saying—with that calm, settled, terrible face—*some prices you pay better if you pay them alone.*

"Oh," Odile said aloud, to the empty green room. "Oh, you wretched, brave, stupid girl."

Zephyrine had known. Had known since the deep-folk elder spoke of the failed speaker, since the marginalia, since the ford—maybe since the waystation, maybe since the day she was born under the winter sign. She had known what the unbinding cost and she had told no one, and she was upstairs right now, or beneath the throne right now, arranging herself like a coin on a counting-table, preparing to pour out her mother's voice and the lynx and every road she ever loved into the hungry roots of a sleeping god—to buy the life of a king she'd never met, for people who'd have burned her for the gift in her blood, and to do it without even the dignity of being mourned by name, because the woman they'd mourn would no longer exist to be mourned.

Love, Odile thought. This was what love looked like when it grew wild: not sonnets. Disobedience. Refusing, absolutely refusing, to let the people who cared about you have a vote.

She had argued with Zephyrine about the refugees. Had stood in the snow and called her selfish for choosing the Spire. And all the while Zephyrine had been carrying this—had known that choosing the Spire meant ceasing, and had chosen it anyway, and let Odile rail at her because Odile needed someone to rail at and Zephyrine had never once, not once, asked to be forgiven in advance.

The courtier was watching her from the doorway. He had not moved in hours. Roots had crept up around his ankles while he stood, and he had not shaken them off, and Odile understood with a lurch that he hadn't noticed—that the garden drank from him slowly, constantly, the way soil drinks rain, and he had stopped minding.

"Well?" His voice was ash. "Is there anything in it?"

"Yes." She folded the pages along their old creases. "There's a way to save him. And a price, and the price is exactly what you fear it is."

Serren Vail closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he smiled like a man hearing his own death sentence read in a language he loved. "Then it's already begun, isn't it. Someone's come to pay it."

"I intend to stop her," Odile said, and heard her own voice go hard and bright as a blade drawn in a dark room. "Or help her. I haven't decided. Possibly both. Possibly I'll decide in the next quarter-hour and change my mind twice."

"That sounds like the archives," Serren said faintly. "That sounds exactly like the archives." He stepped aside from the door. Behind him the corridor ran green-lit and root-threaded toward the heart of the Spire, and far below, Odile could feel it now, a hum in the stones, a pressure like a held breath—the solstice threshold gathering itself, the working waking, the roots beginning, somewhere deep beneath the throne, to open like a throat.

"She'll descend at the turning of the night," Serren said quietly. "The garden always feeds at the turning. You have until then."

Odile was already moving, the letter pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, running through the blooming dark toward the only person in the world she could not bear to lose and could not, now, bear to save.
