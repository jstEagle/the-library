## 18. A King, Unbound

The root-chamber breathed.

Zephyrine felt it before she saw it—the slow tidal pull of the air, in and out, in and out, as though the whole mountain beneath the throne were a chest rising and falling. The walls were not stone here. They had been stone once; she could see the pale Spire masonry ghosting through what had grown over it, but a century of roots had swallowed the work of masons and made of it something organic and waiting. Great braided columns of wood descended from the dark above her, thick as watchtowers, their bark split with seams that glowed a faint sickly green. The light came from within them. It pulsed with the breathing.

And at the center, where the roots converged into a single knot the size of a chapel, King Aldric hung.

Not sat. Not lay. *Hung*—suspended in the cradle of the great root, his back against its heartwood, his arms slack, his head tipped forward as though he had fallen asleep mid-prayer a very long time ago and no one had thought to wake him. Vines crossed his wrists, his throat, his brow—not binding him, she understood suddenly, not exactly. *Feeding* from him. Thin filaments ran from the vines into his skin, vanishing beneath, and where they entered, the flesh was green-veined, luminous, like leaves held up to sun.

He was thinner than any coin-portrait of him. His hair had gone half white though he could not be past forty. But his face—his face was still a king's face, patient and worn, and even unconscious it carried an expression she recognized from the beasts she had healed: the look of something enduring pain so long it had become weather.

"Zephyrine."

Odile's voice, behind her, hushed to almost nothing. Zephyrine did not turn. She had left Odile at the chamber's mouth with instructions neither of them had believed would be obeyed, and sure enough the scholar's footsteps came soft across the root-floor until she stood at Zephyrine's shoulder, breath ragged, one hand pressed to the gash on her arm where the courtier's knife had found her.

"You shouldn't be here," Zephyrine said. Her own voice sounded strange to her. Far away. The chamber pulled at sound the way it pulled at everything.

"And yet." Odile looked up at the hanging king, and Zephyrine heard the scholar's breath catch. "Gods below. He's still in there. I spent ten years telling myself he was gone—that whatever wore his face wore all of him. But he's *there*."

"I know." She did know. She could feel him the way she felt every living thing in this mountain: a small hot coal buried deep in wet green dark, banked but not out. Waiting for someone to breathe on it.

The entity knew she was there too. Of course it did. The whole chamber had begun to lean toward her, subtly, the way wheat leans toward the last light of day. The glow in the root-seams brightened by degrees. Somewhere far above, through leagues of stone and the false orchards and the blooming blizzard-bright courts, she felt the Verdant King smile.

*You came,* said a voice that was not a voice, that arrived not through her ears but through the marrow of her, warm as loam, patient as winter. *I dreamed you before your mother dreamed you. I have been dreaming you for a hundred years, little anchor. Come. You need not fight me. There is nothing here to fight.*

"I brought the words," Zephyrine said aloud, because speaking aloud mattered, because her own voice in her own ears was a rope she could hold. She touched the pouch at her belt where the deep-folk elder had pressed the bone tablets into her hands, murmuring the old syllables against her knuckles like a blessing or a warning. "The unbinding words. The ones your roots grew around and couldn't digest."

The warmth in her marrow cooled by one degree. Only one. The patience held.

*Words are wind,* it said. *I am a hundred years of spring.*

"We'll see," Zephyrine said, and stepped forward into the breathing dark.

* * *

The first tablet cracked in her hands like ice on a pond in early thaw, and the sound went through the chamber the way a scream goes through a flock of birds.

She spoke the syllables the elder had taught her—words with no trade in any human tongue, words the deep-folk had carried down through the last long winter like embers cupped in bare palms—and where they fell, the green light guttered. The great root shuddered. Filaments along the walls spasmed and withdrew into the wood like worms retreating from salt.

The entity stopped pretending to be warm.

*COLD,* it said, and the word was a pressure that bent her spine, that filled the chamber floor to unseen ceiling, that shook dust from the root-columns in fine green snow. *YOU WERE PLANTED TO KEEP ME. A HUNDRED YEARS I WAITED IN YOUR BLOODLINE, TENDING, TENDING, AND YOU COME WITH COLD AND OLD WORDS TO UNMAKE THE ONLY SPRING THIS WORLD WILL EVER—*

"You're not spring," Zephyrine said. Her voice cracked and she let it crack. "Spring doesn't eat its garden."

She spoke the second line.

The king gasped.

It was a small sound in all that vastness—a man's breath dragged in through teeth—but it went through Zephyrine like a spear. Above her, Aldric's head lifted. His eyes opened, and they were human eyes, brown and bloodshot and *terrified*, and they found her across the chamber with the desperate clarity of a drowning man finding a single star.

"*Run,*" he rasped. His voice was rust and disuse. "*Girl—whatever it promised—it lies—it will give you anything, it will show you anything—run while you still remember why—*"

"I'm not running," Zephyrine said, and spoke the third line.

The world tore.

There was no other word for it. The great root split along its length with a sound like a forest falling in slow motion, and the entity came out of it—not as smoke, not as serpent, but as *everything*: a flood of green light and growing things, a century of stolen seasons bursting from the wound, blossoms and briars and the smell of turned earth and rotting fruit all at once, filling the chamber, filling her, pouring against her skin like a river against a standing stone.

*TAKE IT BACK,* it screamed, and then, instantly, sweetly, terribly: *Zephyrine. Zephyrine Galloway. Listen to me. Your mother sang to you—you were four, there was fever, she sang the smuggling song about the girl who outran the tide—let me give her back to you, you barely remember her voice, let me—*

"Stop." Her knees hit the root-floor. The flood pressed against her, into her, seeking cracks, and oh, there were cracks, there had always been cracks—

*The lynx,* it whispered. *The scarred one. You fed it marrow from your own hand when it was dying. It remembers your smell the way you remember your name. I can make that memory live forever instead of dying slowly in you—I can keep them ALL, everyone you ever—*

"It's lying," Odile shouted from somewhere, from very far away. "Zeph, it's LYING, don't listen—"

"It's not lying," Zephyrine said, and tasted blood. "That's the worst part. It never lies."

She raised the fourth tablet.

But the entity was faster than grief. The flood gathered itself, coiled, and struck—not at her body, at the coal inside her, at the small hot stubborn thing that was Zephyrine Galloway—and for one endless moment she was nowhere, she was being *unmade*, she was a name dissolving in warm water—

And then she understood.

The severing demanded payment. The elder had said it plainly, and she had nodded, and she had known, and she had told no one. A binding a century deep could not be torn for free. Something of equal weight had to fill the space the entity left, or the space would simply close around the king again, or worse, around *her*. The roots were dying now, all through the mountain, the false orchards browning above her, the whole vast hungry system starving for want of its host—and they needed feeding. The unbinding needed *feeding*.

Life, the elder had said. Given freely. That was the coin.

Zephyrine had assumed it meant her life. She had made her peace with that, walking down through the green courts with a plague-cart's stink on her clothes. She could pay death; death was easy; death was just the last road out of Thornfell and she'd walked every road there was.

But kneeling in the flood of the entity's screaming, feeling it claw at the coal of her, she understood the true price, and it was worse, and it was also the only door.

Not her life. Her *loves*. Every memory of everyone she had ever loved, poured out into the dying roots to feed the spring that would follow. That was the equal weight. That was what a century of binding cost. Because the entity had bound Aldric with belonging—with the promise of harvest, of home, of hands in the soil beside other hands—and only belonging given away could unmake it.

*No,* said the entity, and for the first time its voice was afraid. *No, you cannot, those are MINE, I grew them, I tended them in you—*

"They were never yours," Zephyrine said.

She opened herself.

* * *

She gave them the lynx first, because it seemed right to start there.

The scarred snow-lynx, half-dead in the rocks above Highmere, its ribs a ladder of bone under filthy fur, its one good eye tracking her hand as she offered marrow and then meat and then, over three nights, her own warmth. The sound it made when it finally slept against her side. The weight of it. The trust of a wild thing, which is the rarest currency in the world, and which she had earned once and never spent.

The roots drank it. All through the chamber the dead green flushed faintly gold, and the gold spread outward, up through the mountain, and somewhere far above she knew the false orchards were letting go of their impossible fruit.

Her mother next. The fever-song. Rough hands that smelled of tar and salt. The last time she'd seen her, at the trailhead, saying *don't look back, Zeph, looking back is how they catch you.* She gave the memory whole, refusing to flinch from any part of it, and the roots drank, and the gold spread further, and the flood around her began to thin.

Then the others, faster now, because the payment had momentum, because giving away love turns out to be like bleeding—one wound opens all the rest:

Odile, sharp-tongued and terrified and brave, arguing maps by candlelight. The crow landing on her wrist with the Spire seal. The deep-folk elder's gnarled hands pressing bone tablets into hers. The villagers of Highmere, the children running beside her cart. The ibex herd on the high pass, their ancient eyes. Every road. Every cache. Every night alone under stars that she had talked to because talking to stars was safer than talking to people, and the stars had listened, and that too was a kind of love, and she paid that too.

She paid and paid and paid, and the entity screamed and thinned and screamed again, tearing loose from Aldric strand by strand, each strand bought with a face, a voice, a name—and she kept speaking the words, she realized distantly, her mouth moving through the fifth and sixth lines without her mind's help, some deeper Zephyrine holding the rope while the rest of her poured out.

The last thing to go was not a person. It was the knowledge of what she was doing. She felt it lift from her like a coat handed off to a friend, and her final coherent thought was not fear, and not regret, but a strange clear gratitude—*whoever I am after this, I hope she knows I chose it*—

And then the roots blazed gold from end to end, the entity came out of King Aldric like a splinter drawn from a wound, shrieking down into nothing, into cold, into the honest dark where growing things sleep in winter—

And the chamber went quiet.

* * *

Silence, after that, was enormous.

Zephyrine knelt on the root-floor among drifting motes of golden light, and the light was fading now, sinking into the wood, going wherever paid debts go. The great root had split and blackened and was already, incredibly, beginning to soften into ordinary dead timber. Real timber. Timber that would rot honestly and feed beetles and fall down someday and not be missed.

Aldric fell.

She caught him—or her body did, some trained reflex of a woman used to catching stumbling animals—and lowered him gently to the floor. He weighed almost nothing. Up close, the green veins were fading from his skin, leaving him gray and human and trembling.

His eyes opened. Brown. Human. Wet.

"Is it—" His voice failed. He tried again. "Is it gone?"

"It's gone," Zephyrine said.

She meant to say more. She searched for more and found a strange smooth emptiness where more should have been. She knew this man was a king. She knew he had been bound. She knew, in the flat factual way one knows the contents of a book one hasn't read, that these things mattered enormously. But when she reached for why they mattered to *her*—for the road that had brought her here, for the faces that should have been attached to this moment—she found only clean white quiet, like fresh snow over a field.

Aldric was crying. Quietly, the way kings probably learn to cry, with no sound and no permission. His hand rose and closed around her wrist—gently, so gently, as if she might be another dream the thing had shown him.

"You paid," he whispered. "Oh, gods. I felt it. I felt what it took. Girl—*child*—what did it take from you?"

Zephyrine considered the question with genuine care, the way she might consider whether a pass was safe to cross.

"I don't know," she said honestly. And then, because the stranger weeping on the floor seemed to need it, she smiled at him politely. "But I think it will grow back. Things do. In spring."

Something in his face broke anew at that—he turned his head aside, shoulders shaking—and she patted his shoulder twice, awkwardly, kindly, the way she supposed one comforted strangers, and wondered distantly why the gesture felt like something she had done ten thousand times before, and to whom.

Footsteps. Odile, limping into the golden-fading light, her face doing several things at once—joy and horror and a grief so large it had nowhere to put itself. She stopped three paces away. Her mouth opened. Closed.

"Hello," Zephyrine said politely. "Are you hurt? You're bleeding."

Odile made a sound that was half laugh and entirely sob. "It's nothing," she managed. "It's—an old wound. Older than tonight."

"You should have someone look at it," Zephyrine advised. She looked around the ruined chamber, at the dying roots, at the weeping king, at the scholar with the old wound, and felt the strangest sensation: that she was standing in the middle of a story whose ending had been cut out, holding the blank page.

Above them, far above, through leagues of stone, something shifted. The pressure of the long winter—the siege-snow, the upward-falling white, the silence on the forests—stuttered.

Paused.

And then, softly, like a held breath released after a hundred years, it began to thaw.
