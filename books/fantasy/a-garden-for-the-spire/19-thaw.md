## 19. Thaw

The winter broke on a morning so quiet that the silence itself seemed to crack.

Odile Vasse was awake when it happened—she had been awake most nights since the solstice, sleeping in fragments, listening to the Spire groan around her like a ship taking water. She stood at the window of the guest chamber they had been given in the palace, the one with the leaded glass and the view of the inner court, and she watched the false orchards die.

They had bloomed through the whole of the siege. Apple and plum and pear, flowering in blizzard-light, their branches heavy with fruit that no one dared eat—the Verdant King's bounty, fed by roots that threaded the palace foundations like veins through a body. For months they had been beautiful the way a lure is beautiful. Now, as the first true dawn of the thaw came up gray and honest over the mountains, the blossoms browned before her eyes.

It happened fast. Faster than rot had any right to move. One moment the orchards stood in full and terrible flower; the next, petals curled and blackened, fruit split and slumped from its stems, and the sweet smell of them turned to something closer to a bog at midsummer. The roots beneath the foundations were dying too—she could feel it in the way the stones had begun to settle, a faint continuous settling, like a house exhaling after holding its breath for a hundred years.

*Honest rot,* Odile thought, and found she could not look away. *At last.*

Below, in the courtyard, King Aldric stood alone in the wreckage of his own captivity, and he was weeping.

She watched him for a long time. He did not wipe his face or make any attempt at dignity; he simply stood among the blackened trees with his crown in his hands—he had taken it off, she realized, actually taken it off, the first time in living memory—and let himself grieve. For what, she could not say. For the years hollowed out of him. For the harvests that had never come. For the people who had starved at the edges of his kingdom while something wearing his smile promised them spring.

When she went down to him, he did not startle. He had learned, in the weeks since the unbinding, to hear footsteps as footsteps rather than omens.

"Archivist," he said. His voice was rough, underused. "Or should I say Vasse. I'm told I owe you my mind."

"You owe Zephyrine," Odile said. "We only carried the lantern."

He looked at the dead orchard. "I remember her," he said slowly, "the way you remember a room you woke up in once, as a child. Warmth. A voice speaking words I didn't know, down in the dark where the roots were. And then—" His hand closed briefly, helplessly. "Then nothing. Then morning."

"She paid for your morning," Odile said. "You should know what it cost."

"I intend to find out." He turned to her, and for the first time she saw the man the entity had worn like a glove—a tired man, forty years old, with gray coming in early at his temples and eyes that flinched from green things. "Every day of it. But not today. Today I want to see her."

---

Zephyrine Galloway sat in the infirmary garden with her hands in her lap and no idea whose garden it was.

She knew her own name—that much had survived, a single plank of a wrecked ship. She knew she was twenty-six years old, that she had been born somewhere cold, that her body remembered things her mind did not: how to walk silently on scree, how to read weather in the ache of an old break in her wrist, how to sit perfectly still for long stretches of time, which made the sparrows come close enough to touch. She did not know why she knew these things. They simply lived in her hands, like songs whose words had been lost but whose tunes remained.

The garden was wrong. She had known that within moments of being carried into it, three days ago, though she could not have said how. Everything in it grew too fast and too green, and now, this morning, it was all dying at once, and some deep animal part of her found the dying *relieving*, the way a fever breaking is relieving.

A man came across the grass toward her. Tall, travel-worn despite fine clothes, moving carefully, as if the ground might be listening. Behind him, at a slower pace, came a small round woman with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who had spent decades apologizing for existing in rooms full of important books.

"You're awake," the man said. He stopped a few feet away, the way one approaches a strange dog. "I'm Aldric. This is Odile."

"Zephyrine." She said it because they seemed to expect it, and because it was hers, the one thing in her pockets. "I'd stand, but I don't know if my legs are mine yet."

"They're yours," Aldric said quietly. "Everything's yours. That's rather the point."

She looked at him. There was something in his face that hurt to look at—not pity exactly, but grief wearing pity's clothes. As if she were a grave he owed flowers to.

"I'm told I did something," she said. "For you. Everyone keeps saying it sideways, like they're afraid of spooking me. The healers won't meet my eye. You—" she nodded at Odile, "—you keep looking at me like I'm a page torn out of a book you loved."

Odile's breath caught audibly.

"I don't remember doing anything," Zephyrine went on. Her voice stayed level, almost mild, and that mildness frightened her more than tears would have. "I don't remember *you*. Either of you. I don't remember—" and here her hands moved, restless, gesturing at mountains that weren't there, "—anything. There's just this hole where a life should be, and everyone tiptoeing around the edge of it telling me how grateful they are."

"We can tell you," Odile said. Her voice shook. "All of it. We have it written down. We have—" she pressed her hand flat against her chest, against the shape of a book bound in gray leather that she carried everywhere now, "—we have everything. Every road. Every name. What you loved. Who loved you."

Zephyrine was silent for a while. A sparrow landed near her boot, cocked its head at her, and stayed—which, though she didn't know it, was answer of a kind.

"Will it bring anything back?" she asked finally. "If you read it to me. Will I feel it?"

"No," Odile said honestly. "Knowing isn't remembering. It will be like reading about a stranger who happens to wear your face."

"Then why tell me at all?"

"Because," Aldric said, "you deserve to know what you bought with yourself. And because—" he hesitated, and Odile saw him choose honesty over comfort, "—because whoever you were deserves not to be erased twice. Once by the price, once by our kindness."

Zephyrine looked out at the dying garden, at the honest rot spreading through the false green, and something in her chest pulled hard toward the east, toward the jagged line of the Thornfells visible beyond the walls, sharp-toothed against the pale sky. It pulled the way a hook pulls. It had been pulling for three days.

"The mountains," she said. "Why do I keep looking at the mountains?"

Neither of them answered. None of them had an answer that wasn't a wound.

---

That evening, in a small room lit by one lamp, Odile opened the prophecy book.

It lay between them on the table—gray leather, water-stained, a century old, smelling of ash and cedar. Zephyrine sat across from her with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking. Aldric had wanted to come; Odile had argued him out of it. Some truths needed fewer witnesses.

"This belonged to a scholar named Emeric Doss," Odile said. "He spent thirty years collecting prophecies the Spire condemned. Most of it is madness or poetry or both. But the last section—" she turned to the pages near the end, to the entry circled in faded brown ink, "—this part was true. We lived it. All of it."

She began to read.

She read the way she imagined a surgeon cuts: precisely, without flourish, naming each thing as she passed it. The king bound by something ancient wearing his crown. The winter-born speaker, found by no one, made by something older still. The choice at the frozen ford. The unbinding hour. And then the price—she read the price slowly, and her voice did not break until the very end, and Zephyrine noticed, and said nothing, and let her finish anyway.

"—and the unbinding demands payment," Odile finished, "and the payment is memory. Not all of it. Only the memories of those she loves. Poured into the dying roots, to feed the spring. Which means that every person Zephyrine ever cared for was the fuel of your freedom, Your Majesty. And she knew it. She knew the price going in, and she paid it without telling anyone, because she knew that if she told us, we would have tried to stop her."

The lamp hissed softly. Outside, real snowmelt ticked against the window—honest water, falling honestly.

Zephyrine set down her untouched tea.

"All right," she said.

Odile blinked. "All right?"

"I asked you to tell me. You told me. It's a good story." Zephyrine's voice was steady, and behind the steadiness Odile could see her holding herself together the way one holds together a cracked jar—with pressure and attention and no sudden movements. "There's a woman in it who gave away everyone she loved to save a king she'd known for a season. I believe she existed. I believe she was me." She paused. "But I don't know her. I can't mourn her properly. Mourning needs memories, and I've got none. So."

"So?" Odile whispered.

"So I'd like to know what she would do next." Zephyrine leaned forward, and for the first time her eyes—gray eyes, storm-colored, the same eyes that had stared down inquisitors and avalanches and a god wearing a friendly face—fixed on Odile with something that was almost hunger. "You knew her. Better than anyone living. If she walked out of here tomorrow with nothing but her name and two strong legs—where would she go? What would she do?"

Odile's throat worked. She had rehearsed this conversation a hundred times in her head, and in none of those rehearsals had Zephyrine been so *practical* about her own annihilation.

"East," Odile said. "Into the Thornfells. Always east. She hated the lowlands—said they were too soft to hide in. She had routes up there nobody else knew. Caches. Friends." Her voice caught on the word. "*Friends.*"

"The animals," Zephyrine said suddenly. "The beasts. The book says she spoke with them."

"Yes."

"Would they remember me?"

And there it was—the question Odile had been dreading and hoping for, the question with no kind answer, and she gave the truest one she had:

"I don't know. But I think—" she stopped, steadied herself, started again. "I think if anything in this world remembers you, it will be them. Words fade. Gratitude fades. Kings forget. But a debt written in blood and trust, on a mountainside, in a wounded lynx's own tongue—" she shook her head. "That doesn't fade. That's carved deeper than memory."

Zephyrine absorbed this in silence. Then she nodded once, briskly, the nod of a woman closing an account.

"Then that's where I'll go," she said. "East. As soon as my legs are mine again."

"You could stay," Odile said, and hated how small her voice came out. "Here. With us. We'd—Aldric has offered you anything. A title. Land. A place at court. You'd never want for—"

"I'd be a stranger being thanked forever," Zephyrine said. "No offense meant to either of you. But gratitude makes a poor houseguest and a worse home." She rose, unsteadily, testing her legs, and crossed to the window. Beyond the glass, the Thornfells stood black against the last light, and the pull in her chest yanked so hard she swayed. "I need to go where the ground is honest. Where things die in their season and grow back in theirs. I've been living in a garden that lied to me for—I don't even know how long. I want snow that means it."

"When?" Odile asked.

"When the passes open." Zephyrine turned back, and smiled—a quick, crooked, unfamiliar smile that nonetheless felt, to Odile, heartbreakingly like the old one. "Soon. Can you feel it? The mountain's waking up. Something's calling me home, and I'd rather not keep it waiting."

---

Three days later, the passes opened, and Zephyrine Galloway left the Spire on foot, carrying a pack Odile had packed for her—dried meat, a flint, a wool cloak, and, sewn secretly into the lining, a purse of coins and a folded map marked in Odile's careful hand with every cache, every route, every village that owed her.

At the gates, Aldric pressed something into her hand: a small knife, plain-hilted, well-used. "Yours," he said. "You'll know that when you hold it."

She held it. It fit her grip like a sentence fits its meaning. She didn't remember it, but her hand did, and that was enough.

"Thank you," she said politely, and walked east, and did not look back.

Odile and Aldric watched her go from the wall, a small figure diminishing along the white road, until the mountains took her.

"She didn't cry," Aldric said. "Not once. Not when you read it to her. Not at the gate."

"No," Odile said. "She didn't."

"Is that strength, or is it the wound?"

Odile was quiet a long moment. Below them, in the courtyard, the last of the false orchard was being cut down and burned, and the smoke rose straight and clean into a sky that finally, honestly, meant spring.

"Both," she said. "I think it's both. And I think—" she exhaled, watching the road, willing the small figure onward, "—I think the mountains will give her back to us. Not the woman she was. But something true. Something *hers*. Memory's not the only thing that makes a person, Aldric. Sometimes action does. Sometimes the choosing."

"And if the choosing isn't enough?"

"Then we go to her," Odile said simply. "As often as it takes. For as long as it takes. You owe a debt, Your Majesty, and so do I, and neither of us is going to be permitted to forget it just because she has."

Aldric laughed—a startled, rusty sound, the first real laugh since the unbinding—and put his arm around the little archivist's shoulders, and they stood together on the wall as the thaw ran silver down the mountainsides, carrying winter's debris toward the valleys, toward the villages, toward the waiting world.

Far to the east, high on a ridge above the tree line, a scarred snow-lynx lifted its head from a kill it had been guarding, ears pricked, golden eyes fixed on a tiny figure climbing steadily upward through the melting snow.

It had been waiting a long time.

It could wait a little longer—but not forever.
