## 20. The Beast Who Waited

The palace wall was the only place in the whole green city where Zephyrine could breathe.

Not because the air was different—though it was, faintly, sweetened by those impossible orchards that bloomed in defiance of the blizzard still gnawing at the world beyond the gates—but because up here, above the terraces and the pale colonnades, nothing grew. The stone was old and honest. It had been quarried from the mountain's bones a century ago or more, and it had no interest in flowering. She liked that about it. She liked things that did not pretend.

She came up most mornings now, when the light came gray through the thaw-mists and the court below was still too asleep to need her. They were kind to her here—Aldric especially, with his careful gentleness, as though she were made of frost-flower petals—but kindness from strangers has a particular weight, like coins pressed into the hand of someone who cannot remember what money is for. She took it. She thanked them. And every night she lay in a bed softer than any she'd ever known and stared at the ceiling and felt the shape of something missing, the way you feel the shape of a tooth after it's gone: not pain exactly, but the ghost of where the pain used to live.

She did not know what she had lost. That was the strange mercy of it. Odile had told her plainly, on the third morning, reading aloud from a book whose pages smelled of ash and old smoke—the truth of what she'd done, what she'd paid. Zephyrine had listened the way you listen to a story about someone else, someone brave and doomed, and she had wept without knowing for whom. The tears had come from somewhere deeper than memory. That was the thing she kept returning to, turning over like a river-stone: grief does not live in the remembering. It lives somewhere else entirely.

This morning the mist was thick off the mountains, rolling down into the valley in slow white tongues, and for a moment—just a moment—it smelled right. Cold pine and stone-dust and the iron tang of coming snow. Her chest went tight with a longing so sharp she had to grip the parapet.

*East,* said something in her, wordless and certain. *That smell comes from the east.*

And then she heard the sound.

It was small, at first. A scrape of claw on stone, deliberate, unhurried. She turned, and there on the wall—not three paces away, though the wall was high and sheer and no creature should have climbed it—sat a snow-lynx.

He was old. That was the first thing she understood, before beauty, before fear. His coat had gone thin at the haunches, silver-shot, and one ear was ragged where something had torn it long ago. A scar ran from his jaw across his throat in a pale seam, the kind of wound that should have killed a smaller animal. He sat the way old soldiers sit, as if sitting were a discipline he had practiced for years, and he looked at her with eyes the color of winter dawn—pale gold, ringed in ice-blue—and he did not look away.

Zephyrine knew, distantly, that she should be afraid. Lynxes did not climb palace walls in the heart of a city. But fear required a self to protect, and hers felt provisional these days, borrowed, like a coat left behind by its owner. Instead she felt something else, something that rose in her like water finding an old channel:

*I know you.*

She didn't. She was sure of it. She would remember a lynx; Odile had told her enough of who she'd been—a smuggler, a speaker to beasts, a woman of the Thornfell passes—that surely a cat with a torn ear and a soldier's posture would have surfaced somewhere in the wreckage. But knowing and remembering, she was learning, were different countries, and sometimes the borders between them were thinner than anyone guessed.

"You shouldn't be here," she said softly. Her voice, she noticed, had already dropped into the low, even register one uses with wild things. When had she learned that? "There's nothing for you in this city. Nothing honest."

The lynx blinked, slow, deliberate—the way cats say *I hear you, and I am not impressed*—and then he did the last thing she expected. He stood, stretched each foreleg long, arched his scarred throat toward the sky, and yowled.

It was not a pretty sound. It was a sound like a door being forced, like grief given a voice, and it rang off the pale stones and out over the blooming orchards, and somewhere below a guard shouted in alarm. The lynx ignored him. He fixed his winter-dawn eyes on Zephyrine again, and this time there was no ambiguity in them at all. This time they said, as clearly as words:

*You are coming with me. I have walked a very long way, and I am not leaving without you.*

"I can't understand you," she told him—and heard, even as she said it, how untrue it was. She understood him perfectly. That was the horror and the wonder of it. Whatever gift she'd carried, whatever she'd paid away, some root of it had survived the cutting, deep down where the shears couldn't reach. Or perhaps—she thought of Odile's careful voice, reading from the burned book—perhaps the gift had never lived in her memories at all. Perhaps it lived in the place the tears came from.

The lynx took two steps closer along the wall. Then he sat again, directly in her path, and began, with great dignity, to wash one ragged ear.

As if to say: *I can wait. Waiting is what I do.*

* * *

They let her leave. That surprised her more than anything else.

She had braced for refusal—for Aldric's gentle concern, for Odile's scholarly objections, for guards at the gates with orders dressed up as invitations. She had rehearsed arguments all through the previous night while the lynx sat on her windowsill outside, patient as a saint, watching her through the glass with those unblinking eyes.

But when she stood in the hall the next morning, travel-worn pack on her shoulder—packed, she realized, with an efficiency she had no memory of learning, every strap cinched just so—and said, "I'm going east," Aldric only looked at her for a long moment with eyes still shadowed by everything he'd woken from, and nodded.

"He'll want to come with you as far as the gates," Odile said. She was thinner than when Zephyrine had first met her—or thought she'd first met her; the days blurred—and there was ash-gray in her hair that hadn't been there a season ago. She held the prophecy book against her chest like a child holds a doll. "The king, I mean. Don't be cruel to him. He's carrying more than you know."

"Am I cruel?" Zephyrine asked. It was an honest question. She genuinely did not know what she was anymore.

Odile smiled, and it was a sad, crooked thing. "You're honest. Sometimes those look alike from the inside." She pressed something into Zephyrine's hand: a small cloth bundle, tied with string. "Seeds. For when you get wherever you're going. Things that grow slowly. Things that don't lie." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the last word. "Plant them somewhere high."

Zephyrine turned the bundle over in her fingers. Through the cloth she could feel the tiny hard promise of them, dozens of futures waiting in the dark. "Why do I feel like I've done something for you?" she asked. "All of you. Why do you all look at me like—" She searched for it. "Like I'm a debt someone paid on your behalf."

Odile's composure held, but barely; Zephyrine saw the tremor move through her like wind through wheat. "Because you did," she said simply. "And someday, if you ever want the whole of it, I'll tell you every word. Not today. Today you should follow your cat."

"He's not my cat."

"No," Odile agreed, and something flickered behind her eyes—grief, or laughter, or both wearing the same coat. "I suspect he never was. I suspect he was always the other way around."

At the gates, Aldric stood in the cold without cloak or crown—he had sent the crown away, Odile had told her, sent it to be melted down into plowshares, and the court had wept and called it wisdom—and he bowed to her. A king, bowing to a woman who could not remember why. His face was lined and human and still bore, faintly, around the mouth, the ghost of a smile that had never been his own.

"Thank you," he said, "for whatever it was."

"You're welcome," she said, "for whatever it was."

And they both laughed—short, startled, real—and it was the first time since waking that Zephyrine had laughed and felt it land anywhere other than the surface of her.

Then the gates opened, and the east wind came through like a hand, smelling of pine and stone and distance, and the lynx flowed past her ankles, out onto the road, and stopped, and looked back.

*Well?* said the look. *The world is large and I am old. Walk faster.*

* * *

They traveled the way water travels downhill: without argument, without haste, following the path of least resistance, which in the thawing mountains meant following the lynx.

He led her along routes that made no sense to her map-sense—which roads to avoid, which bridges were rotten, where the fords ran shallow—and every time she obeyed him, every time she trusted the turn he chose over the turn reason suggested, she found afterward that he had been right. A bridge collapsed two hours after they crossed it. A pass she'd have taken was choked with slide-scree. Once, camped in the ruins of a waystation with rain hammering the broken roof, he refused absolutely to let her sleep in the eastern corner, planting himself on the spot and yowling until she moved her bedroll, and in the night part of the wall gave way exactly there, burying the corner in wet stone.

She lay awake a long time after that, listening to the rain and the lynx's slow breathing beside her, and tried to understand what was happening inside her.

Because here was the thing: she remembered none of this. Not the waystations, not the routes, not the man whose name she must once have known. But her body remembered. Her hands built fires in the pattern of someone who'd built ten thousand of them, stacking the wet wood just so, shielding the flame with her body at the angle that worked. Her feet chose campsites before her mind had finished looking at them. When wolves sang far off in the night, she answered—not with words, but with a low sound in her throat she hadn't known she could make—and the singing changed, softened, acknowledged her, and moved on, and she lay there with her heart pounding, understanding that she had just been *introduced*, that somewhere out in the dark she had a reputation among creatures she could not recall ever meeting.

Memory, she was beginning to see, was not one thing. It was a country with many provinces, and whatever blade had cut through her had taken the cities—the names, the faces, the stories—but the wild country remained. The hands. The feet. The throat. The part of her that read weather in the smell of the wind and lies in the set of a stranger's shoulders. All of it intact, all of it hers, waiting under the snow of her loss like seeds under drifts, patient, certain of spring.

"The scholar says I saved a king," she told the lynx one evening, as they watched the sun go down behind them and the peaks ahead catch fire with alpenglow. "She says I paid something terrible for it. Everyone looks at me like I'm a saint or a ghost, and I just feel—" She turned her hands over, studying them. Scarred knuckles. A burn on the left wrist, old, white-puckered. Hands that had lived a life. "Empty. Not sad-empty. Just—like a room after the furniture's been carried out. You can still see where everything stood."

The lynx regarded her with his winter eyes. Then he rose, crossed the space between them, and laid his scarred throat against her knee—the scar, she noticed, the long pale seam of it, warm against her skin—and stayed there, purring, a sound like distant thunder deciding to be kind.

And it hit her, all at once, standing in the ruins of herself on a mountainside in the gathering dusk: *he remembers me.* Every person she had loved was gone from her—she knew it, knew it the way you know a room is empty—but this animal had crossed half a mountain range, an old and tired animal, climbing walls and defying every instinct of his wild kind, to sit beside a stranger who smelled, faintly, underneath everything, like the person he had chosen.

Memory outlived memory. Love, apparently, kept its own books.

She put her hand on his head, between the ragged ears, and wept—not the bewildered tears of the first weeks, but something cleaner. Grief with a direction. Grief pointed forward.

"All right," she said, when she could speak. "East, then. Show me who I was."

* * *

They crossed into Thornfell on the ninth day.

She knew it the moment it happened, though no sign marked the border—no cairn, no carved stone. It was the smell first: pine resin and cold granite and the mineral breath of snowmelt, and beneath it something wilder, musk and fur and the green rot of the deep forest floor. Then the land itself, rising around them in shoulders and spines of rock, the passes threading through like old scars. And then—everywhere, all at once, from every shadowed fold of the slopes—the attention of the mountain turned toward her.

Birds went quiet and then resumed, differently. Something large and unseen shifted its weight in the timberline shadows. Far above, on a crag, a herd of ibex stood silhouetted against the sky, and every horned head turned to watch her pass.

The lynx stopped on a ridge where the whole eastern range opened below them—valley after valley falling away into blue distance, the high snowfields catching the afternoon light like hammered tin—and he looked back at her, and made a sound she had not heard from him before: a low, rough chirrup, almost tender. An invitation home.

And Zephyrine stood in the wind with her empty rooms and her full hands, and felt it rise in her—not memory, not quite, but the *shape* of memory, the way a body knows a bed it has slept in for years even in the dark. Somewhere down in those valleys were caches she had hidden and could not find. Villages she had fed and did not know. Graves, maybe. Friends, certainly. A life, packed away in the dark like winter stores, all of it waiting for her with the patience of the mountain itself.

Her eyes stung. She laughed instead, wiping her face with a sleeve that smelled of woodsmoke and lynx.

"You could have just told me," she said to the cat. "You could have written it down."

He chirruped again, unrepentant, and started down the ridge.

She followed. Below them, in the first valley, a thread of smoke rose from a village chimney—someone baking bread, maybe, someone living—and as she watched, a figure came out onto a rooftop and shaded their eyes against the light, looking up at the ridge, at the two small shapes descending through the scree. Even at that distance Zephyrine saw the figure go rigid. Saw them turn and shout something down into the village, urgent, joyful, disbelieving.

And then the bells began.

Small, cracked, half-forgotten bells, ringing out across the valley in a pattern that was not alarm—she knew alarm when she heard it, her body knew it before her mind—and the sound rolled up the mountainside and broke over her like a wave, and the lynx paused on the slope and glanced back at her with his winter-dawn eyes, and she understood, with a certainty that needed no memory at all:

Whoever she had been, she was about to meet her.

The bells rang on. She went down toward them.
