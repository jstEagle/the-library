## 21. A Garden of Her Own

The valley had no name that Zephyrine knew of, which suited her. She had learned, in the year since the thaw, that names were things she could not carry—hers had been poured out of her like water from a cracked jug, and what remained was a person shaped mostly by habit and by hands. But a valley did not need her to remember it. A valley only needed her to stay.

She had found it in the first week of spring, following the lynx.

The lynx had a scarred face and an opinion about everything, and it had not left her side since the morning it appeared on the palace wall in the city of pale stone, staring at her with yellow eyes full of a recognition she could not return. The court physicians had called it a hazard. The king—Aldric, they said his name was, Aldric who wept when he looked at her and could not explain why—had called it a companion, and had given her leave to go wherever it led.

It had led her east, through the passes, up into Thornfell, where the snow still lay in blue shadows on the north faces and the wind spoke a language she understood without understanding how. It had led her to this valley: a cup of green held between two granite shoulders, watered by a stream that fell off the glacier in long white threads, sheltered enough that the frost came late and left early. There was an old shepherd's hut with a good roof and a hearth that drew well. There was soil—black, cold, smelling of centuries of patience.

She had stood in the middle of it with the wind in her hair and felt something in her chest open like a door.

*Here,* the feeling said. Not in words. She did not seem to have words for things that mattered anymore; she had words for salt and rope and weather, and beyond that a great quiet. But her body knew things. Her hands knew things. When she knelt and pushed her fingers into the soil, they moved with the certainty of a woman who had been doing this, or something like this, all her life.

*Here,* said the feeling again, and the lynx sat down on a flat stone in the sun and began to wash its scarred face, and that was that.

* * *

The seeds had come in the spring's first caravan, in a box of pale mountain-pine wood, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with a scholar's knot—two loops and a tuck, precise as handwriting. The card inside was written in a small, careful script:

*These grow slowly. They do not lie about what they are. I thought you should have them. — O.V.*

Zephyrine did not know who O.V. was. She knew the handwriting the way she knew the lynx—by its shape, by the way it made her chest ache in a place that had no name. She had sat with the card for a long time that first evening, turning it over, holding it near the fire, holding it against her sternum as if the paper might remember something her blood had forgotten.

It did not. But it was a good ache. She had decided to keep it, the way one keeps a stone from a beach one can no longer find.

There were seeds of many kinds in the box. Barley with a grey beard. Turnips the color of dawn. A twist of paper containing beans so old and hard they rattled like dice. Dried seed-heads of something she did not recognize, labeled in the same careful hand: *yarrow, from the low meadows; it remembers the ground for you.* And at the bottom, wrapped separately, a small pouch of seeds she could not identify at all—dark, crescent-shaped, faintly fragrant, with a note that said only: *Plant these last. They will know when.*

So she planted in the order the box suggested, and the valley began, slowly, to fill.

She learned the work the way she seemed to learn everything—her hands ahead of her mind, her body remembering what her memory could not. She built raised beds from stones she pried out of the streambed, stacking them dry-wall style with an ease that made her pause and frown at her own fingers. She composted. She mulched. She talked to the soil, low and wordless, the way she talked to everything, and the soil seemed to listen.

That was the other thing about her, the thing the villagers whispered about and the beasts simply accepted: she spoke, and things answered.

Not in words. Never in words. But when she told the ravens there was marrow on the midden, the ravens came. When she told the marmots to keep out of the bean beds, they grumbled and obeyed, mostly. When the stream flooded its banks in the early summer melt, she stood at the water's edge and *asked*, and the water, which had been chewing at her stone walls for two days, sighed and slid back into its channel like a dog called to heel.

The lynx watched all of this with an expression of long-suffering familiarity, as if to say: *yes, this is what she does, you get used to it.*

Zephyrine did not know what to make of it herself. She had asked the king's scholars, before she left the city, what she was. They had exchanged glances over her head. One of them, a thin woman with ink-stained fingers, had said gently, *You are the woman who unbound the king. What else you were—that is yours to grow into.* Which was no answer at all, but it was the only one anyone would give her, and in time she stopped asking and started simply doing, and the doing began, slowly, to become an answer of its own.

* * *

The beasts came first.

They arrived the way rumors arrive—individually, then all at once. A fox in its summer coat, who took up residence under the hut and pretended not to live there. A pair of choughs who rode the thermals above the valley and reported, in their rattling voices, on everything that moved in the neighboring passes. A matriarch ibex with a coat like grey smoke, who appeared one morning on the ridge above the garden and stood there for an hour, watching, before descending to drink at the stream as if she had always done so.

Zephyrine did not remember the ibex. She was almost sure of that. And yet when the old female looked at her across the water, something moved through her—grief, or its echo, the way a bell keeps trembling after the clapper has gone still. She had said, without deciding to, *I'm sorry. I don't know what for. But I'm sorry,* and the ibex had breathed out through her nose, a long white plume in the cold morning air, and dipped her head once, and that was somehow enough.

The herd followed the matriarch. Then came the hares, the weasels, a badger with a torn ear and terrible manners, a family of red deer who grazed the meadow's edge at dusk and let her walk among them so long as she did not reach out her hands. The valley filled with eyes. At night she could lie in the hut's doorway and feel them all around her in the dark—a whole congregation of breathing things, holding her in the middle of themselves like a coal passed hand to hand.

She did not know why they came. She suspected, in the way she suspected most things about herself—obliquely, with a kind of tender dread—that they had known her before. That she had owed them, or they her, and the debt was older than her memory and just as binding.

She fed them anyway. It seemed the thing to do.

* * *

The villagers came in the high summer, on a bright day when the barley was knee-high and the turnip greens were lush enough to shame a lowland garden.

She heard them before she saw them—boots on the switchback trail, more boots than the trail usually carried—and she stood up out of the bean bed with dirt to her elbows, wary in a way that lived in her spine rather than her mind. The lynx rose from its stone and stood at her hip, ears forward.

There were nine of them. Farmers, by their hands and their sunburns; a woman with a flour-dusted apron folded under one arm; two children hanging back at the rear with the particular courage of the young, which is to say none, and then too much. They stopped at the edge of the garden and looked at it, and at her, and the looking had a weight to it that she did not understand.

Then the woman with the apron stepped forward, and her eyes were wet, and she said, "You don't know us."

It was not a question, but Zephyrine answered it. "No," she said. "I'm sorry. I don't."

"We know." The woman's voice cracked and steadied. "We were told. Odile—the scholar, she wrote to us—she said you might not remember, and that we weren't to—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "My name is Marta. Marta Kess. You brought salt to Hollowbridge three winters running, when the roads were closed and the Spire's tithe had taken everything. My youngest would have—" She looked back at the children. "The little one. She wouldn't have seen her fourth winter without that salt."

Zephyrine looked at the children. The little one, a girl of perhaps six, with a cowlick and a fierce chin, stared back at her with an expression of absolute, uncomplicated adoration, the way children look at figures out of stories.

"I'm glad," Zephyrine said, and found that she meant it in her bones, in the wordless place where her real life seemed to be kept. "I'm glad she saw it."

"We brought bread." Marta lifted the apron-wrapped bundle like an offering. "We brought names, too, if you want them. We thought—Odile said you were making a garden, and we thought a garden needs neighbors, even if the neighbors are only names."

They laid the bread on a flat stone at the garden's edge, dark rye loaves still warm from ovens two valleys away, and then, one by one, the villagers gave her their names the way one gives coins to a shrine: quietly, carefully, as though each one might be too heavy to carry.

*Hallow Kess. Sef and Anja Torvald. Old Bram, who taught you the high pass when you were seventeen—though he says you'll not remember that, and he says it laughing, so don't you fret.* The names came and Zephyrine took each one and held it, and did not try to make it mean more than it did. She could not grieve people she did not remember. But she could receive them. She was learning that receiving was its own kind of work, and that she was not bad at it.

The little girl, the one who owed her fourth winter to a sack of contraband salt, hung back until the others had said their piece. Then she marched forward through the bean beds—trampling one, Zephyrine noted, with the impunity of the very young—and planted herself in front of the stranger and stared up at her.

"Did you really talk to a snow-lynx?"

"I talk to this one," Zephyrine said, and the lynx, who had been observing from its stone with the air of minor royalty, flicked one ear.

The girl's eyes went round. "What did it say?"

Zephyrine considered. The lynx's opinion of the girl, transmitted in the usual way—posture, whisker-angle, a low chirring rumble—was something between *tolerable* and *potentially useful.* "It says you're brave," she said, "and that you stepped on my beans."

The girl looked down at the flattened bean plant, and back up, and grinned with every tooth in her head. "I'll bring you seeds next time," she said. "Real ones. From the lowlands."

"I'd like that."

They stayed the afternoon. They weeded, because farmers cannot look at weeds and leave them; Marta showed her how to stake the beans in a way her hands had somehow never learned; Old Bram sat on the flat stone eating bread and telling stories about a smuggler girl who had known the passes better than the mountain goats, and Zephyrine listened to the stories the way one listens to tales of a sister one never had—proud, and puzzled, and a little homesick for a home that had never been hers to remember.

When they left, at the long gold end of the day, the little girl turned at the first switchback and shouted down the valley, "We'll come back at harvest!" and the whole mountainside took up the words and carried them, and Zephyrine stood in her garden with bread and names and dirt on her elbows, and found that she was crying without any idea why, and let herself do it, because it seemed like the kind of thing the woman in the stories would have done.

* * *

Autumn came early to the high valley, as it always does, and with it the first frosts, silvering the bean beds and turning the barley to a standing gold. Zephyrine harvested in the blue mornings with her breath steaming and the choughs supervising from the fence posts, and she found that the work had a rhythm her body knew, and that the rhythm was slowly, patiently, becoming a self. Not the self she had lost—she had begun to suspect that self was gone the way a river is gone when it reaches the sea, not destroyed but dispersed, present in everything it had touched and recoverable from nothing. But a self. A woman who grew things. A woman beasts trusted and children adored and neighbors brought bread to. A woman who, when she stood at the valley's mouth at dusk and looked east toward the lowlands and the distant pale gleam of the Spire, felt an ache that was no longer only grief, but had something like direction in it.

The last seeds she planted on the eve of the first snow.

The dark crescent ones. *Plant these last,* the note had said. *They will know when.* She had kept them all summer on the hut's windowsill, and they had seemed to wait, and she had waited with them, and on that cold evening in late autumn, with the lynx watching from the doorway and the first flakes coming down out of a sky the color of iron, she knelt at the head of the garden—in the place of honor, where the soil was deepest—and pressed them into the dark earth one by one.

She did not know what they were. But as she covered them, her hands moving with a tenderness that felt older than her, she spoke to them in the wordless way she spoke to all living things, and what she said—what rose out of her without her choosing it—was: *Grow. Take your time. I'll be here.*

The snow thickened. The lynx came to stand beside her, its warm flank against her leg, and together they watched the garden disappear under white, row by row, the whole green year folded away and kept.

And then, from the switchback trail below—footsteps. One set, light and deliberate, and the crunch of them was wrong for a villager and right for a scholar, and the lynx's ears went forward, and Zephyrine's heart did something complicated that she chose not to examine.

She turned. A figure was climbing the last stretch of the trail through the falling snow, small and robed and burdened with a satchel, and as it came into the firelight spilling from the hut's door, Zephyrine saw that it was a woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that were already wet, and that she was carrying a second box of pale mountain-pine wood, tied with a scholar's knot.

"Hello," the woman said, breathless, snow in her hair. "I hope it's not too late in the season. I've brought seeds."

And in the wordless place where Zephyrine kept the things that mattered, something turned over and woke, like a seed that had been waiting all winter to know when.
