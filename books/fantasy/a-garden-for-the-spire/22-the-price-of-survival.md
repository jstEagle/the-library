## 22. The Price of Survival

The first year ended the way the winter had begun: with frost on the inside of the window.

Zephyrine found it when she woke, a lacework of ice feathering across the glass of her small house above the valley, and she did not flinch from it. A year ago—though she had no year to measure against, only Odile's careful telling and the prophecy book's patient ink—a frost like this had been a promise of ruin. Now it was only weather. It would melt by midmorning. The garden would shrug it off the way an old dog shrugs off rain.

She dressed in the dark, boots laced by feel, and stepped out into the blue hour before dawn.

The valley below was silvered and still. Smoke rose from the village chimneys in straight, unhurried columns—the smoke of people who expected to see spring, who had banked their fires against an ordinary cold instead of huddling around them wondering if the sun would ever come back. Somewhere down there, Maren was baking bread before the ovens got busy. Zephyrine could smell it on some mornings when the wind turned right, and the smell always did something strange to her chest, a warmth with no name attached, like a letter addressed in handwriting she almost recognized.

The lynx was waiting for her at the gate, as she'd known he would be. He was old now—or had been old when she met him, though *met* was a generous word for a memory she didn't have; Odile said they'd crossed paths in the high passes during the bad days, and the beast had never left her after. His scarred flank caught the first grey light. His yellow eyes watched her come the way they always did, with an attention that felt less like an animal's curiosity and more like a man keeping vigil.

"Morning," she said.

He blinked slowly. Once. The way he did when he agreed with something.

They walked the rows together while the light came up. The garden was hers in every way that mattered, which was to say she had put her hands in its dirt and it had answered. She'd planted it from the seeds Odile sent south the first autumn—slow things, honest things, the scholar had written on the packet in her cramped archivist's hand. *Nothing here will lie to you about how long it takes to grow.* Barley that came up when barley comes up. Root vegetables that swelled underground without spectacle. A row of apple saplings that would not fruit for three years, maybe five, and Zephyrine loved them best of all for exactly that reason. Everything in this garden kept its promises on schedule. Nothing bloomed out of season. Nothing bloomed because something beneath the world was whispering to it.

She checked the snowline as she always did. It hung above the valley like a hem, white against the dark rock of Thornfell's shoulders, and along its ragged edge—here was the thing visitors never quite believed—the first crocuses were opening. Purple and white and a gold so fierce it looked deliberate. They grew right up to the ice and stopped, as if the two had agreed on a border and signed it. The villagers called it Zephyrine's Line, though she hadn't named it and couldn't take credit for it. Things simply grew where she tended them, and the growing crept uphill a little further each year, patient as water.

She crouched at the edge of the bed nearest the snow and pressed her bare fingers into the frozen ground. Cold bit deep. Beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, she felt the roots turning in their sleep.

"Another year," she told them.

The ground said nothing, but the saying-nothing had a texture she had learned to read, and this one meant *yes.*

* * *

Odile arrived at midday, riding a mule she complained about in four languages.

Zephyrine heard the bell on the gate before she saw them—the lynx lifted his head from his patch of sun and made a low sound in his chest, not quite a growl, not quite a greeting—and then the scholar came around the hedge with her satchel over one shoulder and her grey-streaked hair escaping its braid in every direction, looking exactly like a woman who had spent three days crossing the passes in early spring and had opinions about it.

"You've moved the bridge," Odile said, by way of hello.

"The old one rotted. I built it lower. Beasts don't like heights."

"Beasts," Odile said, "are the only ones whose opinions you consult." She dropped the satchel on the bench by the door with the groan of a woman twenty years older than she was, then straightened, and her eyes went soft in the way they sometimes did—quickly, and then quickly away, as if softness were a document she wasn't cleared to read. "You look well."

"I am well."

"That's not the same thing."

"It is now." Zephyrine smiled and took the satchel before the scholar could protest. Inside were books, of course. There were always books. And wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, a jar of honey from Lowmark, and a folded paper that smelled of ink. "Come inside. Maren sent bread up yesterday. You can tell me what the Spire smells like now."

"Burnt orchards," Odile said, following her in. "Honestly burnt, which is the best thing anyone has ever said about that city. They're pulling the roots out of the foundations stone by stone. It'll take a decade. Aldric insists on supervising personally, which means he stands in the mud in his shirtsleeves getting filthy while the courtiers pretend not to be horrified." She settled into the chair by the fire—the chair she always took, though she'd only sat in it a handful of times, as if chairs had ranks and she remembered hers. "He asks after you."

"He doesn't know me."

"No," Odile agreed quietly. "But he remembers what you did. Memory and knowing aren't the same country. You of all people should know that."

Zephyrine set the kettle on and let that sit between them, unargued. It was true, and they both knew it was true, and a year of living side by side with that truth had worn its edges smooth enough to hold.

* * *

They ate bread and honey and talked of small things—the thaw festival in Lowmark, the ibex herds coming back to the high meadows in numbers no one had seen since the last century, the deep-folk's new trade road that surfaced near the village and had made everyone rich in mushrooms and gossip. Odile talked with her hands when she forgot herself, sketching maps in the air, and Zephyrine listened and laughed in the right places and felt the peculiar double vision that never entirely left her: the pleasure of the moment, and beneath it, the sense of a room just past the wall of it where someone else was sitting. Someone who had walked these mountains before she knew they existed. Someone whose boots had broken this trail.

Afterward they went out to the garden, because Odile always asked, and Zephyrine always wanted her to.

The scholar walked the rows slowly, reading the plants like a page. She stopped at the snowline, at the crocuses holding their bright line against the ice, and stood there a long time with her breath smoking in the cold.

"A year," she said.

"So you keep reminding me."

"Do you want me to stop?"

Zephyrine considered it honestly. "No. If you stopped, I'd think you'd given up on me remembering. And I haven't given up either. Not exactly. I just—" She searched for it, the shape of the thing she'd been turning over all winter. "I've stopped digging for it. The memories. At first I used to sit up nights trying to force the door. Like grief, Odile. Like standing at a grave calling a name into the ground. And the name wouldn't come, and I'd wear myself hollow." She crouched, pulled a weed from among the barley—her hands doing the work while her mouth did the other work, the way hands and mouths divided labor in this valley. "Now I tend what's here instead."

Odile was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness she used when she was about to ask something she'd rehearsed. "May I ask you something? I've been carrying it the whole ride up, and I promised myself I'd say it plainly or not at all."

"Say it plainly."

"Do you regret it?"

The question landed in the cold air between them and stayed there, steaming.

Zephyrine finished pulling the weed. She stood, brushed the soil from her hands, and looked west toward the Spire, invisible beyond the mountains but present in the way a scar is present—known by its absence, mapped by what no longer grew there.

"You asked me that in autumn," she said. "I gave you a different answer then."

"You said you didn't know."

"I said I didn't know. That was honest. But it wasn't an answer, and we both knew it, and you've ridden three days through the passes to hear a real one, so here it is." She turned to face the scholar fully. "I don't remember enough to regret. That's the plain truth of it. Regret needs material, Odile. It needs the mother's voice and the smuggler's roads and whatever faces I loved hard enough to pay for. I have none of it. What I have is—" she gestured at the valley, the garden, the lynx drowsing against the warm south wall, the smoke rising straight and confident from the village below— "*this*. And this is good. This is better than good. So no. I don't regret. I *can't* regret, and pretending otherwise would be a kind of performance, and I've decided I'm done performing."

Odile's jaw tightened. "That's not—"

"It's not the whole answer. I know. Here's the rest." Zephyrine stepped closer, close enough to see the tiredness in the scholar's eyes, the guilt that lived there permanently now, rent-free, a tenant Odile had never managed to evict. "You want me to say I mourn her. The woman I was. And I do, in my way—I feel her the way you feel weather behind a wall. Pressure. Shape. Something moving that isn't me anymore. But here's what I've learned this year, tending things that grow slow and honest." She held up her hands, earth under the nails, the same hands that had done whatever terrible necessary thing had been done in the root-chamber beneath the throne. "A person is not their memories. I know that sounds like comfort, and maybe it is, but I mean it as *fact*. A person is what they do. Every morning. Over and over. Whoever I was—she knew that. She must have, because look at what she built. She fed villages. She barged with wounded animals and kept her word. She walked into the worst place in the world and paid everything she had so that a king could wake up and a winter could end, and she did it knowing—she *must* have known—that she wouldn't get to enjoy any of it."

The wind moved through the barley. Above them, on the snowline, the crocuses burned their small gold fires.

"So I've made a decision," Zephyrine said. "It took me the whole winter to make it, and I'm making it out loud now so you can hold me to it. Whoever she was, she chose well. I can't verify that. I can't check her work. But everything around me says she did—the thaw says it, the beasts say it, the fact that Maren's grandchildren are alive to steal apples from trees that won't fruit for three years says it. So I've decided to believe her. And more than believe her." She smiled, and felt it reach her eyes, and knew it was the truest thing on the whole mountain. "I intend to live up to her. To be worth what she paid. Not by mourning her—by *continuing* her. By doing the next right thing, and the one after that, until the sum of my mornings adds up to something she'd have approved of."

Odile made a sound—not quite a laugh, not quite anything else—and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes. When she lowered it, she was smiling the way people smile at funerals that turn into weddings.

"She'd have liked you," the scholar said. "The you that you are now."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I have that on excellent authority. She left instructions."

* * *

They stayed out until the light went long and amber, and Odile told her about the Spire archives being rebuilt with open shelves—*open*, the word still foreign in her mouth, she admitted, like a borrowed coat—and about the young archivists who had never known the burning times and asked questions that would have gotten their grandmothers pyred. She talked about Aldric's letters, which came twice a year, addressed to *The Keeper of the High Garden*, and which Zephyrine read by the fire and answered in short practical lines about seed stock and pass conditions, and which neither of them ever acknowledged were anything more than that.

At dusk, Odile stood to go down to the village, where a bed waited at Maren's and, knowing Maren, a pie she'd be unable to refuse.

At the gate she paused. The lynx rose from his wall and pressed his scarred shoulder once against Odile's leg—an honor, she'd been told, he extended to no one else—and then sat looking up the path toward the snowline, toward the crocuses, toward the east where the high passes opened their white throats toward Thornfell's heart.

"He still watches the passes," Odile said softly. "Every evening. Did you know that?"

"I know." Zephyrine came to stand beside her. "I watch with him sometimes. I don't know what I'm watching for. But it doesn't feel like loss. It feels like—" she tried the word on, found it fit— "*orientation.* Like a compass needle that knows north even if it's forgotten why north matters."

"Maybe that's all a soul is," Odile said. "Orientation. Everything else is luggage."

They stood together while the first stars came out over the peaks, sharp and cold and honest, and far below the lamplight of the village kindled one window at a time, each flame a small refusal of the dark, each one lit by someone who had survived a winter that was never supposed to end and had decided, in the plain stubborn way of survivors, to go on living anyway.

"One more thing," Odile said, digging in her satchel. "Before I forget. From the archive." She handed over a slim bundle wrapped in linen, tied with cord. "We finished the restoration last month. The Vasse cache book—the prophecy. It's going to the Spire vaults eventually, under glass, with a plaque and all the solemnity scholars require. But I argued they owed a debt first." Her voice roughened slightly. "Debts should be paid to the person who earned them. Even if she can't read it the same way anymore."

Zephyrine untied the cord carefully.

Inside was the book itself—she knew it instantly, the way you know your own hands, the way the lynx had known her on the palace wall a year ago. The cover was scorched at one corner. The pages were soft as cloth with age. And there, on the page Odile had marked with a ribbon, circled in faded brown ink a hundred years ago by a dead scholar's hopeful hand: a date. Her date. A name beside it that no living person had spoken until the world broke open.

She looked at it for a long time. The letters didn't ache, exactly. They hummed. Like a tuning fork touched to bone.

"Keep it," Zephyrine said at last, and closed the cover gently, and pressed it back into Odile's arms. "Not forever. But for now. I'm not ready to live next to that name on a shelf." She looked up, and her eyes were clear, and the last light caught the gold in them. "Ask me again next year. Maybe by then I'll want it on my wall. Or maybe I'll plant something over it and let it grow into the ground where it belongs."

Odile hugged her—briefly, fiercely, the way people hug when they are saying something too large for words—and went down the path toward the lamplight, and the mule complained, and the night swallowed them both kindly.

* * *

Zephyrine stayed out.

She walked the rows one last time in the dark, her feet finding the paths without needing light, and at the snowline she stopped and crouched where the crocuses met the ice. She put her palm flat against the frozen ground, and beneath the cold she felt them—the roots, the whole hidden nation of them, threading downhill from the dead Spire gardens, threading uphill from her beds, meeting somewhere in the middle of the mountain in a slow green handshake that no one had planned and no one could undo.

"Goodnight," she said.

And because it was the anniversary, and because anniversaries are for honesty, she said the other thing too, the thing she said only on this one night of the year, quietly, to the dark and the dirt and whoever she had been:

"I hope it was worth it to you. I think it was. I'm going to make sure."

The lynx came and leaned his warm weight against her side, and together they watched the stars wheel over Thornfell, and below them the garden slept against the snowline, blooming at the border of the ice—patient, unhurried, certain of spring—like a promise the world had finally learned to keep.
