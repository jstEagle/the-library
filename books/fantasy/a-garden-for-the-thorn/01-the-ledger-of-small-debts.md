## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The counting house of the Vellum Guild smelled of tallow, iron gall ink, and old money—the last of these being less a scent than a pressure, a weight in the air like weather about to break. Lior Iversen had worked beneath that pressure for eleven years, and she had learned to love it the way a stone loves the riverbed: not because it was kind, but because it held her in place.

She sat at the third desk from the window, where the light came in slanted and gray through leaded glass, and she balanced accounts.

Not ordinary accounts. No one at the Vellum Guild kept ordinary accounts. The ledger open before her was bound in calfskin dyed the color of dried blood, and its columns recorded trades that would have gotten any other merchant in Cindral Vale hanged, or worse—laughed at first, and then hanged. *One bottled storm, coastal vintage, harvested off the Teeth during the equinox gale.* *One year of another man's luck, provenance verified, seller compensated in land.* *Six hours of borrowed courage, decanted, slightly cloudy.*

Lior's quill moved down the page in small, precise strokes. She did not think about what the entries meant. Thinking about them was how clerks went strange; there had been a woman named Pell who'd worked the storm-vintages before her, and Pell had begun whispering to the stoppered jars, and then the jars had begun whispering back, and then Pell had been escorted out by two guild wardens and no one ever said her name again. Lior had learned from that. She kept her mind flat as vellum. She let the numbers be numbers.

*Debit: one storm, three-eighths remaining after client drawdown. Credit: forty crowns, paid to the harvester's widow per contract clause nine.*

Numbers were safe. Numbers added up.

Beneath the floorboards, the rats were gossiping.

Lior did not move. She did not so much as let her breath change rhythm, though every hair on her arms stood up under her sleeves like a field of grain bending toward a wind only she could feel. This was the discipline of her life, honed finer than any clerk's penmanship: to hear a thing and be unmoved by it. To know a thing and give no sign of knowing.

"—the third one this week," a rat was saying, its voice a dry scratch of thought against the underside of her skull, like a fingernail drawn along paper. The rats' speech was not words exactly, not the way people meant words. It was smell and image and intent braided together, and Lior's gift translated it into something like language, the way a bruise translates into color. "Third rat gone missing near the granary. Not dead. *Gone.* No blood, no bones. Marta says they don't even leave a smell behind."

"Rats go missing all winter," said a second voice, older, slower, thick with the memory of better garbage heaps. "Cats take them."

"Cats leave blood." The first rat—Lior thought of her as Marta's neighbor, quick and nervous, a young female with a torn ear—was pacing somewhere beneath the wainscoting along the east wall. "This is different. This is *quiet* different. You go near the granary wall now and there's nothing. Not fear. Nothing. Like walking into a room where someone just stopped talking."

Lior wrote: *Balance carried forward: 12 crowns, 7 shillings, 3 pence.*

Her hand did not shake. Eleven years of practice lived in that hand.

The gift had come to her early, the way such things do—as naturally as breathing and twice as dangerous. When she was six she had told her mother that the neighbor's cow was going to die in the spring, because the cow had told her so herself, in the bovine way of such things, a slow gray resignation like fog rolling through a barn. Her mother had laughed. Then the cow had died. Then her mother had stopped laughing, and started watching her, and by the time Lior was nine her mother had taught her the two rules that governed everything afterward: *never speak of it*, and *never let them see you listening*. By twelve, when a traveling priest had burned a boy in Harrow's Cross for charming crows, Lior understood that the rules were not caution. They were survival, written in smoke.

So she had come to the Guild, where impossible things were traded with discretion, and made herself indispensable and invisible in the same motion. A clerk who was quiet, accurate, forgettable. A woman whose face you could look at and not remember an hour later. She had cultivated it the way other women cultivated beauty: patiently, deliberately, stroke by stroke. Plain brown dress. Plain brown hair pinned without ornament. A voice pitched to neither command nor please. When clients came to the counting house, their eyes slid past her to the guildmaster's chair, to the silver scales, to the ledgers bound in strange skins—and that sliding-past was her armor, and she polished it daily.

It was comfortable, being invisible. That was the truth no one told you about hiding: it fit like a well-worn glove. It was warm in here, behind the ledger lines. Nothing could reach her among the numbers.

"Still," the old rat rumbled below, "Marta won't sleep near the east wall now. And Marta's slept near that wall since before your mother was whelped."

Below the floorboards, in the dark, something shifted—not a rat. Something larger, or else many small things moving as one, a rustle like dry leaves. The rats fell silent all at once, the way birds go silent before a hawk crosses the sun.

Then, faintly, the scratching resumed, farther away, retreating toward the kitchens.

Lior set down her quill. She blotted the line. She reached for the next ledger in her stack, and her fingers were steady, and inside her chest something small and cold turned over like a coin dropped in a well.

*Rats don't abandon territory,* she thought. *Not unless—*

She shut the thought away. There was work to do. There was always work to do.

* * *

The hooded client arrived at the fourth bell, which was unusual, because the counting house received clients at the second and seventh bells only, and never between, and never unannounced.

Lior knew he was there before the door opened—knew it from the pigeons on the roofline, who had gone up in a clatter of wings, and from the stable cat, who had pressed herself flat under the feed bins and was thinking, in her narrow carnivorous way, *wrong wrong wrong* about whatever stood in the alley outside. Lior kept her eyes on her ledger and listened to the front door admit a draft of cold air and a figure whose footsteps made no sound at all on the oak boards.

Every footstep makes some sound. Boots creak. Soles scuff. Even bare feet whisper against wood. These footsteps made nothing, and the absence of sound arrived in Lior's ears with a wrongness so profound that her hand actually trembled—a single blot of ink spreading like a wound across the balance column.

"Master Corvane is at his supper," she said, without looking up, pitching her voice into the register she used for turning people away: polite, final, empty as a locked drawer. "The counting house receives clients at the second and seventh bells. If you'll leave your name and commission with the doorwarden—"

"I'm not here for Corvane." The voice was low, roughened, and—she registered this with a small start—female. "I'm here for the clerk who balances the sealed ledgers. Third desk from the window."

Lior looked up.

The figure stood in the middle of the counting house floor, and the room's tallow light did something strange around her—or rather refused to do anything at all, pooling everywhere except where she stood, so that the hood and cloak read as a silhouette cut out of the world. Beneath the hood's shadow there was a jaw, a mouth, nothing more that Lior could fix upon. The pigeons had known. The cat had known. Everything alive in the building had known, and had said so, and Lior had been too busy balancing accounts to listen properly.

That was the crack, right there. She felt it run through her like a flaw through glass. Eleven years of discipline, and she had almost missed the warning because the numbers had been tidy.

"The sealed ledgers are guild property," Lior said. "Access requires a master's key and a master's presence. I balance what I'm given."

"You balance everything you're given." The hood tilted, considering her. "That's why I asked for you specifically. Do you know what's in the black cabinet, third shelf, behind the storm-vintages?"

"No."

"Liar," the woman said, but without heat, almost with approval. "Good. You're supposed to say no. But your ears went red when I named the cabinet, which means you've at least wondered. Curiosity is a debt, clerk. It accrues interest. I'm here to collect it."

Lior folded her hands atop the ledger. Under the desk, out of sight, her knee had begun a small tremor, and she stilled it with the heel of her other foot. *Be plain. Be forgettable. Be furniture.* "I think you've mistaken me for someone else. I'm a bookkeeper. I add and subtract. Whatever you're selling, the Guild buys it through proper channels, at proper bells, with Master Corvane present."

"What I'm offering isn't for sale." The hooded woman took one step closer, and still made no sound, and the tallow flames bent away from her like reeds from a current. "What I'm offering is a commission. One job. Read me a set of entries—dates, sums, routing marks, nothing more—and walk away richer than most clerks see in a lifetime. And if you refuse—" a pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on silk, "—then I'll assume the Guild has already sold what I'm looking for to the party I'm hunting, and I'll conduct my search somewhat less politely."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a door closed; the evening clerks were leaving, one by one, their footsteps fading toward the street, each of them passing within ten feet of the silent stranger and seeing, apparently, nothing at all. Lior watched the last of them go and felt the familiar comfort of invisibility close around her like a blanket—and then felt, for the first time in years, how thin the blanket was.

Because the woman had seen her. Out of an entire counting house of clerks, wardens, and doorwards, the hooded stranger had walked straight to the third desk from the window and named her trade. Which meant either that Lior's camouflage had failed—

—or that the woman had been told precisely where to look, by someone who knew exactly what Lior was.

The cold thing in her chest turned over again.

"I can't help you," Lior said quietly. "Whatever this is—whatever you're hunting—it's above my station. I keep accounts. I don't ask what they're for. That's not cowardice; it's how I've stayed alive in this building for eleven years. People who ask questions here end up like Pell."

"Pell," the woman repeated. "The jar-whisperer. Yes, I know about Pell. I also know about Harrow's Cross, and a burned boy, and a girl of twelve who stood in the crowd and heard every crow in the town screaming his name while the priests called it a devil's silence." The hood did not move, but Lior had the sudden, vertiginous certainty that she was being looked at—truly looked at—for the first time since childhood, seen straight through every careful layer of plainness down to the listening thing underneath. "I know what you are, Lior Iversen. I've known for three weeks. I chose this desk, and this hour, and this conversation very carefully. So understand me: I am not threatening you. I am the only person in this city offering you honesty, and I will not offer it twice."

The use of her name landed like a slap. No one used her name. Clients called her *clerk*; Corvane called her *girl* when he bothered; the other staff called her nothing at all. Her name had been packed away with her childhood, mothballed, safe. Hearing it spoken aloud in the counting house felt like watching someone open a drawer she'd nailed shut.

"How—" she began, and stopped, because the answer was obvious, and terrible. *Someone told you. Someone inside these walls knows what I am and sold the knowing.* Just as the Guild sold storms and luck and courage, it had apparently sold her—one more entry in a ledger she'd never been allowed to read.

The hooded woman waited. She was good at waiting; it rolled off her like patience distilled to oil.

Lior looked down at her ledger. At the neat columns, the balanced sums, the twelve crowns seven shillings three pence carried forward. The arithmetic of a small, safe, hidden life. *Nothing reaches you among the numbers,* she had told herself an hour ago, and had believed it, the way you believe a floor will hold you until the day it doesn't.

"I'll bring your request to Master Corvane," she said at last, each word placed like a coin counted out. "If he approves the commission, the Guild will contact you through proper channels."

"That's a no."

"That's the only answer I have."

For a long moment the hooded woman simply stood there, and the candles leaned away from her, and the silence where her footsteps should have been seemed to fill the whole room. Then she reached into her cloak and set something on the desk—something small, wrapped in gray cloth, heavy for its size. It clicked against the oak with the sound of metal on metal.

"A token," she said. "When the dark comes—and it is coming, clerk, sooner than anyone in this fat comfortable city believes—set that on your windowsill. Someone will come for you. Not me, maybe. But someone who knows what your voice is worth." She stepped back, and the shadows closed over her like water over a dropped stone. "You can spend your life being small, Lior Iversen. Most people do. But small things get swallowed. Remember that. Remember who told you."

And then she was gone—through the front door, which opened and shut without a sound, into the blue dusk of Cindral Vale, leaving behind only the wrapped token on the desk and a faint smell of cold iron and road dust.

Lior sat very still for a long time.

Then, slowly, she picked up the bundle and unwrapped one corner. Inside lay a coin—but not any coin minted in the kingdom. It was black, older than any currency she knew, and stamped with a sigil she recognized only from the margins of the oldest sealed ledgers: a crown made of thorns, encircling a star.

Under the floorboards, far away in the dark by the kitchens, the rats had begun to scream.

Lior closed her hand around the coin, and it was cold as a winter river, and she understood—with the first full, hairline crack running through the comfortable shell of her hidden life—that the accounts were about to stop balancing.
