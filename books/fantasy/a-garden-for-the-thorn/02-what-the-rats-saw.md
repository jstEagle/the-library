## 2. What the Rats Saw

The messenger doves arrived at the Vellum Guild on a Tuesday, which Lior Iversen would later think of as the last ordinary day of her life.

She noticed them first as a wrongness in the morning's arithmetic. The counting house kept its own rhythms: the scratch of quills in the scriptorium above, the groan of the great iron scale in the weighing room, and beneath it all—the part only she heard—the soft industry of the rats in the walls. Forty-one of them lived in the Guild's foundations that winter. She had names for nine. The rest she thought of by their debts: the one who owed her a stolen rind of cheese, the one who had led her to a dropped signet ring worth three months' wages, the old scarred matriarch she called Grandmother who took payment in nothing but warmth, curling against Lior's ankle in the cold hours when even the hearth coals went grey.

On Tuesday morning, the rats were quiet.

Not absent—she could feel them, a pressure like a held breath behind the wainscoting—but quiet in the way of animals listening to something far away. Lior sat with her ledger open to the column for inbound correspondence and found herself staring at the blank line where the doves should have been logged, hearing nothing from the floorboards but silence.

Then the doves came anyway, all seven of them at once, tumbling through the cote window in a clatter of grey wings, and the clerks looked up from their work with the mild irritation of men interrupted in the middle of a sum.

"Seven together," muttered Pell, the senior tallyman, catching them with practiced hands. "That's the whole border circuit. Someone's paying for a full report." He began working the message capsules off their legs, each one no bigger than a thumbnail, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. "Border posts don't have that kind of coin."

Lior said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing; it was the foundation of her entire life, load-bearing and invisible, like the cellar wall beneath the counting house floor. But when Pell set the capsules in the intake tray to await the archivist, she contrived to be the one who carried the tray upstairs, because carrying things was what junior accountants did, and no one ever looked twice at a girl with a tray.

No one ever looked twice at Lior Iversen at all. That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of her existence, built stone by careful stone over twenty-six years: be useful, be quiet, be small. The Vellum Guild traded in impossible things—a bottled storm, a year of someone else's luck, a dead man's last word spoken into a locket—and impossible trade required discretion, and discretion required people who could keep secrets even from themselves. Lior kept hers so deep she sometimes went days without letting herself think it in full sentences. *I hear the beasts.* Three words, and every one of them was kindling. In the kingdom of Cael Morath, they burned beast-speakers. Not exiled. Not imprisoned. Burned, in the square outside the Cathedral of the Unblinking Sun, while children were given the day off school to watch, because the priests said it was educational.

So she carried the tray up three flights of stairs, past the map room and the vault of unclaimed futures, and in the archivist's antechamber, alone for ninety seconds, she pressed her thumb through the wax of the nearest capsule and read what the border had sent.

The handwriting was a warden's—clipped, official, the letters of a man writing quickly and pretending he wasn't afraid.

*Third week of silence. Songbirds gone mute along the whole of the Thornmark stretch. Not fled—gone mute. They sit in the trees with their throats working and nothing comes out. Ward-stones humming a half-tone flat. Requesting instruction.*

Lior put the capsule down. Her hands were steady. She had trained them to be steady the way other girls were trained to embroider.

*Requesting instruction.* As if the Guild were a mother who would know what to say. As if there were an answer to birds forgetting their own voices.

She resealed the wax with the archivist's stamp—it was a forgery-adjacent act, technically, but the archivist was half-blind and everyone knew it—and carried the tray onward, and told herself, all the way back down the stairs, that it was a border matter. Border matters were weather. They happened to other people, at a distance, in places she would never go. The Thornmark was forty leagues north. Birds going quiet forty leagues north was a curiosity, a column entry, a thing to be filed under *anomalies, natural, pending*.

Beneath the floorboards, the rats still hadn't made a sound.

* * *

They stayed silent for six days.

Lior learned to hate the silence the way you learn to hate a toothache—by parts, incrementally, until it organized your whole attention. She caught herself kneeling in the archive cellar at midnight with her palm flat against the cold flagstones, listening so hard her ears rang, and had to stand up and laugh at herself, quietly, in the dark. Sixty years of ledgers down here. Debts and deeds and the prices of impossible things. Stone didn't gossip. Rats gossiped, magpies gossiped, the Guild's messenger foxes—kept in the mews for the fast northern routes—gossiped in their sharp barking way about which handlers fed them best. But stone kept its counsel, and Lior Iversen was a woman built of stone, and everything was fine.

On the seventh day, the magpies stopped coming to the city.

This she noticed because the Guild's kitchen scraps went out to the mews every evening, and the magpies always came, dozens of them, black-and-white thieves with opinions about everything. On the seventh day the scrap pails sat untouched, and Cook complained about it, and Pell said the weather must have turned, though the weather hadn't turned, and Lior stood in the yard with her breath smoking and felt the hair rise on her arms.

Because the magpies hadn't gone somewhere else. She could feel the absence of them the way you feel a missing stair in the dark. Something had frightened them out of the sky itself—not off the rooftops, not across the river, but out of the whole idea of coming here. And whatever could frighten a magpie out of the sky was a thing Lior's gift had no name for, and she resented it, fiercely, personally, the way you resent a stranger reading over your shoulder.

That night she dreamed of the fox.

Not one of the Guild's messenger foxes—those were sleek, half-tamed creatures in leather harnesses, vain about their routes. This fox was red as a coal going out, thin as a winter branch, and in the dream it stood at the edge of a field of snow and looked at her with eyes like struck flint, and it opened its mouth, and no sound came out. Its throat worked. Nothing came. It tried again, harder, body shaking with the effort, and Lior woke with her heart slamming and her hand already reaching under the bed for the knife she kept there and never spoke of.

She lay in her narrow room above the counting house and watched the ceiling go from black to grey, and told herself: *a dream is a dream is a dream.* She had spent her whole life translating the world into entries and columns, debits and credits, and there was no column for this. There was no ledger line for the feeling crawling up her spine, which was that the silence was not empty. Silence was never empty. Silence was a held note. Somewhere far to the north, something enormous was holding its breath, and everything between it and her was holding its breath too, all the way down the long throat of the kingdom, bird by bird, ward by ward—

The wards.

She sat up. The border wards of Cael Morath were older than the crown, older than the Guild, older than any history anyone would admit to owning. They ran the length of the northern marches, a lattice of standing stones and buried silver, and every child learned the rhyme about them before they learned their letters: *the Wards hum high, the Wards hum true, and what walks outside walks round, not through.* The warden's letter had said the stones were humming flat. A half-tone. Like an instrument going out of tune.

Like an instrument being *unstrung*, one string at a time.

Lior got dressed in the dark. This, at least, was a language she was fluent in: moving without being seen, small and quick and silent, down the back stairs, through the counting house where the banked fire breathed its orange light across the ledgers, out into the yard where the frost had furred the cobblestones white.

The mews gate was latched but not locked. She slipped inside, and the smell hit her—hay and musk and the copper tang of blood—and her stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because the messenger foxes were awake, all four of them, pressed into the far corner of their run, hackles up, making a sound she had never heard a fox make. Not a bark. Not a scream. A low continuous whine, like dogs before an earthquake.

And the northern run—the one that faced the Thornmark road—was empty.

Its gate hung open. Not broken. Opened. From the outside or the inside, she couldn't tell, and the harness lay in the straw with the buckles still fastened, which meant the fox had slipped it, which meant—

Which meant nothing, she told herself. Foxes slip harnesses. It happens. It's a Tuesday-morning problem, a column entry, *one (1) messenger fox, escaped, value deducted*—

The yard bell rang.

Once, then again, urgent, the night porter's alarm, and Lior was moving before she decided to move, out of the mews and around the corner of the counting house toward the street gate, where torchlight was jumping yellow against the wall, where voices were shouting, and where—when she reached the arch of the gate and stopped dead—the dying fox lay on the frozen cobblestones.

It was the escaped messenger. She knew it instantly, knew it the way she knew her own hands: the notch in its left ear, the Guild's brand scarred faintly into the harness-line of its shoulder. But it had come a long way since the mews. Forty leagues, if her skin was right, and her skin was right. It was starved down to wire and bone, its red coat gone dull as rust, its flanks heaving in great shuddering pulls, and its paws—she saw this with a clarity that would stay with her for the rest of her life—its paws were worn to the quick. It had run the whole distance. Through the silent country. Through the flat-humming wards. Down the Thornmark road, past the mute songbirds sitting in the trees with their throats working, running on ruined feet toward the one place, the only place, where someone might understand what it had to say.

The night porter was shouting for the handler. Clerks were spilling out in nightshirts, holding candles, complaining. The fox lay in the middle of it all, ignored, a piece of lost property, and Lior walked to it through the crowd the way she did everything—unremarked, unhurried, invisible—and knelt.

Up close she could see it wasn't just starved. There was a wound along its ribs, long and shallow, already blackening, and the flesh around it was wrong—not torn, not infected, but *faded*, like cloth left too long in the sun, like something had passed through it and taken the color and the warmth and left the shape. She had never seen a wound like it. No beast had made it. No blade.

The fox turned its head. Its eyes found hers, and they were not the flat amber eyes of an animal. She had spent her life among animal eyes; she knew their whole vocabulary, hunger and fear and the simple bright present tense of a creature that does not know it will die. These eyes knew. These eyes were looking at her from a long way off, from the top of a hill it had run all night to reach, and they were full of something she had no ledger line for.

Its mouth opened. Its throat worked.

"*Lior.*"

One word. Ragged, cracked, more breath than voice, shaped by a mouth that had never been built for human speech—and yet unmistakable, in the accent of the northern farms, in a voice like dry leaves. Her name. Her actual name, the one written in no Guild record, the one only her dead mother had ever used aloud.

Around her, the clerks heard nothing. A fox crying in pain, they said. The handler should put it down. Someone fetch the brand-iron, log the loss. Their voices came to her from very far away, through water, through wool.

The fox's sides heaved. Blood flecked its muzzle, black in the torchlight. It dragged itself the last few inches across the cobbles—not toward the gate, not toward the warm mews, but toward *her*, pressing its ruined face against her knee with the desperate certainty of a creature delivering a message it had killed itself to carry.

"*Lior.*" A breath now, barely. "*It knows the words. It's learning the words. The king's voice—it wears the king's voice—it ate the singing, girl, it ate the singing and now it's learning—*"

The voice stopped. Not faded. Stopped, cut clean, like a string snapping.

The fox's body stayed warm for a while. Lior knelt on the frozen cobbles with her hand on its cooling flank, and the crowd moved around her, and no one looked at her, and for the first time in twenty-six years she understood that invisibility was not safety. It had never been safety. It had only ever been the darkness before the searching light finds you.

Above the rooftops of Corvane's city, the stars were burning as they had always burned, indifferent and bright. But to the north, low on the horizon, past the towers and the smoke, there was a patch of sky that seemed to her—though she would spend years wondering if she had imagined it—slightly, terribly, less full than the rest.

She rose. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, once, efficiently, the way she balanced a column. Then she went inside, past the sleeping ledgers, down to the archive cellar, and pulled the oldest book on the shelf of anomalies: *Ward-Lattice, Maintenance Of, Historical*, and opened it to the last recorded audit, dated eleven years ago.

Eleven years. The same span, she realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the frost, since King Aldric had withdrawn from public life. Since the beloved king had become the reclusive one. Since the court had stopped speaking of his health in specifics.

Under the floorboards, very softly, the rats began to whisper.
