## 3. The Night Without Stars

The fox's name had been a wound all day.

Lior carried it through the counting house like a splinter under the skin, too deep to draw out, too sharp to forget. *Lior Iversen.* A dying animal had spoken her name in the street below her window, in a voice like dry leaves and blood, and she had spent every hour since pretending it hadn't happened. Pretending was a skill she had practiced for twenty-six years. She was very good at it. She balanced columns of figures with hands that did not shake—mostly—and she did not look at the floorboards, where the rats had gone strange and quiet since dawn.

That quiet should have warned her. Rats were never quiet. The guildhouse rats were the most opinionated creatures she knew, a chattering parliament of whiskers and grievance, and their silence hung beneath the floors like held breath. She caught herself listening for them between entries—a bottled storm, sold twice, second sale unrecorded; a widow's year of good luck, purchased by a shipping magnate with more coin than conscience; three vials of dream-salt, one cracked in transit, which explained why half of Copperlane had dreamed of drowning on the same night last month. Ordinary miracles. Ordinary debts. The ledger lines ran straight and true, and if she kept her eyes on them, the world stayed small enough to survive.

*It knows your name,* the fox had said. *It is coming to collect.*

She had told herself the fox meant nothing. Foxes lied the way children lied—for drama, for attention, because dying made everything feel urgent. But the rats knew something. All day they had been moving in patterns she'd never seen: not fleeing, but gathering. Down in the deep foundations, under the vaults where the Guild kept its most impossible inventory, the rats were congregating in the dark like mourners at a graveside. When she'd pressed her palm flat against the cool stone of the cellar stair that morning, ostensibly checking for damp, she had felt them through the soles of her feet and the bones of her hand—a vast, silent congregation, hundreds of them, facing the same direction.

Facing north. Toward the border.

She wrote *storm, bottled, 2nd sale* into the margin column and her pen dragged, leaving an ink blot like a bruise.

"Master Iversen." Pell, the junior clerk, hovered at the end of her desk with the anxious energy of a man who had been rehearsing a question all day. He was nineteen, earnest, and terrified of her, which suited her fine. "The Guildmaster requests the evening accounts be sealed by midnight. All of them. Even the—" he lowered his voice as though the shelves might gossip, "—the sealed ones."

"The sealed ledgers don't leave the vault," Lior said. "Guild law."

"The Guildmaster's instruction overrides—" Pell swallowed. "He says midnight. He was very specific about midnight."

Corvane was always specific about midnights. Lior looked past Pell's shoulder toward the high windows of the counting house, where the last grey light of dusk was draining out of the sky. There was nothing unusual about the evening. That was what unsettled her—the utter ordinariness of it, the sky behaving exactly as skies behaved, indigo bleeding down to black over the rooftops of Vellamark while lamps bloomed in the streets below like a rash of small warm fires.

"I'll have them ready," she said, and Pell fled with the relief of the dismissed.

She worked. That was the whole of her armor: work, ink, arithmetic, the beautiful clean logic of numbers that never lied even when everyone who wrote them did. Outside, the city settled into its nightly rhythms. Cart wheels on cobbles. A drunk singing three streets over. Somewhere a dog barking—not the frantic bark of alarm but the low, continuous, miserable barking of an animal that wanted to stop and couldn't, and Lior's pen paused again, because she could hear it the way she heard everything, whether she wished to or not.

*I can't find my house,* the dog was saying, or thinking, or broadcasting in that wordless way animals spoke, meaning folded inside sound. *I can't find my house I can't find my house I can't—*

Dogs lost their bearings sometimes. Old dogs, confused dogs. It didn't mean anything.

She finished the evening accounts as the clock in the guildhall tower crawled toward eleven. Around her the counting house emptied, clerks snuffing lamps and gathering cloaks, and no one asked her to walk with them, which was how she preferred it. Invisibility was a craft, and she had mastered it: the beast-speaker who was merely a clerk, the woman who was merely a quill behind a desk, the girl from the burning country whose family had learned too late that smallness was not the same as safety. Her mother had made herself small her whole life and the pyre had found her anyway. So Lior had chosen a different kind of hiding—not smallness but *blandness*, the gray wool of anonymity, a face the city's eye slid past.

At quarter to midnight, the rats began to scream.

Not the squeal of a trapped animal or the shriek of a fight. This was a chorus—a single vast note of terror rising through the foundations of the guildhouse, through the stone, through the soles of her boots, and Lior was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, both palms pressed to the floor, gasping, because the rats weren't screaming words. They were screaming *direction*. Every rat in the building, every rat for perhaps a mile in every direction, was screaming the same thing:

*Down. Hide. It's coming. DOWN. HIDE. IT'S COMING—*

And then, at the stroke of midnight, the stars went out.

*

She saw it through the high windows, and part of her mind—the clerk's part, the part that recorded and filed and refused to feel—would preserve the image forever: the night sky over Vellamark simply *closing*, constellation after constellation winking out not like candles but like eyes shutting, in a wave that rolled from north to south, from the border toward the heart of the kingdom, as though something enormous were swallowing the heavens one mouthful at a time. No clouds. No smoke. The stars were there, and then they were not, and the darkness that replaced them was not the ordinary darkness of night but something thicker, a black with texture, a black that seemed to press against the glass.

Lior stood in the empty counting house with her hands still flat on the floorboards and felt the wards fall.

She had never seen the border wards. Almost no one had—they were older than the kingdom, older than the Guild, a lattice of woven light strung across the northern marches that every child learned about the way children learn about the shape of their own country: it exists, it protects, do not think about it further. But she felt them now, felt them the way you feel a harp string snap somewhere in another room—a vibration through the structure of things, a wrongness in the world's tuning. One string. Then another. Then dozens, then hundreds, the whole great lattice unraveling note by note, each snapped ward ringing through her teeth and the marrow of her spine until she made a sound she didn't recognize as her own voice.

When the last string broke, the silence afterward was absolute.

Even the rats stopped screaming.

Lior became aware, distantly, that she had fallen to her knees among the desks. The darkness outside the windows was total now. Not even the moon—she searched for it with a rising, animal panic and found nothing, no moon, no star, no glow of cloud-reflected light, only that pressing textured black, and beneath it the city beginning to wake. A window shattered somewhere near the docks. A bell began to ring—not the measured tolling of an alarm but frantic, arrhythmic, someone hauling the rope in blind terror. Voices rose in the streets, dozens of voices, then hundreds, the whole sleeping organism of Vellamark convulsing awake into nightmare.

*The fox,* she thought, with a clarity that cut through the panic like a blade through silk. *The fox came three days ago. The birds went mute along the border. The wards hummed wrong. And tonight—*

Tonight the warning and the catastrophe arrived together, and she understood, kneeling in the dark with her heart slamming against her ribs, that they had never been separate events. The fox hadn't been crying doom. The fox had been reporting it. Something had crossed the border long ago—days, weeks, months—and everything since had been the sound of it feeding.

The counting house door banged open. Lamplight—real lamplight, trembling oil-flame—spilled across the floor, and Pell stood in it, white-faced, his cloak half-on, his keys jangling in a shaking fist.

"Master Iversen—the sky—the stars are *gone*—"

"I know."

"The Guildmaster's sealed his own wing. He's ordered the vaults locked—all of them, everything—and the doors barred. Nobody in or out. He says—" Pell's voice cracked. "He says the Guild rides out whatever comes, and anyone outside the walls when they close can stay outside."

Lior rose. Her knees ached. Her palms, she noticed distantly, had left two sweat-damp prints on the floorboards, and around those prints, faint in the lamplight, the wood grain seemed almost to darken—as though the boards themselves remembered the pressure of her gift and flinched from it.

"Pell," she said. "The rats. Have you seen the rats?"

"What?"

"Never mind." She took her cloak from its hook. "Where are the doors being barred?"

"The main gates. But Master Iversen, the Guildmaster said—"

"The Guildmaster isn't out here," Lior said, "in the dark, with whatever dropped the wards coming south." She pulled the cloak around her shoulders and found, to her surprise, that her hands had stopped shaking. That was the terrible thing, the thing she would examine later with something like horror: beneath the fear, beneath the cold flood of dread, there was a heat she recognized and hated. Exhilaration. Twenty-six years of hiding, and the world had just torn the hiding away from everyone at once. Whatever came next, no one would be looking at the plain clerk in the gray wool. They would be looking up—at the empty sky—or down—at the dark—or anywhere but at her.

Except that wasn't true anymore, was it. Something out there knew her name.

She followed Pell down the stairs into chaos. The guildhall's ground floor was bedlam—clerks and factors shouting over one another, porters hauling crates of inventory up from the lower vaults, hoarding in the oldest and most human sense: *mine, mine, get it inside the walls before the world ends.* Someone had knocked over a shelf of preserved curiosities, and a jarred salamander—dormant, thankfully—rolled to Lior's feet, and through the glass she felt its dim sleeping terror pulse against her senses like a second heartbeat. She stepped around it. Everything had a heartbeat tonight. Everything was afraid.

Through the great arched windows of the entrance hall she could see the street beyond, lit by torches now, full of people. Crowds always frightened her, but this crowd frightened her differently, because this crowd was looking up. Hundreds of faces tilted toward a sky with nothing in it, mouths open, and the collective sound they made was not screaming—it was quieter than screaming and far worse. It was the sound of people realizing the dark above them had never been empty, and had only been *waiting* to be empty.

A woman near the gates was holding a birdcage. Inside it, a songbird sat motionless, head tucked, silent—had been silent, the woman was telling anyone who would listen, since yesterday, since the birds all went quiet, and now look, now *look* at the sky—

Lior pushed through the hall toward the doors, and the night watchman grabbed her arm.

"Closed! Guildmaster's orders—nobody leaves!"

"I'm not leaving," Lior said. "I'm standing in the doorway."

Which was true, and also a lie, because standing in the doorway she could hear everything. The city poured its terror into her ears: dogs howling in strings of broken harmony, horses screaming in stables, pigeons erupting from bell towers in blind spirals because their roosts no longer faced the right direction, and underneath it all, fainter than any of it, from far to the north—from the border, from the broken lattice—a silence so complete it registered as sound. A hole in the world's hearing. Something was walking south through that hole, unhurried, and where it walked, the animals did not flee it.

They went quiet before it. Like courtiers before a king.

She stood in the doorway a long time, letting the dark look at her, letting herself look back. Somewhere behind her, Corvane's voice rang through the hall, smooth and unafraid, ordering the hoarding, calming the factors, promising that the Guild had weathered worse—which was a lie, because nothing like this had ever happened, and from the tone of him Lior suspected he knew precisely what had happened, and had known for some time. Midnight. He had wanted the accounts sealed by *midnight*. Before the sky closed. As though he'd had a schedule.

*Later,* she told herself. *Survive first. Understand later.*

But understanding was already arriving, unwelcome, in the voice of a dying fox: *it knows your name, it is coming to collect.* And in the silence of the rats, gathered in the dark beneath her feet, all facing north. And in the texture of that black sky, which was not absence but *appetite*.

The exhilaration scared her more than the terror did. Terror was familiar; she had carried it since childhood, the low constant hum of a life built over a fire. But this heat in her chest—this wild, traitorous lift—came from a truth she had spent twenty-six years denying: that she had never once, in all her careful invisible years, been *needed*. And now the world had broken open, and the only person in Vellamark who could hear what was coming was a woman the law said should burn.

The watchman's grip tightened on her arm. "Back inside, mistress. Curfew's falling. King's men are calling it—"

"King's men," Lior repeated softly. Of course. The palace would respond; the palace always responded; the beloved reclusive king whom no one had seen in years would send his guards into the dark to restore order. She thought of the ledgers she had balanced that evening, the neat columns, the ordinary trades—and beneath them, in the vault, the sealed ledgers she was never permitted to read, the ones Corvane had wanted locked away before midnight struck.

Before the sky closed. As though he'd known.

She turned from the doorway. Above the city, the black pressed down like a lid. Beneath the city, the rats had begun to move—not fleeing now, but flowing, a river of them pouring through the foundations toward the southern wall, away from the north, away from the border, away from the thing that walked in silence. Except for one. One rat had stopped at the base of the cellar stairs, and it sat up on its haunches in the lamplight, whiskers trembling, and looked directly at her with small black eyes that reflected the flame like drops of ink.

It did not speak. Animals rarely used words; meaning simply arrived. But the meaning that arrived, arriving cold and clear as a struck bell, was this:

*You heard us. Now we need you to listen.*

Behind her, the great doors of the Vellum Guild boomed shut, bar by bar, sealing the hoard and the hoarders inside. Lior stood alone in the entry hall, in the torchlight, in the first starless night in the history of the kingdom, and understood that the fox had not come to warn her.

The fox had come to choose her.

* * *

She did not sleep. Sleep required a world with a sky in it, and the sky was gone, and in its place hung that textured black which seemed less like emptiness than like a pupil—vast, dilated, fixed upon the city below it. Toward dawn—if it could be called dawn, if anything would ever be called dawn again—the torches along the Grand Processional burned low, and no one dared relight them all, and the darkness between them grew teeth.

Lior spent the hours in the counting house, because the counting house was hers, the one territory in the world where she knew every board and shadow. She lit a single lamp and set it where it would not show through the windows, and she took out the evening accounts, and she did not read them. Instead she listened.

The city talked all night. Animals talk constantly—that was the secret no one believed, that the world was never silent, that every stable and gutter and rooftop chimed with the small ceaseless gossip of the living—but tonight the talk had changed key. Fear, yes, fear everywhere, fear in every register from the shrill hysteria of caged finches to the low groaning unease of the carthorses. But threaded through the fear, stranger than fear: confusion. Animals navigate by the sky. Stars are their maps, their clocks, their compasses. With the stars gone, the pigeons circled their lofts like thoughts that could not land. The moths, poor creatures, battered themselves against every flame in the city, seeking a light that had been taken from them. Out on the moors, she sensed distantly, the wolves had stopped singing altogether—not silenced but *withheld*, the way a speaker stops mid-word when he realizes someone is listening.

Something was listening to the world. And the world knew it.

Near the coldest hour, the rats returned to her. They came up through the wall cavities and gathered along the rafters above her lamp, dozens of them, silent, watching her with their bead-bright eyes, and their meaning pooled down onto her like cold water:

*The quiet thing has passed the river. It walks the king's road now. It does not hurry. Nothing hurries that has already won.*

"Passed the river," Lior whispered. Her breath fogged in the unheated room. "The border is a hundred leagues north."

*It does not live at the speed you live,* the rats answered, in that wordless way, meaning arriving whole. *It ate the stars the way you eat the crumbs off a table. The wards were not broken, clerk. The wards were swallowed. And it is not coming here.*

Her mouth had gone dry. "Then where—"

The rats flowed down from the rafters and out through the wall, all together, all at once, and their departing meaning hung in the empty air like the after-ring of a bell:

*It is already here. It has been here for years. Ask yourself, little listener, what the palace has been paying for.*

Lior sat very still in the circle of her lamp. Outside, the first grey suggestion of a sunless morning crept against the windows, and the bells of Vellamark began to ring—not alarm this time, but the slow mourning peal, tower answering tower, the city telling itself what it had lost. Somewhere in the vaults below her feet, behind locks she had no key to, the sealed ledgers waited in the dark with their patient, poisonous arithmetic.

*What the palace has been paying for.*

She thought of the hooded client who had come to her desk a week ago, offering a commission she had refused—a commission involving the palace, involving ledgers, involving sums she had pretended not to see. She thought of the fox speaking her name. She thought of Corvane sealing accounts before midnight, precise as a man closing a shop before a storm he'd ordered himself.

Her hand, without consulting her, had already begun pulling a fresh sheet of paper toward the lamp.

For the first time in twenty-six years, Lior Iversen did not want to be invisible.
