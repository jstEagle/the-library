## 4. A Commission in Ash

The city had not slept since the stars went out, and neither had the Guild.

Lior felt it through the floorboards before she heard it: a thousand small feet moving through the walls of the Vellum Guild's counting house, mice and rats and things with too many toes, all of them restless as water about to boil. The beasts had been chattering for three days now—*the dark, the dark, the sky is wrong, the sky is wrong*—and she had stopped trying to quiet them. There was nothing to say. She had looked up at the empty black where the constellations should have been, standing on the counting house roof with her ledger clutched to her chest like a shield, and had understood, finally and completely, that the fox had been telling the truth.

*The wards are down. They are coming. The one who wears the crown—*

She had buried the fox herself, out past the tanneries where the ground was soft, and she had cried while she did it, which was foolish, because she had never even learned its name.

Now she sat at her desk in the third hour after dawn, or what passed for dawn—a gray smear of light that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, since the sun still rose but gave no comfort—and balanced accounts that suddenly meant nothing. A bottled storm, sold twice by accident of clerical error. Six months of a cooper's luck, traded away and regretted. A widow's grief, purchased fair and resold at markup to a merchant who wanted to know how it felt so he could avoid it. The numbers were clean. The columns added. Lior Iversen had spent eleven years making sure that everything in this building balanced, and for eleven years the balancing had been enough.

It was not enough anymore. Nothing was.

"You look terrible," said Master Quill, the head clerk, shuffling past with an armful of correspondence. He was seventy if he was a day, half-blind, and the only person in the Guild who had never once made her feel like something that needed hiding. "Everyone looks terrible. The whole city looks terrible. But you look like you've seen a ghost, and I mean that as a professional observation."

"I didn't sleep," Lior said.

"Nobody slept." He set the letters down and lowered his voice. "Guildmaster's locked himself in the vault since it happened. Won't see anyone. And there's been—" he hesitated, glancing toward the great doors at the front of the counting house, "—someone asking for you. By name."

Lior's pen stopped moving.

"By name," she repeated.

"A woman. Hooded. Been waiting in the receiving room since before first bell. Wouldn't leave a message, wouldn't give a name. Just said she'd wait." Master Quill shrugged. "I told her the Guild doesn't do house calls during a crisis. She said she wasn't here for the Guild." He looked at Lior over his spectacles, and his watery old eyes were sharper than they had any right to be. "She said she was here for *you*, girl. Said you'd refused her commission once already. Said she'd come back when the world ended, and—" he checked a scrap of paper, though Lior suspected he remembered it perfectly, "—'it has.'"

The hooded client. Three weeks ago—or was it four? Time had gone strange since the fox—the figure had come to her desk at the close of business, sliding a sealed letter across the ledgers like a knife. *I need someone who can read what the Guild keeps sealed. Payment negotiable. Discretion absolute.* Lior had read the letter twice, felt the shape of danger underneath the words the way she could feel the shape of a beast's mood under its skin, and had slid it back.

*The Vellum Guild does not open its sealed records,* she had written beneath. *Not for any price.*

She'd thought that would be the end of it. Clients who wanted sealed things opened were clients who got people burned, and Lior had built her whole life around not being burned.

"She's still waiting?" Lior asked.

"Went to warm herself at the brazier not twenty minutes past. Doesn't seem inclined to leave." Master Quill gathered his letters. "For what it's worth—I don't think she's Guild. Wrong boots. Wrong hands. And she watches the door like someone taught her to watch doors, if you take my meaning."

Lior did. Wardens were taught to watch doors. Royal wardens especially.

Her heart was doing something complicated and unpleasant against her ribs. Every instinct she had honed over eleven years of careful, deliberate invisibility said: *walk away. Let her wait until the building burns down around her.* But the fox had died saying her name, the stars were gone, and the wards—whatever remained of them—were down. Invisibility had failed. The world had already found her; the only question left was whether she would meet it on her feet or dragged.

She capped her ink. She squared her ledger. She stood.

"If I'm not back in an hour," she said, "tell the Guildmaster I went to check on the storm in the cellar."

"The storm in the cellar is a rumor we use to frighten apprentices."

"Yes," Lior said. "Tell him that."

* * *

The receiving room of the Vellum Guild was designed to intimidate. It was all dark paneling and high windows, hung with framed contracts from famous trades—a duke's memories, a saint's patience, three minutes of a dead man's time—each one displayed behind glass like a threat. Clients waited here and felt small. That was the point.

The hooded woman did not look small.

She stood at the brazier with her back to the door, and even hunched slightly under the gray hood, there was nothing apologetic about her posture. She was tall—not taller than Lior, but broad-shouldered, solid, the kind of body that had been trained rather than merely lived in. Her boots were travel-worn leather, road-dusted, military in their cut. A sword hung at her hip in a plain scabbard, and she wore it the way other people wore a hand: naturally, constantly, without thinking about it. When Lior closed the door behind her, the woman did not turn all at once. She turned the way a cat turns—first the head, then the shoulders, unhurried, giving nothing away.

"You came," the woman said. Her voice was low and roughened, like gravel washed in a stream. "I half expected you to have me thrown out."

"You've been sitting in my Guild's receiving room for two days asking for me by name. That tends to get a person thrown out." Lior kept her distance, near the door, near the exit. Old habits. "You came back."

"I said I would."

"You said you'd come back when the world ended." Lior crossed her arms. "Was that a prophecy, or just very good timing?"

Something moved under the hood that might have been the ghost of a smile. "A little of both." The woman reached up, and after a pause long enough to be deliberate—long enough for Lior to stop her, if she wanted to, and they both knew it—she pushed the hood back.

Brown hair, cropped short and practical, going gray at the temples. A face that had been handsome once and was now mostly weathered: a scar through the left eyebrow, another along the jaw, skin browned by years of border sun. Eyes the color of wet slate, sharp and tired and watching Lior the way Lior watched ledgers—for what was written between the lines.

"Sera Vane," the woman said. "Formerly Warden-Captain of the Third Border Company. Currently—" she gestured vaguely at herself, the dust, the sword, the whole ruined state of her, "—currently nothing much."

Lior knew the name. Everyone in the Guild knew the name, though they spoke it rarely and quietly. Sera Vane, the warden who had accused her own superiors of selling out the border garrisons. Sera Vane, court-martialed on charges nobody quite believed and stripped of rank anyway. Sera Vane, who had walked out of the capital five years ago with her sword and her reputation and nothing else, and who had spent those five years, according to rumor, doing exactly what disgraced wardens do: drinking, brooding, and occasionally turning up wherever trouble started, like a bad penny with a blade.

"They said you were dead," Lior heard herself say.

"They say a lot of things." Sera Vane looked around the receiving room—at the glass-cased contracts, the intimidating height of the ceiling—with the flat appraisal of someone cataloguing exits. "They also said the border wards were eternal. Crafted by the founders, blessed by nine generations of kings, impregnable. Those wards fell in a single night, Mistress Iversen, and every star in the sky fell with them. So perhaps we should agree that 'they say' isn't worth much anymore."

"Why are you here?"

"Because you're the person I need." Sera stepped away from the brazier, and Lior tensed—but the woman stopped, deliberately, keeping the distance between them. "Three weeks ago I sent you a letter asking for help reading sealed Guild records. You refused. That was sensible. At the time, it was a matter of corruption—ugly, but survivable. Someone in the palace was buying things through your Guild that no loyal servant of the crown should buy, and I wanted to know who and what and why." Her jaw tightened. "Then the stars went out."

"And now?"

"And now it's not corruption anymore. Now it's war." Sera's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun to burn, slow and banked, like coals that had been waiting years for air. "I spent ten years on the border, Mistress Iversen. I knew those wards. I helped maintain them. When they fell, they didn't break—they were *released*. Unwoven from the inside, by someone with access to the anchor-stones. And the night they fell, every star in the kingdom's sky went dark at once, in a pattern too clean to be natural. Something fed on them. Something that has been feeding for a long time, quietly, while everyone looked the other way."

The room felt colder. Lior became aware of her own heartbeat, and beneath it—faint, through stone and timber and the bones of the old building—the skitter of the mice in the walls, agitated, whispering among themselves. *Cold cold cold. The tall one smells of ash. The small one smells of fear and fox.*

"I can't help you," Lior said.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"It doesn't matter. Whatever you want—sealed ledgers, Guild secrets—it will get me killed. Slowly, if I'm lucky. The Guild survives because it stays out of things. Because its clerks are furniture. Because no one—" she stopped, hearing the crack in her own voice, hating it.

"No one sees them," Sera finished quietly. "Yes. I imagine that's comfortable, right up until the moment it isn't." She reached into her coat—slowly, telegraphing every movement—and drew out a folded piece of vellum, worn soft at the creases. She did not offer it. She simply held it where Lior could see the broken wax seal: the crowned stag of the royal house, split cleanly in half. "Do you know what this is?"

Lior did. Anyone in the trade would. "An old writ. Pre-collapse issue."

"A writ of immunity. Signed by the Crown Prosecutor's office five years ago, when I still had allies, back when my accusations almost mattered. It grants legal protection to any individual whose testimony is deemed material to an investigation of treason at the highest level." Sera's slate eyes held hers. "It was meant for witnesses. People who knew things. People who were afraid."

"People like me," Lior said slowly.

"People exactly like you." Sera set the writ down on the table between them, carefully, the way one sets down something fragile—or dangerous. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once. Help me read the Guild's sealed ledgers—all of them, the real ones, not the pretty copies. Help me trace the payments. In exchange, this writ covers you. Whatever you are, whatever you've hidden, whatever the old law says should happen to people like us"—and here, for the first time, something flickered behind her eyes, some old wound briefly visible—"it won't matter. Immunity. Protection. Legal existence, for the first time in your life, in the open."

The silence stretched. In the walls, the mice had gone utterly still, listening with her.

"You don't know what I am," Lior said at last.

"No," Sera agreed. "But I know what the fox was."

The words landed like a dropped stone in still water. Lior went cold all over, from the scalp down.

"There was a fox," Sera continued, relentlessly gentle, "found dead outside the Tanners' Gate three weeks ago. Burned from the inside—warden's term for it, when something speaks through an animal and the animal's body pays the toll. A hedge-witch I trust pulled what remained of the message before the body cooled. One phrase, spoken in a voice not the fox's own. *The wards are down. The one who wears the crown.* Addressed, by name, to a clerk of the Vellum Guild." Sera tilted her head. "So either you're the most remarkable ordinary bookkeeper in this kingdom, Mistress Iversen—or you hear things you shouldn't be able to hear. And given that the kingdom's law burns people for the second thing, and the kingdom's enemy seems to need the second thing..." She spread her hands. "I'm betting on remarkable."

Lior could not speak. The fox. The fox's death had traveled; the fox's words had traveled; somewhere between the tanneries and this room, her secret had grown legs. She should have been terrified. She was—her hands were shaking—but underneath the terror was something else, something she hadn't felt in eleven years of careful furniture-hood:

Being *seen*. And finding, to her astonishment, that she was still standing.

"The law burns beast-speakers," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Has for two hundred years. That's not a superstition. That's statute."

"I know the statute. I used to enforce it." Sera's face did something complicated—shame, maybe, worn smooth by years of carrying it. "That's part of why I'm offering immunity instead of arrest. I've done my share of enforcing. I'd like, before the end, to do one right thing with the same authority." She tapped the writ. "This is real. My remaining allies in the Prosecutor's office honor it. And I'll add a personal guarantee on top: no one learns what you are from me. Not ever. Not under torture, not at the end of the world. You have my word, and my word is the only thing I have left that's worth anything."

Outside the high windows, the false gray light shifted. Somewhere in the city a bell began tolling—not the alarm, Lior realized, just the hour. The city had taken to ringing bells constantly, day and night, because silence reminded everyone too much of the soundless sky.

"And what happens," Lior asked, "if the ledgers show what I think they'll show?"

Sera Vane met her eyes without flinching. "Then we'll know who killed the sky. And knowing is the only weapon anyone's offered me in five years."

Lior looked at the writ on the table. Broken seal. Old wax. Five years old and probably worthless, legally speaking—she could read contracts, she knew exactly how many loopholes a document like that contained, and there were dozens. It would not protect her. Not really. Nothing would protect her, not truly, not once she opened the sealed ledgers and wrote her name into the story of the falling sky.

But the fox had crawled across half the kingdom to die at her feet with her name in its mouth. The stars had gone out. The world had already torn the mask off; the only choice left was whether she picked it back up or let it burn.

And underneath everything—underneath the terror and the trembling hands—there was that exhilaration again, terrible and bright, the feeling of the cage door swinging wide whether she was ready or not.

"If I do this," Lior said slowly, "we do it my way. I read the ledgers here, in the Guild, where I control the room. We tell no one. Not your allies, not the Prosecutor, no one. And when this is over—whatever 'over' means now—you walk away and you never use my name again."

"That sounds like a man planning his escape route."

"It sounds like a woman who intends to survive this."

For a moment—one unguarded moment—something warmed in Sera Vane's weathered face. Not a smile, exactly. Recognition, maybe. One careful creature seeing another.

"One condition of mine, then," Sera said. "When we find the truth—and we will—we don't bury it. Whatever it costs. Whoever it implicates." She extended her hand across the table, rough-palmed, sword-callused, steady. "Deal?"

Lior looked at the hand. Then she took it. The grip was firm, brief, honest—a soldier's handshake, sealing a bargain neither woman entirely trusted and both entirely intended to keep.

"Deal," Lior said.

* * *

They worked through the rest of the false morning and into the grayer afternoon, in the records room deep beneath the counting house, where the Guild kept what it did not display. Lior had keys for three of the seven locks on the sealed archive; she borrowed a fourth from Master Quill with a lie about a tax audit, improvised a fifth with a hairpin and thirty years of watching locksmiths, and simply stared at the last two until, with a creak of ancient hinges that sounded almost like laughter, she discovered they had never actually been locked at all. Corvane's confidence, presumably. Or his arrogance. She filed both possibilities away.

Inside: the sealed ledgers. Black-bound, iron-cornered, chained to their shelf like prisoners. Lior lifted the first one down and felt the weight of it settle into her arms—years of transactions the Guild pretended did not exist. Sales of things that should not be sellable. Purchases by clients who should not exist. And, threaded through all of it like a vein of ore through worthless rock, payments.

Sera cleared a space at the worktable and laid out her own materials: maps of the border, copies of warden dispatches, a list of dates. "Start with the year the king withdrew from public life," she said. "Ten years ago. Everything changed after that. If your ledgers go back that far—"

"They go back further," Lior said. "The Guild has been trading in impossible things for three hundred years." She broke the ledger's chain-lock with the small key Corvane believed she didn't know she had, and opened it to the first page.

The entries were coded, of course. Everything important was. But Lior had spent eleven years learning the Guild's private shorthand the way she'd learned birdsong: patiently, obsessively, by ear. She translated aloud, line by line, while Sera cross-referenced dates and locations, and slowly, entry by entry, a pattern began to surface from the black pages like a body rising through dark water.

Payments. Enormous, systematic, recurring payments—routed through cutouts and shell trades, laundered through a dozen fictitious commissions—but always flowing in the same direction. Toward the palace. Toward, if the final routing marks meant what Lior thought they meant—

"Maintenance of the crown," she read aloud, and stopped.

"What?" Sera leaned closer. "What is it?"

Lior turned the ledger toward her. "Every quarter. For ten years. Payments to the palace, marked 'maintenance of the crown.' Increasing every year. Escalating sharply these last three." Her finger traced the column of sums, each one large enough to buy a ship, an estate, a small town. "Wardens maintain the wards. Masons maintain walls. What—" and her voice went quiet without her willing it, "—what exactly does the crown need *maintained*, Captain Vane? Ten years of payments, growing every quarter, for the upkeep of a single object on a single head?"

Sera had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice had the flat, controlled tone of a woman holding something enormous at arm's length. "Ten years ago, King Aldric stopped appearing in public. Ten years ago, the border reports start mentioning... irregularities. Silences. Animals fleeing the passes." Her eyes came up to meet Lior's, and in them was the same dread Lior felt spreading through her own chest, cold and certain as floodwater. "Ten years ago is when the fox's warning starts making sense."

*Maintenance of the crown.* The words sat on the page, neat and clerical and monstrous.

Somewhere above them, muffled by floors and stone, the Guild's front bell rang. A visitor. Lior barely heard it. She was looking at the ledger, and the ledger was looking back, and the trail of gold ran upward, entry by entry, quarter by quarter, decade by decade—

Straight to the throne.

Above them, faintly, they both heard Master Quill's voice, strained and formal: "Guildmaster Corvane will see you now—"

And beneath the floorboards, in the dark, the mice began, very softly, to scream.
