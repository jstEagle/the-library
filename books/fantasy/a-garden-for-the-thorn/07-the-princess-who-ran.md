## 7. The Princess Who Ran

The bells of Saint Verity's rang at dawn, and Isolde Vael-Aldric did not hear them.

She had learned, over ten years of practice, to sleep through the convent's summons the way she had once learned to curtsy: thoroughly, mechanically, and without any part of her heart involved. The bell meant prayer. Prayer meant the chapel. The chapel meant an hour on cold stone with her knees aching while Sister Maerin droned through the liturgy of the Unseen Hand, whose doctrine held that all things were watched, all things were weighed, and all things—eventually—were forgiven.

Isolde had stopped believing in forgiveness around the same time she'd stopped believing her father's smile.

She rose instead in the grey hour before the bells, as she always did, and dressed in the dark by touch. The habit they had given her was rough wool, undyed, the color of ash and penance. It fit badly. Everything here fit badly—the shoes, the silence, the shape of a life that had been cut down and folded smaller and smaller until what remained could be hidden in a cell no larger than a tomb. She had arrived at Saint Verity's at nineteen years old, screaming inside where no one could hear it. She was twenty-nine now, and she had stopped screaming, which frightened her more than the screaming ever had.

Her cell held three things that mattered. A cot. A candle stub she was technically forbidden to burn after compline. And a floorboard beneath the cot, loose at one corner, beneath which lay ten years of proof.

She knelt and lifted the board the way other women might have lifted a lover's hand.

Inside: papers. Dozens of them, hundreds, each one copied out twice in her own small, careful script—the handwriting of a girl who had been taught that documents were weapons and signatures were wounds. Letters from palace servants, bribed with her jewelry and then with her word. Accounts from a laundress who had noticed that the king's linen stopped bearing sweat-stains in the same season his eyes stopped blinking properly. A physician's marginalia, purchased for the price of a pardon, describing how His Majesty's pulse had come to beat *twice for every breath, regular as machinery, like no living heart she had ever listened to.* A drawing Isolde had made herself at fourteen, from memory, of the exact angle at which her father's pupils had caught the candlelight that last winter—flat, reflective, like a cat's, like glass.

And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, the oldest piece: a letter in her father's own hand, dated nine days before everything changed. Nine days before he stopped knowing her.

*My little wren,* it read. *I have signed something I should not have. There is a thing in the crown that speaks when I sleep, and I fear I have given it permission to speak when I wake. If you are reading this, do not confront me. Whatever wears my face will use your love against you. Go to the border. Be patient. Be small.*

*Be small.* As if she were capable of anything else. As if she had not stood in the great hall before four hundred courtiers and a visiting delegation from the Salt Reaches, nineteen years old and shaking, and said, clearly enough to be heard in the back galleries: *"That man is not my father."*

They had not even let her finish the sentence. Her aunt had fainted theatrically. The Lord Chamberlain had called it grief-madness, then hysteria, then—when she would not recant—treason of the blood. The word *exile* had never actually been spoken aloud; the crown preferred euphemisms the way it preferred its kings breathing and docile. They had said she was being sent to *devotional retreat*. They had said it gently, the way you tell someone the water is fine while holding their head under.

Ten years. Ten years of copying letters by candlelight, of memorizing guard rotations from the convent's hilltop view of the border road, of befriending pilgrims and merchants and one extremely talkative wool-factor who passed through twice a year and never asked why the princess in the ash-colored habit paid him in silver to carry sealed packets to a woman in the capital who owed Isolde favors older than either of them. Ten years of building, thread by thread, a net of evidence so complete that no honest court in the world could look at it and deny her.

And every year the court grew less honest, and the world grew less likely to ever ask.

She sat back on her heels in the pre-dawn dark, the letter open on her knees, and permitted herself—as she permitted herself perhaps twice a year—the full weight of what she had lost. Not the crown. She had never wanted the crown. She had lost the *belief*, the simple animal faith of a child who had once fallen asleep against her father's shoulder listening to stories about the star-wards and the old magic, safe in the certainty that the man holding her would rather die than let harm cross the walls. That faith had died in a single winter. What replaced it was this: a woman who kept proof under a floorboard and knew, with the cold certainty of a decade's accumulation, that she had been right.

Being right had cost her everything, and bought nothing. That was the arithmetic no ledger balanced.

Below the window, the valley of Saint Verity's spread out grey and silver in the first light. The convent sat on the last fold of hills before the borderlands began—the reason she'd been sent here, she'd understood later. Not punishment. *Containment.* Close enough to the wild country to be forgotten, far enough from the capital never to be believed. Beyond those hills lay the wards, the great humming lattice of old magic that had guarded the kingdom since before there were kings, and on clear nights she could sometimes feel it through the stone—a faint pressure behind her teeth, like a storm deciding whether to arrive.

Lately, the pressure had been wrong.

For three weeks now the birds along the valley road had gone quiet—not flown off, just *silent*, sitting in the hawthorns with their feathers puffed and their heads cocked as though listening to something underneath the world. The convent's few goats refused to graze the eastern meadow. And two nights ago, Sister Anwen had come running from the laundry babbling about the foxes: a whole family of them standing in a line at the wall, staring at the sisters, until one of the novices screamed and they turned and walked away together, unhurried, *in step*, like soldiers.

Isolde had written it all down. That was the only prayer she still performed faithfully: observation, transcription, witness.

She was wrapping the letter back in its oilcloth when she heard the knock.

Three raps. Paused. Two more. The signal she had established years ago with exactly one person alive, and that person was supposed to be a hundred leagues away in the capital, and Isolde's heart went from sleeping to galloping between the second rap and the third.

She slid the floorboard closed, stood, and opened the door.

Sera Vane filled the doorway like a blade fills a sheath—wrong shape for it, too sharp, held there by habit alone. The former royal warden looked worse than the last time Isolde had seen her, two years past, when Sera had come seeking corroboration for her own disgraced suspicions and left with copies of half Isolde's archive. Her cloak was travel-ruined. There was a healing cut above her eyebrow. And her eyes, which had always been the flat grey of a soldier's patience, were burning now with something Isolde had never seen in them before.

Fear.

"You shouldn't be here," Isolde said quietly. "If you're seen—"

"Everyone who could see me is busy." Sera slipped inside and shut the door with the softness of long habit. "The city's in chaos. Half the palace guard is drunk and the other half is praying. Isolde—" She stopped. Started again, differently, the way people speak when the words themselves frighten them. "The stars went out."

"I know. We saw it here. Three nights ago."

"Not just here. *Everywhere*. And the wards fell with them—all of them, the whole lattice, like strings snapping. The northern beacon towers are dark. Things are already coming across the border that haven't crossed in living memory." Sera pulled off her gloves finger by finger, a gesture Isolde recognized: the warden buying time to say the unbearable thing. "But that's not why I'm here."

"No," Isolde said. "It isn't. You've found something."

"I found a beast-speaker."

The words landed strangely, and Isolde waited.

"She works for the Vellum Guild—hid herself in their ledgers for years, hid her gift so deep even the Guild didn't know what she was. Or thought they didn't." Sera's mouth twisted. "She can hear animals. Speak with them. And she followed a trail through the Guild's sealed accounts—payments, enormous ones, routed into the palace for a decade. Line items marked *maintenance of the crown*."

The candle stub. Isolde's hands moved before her mind did—struck light, cupped the flame, and set it on the sill so that Sera could see her face, and she could see Sera's. This was how they worked, these two women who trusted no one: everything important was confirmed in visible expressions, nothing left to shadow.

"The ledgers," Isolde said. "You brought them?"

"Brought what we could copy before the Guild locked down. Lior copied the rest into her memory—she has a head for figures that frightens me." Sera reached into her coat and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, and despite everything—despite the dead stars and the fallen wards and ten years of ash-colored wool—Isolde felt her mouth twitch.

"We match," she said. "Oilcloth."

"Habit of careful women." Sera set the packet on the cot between them but did not let go of it. "Before you open that, you need to hear the rest. Because the payments aren't the worst of it. Your beast-speaker—Lior—she talked to the animals at the broken border. Wild things, old things. And they remember." Sera's voice dropped. "Something crossed into the palace a long time ago. Something that wears the king. It drank the stars to stay fed, and it's been drinking the kingdom's magic for years, and the payments—the *maintenance of the crown*—that wasn't the court paying to protect Aldric. That was the court paying to keep whatever is wearing him *comfortable*."

The candle flame bent in a draft from under the door and straightened itself. Somewhere below, the dawn bells finally finished their peal, and the silence afterward seemed louder than the ringing.

Isolde looked at the packet. Ten years. Ten years of floorboards and bribed laundresses and a physician's trembling marginalia, ten years of being called mad by people who preferred a comfortable lie to an uncomfortable princess—and now a stranger's stranger had walked out of the capital carrying confirmation in a merchant's accounting hand, because a woman who spoke with rats had followed the money where Isolde could never go.

Vindication should have tasted like triumph. It tasted like copper. It tasted like the moment she had imagined a thousand times—*I told you, I told you, I was right*—arriving at last, only to reveal that being right changed nothing, saved no one, restored nothing. Her father had not been a traitor. Her father had been *food*. He had been drowning for twenty years while she stood on the bank calling him a monster, exiled herself gladly, built her life around the elegant architecture of her own righteous certainty—and the truth was crueler than treason. The truth was that the man she had accused might have been begging, all along, in the only language his captor allowed him.

*Do not confront me. Whatever wears my face will use your love against you.*

He had tried to protect her. Even drowning, he had tried to protect her, and she had spent ten years hating the shape of his drowning.

"Isolde." Sera's voice, gentle in a way that clearly cost her. "Breathe."

"I'm fine." She was not fine. She opened the packet anyway, because grief was a luxury and the night was short, and because there would be time—God, she hoped there would be time—to fall apart later, privately, in the fashion of princesses: thoroughly, mechanically, and without any part of it showing.

The pages inside were copied in a small, precise hand, columns of figures marching in ranks, and interspersed among them notes in the same script, sharper, more urgent. At the top of the first page, one line was underlined twice:

*The crown is the wound. Follow the payments to the source.*

"Who wrote this?" Isolde asked, though she already knew.

"Lior. The beast-speaker." Sera hesitated. "There's more you should know about her. About what she can do. The animals at the border—they didn't just tell her about the Regent. They *obeyed* her. Hundreds of them, all at once. I've seen wardens do a lot of impossible things, but I've never seen that." A pause. "And neither has the Guild. Or the palace. Which means if anyone learns what she is—"

"They'll burn her," Isolde said flatly. "The way they burned the hedge-witch at Carrowmere when I was twelve. The law hasn't changed. It's just been waiting."

"It's waiting for her specifically now."

Isolde looked up from the pages. Outside, the sun was clearing the hills, and the light that came through her cell's narrow window was wrong—it had been wrong for three nights—thin and slightly grayed, as though the sky itself were convalescing from some terrible expenditure. The stars had drunk the night dry and left the days tasting of ash.

"Then she'd better stay hidden," Isolde said, "until we've finished. Because I am going to the palace, Sera. I'm going to stand in front of that thing wearing my father's face, and I'm going to look into its eyes, and I'm going to know—for certain, with my own sight instead of a decade of paper—if any part of him is still in there." Her voice did not shake. She had practiced that for ten years too. "And if there is—even a sliver, even a drowning man's last bubble of air—then everything changes. Then this stops being an accusation and becomes a rescue."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then it becomes justice." She squared the pages, tied the packet, and slid it beneath the loose floorboard beside her father's letter—old proof and new, lying side by side in the dark like two halves of a sentence. "Either way, I stop running today. Ten years is enough. I want my name back."

Sera studied her for a long moment. "The palace won't let you within a league of the gates. You're still treason-of-the-blood. The moment your face shows on the capital road, every guard between here and the keep gets orders."

"Then I won't show my face." Isolde rose, and took from the bottom of her trunk—the trunk she had not opened since the day she arrived—the one possession she had carried out of the palace a decade ago: a plain traveling cloak, merchant's grey, and beneath it a courier's satchel bearing the seal of the Vellum Guild, acquired years ago from her wool-factor friend for precisely this eventuality, filed away under *someday* alongside all her other weapons. "Guild auditors move freely. Even now. *Especially* now—half the kingdom's accounts need settling after the wards fell, and the Guild guards its commissions like hawks. If your beast-speaker got in as an auditor once, she can get us in again."

"She?" Sera's eyebrows rose. "How do you know Lior's plan involves going back?"

"Because she underlined the sentence twice." Isolde fastened the cloak at her throat. Under the wool, over her heart, hung the one thing she wore that the convent had never given her: a thin cord, and on it a child's cipher scratched into a wooden bead—*wren*, in the private alphabet her father had invented for her when she was six, back when he was still entirely her father. "People who underline twice are people who intend to return. I know the type. I've been one for a decade."

She blew out the candle, and the grey daylight took the room.

"Saddle whatever the convent owns that looks like a merchant's mule," she said. "We ride within the hour. And Sera—" she paused at the door, hand on the latch, and for one unguarded breath the princess showed through the exile like fire through a shutter-crack— "if my father is still alive in there, after everything, after ten years of my hatred—then I owe him a decade of apologies, and I intend to start paying them the moment that thing stops wearing his mouth."

She opened the door onto the corridor, and the bells of Saint Verity's began to ring the hour—and stopped, mid-peal, all five bells at once, as though a hand had closed over them.

In the ringing absence, from the hawthorn tree outside the window, a single bird began to sing.

It sang her name.
