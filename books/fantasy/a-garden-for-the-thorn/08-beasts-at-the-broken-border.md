## 8. Beasts at the Broken Border

They left the city before dawn, when the ash-colored light made even the beggars look like ghosts, and Lior spent the first hour of the ride pretending she was not terrified.

It was not the journey that frightened her. Three days north to the border wards was nothing—she had walked farther for the Guild, hauling ledgers through worse weather with less company. It was the company itself. Sera Vane rode ahead like a woman who expected the road to betray her, one hand never far from the short sword at her hip, and Princess Isolde—*the princess*, though she had forbidden the title with a look that could freeze a river—rode beside Lior in silence, her hood up, her eyes on the horizon as if it owed her money.

Three women, three secrets apiece, and one dying fox's warning between them.

Lior kept her hands busy with the reins and her mind busy with arithmetic. Numbers were safe. Numbers stayed where you put them. She calculated their pace against the light, their provisions against the days, the cost of three horses against the Guild account she had emptied in the night—a theft so clean Corvane would not find it for a week, and by then, if the fox spoke true, he would have larger concerns.

Beneath the arithmetic, though, the other thing waited. The thing she had spent thirty years folding smaller and smaller, until it fit inside a ledger line.

*You can hear them.*

The fox had said it with its blood foaming black at the muzzle, its amber eyes fixed on her across the counting-house floor while every clerk in the room saw only a dying animal staggering out of the dark. *The wards did not fall, beast-speaker. They were drunk.*

She had told no one what the fox said. Not all of it. Sera knew about the gift—the warden had pried it out of her in the Guild's back rooms with the patience of a woman dismantling a lock—and Isolde knew something, because Isolde missed nothing, but neither of them knew the whole of it. Neither of them knew that Lior had understood the fox perfectly, or that the fox had known *her*, had spoken her name like a summons, like a debt called in after decades of quiet.

The road climbed through farmland gone strangely still. That was the wrongness none of them could stop noticing: no crows over the plowed fields, no sparrows in the hedgerows, no dogs barking at farmsteads as they passed. The farms themselves stood empty—not abandoned, but *emptied*, doors hanging open, meals half-eaten, as if the people had been called away mid-bite. Twice they passed refugees streaming south, carts piled with children and chickens, and both times the refugees stared at the sky as they walked, as if they'd left something up there they meant to go back for.

The stars were still gone. Four nights now. The sun rose and set, sullen and dim behind a haze that no one could explain, and the moon showed herself as a bruised coin, but the stars were simply *absent*, a hole where the night should have been sewn shut.

"They're saying in the villages it's an omen," Isolde said, breaking her silence for the first time since morning. Her voice was low, roughened—she had spent ten years in a convent on the border, and it showed in her accent now, the soft court vowels worn down to something plainer. "That the gods have turned their faces away."

"Gods don't drink," Sera said from ahead, without turning. "I've read the old warden records. Whatever took the stars took them the way a leech takes blood. Methodically."

"And you believe your records?"

"I believe patterns." Sera reined in slightly, letting the other two draw level. Her eyes were gray and unromantic, the eyes of a woman who had been disgraced for being right once too often. "Wards don't fail all at once. A ward is a woven thing—starlight and stone and oath. If it frays, it frays from the edges inward, over years. Ours fell in a single night, all along four hundred miles of border, like someone cut the warp threads with scissors. That's not failure. That's harvest."

Lior said nothing. But her stomach tightened, because the fox had used almost the same word. *Drunk.* Harvested. Fed upon.

Isolde glanced at her. The princess had a way of looking at Lior that felt like being read—like being a ledger opened to a page Lior had forgotten to blur. "You've been quiet," Isolde said. "Quieter than quiet."

"She's listening," Sera said.

"To what? There's nothing alive out here."

"That," Lior said, and was surprised by how steady her own voice came out, "is exactly what I'm listening to."

* * *

They made the border on the evening of the third day, and Lior felt it before she saw it.

It began as a pressure behind her ears, like descending too fast into a valley. Then a ringing, faint and wrong, the ghost of a sound that should be there and wasn't. By the time the land crested and she saw the ward-line, her teeth ached and her eyes were watering and every hair on her arms stood on end.

The old ward-stones marched across the hills like a spine of giants—monoliths taller than three men, each carved with the lattice-script of the First Kingdom, each one lit for six hundred years with a pale inner glow that farmers had built their calendars around. Lior had seen them once as a child, from a distance, a string of cold stars pinned to the earth.

Now they were dead. All of them, as far as the eye could follow the line into the haze. Gray stone, dark script, and around the base of each monolith a circle of earth so bleached it looked like snow. Nothing grew there. Nothing *would* grow there; Lior could feel that in her bones, in the place where the gift lived, the way you feel a tooth missing with your tongue.

And the silence—

Lior swung down from her horse and nearly fell, because the silence had weight. It pressed on her like deep water. In the city there was always noise beneath noise: rats gossiping in the walls, pigeons muttering on the sills, the great slow murmur of ten thousand small minds going about their small business. She had lived her whole life inside that murmur the way other people live inside the sound of the sea, so constantly that she had stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Here, the murmur was gone. Not quiet—*gone*, scooped out, and in its absence she could feel the shape of what had been taken, the way an amputee feels the hand.

"There's nothing," she whispered. "Sera, there's nothing *alive* here. No mice. No beetles. No worms in the soil. For—" she cast along the dead line with her mind, carefully, the way you test ice, "—for miles. Everything that could crawl or fly or burrow has either fled or—"

"Or what?" Isolde asked quietly.

"Or been eaten."

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well. Sera dismounted, drew her sword, and began walking the ward-line with the measured tread of a woman inspecting a fortification, which was, Lior supposed, exactly what she was doing. Isolde stood at the base of the nearest monolith, her bare hand hovering an inch from the stone, not touching, her face lit gray by the starless dusk.

"It's warm," she said. "Or it was. You can feel where the heat went out of it. Like a hearth this morning."

Lior barely heard her. Because she had found something.

At the very edge of her range—farther than she had ever reached, farther than she had ever dared reach, because reaching was dangerous, reaching was how they found you, reaching was the pyre—there was life. Faint. Clustered. Huddled the way sparrows huddle in a hedge before a storm.

Not gone after all. *Pushed.* Everything within miles of the ward-line had fled outward, and everything that fled had gathered somewhere beyond the dead ground, and they were waiting, and they were afraid, and they had been waiting for someone who could hear them.

For her.

Lior stood in the bleached grass with her heart slamming against her ribs and understood that she had a choice, and that she had already made it, sometime in the last three days, maybe sometime in the last thirty years. Every step north had been a step toward this. The fox had come to her door for a reason. The silence had let her hear its shape for a reason.

"Lior?" Isolde's voice, sharp with alarm. "Your nose is bleeding."

She touched her upper lip. Blood, bright against her fingers. Reaching hurt when you reached that far—but the pain was oddly clarifying, like cold water. She wiped it away and looked at the two women watching her with such different expressions: Sera wary and ready, Isolde fierce and frightened on her behalf.

"There are animals," Lior said. "Beyond the dead zone. Thousands of them. They came here when the wards fell—they've been waiting at the edge of it for three days, and they won't cross, and they won't leave." She swallowed. "They're waiting for me. I think—I think they've been waiting for anyone who could listen. And no one has come. No one ever comes."

"So talk to them," Sera said.

As if it were simple. As if it weren't the thing that got people burned. Lior laughed—a cracked, breathless sound—and the laugh surprised all three of them.

"If I do this," she said, "you'll see what I am. Fully. There's no ledger-line to hide it behind. Are you certain—"

"We crossed a kingdom on the word of a dying fox," Isolde said. "I think we're past certainty. Go."

* * *

She walked out alone, because some things cannot be witnessed comfortably, and because part of her—the old part, the hiding part—still wanted a few more steps of distance between herself and anyone who might later describe what they saw.

The dead ground crunched under her boots like old parchment. The ward-stones loomed and gave nothing back. And at the edge of the bleached circle, where living grass began again in a ragged fringe, the animals were there.

They rose out of the dusk like a tide coming in.

Deer first, dozens of them, standing in a ring with their heads high and their nostrils wide. Then wolves—wolves, sitting calmly among the deer, which told Lior more than any speech could have about how badly things had broken. Crows in the thorn trees, hundreds, silent. Foxes and badgers and hares, a brown bear at the rear like a hill that had decided to attend, hawks on the stones, voles and weasels threading between the larger bodies. Thousands of eyes catching the last gray light.

Every mind among them was open. That was the horror and the mercy of it: wild animals keep their thoughts behind walls of instinct, a blur of hunger and territory, and a speaker must knock and knock to be let in. These minds were flung open like houses after a fire, all their doors burnt away.

*Liar,* said a voice, and it took Lior a moment to understand it came from everywhere—from all of them at once, a chorus speaking with one throat. *Man comes. Man always comes. Man takes.*

"I haven't come to take," Lior said aloud, and her voice sounded absurdly small, and also, for the first time in her life, entirely hers. "I've come to ask."

Silence. The vast, weighted attention of thousands of creatures narrowing onto one small woman with blood dried on her upper lip.

Then the bear came forward.

It moved slowly, gravely, the way old men move in council, and when it stood before her she saw that it was ancient, its muzzle silvered, one ear torn by some long-ago fight. It sat back on its haunches like a storyteller taking a chair, and when it spoke, its mind-voice was deep and slow as a glacier, and it spoke in images first, because bears remember in images:

*We will show you.*

The night of the falling came into her head—but not as she had imagined it. Not as a catastrophe. As a *meal*.

She saw the ward-line as the animals had always sensed it: a fence of cold light running along the earth, humming a note too high for human ears, fed drop by drop by the stars overhead. She had known, distantly, that the wards drank starlight—that was in the oldest Guild records, buried in footnotes. What she had never known was that the animals could hear the drinking. Six hundred years of a soft, endless sip, the world's longest meal, and the wild things had made their peace with it, the way livestock make peace with the farmer.

Then, on the night the stars went out, the sipping had stopped.

And something had begun to *gulp*.

Lior gasped aloud, and the bear held her steady with one dark eye, pouring the memory into her whether she could bear it or not. She saw—through a thousand scattered pairs of eyes, deer and crow and vole, a mosaic of witnesses—the darkness moving along the ward-line. Not wind. Not smoke. A *shape*, vast and patient, bending over the monoliths the way a man bends over a bowl, and where it bent, the light ran backward out of the stones, up into the dark, and the dark grew denser, richer, *pleased*. And threaded through that darkness, faint as a watermark, she saw a second shape: smaller, upright, crowned.

A king's silhouette, worn like a glove.

*Long,* the bear rumbled, and the word arrived with the weight of centuries. *Long ago. Before your mother's mother. It came across the border in the old war, when the wards were young and thin. Small then. A splinter. It crawled into the strongest vessel it could find—the man who wore the crown—and it slept in him, and it grew, and it learned to steer him gently, so gently, the way a rider steers a horse it does not wish to spook.*

"The king," Lior whispered. "Aldric."

*The crown is a door,* the bear said. *It has been drinking him for ten years, growing fat enough to reach. The wards fed it starlight for centuries, drop by drop—it learned the taste from outside the fence. Ten years ago it was strong enough to begin. It steered the king's hand. It bought and sold through the king's mouth. And when it had grown vast, it drank the wards dry in one night, and the stars with them, because the stars are where it keeps its larder, and it means to empty the sky before it moves on.*

"Moves on," Lior repeated. Her mouth was dry. "To where?"

The bear was silent a long moment. When the answer came, it came not in words but in a single image, and the image was worse than anything the words could have carried: the shape of the darkness lifting its head from the drained border, turning south, toward the glittering scatter of the city at night—toward every lamp, every ember, every sleeping human spark.

Toward everything that shone.

Behind her, she heard hoofbeats—Sera and Isolde, unable to bear the distance any longer, riding out across the bleached ground. They pulled up at the ring's edge, and the animals parted for the horses without fear, which frightened Liro more than anything else so far, because wild things do not part for anything unless they have agreed to.

"What did it tell you?" Sera demanded, sword drawn, scanning the treeline out of habit.

Lior turned in the saddle—or rather, she realized she was standing again, she didn't remember standing—and looked at the two of them, and heard herself say, in a voice she did not recognize as her own because it was not small anymore:

"It's been in the palace for twenty years. It wore Aldric like a coat and it ate his kingdom by degrees, and now it has finished the wards and it will finish the stars, and when the sky is empty it will turn on us, because we burn too." She wiped the last of the blood from her lip. "And the beasts have known for twenty years. They've been trying to warn anyone who would listen. Farmers drove them off. Hunters shot the messengers. Priests called them omens and burned the wise-women who listened." She laughed again, that cracked sound, but steadier now. "I spent my whole life hiding the one thing that was ever asked of me. The whole kingdom was shouting, and I stuffed my ears with ledger paper."

"You couldn't have known," Isolde started.

"I know *now*." Lior looked east, where the road ran back toward the capital, toward the silent palace and the second heartbeat she had not yet heard but already dreaded. "And knowing is a debt. I've spent my life balancing accounts for other people. Time I settled one of my own."

Sera sheathed her sword slowly. "The palace is three hundred miles south, and whatever wears the crown already knows we're coming. Warden records say the Regent—"

"The Regent," Lior said, and the animals behind her stirred, a ripple of hatred passing through five thousand bodies like wind through wheat. "Yes. They have a name for it too. An older name than yours." She met Sera's eyes. "And they've just told me something else. Something it will do soon, something it must do, and when it does, there will be one night—one single night—when the palace goes completely dark."

"When?"

Lior looked south, at the bruised and starless sky, and felt the vast attention of the wild things gathered at her back like an army that had finally, after twenty years, found its general.

"Soon," she said. "It has to feed again before it can finish. And when it does—we ride."
