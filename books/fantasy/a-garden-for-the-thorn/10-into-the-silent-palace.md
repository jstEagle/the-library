## 10. Into the Silent Palace

The Guild's audit cart smelled of tallow and old lies, which was fitting, because that was what it carried.

Lior sat wedged between two crates of "reconciled accounts"—empty boxes, in truth, weighted with river stones to give the guards at the gate something heavy to pretend to inspect—while Sera Vane adjusted the gray Guild livery at her shoulders as though she had worn it all her life. Isolde sat across from her, hooded, hands folded in her lap with the stillness of someone who had spent ten years learning not to be seen. The princess was better at invisibility than Lior had ever been. She wore it like a discipline rather than a refuge.

"Remember," Sera murmured for the third time since dawn, "we are clerks. We are bored. We are the most forgettable people in any room we enter."

"I've been practicing being forgettable my whole life," Lior said. "It's the pretending to be official that worries me."

The cart lurched through the palace gates without stopping. A guard glanced in, saw the Vellum Guild seal on the manifest, and waved them through with the weary indifference of a man who had been standing in the cold too long to care about arithmetic. That was the thing about the Guild's name, Corvane had made clear before they left: it opened doors precisely because no one wanted to look too closely at what came through them. The Guild was everywhere and accounted for everything, and therefore was seen by no one.

Lior had hated how much sense that made.

* * *

Inside, the palace was wrong.

She felt it before she could name it—the way one feels a room where someone has just died, or a house where the argument happened hours ago and the silence is still holding its shape. The great entry hall was clean, bright, gilded, everything a royal hall should be. And yet every person in it moved as though wading through deep water.

A chambermaid crossed the marble with an armful of linens, her steps perfectly even, metronome-even, and her eyes fixed on nothing. Not downcast—Lior knew downcast; she had invented half its variations herself—but *unfocused*, as if the eyes had been let out of their work and were idling somewhere behind the face. Two footmen stood at their posts by the far doors, straight-backed, correct, and when a steward passed between them neither man turned his head a fraction of an inch. No one spoke. The hall should have roared with the business of a working court—calls and answers, the scuff of boots, the small courtesies of servants who have worked beside each other for years. Instead there was only the sound of fabric moving and feet meeting stone, soft and regular, like breathing in a sickroom.

"They're not asleep," Isolde whispered. Her voice had gone thin. "I know these people. Marta there—she held me when I was six and feverish. She used to sing. Loudly. Badly." The hood did not quite hide the way her mouth trembled. "She isn't singing."

Sera walked like a clerk, eyes forward, but Lior saw her hand rest near the knife beneath her coat and stay there.

They were escorted—or perhaps *collected* was the better word—by a pale young undersecretary who introduced himself as Master Ellery of the Lord Chamberlain's office. He smiled when protocol required it, a smile assembled from parts, arriving on schedule and leaving on schedule. His ledger-hand was steady. His eyes never once landed on any of theirs.

"The crown thanks the Vellum Guild for its diligence," he recited, leading them down a corridor hung with portraits of kings. "The audit of household expenditures will proceed under standard terms. Questions regarding irregular entries may be submitted in writing."

"Master Ellery," Sera said pleasantly, "there are rumors of irregularities in the crown's maintenance accounts. Enormous sums. Might we see the original vouchers?"

Ellery's smile held. Something behind it did not.

"All entries are regular," he said. "All sums are accounted for. The crown is well maintained."

He said it the way the sleepwalking maid walked: evenly, correctly, and from somewhere very far away. Lior had spent years listening to rats gossip under floorboards, and she knew the sound of a voice that was not entirely the speaker's own. It was a borrowed cadence. A coat worn by something else.

She kept her gaze on the portraits as they passed. King after king after king, and then, larger than the rest, Aldric himself—younger in the paint than Lior had imagined, warm-eyed, with the beginning of a smile that suggested he had been a man who laughed easily. Ten years of reclusion had made him a rumor. Looking at the portrait, Lior thought: *something ate the laughing.*

* * *

They were given a records room on the second floor: a long table, tall windows, shelves of household ledgers going back decades. Ellery deposited them with a bow and withdrew, and his footsteps faded down the corridor at that same metronome pace, never hurrying, never dragging.

For a long moment none of them spoke. Then Isolde pulled off her hood and pressed both hands flat against the table, breathing.

"That was Marta's corridor," she said. "I grew up running down it. There were always people. Always noise. Cooks shouting, pages racing—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "My father used to complain he couldn't hear himself think in this palace. Now I believe no one in it can."

"It's worse than quiet," Lior said.

She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But they both looked at her, waiting, and she realized she had stopped being able to keep the two halves of her life separate. The woman who balanced ledgers and the woman who listened to vermin had to be the same woman now, or none of this would ever be understood.

"There are mice in these walls," she said. "There are mice in every wall in this city, but here—" She closed her eyes and let herself listen, really listen, the way she had not dared since the fox died speaking her name. It was like opening a door she'd been leaning against for years. The chatter poured in: small, quick minds, all hunger and warmth and territory. Mice thought in smells and shapes and safe-ways, not words, but she translated as she always did, the way other people translated music into feeling.

And the translation kept coming back wrong.

"They're afraid," she said slowly. "Not of cats. Not of traps. They're afraid of the *center*. Of the middle of the building. They won't go near the throne room. They route around it like—" she searched for the image the mice themselves carried— "like water around a hot stone. And the ones that live closest to it, in the walls of the great hall—" She hesitated.

"What?" Sera asked.

"They're quiet. Not dead. Just... quiet. Like they're listening to something. All day. Every day." She opened her eyes. "Mice don't listen, Sera. Mice *forage*. These ones sit against the wall with their ears up, facing the throne room, for hours. And then they leave and don't remember why they were there."

Isolde had gone white. "What are they listening to?"

"I don't know yet." Lior flexed her fingers, which had gone cold. "But I think I can find out. If I get close enough to the walls below the great hall, close enough that the little ones will talk to me instead of just—sit there."

"We're auditors," Sera said. "We have legitimate reason to inspect the cellars. Household accounts require verification of stores." A ghost of grim humor touched her mouth. "Corvane's forged credentials finally earn their keep."

* * *

The descent took them past kitchens where cooks stirred pots with the same unhurried precision, tasting nothing, adjusting nothing, producing food that Lior would bet her life no one tasted either. Past a guardroom where soldiers polished armor that gleamed and eyes that did not. The deeper they went, the fewer people there were, until the last stairwell was empty except for them and the sound of their own breathing—and even that sounded loud, guilty, human.

The cellar walls were old here, older than the gilded floors above, rough stone sweating with damp. And the walls were *full*.

Lior crouched by the base of the stones and let the door in her mind open all the way.

The mice came cautiously at first, then in a flood—dozens of small presences pressing close along the mortar, whiskered faces appearing in cracks, black eyes reflecting the lantern light. To anyone else it would have looked like an infestation come to watch. To Lior it was a crowd pressing toward a speaker, and every one of them carried the same fragment of the same story.

*Warm-warm-dark, the big-listening, the deep-beat, do-not-go, do-not-go, the deep-beat under the floor, the hungry-silence, we sit and we listen because it tells us to sit and listen—*

Her breath caught.

"Sera." Her voice came out cracked. "There's a heartbeat."

Isolde frowned. "Whose?"

"No one's. That's what I mean. Under the throne room—directly beneath it, deeper than the cellars, down in the old foundations—there's a second heartbeat. Not a person's. It doesn't beat like blood. It beats like..." She pressed her palm against the cold stone, and the mice went silent around her hand, dozens of them, all listening with her now, all facing down through the floor. "Like a drum that learned to want things. Slow. Enormous. Patient. It's been beating so long the whole palace has fallen asleep to it. That's what's wrong with them. They're not enchanted, exactly. They're *rocked*. Like children in a cradle that never stops moving. Everyone above us is half-dreaming inside that rhythm, and no one has woken up in years."

"How far down?" Sera asked, and her hand was on her knife again, though a knife was suddenly the most absurd object in the world.

"Deep. Older than the palace. The mice say the foundations go further than the builders' plans show—that there are rooms under rooms, and the deepest one isn't a room at all. It's a *throat*." Lior heard herself say it and felt the horror land twice, once in the words and once in the truth underneath them. "That's where the beat comes from. Something down there has been eating for a very long time. Slowly. Carefully. Starting with the smallest things and working upward. First the mice's courage. Then the servants' attention. Then—" she thought of Marta's empty eyes, of Ellery's borrowed smile, of a king ten years behind a locked door— "then everything else."

Isolde sank slowly onto a wine cask, and for a moment she was not a princess or an exile or a woman with ten years of proof in a satchel. She was a daughter hearing, at last, the shape of the thing that had taken her father.

"He complained," she whispered. "Near the end, before I—I accused him—he told me he couldn't sleep. That the palace hummed. That he could feel it through the bedframe, through the floor, through his *teeth*. Everyone thought he was ill. Mad, maybe." Her jaw tightened. "He wasn't mad. He was the only one awake enough to hear it."

"And it silenced him the way it silences everything," Sera said quietly. "Not with violence. With lullabies."

Above them, faint through layers of stone, the palace went on breathing its slow, even, terrible breath. Lior kept her hand on the wall a moment longer. Deep beneath her palm, patient as winter, the second heartbeat rolled on—and this time, as if aware of her listening, it seemed to roll *toward* her, one vast pulse swelling up through the stone like a wave deciding to notice the shore.

The mice scattered.

Every one of them, all at once, fleeing along the walls away from the foundation stones, and in the sudden emptiness they left behind, Lior felt unmistakably, appallingly *seen*.

She snatched her hand back. "It knows I'm here."

"Then we're done auditing," Sera said, already moving toward the stairs. "We get our findings and we get out before—"

The bell above began to toll.

Not the hour bell—it was the wrong time, and the wrong rhythm, three strokes and a pause, three strokes and a pause, ringing through the palace like a finger tapped on glass. Above them, through the ceiling, came the sound of hundreds of feet all changing direction at once, in perfect unison, and beginning to walk—not toward the doors, not toward the alarm—

Toward the throne room.

And beneath the floor, slow and satisfied, the second heartbeat quickened.
