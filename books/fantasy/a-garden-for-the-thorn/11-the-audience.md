## 11. The Audience

The palace had a smell Isolde had forgotten she knew: beeswax and cold stone and, beneath it, the green rot of lilies kept too long in standing water. Ten years, and her body remembered it before her mind did. Her stomach tightened the way it had at seven years old, waiting outside her father's study with a drawing clutched in both hands, hoping to be called in.

She was not seven years old now. She was twenty-six, and the paper in her hands was not a drawing but a ledger page, and she had not come to be called in. She had come to accuse.

"Keep your eyes down," Sera murmured beside her. The disgraced warden wore her old uniform stripped of its insignia, and it fit her like an apology—everything right except the meaning. "Servants here don't look up. Don't be the one who looks up."

"I grew up here," Isolde said.

"That's exactly why you can't." Sera's voice stayed flat, warden-flat, the voice of someone who had learned that tone in rooms where looking wrong got people killed. "You walk like a princess, Isolde. You stand like one. Right now you have to be Guild auditor number three, bored, tired, and invisible."

*Invisible.* Isolde almost laughed. That was Lior's word, Lior's whole philosophy—the little beast-speaker had spent her life perfecting what Isolde had been exiled for failing to do. *Be small. Be nothing. Be no one's daughter.* She had run to the border convent and made herself a ghost in grey wool, and it had saved her life and cost her everything else, and she still did not know if the trade had been worth it.

They walked. The corridor to the audience chamber ran long and pale as a throat, hung with banners of the nine provinces, and Isolde counted them out of habit—she had embroidered three of them herself as a girl, sitting at her mother's knee before her mother died. The banners were faded. Nobody had replaced them. Nobody had replaced anything, she realized, looking closer. The sconces held candles burned down to stubs. A tapestry near the third turning sagged off its rod, and no servant had straightened it. The palace of her childhood had gleamed like a jewel; this palace was a body being slowly abandoned by whatever lived inside it.

The servants moved strangely. She had noticed it from the moment they passed the gates—Lior had noticed it first, going rigid in the cart, whispering that the mice in the walls were silent, that mice were never silent—but seeing it was worse than hearing about it. A maid crossed the gallery carrying a tray of untouched cups, walking with the smooth even glide of something on rails. A footman stood at his post by a window, and when a shaft of afternoon light fell across his face he did not squint or shift. He simply stood, eyes fixed on the middle distance, breathing.

"They're asleep," Lior whispered. She walked between them, small and quick, her ledger satchel hugged to her chest like a shield. "Not sleeping. Asleep is different. They're—" She searched for the word and didn't find it. "Hollowed. There's nothing looking out."

"How much of the court?" Sera asked.

"All of them I've heard so far." Lior's voice dropped further. "And under the throne room there's a second heartbeat. Big. Slow. It doesn't sound like a person's heart sounds. It sounds like a bell underwater."

Isolde made herself keep walking. Ten years she had imagined this day, and in every imagining her father was either innocent and imprisoned or guilty and monstrous, and she had rehearsed her speech for both. She had not rehearsed for this—for a father who might be neither, who might be a man drowning inside his own crown while something else breathed through his mouth.

The doors of the audience chamber opened without anyone opening them.

* * *

The room was smaller than memory insisted. That was the first cruelty of returning anywhere: memory built palaces inside palaces, and reality came in meaner proportions. The dais stood where it had always stood, beneath the great sun-disc of Aldric's line, but the gold leaf on the disc had gone dull, tarnished black in patches, as though something had been drinking the shine out of it. The throne itself—

Her father sat in it like a coat hung on a nail.

That was the image that arrived, unbidden and terrible, and would not leave. King Aldric had been a big man once, broad through the shoulders, a bear of a king who had carried Isolde on one arm and a hunting hawk on the other and complained cheerfully about the weight of both. The thing in the throne had his shape the way a glove has a hand's shape. His skin lay close over the bone. His hair, once black, had gone the color of ash and hung lank past his jaw. He wore the crown, and the crown seemed to sit too deep, as though it had grown roots into his skull, and around his temples—Isolde's breath caught—around his temples the skin was marked with fine dark lines, branching, delicate, like the veins of a leaf pressed into wax.

He was looking at the floor. Not at them. At the floor, with the same fixed middle distance the footman had worn, and for a moment Isolde thought they had walked into a room with a corpse in it, dressed and crowned and propped upright, and the kingdom had simply gone on pretending.

Then the herald—there was still a herald, a hollowed boy with a voice like dry paper—announced them. "Auditors of the Vellum Guild, come to review the accounts of the crown."

"The accounts," said the thing in the throne, and its voice was her father's voice, and it was not her father's voice. It had all the notes and none of the music. It was a voice played back from memory by something that had studied the original very carefully. "Yes. The accounts must always balance. Come forward, auditors. Let me see your faces."

Sera bowed. Lior bowed, low, her hood shadowing her face. Isolde bowed last and lowest, because bowing to him was the hardest thing she had ever done with her body, harder than the flogging she'd taken at the convent for speaking out of turn, harder than burying the Mother Superior two winters past. Her knees bent. Her head went down. She looked at the floorboards and saw, absurdly, the scratch-marks she'd made there as a child with her boot heel, counting her way through boring audiences, and her eyes burned, and she made herself rise again with a face like paper.

The king's gaze moved over them. Over Sera, without interest. Over Lior, with a flicker—a pause, a slow blink, something turning over behind the eyes—and then to Isolde.

It stopped.

For ten years she had told herself she wanted him to look at her. Now that he did, she discovered she wanted nothing so badly as for him to stop. The attention of the thing behind those eyes had weight, pressure, cold, like deep water closing over her head. It examined her face the way an appraiser examines a stone, and somewhere underneath her terror a small, stubborn voice observed: *it doesn't recognize me. Whatever wears him never bothered to learn his own daughter's face.*

"You may approach," it said. "The Guild brings figures. I will hear the figures."

They approached. Sera unfurled the false audit rolls they'd prepared, and began to read them aloud in her flat warden voice—inventories, disbursements, the plausible drivel Corvane had scripted for them—and Isolde barely heard a word of it. Because as they drew near the dais, as the light from the high windows fell full across her father's face, something happened.

His left hand twitched.

It was a small thing. A tremor, the kind grief gives you, the kind cold gives you. But the hand was gripping the armrest of the throne, knuckles bloodless, and the tremor traveled up the arm, into the shoulder, into the chest, and Isolde saw his breathing change—saw the shallow mechanical rhythm stutter, break, deepen, as though a man surfacing from very far underwater had finally broken the surface and dragged in air.

His eyes came up. And they were *his.*

Isolde had spent ten years hating her father's eyes. She had painted them cruel in a hundred private imaginings, cold and dismissive, the eyes of a man who could look at his only child and say *you are mad, you are a liar, you are no daughter of mine.* She had built her exile on those eyes. And now here they were, wide and wet and terrified, and they were the eyes of the man who used to lift her onto his shoulders to pick apples, and they were drowning.

His lips moved. No sound came. The Regent—that was the name Lior used, borrowed from some old warden text—was reading its audit figures, or pretending to, its mouth producing the herald-smooth voice while Aldric's true face fought underneath it like a man beating on the underside of ice.

Then, so quietly she nearly missed it, a sound did come. A breath shaped into words, squeezed through teeth that were not fully his to command:

"*Grey goose, grey goose.*"

The world went white and then very still.

*Grey goose, grey goose, how many miles to morning?* It was nothing. It was a nonsense rhyme, a child's cipher, theirs alone—he had invented it when she was five, a game for boring ceremonies, a way of talking across a crowded hall without anyone knowing. One line meant *I love you.* Two lines meant *be careful.* Three lines meant—

Three lines meant *run.*

His eyes held hers, and his ruined mouth worked, and he gave her the second line in a thread of sound: "*Over the water, over the stone.*"

*Be careful. Be careful. Be careful.*

And then, with what it must have cost him—she saw the effort rack his body like a seizure, saw the dark veins at his temples pulse black—he gave her the third.

"*Fly, little goose, fly.*"

Run. He was telling her to run. Ten years she had waited for him to see her, and now he saw her, truly saw her, and the first and only thing he could say to her was *run*, because he knew what she had come to do, or guessed it, or simply knew her—knew that his daughter did not cross half a kingdom to read ledgers—and he was spending the last of himself, the last coins of a purse ten years emptied, to buy her escape.

Isolde's vision blurred. She did not let herself weep. Weeping was a luxury with a price tag, and the price was everything. She looked at her dying father and gave him, silently, the answer she had given him at five years old whenever he told her to run and she refused:

She shook her head. Once. Small. Stubborn as a burr.

Something broke in his face. Relief or grief or both—they had always been the same expression on him, she remembered that now, remembered how he'd cried laughing when her mother married him—and his hand loosened on the armrest, and he sank back, and the light went out of his eyes like a lamp shuttered.

* * *

"—and the southern granary disbursements reconcile against the third-quarter tallies," Sera was saying, her voice steady as a metronome, giving Isolde the time she needed to rebuild her face. "With the crown's permission, our junior auditor will verify the sealed entries directly."

"There are no sealed entries," said the Regent.

It said it pleasantly. That was the worst part. It said it the way a host says *there's plenty more where that came from,* warm and easy, and its eyes—its eyes were open now, and they were not Aldric's eyes at all. They were not any person's eyes. They were patient the way a trap is patient, old the way winter is old, and they rested on Lior with an interest that made Isolde's skin crawl.

"But you may look anyway," it continued. "Audit is a holy work. Everything in my house is written down. Every debt." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Every payment. Every *price.*"

Its gaze slid from Lior to Isolde, and settled there, and this time it did not pass over her. This time it looked, and it knew what it was looking at, and its mouth—her father's mouth—curved into a smile that used none of her father's muscles correctly.

"And you," it said. "The Guild sends me a girl with the royal jaw and the queen's stubborn chin, and introduces her as an auditor. How charming. How very like a family reunion." It leaned forward on the throne, and the crown's shadow fell across its face, and beneath the tarnished gold of the sun-disc the dark veins at its temples pulsed once, slowly, like a heart. "Welcome home, Isolde. I wondered which of you would come first. Your father has been *so eager* to see you."

Beside her, Lior had gone white as vellum. Sera's hand had drifted, casual as adjusting a sleeve, to the knife at her belt. And Isolde stood in her dead mother's audience chamber, ten years of proof crammed against her ribs, and understood with perfect clarity that the thing wearing her father had known she was coming—had perhaps arranged it, had perhaps been waiting the way a spider waits at the center of a web it has spent a decade spinning—and that the rhyme her father had spent his last strength to give her had not been a warning about the road.

It had been a warning about the house.

"Come closer, child," said the Regent, in her father's voice, holding out her father's hand. "We have ten lost years to discuss. And I find I am *hungry* for family."
