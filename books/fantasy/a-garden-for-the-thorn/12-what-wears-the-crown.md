## 12. What Wears the Crown

Sera Vane had spent eleven years learning to read a room the way other people read letters—quickly, silently, and with an eye for what was written between the lines. The throne room of Aldric's palace taught her more in one hour than a decade of wardenship ever had.

It was not the guards who frightened her, though there were too many of them and they stood too still, like chess pieces left on a board by a player who had grown bored. It was not the hollowed courtiers gliding through their duties with sleepwalker smoothness, nor the servants who never once let their eyes meet hers as she passed among them in her borrowed auditor's gray. It was the light.

There was no light. Not truly. The great windows along the eastern wall should have been flooding the chamber with morning, but the glass showed only a gray so complete it seemed painted on, and the hundred candles burning in their iron stands gave off flame without warmth, illumination without comfort—as if the fire itself had forgotten what it was for. The last stars were failing out there beyond the ceiling of ash-colored sky. She could feel it in her teeth, a faint subsiding hum, like a song winding down to its final note.

*And I know that song,* she thought, standing at the edge of the dais steps with Lior and Isolde flanking her, three auditors come to count a kingdom's debts. *I have read that song before, in a cellar full of records no one was supposed to survive reading.*

The Regent sat upon the throne wearing King Aldric the way a man wears a coat cut for someone broader in the shoulder.

Sera had loved Aldric once—not romantically, nothing so simple, but with the fierce loyalty of a young warden sworn to a young king who had actually listened to his wardens, who had walked the border walls himself in the rain, who had laughed. That man was gone from the face above her now. What remained was the shape of him: the same grey-streaked hair, the same strong jaw, the same hands resting on the arms of the throne. But the eyes were wrong. They were patient in a way no human eyes are patient. They held the stillness of something that had waited ten years inside another creature's skull and had learned to enjoy the waiting.

She had seen those eyes before. Not in the flesh—in ink.

Three years ago, disgraced and half-starved, she had broken into the sealed archive beneath the Warden's Hall in Vhale, hunting proof that her expulsion had been engineered rather than earned. She had not found that proof. She had found something else: a ledger-bound record, four centuries old, catalogued under a number so dull no clerk would ever pull it. *Registry of Crown Counsel, First Binding.* A spirit bound into service by the first kings of this land—a thing of vast patience and vaster hunger, collared by rite and circle, set to whisper counsel into royal ears down the generations. The old wardens had called it the Regent, because that was the joke of it: a king's advisor who never died, never slept, never wanted anything except, eventually, everything.

The record ended abruptly, mid-entry, the final line a single sentence in a hand gone shaky:

*If the binding weakens, do not negotiate. It offers bargains because bargains are how it feeds.*

Sera had told herself then that it was allegory. Wardens' folklore dressed up in administrative dress. She had been very good, over the years, at telling herself things.

Now the thing itself looked down at her from a stolen throne, and its mouth—the king's mouth—curved into a smile of perfect, terrible courtesy.

"You have questions," it said. Its voice was Aldric's voice, but layered somehow, the way a bell's note contains harmonics the ear can't separate. "You have traveled far, spent much, risked everything. Ask."

Beside her, Isolde went rigid. Sera felt it more than saw it—the princess's whole body pulling taut as a bowstring, ten years of exile and accusation and grief gathering behind her ribs. Lior, on Isolde's other side, had gone very pale and very quiet, which Sera had learned was how the beast-speaker did her loudest screaming.

"We know what you are," Sera said. She kept her voice level, warden-flat, the voice she'd used on smugglers and oath-breakers in another life. "The Registry of Crown Counsel. First Binding. You were meant to advise. Instead you ate your way through every host they gave you until you got one fat enough to keep."

Something flickered across the Regent's borrowed face. Not anger. *Pleasure.*

"Warden," it said softly. "Yes. I remember wardens. You were the teeth of the old circles, once. And now look at you—disgraced, hunted, standing in my house in a dead guildmaster's gray." The smile widened a fraction. "You read the registry. Few do. Fewer understand it. Tell me, Sera Vane—what does the record say I am?"

"A parasite."

"A *steward*," it corrected, with the mild patience of a tutor. "Your kings were children, all of them, crying and dying and squandering. I kept the kingdom breathing while they played at crowns. I advised. I counseled. I *maintained*. The word is even in your ledgers, is it not? Maintenance of the crown." It spread Aldric's hands, an open gesture, almost wounded. "I have done nothing these ten years that a good regent would not do. I simply never asked permission to begin."

"The wards," Lior said. Her voice came out small and scraped. "The border. The stars. Was that maintenance?"

The Regent turned its attention to her, and Sera watched Lior bear the weight of that gaze the way a sapling bears snow—bending, bending, not yet breaking.

"The stars were a larder," it said simply. "The wards were a fence around a field I had already purchased. You may blame the Guild's seller and the crown's buyer; both signatures were genuine. I am, if nothing else, honest in my accounting." Its gaze slid back to Sera, and the temperature of the room seemed to drop around her alone. "But we are not here to discuss me. We are here because the three of you have walked a long road toward a door you cannot open. And I—" the smile again, gentle now, almost kind, "—am generous to travelers."

*Here it comes,* thought Sera, and some cold, clear part of her that had survived eleven years of disgrace whispered: *remember the last line. Remember what bargains are.*

"To the beast-speaker," the Regent said, and its voice softened into something warm as banked coals, "I offer a kingdom that will never burn her kind again. No pyres. No hiding in counting houses, listening to rats because rats are the only ones who will speak to her. A college, if she likes. A law. Her gift written into the charter of the realm as a treasure instead of a crime. All she must do is walk away, and live to see it."

Lior made a sound Sera had never heard from her—half breath, half wound—and Sera reached back without looking and found the younger woman's wrist and gripped it hard. *Stay here. Stay with us.*

"To the princess," the Regent continued, turning, and its voice took on a horrible tenderness, "I offer her father. Whole. Awake. Ten years returned to him, and a coronation beside him, and every slander against her name struck from every record in the land. He is still in here, Isolde. I have worn him a long time, but he is *here*, dreaming, and dreams can be honored. Walk away, and when the last star gutters out, I will let him surface long enough to say goodbye properly. Or—" the pause was surgical, "—long enough to say hello."

Isolde's breath broke. Sera heard it crack right down the middle.

"And to the warden." The Regent leaned forward on the throne, and for just a moment the mask slipped and Sera saw the thing underneath—not a shape, not a face, but a *hunger* wearing the shape of interest. "Ah, Sera. My Sera. Eleven years ago you knelt in the Warden's Hall and swore an oath to protect this realm from exactly what sits before you. And eleven years ago they took your badge, your name, your purpose, and threw them in the gutter—because you asked one question too many about where the border money went. You were right, weren't you? Even then. You were always right, and it cost you everything."

"Stop," Sera said.

"I offer you reinstatement," it said, unstoppered and unstoppable. "Public. Formal. Your name cleared by royal decree, your rank restored, your enemies—every warden who signed your expulsion, every magistrate who looked away—named in the same decree as the corrupt creatures they are. A new order of wardens, reformed, honest, *yours*, answering to a crown that finally deserves guarding. You wanted justice within the law. I am the law now, Sera. I am the only law left that functions. Take my offer and you will die an old woman, honored, having fixed everything you broke your heart trying to fix."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Sera stood in it and felt the offer go into her like a blade finding the gap in armor she hadn't known was there. Because it wasn't power it offered her—that was the genius of it, the obscenity of it. Power she could have refused. Gold she could have refused. It offered her *vindication*. It offered her the exact shape of the hole eleven years had carved in her, filled to its edges, seamless, real.

She saw it. She saw the hall of the Warden's Hall restored, her name chiseled back onto the roll of honor. She saw Magistrate Corren, who had smirked at her sentencing, in irons. She saw young wardens trained properly, borders warded honestly, a kingdom with clean teeth. She saw her father's grave, and herself kneeling at it at last with nothing to be ashamed of.

She saw it so clearly that for three full heartbeats she forgot it was being shown to her.

"Sera." Lior's whisper, barely air. "Sera, your hand—you're gripping—"

Sera looked down. Her own hand had drifted to her belt, to the place where a warden's seal used to hang, touching empty leather. Touching it the way a woman touches a locket.

Cold flooded her, head to heel.

*It offers bargains because bargains are how it feeds.* Not lies—she understood that now, standing in the furnace of its attention. That was the trap's true architecture. Every word was true, or could be made true. The Regent didn't tempt with fantasy; it tempted with *invoices*, with terms it fully intended to honor, because a granted desire opened a door, and through that door went whatever it wished to take. It had offered Aldric a legacy. It had offered the Guild profit. It was offering her a clean name, and the price was written nowhere because the price was simply *her*—her walking away, her turning her back on the two women beside her, her consent given freely to the thing that had eaten a king.

A bargain freely accepted was a binding in reverse. She saw that now. It didn't need to bind her. It needed her to bind *herself*.

"No," Sera said.

The word came out rough. She planted her feet on the cold stone and said it again, louder, warden-flat, the voice that had once halted smugglers on the ice-road at Vhale with nothing behind her but a badge and a bad attitude.

"No. To all three of us. Whatever you're selling, we're not buying."

The Regent regarded her for a long moment. Around the chamber, the candle flames bent, all together, all in the same direction, though there was no wind.

"Pity," it said, and meant it, and that was the worst thing yet. "You would have been excellent. Better than the Guildmaster—he has no imagination, only arithmetic. But very well." It settled back, and the king's face rearranged itself into courteous blankness, a door closing over deep water. "Then we return to procedure. You came as auditors. Audit."

It lifted one hand—a small gesture, almost lazy—and the double doors at the far end of the throne room swung inward without anyone touching them.

Beyond them, in the corridor, stood a dozen of Corvane's hired guards. Not palace guard. *Guild* guard, in merchant colors, and at their head a narrow-faced man Sera recognized from the Guild's payroll ledgers: Captain Doss, whose signature appeared beside payments for "route security" that had never secured any route she'd ever heard of.

They had been sold. Somewhere between the gates and the throne room, Corvane had sold their route, their names, their faces. Of course he had. Power was a ledger with better margins.

"Arrest them," said the Regent pleasantly, in the beloved voice of King Aldric the Good. "Treason against the crown. Three counts. See that they're comfortable—guests of the realm should be comfortable." The eyes flicked to Lior, and lingered there a moment too long, and something moved behind them that was not courtesy at all. "*Especially* the small one. I find I have further questions for her."

The guards began to move. Isolde was shouting her father's name, her real father's name, into the face of the thing that wore it. Lior's hand found Sera's sleeve. And Sera, hauling them both backward toward the narrowing gap between the closing doors, thought with strange clarity:

*I nearly said yes. Gods help me, I nearly said yes—and it knew. It counted my heartbeats while I decided.*

They ran. Behind them, unhurried, the Regent watched them go with a king's stolen smile, and in the walls of the silent palace, Sera would swear until her dying day, a thousand small claws went scurrying—all of them, every rat and mouse in Aldric's house, fleeing in the same direction.

Away from the throne.

Toward Lior.
