## 13. The Price of Knowing

The Regent's offer still hung in the air like smoke that refused to rise.

*Walk away,* it had said to each of them in turn, wearing King Aldric's voice the way a thief wears a stolen coat—familiar, warm, wrong. To Sera it had offered her commission restored, her name scrubbed clean of disgrace. To Isolde, a father whole and remembering. Lior had watched them both stand at the edge of wanting, had felt the pull of it herself when the thing on the throne turned its attention toward her—

And then it stopped.

The change was small. A stillness in the hollowed court behind them, two hundred bodies that had been swaying like reeds going suddenly, perfectly motionless. The torches guttered though there was no draft. And Aldric's face—the face that was not Aldric's face—tilted by degrees, the way an owl turns when it hears a mouse beneath the snow.

"There you are," the Regent said.

Lior's mouth went dry. "There who is?"

"Do not insult us both." The king's lips curved, but the smile belonged to something older, something that had learned smiling secondhand and never quite mastered the mechanics of it. The eyes were the worst part. They were the color of a starless sky, and they did not blink often enough. "You have been listening this entire time. Not to me. To *them.*" One ringed finger lifted, indicating—not Sera, not Isolde—the walls themselves, the rafters, the dark seams between the stones where the mice lived and breathed and told each other everything.

Lior's heart slammed against her ribs. Ten years of practice kept her face smooth. Ten years of being nobody, of balancing ledgers and lowering her eyes, of letting people look through her as though she were furniture. She reached for that invisibility now the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood.

It did not come.

"You hear them," the Regent said softly. "The small voices. You have heard them your whole life, haven't you? The rats gossiping under floorboards. The horses complaining about their bridles. The sparrows mapping the city street by street." It leaned forward on the throne, and the crown—it was not gold, Lior realized with a lurch; it had never been gold—seemed to drink the torchlight deeper. "Do you know how I know?"

"Because you're mad," Sera said, stepping half in front of Lior, one hand drifting toward the warden's blade at her hip. "That's my working theory."

"I know," the Regent continued, ignoring her with the patience of a glacier, "because I can feel you *listening.* Like a draft under a door. Like a tooth of ice against my bindings." Its gaze crawled over Lior, and she felt it the way she imagined a specimen must feel the naturalist's lens—catalogued, opened, understood. "Beast-speaker."

The word dropped into the throne room like a stone into a well. Lior heard it fall all the way down.

"No," she said.

"Yes." The Regent sounded almost gentle now, which was infinitely worse. "Say what you are. You have spent your life refusing to say it. I am offering you the courtesy of honesty, little ledger-keeper. In this room, at least, no one will burn you for it." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "Yet."

Behind her, Isolde made a small sound. Lior did not turn. Turning would be admitting. Admitting would be dying—the law was old and simple and written in ash: those who spoke with beasts kindled first, because their words could not be trusted to stop at beasts.

But the Regent already knew. The Regent had known from the moment she walked in, and the knowledge sat in its borrowed face like satisfaction.

"Why does it matter?" Isolde demanded, and her voice cracked beautifully across the silence. "Whatever she is, whatever any of us are—you're the one wearing my father. You're the one who drank the stars."

"The stars," the Regent mused, "were an expense. Necessary. Sustenance always is." It rose from the throne, and the court flinched as one body, two hundred sleepwalkers bowing without being asked. It descended the dais steps slowly, rings clicking against stone, and Lior discovered she could not move backward. Her feet had become part of the floor. "But the girl matters very much. Let me tell you a story, since your historians have lost the ending."

It stopped three paces away. Close enough that Lior could smell it—not rot, exactly, but the absence of smell, a void where a living man should have had breath and sweat and warmth. Cold came off him like off a cellar wall.

"Before your kingdom had a name, there were binders. Men and women who knew how to tie a spirit to a service, a service to a stone, a stone to a line of kings. I was bound by such hands. Bound to advise. Bound to obey. Bound—" the word came out frayed, "—to *serve*, generation upon generation, while lesser minds wore the crown and mistook my counsel for their own cleverness." Its jaw tightened. Aldric's jaw. "Bindings are knots, child. Every knot has a language. And every language has speakers who can worry a knot loose."

Lior's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the throne room's chill.

"The old binders feared one voice above all others," the Regent said. "Not the fire-callers. Not the storm-speakers. Those are loud gifts, obvious gifts, easily caged. No—they feared the quiet ones. The ones who could speak to things that have no stake in human law. Do you know why beasts cannot lie?" It smiled again, that secondhand smile. "Because they have nothing to hide. And a voice that speaks truth to binding—that hears the shape of a knot and knows where the loose end lies—is the one voice in creation that can unmake me."

The torches guttered again. Somewhere high above, in the dark of the rafters, a mouse squeaked—a tiny thread of sound—and Lior clung to it like a rope over an abyss.

"That is why they burned you," the Regent said, and now it was speaking only to her, the others forgotten, the whole ruined court forgotten. "Not for superstition. Superstition is for peasants. They burned beast-speakers because some binder, a thousand years dead, wrote down in a margin what you are: *the unbinding voice.* And every king since has paid good gold to keep that page buried and your kind scattered like seed corn on wind." It tilted its head. "I have simply been more thorough than most."

"You're lying," Lior whispered.

"I do not lie. I *account.*" The Regent spread its hands, Aldric's hands, elegant and terrible. "Consider the arithmetic. For ten years I have fed, and grown, and tightened my hold on this kingdom's magic until the wards sang to my tune and the stars poured down my throat like wine. One obstacle remains. Not armies—I have bought the generals. Not faith—I have starved the gods' wellsprings myself. One obstacle: a girl who hides behind ledgers and talks to rats." Its voice dropped to something almost tender. "Kill the knot-loosener, and my binding holds forever. There is no price I would not pay for that. There is no price *you* would not pay, in my place. Do not pretend otherwise. You balance accounts all day. You know what things cost."

"Lior." Isolde's hand found hers, gripping hard enough to hurt. "Don't listen to it."

"Oh, she must listen," the Regent said pleasantly. "Listening is her nature. It is the rest of her life that is now in question." It turned back toward the throne, unhurried, a landlord inspecting property. "Guards will attend to it shortly. Or perhaps I shall attend to it personally, once I have finished being gracious to the princess. There is time. There is always time; I have eaten centuries of it."

Sera moved then—quick, silent, a disgraced warden's reflexes still sharp beneath the disgrace—and put herself bodily between Lior and the throne. "You'll go through me."

"How tedious," the Regent said, without heat. "Wardens always say that. And yet here I stand, and where are the wardens of ten years ago?" It settled back onto the throne, arranging itself in Aldric's body with a care that was obscene, a puppeteer smoothing its puppet's robes. "Go home, all of you. Consider my offer. The princess knows what I promised her. Warden Vane knows what I promised her. And the beast-speaker—" the starless eyes fixed on Lior one last time, and in them she saw something vast turning over, patient as winter, "—the beast-speaker knows what I promise *her.*"

The doors at the far end of the throne room boomed.

Once. Twice. A third time, iron on iron, and the sound went through Lior's chest like a bell through water.

The Regent's expression did not change, but something behind it did—a flicker, a recalculation, the ledger-mind interrupted mid-entry. "Ah," it said. "Early."

* * *

They ran because Sera made them run.

Her hand closed on Lior's sleeve and hauled, and then they were moving—Isolde stumbling, Sera counting doorways, muttering the palace's bones from memory of old warden drills. Behind them the throne room doors shuddered under the third blow, and Lior heard, with horrible clarity, the Regent's voice following them, unhurried, almost amused:

"Run if you like. Everything runs, eventually, into a ledger."

Then a side passage swallowed them, torchlit and narrow, smelling of tallow and cold stone, and the voice was gone.

"They'll cut off the gates," Sera gasped, taking corners at speed. "If that's the guard, we're rats in a jar. There's a servant stair down to the old kitchens—"

"It's not the guard," Lior said.

She didn't know how she knew. That was a lie; she knew exactly how she knew. The mice had been screaming since the first boom, a thin silver wire of panic running through the walls, and their terror had a flavor, and the flavor was *uniforms she recognized.* Not royal blue. Not warden grey.

Vellum Guild colors. Corvane's colors.

She said so, and Sera stared at her for half a stride too long, and nearly missed the turn.

"You're certain."

"The mice are certain." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Corvane sold us. He sold our route, he sold our faces, he probably sold the shoes we're standing in. He's been selling since before we left the city—he just hadn't decided the price yet."

Isolde caught up to them at the landing, breathing hard, her braid half-collapsed, and there was something in her face Lior had not seen before—not fear, or not only fear. Recognition. "He wants a controlled crisis," Isolde said bitterly. "I've read his ledgers too, remember. A scandal, arrests, treason trials—the Guild becomes indispensable to whoever sits on the throne afterward. He doesn't care whose throne."

"He cares that it isn't ours," Lior said.

Boots above them. Many boots. The mice counted them—she counted with them, helpless to stop, thirty-one men coming down the grand stair and twelve sealing the lower corridor and six, six, six already waiting at the bottom of the servant stair with lanterns and manacles and the bored efficiency of professionals doing a job that would pay well.

"We can't fight thirty-one," Sera said flatly. "Or twelve. Or six."

"I know."

"And the Regent wants you dead the moment it gets its hands on you."

"I know that too." Lior pressed her back against the cold wall and closed her eyes, and for one breath she let herself hear everything—the boots, the mice, the rats in the cellar stacks, the moths battering themselves against the lantern glass, the whole chattering, terrified, magnificent chorus of small lives the palace had never bothered to notice. Her gift. Her shame. The unbinding voice.

The thing that had just signed her death warrant.

"So we run," Isolde said. "There has to be—"

The lantern-light bloomed around the corner before she finished, and there was nowhere left to run.

They came out of the dark in a wedge of yellow light: six men in Guild livery, crossbows leveled, and behind them a sergeant Lior had seen a hundred times in the counting house, collecting Corvane's discreet parcels, tipping his hat to her as though she were furniture. He looked at her now. That was the worst of it. After ten years of invisibility, the world had finally decided to see her, and what it saw was a criminal.

"Lior Iversen," the sergeant read from a paper, his voice flat as a ledger line. "Seraphine Vane. And—" he squinted at Isolde, and something flickered across his professional blankness, quickly smothered, "—the Lady Isolde, returned from... exile. By order of His Majesty's justice and the Vellum Guild's cooperation therewith, you are arrested for high treason: conspiracy against the crown, sabotage of the border wards, and the murder of Warden-Captain Hale."

"I've never heard of Hale," Sera said icily.

"You will hear plenty of him at trial." The sergeant snapped his fingers, and the manacles came out. "Save it for the magistrates. Or don't. Traitors generally hang either way."

As they bound her wrists, Lior looked past the lanterns, past the men, down the long throat of the corridor toward the distant glow of the throne room—and felt it looking back. The Regent had not needed the guards. It had *allowed* them. Because a public trial, a public hanging, a traitor's corpse on a scaffold—was that not cleaner than a private death? Was that not an entry properly filed?

*Kill the knot-loosener,* it had said. But better still: discredit her first. Burn her in the town square with the law's blessing, and no one would ever wonder whether the traitor girl had died saying something true.

The manacles bit. Isolde was saying something fierce and low to the sergeant—*do you know who I am*—and Sera was calculating escape routes with her eyes even as they chained her, and Lior stood among them, wrists locked in iron, and felt the strangest thing rising through the despair.

Not hope. Not yet. But arithmetic.

The Regent feared her voice. Feared it enough to spend a decade burying her kind, enough to interrupt its own triumph to order her death. Corvane had sold them—but Corvane sold everything, which meant Corvane could be sold to. And somewhere between the throne room and these chains, in the walls, in the floors, in the dark seams of the oldest palace in the world, the mice were still talking.

They were saying her name. All of them. Passing it along like a coin, paw to paw, into the deep places of the city where no guard's boot had ever reached.

*The unbinding voice,* the Regent had called her. It thought naming her power was the same as owning it.

Lior lowered her eyes, the way she had done for ten years, the perfect picture of a small defeated woman—and whispered, so quietly that only the wall beside her cheek could hear:

"Tell everyone. Tell them where they're taking us."

Below the flagstones, faint as a heartbeat, the answering squeak ran outward into the dark, cell by cell, rat by rat, like a spark set loose in dry grass.

The sergeant shoved her forward. "Move."

And Lior Iversen, traitor, beast-speaker, keeper of impossible accounts, walked into the belly of the palace with her head bowed and her debtors gathering.
