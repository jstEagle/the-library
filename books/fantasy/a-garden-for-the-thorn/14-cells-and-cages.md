## 14. Cells and Cages

The cell beneath the palace had been built for someone larger than Isolde, or perhaps for no one at all. The ceiling arched away into darkness above her, lost somewhere past the reach of the single guttering lamp set in an iron sconce by the door, and the walls were dressed in stone so old that the chisel-marks had softened into something like skin—pocked, scarred, faintly warm to the touch when she pressed her palm against it, as though the mountain itself were sleeping under the weight of everything built upon its back.

She had counted her steps. It was the first thing she'd done, before the fear arrived, before the grief, because counting was what she did instead of screaming. Eleven paces along the long wall, seven across the short one. A cell of seventy-seven steps if she walked the perimeter, and she walked it often enough that she could have done it blindfolded, could have done it asleep, could have done it in the dark that came whenever the guards doused the lamp to remind them all three prisoners that light was a courtesy, not a right.

Treason, they had called it. Fabricated, theatrical treason, delivered with the flat ceremony of men reading words they had not written. *Conspiracy against the crown. Conspiracy against the king's health.* She had almost laughed at that last one—the king's health, as if anything in this palace still had health to conspire against—and the laugh had died in her throat when she saw Lior's face go white as vellum beside her.

Now Sera occupied the cell across the corridor, close enough that Isolde could hear her moving in the dark: the slow deliberate pacing of a soldier keeping her body from stiffening, the occasional rattle of her manacles, once—only once—a low curse bitten off at the root. And Lior was somewhere beyond that, deeper in, in a cell with no lamp at all, because the guards had taken one look at the small quiet woman who spoke to beasts and decided that darkness was the only chain they could trust on her.

Three cells. Three cages. And between them, running through the mortar like veins through flesh, the rats.

Isolde heard them before she understood what she was hearing. A scrabble in the wall behind the straw pallet, then another, then a soft wet scratching that resolved—impossibly, wonderfully—into rhythm. Scratches in pairs. Pauses. More scratches. She sat up so fast her head swam.

*Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap. Tap.*

The cipher. Her father's cipher. The one they had invented together when she was six years old and he was still whole, scratched out on the flagstones of the nursery with a stick, a secret language for a princess who couldn't yet write her own name. *Two taps for yes. Three for no. One for wait.* He had told her it was how kings spoke to their children when the court was listening, and she had believed him, and she had used it for years, and then she had stopped believing in him entirely and buried the cipher with everything else she'd buried.

Someone was tapping it now, from inside the wall.

Her hands shook as she raised them to the stone. "Who—" she whispered, and stopped herself, because sound carried down here the way water carried blood. She pressed her ear to the cold rock instead.

*Tap-tap,* said the wall. *Tap-tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap. Tap.*

*Yes. No. No. Wait.*

"I don't understand," she breathed. "Wait for what?"

The answer came not from the wall but from the floor, a grey shape flowing out from beneath the door into the lamplight, and Isolde's whole body flinched backward before her mind caught up with what Lior had told her on the road, what she had seen with her own eyes in the broken borderlands: *the beasts are ours, or they can be; the Regent's hold is slipping; they will not obey it anymore.* The rat sat up on its haunches barely an arm's length away and regarded her with eyes like two drops of ink, unafraid, patient, waiting for her to be clever enough to follow.

It turned. It ran three steps toward the door, stopped, looked back.

"You want me to listen," Isolde said.

The rat's whiskers twitched. Behind the wall, the tapping resumed, faster now, urgent, and Isolde closed her eyes and let ten years fall away and listened the way she had listened as a child, sprawled on nursery flagstones with her cheek against the cool stone while her father tapped out messages from the other side of the world.
