## 15. The Turncoat's Ledger

The cells beneath the Silent Palace had been built for men who screamed.

Corvane knew this because he had read the palace's construction ledgers—of course he had; he had read every ledger the crown ever kept that money had touched—and the accounts from the reign of Aldric's grandfather listed, among other line items, *iron for the weeping rooms, forty pounds; pitch for the walls, to deaden sound; a stipend for the man whose duty it was to listen*. The pitch was still there. He could see it in the walls as he descended the spiral stair, black and glassy, swallowing the echoes of his own footsteps before they could reach the prisoners and give them hope of company.

He found this efficient. A jailer who could not hear his charges was a jailer who could not be bribed by them, and Corvane had never in his life trusted a man who could not be bribed. Trust was simply credit extended without collateral, and he extended none.

The guard at the bottom did not look at him. None of them looked at anyone anymore—that was the strangest thing about the palace now, the thing that had taken even Corvane three days to stop noticing and start cataloguing. Servants moved through the corridors like figures in a dream another man was having. Guards stood their posts with their chins dipped, eyes down, as if the floor were fascinating. Once, passing a scullery boy in the buttery, Corvane had said good morning out of old habit, and the boy had not flinched, not answered, not changed his pace at all. Just drifted on, carrying his pail of water with the perfect economy of something that had been told once, long ago, what its function was.

*Half-devoured already,* Corvane thought, adjusting the ledger case under his arm. *And the other half hasn't noticed.*

It should have frightened him. He noted, with a kind of clinical detachment, that it did not—or rather, that what he felt instead was appetite. The Regent wanted the kingdom quiet, obedient, drained of everything unruly. The Guild had spent four hundred years teaching the kingdom that everything unruly had a price. They were, in a sense, natural partners. All that remained was to establish terms.

That was why he had come down here tonight, past the weeping rooms, to the deepest cell—the one the construction ledgers called *the vault*, though it held no gold, only three women who had made the mistake of being interesting.

The lock was new work, warded iron, and it opened to his key with a sound like a swallowed word.

* * *

They had put the beast-speaker in the center cell, which told Corvane something about whoever had arranged them there. The Regent, presumably, or whatever passed for its administrative mind. It had ordered her death within an hour of identifying her gift—and then Corvane's guards had arrived first, and the order had been amended to *containment*, and Corvane had understood immediately that he was watching an entity learn caution. It needed her voice unbound no longer. But it had not killed her either. Things that had eaten stars for ten years did not develop scruples. They developed *uses*.

She sat on the cell's stone bench with her back straight and her hands folded, and she looked up at him when the door opened, and she did not look afraid.

That was the first wrongness. Corvane had interrogated smugglers and assassins and one archbishop, and they all looked afraid in the first thirty seconds, because fear was simply the body's honest arithmetic: *this room is stronger than you are.* Lior Iversen sat in the strongest room in the kingdom and regarded Guildmaster Corvane the way a clerk regards a misfiled invoice.

"Guildmaster," she said. "You're spending your goodwill quickly. Three visits in two days. People will think you care."

"People think what I pay them to think." He let the door swing shut behind him—he preferred conversations with locked doors; it clarified everyone's position—and set the small stool he'd brought into the middle of the floor, and sat, and opened the ledger case across his knees. "The princess is asleep. Or pretending to be. The warden is doing neither; she's been staring at the wall since sundown, counting the seams in the stones. Forty-one, incidentally, if she's keeping score. She'll want to know someone checked."

"You counted."

"I count everything. It's the only prayer I know." He smiled at her, the smile that had closed more deals than any contract in the Guild's history—the smile that said *I am reasonable, and reasonableness is rarer than gold.* "Shall we skip the part where you ask me why I've come? You already know. I sold your route to the palace guard. You're angry. I'm not sorry. Let's move to the interesting part, which is what happens next."

Lior unfolded her hands. "What happens next is you tell yourself a story on the stairs down here. I can hear you thinking it. That the Regent is a customer like any other. That a kingdom that runs on silence will need a guild that trades in secrets, and that you'll be indispensable to whatever wears the crown next. That power is just a ledger with better margins." She tilted her head. "Did I get it right? I've been listening to the rats. They say you rehearse."

The rats. Of course the rats. Corvane felt a flicker of genuine admiration, which he banked carefully, the way he banked all assets. "Rats are poor witnesses," he said. "They exaggerate. And they take payment in bread crusts, which makes them the cheapest informants in Vell history, and therefore the least loyal."

"And yet they hear everything." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the second wrongness. He had expected fury. Fury he could trade against. "You came to negotiate, Guildmaster. So let's negotiate. But first—" she nodded at the ledger case on his knees— "you brought something to show me. You always bring something. It's how you make people feel behind. So open it, and let's both feel behind together."

For a moment—one heartbeat, no more—Corvane considered standing up and leaving. He was not a man who enjoyed being read, and this woman had just read him like a shipping manifest. But leaving would confirm the reading, and staying might correct it, and besides: he had come to gloat, and gloating required an audience of sufficient quality. She was, annoyingly, sufficient.

He opened the case and drew out the folio.

* * *

Here is what Guildmaster Corvane believed, as he sat down on his stool in the deepest cell of the Silent Palace, and every part of it was true, and every part of it was about to become insufficient:

He believed that the Regent was a force of nature wearing a crown, and forces of nature could not be fought, only harnessed. He believed that the Guild had survived four centuries of kings, wars, plagues, and one memorable dragon by the single doctrine etched above his own desk in the counting house: *the house does not bleed for the game.* He believed that Sera Vane was a broken instrument, that Princess Isolde was a grievance in search of an army, and that Lior Iversen was the most dangerous person he had ever met precisely because she had spent her whole life learning to be overlooked, and overlooked things saw everything.

And he believed—this was the load-bearing belief, the beam under the whole structure of him—that he was necessary. That whatever emerged from the ruin of Aldric's reign, whether it wore the king's face or Isolde's or no face at all, would need the Vellum Guild, because every power in history had needed someone to move its impossible money, and Corvane had spent thirty years making himself the only someone.

He laid the folio on his knees and turned it so she could see the cover, and he began, in the warm cadence of a man presenting terms, to explain his offer.

"The Regent wants three things," he said. "The stars—it's nearly finished with those, you can feel it in the air, the nights are getting *heavier*, have you noticed? Like the sky is pressing down. Then the gifted—your kind, girl, the speakers and the seers, everyone whose blood carries anything it can't digest quietly. And last, the loose ends. Witnesses. People who read ledgers they shouldn't." He tapped the folio. "Now. I can deliver the first two lists. Names, addresses, habits, the little doors in every life that only the Guild knows. In exchange, I keep my head, my guild, and my margins, and when the dust settles, the Vellum Guild becomes the treasury of the new order. That's the offer. I'm telling you first because you're going to live—you've noticed that too, haven't you, that it hasn't killed you?—and because a woman who reads ledgers understands leverage better than any creature alive. So. Look at my numbers, and tell me where I've mispriced myself."

He turned the folio open.

It was not his folio.

* * *

Later—and Corvane would spend a great deal of later examining this moment, turning it over the way a miser turns a suspect coin—he would identify the exact instant the trap closed. Not when he saw the binding, which was wrong, the leather pale and soft as skin rather than the Guild's oiled calf. Not when he saw the hand, which was not a clerk's hand at all but something older, letters pressed deep enough into the vellum to read from the reverse side, as if the writing had been done from inside the page. No. The trap closed the instant he touched it, because the pages were cold, and cold paper in a warm room meant the thing that wrote it had drawn the warmth out to feed itself, and Corvane had spent thirty years handling the Guild's oldest documents and knew exactly one other thing that drank warmth from its surroundings.

"Where did you get this?" His voice came out level. He was proud of that, afterward. Level.

"It was delivered," Lior said. "Under our cell door. Last night. No rat carried it—I asked. It simply arrived, the way debts arrive." She leaned forward, and in the lamplight her eyes were very steady. "The Regent keeps books too, Guildmaster. Did you really think a creature that has consumed a kingdom's magic for ten years wouldn't keep accounts? It thinks in ledgers. That's why you liked it. That's why you never feared it. You looked at it and saw a colleague."

"I don't have colleagues. I have counterparties." He was stalling and he knew it, and he hated that he knew it, and he turned the page anyway, because a man who reads ledgers cannot not-read one placed in front of him. It was the oldest compulsion he had. It was, he understood with sudden terrible clarity, the hook the thing had used.

The entries were beautiful. That was the horror of it—they were *beautiful*, ruled true, balanced to the farthing, every figure in that deep-pressed antique hand. Starlight consumed, columned by month, a decade of gentle astronomical theft. Magic drunk from the border wards, itemized. The king himself, entered not as a host but as an *asset, depreciating*: *Aldric, remaining value, negligible; maintain until replacement located.*

"Replacement located," Corvane read aloud, because reading aloud made things real and he needed, desperately, to make this real in the ordinary way, the way numbers were real. "Replacement located. Located what?"

"Keep reading," Lior said softly. "Go to the end. Go to the liabilities section. Every ledger has one. Yours does. Mine does. Go on."

His fingers were steady. He made them steady. He turned past the assets, past the decade of consumption, to the final leaf, and there, under the heading *OUTSTANDING EXPENSES — SETTLEMENT UPON COMPLETION*, in the same deep, patient, balanced hand:

*Corvane, M., Guildmaster, Vellum — knowledge of supply routes, buyer identities, and binding-lattice provenance exceeds operational requirement. Retention cost: high. Discretion risk: total. Settle upon full consumption of stellar reserve. Method: quiet. Note: subject believes himself a partner. This belief is an asset. Do not correct until settlement date.*

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, faint through forty feet of pitched stone, the palace breathed its sleepwalker breath.

Corvane read it twice. Then a third time, because the third time was not comprehension but bargaining, the mind's last-ditch audit, checking for transcription error, forgery, fraud. There was no error. The arithmetic was perfect. It was always going to be perfect; that was the whole nature of the thing that wrote it. He had looked at the Regent and seen a colleague, a fellow keeper of accounts, a power that understood margins—and he had never once, in all his calculations, run the numbers on what it must think of *him*. A man who knew where every body was priced. A man who had sold the route of three women to its guards and would sell anything else, at any time, to anyone. Knowledge exceeding operational requirement. Discretion risk: total.

*Subject believes himself a partner. Do not correct until settlement date.*

"Well," Corvane said.

The word hung in the cold air of the vault, and it was the smallest word he had ever spoken, and he heard it for exactly what it was: the sound of a man's entire philosophy failing to balance. Power is a ledger with better margins. Yes. And he had finally been shown his own page, and discovered he was not the keeper of it. He was a line item. An expense. *Settle upon completion. Method: quiet.* The house does not bleed for the game—but he was not the house. He had never been the house. He was the game, and he had been the game for years, and the thing upstairs had simply been patient, because patience was free and loyalty was expensive and the Regent had never paid for anything it could wait for.

Lior watched him with those steady eyes, and when she spoke, her voice was not cruel. That was somehow worse. Cruelty he could have fought.

"Now you know what I felt in chapter after chapter of these ledgers," she said. "Reading your sealed books with Sera, finding the payments routed to the palace. Finding *maintenance of the crown* in black and white. The moment you realize the shelter was the cage." She rose from the bench, and the lamplight moved on her face. "I'm not offering you forgiveness, Guildmaster. You sold us. I haven't forgotten it, and neither have the rats, and neither, I promise you, has Sera Vane, who is currently awake and listening at the wall seam number thirty-nine, because iron carries sound and she's a warden and wardens know walls."

From the left-hand cell, muffled but clear, came Sera Vane's voice, dry as ash: "Forty-one seams. And hello, Corvane. Enjoying the audit?"

Corvane did not answer her. He was looking at the last page again—at *settlement date*—and doing the one calculation left to him, the only one that mattered, the one he should have run years ago: *how long?* The stars were nearly gone. He had felt the nights growing heavier, pressing down. When the last star failed, the Regent would have no further use for starlight, and would turn, unhurried, to the smaller consumptions. To the gifted. To the loose ends. To the quiet method.

Days. Perhaps less.

"Here are my terms," Lior said. "Not a partnership. I learned about partnerships from watching yours. Terms. When we leave this place—and we are going to leave, Guildmaster, the mice have shown me more ways out of this palace than your architects ever built in—you will open the Guild's ledgers. All of them. Sealed and otherwise. Public audit, sworn testimony, your name attached. You will do it because the alternative is a line item marked *quiet*, and because unlike the Regent, I keep my accounts honestly: help us, and when this is over, you go to trial instead of to a grave. That's the best margin left in your ledger. Take it or don't."

Corvane sat very still on his stool in the deepest cell of the Silent Palace, holding the future written in another creature's hand, and for the first time since he was nine years old and had watched his father lose the family ferry to a creditor's arithmetic, Guildmaster Corvane did not know the number.

Then, above them, through forty feet of stone, the dark itself seemed to lean closer—and every lamp in the cells guttered at once, and Lior went rigid, her eyes flying wide, and from somewhere far overhead came a sound like the sky drawing breath.

"It's feeding," she whispered. "The last stars. It's starting *now*."

The lamps died. And in the black, Corvane heard the cell door unlock—not with his key, and not with hers.
