## 16. Escape by Tooth and Claw

The cell beneath the palace had no window, but Lior had learned to read the dark the way she once read ledgers—by what moved through it.

Rats came and went along the mortar seams like clerks carrying messages between branches of a bank. She had spent three days teaching them the shape of her plan in squeaks and whisker-touches: *the grate, the drain, the cistern, the outer wall.* Three days of Isolde pressing her ear to stone while Lior whispered translations. Three days of Sera counting her own breaths because counting was the only prayer a disgraced warden still trusted.

On the fourth night, the lights went out.

Not the lamps—the lamps had been dead for hours, oil rationed since the wards fell. The lights that went out were the ones behind the walls, the pale amber seepage that had haunted the palace's bones ever since they'd been dragged down here, the glow that leaked from the Regent's feeding like marrow through a cracked bone. It guttered once, twice, and then the whole palace exhaled into true darkness, and Lior felt the hunger behind it stretch wide as a mouth.

"It's feeding," she breathed. "It's pulling everything down to feed."

"Then we go now." Sera's voice came from the black, level as a drawn blade. "While it's drunk. While the guards are blind."

"The guards aren't blind," Isolde said. "They're asleep. All of them. Can't you feel it? The whole court just... sat down." A pause, and then, quieter, with something almost like pity: "It doesn't even leave them their fainting. It takes them mid-step."

Lior pressed her palm flat against the wall and opened the door in her mind that she had kept locked her whole life.

*Come,* she said to the rats. *Now. All of you.*

*

Corvane arrived before the rats did.

She heard him first—his boots on the stair, unhurried, the boots of a man who had never once needed to run—and then the scrape of his key, and then his voice, dry as vellum, floating into the cell ahead of his lantern.

"I do hope you're decent. Treason is no excuse for slovenliness."

The lantern light found him standing in the corridor with a ring of keys in one hand and an expression of profound inconvenience on his face, as though he had come to complain about the wine at his own execution.

"You're letting us out," Sera said. Not a question. Her hand had already found the loose flagstone where they'd hidden a shiv made from a broken quill, because Sera Vane did not believe in gifts.

"I am facilitating a departure," Corvane corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference is deniability. The Regent has ordered your executions for dawn. I have reviewed the order and found it—" he examined his fingernails— "premature. Dead assets yield nothing. Live assets, properly managed, yield interest." He looked at Lior over the lantern flame. "You showed me my page, Mistress Iversen. Expense line item forty-one. *To be settled.* I have read enough ledgers to know what happens to settled accounts. So. I am settling myself elsewhere, and I find I require three witnesses who can testify, credibly and publicly, that Guildmaster Corvane aided the crown's enemies only after the crown stopped being the crown."

"You're buying your acquittal," Isolde said.

"I'm diversifying," Corvane said. "It's the same thing, done politely."

"And if we refuse?" Sera asked.

"Then I lock this door again and go back to negotiating with whatever wins." He smiled, and it was the smile from the counting house, the one that had welcomed her for eleven years and never once reached his eyes. "But you won't refuse. You need the guard rotations, the postern gate codes, the schedule of when the Regent sleeps—which it does not, by the way, which is why it eats instead. And I need you gone before dawn, because if the Regent wakes and finds its prisoners fled through a door I unlocked, my page gets settled early." He jingled the keys. "We are, for one ugly night, partners. Try not to make it sentimental."

He unlocked the cell, handed Sera a folded paper covered in his small precise hand, and turned up the stair without waiting for thanks. At the top he paused.

"Mistress Iversen. A professional courtesy." He did not look back. "Whatever you are—I always knew, you understand; I have known since you balanced the Marrowfield account at fourteen and the mice brought you the missing page—I want you to know that hiring you was the best investment I ever made. Do try to survive long enough to appreciate the return."

Then he was gone, and the stairwell was dark again, and Lior stood in the doorway of her cage feeling the strangest grief, because the cage had been the only shelter she'd ever known, and even the jailer's betrayal could not quite make her stop being grateful for the bars.

*Come,* she told the rats again. *All of you. Please.*

And the walls answered.

*

They came up through the drain as the palace slept its drugged sleep, three women soaked to the waist in cistern water, following a river of rats that flowed ahead of them through the dark like a tide with opinions. Lior held the connection open the way she'd hold a door against wind—with her whole body, teeth gritted, sweat freezing on her spine.

It was different out here. In the cells, speaking to the rats had been like whispering through a keyhole. Out in the palace's sleeping dark, with the Regent gorged and slow and the old bindings of fear burned away, the voices came flooding in from every direction at once.

The rats, first—a thousand of them, their minds quick and silver, thinking in smells and urgencies, *warm-warm-warm, food-food, follow-follow-follow*. Then the mice in the wainscoting. Then the moths battering themselves against the shuttered windows, each one a tiny burning confusion of *light-light-light*. Then, as they climbed past the kitchens, the cats—six of them, palace cats grown strange and half-feral under the Regent's reign, their minds cold and green and patient as winter ponds.

Then the dogs in the kennel yard. Then the horses screaming softly in their stalls. Then the pigeons roosting in the eaves, hundreds of them, their dreams raining down through her like grey snow.

Lior staggered against a wall.

"Lior?" Isolde caught her arm.

"I'm fine. I'm—" She wasn't fine. She was everywhere. She could feel the worms turning in the garden soil and the beetles ticking in the woodwork and something vast and slow and patient moving in the river beneath the city—an eel older than the guild, older than the kingdom, dreaming in the mud. A thousand minds, ten thousand, all of them touching hers, all of them *open*, and underneath every voice ran the same current, the same warm dark pull:

*Belong. Sink. Stop being one thing. Be the swarm.*

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it. Every hour she'd spent making herself small, every ledger line she'd hidden behind, every year of silence—and here at the edge of her own gift was an exit from selfhood entirely. No more Lior Iversen, beast-speaker, criminal, girl. Just the chorus. Just the flood. She could put down the weight of having a name.

Somewhere far away, Sera was saying her name. "Lior. *Lior.* Blink twice if you can hear me."

"She's hearing too much," Isolde said, and there was tears in it, or fury, or both. "Lior, listen to me. You listen to me. My father used to say the crown isn't a thing you wear, it's a thing that wears you. He was more right than he knew. Don't you dare let it wear you too. Come back. *Come back and be small if you have to, but be Lior.*"

*Be Lior.*

The name went through the flood like a keel through water.

And the beasts—all of them, the rats and the cats and the dreaming eel—went quiet for one heartbeat, listening to it too, as if the name itself were a sound worth hearing. As if somewhere in the chorus, her name meant something: *the one who speaks. The one who comes back.*

Lior gasped, and the world snapped back to one shape, one pair of eyes, one cold wet woman standing in a sleeping palace holding a princess's arm.

"I'm here," she said. Her voice sounded scraped. "I'm here. I—thank you." She steadied herself against the wall. "Isolde. If I start to go under again—if I start talking in we—"

"I'll say your name," Isolde said. "As many times as it takes."

"That," said Sera, checking the paper Corvane had given her against the corridor junctions, "is either very beautiful or a serious tactical liability, and I genuinely cannot tell which. Postern gate's two floors down. Move."

*

The postern gate stood at the base of the old guard tower, iron-banded, locked with a mechanism older than the Regent and blessedly mechanical, because whatever else the thing in the king had devoured, it had never learned to pick locks—it simply persuaded the people who held keys, and tonight everyone who held keys was face-down in a dreamless sleep.

Sera worked the mechanism. Isolde watched the yard. Lior listened.

That was how she felt it coming: a wrongness rolling across the city from the direction of the keep, a pressure drop, a silence opening like a wound. The Regent, gorged on the court's stolen light, was reaching further tonight—out past the walls, past the wards' black stumps, drinking from the sky itself. She felt the last stars above the city flicker like candles in a draft.

And she felt the beasts feel it.

Every creature in Vellamark had lived under that hunger for years without knowing what it was, the way livestock live under weather. But tonight, with Lior's voice connecting them all into one vast listening nerve, they *knew* it. She felt the knowledge move through the chorus like fire through dry grass: the rats in the granaries, the cats on the rooftops, the dogs straining at their kennel chains, the pigeons wheeling blind above the starless towers. A thousand small hearts arriving at the same conclusion at the same moment.

*It is the thing that emptied the burrows. It is the thing that silenced the birds. It is the reason for the hungry winters and the empty nests.*

*And the speaker says it can be fought.*

"They're going to come," Lior whispered. Her hands were shaking—not with fear now, or not only. "Sera. Isolde. They're all coming. I didn't ask them to. I just—they heard, and they're *coming*."

The first wave hit the yard like a brown flood pouring out of the drains: rats in their hundreds, streaming across the cobbles toward the gate. Behind them came the cats, six shadows moving like spilled ink, and the kennel dogs had begun to howl—not the frightened howling of the past weeks but something older, deeper, a sound like the border itself remembering its voice. Overhead the pigeon lofts erupted, a thousand wings hammering the dark.

"The gate's open," Sera said, staring out at the seething yard with an expression Lior had never seen on her face before—awe, naked and unarmored. "Gods above. It's actually open. We just walk out."

"No," Lior said. "We walk out *loud*. Listen—the Regent will feel this. When the beasts move together, it feels it, the way a man feels lice. It'll wake. It'll send the hollowed ones after us. So we don't sneak." She stepped out into the yard, into the middle of the swirling tide of creatures, and opened herself wider than she had ever dared.

*To me,* she said, to all of them at once, and her voice was not a whisper anymore. It was a bell. *To me, all of you. Tonight we are loud.*

The yard answered. Dogs poured from the kennels, chains snapping where the locks had rusted. Horses screamed and kicked their stable doors until the latches gave. From the gutters and the granaries and the bell tower they came, foxes and ferrets and a badger the size of a barrel who had, Lior gathered dimly, been holding a personal grudge against the palace kitchens for a decade. The noise of them rose into the night like a second storm, and above it the sky stayed black, and somewhere in the keep something ancient and starving began, slowly, furiously, to wake.

They went out through the postern gate at the head of an army of everything the kingdom had forgotten it contained, and the streets of Vellamark filled around them—cats dropping from windowsills to join, rats pouring from sewers, sparrows rising off the rooftops in clouds. Lior rode in the center of it on a stolen cart-horse, holding the chorus in her arms like an armful of live wire, and she could feel herself fraying at the edges, dissolving, the borders of *Lior* going soft as wet vellum—

*Be Lior.*

Isolde's voice, again, riding beside her. And Sera's hand, gripping her shoulder, hard, human, painfully singular.

They reached the ward-tower ruins as the eastern sky began, impossibly, to show the faintest bruise of grey. Behind them the palace blazed with waking fury. Ahead lay the broken circle where the binding rite waited, and Sera's rite, and the price Lior had already agreed to pay.

She slid from the horse on legs that barely belonged to her, and let the chorus go—one strand at a time, gently, thanking each mind in turn—and stood alone in her own skull for the first time in hours, ringing with silence.

"It felt us," she said. "It knows where we're going. We have until full dark tomorrow, maybe less."

"Then we work," Sera said, already climbing the ruin.

Lior looked back once, at the city behind them, at the thousand small eyes watching from the dark, and understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound, that the Regent was not the only thing in this kingdom capable of devouring a person whole.

Her gift had nearly done it in a single night.

Tomorrow she would walk into its throat on purpose—and she would need to be more Lior than she had ever been to come back out.
