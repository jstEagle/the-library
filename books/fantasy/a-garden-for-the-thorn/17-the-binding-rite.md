## 17. The Binding Rite

The ward-tower had been beautiful once. Sera could see it in the bones of the thing—in the way the fallen stones still curved toward a center that no longer existed, in the hairline channels cut into the surviving walls where light had once run like water through glass. She had stood in towers like this as a young warden, back when the order still mattered, back when she'd believed that keeping a border was the same as keeping a promise.

Now the tower was a wound in the earth, and she was going to ask someone to bleed into it.

She worked by lamplight and memory, kneeling on the cold flagstones of what had been the rite-chamber, chalking lines between the old grooves where the original binding had been carved. The stone remembered. That was the terrible, hopeful truth of ward-work: stone remembered everything, even when men chose to forget. Her fingers traced a spiral that predated the kingdom itself, older than Aldric's line, older than the Vellum Guild's first ledger. The warden records she'd memorized in another life—the ones that had gotten her stripped of rank when she asked too many questions about them—described this pattern in language so old it had to be sung rather than read.

*The Regent is bound,* the oldest record said, *by voice within circle, by name within throat.*

She had read that line a hundred times over the past two days and understood it fully only now, chalk dust on her knuckles, her breath fogging in the tower's dead air.

A voice the binding could not refuse. Spoken from inside the circle.

From inside the thing itself.

Behind her, boots on broken stone. Sera didn't turn. She knew Isolde's stride—quick, clipped, a woman who walked like someone perpetually late for an argument—and beneath it, softer, the particular careful tread of Lior Iversen, who moved through the world as if apologizing for occupying space. Sera had spent ten years learning to read people the way other wardens read wards, and these two she could have read blindfolded by now.

"The rats came again," Isolde said. "Half the city's beasts are heading for the high ground. Deer moving through the streets like they own them. Whatever the Regent did last night with the blackout—it's feeding faster."

"It's afraid," Sera said. "That's why it feeds faster. Fear makes things greedy." She sat back on her heels and finally looked at them. "I've finished the rite. Or finished finding it. There's a difference."

Lior stepped past her, crouched, and studied the chalked spiral with the same attention she gave ledgers—head tilted, eyes tracking, lips slightly parted. Even now, even knowing what was being asked of her, Sera thought, the woman looked like an accountant examining a suspicious column of figures.

"Talk me through it," Lior said.

Sera rose, joints aching, and walked the circle's edge. "The original binding wasn't a cage. Everyone assumes it was—a spirit locked in a box. It wasn't. It was a *conversation*. The Regent was bound into service by a voice speaking from inside its own circumference. The rite doesn't imprison it. It *persuades* it, permanently. It convinces the Regent, at the deepest level of its being, that obedience is its own nature." She tapped the outer ring. "That's why it lasted three hundred years. You can't break out of something you believe is your own skin."

"And when the kings got greedy," Isolde said quietly, "and unbound it to use it as a weapon—"

"Then the persuasion ended, and everything the Regent actually was came flooding back. Hunger without instruction. Appetite without purpose." Sera met her eyes. "To rebind it, we have to restart the conversation. A living voice, inside the circle, speaking the words of the rite while the Regent is present and listening. Not shouting over it. Not warding against it. *Inside* it. The voice has to come from within its own reach, or the binding won't take root. It will simply refuse to hear, the way you refuse to hear a stranger calling from across a river."

The silence that followed had weight. Sera let it sit. She had decided, somewhere in the last sleepless night, that she would not soften this. Softening it would be theft—stealing from Lior the right to understand exactly what was being asked.

"Inside its reach," Lior repeated. Her voice was steady. Too steady; Sera recognized the ledger-voice, the one that kept accounts of feeling the way she kept accounts of coin. "Meaning it can take me. Like it took the king."

"Yes."

"While I'm speaking."

"Yes."

"And if it takes me before I finish—" Lior paused, and Sera watched her do the arithmetic no ledger had prepared her for. "Then it wears me, and it knows every word of the rite, and it makes sure no one ever speaks it again."

"That's the risk. That's the whole of it." Sera crouched and drew a second, smaller circle within the first. "There's one mitigation. One. The rite requires a true name spoken at the moment of binding—not the speaker's own. A name held outside the circle, by someone the speaker trusts, called at the critical moment. The name anchors the voice. Gives it something to return to when the Regent pulls." She looked up. "It's not much. But it's what the old wardens left us."

Isolde had gone very pale. Sera watched her work through it—the princess who had run for ten years, who had learned that being right cost everything, whose whole arc since the cells beneath the palace had bent toward one lesson: *stop running.* Her hands were fists at her sides.

"No," Isolde said.

"I haven't asked anyone anything yet," Sera said.

"You don't have to. I can do arithmetic too." Isolde turned to Lior. "You heard her. Inside its throat. It took a *king*, Lior—a man with a kingdom's worth of will and a lifetime of practice holding himself together. What are you going to hold onto? Ledgers?"

"Myself," Lior said.

The word landed strangely in the ruined chamber—small, plain, absolute. Sera saw Isolde flinch as if struck.

"You don't even know if you *have* yourself," Isolde pressed. "You spent your whole life hiding. You told me that. In the cells, you told me—you made yourself small so long you're not sure there was ever anything underneath. And now you want to bet the kingdom on—"

"On the possibility that there was." Lior straightened. For the first time since Sera had known her, she stood at her full height, which was not considerable, and somehow filled the chamber anyway. "Isolde. Listen to me. I have spent thirty years being nobody. I balanced accounts for monsters and called it safety. I listened to rats because rats were the only voices that never asked me to be less than I am." She gestured at the chalked spiral. "This rite needs a voice the Regent cannot refuse to hear. Do you understand what that means? It can't be just any voice. It has to be a voice that speaks the language of bindings. A beast-speaker. *Me.* There is no version of this where Sera stands in that circle, or you do. The Regent would eat either of you before the second word."

"She's right," Sera said quietly. "I checked. Three times. The rite's grammar requires a speaker fluent in the old tongue of compulsion—the same root-language beasts speak and bindings are written in. We burned that fluency out of the kingdom generations ago. Almost entirely." She held Lior's gaze. "Almost."

"So it's me or nothing," Lior said.

"It's you or nothing."

Isolde made a sound low in her throat, half fury, half grief, and turned away, pressing the heel of her hand against her eyes. Sera let her. Some griefs needed privacy even in company.

*

They spent the rest of the day preparing, because preparation was the only prayer any of them trusted.

Sera rebuilt the circle in the old grooves, clearing debris from channels that hadn't carried power in centuries, mixing chalk with ash from the burnt warden-records she'd carried out of the palace archives strapped to her chest like a wounded child. At each of the seven anchor points she set a stone from the original binding—one of which, she noted with grim satisfaction, bore claw-marks deep enough to fit the Regent's description in the oldest texts. The thing had fought its first binding too. That meant it could be fought.

Isolde worked beside her in silence for hours, and then, near dusk, said without preamble: "If it takes her—if it finishes taking her—what do we do?"

Sera set her stone. "We finish the rite around her. The circle holds the shape even if the voice fails. And then—" She made herself say it plainly. "Then we do what the old law always wanted to do to her kind. What we should have been ashamed of all along."

Isolde was quiet a long moment. "Burn her."

"Free her," Sera corrected. "Fire is just the door. The mercy is in how fast we open it." She looked at the princess. "Can you do it? If it comes to that? Because if you can't, tell me now, and I'll put someone else at the outer anchor. Someone who can."

Isolde stared at the claw-marked stone for a long time. When she answered, her voice had gone flat and royal, the voice of a woman who had accused a king to his face in front of his court: "I watched my father get eaten for ten years and did nothing but collect evidence. I am done doing nothing. Yes. I can do it."

Sera believed her. It was the saddest belief she'd held in years.

*

Night came down over the ruined tower like a lid.

They had chosen midnight deliberately—the hour the stars had first failed, the hour the Regent's hunger ran thinnest between feedings, according to the patterns Lior had charted from the rats' reports. Above them, the sky was wrong. Not starless anymore—the Regent couldn't drink what it had already swallowed—but dimmed, patched, as if the heavens were a tapestry someone had been picking apart thread by thread. Only a few dozen stars remained in the whole bowl of the sky, huddled near the horizon like survivors at the edge of a battlefield.

Lior stood at the circle's center, and Sera performed the last preparations with hands that refused, entirely, to shake. She would not permit them to shake. Whatever happened in the next hour, her hands would be the steadiest thing in this ruin.

"The moment it notices you," she said, walking the final checks, "it will try three things. First, fear—it will show you its hunger until your mind buckles. Second, desire—it will offer you whatever you most want. For you, I'd guess, invisibility. Safety. A small room where no one can ever find you again. Third—" she hesitated, then finished honestly, "—it will simply take you, if the first two fail. The rite gives you maybe forty heartbeats between its attention and its grip. The words take sixty. The gap is where the true name matters. When you feel yourself slipping—when you lose the shape of your own thoughts—hold onto the sound of Isolde's voice saying your name. Let it be a rope. Climb it."

"And if I climb the wrong way?" Lior asked. "Toward it instead of out?"

"Then we burn the rope," Sera said, "and pray the old wardens were right about what fire does to a binding."

Lior nodded slowly. Then she did something Sera had not expected. She reached into her coat and drew out a small, battered ledger—her personal one, the book of small debts she'd kept since her first days at the Guild—and pressed it into Sera's hands.

"If I don't come back to myself," she said, "give this to whoever teaches the gifted children after. They should know what hiding costs. Line by line."

Sera took the book. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the tower.

"Lior," she said, and found she had to stop and start again. "I hunted your kind once. Early in my warden years. A girl in the fen-country who talked to herons. I was following orders, and I told myself that mattered, and I have hated myself for fifteen years in a way I never managed to say out loud." She closed her fingers around the ledger. "Whatever happens tonight—I'm glad it's your voice in that circle. If it has to be anyone's."

Lior smiled. It was a real smile, tired and crooked and entirely her own. "That's the nicest treason anyone's ever confessed to me."

From the outer wall, Isolde called: "Movement. Lots of it." And beneath the words, rising from the dark countryside like a tide, came the sound Sera had been hearing in fragments for days—wings, hooves, the soft massed breathing of creatures converging. The city's beasts, the border's beasts, drawn by something none of them could name. Drawn, perhaps, by the same compass that had pointed Lior toward the truth from the beginning.

"They're coming," Lior said softly. She tilted her head, listening to a conversation no one else could hear, and her expression shifted through wonder into something like sorrow. "They know what she is. What it is. They've been fleeing it for years, and tonight they've stopped running." She looked up at Sera. "They want to watch. They want to see if the small loud creature can do what the big hungry thing couldn't—keep a promise."

Sera took her place at the western anchor. Isolde took the eastern, the fire-pitch and torch ready at her feet, her face lit orange and set like carved granite. The circle stood complete: seven stones, seven anchors, one voice.

"One more thing," Sera said. "When it's done—when the binding takes—there may be a moment where you're still inside its reach and it's already bound. A moment where it hates you more than it has ever hated anything. Don't linger. Walk out. Live. That's an order from a disgraced warden, and I'm told those don't count for anything, so consider it a request from a friend."

Lior rolled her shoulders. Cracked her knuckles, absurdly, like a clerk settling in for a long audit. And then she looked at both of them—at the traitor and the runaway, the two women the kingdom had discarded, standing guard at the edges of her sacrifice—and said, in the voice of someone closing a ledger for the last time:

"Begin."

Sera began to sing.

The old words came up out of her like water from a deep well, syllables that predated the kingdom, and the chalked channels caught the sound and ran with it, light crawling the spiral's length for the first time in three hundred years. The seven stones woke one by one, humming their separate notes. And far away—too soon, far too soon, though Sera had known it would be soon—something vast and cold turned its attention across the darkened miles, and the temperature of the ruin dropped as though winter had leaned down to look through a window.

At the circle's center, Lior lifted her chin and spoke into the gathering dark, her voice steady, her name waiting in Isolde's mouth like a blade:

And the darkness answered.
