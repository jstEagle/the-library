## 18. Throne of Hunger

The last star went out as they crossed the palace threshold.

Lior felt it the way a deaf woman feels a door slam—a pressure through the soles of her feet, through her teeth, through the small bones behind her ears where the world's hum lived. For ten years she had kept that hum at arm's length, muffled under ledgers and ledgers and ledgers, and now it stopped altogether, and the silence was worse than any sound.

Above them, through the shattered skylight of the eastern gallery, the sky was a black so total it looked like a wound in the world. Not clouded. Not overcast. *Emptied.* The stars had not gone anywhere. They had been eaten, one by one, over ten patient years, and tonight the Regent had swallowed the last of them to feed whatever came next.

"It knows we're here," Sera said. She moved ahead of them with her warden's blade drawn low, its edge drinking what little light remained from the emergency lanterns the Guild smugglers had wired along the walls. Her voice was flat, professional—the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this moment for years and found, upon arriving, that rehearsal had prepared her for nothing. "The whole building just changed. Can you feel it? The air's gone thick."

"I can feel it," Isolde said.

Lior could feel more than that. She could feel the palace itself, every rat in its walls, every moth beating against every lamp, every spider frozen mid-web—all of them pressed flat with terror, all of them whispering the same thing in a hundred small voices: *it is awake, it is awake, it is hungry, run.*

She did not run. She put her hand flat against the marble wall and opened herself instead, wider than she ever had, past the edge of safety, into the chattering dark of the palace's hidden life.

*Tell me,* she said to them. *All of you. Tell me everything.*

The answer came back like a flood through a broken dam.

*The servants do not sleep anymore but they do not wake either. They walk their rounds with nothing behind their eyes. There are things in the throne room that were men once. The king has not eaten in three days but he does not die. It is saving him. It likes him fresh.*

Lior pulled her hand back from the wall as though it had been burned. In a way, it had.

"What did they say?" Isolde asked. She stood very straight, wearing armor she'd borrowed from a dead guard—too big in the shoulders, perfect everywhere else, as if she'd been fitted for it in some other life. Ten years in a convent at the border had taught her how to stand still while the world ended. "Lior. What did they say?"

"That the court isn't going to let us through politely."

"Then we go impolitely," Sera said, and started down the gallery.

* * *

They met the first of the hollowed in the Hall of Mirrors.

Lior had heard of it—everyone had, in the old stories, before the king closed himself away: a corridor lined floor to ceiling with silvered glass, built so that a queen long dead could see herself multiplied into infinity. The mirrors still hung there. But the faces looking out of them now were wrong.

The courtiers came walking slowly out of the dark ends of the hall, and they were beautiful, and they were terrible. They wore silk and velvet and powdered wigs, and their clothes were immaculate, and behind their eyes there was nothing at all—not cruelty, not malice, not even emptiness in the way an abandoned house is empty. An abandoned house remembers being full. These faces had been scraped. Wiped clean the way a ledger page is wiped clean, ready for new entries, and the entry written on each of them was the same:

*Nothing matters. Nothing has ever mattered. Come and be wiped too.*

"Hollowed," Sera breathed. "Warden records called them hollowed. I thought—I hoped—it was metaphor."

"It wasn't metaphor," Lior said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Around her, in the walls and under the floor, the mice were screaming, and she held their screaming like a rope thrown to a drowning woman. *It will make us like that,* the mice said. *It will make everyone like that. It calls it peace.*

There were perhaps thirty of them filling the far end of the hall, arranged in loose courtly clusters as though waiting for a ball to begin. One of them—a man in ambassador's blue, his face smooth as an egg—turned toward them with unhurried grace and smiled.

"Guests," he said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a host greeting arrivals, warm and welcoming and utterly vacant. "How lovely. His Majesty is receiving."

"We're not here for an audience," Isolde said, and her voice cracked across the hall like a whip. "We're here for my father."

Something flickered across the ambassador's face—something almost like amusement, if amusement could exist in a thing with no self left to be amused. "His Majesty receives all his children," it said. "He will receive you too. He receives everyone, eventually." And then, softly, to the courtiers around it, in the tone of a man ordering wine: "Bring them."

They came.

Not running—walking, gliding, in that horrible courtly shuffle, hands open, faces serene, thirty sets of empty eyes fixed on the three women standing in the lamplight. Sera swore once, quietly, and set her feet.

"Lior—" she began.

"I know." Lior had already reached out, already flung her voice wide into the palace's dark corners, into the attics and cellars and chimneys where the city's beasts had fled when the last star died. *Come,* she told them. *Please. I know what I'm asking. I know what it costs you. Come anyway.*

And the palace answered.

It began as a sound—a scratching, a scrabbling, a thunder of small claws—and then it was not a sound but a flood. Rats poured from the walls in silver rivers. Crows and starlings dropped shrieking through the broken skylight. Cats came down from the rafters with their fur standing in spikes, dogs burst through service doors, foxes and ferrets and one enormous pale owl that swept the length of the hall like a ghost with a grudge. The hollowed courtiers paused, and for the first time something moved behind those scraped eyes—not fear exactly, but recognition, the recognition of a debt collector seeing the account book burn.

"You cannot," the ambassador said, to Lior directly now, and its voice lost its warmth. "They are ours. Everything soft in this kingdom is ours. We have been eating it for ten years."

"You've been eating it," Lior agreed. Her heart was slamming against her ribs. A thousand minds were touching hers—a thousand small fierce lives, all of them looking through her eyes at the empty people in the pretty clothes. She had never held so much. She could feel the edges of herself going soft, the borders of *Lior* dissolving into the great gray river of *we*, and she understood suddenly, viscerally, why her gift ended in fire. This was the precipice. This was the fall.

But the beasts had chosen her, not the other way around. And choice, she was learning, was the only thing the Regent could not digest.

"They were never yours," she said aloud. "You borrowed them. The loan is called."

The hall erupted.

It was not a battle so much as an avalanche with intentions. The crows struck first, diving at the courtiers' eyes, and the hollowed did not scream—they simply raised their arms, serene, unbothered, swatting birds from the air with the mild annoyance of wealthy people shooing flies. A dog took an ambassador's throat and shook it, and the ambassador did not bleed properly, did not cry out, just folded like a puppet whose strings had been cut. Rats ran up trouser legs and under silk hems. The owl fell on a duchess in ermine and drove her to the marble.

And through it all the hollowed kept walking forward. That was the horror of it. You could knock them down and they rose; you could break their arms and the arms hung useless while the rest of them continued, smiling, reaching, murmuring *come, come, come* in voices like lullabies. They did not fight to win. They fought to touch. Every hand that grazed Sera's shoulder, every fingertip that brushed Isolde's hair, carried something in it—Lior felt it through the mice, a cold thread seeking skin, seeking the warm place behind the eyes.

"Don't let them touch you!" she shouted. "Don't let them—"

A courtier in a general's coat broke through the beast-swarm. He moved faster than the others, or perhaps he had simply been closer, and he came straight for Isolde with both hands outstretched and that terrible gentle smile, and Lior saw it happen the way you see a cart crash in slow motion—from too far away, with your lungs full of ice.

Sera got there first.

Of course she did. Sera Vane, disgraced warden, traitor to her own order, a woman who had spent ten years cataloguing her failures—she moved like all of it meant something at last. She stepped between the general-thing and the princess and drove her blade up under its chin, and the thing collapsed around the steel, and its falling hand closed around Sera's forearm.

That was all. One hand. One touch.

But Lior heard Sera's breath change. Heard it snag, stutter, go thin. Saw her stagger back, wrench free, press her free hand to her temple as though staving off a headache—as though something cold had just slipped a finger inside her skull and begun, delicately, to read.

"Sera!" Isolde caught her as she went down. The warden's blade rang on the marble. Her eyes were still her own—that was the mercy—but they were wide and glassy and tracking something none of them could see. "Sera, stay with me. Stay with me, you stubborn—"

"Princess." Sera's voice was slurred, distant. She smiled, and it was the worst smile Lior had ever seen, because it was genuine. "Told you... I'd get you home..."

The cold thread was pulling. Lior could feel it through the rats pressed close against her boots—feel it tugging at the edges of Sera like a tide going out, patient, gentle, offering rest. *Come and be wiped,* it whispered in whatever language such things used. *Come and stop carrying it all. You're so tired. We can see how tired you are.*

"No," Lior said.

She knelt, seized Sera's face in both hands, and shouted—not with her throat, but with the gift, with everything she had, straight down the cold thread into whatever waited at the end of it: *SHE IS NOT YOURS TO READ. GET OUT OF HER.*

The palace flinched. Lior felt it flinch—in the walls, in the water pipes, in the teeth of the building itself. And the cold thread snapped like rotted cord, recoiling, and somewhere far above them, deep in the heart of the palace, something vast turned its attention toward the little beast-speaker kneeling in blood and feathers and broken glass, and considered her.

For one endless moment, everything in the hall stopped. Hollowed and beast alike froze, heads turning, all eyes—empty and living—finding her.

Then the ambassador's corpse twitched, and its ruined jaw worked, and the voice that came out of it was not the ambassador's. It was layered, resonant, many-voiced, the sound of a choir singing through a single broken mouth.

"*Little speaker.*"

Every hair on Lior's body stood up.

"*You should have stayed in your ledgers.*"

"Probably," Lior said. Her voice shook. She let it shake. Let it see her afraid—let it look at the fear and find nothing there worth keeping, because fear was the one thing she had never once been able to trade away. "But then who would balance your accounts?"

The ruined jaw curved. "*Come to the throne room, then. Bring your beasts. Bring the little runaway princess. Bring the warden, if she can walk. We have been so patient, and the table is set, and the last light is nearly finished.*" The head rolled toward Isolde, toward the borrowed armor, and the many voices softened into something horribly like tenderness. "*Your father asks for you, Isolde. Every day. He asks so beautifully.*"

Isolde made a sound like a blade being drawn.

The corpse went slack. The remaining hollowed—half of them torn apart now, the rest retreating in that gliding, unhurried way—melted backward into the dark ends of the hall, and the beasts parted to let them go, watching, waiting for her word.

Lior gave no word. She was already moving to Sera.

* * *

They propped the warden against a pillar in the antechamber beyond the Hall of Mirrors, where the beast-light of a hundred glowing moths the crows had herded together cast a soft green shimmer over the marble. It was the best they could do. Sera was conscious—barely—one hand pressed hard against her own sternum as though holding a door shut from the inside.

"I'm fine," she said, before either of them could speak.

"You're lying," Isolde said.

"I'm a warden. Lying is most of the job." But her grin was crooked and gray, and her eyes kept sliding out of focus, chasing the ghost of the thing that had touched her. She flexed her fingers deliberately, one at a time, like a woman proving to herself that her hands were still her own. "Listen. Both of you. Whatever's in there—if it gets its hooks into me again, don't hesitate. Don't negotiate. Cut me loose and keep moving. Promise me."

Neither of them answered fast enough, and Sera's laugh was dry as old paper.

"Wonderful. Two sentimental fools and a thousand rats. We're doomed." She tried to rise, failed, and gripped Isolde's offered arm with white-knuckled determination. "Fine. Then help me up, because I am not missing this. Ten years I hunted the thing that broke the wards. I'll crawl to that throne room if I have to."

"You won't have to," Lior said quietly.

Because she could hear them coming.

Down every corridor of the palace, through every vent and duct and drainpipe, the beasts of Vaelholm were converging—not just the palace's own creatures now but the city's, summoned through the open windows and broken gates, flowing toward her through the dark like iron filings toward a lodestone. Wolves had come in from the borderlands, following the scent of her voice on the wind. The great pale owl rode the thermals above the broken roof. Somewhere below, in the flooded crypts, even the fish had gathered in the black water, facing upward, waiting.

And threaded through all of it, faint and steady, another sound: a heartbeat. Two heartbeats, rather—one vast and slow beneath the throne room floor, and one human and failing somewhere near it. The king. Still alive. Still drowning. Still asking, in whatever was left of him, for his daughter.

Lior stood, and the light of the moths caught her, and for the first time in her life she did not try to make herself small.

"The Regent wants an audience," she said. "Let's not keep it waiting."

They went down the long corridor together—Sera limping between them with her sword recovered and her jaw set, Isolde walking like a storm given legs, and Lior at the center of a river of claws and wings and burning eyes, ten thousand small lives flowing with her toward the doors of the throne room.

The doors were open. Of course they were open. It wanted them to come in.

Beyond them, lit by the dying gold of the last emergency lanterns, the throne room stretched away into shadow—and on the throne sat a thin figure in a crown, and beside the throne, waiting with folded hands and a scraped-clean smile, stood Guildmaster Corvane.

"Well," Corvane said pleasantly, into the ringing silence. "I don't suppose we could discuss this like reasonable people."

Behind Lior, Sera exhaled a word their mothers would have washed their mouths for, and raised her sword.

* * *

The last starlight failed completely as they crossed the threshold, and the throne room went dark, and in the dark, the Regent began to laugh.
