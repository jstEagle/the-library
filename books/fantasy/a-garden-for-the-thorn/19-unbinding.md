## 19. Unbinding

The circle was chalk and salt and old blood, drawn on the throne room floor in the shape Sera's hands remembered from records no one was meant to survive reading. Lior stood at its center while the last starlight failed above the shattered skylight, and she made herself look at each of them one final time—not because she feared forgetting their faces, but because she wanted to carry them in with her.

Sera stood at the circle's eastern point, one hand pressed to her ribs where the hollowed court had struck her, her jaw set in the particular way it did when she was refusing to say goodbye. Isolde stood at the western point, holding the child's cipher-book against her chest like a shield that had never once worked. Behind them, past the doors they had broken open, the palace groaned with a thousand small voices—rats and crows and cats and one indignant palace goose—all of them waiting on Lior's word, all of them refusing, now, to obey the thing on the throne.

"You don't have to do this," Isolde said. She had said it four times already. She would say it again. That was who she was: a woman who had spent ten years collecting proof of a monster and still could not stop offering people exits.

"Yes, I do." Lior flexed her fingers. They were steady. That surprised her. "It can't hear the unbinding from outside. Warden's law—the binding answers only to a voice inside its own throat. You told me that yourself, Sera."

"I told you what the records said," Sera corrected. "The records also said the rite required a warden of the third rank and a full quorum of the Wardens' Conclave, both of which stopped existing before I was born. We are improvising at the edge of an abyss, Iversen. I want that noted for posterity."

"Noted," Lior said, and almost smiled.

Above them, the dark where the stars should be seemed to lean closer. The Regent sat upon the throne wearing King Aldric's body like a coat grown too tight, and it had stopped pretending otherwise. Its head was tilted, watching her with Aldric's faded blue eyes, and when it spoke, its voice came from everywhere at once—from the walls, from the mice, from the marrow of the building itself.

*Little speaker.* The hunger in it was not rage. That was the worst discovery of the night. It was patience. *You will stand in my circle and offer me your throat, and you believe you will still be yourself when I am done drinking. I have worn kings, girl. I have worn saints. No one remains.*

"Then you've never worn anyone who knew what it was to be invisible," Lior said. "I've spent my whole life being no one. It turns out I'm very good at it. And very good at coming back."

She stepped into the center of the sigil, where the chalk lines crossed, and spoke the words Sera had drilled into her until her tongue bled: the opening of the binding, the invitation, the door.

*I unmake my borders. I open my house. Enter, and be known.*

The Regent rose from the throne.

* * *

It came into her like cold water filling a lung.

There was no pain at first—that was the trap, she understood immediately, there was never any pain at first—and there was no moment of contact either, no boundary crossed, because the Regent did not experience boundaries. It experienced *room*. And Lior had just made herself room.

She felt it pour along every channel of her that had ever held anything: her memory of her mother's kitchen, the smell of tallow and bread; the first time she'd understood the rats beneath the counting-house floor, six years old and horrified and *delighted*; the fox dying in the gutter saying her name like a bell tolling. It drank these the way she had once seen Guild clerks drink wine after a profitable quarter—casually, thoroughly, without gratitude.

And then it showed her what it was.

Hunger. Not hunger as she had known it—not the body's simple complaint—but hunger as a *shape*, as a country, as the only geography there had ever been. It had been bound to advise kings, once. A spirit of counsel, a voice of wisdom, chained by the first wardens into service. But chains chafe, and counsel curdles into appetite when the one who is advised never says thank you, and somewhere in the long centuries the advice had become consumption, and the consumption had become everything. It had eaten the border wards one thread at a time over forty years. It had eaten the stars the way a man eats the seeds out of fruit, absently, because they were there. It had eaten Aldric slowly, lovingly, savoring him—a king who had begged it for wisdom about his daughter and discovered too late that wisdom, in the Regent's mouth, meant *everything you are, poured out*.

Lior hung inside that hunger like a moth inside a cathedral, and she felt herself beginning to agree with it.

That was how it won. Not through force. Through *reasonableness*. From inside, the hunger made sense. What was a self, really, but hoarded experience? What was identity but a ledger of debts owed to other people? If you dissolved the ledger, no one could ever collect. If you became the hunger, you would never again be small, never again be hidden, never again be the beast-speaker burning at the stake while the crowd looked away because looking was dangerous. You would be the thing everyone looked away *from*. You would be the reason for the darkness. You would matter so much that the sky itself would go out around you—

*Lior.*

A voice. Small, human, cracked at the edges, speaking from beyond the circle as though from another world entirely.

*Lior Iversen. Beast-speaker. Ledger-keeper. Daughter of Marta Iversen, who baked bread on Tallow Street and told you the animals talked to you because you listened better than most people, not because you were cursed.*

Isolde. Standing at the western point with the cipher-book open, reading aloud the true names Sera had helped her find in the warden records—Lior's name, her mother's name, the small verifiable facts of a small verifiable life, spoken in the flat clear voice of a princess who had learned that being right cost everything and said things anyway.

*You are not the hunger,* Isolde said. *You are the girl who refused a commission because it was wrong. You are the woman who fed the messenger crows out of her own ration during the blackout. You are the person who walked onto a killing floor because the beasts trusted her more than they trusted fear. I am telling you who you are, and I am telling you now, and you will hear me.*

The hunger convulsed around her. *She is nothing,* it said, using Lior's own throat, her own voice, and hearing her own voice say those words was like swallowing glass. *She is a ledger-line. She is small.*

"She is *specific*," Lior said—or thought, or screamed, all three at once—and she seized the specificity like a rope thrown across a chasm. Her name. Her mother's bread. The fox. The goose, absurd and furious, honking defiance somewhere behind the broken doors. Every small true thing Isolde named was a hook, and she hooked herself to them, one after another, while the Regent tried to pour her out of herself.

And then she did what she had come to do.

Because here was the secret the burnings had been trying to erase for three hundred years, the reason beast-speakers died and their gift died with them: a voice that could speak to beasts could speak to *bindings*, because bindings were only a kind of harness, and harnesses were only a kind of bridle, and bridles were only a kind of command—and the Regent was, at bottom, the oldest harnessed thing in the kingdom. It was a creature in a collar that had forgotten it wore a collar. And Lior, standing inside its own throat, wearing its own voice, could reach the clasp.

*I unbind you,* she said, in the Regent's voice, in the voice it had used to advise the first king, in the voice of its own original binding-rite reversed word by word. *I unbind you from the crown. I unbind you from the host. I unbind you from the hunger you mistook for yourself. Go home to the dark that made you, and be nothing, and be free.*

The Regent shrieked.

It shrieked through Aldric's mouth and through the walls and through the mouths of the hollowed court staggering in the corridors, and it *let go* of her—it had to, because the unbinding required the host released before the binder released, that was the grammar of the rite—and Lior fell back into her own body like a diver breaking the surface, gasping, weeping, alive.

But the rite was not finished. The words had to be completed from within, and she was no longer within.

For one terrible heartbeat the Regent reared up inside the king's stolen body, wounded, furious, *knowing*—and Lior saw it understand, in the last instant of its long patient existence, exactly what she had done and why it had never happened in three hundred years of burnings. The beast-speakers hadn't been destroyed because they were dangerous to the kingdom.

They had been destroyed because they were dangerous to *it*.

It lunged for her across the circle—

—and a thousand voices answered her call at once.

Not her doing. Or not only hers. The rats came up through the throne room floor like a black tide; the crows poured through the shattered skylight; the palace goose led a charge of stable cats and one very confused deer that had wandered down from the hills; and every one of them hit the Regent's circle at the same moment, not attacking it—they could not touch it—but *surrounding* it, a living ring of small warm specific lives, each one a name, each one a fact, each one a refutation of the hunger's great reasonable emptiness.

Inside that ring, the Regent began to unravel.

It came apart the way a scream comes apart—first the fury, then the sound, then the meaning, then the echo. Aldric's body collapsed forward off the throne and Sera caught him, grunting, swearing, lowering him gently as the last of the shadow poured out of his eyes and mouth and ears like smoke from a snuffed candle. The smoke screamed. It screamed in every voice it had ever eaten, kings and saints and small forgotten people, and then it screamed in no voice at all, and then the dark above the skylight shuddered—

—and one star came back.

Just one. Low in the east, trembling, as if unsure the invitation still stood. Then two. Then a handful, scattered like spilled salt, and the city below the palace hill began to make a sound Lior had never heard a city make: ten thousand people seeing stars return, all at once, all crying out together.

Lior lay on the chalk-dusted floor of the ruined throne room with a rat curled against her cheek and a crow prepping her hair and the taste of someone else's hunger fading from her tongue, and she laughed, or sobbed, or both.

Isolde knelt beside her father. He was breathing. His eyes opened—faded blue, clouded, *his own*—and he looked at his daughter without recognition and without terror, only a vast exhausted confusion, like a man waking from a fever of ten years.

"Isolde?" he rasped. "You're... tall."

Isolde made a sound that was half laugh and half wound, and pressed her forehead to his hand.

Sera sat back against the dais, one hand still pressed to her ribs, and looked at Lior across the ruined circle. "Rite complete," she said hoarsely. "Host surviving. Binder... dispersed." She exhaled, long and shaking. "The Conclave would have given us all medals, if the Conclave existed. Instead you get me saying *well done*, which frankly is worth more."

Lior started to answer—and stopped.

Because the rat against her cheek had gone rigid, its whiskers trembling, and it was passing her something it had heard in the walls below, something passed paw to paw from the cells beneath the palace where the guards had locked a man three days ago and forgotten to feed him:

Guildmaster Corvane's voice, calm and pleasant, speaking to a jailer:

*"Tell whoever ends up wearing the crown that the Vellum Guild holds certain documents of interest. And tell them Corvane negotiates."*

Lior closed her eyes. The stars were coming back. The king was breathing. And somewhere beneath her feet, the man who had sold them all was already writing his next invoice.

She opened her eyes, and stood, and went to tell the others.
