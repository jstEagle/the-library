## 20. The King in the Ruin

They moved him to the queen's old solar, because the throne room was a ruin and the royal bedchamber smelled of ten years of something that no soap would ever wash away. Isolde had argued for neither. She had argued for nothing at all—had stood in the corridor outside the infirmary with her arms wrapped around herself while Sera Vane bled through bandages on a cot nearby and Lior Iversen sat very still in a chair, staring at nothing, still wearing the echo of another voice inside her own mouth—and when the physicians asked where the king should be taken, she heard herself say, "the solar," as if she had been consulted all her life.

The solar was where her mother used to press flowers. That was the only reason. Isolde did not examine it further.

For three days, Aldric slept.

The physicians came and went with their tonics and their grave murmurs, and Isolde learned to hate the sound of their soft-soled shoes on stone. They spoke of him in the careful voices people used for the dying or the mad: *remarkable that he survives at all; the body has been lived in by something else; we cannot say what he will be when he wakes.* They said *if* at first, then quietly changed it to *when*, because the heart was stubborn and the lungs were sound, and everything else about him—the hollowed cheeks, the hands like folded paper, the hair gone white as frost down to his ears—was merely evidence of theft.

Ten years. The thing had worn him for ten years, and the court had watched, and the kingdom had watched, and no one—not the wardens, not the priests, not one of the hundred gilded faces that bowed each morning in the throne room—had seen fit to notice that the king behind the eyes was gone.

*You noticed,* whispered a part of her, the part that had carried her accusation across a decade of exile like a stone in her shoe. *You were nine years old and you noticed, and they sent you away for it.*

She had thought, all these years, that vindication would taste like triumph. It tasted like ash and copper and the memory of a cipher scratched into wax: *RUN.*

He woke on the third night, near dawn, when the candles had burned to stubs and Isolde had fallen asleep in the chair beside the bed with her cheek against its hard armrest. What woke her was not a sound but a silence—a change in the rhythm of his breathing, the particular quality of quiet that meant someone was awake and listening.

She lifted her head. Her neck protested. Across the room, gray light was just beginning to touch the window where her mother's flower press still sat on the sill, empty now, its felt liners stained brown with a decade of forgotten petals.

"Aldric?" she said. Then, catching herself, "Your Majesty."

His eyes opened. They were blue. She had remembered them as blue, but memory had made them larger and brighter than this—this faded winter-blue, ringed with red, blinking slowly against the candlelight like eyes that had not needed to see for a long time. He looked at the canopy above the bed. He looked at his own hands, turning them over with an expression of mild, exhausted surprise, as though checking whether they still belonged to him.

Then he looked at her, and Isolde's heart stopped, started again, wrong.

Because there was a flicker—just a flicker—in which those eyes went flat and vast and ancient, and she was nine years old again standing in the throne room saying *you are not my father*, and every candle in the palace guttered—

And then it passed. His face crumpled into something human and bewildered, and he said, in a voice rusted from disuse:

"Isolde? You're—" He stopped. Swallowed. Tried again. "You're grown."

"Yes," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. She had rehearsed this moment for ten years in a hundred versions—in the convent dormitory, in Sera's camp, on the road—and in none of them had she managed anything but fury or tears. In the rehearsal she always said something devastating. In the rehearsal he always wept and begged forgiveness and she granted it or withheld it with perfect, terrible grace.

In reality she sat in a chair with a crick in her neck and said, "Yes. I'm grown," like an idiot, and then, because the silence was unbearable: "How much do you remember?"

He was quiet a long time. The light grew. Somewhere below them, the palace was stirring—servants who no longer moved like sleepwalkers but who still did not quite look at one another, a household learning again how to be people.

"I remember the garden," he said finally. "The year the plums did well. I remember—" His brow furrowed. "I remember being very cold. And far away. Like standing behind glass in a dark room, watching someone wear my coat." His hands curled slightly against the blanket. "I could see out sometimes. Through his eyes. It was like—" He stopped again, and Isolde understood that some things did not have words yet, might never have words, and that this man had spent ten years screaming inside a locked room while his mouth praised harvests and signed treaties and smiled at her mother's funeral.

"Do you know what it was?" she asked.

"The Regent." He said the word carefully, tasting it. "It called itself that. It told me, at the beginning—it liked to talk, in the early years—that it was bound to advise the kings of Vellamar. Bound, not to serve. There is a difference, it said, that your grandfather's grandfather should have remembered." A ghost of something crossed his face—not quite a smile. "It was very proud. Even then. Even before it learned to eat."

"It drank the stars."

"It drank whatever it could reach." He looked at his hands again. "Magic goes into a king like rain into a field. Coronation oaths, ward-oaths, the fealty of a thousand small sworn things. I never understood how much of me was a conduit until something else held the spigot." His voice frayed. He pressed his lips together, and when he continued, it was lower. "Isolde. The last clear day I remember—you were eight. You had a fever, and you wouldn't sleep, and I sat up with you reading the history of Queen Maren and you kept asking me if the dragon was real. Do you remember?"

Her throat closed. "No," she lied, because yes, yes, she remembered everything, she had built her whole exile on that night like a keystone.

"After that there's—" He gestured vaguely, helplessly, at himself, at the ruined decade. "Patches. Watching. The feeling of my own mouth moving without me. And then, three nights ago, a girl's voice speaking words I didn't understand, and it was like a key turning in a lock I'd forgotten I had." He turned his head on the pillow and looked at her fully, and his eyes were wet. "That was you."

"That was Lior," Isolde said. "I only spoke her name."

"She let it wear her," he said. "I saw. From inside. For a moment there were two of us in the dark room, and then it was screaming and coming apart, and the door was open, and I walked out." A shudder ran through him, head to foot. "Ten years, and I walk out through a stranger's sacrifice."

"You'll want to thank her, then," Isolde said, and was appalled to hear how cold it came out. "When you're stronger. She's downstairs. Or nearby. She doesn't stay anywhere long."

Aldric studied her. Ten years stolen, and still, apparently, enough father left to read a daughter's face.

"You're angry with me," he said.

"No," she said.

"Isolde."

"*No.*" The word cracked out of her louder than she intended, and somewhere in the corridor a guard shifted. She lowered her voice to a fierce whisper. "You don't get to be gentle with me right now. You don't get to be reasonable. I spent ten years hating you for a thing that wasn't your fault, do you understand that? I built my entire life on hating you, and it was *correct*, it was the truest thing I knew, and it didn't matter, because the man I hated wasn't even *there*—"

"Isolde—"

"And I can't give it back!" Her voice broke at last, all her rehearsed composure gone to splinters. "There's nowhere to put it. I accused you. Publicly. At fourteen. I called you a monster in front of the court and they dragged me to a convent and I *let them think I was mad* rather than admit maybe I was wrong, and I wasn't wrong, I was right, I was right about everything except the only thing that mattered, which is that my father didn't abandon me—he was *eaten*, and I ran, I ran for ten years instead of staying to fight, and Sera almost died, and Lior—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Lior let a god-shaped hunger pour itself into her skull so that I could have my kingdom back. And I hid in a convent collecting documents."

The room was silent. The gray light had become gold. Down in the courtyard, a rooster crowed—the first one in months, the birdsong returning to the city in patches, uncertain, like a memory coming back.

Aldric pushed himself up against the headboard. It took him three attempts, and Isolde found herself rising without deciding to, steadying his elbow, and he looked at her hand on his arm with an expression she couldn't name, and she snatched the hand back.

"I'm not asking for forgiveness," he said quietly.

"Good. Because I haven't decided if there's any."

"I'm telling you what I remember of the fever-night." He drew a breath. "You asked if the dragon was real. And I said, *what do you think?* And you said, *I think stories lie.*" His mouth twitched. "You were eight years old and you already believed that the truth and the official version were different animals. I remember thinking: the gods help whoever rules after her. I remember thinking it with love. I need you to know that whatever else was stolen, that night was mine. They can't have it. It was mine."

Isolde stared at him. Something in her chest had come loose and was bleeding freely, and she could not stop it and did not entirely want to.

"That's not fair," she whispered.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Grief rarely is. Neither is ten years." He reached out—slowly, telegraphing it, giving her every chance to pull away—and touched the back of her hand where it gripped the blanket's edge. She did not pull away. She did not turn her hand over either. It was not forgiveness. It was, perhaps, the ground forgiveness might someday be planted in, if either of them lived long enough and was brave enough to tend it.

"Beginning," he said, as if he'd read the thought. "Not repair. Some things come back from the fire changed. We'll learn what we've become." He settled back against the pillows, suddenly, hugely tired, the effort of three minutes of conversation visibly costing him what it once would have cost him nothing. "Now. Tell me the state of my kingdom. All of it. Don't soften it—I wore a parasite for a decade, I imagine I can bear arithmetic."

So she told him. The wards fallen and the border open. Sera wounded holding the line in the throne room. Corvane in custody, his ledgers seized—or half-seized, the Guild's clerks already burning what they could reach. The regency council squabbling in the absence of any authority, three factions deep by the second day, because power abhors a vacuum and fills it with committees. The people in the streets, afraid of the dark that had eaten their stars and unsure whom to thank for their return.

And the law. She told him about the law, too—the old statutes, still on the books, still unspeakably alive: that beast-speech was witchcraft, that witches were burned, that the woman who had saved the crown and the kingdom both was technically guilty of a capital crime and everyone in the palace knew it and no one knew what to do about it.

At that, Aldric was silent for a long moment.

"They burned such folk in my grandfather's time," he said at last. "I remember the smoke from the walls when I was a boy. I thought it was rubbish fires." His jaw tightened. "And the woman who unmade the thing that ate me is one signature away from the pyre."

"There are those who want to pardon her," Isolde said. "Quietly. Make it go away. And there are those—" she thought of Lior's face in the infirmary corridor, that terrible stillness— "who want to make an example of her. Celebrate her. Put her on coins. She refuses both."

"Of course she does." Something moved behind his eyes—weariness, and under it, a spark of the man he must have been before. "What does she want?"

"To be left alone to plant a garden," Isolde said. "And for the law to change so that no child ever has to hide what she is again. Not a pardon. An amendment."

Aldric laughed. It was a dry, cracked sound, rusty as his voice, and it startled them both.

"An amendment," he repeated. "From the kingdom that burned them. Yes. Yes, that's the harder thing, isn't it. Anyone can grant mercy. Amending yourself costs more." He was quiet again, and when he spoke next, his voice had changed—flatter, more resolved, the voice of a king despite everything. "Then I have work to do before anyone puts a crown near me. Bring me the statute rolls. And bring the council in—no, not the council. Bring the archive. If we're going to remake the law, we start from the originals, not from whatever the last century's favorites wrote in the margins."

"You're planning to rule," Isolde said. It came out half question, half accusation.

Aldric looked at her for a long moment. Then he shook his head, slowly, and the gesture cost him, and he made it anyway.

"No," he said. "I'm planning to leave."

* * *

The abdication happened eleven days later, in the same shattered throne room where the Regent had held court, because Aldric insisted. He wanted the witnesses to see the place. He wanted the scorched stones and the broken dais and the great doors still off their hinges to be part of the record.

He came down the aisle leaning on a cane, dressed not in regalia but in plain black wool, and the assembled court—what remained of it, the survivors and the opportunists and the genuinely grieving—fell silent in a way the Regent's presence had never commanded. The Regent had demanded silence. This silence was given freely, and it was heavier.

Isolde stood at the base of the dais. She had refused a seat among the nobility. Lior stood near the wall with Sera, whose arm was in a sling and whose scowl dared anyone to comment on it. Guildmaster Corvane was absent—chained in a cell, awaiting trial, his ledgers stacked in evidence chests taller than a man. Someone had told Isolde that when the guards came for him, he had asked only one question: *what were the margins?*

The herald read the proclamation, and then Aldric raised a hand and the herald stopped, because the king wished to speak for himself.

"I was not present," he said, and his voice, though thin, carried in the ruined hall, "for the last ten years of my reign. Something wore me, and fed on you, and I cannot give you back the stars it drank or the years it stole. Justice demands I say plainly: the kingdom watched and did not see. So did I, in the only way left to me. We were eaten together." He paused. "A king who can be replaced by a parasite for a decade without his kingdom knowing is a warning, not an institution. I will not be the institution that failed you twice."

He looked at Isolde then, in front of everyone, and she fought the urge to look away.

"Therefore I abdicate. Not in favor of a successor crowned in haste over ruins—but in favor of a regency. My daughter, Isolde, will hold the realm until such time as the estates and the people judge who should sit the throne, if any should." He descended one step, painfully, so that he stood nearly level with her, and his voice dropped to something meant for the whole hall to hear anyway. "I name her regent not because she is my blood, but because she was the only person in this kingdom who looked at the thing wearing my face and said, aloud, *that is not my father*. May we all learn from her."

The hall erupted—not in cheers, not at first, but in that stunned murmur that precedes either acclamation or riot. Isolde stood frozen. Ten years ago, speaking truth had cost her everything. Now the same truth, spoken by another mouth, was handing her a kingdom.

She climbed the broken dais steps to stand beside him, because refusing publicly would shame him, and whatever else lay between them, she would not add humiliation to his pile.

"The seal," Aldric murmured, holding out the signet he had worn since before her birth—worn by the Regent too, she realized, and she took it anyway, because someone had to.

Their fingers brushed. Neither of them flinched.

"Beginnings," he said quietly, only for her.

"Not repairs," she answered.

Later—much later, after the court had emptied and the light had gone amber and long across the broken floor—she found him alone at the edge of the hall, looking up at the hole in the roof where the last starlight had failed.

"What will you do?" she asked. "Truly."

He was quiet a while. When he answered, he did not look at her.

"There's a house in the country," he said. "Your mother's. The garden's gone wild. Plums, probably, if anything survived the dark." He turned, and in the fading light he looked less like a king than like an old man at the end of a long war, which was, Isolde supposed, exactly what he was. "I thought I might learn to grow things. It seems a reasonable occupation for a man who spent ten years watching something consume." A pause. "You could visit. Not as regent. As my daughter. If the regent can spare the daughter."

Isolde looked at him—at the ruin of him, at the stubborn living kernel of him, at the father who had existed all along behind the glass, waiting for a key.

"I'll write first," she said. "Before I come. So you know to expect me."

"That," said Aldric, former king of Vellamar, "sounds like a beginning."

Behind them, unseen in the doorway, a messenger waited with dispatches from the west—and among them, sealed in black wax that no guild and no government had issued, a letter addressed in a hand Isolde did not recognize, bearing only two words:

*To the Regent.*
