## 21. Justice Outside the Law

The summons came on vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, sealed with the regent's sigil in wax the color of dawn: a rose and a thorn crossed beneath an open crown.

Lior held it up to the window of her rented room above the chandler's shop on Tallow Row and read it for the third time. *By order of Her Highness Isolde, Regent of Vellamark and Protector of the Realm, Lior Iversen is summoned to appear before the Court of Restoration, there to be honored for services rendered to crown and kingdom.*

*Honored.* The word sat strangely on her tongue, like a coin she'd found in the street—bright, but she kept wondering whose pocket it had fallen out of.

Below her window, the city was rebuilding itself into something almost recognizable. Carpenters' hammers rang from the ward-tower district, where scaffolding climbed the ruined tower like ivy. The night markets had reopened. And three weeks ago, on the first clear evening since the Night Without Stars, the sky had given them back their stars one by one—not all at once, as they'd been taken, but grudgingly, like a miser counting out coins. The oldest constellations had returned first. The new ones—the ones the star-charts had recorded only since Aldric's reign began its long silence—had not come back at all. The astronomers said some stars had been eaten past any point of return. Lior thought of the fox dying in the guildhouse gutter, speaking her name with its last breath, and understood that some debts were simply never repaid. You could only stop adding to them.

She set down the summons and looked at the other paper waiting beside it on the table. This one was not fine vellum. It was cheap stock, the kind used for broadsheets and warnings, and it bore no seal at all—only a printed line in blunt black type:

*NOTICE TO ALL PERSONS OF THE BEAST-GIFT: In accordance with the Edict of Ashes (Old Law, 3rd Aldric), persons exhibiting speech with beasts are required to present themselves for assessment at the Temple of the Flame within thirty days. Failure to comply will be treated as heresy against the realm.*

The old law. Still breathing. Still hungry.

Isolde's regency had been in power barely two months, and already the petitions were stacking up on her desk like snowdrifts. Lior knew because Isolde told her, in the flat voice she'd learned from her father's ledgers rather than her father's warmth. Half the council wanted the Edict of Ashes repealed; the other half wanted it enforced *more strictly now*, arguing that if a beast-speaker could unbind a spirit that had eaten a king, then beast-speakers were weapons, and weapons belonged to the state or nowhere. There had even been a proposal—circulated quietly, never signed—to establish a *royal corps* of gifted speakers, collared and licensed, their voices conscripted for the crown's use.

Honor on one hand. Fire on the other. And underneath both, the same grasping logic: *your gift is useful, therefore your gift is ours.*

Lior folded both papers together and put them in the fire.

* * *

The Court of Restoration convened in the great hall of the palace, which still smelled faintly of ash no matter how many times the floors were scrubbed. Sera Vane walked beside Lior through the doors, moving stiffly, her left arm bound in a sling that the healers said she'd wear until midwinter. The wound the Regent's hollowed courtiers had given her ran deep, but Sera wore the sling like a decoration, chin high, warden's braid—restored to her by decree—hanging silver at her shoulder.

"They're going to try to give you a medal," Sera murmured as they crossed the hall. "And a title. Probably a house."

"I don't want a house."

"You want what you want, which has always been nothing, which is exactly why they want to give you everything." Sera's mouth twitched. "It confuses them. Power understands hunger. It doesn't know what to do with someone who isn't hungry."

The hall was full. Councillors in their chain of office, temple delegates in flame-orange, guild factors in sober gray, and behind them rows of ordinary citizens who had been let in for the first time in living memory—Isolde's doing, one of her first acts as regent. *If the court speaks for the kingdom,* she'd declared, *the kingdom will hear it speak.* The benches creaked under the weight of watching eyes.

At the center of the dais, on a plain wooden chair rather than the throne—that throne had been ordered broken up for kindling, and Isolde had personally fed the first plank to the fire—sat the Regent of Vellamark. Isolde wore mourning gray trimmed in white, her hair braided back severely, her hands folded on a ledger open before her. She looked, Lior thought, less like a princess than like an auditor. Which, these days, was perhaps the most royal thing a ruler could be.

King Aldric was not present. He lived now in the river house at the edge of the palace gardens, tended by physicians and by his daughter's daily visits, an old man in a young man's body, ten years stolen and never to be returned. Some days he knew her. Some days he asked after a wife who had died while he slept inside his own stolen skin. Isolde never spoke of those days to anyone but Sera, and Sera told no one at all.

"Come forward, Lior Iversen," Isolde said, and her voice carried the way her father's once had—trained for halls like this, though hers had a harder edge, honed in a convent where no one had wanted to hear her.

Lior walked the length of the hall. She felt every eye like a physical touch. For twenty-six years she had built her whole life around being unseen—the bent posture, the ledger-keeper's shuffle, the careful dullness of her face. Now there was nowhere to hide, and she discovered, walking that long floor, that she did not want to hide. That was the strange inheritance of the throne room, of the circle, of the Regent's hunger pouring through her own throat: she had stood inside the thing that devoured everything, and she had come out still herself. What was a crowd of strangers compared to that?

"Lior Iversen," Isolde read from the summons, "for services rendered to crown and kingdom beyond all measure, the Court of Restoration offers you the thanks of the realm, a grant of lands in the northern valleys, and the title of Voice Royal, First of Her Name."

A rustle went through the hall. *Voice Royal.* A new title, minted for her alone. Somewhere in the temple delegation, a man in orange went rigid.

Lior stopped at the foot of the dais. She did not kneel.

"Your Highness honors me," she said, and her voice did not shake, though her hands wanted to. "I refuse."

The silence that followed had texture. She heard it the way she heard everything now—with more than her ears. The mice in the walls went still. The ravens on the roof-slates tilted their heads.

"Refuse," Isolde repeated. Not angry. Careful. They had spoken of this, obliquely, in the garden, among the roses that were already climbing the ruin of the binding-stone. But speaking of a thing and doing it before a full court were different animals, and Isolde knew animals.

"The land grant would make me a landholder," Lior said clearly, "and landholders keep quiet. The title would make me an officer of the crown, and officers speak with the crown's voice, not their own." She turned, slowly, to face the hall—all those watching faces, the orange of the temple, the gray of the Guild. "Two months ago, a spirit wearing your king's face offered me my heart's desire if I would walk away. It offered each of us exactly what we already wanted. That is how it fed for ten years. Not by force. By *gifts*." She looked back at Isolde. "I will not accept gifts from thrones. Mine or anyone's."

"Then what," said a voice from the temple delegation—a thin, dry voice, Abbot Ferran, who had signed the notice currently sitting in ashes on Lior's hearth—"does the woman propose? That the realm simply forget its debts to her?"

"No, Abbot." Lior met his eyes. "That the realm remember its debts to everyone else."

She lifted the satchel from her shoulder and drew out the ledger.

Not Corvane's ledgers—those were locked in the Guild vault, and Corvane guarded them like his own heartbeat. This was her own book: the Ledger of Small Debts, the one she had kept since her first day in the counting house, recording every impossible trade, every quiet cruelty, every payment routed through the Vellum Guild's clean white pages into dirty dark places. Ten years of entries in her small, precise hand. Including the ones she had copied out by lantern light while Sera kept watch at the door—including the payments routed to the palace itself. *Maintenance of the crown.*

"With the Regent's permission," she said—and the phrase was deliberate, a blade slipped between old law and new—"I would enter testimony before this court. Not to be honored. To be *heard*."

Isolde studied her for a long moment. Then the Regent of Vellamark closed her own ledger, folded her hands, and said, "The court hears testimony."

* * *

She spoke for two hours.

She spoke of the bottled storm sold to a southern lord who used it to drown a rival's harvest. Of the year of luck bought by a merchant and paid for, per the fine print, by the unluck of the seller's family—a debt collected in fevers and broken bones across three generations. Of the ward-lattice itself, the great woven spell that had shielded Vellamark's borders for four hundred years, sold by the Guild to an anonymous buyer in the spring of Aldric's tenth year, with Guildmaster Corvane's personal countersignature on the bill of sale. She named prices. She named dates. She named names.

The hall did not breathe. The temple delegation sat very straight and very still, because the Edict of Ashes had burned beast-speakers for six generations on the grounds that their voices were unclean—and here was one of the unclean, standing in the sight of gods and court, doing more justice in two hours than the flame had done in two hundred years.

When she reached the entries concerning the crown itself—the sums paid year upon year for "maintenance," the quiet purchases of silence, the servants dismissed and the physicians bribed and the princess exiled to a convent for asking the wrong question aloud—her voice finally did shake, but she did not stop. Isolde sat unmoving on her plain chair, and only Lior, who had spent a lifetime reading the small languages, saw what the cost of that stillness was: the whiteness of the knuckles, the breath held and released, held and released, like a woman swimming underwater.

"And the Guildmaster?" someone called from the gray benches. "Where is Corvane in all this?"

"Corvane," Lior said, "sold your borders, profited from your famine years, and brokered the feeding of your king. And when the wards fell, he planned to sell the crisis too—to make the Guild indispensable to whatever wore the crown next. He believes power is a ledger with better margins." She let that sit in the air. "He is half right. It is a ledger. But margins are not the only column. There is also the column marked *owed*. And Corvane owes."

"The Guild's books are sealed by charter," said the Guild's advocate, rising, smooth as oil. "Charter-law predates this regency. No audit may—"

"Then the charter is complicit," Lior said, "and should stand trial with him."

Uproar. The advocate's voice rose over it, citing precedent, invoking the Old Law, and then—Lior heard it before the words fully formed, heard it in the shift of the room's attention, in the sudden alertness of the mice—Abbot Ferran was on his feet, finger extended, voice cracking with righteous fire:

"—and moreover, this *testimony itself* is tainted! The woman is a speaker of beasts, anathema under the Edict of Ashes, and by the Old Law she stands in this hall under sentence already! We honor her today and burn her tomorrow—is this the regency's justice? Pick and choose which laws bind and which do not?"

The uproar cut off. Every face turned toward the dais.

Isolde did not rise. That was her genius, Lior thought—she governed from a seated position, like a person balancing accounts.

"The Abbot raises a fair question," Isolde said. "Which laws bind, and which do not. So let us answer it plainly." She opened her ledger again. "The Edict of Ashes was passed in the third year of Aldric's reign, by a council of which the historical record shows eleven members—four of whom, we now know from the ledgers just entered into evidence, were in the Guild's pay. The Edict was not law. It was *inventory*. A method of controlling supply." She turned a page. "However. I am not prepared to govern by burning the past's laws wholesale, because kingdoms that burn their laws tend next to burn each other. So the Edict of Ashes will be neither repealed nor enforced. It will be *replaced*—by a statute written in this hall, in the hearing of the commons, with the commons' consent. And the first clause of that statute will be this: that no gift shall be grounds for burning, collaring, or conscription, and that testimony freely given shall outweigh any seal, charter, or crown."

She looked down at Lior.

"That is the honor I offer instead, Lior Iversen. Not a title. A law with your name in its foundations. Will you take it?"

Lior thought of the fox in the gutter. Of the rats beneath the floorboards, gossiping their small gossip, ignored by everyone who mattered. Of a thousand minds opening to hers in the dark and nearly swallowing her whole. Of a thorned rose growing over a binding-stone.

"Yes," she said. "That I'll take."

* * *

Corvane surrendered his ledgers nine days later, and it was not the regency that made him do it.

It was arithmetic.

Lior was there when it happened—in the Guild's counting house, where it had all begun, standing at her old desk with its familiar ink-stain shaped like a running fox. Corvane sat across from her, immaculate as ever, his gray robes pressed, his rings catching the lamplight. Between them lay the final page of the Regent's recovered records—the pages Sera had pulled from the palace archive, the ones the spirit had kept on its own assets.

"You've read it," Corvane said. It was not a question. His voice was level, but Lior had spent ten years learning the small sounds under people's words, and she heard the hairline fracture in his.

"I've read it. Line forty-one. *Asset: V. Corvane. Status: expendable upon completion of consumption phase.*" She slid the page toward him. "You were merchandise, Guildmaster. From the beginning. The Regent didn't buy the ward-lattice from you. It *recruited* you. And it wrote off its investment the moment the stars were gone."

Corvane looked at the page for a long time. Outside, the city hammered and sang and rebuilt. Inside, the counting house was as silent as a tomb full of money.

"In my experience," he said at last, "every ledger balances. Every debt comes due. I have made a life of being on the collecting side of that equation." His ringed fingers drummed once on the table. "I find I dislike, intensely, appearing in the liability column."

"So surrender the books."

"To the crown." His eyes came up, sharp again, the old Corvane surfacing like a pike. "An open audit, yes—but *I* will deliver them publicly, framed as my own act of conscience, my service to the restoration. I will testify. I will name my partners in the ward-sale, several of whom sit on the regency's council, and the regency will owe me for the heads I hand them. Do you imagine I intend to be *ruined*, girl? I intend to be *indispensable*. Again."

"You intend," Lior said, "to survive by the same methods that fed the Regent."

"I intend to survive," Corvane said simply. "It is the oldest trade there is."

Lior looked at him—at the jailer who had sheltered her, the merchant who had sold a kingdom, the man who genuinely could not conceive of a ledger without himself on the winning side of it. And she felt, unexpectedly, not hatred but a kind of tired recognition. He was what happened when a person made themselves invisible to everything except profit. She had made herself invisible out of fear. He had done it out of appetite. The Regent had been made of nothing else.

"The books go to open audit," she said. "Public reading. Every entry, read aloud in the market square, the way I read yours in the hall. Not sealed archives. Not traded favors. If the commons can't hear it, it isn't justice—it's just a quieter corruption."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. Then Corvane smiled—the Guildmaster's smile, the one that had welcomed her into this building a lifetime ago—and inclined his head a fraction, a merchant conceding terms.

"Read aloud in the square," he agreed. "Very well. But you'll do the reading, Iversen. Your voice carries. It always has." He rose, gathered his rings about him, and paused at the door. "Do you know the difference between us? You spent your life hiding because you feared what you were. I spent mine hiding because I knew precisely what I was, and found the market for it excellent." The door opened onto the noise of the rebuilding city. "One of us, at least, has learned to stop hiding. I'm told it's very bracing."

He left. Lior sat alone in the counting house, at the desk with the fox-shaped stain, and listened to the building around her—the mice in the walls going about their small essential business, the pigeons under the eaves, the old house-rat with the torn ear who had gossiped beneath these floorboards for years and who now, when she listened, fell respectfully silent, the way people did near something they had decided to trust.

Outside, in the market square, carpenters were already raising the reader's platform.

* * *

They read the ledgers aloud for eleven days.

Sera took the first watch, standing armed at the platform's edge in her restored warden's braid, daring anyone to object. Isolde attended in plain clothes, unannounced, standing in the crowd like any citizen—which caused a sensation on the first day and became, by the third, simply part of the ritual. Priests came, and stayed, and some of them wept. Factors came to protest and left pale. On the sixth day, three councillors resigned before their names were reached. On the eighth, the Temple of the Flame formally petitioned to have the Edict of Ashes struck from canon entirely, its abbot declaring before the crowd that "the fire was never god's; it was the Guild's, and we were its bellows."

And every day, Lior stood at the platform and read.

Her voice held. It held through the bottled storms and the purchased famines, through the lists of the silenced and the exiled, through the long terrible columns of *maintenance of the crown*. It held on the eleventh day, when she read the final entry—the Regent's own accounting, the page that listed kings and guildmasters and whole constellations as line items—and closed the book and said to the assembled city:

"This is what happens when power keeps its books in the dark. So we will keep ours in the light. All of them. Forever. That is the whole of the law, and it is enough."

The cheer that went up shook pigeons from the rooftops, and the ravens circled twice overhead, and far below the flagstones, in the warm dark, the rats of Vellamark carried the sound down into the earth like a seed.

That night, Isolde found her on the steps of the platform, watching the workmen strike the scaffold.

"They'll want to put your face on the new coinage," the Regent said, sitting down beside her without ceremony. "I give it a week."

"Tell them I've already been minted," Lior said. "Small copper. Very common. Found in gutters."

Isolde laughed—a real laugh, rusty from disuse, and Lior treasured it the way she treasured all rare recoveries. Then the Regent grew quiet, looking out at the city lights, at the stars coming back overhead in their grudging, partial way.

"There's a delegation arriving Thursday," Isolde said at last. "From the eastern convents. Sisters of the Thorn—they've heard what happened at the binding-stone, and they've heard the new statute." She glanced sideways. "They're bringing children, Lior. Gifted ones. Ones the temples were going to burn, before Thursday's news traveled. They're asking—" Isolde hesitated, and Lior realized the Regent of Vellamark was nervous. "They're asking whether you would teach them. Their voices. How to hold them, and be held by them."

Lior looked up at the sky, at the returning stars, at the black gaps where eaten lights would never shine again. She thought of a garden not yet planted, on ground where a tower had fallen. She thought of small hidden children, making themselves small, and a world that had just been shown—by ledger, by testimony, by law—that smallness was a lie the powerful told to stay comfortable.

"Tell them," Lior said, "that the garden opens in spring."
