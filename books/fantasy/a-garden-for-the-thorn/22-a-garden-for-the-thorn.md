## 22. A Garden for the Thorn

The ground where the ward-tower had stood was still wrong in the way old wounds are wrong—knitted closed, but never quite forgetting. Lior felt it through her boots every morning when she walked the boundary of the ruin: a low, sour hum in the stone beneath the soil, the memory of a binding that had held for three hundred years and then, in one screaming night, had not.

She had chosen this place deliberately. Everyone said she was mad to build anything on it. The city council offered her land in the terraced gardens above the Lantern Quarter, soft loam and southern light, a view of the harbor. She declined with the same polite firmness she had once used to refuse dangerous commissions across a counting-house desk. *This* ground, she told them. The broken ground. Because things that break in the dark should be mended in full daylight, or not at all.

So the garden grew out of rubble.

They had hauled away the tower's fallen stones through the winter—Sera, still walking with a cane and refusing all sympathy, directing the cart-drivers with the crisp authority of a woman who had once commanded border garrisons and saw no reason to stop commanding cart-drivers. Isolde sent prisoners from the palace work-gangs, volunteers all, men and women who had been hollowed by the Regent's court and wanted, more than freedom, something useful to do with their hands. And Lior asked the moles what lay buried where, and the crows which corners of the ruin the frost reached first, and between the three of them—the warden, the regent-in-waiting, and the beast-speaker—they cleared a scar the size of a chapel floor down to honest earth.

The first thing they planted was not a flower. It was a plum tree, because Sera insisted that any garden worth defending ought to produce something you could eat while defending it, and no one argued with Sera about defense. It stood now at the garden's northern corner, bare-armed and stubborn, its roots wrapped around a chunk of the old ward-lattice that no one had been able to dig free. Lior liked to think the tree didn't mind. Trees were patient about being planted on top of history. They did it all the time.

* * *

The children came on Thirdday mornings, as they had come every Thirdday morning since the thaw.

There were nine of them now. In the autumn there had been two: a miller's daughter who could make flour rise from an empty sack if she sang to it, and a chimney-sweep's boy who spoke fluent smoke. Word had traveled the way word always traveled among people who had spent their lives being careful—whispered in market lines, passed hand to hand like contraband, *the beast-speaker takes students, the one who unbound the king, she teaches at the ruined tower, and no one burns there.*

Lior met them at the gate each week and counted them the way she had once counted ledger entries, and every time the number came back larger than she expected, something in her chest performed a small, disorienting maneuver that she had eventually identified, with the help of a very old book on botany, as *hope taking root.*

Today there was a tenth child hovering outside the gate, not coming in.

She was perhaps seven, small enough that the winter coat she wore made her roughly spherical, and she stood with her back pressed against the gatepost as though the post were the last solid thing in a dissolving world. Her eyes went everywhere except to Lior's face. Lior knew that look. She had worn it for thirty years, behind ledgers and lowered eyes and the careful architecture of invisibility. It was the look of someone who had learned that being seen was the first step toward being burned.

"You're the new one," Lior said, not approaching, keeping her voice at the register she used for frightened foxes and skittish horses—low, unhurried, interested without demanding.

The girl shook her head violently.

"All right," Lior said. "You don't have to be. You can just stand there. The gate doesn't require anything of you." She turned back to the others, who were gathered around the cold-frame beds where the early lettuces were coming up, and began the morning's lesson as though nothing else were happening—which was, she had found, the only way anything important ever happened for children like these.

"Who can tell me what a voice is *for*?"

"To talk!" shouted Tam, the chimney-sweep's boy, who answered everything loudly, having spent years learning that loudness was safer than the truth.

"To talk, yes. Anything else?"

Maren, the miller's daughter, raised her hand halfway, the way children do when they suspect the answer might get them in trouble. "To... ask things? Of things that aren't people?"

"To ask things of things that aren't people," Lior agreed. "And here is the secret nobody told us, so I'm going to tell you now, and I want you to hold onto it even when people say otherwise." She knelt in the dirt so she was below their eyeline, looking up at them—a trick she'd learned from the rats, who understood better than anyone that the ones who seem smallest are often paying the closest attention. "A voice isn't a curse. A voice is a door. And doors aren't good or evil. They're just doors. What matters is what you open them for."

She put her fingers to her lips and whistled—not a tune, but the rising triple note that meant *attention, friends, something worth seeing* in the grammar of sparrows. The hedge beside the wall erupted. A dozen sparrows lifted, wheeled once over the children's heads close enough to stir their hair, and settled again into the blackthorn, chattering indignantly about the disturbance.

Tam shrieked with delight. Maren laughed. And at the gate, the spherical girl in the too-big coat took one step inside the garden, then another, her eyes fixed not on Lior but on the sparrows, with an expression Lior recognized from mirrors: hunger and terror in equal measure, the longing to touch the fire and the certainty that touching it would end you.

"What's your name?" Lior asked her quietly, when the lesson had dissolved into controlled chaos of seed-scattering.

The girl looked at the ground. "Pell," she whispered. Then, after a long moment, in a voice even smaller: "I hear the worms."

Lior crouched. "Do you now."

"They're always talking. Under everything. Papa says I make it up. Mama crossed herself and cried." Pell's chin trembled. "Am I bad?"

Lior thought of a counting house, years ago, and a girl balancing impossible accounts while rats gossiped under the floorboards, and how she would have given anything—anything—for one person to kneel down and tell her the truth.

"The worms," Lior said, "are the most honest creatures in this entire kingdom. They turn death into gardens. If they've chosen to talk to you, that tells me they think you're a gardener." She held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer it to a bird you want to land. "Come see what we're planting."

* * *

At midday, when the children had gone home trailing dirt and sparrow-feathers, Lior walked to the center of the garden, where the binding-stone sat.

They had unearthed it in the second month of digging: a flat slab of grey granite, wide as a table, carved all over with the old binding-script that Sera could read and Lior could feel—a texture under her fingertips like braille written in frost. This had been the heart of the ward-tower, the anchor-stone of the lattice, the place where some long-dead warden had bound the Regent the first time, before greed unbound it, before it crawled into a young king's ear and began to drink.

Isolde had wanted it moved to the palace, displayed in the great hall as a monument. *Let them see what was done,* she'd argued, with the fierce, exhausted righteousness of a woman who had spent ten years collecting proof that no one read. Sera had wanted it sunk in the harbor. Lior had wanted neither, and in the end hers was the deciding voice, because the kingdom had developed a habit, lately, of listening to the beast-speaker on matters of hunger and binding.

"It stays," Lior had said. "But it stays *under*. We plant over it. We grow something through it. The stone remembers so we don't have to—but we'll cover it in living things, so that what it remembers feeds something green."

Now the rose grew there.

It had come up wild, self-sown, in the first spring rain—no cutting, no planting, simply *there* one morning, a whip-thin shoot rising straight out of the seam between two of the binding-script's characters, as though the stone itself had decided to sprout. Lior had watched it all season with the wariness of her trade. Plants grown from cursed ground sometimes came up strange; she had asked the roses' roots what they tasted, expecting rot or bitterness.

*Sweet,* the roots had said. *Sweet, and underneath, like all deep things, a little sad.*

By midsummer it was a thicket, and by autumn it was a rose unlike any in the royal catalogs: blooms the color of iron left out in rain, petals edged in a thorn-red so dark it was nearly black, and canes armored in thorns that ran from half an inch to, near the base, nearly two—thorns like talons, like hooks, like the barbs of a harp string snapped mid-note. It was, everyone agreed, magnificent. It was also, everyone agreed, slightly frightening. You could not approach the binding-stone anymore without negotiating with the plant. You could not remember what had happened here without being scratched.

Lior sat on the stone's edge—it was warm in the noon sun, granite holding the day's heat—and let her fingers rest among the thorns without touching them, the way you rest a hand near a sleeping animal you respect.

People had started calling it the Thorn Rose. Pilgrims came, sometimes, though Lior discouraged pilgrimage; she had seen what crowds did to gifts, how quickly wonder curdled into demand. But they came anyway, and they touched the iron-colored petals, and they told their children: *this grew where the stars came back.*

Because that was the other thing about this ground. On the night the Regent unraveled—screaming out of Lior's own throat, out of the circle of its own binding, into whatever dark waits for things that were never meant to be unmade—the stars had returned. All of them, at once, in a flood, like a held breath finally released. Half the city had been in the streets. Isolde had said later that she'd looked up and seen the constellations and thought, absurdly, *Father used to name those for me*, and had cried in front of four hundred strangers, which for Isolde constituted a complete emotional collapse.

The stars hadn't all come home to the sky, exactly. Some of them, the beasts said, had gone *elsewhere*—back into the land itself, into the wards' old channels, into roots and rivers and the marrow of living things. The kingdom was slowly relearning what it felt like to be full. The border wards were rebuilding themselves, thread by thread, and the animals along the frontier reported—through Lior, through the network of speakers she was training—that the silence was lifting. Birds had begun singing at the marches again. Last month, a wolf had brought her a message dictated by an elk, which was the sort of sentence that would have gotten her burned five years ago and now merely required a follow-up visit.

Lior sat with the rose and did the accounting she did every day now, the only ledger she kept anymore. Not debts. Not prices. Just names.

*Sera,* who limped the wall-path every evening at dusk, checking the sightlines out of habit, and who had begun, terrifyingly, to smile at things that were not tactical problems. *Isolde,* who ruled as regent with her father's old counselors and her own new spine, and who visited the garden on rest-days and weeded in silence beside Lior, two women who had once trusted no one, trusting each other completely now, which had turned out to be less like falling and more like setting down something heavy. *Aldric,* who lived in a small house by the sea and wrote letters to his daughter that began awkwardly and ended honestly, which was more than most reconciliations ever achieve. *Corvane,* who had surrendered his ledgers to open audit with the face of a man swallowing glass, and who now managed the Guild under a charter he despised, and who had sent, last week, a crate of rare bulbs to the garden with a note reading only: *Consider it margin.* Lior had planted the bulbs. She was not made of stone.

And *Pell,* added just this morning, newest line in the book. Ten students. Eleven, if the girl came back next Thirdday. Lior intended to be at the gate early, just in case.

* * *

She stayed until the light went long and gold, doing the ordinary holy work of watering.

That was the thing no one warned you about, she thought, moving down the rows with the can—about surviving something enormous. Afterward, life does not announce itself as a triumph. It announces itself as lettuce. As weeds that need pulling. As a child's question asked for the hundredth time. The world does not give you a coronation; it gives you a Tuesday, and dares you to find the miracle in it.

She found it anyway. She found it in the way Tam had stopped flinching at loud noises. In the way Maren's mother had come to the gate last week and, instead of dragging her daughter home, had asked—in a whisper, ashamed—whether the gift could be taught to *her*, too, and Lior had said yes before the woman finished asking. In the way the worms under the cold-frames had started greeting Pell by name, or what passed for a worm's name, which was mostly vibration and enthusiasm.

The sun dropped toward the rooftops. The sparrows went to bed in the blackthorn, complaining. Somewhere beyond the walls, the rebuilt border wards hummed their low, healthy note, and the stars—those that had come home—began to arrive one by one in the deepening blue, punctual as debtors, bright as forgiveness.

Lior set down the watering can and stood a moment at the gate, looking back at the garden: the plum tree in its stubborn corner, the lettuce rows, the iron-and-shadow rose standing guard over the stone it had conquered. Beautiful and sharp. Like everything worth keeping.

She was visible now. Anyone in the city could point her out—*that's her, that's the beast-speaker, that's the one who let it wear her so she could unmake it from inside*—and for thirty years that sentence would have been a death warrant, and now it was just a sentence, spoken in markets, fading into the ordinary noise of a kingdom learning to live with its scars.

It did not destroy her. That was the whole of it, the lesson under all the lessons, simple as water: being seen had not destroyed her. Hiding had been the slow kind of dying, and the fast kind had missed her by a thorn's breadth, and here she stood in the ruin of both, alive, watering lettuces.

She locked the gate against the dark, out of habit rather than need—the hedge kept better watch than any lock now—and started up the lane toward the rooms she kept above the old counting house, which she had never sold, because some debts you keep paying in memory.

Behind her, in the settling dusk, the Thorn Rose opened one last bloom to the returning stars.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the binding-stone hummed—not the sour note of old damage, but something new. Something almost like a question.

Lior paused at the corner of the lane, ears pricked the way they'd been pricked her whole life, and listened.

The stone was speaking. And for the first time in three hundred years, it was not reciting a binding.

It was asking for one.
