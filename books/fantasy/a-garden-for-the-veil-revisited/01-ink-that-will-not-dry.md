## 1. Ink That Will Not Dry

The trouble with copying wards, Odile had learned over eleven years in the scriptorium, was that they wanted to work.

Not the way a sword wanted to swing or a bell wanted to ring—wards were quieter than that, more like bread dough that wanted to rise. You wrote them out in silver-ink on good linen paper, and if you did it right, they sat there humming to themselves, patient as a banked fire, waiting for someone to hang them over a door and let them do their one small job of keeping harm outside.

Odile copied four hundred wards a week, and every single one of them hummed at him.

He had developed, over the years, a private taxonomy of the feeling. The simple threshold wards—the ones farmers bought for three coppers to keep weasels out of henhouses—hummed like a drowsy bee. The fever-breaks the infirmary ordered felt like standing near an open window in summer. And the great marriage-blessings, commissioned by families with more coin than sense, which took him two full days each and required gold leaf: those sang. Low and wordless, somewhere below hearing, but singing all the same.

Which was impossible. That was the thing nobody knew but him. Inside the Veil, magic did not work. That was the entire point of the valley, the reason it existed, the reason his mother's people had settled here two hundred years ago when the rest of the world decided that gifted folk were either tax revenue or kindling. The Veil was a promise written into the land itself: *no power crosses here.* Gifted children born inside the valley grew up ordinary. Wards hung over doors were decoration, sentiment, a pretty tradition from the old country. The temple sold them because pilgrims liked them, not because they functioned.

And yet.

Odile set down his pen, glanced at the scriptorium door—it was latched; it was always latched after compline—and picked up the ward he'd finished that afternoon. A fever-break for the infirmary, Sister Anselm's monthly order. He held it flat on his palm.

The warmth came up through the paper like sunlight through a wall.

He closed his hand around it and let it soak into his skin, where it dissolved into nothing, harmless, spent. Then he turned the page of his practice ledger—the little book bound in plain grey board that lived under a loose floorboard beneath his cot—and recorded, in his smallest hand:

*Fever-break no. 311. Held 4 seconds before dissolution. Warmth noted. No ill effect.*

Eleven years of entries. Eleven years of a secret so large he sometimes forgot its shape, the way a man forgets the weight of his own head.

Because here was the other impossibility, the one underneath the first: the magic didn't just work inside the Veil. It worked *for him only*. He had tested this with the thoroughness of a boy who understood that being wrong would mean being dead. When he was nine, he'd convinced Pip—a scribe-orphan like himself, all elbows and conspiracies—to hold a ward while Odile watched. Nothing. Pip had held the paper to his ear, heard nothing, declared it "dead as the Abbess's dancing," and gone back to stealing plums. But in Odile's hands, the same ward warmed and whispered and, once, when he was frightened enough, *answered*.

He had never told anyone. Not the Abbess, who was kind but answerable to the crown. Not the other orphans, who were his family in every way that mattered and therefore the people he could least afford to endanger. Not even old Brother Calloway, who taught the copying and was too deaf to hear confessions anyway, which made him the safest confessor in the valley.

Odile blew out his candle, lay down on his cot, and did what he did every night: he practiced.

Small things. Things that couldn't be noticed, couldn't be traced, couldn't matter. He warmed his own hands in winter by holding a blank scrap of ward-paper. He coaxed a stubborn ink-pot open when the lid swelled with damp. Once—only once, and never repeated—he had made a dying moth's wing glow faintly, briefly, and had cried afterward, though he couldn't have said why.

Tonight he took a fresh scrap and tried the newest trick, the one he'd been working at for a month: making the ink on a finished page hold still. Copyists' ink smudged; it was the bane of the trade. If he could learn to keep a line wet until it dried exactly when he wanted—

The ink in his well lifted, all of it, a quarter inch, and hung there in a trembling black bead.

Odile sat up so fast he cracked his head on the sloped ceiling of his cell.

"*Down*," he whispered, and the ink dropped back with a plop, splashing his knuckles.

He sat in the dark, heart hammering, sucking ink off his fingers, and laughed—one shaky breath of a laugh, there and gone. It was getting stronger. Or he was getting careless. Either way, the ledger needed a new entry, and the ledger needed a new rule: *nothing above the wrist-height of fear.*

He was reaching for the floorboard when the bell began to ring.

Not the compline bell—that had rung hours ago. This was the gate bell, the deep one, rung only for official visitors, and it went on and on, nine strokes, ten, urgent as a fist on a door.

Below, in the dormitory wing, he heard the orphans stirring, Pip's voice carrying up the stairwell: "Whose funeral is it, then?"

---

It was a rider. One man, mud to the hip, holding a lantern that threw his shadow huge against the gatehouse stones. The Abbess met him in the courtyard in her shawl, and Odile watched from the cloister arch with half the household gathered behind him, a rustle of nightshirts and speculation.

The rider handed down a scroll sealed with green wax. Green wax. Odile's stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn-over, because everyone in the valley knew what green wax meant: the crown's business, and none of it gentle.

The Abbess read it by lantern light. Her face did something very small and very controlled, which from the Abbess—who could watch a novice drop an entire tray of communion cups without blinking—was the equivalent of a scream.

"Thank you, courier," she said. "There is straw in the stable and bread in the kitchen. You'll ride on at dawn."

Then she turned to the assembled household, and her voice was perfectly level, perfectly warm, and perfectly wrong.

"It is late. Everyone to bed."

Nobody moved. Pip, beside Odile now, whispered, "What does it say?"

"You heard the Abbess," said Sister Anselm, herding. "Bed."

But Odile had spent eleven years learning to read what people hid, because hiding was his trade and his religion both, and he saw the way the Abbess's thumb pressed white against the scroll's edge. He saw her eyes flick up—once, quick as a fish rising—to find him in the crowd at the arch.

She found him. She looked away.

Odile went to bed. He lay on his cot and listened to the valley sounds: wind in the cedars, the infirmary's dog dreaming, Pip snoring through the wall like a sawmill with ambitions. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. The sounds of a life built carefully, plank by plank, over a cellar full of secrets.

*All persons possessing the gift,* the proclamation surely said, or words to that effect. He didn't need to read it. Everyone knew the crown had been tightening for years—registrations in the lowland provinces, hunts in the hill country, the word *witch-hunter* passing through markets the way bad weather passes, talked about and hoped past. The Veil had always been the exception. The Veil was supposed to be the exception. That was the covenant: you hide us, we stay harmless.

*We stayed harmless,* Odile thought, staring at the ceiling. *I'm the one who didn't.*

Because whatever the proclamation demanded, it would come to the scriptorium eventually. Registrations meant lists. Lists meant ledgers. Ledgers meant names, and names meant signatures, and signatures meant—him. The temple's best copyist, the one who handled all the wards, the one whose wards *worked*, whose ink floated, who was a walking violation of the oldest law in the valley.

He got up, quietly, and pulled the loose floorboard. The grey ledger sat in its nest of dust. He didn't open it. He just looked at it, this record of eleven years of small warmths and floating ink, this diary of a crime he hadn't known he was committing.

Then he heard footsteps on the stair, soft and unhurried, and the Abbess's knock—two raps, pause, one rap—against his door.

He put the board back. He opened the door.

She came in without invitation, which she never did, and stood in the middle of his narrow cell holding a candle, looking older than she had at the gate. On the table between them, uninvited and unnoticed by her, the ink in his well trembled—just the surface, just a shimmer, like a puddle when a cart passes far away.

The Abbess looked at the ink.

She looked at Odile.

"Oh, child," she said, very quietly, and the gentleness in it was worse than any accusation. "How long?"

Odile's mouth was dry. Every answer he had ever prepared for this moment—all the lies, the careful deflections, the eleven years of armor—was suddenly worthless, because she had seen the ink move, and the ink did not lie.

"The wards," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "They work. For me. Only for me."

"I know." She set the candle down. "I've known since you were twelve, when you mended Sister Anselm's fever-breaks faster than any copyist alive. I told myself it was talent." She smiled, and it was a tired smile. "I wanted it to be talent."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because saying it makes it real, and real things can be taken." She picked the candle back up. At the door she paused, and did not turn around. "The proclamation requires a census of the household by week's end. Names, ages, trades. And—" the pause stretched, thin as wire— "a sworn statement from the head of house regarding any irregularities among the residents."

"Will you report me?"

Now she turned. In the candlelight her face was unreadable, which meant she was working very hard at it.

"I am going to write that my scriptorium produces the finest wards in three valleys," she said, "and that my senior copyist is merely very, very good at his trade. And then I am going to pray, child, that whoever reads it is a fool." She stepped into the corridor, then stopped again. "Burn the ledger."

The door closed. Odile stood alone in the dark with his heart going like a hammer on a nail.

He knelt. He lifted the floorboard. He took out the grey ledger, eleven years of his life in a hand small enough to hide in a thumbnail, and he carried it to the candle.

The pages caught easily. Too easily. As the fire ate toward the last entries—the moth, the floating ink, tonight's disaster—he watched the light play on the walls of his cell, and thought: *this is what the end of hiding looks like.*

Outside, far off toward the valley's mouth, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind that came down from the pass: hoofbeats. Many of them, riding hard, arriving where no riders should be at this hour.

The last page curled and went to ash.

Odile pinched out the candle and stood listening in the dark, counting the hoofbeats, and understood with terrible clarity that the fools reading the Abbess's fine words were already too late.

They were already here.
