## 2. The Debt Ledger

The Abbess kept her ledgers the way she kept her bees: behind a door that hummed if you touched it wrong.

Odile had been summoned to the counting room at the hour when the scriptorium's candles were trimmed and the younger scribes sent to supper, which meant either he had transcribed a psalm with a smudge in it or something worse. He tried to remember the last page he'd copied. *Ward against ill-wishing, ward against flood, ward against the small hungry spirits of hedgerows.* His hand had been steady all day. Steadier than usual, in fact, because he'd spent the morning pretending very hard to be an ordinary man copying ordinary wards for an ordinary wage, and pretense, done well, looked exactly like calm.

"Sit," said Abbess Verity.

He sat. The counting room smelled of tallow, dried lavender, and old paper—the smell of every safe place he had ever known, because it was the only one he'd ever known. On her desk lay the debt ledger itself: a fat volume bound in green leather gone soft at the corners, its pages ruled in the Abbess's own cramped, merciless hand. Somewhere in that book was his name. It had been there since before he could write it himself.

"You're not in trouble," she said, which was what she always said first, which meant trouble was coming but she wanted him comfortable for the journey.

"That's reassuring," Odile said. "The last time you said that, Brother Anselm had to fish me out of the cistern."

"You were seven." She folded her hands. She was a broad woman, square as a bread loaf, with ink stains up both forearms like a second set of veins. Twenty years she had run this temple's scriptorium, and twenty years she had signed his wages against a debt no one would explain to him. He had stopped asking at fourteen. He was asking now whether he liked it or not.

"The hunters came through the gate this morning," she said.

The word landed in his chest like a stone dropped into a well. He heard it fall and fall and did not hear it land.

"Royal witch-hunters," the Abbess went on, level as a ledger line. "Three of them, gray cloaks, iron badges. They brought a proclamation—all gifted persons within the realm are to present themselves for registration by the feast of Saint Corvin. Registration, they called it. I've read enough proclamations to know what registration becomes when the ink is dry."

"They were here? In the valley?" His voice came out thin. Inside the Veil, magic did not work—that was the whole point of the Veil, the whole mercy of it. Whatever gift he carried had never once functioned past the boundary stone. He was safe here the way a fish is safe in ice. "There's nothing for them to find here."

"Mm," said the Abbess, in the tone of a woman who had watched him scrub soot off the scriptorium floor at odd hours for fifteen years and drawn her own conclusions and filed them under *not my business until it is.*

Odile kept his face still. It was a skill. He'd had practice.

"They didn't stay long," she said. "They asked after provisions, road conditions, the state of the ford. Ordinary questions. And then their captain—Ilves, her name was, a tired woman with a scar like a stitch across her chin—asked one more question at the door. She asked whether we kept any foundlings whose mothers had come to us from outside."

The well inside him went very deep and very quiet.

"And I told her," the Abbess continued, "that we keep foundlings whose mothers bring them to us, same as any temple, and I did not ask where from, because that is not how charity works. She looked at me a long moment. Then she thanked me and left. But Odile—" and here her voice changed, softened into something he hadn't heard since he was small and feverish, "—she described your mother. Without ever saying she was describing anyone. A woman who came over the mountains pregnant, alone, in the year of the bad frost. Who stayed three days in our infirmary and left before dawn with her boots tied with string because hers had fallen apart on the pass."

"I don't remember her," Odile said. It was true and it wasn't. He remembered a warmth at his back and a heartbeat not his own, which was not memory so much as the memory of being made.

"No. You wouldn't." The Abbess opened the green ledger to a ribboned page and turned it toward him. "But you should see this. You're nineteen. Old enough to read your own account."

He read it. The columns marched down the page in her tidy hand: *Board, wet-nurse, cloth, schooling, apprenticeship fees, board, board, board.* Twenty years of a child's life priced out in copper and silver, each entry initialed, each year's total carried forward. At the bottom, the sum remaining. It was smaller than he'd feared and larger than he'd hoped. Nine years of wages, give or take, if he never bought another candle.

"Your mother couldn't pay," the Abbess said quietly. "So the temple paid, and the debt became yours. That's the law of the valley, and I have hated that law every day of the twenty years I've enforced it, and I will tell you so plainly because you deserve plainness: you were sold to us before you could walk, and I have been your creditor since you were in swaddling clothes, and I am sorry."

Odile looked at the ledger for a long time. The strange thing—he noticed it distantly, as if from the far end of the room—was that he felt less afraid than he expected. The debt had been a wall his whole life, and walls are frightening mostly because you can't see them. Now he could see all of it, every stone, priced and initialed.

"Why did she leave?" he asked. "Why not stay?"

"I asked her that myself, those three days. She said—" the Abbess paused, choosing, "—she said the thing she was running from would follow her anywhere except across a boundary. That the valley was the one place it couldn't reach her. And then she left anyway, back over the mountains, and I have wondered for twenty years what kind of fear makes a mother walk away from safety with her boots tied with string."

*The kind that thinks the danger is her,* Odile thought, and did not say it aloud, and pressed his thumb flat against the desk until the thought went quiet.

"There's more," the Abbess said. "I wouldn't have shown you the ledger at all yet, except—the hunters will come back. Ilves asked about you by implication, and her lieutenant asked about you outright. He had your description. Dark hair, left-handed, works the scriptorium. Someone gave them that description, Odile, and it wasn't me."

*Someone.* The word sat between them. The valley was small. The valley had eyes, and lately some of those eyes had been counting who lingered too long near the boundary stone, who burned candles late, who was quiet in the wrong way. He thought of the miller's son, who had seen him once at dusk doing—nothing, really, nothing at all, just standing with his hand open and the air going strange around it—and had smiled at him too widely afterward, ever since.

"What do I do?" he asked. It was not the question he meant. What he meant was *what happens to them*, to the children, to the little ones who called him brother—but the Abbess heard it anyway, because she was the Abbess.

"You do nothing tonight," she said. "You eat supper. You sleep. Tomorrow I'll write to the diocese; there are protections older than this proclamation, and I intend to invoke every one of them. And Odile—" she closed the ledger gently, like shutting a coffin lid, "—whatever you are, whatever you can do that you think I don't know about: this temple has hidden stranger things than you. We hid a relic of Saint Corvin in a soup pot during the border war. You are not alone in this building. Remember that."

He remembered it, in fact, all through supper, sitting at the long table among the noise of the foundling-scribes—Pell arguing theology with his mouth full, little Wren teaching the new girl to fold napkins into birds, Tam sliding him the last of the honey bread without comment because Tam always did. He remembered it while he washed ink pots. He remembered it while he banked the scriptorium fire.

Which is why it nearly undid him when they ambushed him in the dormitory corridor after lights-out, all six of them, arranged in a semicircle like a tribunal of very short judges, Pell holding a candle and a piece of paper.

"We've done sums," Pell announced.

"It's late," Odile said. "Go to bed."

"We've done *sums*," Pell repeated, with the gravity of a man announcing a siege. He thrust the paper at Odile. By candlelight Odile read it: six names, six figures, six sets of initials. Wages saved. Coin hidden in boot heels and under floor tiles and, in Wren's case, apparently inside a hollowed-out hymnal, which explained several things. Added together, it came to a number that made his throat close.

"That's most of a year of my contract," he said.

"It's a start," said Tam. "We'll have the rest in four years. Maybe five, if the Abbess lets us take winter copying work."

"Five years," Odile said. "And then I'm free?"

"Then you're free," said Wren, who was nine and missing two teeth and absolutely certain of arithmetic.

He looked at them—at Pell, who had arrived half-starved and now argued doctrine for sport; at Tam, who spoke maybe forty words a day and had just spent five years' worth of hoarded silence on this; at Wren and her hymnal full of coins instead of psalms—and felt something crack in him that was not dread. It was the other thing, the worse thing, the thing that made hiding impossible: he loved them so much it showed.

"You can't spend your futures on my past," he said.

"Too late," said Pell. "It's pooled. There's a jar."

"There's a *jar*?"

"A very secure jar," said Wren.

Odile laughed, and it came out wet, and he sat down on the corridor bench because his legs had opinions. They crowded around him, six warm bodies smelling of candle smoke and cheap soap, and for a moment the corridor was the safest place in the world.

Then Tam—who heard everything, who missed nothing—said quietly, "The hunters asked about you today. Sister Brigit told me. They had your face written down."

The cold came back through the warm like water through a floorboard.

"Then we'd best be quick about the jar," Odile said, and meant it as a joke, and nobody laughed, because none of them were fools.

Later—much later, when the dormitory breathed in unison and the candle was a blue bead in its dish—Odile lay awake and did the one thing he never let himself do inside the Veil, the thing that shouldn't be possible here, the secret under all his secrets.

He opened his hand above the blanket.

And the dark leaned toward him, gentle as a cat, the way it always did—magic moving toward him like water finding a hole, alive and eager in a valley where magic was supposed to be dead. Null-born, whatever that meant. A hole shaped like a boy. His mother had run from something that would follow her anywhere except across a boundary, and she had left him on the safe side of it, and now the crown's men had his face written down and were coming back.

In the morning, he decided, he would ask the Abbess about the boundary stone. About what it kept out.

He was beginning to suspect it also kept things in.

* * *

The knock came at the scriptorium door before Prime, three hard raps, and Sister Brigit's voice, pitched low and fast: "Odile. They're at the gate. The gray cloaks—they're asking for you by name."
