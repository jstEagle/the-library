## 3. What the Hunters Bring

The road into the Veil smelled of rain and old stone, and Captain Sera Ilves hated it.

Not the smell—she'd grown up in a fishing town where the harbor announced itself three miles before you saw it, and she had fond memories of smells that told the truth. No, what she hated was the way her own magic went quiet as they crossed the boundary stone, like a lantern snuffed behind a shutter. Twenty years a hunt-captain and she still flinched at it, that sudden hush, like going deaf in one ear. The other hunters complained of it too, though they pretended not to. Only her lieutenant, Corvas, seemed to relish it. He rode through the Veil's border the way other men walked into church: spine straight, eyes hungry, as if silence were a kind of applause.

"Temple's over the next rise," he said, consulting the surveyor's map without really looking at it. "Household of thirty-one. Abbess, scribes, orphans, servants. And the gardener."

"And the gardener," Sera agreed, in a voice she kept carefully flat.

She hadn't slept well. That was nothing new; she hadn't slept well since the proclamation came down from the capital with its wax seal fat as a tick: *All persons gifted by the uncanny arts shall present themselves for registration. Unregistered gifts shall be presumed hostile.* But last night she had sat up with the list of names until the candle drowned in its own wax, and one name had been sitting on the page like a stone in a shoe.

*Head gardener, Temple of the Veil: Vessa Thorn.*

Sera knew that name. She knew the woman who wore it, too, though the file said otherwise. Twelve years ago, in another valley, in another life, she had stood in a doorway while smoke curled off the rafters and a young woman with burned fingers held a pail of water that froze solid in her hands because she was frightened and frightened people's gifts ran wild. Sera had been twenty-six and newly made captain, and she had looked at that girl—Vessa, though the name in her ears then had been different—and written *deceased* in her report.

It was the first lie she'd ever put in an official document. She'd told herself it would be the last. It was neither.

"Captain?" Corvas was watching her. He had a way of watching that made you feel like a door left ajar in winter. "You were smiling."

"Was I? Must have been thinking about my supper."

"You never think about your supper."

"That," said Sera, nudging her horse up the rise, "is because you've eaten my supper twice this month, and I've learned to stop torturing myself."

He laughed at that, easy and warm, and she hated herself a little for how much she liked him. That was the trouble with Corvas. He was clever, he was loyal-looking, and he laughed at her jokes like he meant them, which made him either the best subordinate she'd ever had or the worst thing that could happen to a woman planning to falsify a royal report. She genuinely did not know which.

The temple came into view as the road crested: gray walls softened by ivy, laundry snapping on lines, a bell tower with one bell instead of two because the second had cracked forty years ago and nobody with money had ever cared enough about the valley to replace it. Smoke rose from the kitchen chimney in a lazy ribbon. Somewhere out of sight, children were shouting about something with the pure, unearned joy of people who had not yet read a royal proclamation.

Sera reined in at the gate and looked at all that ordinary, stubborn goodness, and felt the report in her saddlebag grow heavy as a millstone.

* * *

The Abbess received them in a parlor that smelled of ink and dried lavender, and she received them the way stones receive weather: patiently, with the expectation of outlasting them. She was a small, square woman named Abbess Ondine, with ink stains on three of her fingers and the fourth finger of her right hand bent at an angle that suggested a life spent arguing with heavy doors.

"The crown honors the temple," she said, when Corvas had finished reciting the proclamation in his official voice. "And what does the crown want with us?"

"Registration of gifted persons," Corvas said. "Per the king's edict. All members of the household will be questioned. Any found gifted will be taken to the capital for assessment."

"For assessment." The Abbess turned the phrase over like a coin she suspected was clipped. "And if the assessment finds them gifted indeed?"

"Then they'll be registered. Collared, if warranted." He said it smoothly. Too smoothly. Sera had noticed that about him lately; the smoothness had been arriving in layers, like paint over a wall she couldn't quite see anymore.

"And if they run?"

"Then they're presumed hostile," Corvas said, "and the presumption is generally fatal."

There was a silence. In it, Sera heard the bell tower's single bell tolling noon somewhere above them, unhurried, as if the world were still a place where bells could take their time.

"I see," said the Abbess. "Well. You'll want to speak with everyone. I'll have the household assembled in the refectory. It will take some little while; we are thirty-one souls, and souls do not line up quickly." Her eyes flicked to Sera—just once, just briefly—with something in them that was not fear and was not pleading but was perhaps an invoice. *You know what I am asking,* that look said. *I know what I am asking.*

Sera looked back at her with what she hoped conveyed: *I am going to try, madam, and God help us both if my lieutenant is better at his job than I am at mine.*

"Captain Ilves will conduct the interviews personally," Sera heard herself say. "Standard procedure. Senior officer takes primary testimony."

Corvas's eyebrows rose a fraction. "That's not standard procedure."

"It is today," said Sera, "because I said so."

* * *

Thirty-one souls, it turned out, lined up faster than advertised, because the temple orphans had apparently decided that being interviewed by a royal hunter was the most interesting thing to happen since the cracked bell, and had formed their queue with the discipline of veterans.

Sera interviewed them one at a time in the scriptorium, at a desk littered with half-copied ward-pages, and asked each the same gentle questions, and wrote down answers she already knew were useless. No, they had never seen anyone work magic. No, nothing strange had ever happened here. One small boy with flour on his collar informed her gravely that Brother Tam could whistle two notes at once, and wasn't that basically magic, and Sera wrote it down solemnly and said she'd flag it for review, and the boy went away so pleased with himself that she had to press her lips together until he was gone.

It was going beautifully. It was going suspiciously beautifully. And then the head gardener came through the door, and Sera's heart performed a maneuver she had not authorized.

Twelve years changes a face, but not the eyes. Vessa Thorn—or whatever her true name was now, the one buried under twelve years of soil and sun—stood in the doorway in a canvas apron with dirt on the hem, gray threading her dark braid, hands rough and clean. She looked at Sera and did not flinch, did not blink, gave away absolutely nothing, and Sera thought: *you always were the braver of us.*

"Sit, please," Sera said, and closed the door.

They regarded each other across the cluttered desk like two chess players who had both forgotten how the game ended.

"You don't remember me," Sera said quietly.

"No," said Vessa Thorn.

"Good. Keep it that way." Sera pulled the questionnaire toward her, dipped her pen, and began writing before any question was asked. *Subject cooperative. No gift observed. Household appears unremarkable.* "In a moment I'm going to ask you your name, your age, and your duties, and you're going to answer them like a bored woman who wants to get back to her cabbages. Then you're going to leave through that door and tell no one—not the Abbess, not the children, no one—that anything passed between us except tedium. Do you understand me?"

Vessa's jaw tightened. "Why?"

"Because twelve years ago a captain wrote 'deceased' next to your name instead of the truth, and she'd very much like to keep doing that." Sera didn't look up. "Name?"

A long pause. Then, flatly: "Vessa Thorn. Forty-one years of age. Head gardener, Temple of the Veil, fourteen years' service."

"Any unusual occurrences on the grounds?"

"No, Captain."

"Thank you. Next."

Vessa rose. At the door she stopped, hand on the latch, and said, without turning, "The roses along the east wall. Someone planted those the year I came. They bloom out of season. Every year. Nobody knows why." A beat. "Might be worth investigating."

And she was gone before Sera could decide whether she'd just been threatened, thanked, or flirted with.

* * *

The problem with lies, Sera reflected that evening over the temple's excellent barley stew, was arithmetic. Thirty-one statements, all bland, all consistent, all *too* consistent—the whole household alibied each other with the seamless polish of people who had rehearsed, or worse, of people who simply loved each other enough to lie in chorus. A good investigator would notice. A good investigator would notice that the temple's records showed a scribe named Odile Vane whose debt ledger had been requested by name from the capital, and whose interview Sera had scheduled for tomorrow morning, and who had somehow developed a cough overnight that required him to be excused.

She had excused him herself. She'd told Corvas the boy was sick. It had even been mostly true—he'd gone pale as paper when she'd read him the proclamation's requirements, and she'd chosen, again, to call a thing by a gentler name than its own.

"You're favoring them," Corvas said.

He said it lightly, over his bowl, in the guest quarters the temple had given them, with the fire crackling like it hadn't a care in the world. Sera kept her spoon moving.

"I'm following procedure."

"You interviewed all thirty-one yourself. You sent me to count chickens."

"You're very good with chickens."

"Captain." He set down his spoon. His voice stayed pleasant; his eyes did not. "The capital asked about the scribe by name. By *name*, Sera. Somebody upstairs cares about that boy specifically, and you've got him marked down as having a cough. When the assessor's office cross-references—and they will—I'd like to know what I'm supposed to write in my supplementary report."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the single bell tolled some hour or other, patient as ever.

"Your supplementary report," Sera said, "will reflect whatever the primary report reflects. That's how reports work, Lieutenant. They agree with each other. It's practically their whole purpose."

"Is it." He picked his spoon back up, smiled his easy smile, and returned to his stew as if they'd been discussing the weather. But Sera had been reading men for twenty years, and she knew the temperature of that smile. It was the smile of a man filing something away.

She lay awake long after, listening to the Veil's deep quiet, and understood with terrible clarity that there were two hunts now. There was the one with her name on the orders, riding out to catch the gifted and drag them to collars and cages. And there was the other one, the quieter one, the one where she was both quarry and pursuer—and Corvas, with his pleasant voice and his supplementary report, was gaining on her by the day.

Tomorrow morning she would interview Odile Vane. Tomorrow morning she would decide, one way or another, whether the boy with the haunted eyes and the borrowed cough was going to be a name in a ledger—or a body she'd have to bury in another lie.

Outside, far off toward the boundary stone, something in the night went silent all at once, the way birds go silent, and did not start again.
