## 4. Crossing the Veil

The bell rang wrong.

Odile had copied the temple's schedule into his bones over eleven years—matins at the grey hour, the kitchen bell a quarter after, the children's lessons when the mist lifted off the orchard. He knew every bell the way he knew his own heartbeat. This one came at noon, out of sequence, and it did not stop.

He set down his pen. The ward he'd been copying sat half-finished on the desk, its ink still wet, and for one absurd moment his first thought was *the ink will smear*—because that was what eleven years of careful invisibility trained you to think. Small catastrophes. Smudged letters. Never the large ones, never the ones you actually feared.

Then he heard the horses.

Not temple horses. Temple horses walked like they were being paid poorly. These came up the valley road at a canter, iron-shod, many of them, and beneath the bell he caught the thin bright thread of a hunting horn.

"Odile." Pell, the youngest of the scribe-orphans, appeared in the doorway with his shirt half-tucked and his eyes very wide. "There are men in the courtyard. A lot of men. They have the Abbess."

*They have the Abbess.* Odile was moving before the sentence finished, down the stairs two at a time, past the dormitory where the little ones slept in bunks he had helped build, past the washroom where Tam's toothbrush cup still sat crooked on the shelf because no one could ever be bothered to straighten it. Eleven years of small domestic holiness. He knew exactly how much of it he was about to lose.

In the courtyard, the Abbess stood on the steps with her hands folded, which was how she stood when she was about to ruin someone's week. Before her, perhaps thirty soldiers in crown grey, and before them a lieutenant with a face like a ledger—everything entered, nothing forgiven.

"—registered by law," the lieutenant was saying. "All gifted persons. You harbor them, madam. We have names."

"You have rumors," said the Abbess. "Rumors I am happy to sell you at market price, which is nothing."

"The gardener," the lieutenant said. "A woman called Brigit Ostrow. And we are given to understand the temple employs others of her kind."

Odile's stomach dropped through the floor, through the foundations, into the cold stone under the valley. Brigit. Who had taught him which mushrooms would lie to you. Who hummed while she worked and had once, drunk on festival wine, told him that she'd known his mother, and then never spoken of it again.

And behind *her* name, unspoken but hanging in the air like smoke: *and who else?*

His feet made the decision before he did. That was the thing about magic inside the Veil—it didn't work when you reached for it, only when you stopped reaching, when some deeper part of you let go. All those years of night practice, of coaxing sparks from candle flames and making ink crawl, had taught him the shape of the letting-go. So he let go.

The world went quiet and silver.

It is difficult to describe what a null-born's magic feels like from the inside, mostly because null-borns spend their whole lives pretending they don't have an inside. But Odile felt it now as a great soft hush spreading outward from his chest, like snowfall, like the moment between lightning and thunder stretched into something kind. It washed across the courtyard. It touched the soldiers' weapons and made them feel suddenly, absurdly pointless. It touched the children pressed against the refectory windows and wrapped around them like a second blanket.

And it touched the lieutenant's suspicion, and it *unraveled* it—not erased, not lied to, just gently pulled apart, thread by thread, until the man blinked and looked around as though he'd forgotten why he'd come.

"We should search the grounds," said one of his sergeants.

"Why?" asked the lieutenant, honestly puzzled.

The silence held for three heartbeats. Four. Odile stood at the edge of the crowd, hands trembling, holding open a spell that had no right to exist inside the Veil, watching grown men forget their purpose in real time.

Then a horse screamed.

Not a warhorse—a temple mule, spooked by the horn or the crowd or simply by the wrongness in the air, and it bolted straight through the soldiers toward the open gate, and the spell Odile was holding snagged on the sudden chaos and tore like wet paper. The hush collapsed. Sound rushed back in: boots, bells, the lieutenant shouting "Seal the gate! No one leaves!"

No one left, in fact, except one scribe-orphan of no particular importance, who had already turned and run for the orchard wall.

* * *

He went over the wall the way he'd gone over it a hundred times as a boy, except this time he didn't come back down on the other side. He ran. Through the apple rows, past the beehives (he felt briefly, irrationally guilty about the bees), along the stream path where he'd taught Pell to skip stones, up and up toward the ridge where the valley ended.

Behind him, horns. Behind him, dogs. Ahead of him, the boundary stone.

Everyone in the valley knew the boundary stone. It was a grey slab taller than a man, older than the temple, older than the debt ledgers, standing in a saddle of the ridge with moss on its north face and lichen like spilled candlewax on its south. Children dared each other to touch it. Farmers spat to the east of it. The Veil began there—or rather, everything beyond it was somewhere the Veil did not reach, which amounted to the same thing.

Odile had lived his entire life within sight of that stone and had never once crossed it. Nobody crossed it. Crossing it meant leaving the valley's protection, and more than that—for him—it meant stepping into a world where magic actually *worked*, where every hedge-witch and court enchanter and half-trained village cunning-man could do openly what he could only do in secret, badly, at night.

Except.

Except his magic had never obeyed the rules. It worked *inside* the Veil, where magic was supposed to fail. Which meant—

Which meant he had no idea what it would do outside.

The dogs were closer. He could hear the riders fanning out below, could hear someone shout that footprints led toward the ridge. His lungs burned. His legs shook. And some cold, clear part of him—the part that copied wards all day, precise and patient—observed that he was going to die or be taken either way if he stopped, so he might as well find out.

He put his hand on the stone.

It was warm. That was the first strange thing. Eleven years of mountain winters and the stone was warm as a sleeping animal, and under his palm he felt—not a pulse, exactly. An attention. Like the valley itself had been holding its breath around him for thirty years and had just noticed he was leaving.

"I'm sorry," Odile said, to the stone, to the valley, to the Abbess and the children and the bees. "I'll come back. Somehow. If I can."

Then he stepped across.

* * *

The world detonated.

There was no gentler word for it. One stride he stood in the grey familiar air of the valley; the next, color and light and *presence* crashed over him like a wave breaking. Every tree blazed. Not metaphorically—each trunk wore a faint corona of green-gold, roots glowing down into the soil like veins of ember. The stream sang in actual audible harmonics. A hawk overhead trailed light from its wingtips. The grass under his boots shimmered with a thousand tiny enchantments—growth-charms laid down by generations of farmers, weather-wards stitched into fence posts, a blessing on a waymarker so old its original language had been dead for centuries.

Magic everywhere. Magic thick enough to swim in. And Odile stood in the middle of it, gasping, waiting for the wave to break over him and find nothing to hold onto, the way it always had.

Instead, the wave *stopped*.

Around him, in a rough circle perhaps ten paces wide, the blazing world went dim. Grass dulled. The stream's song flattened. Enchantments nearest him flickered like candles in a draft—and then, slowly, they began to lean toward him. He could feel it: the old blessing on the waymarker straining in its setting, the growth-charms in the grass pulling rootward, all of it drawn toward him the way water draws toward a drain.

Magic had never followed him before. Magic had spent thirty years ignoring him, failing him, sliding off him like rain off oiled cloth. Now it *leaned*. Now it wanted him.

"Oh," Odile said aloud, to nobody. "Oh, that's not good."

Because he understood, suddenly, what Maren-of-the-stories and Brigit's drunken hints had been trying to tell him. He wasn't gifted despite the Veil. He was the opposite of the Veil. He was a hole in the world's weave, and holes pull.

Somewhere upslope, a raven stopped mid-caw.

That was the second strange thing. Ravens don't stop mid-caw. This one sat on a leaning post and looked at him—not with bird attention, not with the flat greedy interest of something sizing up bread, but with an attention that had *weight*, that pressed on him like deep water. The temperature dropped. His breath fogged. The light of late afternoon thickened and went the color of brass, and in that brass light Odile saw, for just a moment, the shadow of something vast pass over the mountainside—something with too many joints in its neck, something patient, something that had been asleep under the world for a long time and had just felt a drain open ten paces wide in the middle of its tapestry.

*Interesting,* said a voice that was not a voice, in a language that was not language, directly into the marrow of his bones. *What are you?*

Every instinct Odile possessed said: run back. Run back to the stone, back to the valley, back to the grey safe air where things like this couldn't see him. But the stone was forty yards behind him and the dogs were closer than that, and besides—he was done running toward smaller cages.

"I'm nobody," he said, which was the truth he'd built his life on, and which sounded, here, for the first time, like a lie.

The presence laughed. It was not a comforting sound. It was the sound of ice settling on a lake, of a door unlocking after a hundred years.

*Nobody,* it agreed, amused, terrible, almost fond. *Come along, nobody. Winter is coming to ask you questions.*

The brass light snapped back to ordinary gold. The raven flew off. The world resumed being merely, overwhelmingly enchanted—and downslope, faint but gaining, Odile heard the dogs.

He tightened his grip on the strap of his satchel—ink pot, spare nibs, half a loaf of stolen bread, the complete inventory of his new life—and ran east along the ridgeline, toward the darkening pines, toward whatever waited there.

Behind him, the boundary stone stood warm and silent in the dusk, and the valley it guarded held its breath again, and did not know why.
