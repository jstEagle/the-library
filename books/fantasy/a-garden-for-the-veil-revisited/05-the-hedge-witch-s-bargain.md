## 5. The Hedge-Witch's Bargain

The rope caught Odile around the ankles so fast he had no time to scream, which was probably fortunate, because the king's riders were close enough that screaming would have been a death sentence. He hit the forest floor face-first, tasted loam and pine needles, and was hauled—flailing, undignified, thoroughly caught—into the dark beneath a wall of brambles that closed behind him like a curtain.

"Quiet," said a voice above him. A woman's voice, dry as autumn leaves. "They're on the road. Breathe like you're sorry about it."

He breathed like he was very sorry about it indeed.

Through the weave of branches he could see the road: four riders in crown livery, torches guttering in the drizzle, moving at a walk because they knew their quarry was on foot and tired. One of them turned his head toward the treeline, and Odile felt the man's attention pass over him like a cold hand—and then slide away, deflected, as though something between them had politely declined to be seen.

A spell. Someone had cast a hiding-spell over this hollow, and it was working, which meant—

"You're outside the Veil," Odile whispered, when the hoofbeats had faded. "And your magic works."

"And yours doesn't," the woman said, dropping down beside him with the ease of someone who had done it ten thousand times. She was sixty if she was a day, broad-shouldered, gray-haired under a hood gone green with moss, with a face like a root vegetable that had opinions. "Which is the interesting part, isn't it? Because I watched you come off that mountain, boy, and everything you touched went quiet. My wards. The ward-stones along the boundary. The tracking charm those riders are using to follow you." She smiled, and it was not entirely a friendly smile, but there was warmth buried in it somewhere, like a coal under ash. "You're a walking hole in the world's weaving. Do you have any idea what that's worth?"

"No," said Odile honestly. "I mostly know what it costs. I've been hiding it my whole life."

"Mm." She studied him the way she might study a strange fungus—thoroughly, and without flattery. "Well. You can't stay here, and you can't go back, and you're too stupid to survive alone, so I suppose you'd better come inside before you freeze. Name?"

"Odile."

"Maren. Exiled hedge-witch of the Thornhollow coven, currently resident of a root cellar, formerly of better accommodations." She untied his ankles herself, briskly. "Up. Mind the third step; it lies."

* * *

The root cellar, once you got down the lying third step, was not so bad. It was dry, candle-lit, and crammed with the organized chaos of a life lived sideways: bundles of drying herbs hanging from beams, jars of preserves labeled in a crabbed hand, a bedroll, a kettle, three cats who regarded Odile with the flat suspicion of customs officials. The ceiling was low enough that Maren stooped, but Odile could nearly stand.

"Sit," she said, pointing at an upturned barrel. "Talk. Start with why the crown wants you badly enough to send riders into weather like this."

So he told her. He told her about the Veil, the valley where magic crossed nothing and no one—the temple, the scriptorium, the debt inherited from a mother he barely remembered, twenty years of copying wards by day and practicing small impossible magics by night, magics that should not have worked inside the Veil and did anyway. He told her about the proclamation, the registration, the hunters, the lieutenant kicking open the scriptorium door, the children huddled behind him. He told her how he had thrown up his hands and *pushed*, and every ward in the room had gone dark as snuffed candles, and how he had run, and how for the first time in his life magic had followed him out instead of failing at the border.

Maren listened while making tea, which he took as a good sign. When he finished she handed him a chipped cup and sat down across from him on a crate of turnips.

"All right," she said. "Here's what you are. There's a word for it, though most witches only say it in whispers, because null-born make us nervous." She leaned forward. "Every spell ever cast is a knot in the world's thread. Witches tie knots. Enchanters tie knots. Gods, if they exist, tie very large knots. And a null-born—" she tapped his knee with one blunt finger "—a null-born can put their fingers in the knots and pull."

Odile stared at his tea. "I don't understand."

"You said your little magics worked inside the Veil. Of course they did. You weren't casting anything. You were *untying*. Inside the Veil, where the world's thread runs bare and unknotted, there was nothing for you to unravel—so you made the smallest knots yourself, tiny ones, and picked them apart again, and it felt like magic because you didn't know any better." She laughed, short and delighted. "Oh, that's beautiful. Twenty years of practicing a gift that isn't a gift at all. It's the opposite of a gift. It's a key that fits every lock."

"The wards in the scriptorium," Odile said slowly. "When I pushed—they didn't break. They just... stopped being warded."

"Untied. Cleanly, from the sound of it, which is more than most null-born manage. Most of you tear." She sipped her own tea. "That's why the king wants you, boy. A thing wearing a crown has spent twenty years tying itself to that throne with the biggest knot ever woven—a binding, a usurpation, a theft stitched shut. And somewhere in its cold black heart it knows there's exactly one kind of person alive who could pick that stitching loose."

The cellar seemed colder suddenly. The cats all looked at him at once, which Odile found deeply unsettling.

"So I'm not running from the hunt," he said. "I'm running *toward* what it wants."

"You're running toward what it wants, yes. Which is why you'll need me." Maren set down her cup. "Because here's the other thing about null-born. Untying is easy. Knowing *which* knot to untie, in what order, without bringing the whole garment down on your head—that takes a witch. A binding around a king, a true one, wound through twenty years and a stolen throne? That's not a knot. That's a net. Cut one wrong thread and it strangles whatever's inside it."

Odile thought of the children behind him in the scriptorium, of Sera Ilves's face when she'd let him go, of the boundary stone and the world blazing open beyond it. He thought about going home, and knew he couldn't—not yet, maybe not ever, not while the thing on the throne sent hunters into valleys.

"I need to reach the winter court," he said.

"I know. Everyone with sense knows, and everyone with sense is staying home." Maren scratched one of the cats under the chin. "It's nine days' ride to the capital, and the passes are closing, and the court is full of things that smell people like you and me. But I know the back ways. Old witch-roads, shepherd tracks, a smuggler's tunnel or two. I can get you there."

"What does it cost?" Odile asked, because the Abbess had taught him early that nothing given freely is free, and Maren looked like a woman who kept accounts.

For a moment she didn't answer. She looked at the jars on her shelves, the herbs, the cats, the whole small kingdom of her exile, and something moved behind her eyes that was older than mischief.

"A price," she said finally. "One I'll name when we're closer. Not because I'm cruel—because right now you'd refuse it, and refuse wrongly, and I haven't the patience to argue you around. Call it a hedge-witch's habit. We never sell a remedy until we've seen the whole sickness." She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "You sleep tonight. Tomorrow we walk. And Odile—"

"Yes?"

"If you untie my wards in your sleep, I will feed you to the cats. Slowly."

He almost smiled. It was the first time in a week he'd wanted to. "Understood."

* * *

He woke once in the night, in the narrow dark of the cellar, to find Maren awake at her worktable, stitching something small and bright—a charm, or a knot, or a promise. Candlelight moved on her face.

"You should sleep," he whispered.

"Hedge-witches sleep in winter," she said, not looking up. "This stopped being winter the day the king called the snow down early. Now hush. I'm deciding how much of you to spend."

Odile lay back down and did not ask what she meant, because some part of him already knew, and the knowing sat in his chest like a stone dropped into a well—one he couldn't hear hit bottom yet.

Outside, far off toward the capital, the first snow of the false winter began to fall.
