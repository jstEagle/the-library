## 6. The King Who Isn't

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Sera Ilves would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day she did the ledgers, and the crown had always treated her like an entry to be balanced.

*Captain Sera Ilves, Hunt-Captain of the Third Veil Company, is commanded to present herself at the winter court within the fortnight, there to give account of her progress in the matter of unregistered gifted persons.*

Give account. She'd read the word three times by candlelight, and each time it sounded less like a summons and more like a summons to a trial. Which, she supposed, it was. Lieutenant Dorn—no. *Captain* Dorn now, promoted in her absence from the temple raid, promoted with a speed that had made her stomach drop when she'd heard of it—had sent his report ahead of hers. She knew what was in it. Not the truth. The truth would have ended her career and probably her neck. But a version of the truth with gaps in it shaped exactly like a gardener named Wren and a household that should have been arrested and wasn't.

So she went to the capital.

The road north took nine days, and she spent them doing what she always did before a reckoning: sharpening things that were already sharp, oiling things that were already oiled, and rehearsing lies until they sat in her mouth as comfortably as her own teeth. She was good at lying. That was the shame of it. Twenty years in the hunt had taught her that the difference between a good officer and a dead one was often just the quality of the falsehoods, and she had survived this long because her falsehoods were excellent.

She only wished she didn't need such a good one now.

* * *

The winter court met in the capital's great hall, a room built to make human beings feel like weather—brief, damp, and beneath notice. Sera had attended court twice before, years ago, for commendations, and remembered it as cold and tedious in equal measure. She had forgotten the smell: tallow, wet wool, the sour sweetness of too many bodies breathing under one roof.

And underneath all of it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, something else. Something mineral.

She noticed it first at the doors, where the chamberlains waved her through without checking her name against any list. Odd. Her name should have been checked. Her name was *in trouble*. But the guards looked through her the way men look through rain, and she walked into the hall with the uneasy feeling of being not welcomed but *permitted*.

The king held audience from a dais at the far end, and the crowd parted for petitioners the way water parts around a stone—without enthusiasm, without choice. Sera found a place along the wall among the minor officers and functionaries, folded her hands, and did what twenty years of hunting had trained her to do better than anything else.

She watched.

The king was listening to a grain merchant plead about the price of winter wheat, and he was doing it with his head tilted at an angle of attentive sympathy that Sera recognized, because she had used it herself, on weeping mothers and trembling boys caught with charms in their pockets. It was a good angle. It said *I hear you. Your suffering matters.*

There was just one problem with it.

He never moved out of it.

Sera told herself she was imagining things. Audiences were long; kings were bored; boredom made people still. She catalogued other explanations the way she'd catalogue evidence at a scene, giving each its fair weight. Fatigue. Discipline. A stiff neck. The particular stillness of a man who has learned that every twitch of his face is read like scripture.

Then the merchant finished, and the king answered him, and Sera stopped cataloguing.

Because the answer was perfect. Warm, measured, gracious, exactly the right length, touching on the merchant's late father (whom the king could not possibly have known), closing with a small joke about wheat that drew a ripple of laughter from the court. And while he delivered it, the king's face did nothing. Not the flicker of an eyelid. Not the shift of a lip around the smile. His mouth shaped the words; his eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance over the merchant's shoulder, wide and dark and shining, like two coins left out in the rain.

People blink, Sera thought. Everyone blinks. She'd once watched a man hold his eyes open through a four-hour interrogation, and by the end of it he was weeping blood and begging to be allowed to close them. Bodies demanded it. Eyes dried. It was not a thing willpower touched.

The king did not blink.

She began counting. She couldn't help it. Somewhere past four hundred heartbeats—she counted heartbeats because counting seconds felt hysterical—the merchant bowed himself away and a nervous young woman came forward with a petition about a mill, and the king turned his head toward her, and Sera saw the turn for what it was.

It wasn't a turn. It was a rotation. Smooth, silent, continuous, like a door on well-oiled hinges. No weight shift. No settling of the shoulders. Men's heads were heavy things; they carried them with little compensations, tilts and leans, a whole grammar of effort written in the neck. The king's head simply rotated, and the rest of him stayed exactly where it was, and the smile stayed exactly where it was, and the eyes—

The eyes swept across the hall as the head turned, and for half a heartbeat they passed over Sera, and she felt it like a hand laid flat against her sternum.

Not looking at her. *Reading* her. The way you'd read a ledger line. Checking a figure.

Then the gaze moved on, and Sera discovered she had been holding her breath, and let it out slowly, and made herself stay exactly where she was, because running was the one thing she must not do.

The smell reached her again. Cold iron. Old cold iron, the kind that comes out of deep wells. She'd smelled it once before, years ago, on a captured witch's charms after they'd been burned—the metal itself hadn't burned, and what rose off it had been this exact smell, iron and something under the iron, something that had no business being in a living court.

Her eyes drifted, as everyone's did, to the table beside the dais, where the king's refreshment waited. A goblet of wine. A plate of small cakes. Court etiquette required the monarch to take refreshment publicly; it was a piece of theater older than the dynasty, a demonstration that the king ate the kingdom's bread and was nourished by it.

In two hours of audience, the king did not touch it.

Other kings sipped, nibbled, gestured with a cake for emphasis. This one sat above his untouched meal like a statue above an offering, and the wine in the goblet caught the light and threw it back red, and nobody—*nobody* in that entire glittering hall seemed to find any of this strange.

That was the part that frightened her most, afterward. Not the king. The court. Hundreds of people, lords and ladies and officers, watching the same performance she was watching, and seeing only a king. She caught herself wondering if she was mad. Then she remembered the guards who hadn't checked her name, and the chamberlain who'd waved her through, and a cold new thought slid into place beside the others:

Maybe they weren't failing to see it. Maybe they weren't entirely *there* to see it.

* * *

"Captain Ilves."

Dorn found her in the officers' gallery afterward, and she had to bite down on the reflexive correction—*it's hunt-captain*—because the title had been his for a month now, and correcting him would be a confession.

"Dorn." She kept her voice pleasant. "Congratulations again. Captain suits you."

"It suits me fine." He came to stand beside her at the rail, close enough to be friendly, angled enough to block her view of the stairs. Dorn had always been a man who blocked exits without noticing he did it. "You gave your account?"

"This morning. To the Lord Registrar."

"And how did it go?"

"Well enough."

"Mm." He picked a bit of lint from his sleeve. He'd bought new clothes for court, good ones, and wore them like a man wearing someone else's skin. "Funny thing. I read your report on the temple. Read it twice. Very thorough on the wards, very thorough on the orphans' registration status. Funny how much space you gave the garden."

"The garden?"

"You wrote near a page on the head gardener. Wren. Her planting rotations. Her *character*." He smiled at the rail. "You never write about anyone's character, Sera. In fifteen years I've never seen you waste ink on whether a suspect was kind to children."

"I wrote what was relevant."

"Did you?" He turned and looked at her then, and his eyes were ordinary eyes, human eyes, tired and ambitious and a little afraid, and Sera understood suddenly that whatever else was wrong with this court, Dorn was still a man, and a man could be managed. "Because here's my thinking. Either you're hiding a spared witch in a garden bed, or you've gone soft. And I've known you fifteen years, so I'm hoping it's soft."

"And if it's neither?"

"Then I'll have to report my doubts." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way you'd tell a friend bad news. "They take disloyalty seriously here, Sera. More seriously than they used to. There are officers up here who'd sell their mothers for a commendation, and the commendations are coming fast lately. Fast and strange." He paused. "You'll be at the feast tonight? The king honors the hunt companies."

"I wouldn't miss it," Sera said.

* * *

She went to the feast because not going was impossible, and she spent it doing arithmetic.

The hall blazed with a hundred candles, and the king presided from the high table, and Sera, seated far enough down to be furniture, watched him not eat. Course after course came up from the kitchens—roast boar, glazed eels, a swan dressed in its own feathers, gold leaf on everything—and the king lifted his cup twice, to his lips, never past them. Around him, his inner circle ate and drank and laughed, and Sera studied them too, and found the wrongness there as well, subtler, like a painting hung a finger's width crooked. The Lord Chancellor laughed a half-second after every joke, precisely, mechanically, like a clock striking. The Mistress of Robes never once looked at the king's face. Nobody at the high table touched anybody. In six hours, in a crowded, drunken, elbow-to-elbow feast, no one at the high table brushed another's sleeve.

She slipped out during the ninth course, on the pretext of the privy, and no one stopped her, and no one followed, and that too was wrong. She was a suspected officer in a paranoid court. Someone should have been counting her steps.

Unless, she thought, walking fast through the cold corridors, they already knew everything. Unless the reason no one watched her was that she was already inside the web, and flies don't get watched by the web.

Her rooms were in the guest wing, third floor, and she let herself in and stood a moment in the dark with her heart going hard, and asked herself the question she'd been avoiding since the dais:

*What do you do?*

Report it? To whom? Dorn, who was climbing on the backs of the ambitious? The Registrar, whose seal was on the proclamation that had started all of this? The court, which watched a man-shaped hunger eat nothing and applauded?

Run? With what, and to where, and leaving behind her the knowledge that the thing on the throne was doing something to the realm—she didn't know what yet, but the endless requisitions for grain, the emptied granaries she'd passed on the road north, the villages with their shutters closed in daylight—she knew a hunt when she saw one, even turned inside out. Something was hunting the whole country, quietly, patiently, and calling it governance.

Or fight.

She was fifty-one years old. She had spent thirty of those years doing a job she'd once believed in, hunting people who mostly deserved it and some who hadn't, and the ones who hadn't had names, and she had never spoken most of them aloud, but she knew them. Wren, kneeling in her garden with dirt to the elbows, who had looked at Sera across a hedgerow seven years ago and said nothing, and been spared, and never known why. A boy in the marsh country with a charm against fever. An old woman who could find lost things and had lost everything.

Mercy, Dorn would call it. Softness. Sera had called it the only part of the job worth keeping.

Well. Here, at last, was a quarry worthy of the hunt.

She lit one candle, and while it flared she took stock of her room the way she'd take stock of a cell she meant to leave: window, bars, roof, floor. And then, because she was who she was—a woman who had spent a career learning where powerful people kept the things they couldn't afford to lose—she let herself think about the seal.

Every writ, every warrant, every proclamation of registration and arrest flowed under the royal seal. Whoever held it could command in the king's voice. Could countermand orders. Could, say, void a hunt. Could, say, order the Third Veil Company to stand down, the temples opened, the registries burned.

It lived, according to the old manuals she'd memorized as a cadet, in the king's privy chamber, in a coffer of black oak, attended by the Keeper of the Seal—who was, she'd noted at the feast, one of the laughing ones, one of the *clockwork* ones.

Which meant it might as well have been unguarded.

Sera sat down on the edge of the narrow bed and laughed, once, quietly, at the absurdity of it. Thirty years of service, and it had come to this: planning burglary against the crown she'd served, in her own guest quarters, by candlelight, with a hangover coming.

But her hands were steady. Steadier than they'd been in years. Because for the first time since she'd knelt as a girl and sworn her oath to a king she'd never met, she knew exactly who the enemy was, and it was not a witch, and it was not a hedge-cunning woman, and it was not a scared scribe in a valley full of orphans.

It was the thing that smelled of cold iron and never blinked, sitting on a stolen throne, eating nothing, wanting everything.

* * *

The privy chamber was guarded, of course. Everything was guarded. But the guards outside the king's own door were changed at midnight, and between the changing there was a quarter hour when the corridor belonged to a single sleepy man, and Sera had spent thirty years learning the sleeping habits of men with boring jobs.

She went in through the servant's passage—every palace has one, and every palace pretends it doesn't—and came out behind the arras in the privy chamber with her heart loud in her ears and her hands, thank all the quiet gods, still steady.

The coffer of black oak sat on the writing desk beside a letter tray stacked with correspondence awaiting the seal. Moonlight came through the window in a pale bar. The room smelled of ink and snuffed candles and, faintly, underneath, of cold iron.

On the desk, in the moonlight, lay papers. Requisitions. Granary tallies. Orders moving the winter stores of three provinces into the capital's vaults, into vaults *beneath* the vaults, into places marked on the accompanying diagram with a symbol Sera didn't know and wished to heaven she didn't recognize—a circle, crossed, with a point at its center, drawn over and over in a hand that never varied, never hurried, never hesitated.

A hand that never varied. Like a face that never blinked.

She took the seal.

It was heavier than she expected, warm—warm, though it should have been cold, warm like something recently held—and the wax-ridges of the royal arms pressed into her palm as she wrapped it in a kerchief and stowed it inside her coat. She left, in its place in the coffer, a lead blank of the right size and weight, taken from her own dispatch kit, because a theft discovered tonight would end everything, and a theft discovered in a week might be blamed on anyone.

Then she went back out the way she'd come, past the arras, through the servant's passage, into the cold.

She rode out the west gate an hour before dawn, in plain clothes, with the seal against her ribs and a sword she'd owned for twenty years and a destination she'd chosen in the dark hours: south, to the mountain road, to find a runaway scribe and a hedge-witch exile, because whatever was happening at the winter court, the null-born boy who could unravel spells was either the realm's last hope or its last loose thread, and Sera Ilves had decided, somewhere between the dais and the coffer, that she was done deciding which people got to live.

Behind her, the capital slept under its hundred candles, and on the throne the king sat smiling in the dark, and did not blink, and did not eat, and began, very quietly, to look for his seal.
