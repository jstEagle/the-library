## 7. Three Against Winter

The mountain road taught Odile things the temple never had: how to sleep sitting up against a boulder, how to tell weather by the ache in his teeth, and how much of his life had been spent being quiet for nothing.

Maren walked ahead of him with her staff thumping a steady rhythm, her gray braid swinging like a pendulum counting down to something. She had not told him what she wanted in exchange for guiding him to the winter court. Every time he asked, she said, "You'll know when you know," which was the sort of answer temple elders gave right before assigning extra penance.

"You're limping," she said now, without turning around.

"I'm walking."

"You're walking *badly*. There's a difference. Sit."

They had stopped at a shrine—just a leaning stone with a bowl of rainwater and someone's old offering of bread gone hard as slate. Maren set her pack down and began rummaging through it with the focused violence of a woman who always knew exactly where everything was.

Odile sat on the stone and flexed his ankle. Three days out from the Veil's boundary, and he still caught himself waiting for his magic to gutter out. It didn't. It hummed under his skin like a second pulse, louder here than it had ever been inside the valley, where it should have been silent at all and wasn't. Null-born, Maren called it. A person born able to touch spells instead of cast them—or rather, able to do both, wrong, in a way that made hedge-witches go pale and offer root cellar hospitality.

"Here." Maren thrust a twist of cloth at him. Inside were three dried plums and something that smelled like pine tar. "Chew the bark. It's for the swelling."

"It tastes like a temple floor."

"Then you'll feel at home." She lowered herself onto her own pack with a grunt. "Eat. We make the pass tomorrow, and I want your feet working."

He ate. The wind came down off the peaks carrying snow that wasn't falling yet but was thinking about it. Odile pulled his borrowed coat tighter and let himself, for one unguarded moment, imagine the scriptorium—the smell of ink and lamp oil, Tess arguing with Bram about whose turn it was to trim wicks, little Annet falling asleep over her practice letters again. Were they safe? Had the lieutenant come back? Had Sera Ilves—

The thought arrived with hoofbeats.

Maren heard them too. Her head came up like a hawk's. "Riders," she said. "One horse. Coming fast."

They had perhaps two minutes. The road here ran between a rockfall and a steep drop into pines; there was nowhere to hide and no time to be clever about it. Maren's hand found her staff, and Odile felt the air change as she drew up whatever small protections she carried—a prickling, a pressure, like the moment before a storm.

"Behind me," she said.

"Maren—"

"*Behind me.*"

He went behind her, mostly because arguing would waste the seconds. The rider came around the bend at a canter, and Odile knew the horse before he knew the woman—a big gray gelding with a white scar across its nose, the kind of animal the crown issued to people it expected to survive bad situations.

Captain Sera Ilves reined in ten paces away. She looked worse than she had at the temple raid: thinner, mud-splattered, a fresh cut healing above her eyebrow. Her sword stayed sheathed. Her hand did not stay far from it.

"Hedge-witch," she said to Maren. Then, to Odile, in a voice carefully stripped of everything: "Scribe."

"Captain," Odile said. His heart was doing something embarrassing. "Have you come to arrest me?"

"No."

"That's a relief."

"Don't be relieved yet." She dismounted slowly, showing them both her empty hands, and Odile noticed she moved like someone who had learned recently that sudden movements cost trust. "There's a warrant out for you. And for me, as of four days ago. Treason." She said the word flatly, like naming a weather condition. "Lieutenant Corvas found my report on the temple. He found the gardener I spared eight years ago. He's been promoted for it."

Maren hadn't relaxed. "Why are you here, hunt-captain?"

"Because I stole the king's seal," Sera said, "and I need to give it to someone who can use it, and every road south is crawling with riders wearing my former colors." She reached into her coat—Maren's grip tightened on the staff—and drew out a disc of lead and gold, heavy enough that her wrist dipped with it. The royal seal of Vessany. "And because," she went on, quieter, "I watched that thing sit on the throne and smile at petitioners without ever once blinking, and I decided I would rather die on this mountain than spend one more day pretending I didn't see it."

The wind gusted. Somewhere below them, a crow complained about the weather on all their behalves.

"You could be lying," Maren said.

"I could."

"You could be very good at it. Hunters usually are."

"Yes," Sera agreed. "Usually." She held out the seal anyway, arm steady, eyes on Maren and not on Odile—which he understood, suddenly, as its own kind of courtesy. She was offering herself to the person least likely to forgive her. "I don't expect you to believe me. I'm asking you to test me. You're a witch. Test me."

Silence, except for wind. Odile looked at Sera Ilves—at the woman who had falsified a report to protect a gardener, who had hesitated at the temple gate long enough for children to scatter, who stood now in the cold offering treason with an open hand—and felt something in his chest loosen that had been clenched since the boundary stone.

"Maren," he said. "Let her."

"You don't get a vote, scribe."

"He saved my vote by running," Odile said. "I'd like to spend it."

Maren muttered something that was probably a curse in a dialect better left untranslated. But she stepped forward, took Sera's wrist—not gently—and pressed her thumb hard against the pulse point. Her lips moved. The air went strange and thin, the way it did when magic passed through it, and Odile felt the spell reach out toward Sera like a blind hand feeling a face.

Whatever it found, Maren must have believed, because she let go and stepped back with an expression of profound annoyance, which Odile was learning meant she was moved.

"She means it," Maren said. "Gods help us all. She actually means it."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the rockfall, and Sera built a fire with the competence of someone who had slept outdoors more nights than she'd admit to any superior officer. They shared dried plums and hard bread and, eventually, names for things they'd each seen.

"The court," Sera said, turning the seal over in her fingers. "It doesn't eat. Did either of you notice? Banquets last six hours and the king touches nothing. The wine goes untouched. The food goes cold. And the whole hall pretends, because pretending is cheaper than looking."

"I noticed the not-blinking," Odile said. "The food I missed. I was mostly trying not to exist."

"A skill. I've made a career of it." Something almost like a smile crossed her face, there and gone. "Corvas hasn't. He watches everyone now. He watched me watch the king, and I saw him decide. That's when I took the seal."

"Why does a usurper need a royal seal?" Maren asked. She was feeding the fire pinches of something from a leather pouch, and the flames burned a shade too steadily, throwing heat further than fire should. "It has the throne. It has the army. It has winter coming."

"Winter?" Odile said.

Maren didn't answer at first. She looked at him across the fire for a long moment, and Odile recognized the look—he'd worn it himself in the scriptorium, deciding whether to tell the Abbess about the debt ledger, about his mother, about all the arithmetic of a hidden life.

"There's a price," Maren said. "For my help. For the road, for the teaching, for getting three nobodies past a palace full of hunters. I said you'd know when you knew. You know now."

Odile's mouth went dry. "Say it."

"The binding around the true king—it isn't rope, it isn't chains. It's a woven thing, twenty years deep, grown into him like roots into soil. To unravel it, someone has to go *inside* the weave and hold it open from within. Keep the strands apart while I cut them. If the weave closes even once—" she snapped her fingers, and the sound cracked off the rocks, "—the spell takes the holder. Folds them in. Another strand in the king's face, another year it holds."

"And the null-born," Sera said slowly, watching Odile, "can touch the weave at all. No one else can."

"No one else can," Maren agreed. "And holding it open will burn through everything he is. All of it. Every scrap of gift he was born with, poured out in one night. He comes out the other side empty. No magic. Ever again." She met Odile's eyes. "For anyone else, that price is death. For you—for a null-born—it's only everything you've got."

The fire popped. Snow had begun to fall somewhere above them, fine and dry, sifting down through the dark like ash.

Odile thought about the scriptorium at night, lamplight and stolen practice, the small bright magics that shouldn't have worked and did. The only thing that had ever been entirely his. The thing he'd spent his whole life hiding, and then spent three days outside the Veil finally letting breathe.

"You should have led with this," he said. "Before I crossed the boundary."

"If I'd led with it, would you have stayed?"

He thought about the children behind him at the temple gate, about Annet's practice letters, about a proclamation nailed to a door with his name implied in every line. "No," he admitted. "But I'd have liked the choice to feel like mine."

"It is yours," Maren said. "That's the whole point of telling you now. It was always going to be yours. I just wanted you fed and rested first."

Sera had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice was low. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't. I've looked for twenty years. I was exiled for looking." Maren poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "The coven said some doors shouldn't be opened. I said this one should. They chose their comfort. I chose the truth, and the truth costs what it costs."

Odile turned the seal's weight of the moment over in his mind, and found—to his own surprise—that underneath the fear there was something almost like relief. All his life he had paid for things he never chose: a mother's flight, an inherited debt, a gift that made him a criminal in his own valley. This, at least, was a debt he could take up with open eyes. This one he could choose.

"All right," he said.

"Odile—" Sera started.

"I said *all right*." He looked at her, and then at Maren, and found his voice steadier than he expected. "Not tonight. Tonight I want to hear about the palace guards, and where the binding sits, and how many ways this can go wrong—I want all of it, every terrible detail, because I've spent my whole life making decisions on half-information and I'm done. But yes. If it's me or no one, then it's me."

Maren studied him for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and produced a flask, uncorked it, and handed it over. "Hedge-witch whiskey. Tastes like a barn fire."

"So does the bark."

"You're learning."

He drank. It tasted exactly like a barn fire. Sera refused a turn with a small shake of her head, but she shifted closer to the flames, closer to them, and pulled her cloak around her shoulders, and none of them mentioned that the circle had quietly closed.

"Twenty years," Sera said at last, to the fire. "A man locked behind his own face while that thing wears him. And I saluted it." Her jaw worked. "I'll carry that. But I'll also carry the fixing of it, if you'll have me."

"We'll have you," Odile said, before Maren could say anything complicated.

Maren snorted. "We'll have her. Gods, listen to me. Twenty years alone in a root cellar and now I'm collecting strays on a mountainside."

"You weren't alone," Odile said. "You had the coven's shadow following you the whole time. That's not company, but it's not nothing."

Maren gave him a look that would have curdled milk, and then, briefly, didn't.

The snow thickened after midnight. They slept in shifts—Sera first watch, because she insisted and because neither of them was fool enough to argue with a disgraced hunt-captain holding a stolen seal and a sword. Odile lay with his back against warm stone and listened to the wind comb the pines, and let himself picture it: spring, a throne room with a living man in it, a valley with no debt ledger in it, his name written plainly somewhere instead of scratched out.

Then the wind changed.

It came down off the peak wrong—too cold even for altitude, cold with intention, cold that smelled faintly of iron. Sera was on her feet before Odile finished sitting up, hand on her sword, staring north into the dark.

"What is it?" Odile whispered.

Maren rose slowly beside him. Her face, lit by the dying fire, had gone the color of the snow.

"That's not weather," she said. "That's *him*. He's called the winter down early."

Above them, along the whole spine of the mountains, the snow stopped drifting and began to pour.
