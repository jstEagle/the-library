## 8. The Long Winter Falls

The snow began on the third day out of the mountain pass, and it did not stop.

Odile had seen winter before, of course. He had grown up in the Veil, where the seasons arrived politely and left when they were asked. But this was different. This was a winter with intent. It fell in sheets so thick that Maren's lantern made only a fist of light around them, and it fell without wind, straight down, as though the sky had been told to empty itself and had obeyed with the joyless efficiency of a scribe copying out a sentence he already knew by heart.

"It's not weather," Maren said, for the fourth time that morning. She said it the way other people said *good morning*, as a ritual to anchor herself. "Weather has moods. Weather argues with itself. This—" she swept her staff through the falling white, and Odile watched the snowflakes bend around the motion like iron filings around a lodestone, all of them leaning the same direction, all of them leaning *toward* him— "this is obedience."

"Then we should be flattered," Sera said. She rode at the front, hood back despite the cold, because she claimed a hunt-captain who hid her face looked like a deserter and a deserter looked like prey. Her beard of frost had become, over the past two days, almost decorative. "Someone up there is spending an entire kingdom's worth of magic just to inconvenience three travelers. I've never been so important in my life."

"You're not important," Maren said. "You're *in the way*. There's a difference, and the difference is that important people get invited places."

"I was invited to the palace once," Sera said. "I stole the seal off the table while I was there."

"That was the best decision anyone's made in this story," Maren admitted.

Odile let them talk. He had learned, over the weeks of the road, that their bickering was not friction but mortar—the sound of two women building something between themselves, brick by insulted brick, because neither of them knew how to say anything kind directly. He had learned it because he had spent his whole life doing the same thing with ink: writing around the truth until the truth got tired of waiting and simply appeared in the margins.

The truth appearing in his margins now was the villages.

They came down out of the high country into the river valley, and the river valley was dead. Not sleeping—dead. The first village they passed had its well frozen solid from the lip down to whatever water lived below the frost line, which should not have been possible, which Maren examined for a long moment and then walked away from without comment, which frightened Odile more than any comment could have. The second village had livestock standing in the fields where they'd dropped, preserved mid-shiver, their eyes frosted open. The third village had no one in it at all except an old man sitting in his doorway with a blanket over his knees, watching the road.

He waved at them as they passed. Odile reined in against every instinct of caution.

"You shouldn't be here," Odile said. "The passes are closing. Come with us toward the low country."

The old man smiled. His teeth were very white, which is a terrible thing to notice about a freezing man. "My wife's buried under the pear tree," he said. "Ground's too hard to dig now. I'll wait for spring." He patted the blanket over his knees. "It's coming late this year, isn't it."

"Yes," Odile said.

"Well." The old man settled back. "She always said I'd keep her waiting at least once."

They rode on. Nobody spoke for a mile. Then Sera said, quietly, in her report-voice, the one she used for facts too heavy to carry any other way: "There will be thousands like him. Tens of thousands. A winter that doesn't end doesn't kill with cold first. It kills with hunger, after. Cold kills the old and the foolish. Hunger kills everyone else, slowly, and makes them do terrible things to each other on the way down."

"And the usurper knows that," Odile said.

"The usurper is *counting* on that," Maren said. "Endless winter isn't a weapon aimed at us. It's a press. It squeezes the realm until everyone in it runs somewhere warm—and there's exactly one place in the world still warm, children, and it has walls and gates and a throne in it. Every refugee on these roads is being herded toward the capital like heat toward a hearth. And you can be certain our friend in the crown has a use for a desperate, starving crowd gathered in one place."

"What use?" Sera asked.

Maren didn't answer, and Odile understood why. Some answers, spoken aloud on a frozen road, become true faster than they otherwise would.

* * *

The capital announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and horse and unwashed humanity, ten thousand people pressed into the last warm basin in the kingdom. Then the walls rose out of the snow-haze, black stone furred white, and the gates stood open—not besieged but *ingesting*, swallowing a river of refugees in a slow grey stream.

They joined the stream. It was Sera's idea, and it was either brilliant or suicidal depending on which of them you asked, and since asking Sera would produce a confident answer and asking Maren would produce a lecture, Odile decided to simply do it and find out.

"Three travelers, no papers, arriving during a national emergency," Sera had said, ticking points off on gloved fingers. "Refugees aren't questioned—they're *processed*, which takes days and happens in a pen outside the walls where nobody looks at your face twice. I know the processing yards. I built half the procedures myself." She had paused, and then added, with the particular flatness she reserved for her own conscience, "They'll want names and parishes. Give them real ones. Lying to clerks is how people get caught; telling them sad truths is how people get fed."

So Odile gave his name, his real one, to a hollow-eyed clerk in a fur coat two sizes too big, and watched the man write *Vane, Odile, parish of the Veil valley* without any flicker of recognition. Somewhere in the capital there were wanted-notices with his face on them. But wanted-notices are for hunters, and hunters were busy, and clerks were drowning, and the machinery of cruelty, Odile thought, has this one mercy: it cannot process everything at once.

They were given a ration chit, a straw pallet in a converted tithe-barn, and a printed notice informing them that His Majesty welcomed all loyal subjects into the warmth of the capital, and that order would be maintained.

The barn held four hundred people. Odile lay on his pallet that night listening to the sound of it—coughing, weeping, a baby crying with the thin exhausted persistence of a thing that has cried past anger—and felt the plan they had made on the mountain road turn over in his chest and show him its underside.

On the road, the plan had been a shape: enter the palace, reach the binding, hold it open, unmake the usurper. Clean as a copied page.

Here in the barn, the plan had a cost written beneath it in small characters. Because the endless winter was not aimed at them. It was aimed at *this*—four hundred people on straw, and the four hundred thousand behind them, squeezed and squeezed until they broke or until someone offered them salvation. And if the usurper intended to offer salvation—if it meant to stand on a balcony above a starving city and end the winter with a flourish, buying twenty years of worship in an afternoon—

"Then we're not just racing the guards," Odile murmured into the dark. "We're racing the miracle."

Beside him, Maren's voice came dry as paper. "Good. You've finally done the arithmetic. Now sleep. Null-born burn bright when they're tired, and I need you dull as ditchwater tomorrow."

* * *

Getting from the refugee pens to the palace took three days, and it took them because of Sera.

She moved through the capital like a woman walking through her own memory with the furniture rearranged. Here the guard barracks where she'd trained recruits; here the records office she'd once audited; here the postern gate whose sergeant owed her his life and would owe her a great deal more silence by sundown. She dressed them in the grey livery of palace scullions—stolen, folded, and returned-with-interest from a laundry line—and walked them through the servants' gate past a captain of the watch who saluted her before he could stop himself, then went pale, then decided very visibly to have seen nothing.

"You're enjoying this," Odile whispered.

"No," Sera said. "I'm *grieving*. Enjoying comes later, if we live." But her mouth did something at the corner that Odile chose, generously, not to interpret.

The palace interior was warm. That was the horror of it. After a week of frost, the sudden bloom of heat in the marble corridors felt like stepping into a bath, and Odile had to bite down on the animal gratitude of it, because the warmth was not kindness. It was bait. Every corridor glowed with it; braziers burned in profusion; and through the tall windows the snow fell on without pause, indifferent as ever, so that the palace sat in its warmth like a lit lantern in a dark field, drawing everything living toward it.

And the null-born part of him—the part that had spent a lifetime pretending not to exist—felt the palace's magic the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Enchantments everywhere, layered thick as paint: wards on the doors, charms in the walls, a hum of compulsion laid over the throne room like a second roof. And beneath it all, deep down, a knot of binding so vast and so old that touching it even sidelong made his teeth ache.

"He's home," Odile breathed. "The true king. I can feel him. Like a heartbeat under the floor."

"Then we're close," Maren said. "Too close to be clever. Cleverness is for people with time."

It was Sera who found them the way in—a service stair behind the kitchens, remembered from some old inspection, leading up toward the royal apartments. It was Maren who unpicked the ward on the stair door, muttering apologies to the spell as she unraveled it, because Maren apologized to everything, including, once, a bridge. And it was Odile who stopped, three steps from the top, hand pressed flat against the wall, and went cold in a way the corridors' warmth could not touch.

Because the knot below them had *moved*.

Not loosened—moved. Drawn tighter. In the week since the winter began, the binding around the true king had been pulled taut as a bowstring, and Odile could feel, with the awful clarity of his gift, what it was being drawn tight *for*. Power was pooling around the imprisoned king like water behind a dam. The usurper wasn't holding the binding to keep its prisoner quiet.

It was holding the prisoner to use him. The true king's own magic, siphoned through the binding, feeding the winter. Feeding the dam. And when the city below broke—when the starving crowds finally surged, or froze, or both—the usurper would release the flood, end the winter in a single theatrical act of mercy, and spend the true king's last strength doing it.

"They're going to burn him as fuel," Odile whispered. "And call it a miracle."

Above them, at the top of the stair, a door opened.

Light spilled down. A silhouette stood in it—broad, armored, unhurried—and a voice Odile knew from a tribunal dais and a falsified report and a lifetime of nightmares said, mildly:

"Hunt-Captain Ilves. How good of you to come home. His Majesty has been *so* looking forward to meeting your friends."

Lieutenant Voss stepped aside, gesturing them upward with perfect courtesy, and Odile saw that behind him the corridor was lined with guards, and that every one of them was smiling, and that none of them were blinking.
