## 9. Unbinding

The throne room of the winter court had been beautiful once. Odile could see the bones of it beneath the frost—the gilt gone grey, the banners stiff as shrouds, the great windows that must have thrown colored light across the floor in summer now glazed with ice so thick it turned the daylight the color of old bruises.

He stood at the foot of the dais with Maren's hand gripping his wrist hard enough to leave marks, and he looked up at the king.

The true king was *in* there. Odile could feel him the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall—a faint, stubborn warmth behind twenty years of binding spell, wrapped in layer after layer of work so fine and so cruel it made Odile's teeth ache to look at it with his gift. The usurper hadn't caged the man in a tower. It had caged him in his own face, and then worn the face out in public every day, smiling with a mouth that belonged to someone else.

"You're staring," Sera murmured beside him. She was dressed as a palace guard, having relieved an actual guard of his uniform somewhere between the refugee-choked gates and this hall, and she wore the man's helmet tipped back like she found it personally insulting. "Stop staring. Staring is what got us noticed by the last three people who noticed us."

"I'm not staring. I'm *reading*."

"Read faster."

Maren's grip tightened. "It's time," she said quietly. "The court's changing of the watch is now. We have perhaps four hundred heartbeats before anyone looks too closely at the throne." She turned to Odile, and her eyes—brown and sharp and tired from three weeks of mountain roads—were gentler than her voice ever managed to be. "Last chance to walk away, null-born."

"The last chance was the root cellar," Odile said. "You knew that when you offered it."

"I did," Maren said. "I just wanted to hear you refuse again. It does an old woman good." She pressed something into his palm: a knot of thread and bone and dried rowan berry, tied off with a slipknot. "When I say *open*, you cut the first thread. Only the first. And then you hold the door, whatever it shows you. Whatever it *says* to you. Do you understand me? It will speak in your own voice. It will use your own memories. It lies the way water lies—by being exactly what you want to look at."

Odile closed his hand around the charm. Inside the Veil, magic had always come to him sideways, quiet, half-ashamed of itself. Here, outside, it roared. He could feel the binding on the king the way a deaf man suddenly given hearing feels a cathedral organ—not sound but pressure, vast and structured and wrong.

"I understand," he said.

Sera rolled her shoulders. "And I hold off the guards."

"No one's coming yet," Maren said.

"They're always coming," Sera said. "In my experience the waiting is just the part where they haven't told you yet." She drew her stolen sword an inch from its scabbard and let it slide back. "Go. I'll be loud if I need to be. You know how I feel about being loud."

Odile climbed the dais steps.

Up close, the thing wearing the crown smelled of cold iron and, beneath that, faintly of the temple incense he'd mixed himself for eleven years—which was absurd, impossible, and the first lie. He felt the usurper notice him. Not see—*notice*, the way a trap notices weight.

"*Little scribe*," it said, without moving its mouth. The voice arrived inside his skull, patient and pleasant. "*You copied wards for the temple of the Veil. You were very careful. Very small. Did you think smallness would save you?*"

"Maren," Odile said aloud, and his voice only shook a little. "Now."

"*Open*," Maren said below him, and began to unravel.

The binding was a knot with ten thousand turns in it, and Maren worked it the way she did everything—briskly, muttering, with the air of a woman unpicking a seam some fool had sewn crooked. Each layer she loosened, Odile caught and held open, his gift flowing into the gap like water into a cracked bowl. It didn't hurt. That surprised him. It felt like exhaling after holding his breath for his entire life.

Then the usurper stopped talking and started *showing*.

The throne room dissolved.

He was nine years old, in the scriptorium at night, ink on his fingers, making a paper bird lift off the desk and circle the candle flame—and Abbess Ferren standing in the doorway, and the moment stretching, sweet and terrible, the moment before she either reported him or didn't—

"It's lying," Odile gasped. "It's—it wasn't like this—"

"Hold the door!" Maren snapped.

He held the door.

The memory changed. In the false version, the Abbess's face went cold, and she called for the hunters, and little Odile was dragged out into the snow while the other orphans watched from the windows, and the bird fell and burned. Twenty years of loneliness, condensed into one invented wound. *See*, said the thing wearing the crown, gently, *see how they always abandon you. Put down the door, little scribe. Let it close. No one is coming for you. No one has ever come for you.*

"That's not—" Odile's eyes stung. Below him, Maren was working faster, threads of the binding sloughing away like ash, and each layer she removed gave the usurper more room to push. "That's not what happened. She taught me to hide better. She kept me *safe*—"

*Did she? Or did she teach you that safety and hiding were the same thing?*

The memory shifted again. Now it was Sera on the mountain road, knife drawn, the standoff, the long terrible minute before trust—and in the false version the knife came down. Now it was Maren's root cellar, and the false version had Maren selling him to the riders for the price of her coven's forgiveness. Now it was the orphans pooling their wages in a chipped clay pot, and the false version showed them laughing about it afterward, showing him the empty pot, telling him it had all been a joke—

Odile's knees nearly buckled.

But underneath the falseness, he could still feel the truth of each memory, the way you can feel a splinter under painted wood. The pot had been real. Sera's lowered knife had been real. The Abbess had stood in that doorway with candlelight on her face and said, *Well, don't scorch my curtains, boy,* and become the first person in the world to know him and stay.

"You're using my own heart against me," he said, and found, to his astonishment, that he was almost laughing. "That's your mistake. You reached into a scribe's chest and stole his *rough drafts*. I've seen worse versions of these. I wrote them myself, at fourteen, in the margins of my copybooks." He set his feet. "These are the *revisions*. Try again."

The usurper screamed.

Not with a mouth—with the whole binding, a shriek of constrained fury that shook frost from the rafters, and the throne room came back all at once, and so did the noise. Boots. Many boots. Shouting in the antechamber.

"Sera!" Maren barked.

"On it!" Sera ripped the sword free, kicked over a brazier for effect, and planted herself at the head of the dais stairs like a woman settling in for a very satisfying argument. "Good evening, gentlemen! Yes, this is exactly what it looks like!"

The first guards came through the doors and immediately regretted it. Sera fought the way she did everything—economically, furiously, and with running commentary. Odile spared her one glance, saw her disarm a man with the flat of her blade and say something that made another hesitate, and turned back to the work.

Because the last layers were different.

The innermost binding wasn't cruelty. It was *grief*. Someone had built it—no, the usurper had built it, but it had used the king's own despair as mortar, every turn of the spell anchored to a moment the true king had wanted to stop existing. Odile could feel him now, close, a man pressed against the inside of his own skull for twenty years, and the man was *tired*. The man had stopped believing anyone was out there.

"Your Majesty," Odile said aloud, and his voice cracked straight down the middle. "My name is Odile Vane. I'm a scribe. I'm nobody—I was nobody, I hid my whole life, I know exactly what it is to make yourself small enough that the world forgets you're in it." He poured more of himself into the door he was holding, and felt his gift drain like a lamp burning down its last oil, and did not stop. "But I came anyway. Across the Veil. Through the winter. With a disgraced hunt-captain and a hedge-witch who owes me money, actually, she never paid for the bread—"

"Heard that," Maren muttered, working.

"—and we are *here*, and we are opening this door, and you are going to walk out of your own face, sir, because your kingdom is freezing and your people are starving and there is a garden in a valley that needs a pardon signed, and I have *orphaned children* waiting on that signature, so with respect—"

He cut the final thread.

The binding came apart all at once, and it did not go quietly. The usurper threw everything it had left down the channel between them—every fear, every hunger, twenty years of borrowed life slamming against the door Odile held—and Odile held it, and held it, and felt his gift go out of him like heat from an opened forge, wave after wave, until there was nothing left to give and he gave *that* too, because the door was almost open, because the man inside was reaching, because—

Light.

Not cold light. Warm, gold, ordinary morning light, though it was evening, pouring out of the king's face as the false shape peeled away from it like ice off a spring branch. The thing wearing the crown tore loose with a sound like a glacier calving and came apart into nothing at all, and the man behind it collapsed forward into Odile's arms, gaunt and grey-eyed and weeping without any idea he was doing it.

"Twenty years," the true king whispered. "Who—are you?"

"Nobody," Odile said. His legs gave out. He sat down hard on the dais steps, holding a king, feeling the strangest emptiness in his chest where his gift had lived his whole life—a silence, a stillness, a room with the fire gone out. He waited for grief. What came instead, faintly, absurdly, was relief, like setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Guards were kneeling. Sera stood among them with her sword point-down and her helmet finally off, looking slightly stunned to be alive. Maren climbed the dais steps, checked the king's pulse with two fingers, checked Odile's with two more, and then cuffed him gently on the ear.

"You held the door," she said.

"You owe me bread money," Odile said.

The king lifted his head. Frost was sliding from the windows; somewhere far off, Odile could have sworn he heard the groan of thawing rivers. The old man looked at the three of them—at the hunt-captain, the hedge-witch, the empty-handed scribe—and said, in a voice rusted from disuse:

"Spring," he said wonderingly. "I did it. Is it—is it truly—"

And then the doors burst open a second time, and a lieutenant Odile had hoped never to see again stood in the frame with a drawn crossbow and a face full of triumph, and said:

"Seize them. All four. The king especially—he's been dead to us for twenty years, and whatever's sitting on that throne now, it isn't him."

* * *

They took Odile's hands, which was easy, since he had no magic left to argue with. They took Maren's charms, which she surrendered with a stream of curses in three languages. They took Sera's sword, which she gave up with a look that promised the lieutenant a much longer conversation later.

As they dragged him past the throne, Odile caught the true king's eye—one prisoner to another, one survivor to another—and the old man, kneeling in chains between guards who thought him an impostor, winked.

Odile started, despite everything, to laugh.
