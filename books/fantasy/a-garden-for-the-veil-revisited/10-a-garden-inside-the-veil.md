## 10. A Garden Inside the Veil

The pardon came on the first warm day of spring, which Odile considered a small mercy from a king who had spent twenty years unable to grant anyone anything.

It arrived by courier to the temple of the Veil, sealed in green wax—the true king's color now, though half the realm still called it usurper's green out of habit—and it was read aloud in the refectory because Abbess Verity said that pardons, like bread, should be shared while fresh.

"Let it be known," she read, her voice carrying the particular gravity she reserved for proclamations and scolding, "that all persons registered or pursued under the edicts of the false reign are hereby forgiven, freed, and restored to the full protection of the crown. Let it further be known that all debts contracted to temples under duress of those same edicts are voided, and—"

She stopped. Looked up. Found Odile in the third row between Pip and little Wren, who had wedged himself under Odile's arm the way he did when he wanted to hear something important without admitting he wanted to hear something important.

"—and that one Odile Vane," the Abbess continued, with the faintest smile, "is owed an apology by the crown, which the crown does not intend to deliver in person because the king says, quote, 'the man has suffered enough.'"

The refectory laughed. Odile did not, quite. He sat very still while the words *voided* settled into him like rain into dry ground. Twenty-two years of debt. His mother's flight across the boundary stone, pregnant, hunted; the temple's mercy in taking him in; the ledger with his name at the bottom of every page, the sum recalculated each year with interest he had never once been able to argue down.

Gone. Forgiven. A line drawn through it by a man who had spent two decades imprisoned behind his own face and understood, perhaps better than any living monarch, what it meant to be held against your will by something written about you.

Pip elbowed him. "You're crying on my sleeve."

"You're crying on mine."

"We can both be crying," Pip decided, and put his arm around Odile's shoulders, and Wren burrowed in closer, and that was how Odile Vane learned that his debt was dead: weeping into a fourteen-year-old's sleeve in a room that smelled of porridge and ink.

* * *

He stayed three more weeks before he could bear to leave.

Not the valley—he would never leave the valley again, that was the whole point—but the winter court, where Maren was being reinstated to her coven with conditions she described as "insulting" and accepted with visible delight, and where Sera Ilves was undergoing the strange ordeal of being thanked by a government she had recently burgled.

"I stole their seal," Sera had pointed out, more than once, to anyone who would listen.

"You stole it back," the king's chancellor had replied, "from a thing that was never legally a king. Legally speaking, you committed no crime. Morally speaking, you are owed a medal. Please stop arguing."

Odile found her on the palace steps the morning he left, in her hunt-captain's coat, which she had not yet stopped wearing even though there was nothing left to hunt. She looked like a woman standing in a doorway she no longer recognized.

"They offered me a generalship," she said, without greeting. "Of the new wardens. The ones who protect gifted folk instead of arresting them."

"And?"

"And I told them I'd think about it." She turned a letter over in her hands. It was sealed, but unaddressed. "I've been thinking about it for six days."

"Maren says you think about things the way rivers think about stones."

"Maren says too many true things for a hedge-witch." But she almost smiled. Then: "Go home, Odile. Plant your garden. If I'm meant to find my way anywhere, I'll find my way there."

He wanted to say something. He had been wanting to say something since a throne room in winter, since her hand on his shoulder pulling him out of the unraveling spell, since longer than that, since a mountain road and a knife between them and the strange moment when neither of them had used it. But the words sat in his chest like seeds in frozen ground, and so instead he said:

"There's a bed in the scriptorium loft that's been empty since Pip grew out of it. And the Abbess always needs another pair of hands in the garden. And the valley—" he hesitated, then said it plainly, because he had promised himself he would learn to say things plainly, "—the valley is better with you in it."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the letter in her hands, and tucked it away inside her coat, unopened.

"Six days," she said. "I'll give it until the thaw finishes."

* * *

The boundary stone looked smaller than he remembered.

That was foolish—it hadn't changed, of course. It was he who had changed, crossing it twice now, once fleeing and once returning, and knowing what lay on either side of it. He stood before it in the late afternoon light with a pack on his back and Maren's seed packet in his breast pocket, and he waited for the old familiar sensation: the dampening, the muffling, the sense of walking into water.

Nothing happened. Because nothing was left to happen. He was null-born no longer; the gift that had let him touch magic inside the Veil was spent entirely, poured out in a throne room to hold a binding open while a witch unraveled twenty years of theft. He was, by every measure that mattered, an ordinary man.

He stepped across.

And the world did not go quiet. It went *quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when someone you love falls asleep in it—not empty, but at rest. The Veil settled over him like a blanket rather than a lid. He felt it as absence only because he knew what presence was, and he found, standing on the temple path with the first crocuses pushing through the mud, that he did not miss it. He did not miss hiding. He did not miss practicing small magics at midnight and burning the evidence. He did not miss being a secret.

He was done being secrets. All of them.

The children saw him before he reached the gate. Of course they did; Wren had the eyes of a hawk and the discretion of a church bell, and his shout of "HE'S BACK" carried across three fields and possibly into the next county. By the time Odile reached the gate he was buried under orphans—Wren and Pip and Marta and the twins and solemn little Ansel who pretended he was too old for this and was gripping Odile's coat with both fists—and behind them came the Abbess, unhurried as ever, drying her hands on her apron.

"You look thinner," she said.

"You look older."

"I am older. It's been four months. You were gone four months and returned looking like a hero in a ballad, which is worse than thin." She took his face in her hands and studied him the way she studied manuscripts, for flaws and forgery, and found, apparently, neither. "Well," she said quietly. "There you are."

"Here I am," Odile said. "All of me. No parts held in reserve."

"Good." She released him. "The garden's gone to ruin without you. That's not sentiment; that's report. Bracken everywhere. I expect it cleared by week's end."

"Yes, Abbess."

"And Odile." Her voice softened by a degree, which from Verity was a warmth other women achieved with embraces. "Your mother would have been proud of you. I've thought so for years. I'm glad the world finally agrees."

He managed a nod before Wren demanded to know if he had really fought the Winter King, and whether the palace really had a hundred rooms, and whether it was true that he'd held open a spell with his bare hands, and Odile said yes, no, and also no, in that order, and the lie about the hundred rooms was worth it for the disappointment on Wren's face.

* * *

The garden took him nine days to clear and cost him three blisters and one argument with a bramble that the bramble won.

On the tenth day he opened Maren's seed packet. Inside, wrapped in waxed cloth, were seeds of every kind—beans and marigolds and something Maren had labeled, in her cramped hand, *moonflowers, plant where the evening sun lands, they only open for people who stay*. At the bottom of the packet was a note:

*For the null-born who gave everything and asked for nothing, which was stupid of him, so I'm asking for something: grow these, write to me, and tell me if the moonflowers work. —M.*

He planted them all along the south wall, where the evening sun landed, working in the gold light with dirt to his elbows and no magic in him at all, and it was the happiest labor of his life. There was something in it—the plainness of it. Seed, soil, water, patience. No wards, no bindings, no hidden self. Just a man making a thing grow because he wanted to see it grow.

He was patting down the last row when he heard boots on the path, and did not need to turn around, because he had known those footsteps since a mountain road.

Sera stood at the garden gate in traveling clothes, no coat of office, no seal, no lieutenant. Just a woman with dust on her boots and a letter in her hand—the same letter, he guessed, still sealed, still unaddressed.

"The thaw finished," she said.

"So it did."

"I resigned this morning. The chancellor called me a fool. The king called me wise, which worried me more." She came through the gate and crouched beside the turned earth, studying the neat rows. "What's this?"

"A garden. Maren's seeds. Moonflowers along the wall—they only open for people who stay."

"Is that true, or is that Maren being dramatic?"

"With Maren the two are indistinguishable." He wiped his hands on his knees. "You're staying, then."

It wasn't quite a question, but she answered it like one. "I wrote my resignation six days before I left the capital. I just needed to be sure the reason was still here." She looked at him then, directly, the way she had looked at him across a knife's edge on the mountain road, and Odile understood that whatever she was about to say, she had already fought the battle for it and won. "I'm not good at saying things plainly. Hunt-captains aren't trained for it. But you said once you were learning, so I'll try. I crossed the Veil for you. I'd cross it again. I don't want a bed in the scriptorium loft, Odile. I want—" she stopped, jaw tight, and started again, plainer. "I want to stay. With you. However you'll have me."

The words went into him like water into that dry ground, and Odile found that saying things plainly was easier than advertised, once you'd survived a throne room.

"However I'll have you," he repeated. "That's a wide door."

"Is it open?"

"It's been open since the mountain road. I was just waiting for you to walk through it."

She laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the first he'd heard from her that wasn't aimed at anything—and then, because they were both of them people who had learned that time was not guaranteed, she kissed him, there in the dirt among the future beans and the unborn moonflowers, and somewhere behind them Wren shouted "I KNEW IT" and Pip said "you owe me a copper" and the Abbess, passing with a basket, remarked to no one that it was about time.

* * *

They planted the rest of the garden together over the following weeks, and Odile discovered that Sera weeded with military precision and Maren's moonflower note had been, for once, entirely accurate: on the first night of early summer, the flowers opened, pale and luminous along the south wall, and they opened toward the two figures sitting on the step who had, demonstrably, stayed.

A letter came from Maren reporting triumphantly that her coven had voted her back in and she was already planning her first act of insubordination. A letter came from the king himself, brief and oddly shy, asking whether the apology mentioned in the pardon might be delivered in person someday, as he had never seen the famous Veil, and had heard the valley was beautiful in autumn. Odile wrote back: *Come whenever you like, Your Majesty. Bring nothing. We have a garden.*

And on quiet evenings, when the orphans were abed and the Abbess was at her prayers and the moonflowers glowed along the wall, Odile Vane would sit with Sera's head on his shoulder and feel the Veil settle around them both like a blanket, keeping nothing out, holding nothing in, needing nothing from him but that he remain exactly what he was: a scribe, a gardener, a brother, a beloved. A man with no gift at all, except the ones he had chosen.

He never wrote in the old debt ledger again. But he kept it, on the shelf above his desk, and sometimes he took it down and looked at the last page, where the final entry stood in the crown's green ink: *Voided.*

Below it, in his own hand, added the night he came home, he had written one more line. Not a debt. Not a record. Just the plainest sentence he knew:

*Kept.*
