## 3. What the Maps Refuse

There were three rules Elodie Whitlock had learned at her mother's knee before she'd learned her letters, and she recited them every morning the way other people recited prayers: *Measure twice, trust the instrument, and never map what you have not seen.*

Her mother had been wrong about the third one. Elodie was beginning to suspect she'd been wrong about the second as well.

She stood at the edge of the survey field behind the Guildhouse — a stretch of clipped grass the size of a modest orchard, upon which the Guild of Impossible Things had spent two hundred years perfecting the art of mapping things that did not wish to be mapped. Around her, brass instruments on tripod legs caught the grey morning light: the theodolite with its nested rings, the plumb-bob of suspended starlight that always pointed toward the nearest true north no matter how far north had wandered, and the planchette — a slab of pale wood on three felt feet, rigged with a stylus, that drew the shape of the world as it was rather than as anyone wished it to be.

The planchette was her favorite. It was also, this morning, being insufferable.

"Again," Elodie said, and nudged it with two fingers.

The planchette shuddered, rolled a quarter-turn as though shaking itself like a wet dog, and drew the same line it had drawn four times already: a clean, confident arc sweeping across the survey paper, curving where no curve should be, and then — this was the part Elodie had come to think of as the planchette showing off — it stopped. Dead. Mid-stroke. The stylus pressed down hard enough to tear a small hole in the paper, and then lifted, and the instrument went still in a way that instruments were not supposed to go still. Instruments were supposed to be indifferent. This one looked, if she were being honest with herself, *reluctant*.

"Right," she said. "You and I are going to have a conversation about professional standards."

The planchette said nothing, which was normal, but it also *suggested* nothing, which was not.

She crouched and examined the tear. Four surveys, four identical arcs, four identical holes, all in exactly the same place. She had re-leveled the tripod, re-wound the starlight plumb, re-seated the stylus, and even — in a moment of desperation she would deny under oath — apologized to the instrument. Same arc. Same hole. Same stubborn, pointed silence.

The coordinates of the hole, if she translated the arc's geometry into the guild's standard notation, were these: *the Veil's edge, at the point where the lower city's oldest wall met the river fog, at the hour when the bells disagreed about the time.* She wrote the figures in her field book, and her pen hesitated over them, because she had seen those figures before. Two nights ago. In a ledger that had no business containing them.

She had not told anyone. She had told herself this was because she wanted to be sure before raising an alarm, which was true, and also because Guildmaster Aurelian had a particular way of looking at reports he didn't like — a way that made the report, and occasionally the reporter, feel like problems to be filed — and she did not yet know which category hers would fall into. *Inconvenient* or *heretical.* With the guild, the two categories overlapped more than the charter admitted.

*The Ledger of Small Miracles.* She had gone back to it three times since that first evening, each time telling herself she'd imagined the pattern, each time finding it more definite. Answered prayers, logged in a neat anonymous hand, dated across the last six weeks. And in the margins of three separate entries, in that same hand, a set of coordinates — the same coordinates, always, like a man scratching the same itch. The coordinates of a hole in her map.

Coincidence was a word cartographers used the way sailors used the word *unsinkable*: with hope, and a lifeboat nearby.

"Whitlock!" called Tam Broadstairs from the far edge of the field, where he was wrestling the fog-gauge off its stand. Tam was a junior surveyor with the build of a friendly door and the temperament of a pleased cat, and he was, at this moment, losing an argument with a brass cylinder. "The fog-gauge is reading *upward* again. Fog does not go up. Fog has one job."

"Bring it here," Elodie said. "And bring the humidity bells."

"The bells are disagreeing."

"The bells are always disagreeing. That's how we know they're working."

He lumbered over, cradling the gauge like a colicky infant. "Third time this week it's done this. Juniper says the mechanism's worn. I say mechanisms don't wear *directionally*."

Elodie took the gauge and held it up. The needle — which measured the density of the river fog, a substance that by every law she knew should have been lying flat and damp over the water — was quivering at a steep angle, pointing not at the river but at the survey field. At the arc. At the hole.

"Juniper's wrong," Elodie said quietly.

"So it's not worn?"

"It's not *pointing wrong*, is what it is." She set the gauge down on the survey table, next to the paper with its four torn holes, and watched the needle swing, slowly, until it aligned with the arc — pointing at the hole like a compass needle finding north, if north had been somewhere it wasn't supposed to be. "Tam. What do you know about the Veil?"

He blinked. "That it's the Veil? That it keeps the guild invisible and the impossible things impossible? That we're not supposed to ask questions about it because the last surveyor who asked questions about it was reassigned to the lighthouse in the Sorrowfens, where there is no sea?"

"That's the official curriculum, yes."

"That's the *only* curriculum. I checked. I checked because I was hoping there'd be a second curriculum with more interesting bits." He peered at the gauge, then at the map, and his cat-pleased expression flickered into something more careful. "Elodie. Why is your map torn?"

"Because the world has a hole in it," she said, "and my instruments are too honest to lie about it."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she always did when the world misbehaved: she got more precise.

The Guild's map room occupied the third floor of the west wing, a long chamber with windows of thick green glass that made the afternoon light look underwater. Shelves rose to the ceiling on every wall, holding the guild's great atlases — the honest ones, the ones that mapped what could be mapped. And in a locked case at the room's center, under three keys and a mildew-proof blessing, lay the oldest atlas in the collection: her mother's. *The Whitlock Grand Survey.* Elodie had the first key. Aurelian had the second. The third had been her mother's, and had gone into the ground with her, which Elodie had always thought was either very sentimental or very deliberate, and she had never decided which worried her more.

But it was not her mother's atlas she needed now. It was the working files — the survey records of the Veil's edge, three hundred years of them, kept in the long drawers beneath the windows the way other institutions kept records of births and deaths.

She pulled the oldest drawer and began to read.

Surveyors' notes were a genre unto themselves, and three centuries of them read like the diary of a very long, very patient argument. *The Veil's edge at the river wall holds steady. No drift.* *Drift of two feet eastward; corrected.* *Drift of two feet eastward again; the correction did not hold; recommend we stop correcting and start asking why.* And then, in a hand from a hundred and fifty years back, a note Elodie read three times:

*The edge is not a line. It has never been a line. It is a seam, and today I felt it pull.*

She sat back. Around her the map room ticked and settled in the way old rooms did, and somewhere below, the bells disagreed about the hour, and she found she was holding her own wrist the way she did when she wanted to take her own pulse but didn't want to admit she was frightened.

*A seam.* Seams could be sewn. Seams could also, she thought, with a coldness that started in her fingertips and worked inward, *be picked.*

She pulled more records. The pattern, once she looked for it, was everywhere and nowhere: little anomalies, decades apart, always smoothed over by the next surveyor's tidy correction. A fog-gauge reading upward in the year of the Double Winter. A plumb-bob of starlight that pointed *down* for one recorded afternoon and was attributed to a clerical error. A junior surveyor's marginal doodle — a door, sketched idly, in a hand that had gone on to become very famous — at the exact coordinates that were now tearing holes in her paper.

Elodie looked at the doodle for a long time.

It wasn't a grand door. It was a plain one, the kind you'd find in a cellar or a servant's passage, with a latch and a hinge and a small round window at eye height. The famous surveyor had drawn it in the margin of his field book, next to the coordinates, and beneath it he'd written a single line that the guild's cataloguers had, with the magnificent incuriosity of cataloguers everywhere, filed under *decorative*:

*It was already here. I only drew what was asking to be drawn.*

Her instruments, Elodie thought, had never once in her career drawn anything she hadn't surveyed. That was the entire covenant of her craft. The planchette was a servant of observation, not imagination. If it drew a door —

She got up, went back to the survey field, and stayed until the light went the color of weak tea.

* * *

The fifth survey was done at dusk, alone, because Tam had been sent to file the morning's anomalies — a task she had assigned with a smile and a lie about wanting quiet to concentrate, and because the alternative was doing it in front of witnesses, and she was no longer certain what the planchette would draw, and some things you needed to see by yourself first, the way you needed to see your own face alone before you could bear a mirror held by someone else.

The bells had begun their evening argument. The river fog was coming up off the water in slow grey shoulders. Elodie leveled the tripod, seated the stylus, laid fresh paper, and set her hands on the planchette.

"Now," she said to it, in the tone she used on difficult instruments and difficult juniors and, once, memorably, on a locked door that had been locked for reasons the guild had lost the reasons to. "You've made your point five times. Make it properly. Show me the whole of it."

The planchette trembled under her palms.

Then it moved — not the smooth, gliding motion of a survey but something quicker, *eager*, the stylus skating across the paper in long decisive strokes, and Elodie, who had been trained never to fight an instrument, never to guide it, only to follow, followed. Her breath made small ghosts in the cooling air. The fog crept up the field and curled around the tripod legs like a cat around an ankle. The arc completed itself. The coordinates resolved. And then, at the hole — at the tear, at the seam, at the place where the fog-gauge pointed and the ledger scratched and a dead surveyor had doodled a cellar door a hundred and fifty years ago —

The planchette drew a door.

Not a doodle. Not decorative. A door in perfect measured perspective, with a lintel and a threshold and a latch rendered in the precise, unlovely shorthand of a working survey, and beneath it, in the planchette's stiff cartographic hand, a notation Elodie had never seen in any guild stylebook, in any atlas, in any of her mother's marginalia:

*HERE.*

The stylus lifted. The planchette went still — that same reluctant, pointed stillness — and the fog, which had been curling around her ankles all this while, stopped.

Elodie stood in the grey dusk of the survey field with her hands still resting on the wood, and felt the exact moment her fascination curdled. It was not a dramatic moment. It was quiet, the way ice is quiet when it forms. Fascination was the word she'd been using all day, in the privacy of her own skull, and she realized now that fascination had simply been fear wearing her favorite coat.

Because she knew what the planchette was. It drew what *was*. It was a servant of observation. It could not imagine, could not embellish, could not — by the whole three-hundred-year covenant of her craft — draw a thing that was not there.

And it had drawn a door at the edge of the Veil, at coordinates that matched the answered prayers, and it had labeled it *HERE*, in the tone of a thing that had been waiting a very long time to be noticed.

"Who's there?" she said aloud, and immediately felt foolish, and immediately stopped feeling foolish, because the fog around the tripod had *thinned* — just slightly, just locally, the way air thins when a room's door opens somewhere and the draft goes looking for the window.

Her field book was in her satchel. Her hands, she noted with the detached professionalism of a woman watching her own craft betray her, were steady. Surveyors' hands. Measure twice. She took out the book, and against every instinct for tidiness she owned, she wrote the report she would have to give the Guildmaster, because she had thought about it all afternoon on the walk back and decided that the alternative — sitting on it, sitting on it the way the old records showed anomaly after anomaly being smoothed over and corrected and filed under *decorative* — was how a seam got to quietly become a wound.

*Survey of the Veil's edge, fifth attempt,* she wrote. *Instruments consistent. Anomaly confirmed at the following coordinates.* She wrote them out. *The planchette has rendered a structure at these coordinates. Classification: door. The door was not surveyed. The door was not imagined. The instruments insist the door is there, and my instruments have never lied to me, and I am given to understand that this is the beginning of a longer sentence than I would like it to be.*

She signed it, blotted it, and sat for a moment in the fog with the paper on her knees.

Her mother's voice, three rules at a knee, warm as bread: *never map what you have not seen.* And underneath it, surfacing now for the first time in years, the memory of her mother saying something else, something Elodie had filed for thirty years under *decorative*: *Some things, love, are only visible to the person drawing them. That's not a flaw in the map. That's the map doing its job.*

The fog stirred. The bells, for one clear unarguable moment, agreed on the hour — and then, in the silence after, Elodie heard, very faintly, from the direction of the river wall, from the direction of the coordinates, a sound like a latch being tested from the other side.

She gathered her instruments with hands that were, she was pleased to note, still steady, and went to deliver her report to the Guildmaster before the door finished making up its mind.

She would remember, later, that she had knocked on his study door at the exact minute the bells finally agreed upon. She would remember that he had looked at her report for a long time without reading it, the way a man looks at a letter he has already decided to burn.

But that was later. For now there was only the corridor, and the candle in her hand, and behind her, at the edge of the map, a door that had learned her name.
