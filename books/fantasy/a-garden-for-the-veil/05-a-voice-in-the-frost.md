## 5. A Voice in the Frost

There is a particular kind of silence that lives at the edge of a map, and Elodie Whitlock had spent eleven years learning to listen to it.

It was not the silence of empty rooms or snowbound fields. It was the silence of a question the world had declined to answer — the hush where her instruments went quiet, where ink pooled strangely on the page, where the Guild of Impossible Things' oldest rule (measure everything; trust nothing you cannot measure twice) began to feel less like wisdom and more like a prayer itself.

She stood now at the coordinates that had refused her three times running. The fourth attempt, she told herself, would be different, because she had brought better tea.

"Right," she said aloud, to no one, because talking to oneself was one of the few luxuries of fieldwork and she intended to enjoy it. "You are a patch of moorland. You have grass. You have rocks. You have, I grant you, an unusual number of rocks. But you do *not* get to be un-mappable. That is my job."

The moorland said nothing, which was standard.

She unpacked her kit with the small ceremony she permitted herself: the brass theodolite her mother had left her, the folding table, the ink that was supposed to dry in any weather, the thermos of cinnamon tea that Cassian had pressed into her hands at the guild door that morning with the expression of a man who wanted to say something and had decided, wisely, not to.

*Be careful out there,* he'd said instead, which from Cassian was practically a sonnet.

The morning was cold in the way that early winter is cold when it arrives too early — apologetic but firm, like a landlord raising rent before the lease has technically expired. Frost silvered the grass in patches, and Elodie noted, with professional displeasure, that the frost did not fall evenly. It fell in *lines*. Straight ones. Radiating outward from a point some thirty paces north, as though the ground beneath were a wheel and something at its hub were very slowly turning.

Her instruments confirmed what her eyes suspected. The compass needle didn't spin — spinning would at least have been dramatic, explicable, the sort of thing one wrote up in a report with a wry footnote. Instead it simply *agreed*. It pointed at the hub of frost-lines and stayed there, faithful as a dog, and Elodie found this far more alarming than any spinning.

"Compasses don't agree," she muttered, setting the theodolite anyway. "Compasses argue. That's their whole personality."

She worked for an hour. She measured the angle between two stones that appeared identical and were not; she measured the height of a hill that seemed, each time she looked away from it, to be very slightly closer; she drank her tea while it was still hot, which in her experience of resistant terrain counted as a minor miracle all its own.

And then, at half past ten by her pocket watch, the light changed.

Not dimmed. Not brightened. *Changed* — the way light changes through stained glass when a cloud passes behind the window rather than in front of it. The frost-lines flared faintly blue, and the air took on the quality of a held breath, and every hair on Elodie's arms rose in perfect cartographic unison.

"Oh," she said. "No. Absolutely not. I haven't finished the survey."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which was bad enough. What was worse — what made her knees go strange and her heart forget several of its duties — was that the voice was *warm*. Deep and low and amused, like a hearth-fire that had learned to speak, like someone who had known you your whole life and found you consistently, fondly ridiculous.

***Liora.***

Elodie's pen slipped. A blot of ink bloomed across her survey sheet like a small black flower.

"My name," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "is Elodie Whitlock, Guild of Impossible Things, third rank, and you are trespassing on—"

***Liora.*** The warmth deepened. ***You always did introduce yourself too thoroughly. As if names were fortifications.***

"They *are* fortifications," she snapped, because terror and irritation had always lived close together in her chest. "That's rather the point of them. Who are you? Where am I actually standing? Because according to three surveys, I'm standing nowhere, and I've about had it with nowhere."

A pause. The frost-lines pulsed once, slow, like the gills of something enormous breathing just under the skin of the world.

***You came back,*** the voice said, and the amusement in it cracked down the middle, and underneath was something so old and so raw that Elodie's eyes stung without her permission. ***Three hundred years, little mapmaker. Three hundred years of dark and cold and counting, and you walk back in with a thermos of tea and a theodolite, and you ask me for my credentials.***

"I—" Elodie's mouth opened. Closed. "I don't know what you're talking about. I've never been here before in my life."

***No,*** the voice agreed, gentle now, terrible now. ***Not this body. Not these hands. But look.***

***

The world folded.

There was no gentler word for it. One moment the moor, the next — elsewhere, elsewhen, a memory pressing against her from the inside like a face against wet glass.

She stood in a hall of black stone lit by a thousand candles that burned without heat. Around her, figures moved in robes the color of dusk, and their faces were blurred, as dreams blur faces, except one: a woman at the center, young, dark-haired, ink-stained to the elbows, holding a chain-link the size of a dinner plate and arguing passionately with someone Elodie could not see.

The woman turned. And Elodie knew her own face looking back at her — younger, harder around the mouth, eyes that had not yet learned the particular sadness they carried now — and understood, with the calm clarity of a person falling, that she was looking at Liora. At herself. At whatever portion of herself had lived here first.

*"Say it again,"* Liora was saying, in a voice that was Elodie's voice worn slightly differently, like a coat handed down. *"Say the whole terms. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. I refuse to bind a god on vague language."*

Something answered her — a vast presence at the edge of the candlelight, all edges and patience — and the memory shivered, and Elodie saw:

Chains.

Not iron. Chains of *ink*, drawn link by link across a floor the size of a valley, each link inscribed with words in a notation she recognized with a lurch — her mother's notation, the marginalia-script, the secret shorthand of the Whitlock atlases — except these were older, original, the source-code from which her mother had copied. Liora knelt at the chain's heart with a stylus of white fire, writing the binding into being, and around her the blurred robed figures sang in a language that made the candles gutter, and at the center of it all, caught already in the first links, something immense turned its attention upon her —

Green eyes. Amber-shot. Furious and grieving and *knowing her*.

***You helped them,*** said the memory-Veyra, or the dream-Veyra, or whatever she was, and her voice filled Elodie like water fills a bell. ***You drew the first link yourself. You kissed my brow and called me sister and then you chained me, Liora, and you wept while you did it, and you said — say it. Say what you said.***

And Liora — Elodie — the woman with her face, tears cutting tracks through the ink on her cheeks, lifted her chin and said:

*"I'm sorry. But someone has to hold the line between your mercy and your appetite, and mortals can't survive either of you ungoverned. Forgive me. Or don't. Either way — sleep."*

The chains closed. The green eyes dimmed. The candles went out all at once, all thousand of them, and in the sudden dark Elodie heard Liora whisper, so quietly the memory almost lost it:

*"I'll come back. I'll find a way to make it right. I promise."*

***

She woke on her back in the frost with her lashes rimed white and her breath smoking and the pocket watch reading four minutes past ten — twenty-six minutes before she had fallen, if it had ever fallen at all.

For a long moment she lay still and let the sky be the sky: gray, ordinary, indifferent, blessedly *mapped*.

Then she became aware of a tugging sensation at her left hand, as though the air itself were trying to get her attention, and she raised it.

On her palm, in ink-black letters in a hand that was not hers and yet was — in the hand from the memory, the hard-mouthed young woman's hand, her own hand worn differently — was written a single word:

*unmake.*

"Fascinating," Elodie said to the sky, in the tone she used for instruments that had begun smoking. Her voice only shook a little. "Fascinating. Very good. Ten out of ten. Now never do that again."

She sat up. Checked her kit — all present, tea cold, theodolite intact. Checked her survey sheet — and there stopped checking, because across the bottom of the page, below her own careful measurements, ran a line of notation in her mother's script. Not her mother's handwriting. Her mother's *system*: the hidden shorthand from the old atlas, the one she'd spent three years failing to decode.

Except now, having seen the chains drawn in it, she could read the first phrase as easily as her own name.

*The lock remembers the key.*

Elodie sat very still on the frozen moor, in the coordinates that were not on any map, wearing a dead god's grief and a stranger's handwriting and a word on her palm that smelled of white fire.

"Well," she said finally, packing up her kit with hands that mostly obeyed her. "I expect the Guildmaster will want this report."

She paused. Looked down at the survey sheet, at the notation glowing faintly, patiently, like an ember deciding whether to become a fire.

"And I expect," she added, sliding it into the false bottom of her case instead of the official one, "that he will want it *very* much. Which is precisely why he isn't getting it."

Behind her, as she walked back toward the city, the frost-lines faded one by one — all except the last, which lingered in the shape, unmistakable to anyone who knew how to read ground, of a door.
