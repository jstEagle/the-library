## 6. Interrogation by Candlelight

The summons came at the hour when the guild's corridors smell of tallow and old paper, which is to say: always, but more so at night.

Cassian Vael read the order twice, because the first reading had to be wrong. *Watch the cartographer. Discreetly. Report anything anomalous.* Signed with Aurelian's small, precise hand, the letters so tidy they looked embarrassed to be part of a sentence at all.

*The cartographer.* Not Elodie. Not *Miss Whitlock*, which would have been worse, because the guild only used surnames when it was preparing to make someone into a case file. *The cartographer* was the language of surveillance, and Cassian had written enough surveillance reports to know exactly how they ended. They ended in candlelit rooms and closed doors and people who were never seen in the corridors again, though their desks were always kept tidy, because the guild was nothing if not sentimental about furniture.

He read it a third time. The words had not improved with age.

"You could at least pretend to be pleased," said Merrin, the duty clerk, hovering at his elbow with the particular joy of a man who enjoys delivering bad news to large people. "You've been asking for field work for months."

"I've been asking for *interesting* field work."

"Watching a suspected heretic isn't interesting?"

"It's *homework*," Cassian said. "It's being told to read a book I already know by heart and write an essay about it." He folded the order into his coat, which felt disturbingly like hiding a knife. "Besides, she isn't a heretic."

"Guildmaster thinks she might be."

"Guildmaster thinks the rain is suspicious until he's tested it for intent." Cassian pulled on his gloves. "Where am I posted?"

"Her rooms. Third landing, the old surveyor's wing. You're to observe comings and goings. Nothing more." Merrin paused, and his grin faded into something almost apologetic. "You know I have to log the assignment."

"I know."

"And you know that when you log your first report, it goes to the Second Chair before it goes anywhere else."

"I know that too, Merrin."

"Right." The clerk shuffled his papers. "Only — you looked like a man planning to forget both of those things, and I wanted it on the record that I reminded you. For the tribunal. If there's a tribunal."

"There's not going to be a tribunal."

"Of course not." Merrin beamed at him, the smile of a man who had survived three guildmasters by assuming there was always going to be a tribunal. "Lovely night for lurking."

* * *

The old surveyor's wing was the guild's attic in spirit if not in fact — the place where the institution kept the parts of itself it didn't look at often. The stairs creaked in a key of their own invention. The windows were tall and bad at their jobs, admitting draughts and moths in roughly equal measure. And at the end of the third landing, behind a door with a brass plate reading *CARTOGRAPHY — W. WHITLOCK* that someone had scratched out and rewritten as *E. WHITLOCK* in a hand Cassian knew as well as his own, Elodie Whitlock was working.

He could tell without looking through the gap under the door. There was a particular quality of silence that came from a person deep in a map — a held-breath silence, the silence of someone negotiating with the world's stubbornness — and Elodie's rooms had been making that sound for as long as he'd known her.

He had been given a storeroom across the landing with a view of her door, a chair, and a candle, which the requisition form had optimistically described as *surveillance provisions*. He sat in the chair for exactly as long as it took to realize that he was going to be a terrible spy, and then he got up and knocked.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape: he had been ordered to watch Elodie Whitlock for signs of heresy. And Cassian Vael had loved Elodie Whitlock since before either of them had titles, back when she was a surveyor's daughter who drew maps on the backs of ration chits and he was a gate-boy with a talent for standing very still and noticing things. He had loved her through the years of formal address and the years of less-than-formal address and the one catastrophic year of no address at all, and in all that time he had learned one thing with the certainty of bedrock:

If Elodie Whitlock was doing something the guild would call heresy, she was doing it because it was *true*, and she would do it alone, and she would not ask for help, and she would get caught.

He could watch that happen from a storeroom with bad provisions, or he could knock.

He knocked.

"Who is it?" Her voice, muffled, distracted, already annoyed at being interrupted.

"Your surveillance."

A pause. Then the lock turned, and there she was — ink to the second knuckle, a smudge of blue across her cheekbone like a bruise from a very polite fight, hair pinned up with what appeared to be a compass leg. She looked at him for a long moment.

"You're joking," she said.

"I wish I were. May I come in?"

"You're *my surveillance*?"

"Technically I'm the guild's surveillance, stationed at your door. The distinction seemed important to maintain."

"You knocked."

"I did."

"You're supposed to watch *discreetly*. Knocking is the single least discreet act available to a human being. Knocking is a *declaration*."

"I've never been good at my job," Cassian said, and stepped inside before she could decide whether to let him.

* * *

Her rooms were a cartographer's argument with the universe. Charts everywhere — pinned, stacked, half-rolled and propped against the wall like sleeping soldiers. A worktable buried under calipers and dividers and three separate lamps, because Elodie held the theological position that one could never have too much light or too many ways of measuring. On the corner of the desk, incongruous and domestic, a jar of last summer's sunlight — the famous trade, the one that had started all of this, glowing softly like a wasp caught in amber.

He looked at the jar a moment too long, and she noticed him noticing.

"It's just light," she said. "It's just light, Cassian."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because the guild seems to think it's a *symptom*." She dropped into her chair, arms crossed, and studied him with the flat assessing look she usually reserved for unreliable sources. "They sent you. Of all the knives in the drawer, they sent you."

"I'm not a knife."

"You're the sharpest thing they have and you know it."

"That's the dividers talking."

"The dividers," Elodie said, "have never once lied to me. Which is more than I can say for—" She stopped. Recalibrated. He watched her do it, watched the argument she'd been building get folded up and put away, and felt the old familiar ache of it, because Elodie when she was frightened got *precise*, and her precision had always been a wall with a door in it that only he had ever found, and tonight she was building the wall higher.

"Say it," he said quietly.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you were about to say about the guild. Say it to me. That's why I'm here. Not — " he gestured at the storeroom across the landing, visible through her window if you knew where to look — "not from *there*. From here. As myself."

She was quiet for a moment. The jar of sunlight threw faint gold across the charts, and for a heartbeat the whole room looked like summer, which was its own kind of lie.

"They burned a report," she said finally. "Mine. The prayer-ledger anomalies. I filed it properly, seals and all, and it came back to me as ash in the return tray. Someone read it and decided it was more dangerous to know than to not know. And then, three days later, a shrine in the lower city heals a dying child overnight and my *sigil* is on the wall in handwriting I've never seen." Her voice stayed level, which cost her; he could see the cost. "The guild thinks I did it. I think the guild knows something. And I think both of those things can be true, and I think that terrifies them, and I think it should."

Cassian sat down slowly on the edge of a chart cabinet. "Elodie—"

"Don't." She held up an ink-stained finger. "Don't do the thing where you warn me kindly. I know why you're here. Aurelian ordered you to watch me. You're going to tell me the guild suspects me, and you're going to tell me to be careful, and you're going to do it in that voice you use for delivering terrible news to people you love, and I am asking you — as a professional courtesy between two people who are very bad at their jobs tonight — to skip to the part where you say what you actually came to say."

"What I actually came to say," Cassian said, "is that they're going to arrest you."

The lamp flame leaned in the draught. Neither of them moved.

"Not tonight," he went on. "Not yet. There's a process — there's always a process, Aurelian loves the process like other men love their children. But the Second Chair opened a file on you. I've seen the file. It's thin. Thin files grow. And when it's thick enough, they'll come for you with the charter and the candles and the questions, and Elodie, I have sat in on those interrogations, and I am telling you that the guild does not ask questions to learn answers. It asks questions to teach you which answers are permitted."

"You could stop it." Her voice was very quiet now. "You could go to Aurelian tomorrow and tell him you've known me fifteen years and I am not a heretic, and he'd believe you, because you're the sharpest thing they have."

"He'd believe me right up until the evidence disagreed."

"Then you could lie."

"I don't lie to the guild." He said it simply, without apology, because it was simply true, the way gravity was true, and he watched her hear it — watched the door in the wall close, gently, the way she closed everything, without slamming.

"No," she said. "You don't. That's the whole tragedy of you, Cassian Vael. You are the most honest man I've ever met and you've given your honesty to an institution that *files it*."

"The guild keeps people safe."

"The guild keeps *itself* safe. There's a difference, and I map things for a living, so believe me when I tell you I can see the border." She stood, restless, and moved along the charts like a woman pacing the edge of her own argument. "Something is wrong. Prayers are being answered. Do you understand what that means? The old gods are sealed — everyone says so, it's the first thing they teach you, right after *don't draw doors that aren't there*. Sealed gods don't answer prayers. So either the sealing failed, or it never happened, or — " she stopped at the jar of sunlight and looked down into it — "or someone in this building has been lying to all of us for three hundred years, and I don't think it's me."

"Elodie."

"And you'll report all of this. Won't you. Every word. Because you don't lie to the guild."

The old intimacy rose between them like weather. It was always like this — the argument was never really about the guild, or the file, or the sigil on the shrine wall. It was about a night, years ago, on the roof of the archive, when she had asked him to run away with her and he had said *the guild is my home* and she had said *I could have been your home* and they had both been too young and too proud to notice that those two sentences could both be true at once.

"I would report that you are in danger," he said. "That's what I'll report. That the cartographer is being reckless, that she needs watching, that the file should grow slowly. I can slow a file, Elodie. I've done it before."

"You'd falsify pace but not content."

"I'd *manage* it."

"You'd manage me." She laughed, and it wasn't a kind laugh; it was the laugh she used on bad terrain. "Do you know what the worst part is? If our positions were turned, I'd do the same thing. I'd stand in this exact spot and make the same exact speech about duty, and I'd believe it, and I'd hate myself for it, and I'd do it anyway. We're *symmetrical*, Cassian. It's obscene."

"It's why we work."

"It's why we *don't* work. We keep choosing the same thing. Every time. The guild, the charter, the — the *silence*. And we stand on opposite sides of it calling it loyalty, like it's a virtue and not just — fear with better tailoring."

He should have argued. Some part of him was assembled entirely for arguing, a career's worth of it, and that part reached for the familiar tools — *the Veil protects us, the silence holds the world together, you of all people know what's under the maps* — and then he looked at her, ink-stained and lit gold by stolen summer, and he put the tools down.

"Here's what I know," he said instead. "Someone burned your report. Someone put your mark on a shrine. Someone is building a file with your name on it. And whatever is happening out there — the answered prayers, the anomalies — it's moving faster than the guild's process, and process is the only weapon they've given me." He stood. "So I'm going to tell you the one thing I can tell you that isn't in the file, and then I'm going to go back across that landing and be your surveillance, badly, and you're going to let me."

She waited.

"The coordinates in your anomaly report," he said. "The ones that resisted your survey. I've seen them before. Not in your report. In an older document — one I wasn't cleared to read, and read anyway, because I told you, I'm bad at my job. And Elodie — the guild has known about those coordinates for a very long time. Longer than the charter. Longer than the Veil. Whatever's there, we didn't find it." He met her eyes. "It's *asking to be found*."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere below them, the guild's great clock counted the hour with its usual indifference, and the draught came through the tall bad windows and made the charts stir like something breathing.

"That's not a warning," Elodie said at last. "That's a map reference."

"It's all I have."

"It's enough." She was already turning to her desk, already pulling the anomaly report toward her, already the cartographer again — and he understood, with the resignation of long experience, that he had just handed her a reason and she was going to follow it off the edge of every map they had. "Cassian. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm still going to report you."

"I know." And she smiled — the real one, the one that had survived fifteen years and one catastrophic year and every wall she'd ever built — "Report me *accurately*. That's all I'll ever ask of you."

He went back across the landing. He sat in the bad chair with the bad candle, and he opened his surveillance log, and he wrote, in his small precise hand: *Subject remains in her rooms. Engaged in routine work. Nothing anomalous to report.*

It was the first lie he had ever told the guild. He noticed that it did not feel like falling. It felt like a door.

Across the landing, behind the scratched-out name and the rewritten one, her lamp burned on — and beneath it, though he could not have known it yet, Elodie Whitlock was drawing the coordinates over and over, and the lines were beginning, very faintly, to draw themselves.

And out in the city, in the frost gathering on the lower roofs, something old had just said her name.
