## 9. The God Who Remembers Me

Elodie had prepared for the descent the way she prepared for everything: with lists.

*Boots, waxed.* Check. *Rope, sixty feet, guild-stamped.* Check. *Mother's atlas, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs like a second heartbeat.* Check. *Cassian's warning, delivered in the dark of her doorway with his hand shaking and his voice not:* *They know what you are looking for. If you go back to those coordinates, they will not ask questions first.*

That one she checked twice, and then, being honest with herself, a third time, before deciding — as she had decided every night for a week — that she would go anyway.

The trouble with being a careful person, she thought as she picked her way down through the frost-slicked undercity, was that care only ever told you *how much* trouble you were in. It never once, in her entire career at the Guild of Impossible Things, had told her how to get out of it.

The coordinates sat beneath the old tannery district, in a stretch of tunnel that appeared on no map she had ever drawn or inherited — which was itself an impossibility, because Elodie Whitlock had drawn maps of tunnels that did not exist yet, tunnels that would not be dug for another hundred years. The guild called this foresight. Her mother had called it *listening*. Elodie had never asked what the tunnels were saying, because she had been eleven years old and there had still been time for cowardice.

Now there wasn't.

The frost began where the last lamp died. Not the ordinary frost of winter — this was frost with *intent*, feathering across the brickwork in patterns that made her cartographer's fingers itch. She knelt and looked closer and felt her stomach drop with something between wonder and horror.

They were contour lines.

The ice was drawing elevation maps on the walls. Someone — something — down here understood survey notation better than half the guild's junior scribes, and it was *decorating*.

"Oh, that's showing off," Elodie muttered, and her breath came out silver, and the silver did not disperse. It hung in the air, curling, rearranging itself into a shape that was almost — almost — a word.

She did not wait to read it. She walked into the cold instead, because she had learned that much from the ledgers: the anomalies never came to you. You had to go stand exactly where the world refused to be measured, and then you had to be rude enough to knock.

* * *

The chamber at the heart of the resistance was round, which pleased some deep professional part of her, and impossible, which displeased all the rest of her. It was larger inside than the tunnel could possibly contain — she checked, twice, pacing the wall with her measuring chain and getting a different length each circuit, 40 feet, then 61, then something that made her sit down abruptly on a fallen block until the numbers stopped swimming.

And in the center, where any sane room would keep a well or a pillar, there was a door.

It stood free-standing, frame and all, opening onto nothing but more cold air and a darkness that was not the darkness of an unlit space but the darkness of a space choosing not to be seen. Frost crawled up its frame in the same contour patterns. The door itself was old oak, iron-banded, utterly ordinary — the kind of door found in ten thousand cellars across the city.

Elodie knew it immediately, the way you know your own handwriting.

Because she had drawn it. Three nights ago, in her sleep, in ink she didn't remember buying, on a page of her own sketchbook she didn't remember filling. The sketch was still there, folded against her heart next to the atlas. She took it out with numb fingers and held it up beside the door, and the proportions matched to the hair-width, and somewhere very far above her, three hundred years of guild doctrine quietly caught fire and burned to ash.

"Right," she said aloud, because talking to impossible things was in the manual, chapter one, right after *don't*. "I'm going to assume you can hear me. The frost maps suggest you can. I'm Elodie Whitlock, Journeyman Cartographer, Third Circle, Guild of Impossible Things, and I have some questions about—"

"*Liora.*"

The name went through her like a hook through cloth.

It was not loud. That was the worst of it. It was quiet, and low, and patient, the way water is patient with stone, and it came from everywhere — from the frost, from the dark beyond the door, from somewhere behind her own sternum, as if the word had been waiting in her chest all along and had merely been handed its cue.

"My name," Elodie said, and her voice cracked on the second word, "is Elodie."

There was a pause. When the voice came again, it sounded — and this was absurd, this was *impossible*, and she was beginning to suspect that was rather the point of everything lately — amused.

"You said that with the same stubbornness the first time, too. Three hundred years ago. You stood where you're standing and you said *my name is Liora* and you were wrong then as well, though it took you longer to admit it." A rustle, like wind through a winter orchard. "You've brought my chains' key pressed to your ribs. You always did carry too much. Come in, little cartographer. The cold out here is only my dreaming. In here, we can talk properly."

Every instinct Elodie owned — and she owned excellent instincts, professionally certified — screamed at her to run. She catalogued them, thanked them for their service, and stepped through the door.

* * *

Inside was a garden in winter.

Not dead — sleeping. Rows of pale stems bowed under frost-lace, trellises heavy with ice that glittered like cut glass, and overhead a sky that was not a sky but a ceiling painted with constellations she did not recognize, arranged in patterns that hurt slightly to look at, the way a foreign alphabet hurts when you almost know it. The air smelled of rosemary and snow and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed letter.

A woman stood among the frozen beds.

She was tall, and she was wrong in small ways that added up to enormous ones — her shadow fell toward the light instead of away; her eyes were the grey of a doorway left open onto night; when she turned, Elodie heard the sound of a latch lifting somewhere far off. She wore a gown the color of frost on brick, and she looked at Elodie the way a lighthouse looks at a ship it has been watching for a long, long time.

"Veyra," Elodie said, because her mother's marginalia had finally given her one name to hold, and holding names was how she kept her footing. "Goddess of thresholds. Doors, gates, crossings. The between-places."

"Flatterer," said Veyra. "You've done your reading."

"I do everyone's reading. It's the job." Elodie kept her voice level through sheer professional discipline. "You've been answering prayers. Through the Veil. Through bindings that three centuries of guild records say cannot be answered through. I want to know how."

"Do you?" Veyra drifted closer, and the frost on the nearest roses bloomed open, just a little, just enough. "Or do you want to know why the answers felt familiar? Why the ledger entries arrange themselves into shapes you almost recognize? Why, when you mapped my edge, your instruments drew a door you'd already dreamed?" She smiled, and it was a terrible, beautiful thing, grief wearing patience as a mask. "You want to know why you know me, Liora. Ask that one. It's the true question. You were always good at finding the true question under the polite one."

"Stop calling me that."

"Come and see," said Veyra, "why I call you that."

She extended one hand — and Elodie, who should have refused, who had a list of reasons in her breast pocket as long as her arm, took it.

* * *

The memory arrived the way weather arrives: all at once, from every direction, undeniable.

Snow. A hill above a city that was not this city, older, walled, its lamps lit gold against a gathering dark. And herself — not herself, Liora, but wearing her hands, her stubborn chin, her habit of gripping things too tightly — standing before a woman made of doors. Every step Veyra took opened onto somewhere else; her footsteps left thresholds hanging in the air behind her, little rectangles of elsewhere, and through them Elodie-saw-Liora glimpsed oceans, deserts, a market under three moons.

"You could stop them," Liora was saying, and her voice was Elodie's voice, worn and fierce. "You could open a door under their feet and end this war tonight."

"And tomorrow?" Veyra's voice, but younger somehow, less patient. "Doors open both ways, beloved. Always both ways. If I unmake their armies, what stops me from unmaking their cities when they offend me? What stops any of us? You know what we are. You've seen what we are."

"Yes," Liora said. "I've seen what you are." And she reached up and touched Veyra's face with a tenderness that hit present-day Elodie like a physical blow, because it was *her* tenderness, her exact tenderness, the one she had never once spent on anything but maps. "That's why they sent me. And that's why I came. Not for them. For you. Let me help you make the chains, Veyra. Let me make them gentle. Let me make them *fair*. Because if you don't let someone who loves you hold the hammer, someone who hates you will."

"And the price?"

Liora smiled, and Elodie felt her own mouth make the shape of it, helpless. "You already know the price. You've known since the day you taught me to read star-maps. I'll pay it. I'll pay it as many times as it takes."

The memory folded shut like a book.

* * *

Elodie came back to herself sitting on the frozen ground of an impossible garden, with frost on her lashes and tears freezing on her cheeks and Veyra crouched before her, watching with an expression that on a human face would have been called hope and on a goddess's face was probably something with a much longer name.

"No," Elodie said.

"It was three hundred years ago—"

"*No.*" She pushed herself upright, and her legs shook, and she let them, because pretending otherwise would take energy she needed for fury. "I am not — I don't care what I was. I am Elodie Whitlock. I draw maps. I file reports. I complain about the quality of guild ink. I am *not* some — some ancient lover of yours, I am not a reincarnation, I am not a *linchpin*, I am a person, I am thirty-one years old and I have a favorite teacup—"

"A blue one with the chipped rim," Veyra said softly, "that you hide at the back of the cupboard so Cassian doesn't use it."

Elodie stopped.

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere above, the false constellations wheeled.

"That proves nothing," she managed. "Everyone has a chipped cup."

"Liora had a chipped cup." Veyra rose, unhurried, frost blooming and dying around her hem with each step. "Blue. Chipped on the left. She hid it so the other mappers wouldn't use it. Some things, little cartographer, are not memory. They are *shape*. The soul goes round and round the wheel, and the wheel wears grooves, and you are the groove wearing a person today." She paused by a frozen rose tree and touched one bud, and it opened, impossibly, impossibly alive in all that cold. "I do not say this to take you from yourself. Hear me. I have watched you for a year now — since the bindings thinned enough for me to see through the cracks. You are not her. You are new. You are *yours*. But the knot they tied uses her shape, and so it uses yours, and that is why I called, and that is why you came, and that is why—" her voice frayed, just barely, just at the edge, "—that is why I have been alone in the dark for three hundred years with only the sound of your name to keep me company."

Something in Elodie's chest did a thing she refused to examine.

"You're furious," she said instead, grasping for solid ground. "I can feel it. Underneath all the — the frost and the flowers. You're furious."

"I am *anciently* furious," Veyra agreed, and for one moment the garden shuddered, and every door in the air stood open onto burning places, and Elodie saw what answered prayers unevenly, vindictively, what the winter was feeding on. Then it closed again, banked, controlled, costing its keeper visibly. "Yes. I am. Your guild chained me and mine in the dark and called it peace, and mortals prayed to us for three hundred years into a silence, and now the silence is cracking and what leaks through is *me*, half-waking, dreaming sideways, answering badly because I am answering *blindfolded*." She turned, and her doorway-grey eyes fixed on Elodie with terrible gentleness. "I do not want your fear, Liora-who-is-not-Liora. I want your craft. You come from a long line of women who bound gods — your mother forged these very chains, did your marginalia tell you that yet? — and I am asking you to be the first of your line to bind us *well*."

"Bind you? I thought you wanted freedom."

"I want the war to not happen." For the first time, Veyra sounded tired — genuinely, enormously tired, three centuries of tired compressed into one syllable. "But my siblings are waking too, and they are not me, and they will not ask nicely, and your guild's answer will be to seal the Veil and let your city freeze rather than admit what it did. So yes. Freedom, eventually. But between here and there stands a choice, and you are standing in the middle of it, because you have always stood in the middle of things. It's your best quality." The ghost of the old smile. "It's also how you died the first time."

The cold went through Elodie like a bell being struck.

"Died," she repeated.

"The linchpin does not survive the unbinding, beloved. It never has. There is a reason your mother left instructions written in a language only a Whitlock reads, and a reason she wrote them in *map notation* — the language of people who understand that every border drawn is a border paid for." Veyra stepped back, and the garden began, gently, terribly, to fold itself away like a letter being returned to its envelope. "Go home. Read the rest of her notes. Argue with her ghost; she deserves it. And when you come back — and you will come back, you always come back — bring the enforcer."

"Cassian?" Elodie's heart did something complicated. "He's sworn to the silence. He'd arrest me on sight if he knew I was here."

"Mm," said Veyra, as the last of the garden dissolved into contour lines of frost. "Three hundred years ago, there was a soldier who said the same thing about a stubborn mapmaker. Ask him about his grandmother's ring sometime. The one he never takes off. The one engraved with a compass rose."

The door closed.

Elodie stood alone in the freezing tunnel, holding a sketch of a door she had dreamed, with frost melting on her lashes and her whole self trembling like a plumb line in wind.

Then she took out her notebook, because she was her mother's daughter and her own person and the groove wearing a shape, all three at once, and she wrote at the top of a fresh page, in her own unmistakable hand:

*Cassian. Grandmother's ring. Tonight.*

And underneath, smaller, in the notation only a Whitlock could read, the beginnings of a map of a place she had not yet been — and at its center, a garden.
