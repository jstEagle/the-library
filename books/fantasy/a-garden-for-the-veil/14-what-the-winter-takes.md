## 14. What the Winter Takes

Osk had been a scribe for eleven years before they took his pen away, and in that time he had learned one truth above all others: the difference between a miracle and a disaster is mostly a matter of bookkeeping.

He was thinking about this now, standing in the middle of Candlewick Row with his breath freezing in his beard and a dead man's ledger under his arm, because Candlewick Row was the most miraculous street in the lower city and it was also, as of three days ago, the coldest.

The row had been blessed—there was no other word for it. Every hearth on the street burned without fuel. The baker's ovens ran hot through the night without coal or wood. Old Marta's rheumatism had vanished; the cooper's son's fevered lung had cleared like a window wiped clean. The people of Candlewick called it the Kindness, and they left offerings of bread and ribbon at their doorsteps the way you might tip a generous stranger.

And then, last week, the Kindness had turned its face away.

Osk walked the length of the row and counted. He was good at counting; it was the first thing they taught you in the guild, before they taught you to lie beautifully. Forty-one houses. Thirty-eight of them cold as tombs. The fires hadn't gone out—they had simply *left*, the way a guest leaves a party when someone more interesting arrives. And the warmth had gone somewhere.

Three doors down, the Ferro family's house glowed like a lantern. All of them were inside it—the whole extended clan, plus two neighbors, plus a goat. The hearth there burned so hot the windows sweated. Mrs. Ferro stood in her doorway handing out blankets with the bewildered expression of a woman who has won a lottery she doesn't remember entering.

"Master Osk," she said, pressing a blanket into his hands before he could refuse. "Take it. We've more than we can use. It's—it keeps coming. I stoke the fire once in the morning and by evening it's roaring again and I swear on my mother's grave I haven't touched it."

"Where did it come from?" Osk asked. "The fire. When did it start?"

She frowned. "Months ago? Around when—" She stopped. Around when the prayers started being answered, Osk thought. Around when the world began tilting. Everyone in the lower city dated things that way now: *before the answers* and *after*.

"And the other houses?" he asked gently. "The cold ones."

Mrs. Ferro's face did something complicated. "We take them in," she said. "We're taking everyone in. But Master Osk—" and here her voice dropped, and Osk heard the thing underneath the gratitude, the thing he'd been hearing all week in a dozen voices, "—the Kettleman baby died Tuesday. Right across the street. In a house that was warm in October. What kind of kindness takes from a baby?"

What kind indeed. Osk thanked her, tucked the blanket under his arm—he was not too proud to accept a blanket; pride was for men with heating—and made his way out of Candlewick Row toward the part of the city no miracle had ever visited.

* * *

The Rookery was the neighborhood the maps forgot, which in Osk's experience meant the neighborhood the maps' *makers* had chosen to forget. He'd drawn its streets himself once, years ago, in an atlas the guild had declined to publish. Too poor to matter, Guildmaster Aurelian had said, which was a strange thing for a man to say about four thousand living souls, but Aurelian said many strange things in the tone of a man stating the weather.

Here was the thing that kept Osk awake at night, the thing he'd been carrying around like a stone in his boot for six days:

The Rookery was warm.

Not miraculously warm. Not hearths-that-never-die warm. Just—warm. Ordinary winter, the kind you could survive with a coat and a neighbor. No frozen pipes, no dead babies, no frost climbing the walls like ivy. While Candlewick Row, drenched in answered prayer, buried its children, the forgotten slum where nobody prayed to anything but luck muddled through the way slums always had: badly, but together.

Osk sat in the back room of the Broken Compass—a tavern whose name he appreciated professionally—and spread his evidence across a table scarred by forty years of elbows. He had ledgers. He had temperature notes taken with a thermometer he'd stolen from the guild's survey office, because if they were going to defrock him they could at least furnish his research. He had a map of the city with red ink crawling over the miracle districts and blue ink pooling everywhere else, and the pattern was not random. It was not even subtle.

It looked like a hand dividing a pie. Unevenly. Spitefully.

"You look terrible," said Elodie Whitlock, sliding into the seat across from him with her hood up and her eyes doing that thing they did—cataloguing everything, filing everything, missing nothing. She glanced at the map and went very still. "That's Candlewick."

"That's Candlewick." He tapped the blue. "And that's the Rookery. Guess which one has a mortality rate."

"I've seen the lists." Her voice was flat in the way he'd learned meant grief held at arm's length for later. "Nineteen dead this week. Mostly in the blessed districts."

"Mostly *exclusively*," Osk corrected. "Which brings us to the interesting question, doesn't it? Because here's what the guild will tell you: the gods are waking, the Veil is thinning, miracles are leaking through, isn't it wonderful, isn't it terrible, isn't it *natural*. Weather. Tides. Random grace." He leaned forward. "But grace isn't random, Elodie. Grace has *preferences*. Look at the pattern. Warmth flows toward the loud prayers and away from—"

"From whom?"

"From the ones who stopped praying." He opened the dead man's ledger—the Kettleman ledger, recovered from a house where a baby had frozen while the street around him glowed. "Tomas Kettleman. Cooper. Decent man, mediocre father, excellent drunk. Last autumn his daughter took sick and he prayed at the shrine on Tanner's Lane—loudly, publicly, the whole performance. And she was healed. Overnight. Miracle, right? Except." He turned pages. "Kettleman was also a moneylender on the side. Small loans, brutal terms. And every person he'd ever squeezed—every single one—lived within fifty yards of his house. And every single one of those houses went cold in the same week."

Elodie stared at him. "You're saying the god read his debts."

"I'm saying somebody did." Osk sat back. "I spent eleven years in the guild archives, Elodie. I read the founding charter. I read the *corrections* to the founding charter. Do you know what the old faiths actually believed about Veyra? Before the guild sanitized her into 'goddess of thresholds and gentle passage'? She was the goddess of thresholds, yes. Also of tolls. Also of *turning people away*. The threshold is where you decide who gets in. That was her whole portfolio. Mercy was her sister's job, and her sister's name isn't written anywhere anymore, and I have a theory about why."

The tavern noise seemed to recede. Elodie pulled the map closer, tracing the red and blue with one finger, and Osk watched her do the arithmetic he'd already done—the same arithmetic, arriving at the same terrible sum.

"The chains are weakening," she said slowly. "So what's coming through isn't... diluted anymore."

"What's coming through is *concentrated*. Three hundred years under the Veil, Elodie. Imagine being locked in a cellar for three centuries and then handed a list of everyone who ever wronged you. What would you do?" He didn't wait for her answer. "I'll tell you what I'd do. I'd be *petty*. Petty on a geological scale."

"That's not fair," Elodie said, and then, hearing herself, laughed—a short, unhappy sound. "Listen to me. Defending her. A baby froze, Osk."

"A baby froze because a god is keeping a grudge against a moneylender, yes. And here's the part that should keep you up nights: the guild's answer is to seal the Veil forever. Aurelian's council voted—I have friends who still talk to me, defrocked or no. Permanent seal. Which means the winter stays, the miracles stop, and the poor of this city freeze in the dark so that four hundred guild clerks can keep their invisibility." He tapped the map again. "But the other answer—the one you've been circling for weeks, don't think I haven't noticed the way you flinch at shrines—is to open the cage entirely. Full freedom. Every god, all at once, after three hundred years of cellar."

"And you came here to tell me not to."

"I came here to tell you to *count first*." Osk pulled a folded paper from his coat—his last piece of evidence, the one he'd saved, the one that had cost him two favors and a black eye. "Because Veyra isn't the only one awake. This is a prayer transcript. Raw, unfiltered, collected off a shrine-stone in the Saltmarket by a friend of mine. Listen to the phrasing."

Elodie unfolded it. Read it. Osk watched her face go through recognition, denial, and arrival in the space of four seconds, which was, in his professional opinion, very fast work.

"'*The debt is owed,*'" she read aloud, barely above a whisper. "'*The debt is owed and the door remembers.*' That's not Veyra's voice."

"No. Veyra speaks in thresholds and turning. This one speaks in *accounts*." He met her eyes. "There's something else down there, Elodie. Something that thinks in ledgers. And whatever your goddess-friend told you about freedom and fairness—she is one voice in a chorus, and the rest of the chorus has been singing to itself in the dark for three hundred years, and I promise you the song is not a lullaby."

For a long moment Elodie said nothing. Outside, the wind found the gap under the tavern door and breathed through it, a thin cold whistle, and both of them glanced at it the way people had started glancing at drafts lately—as if the weather might be listening. As if it might have opinions.

"My mother helped forge the chains," Elodie said at last.

"I know."

"She left instructions for undoing them. Hidden in notation only a Whitlock could read." Her finger rested on the map, on the blue stain of the Rookery, on the red bloom of Candlewick. "And I've been assuming the choice was between the cage and the open door. Cage or freedom. Keep or break." She looked up, and there was something in her expression that Osk recognized from the archive days—from the young scribes who read too much and slept too little and arrived at terrible clarity at odd hours. "But you're telling me the door opens onto a room full of creditors."

"I'm telling you," Osk said, "that whoever wrote your mother's instructions understood exactly what was behind the Veil. And she still wrote them. So either she was a monster, or—" he shrugged, "—or she knew something about the chains that the chains' own makers didn't. Or she knew something about *you*."

The wind whistled again. Somewhere out in the dark, in a glowing house on a freezing street, a family was giving thanks for a fire that had chosen them, and three doors down a woman was wrapping her dead neighbor's baby for burial, and beneath it all—beneath the cobbles and the cellars and the mapped and unmapped earth—something old was keeping accounts, and the accounts were coming due.

Elodie folded the prayer transcript with careful, cartographer's hands, and put it in her coat, next to her heart, where she kept everything that frightened her.

"Saltmarket," she said. "Your friend who collects off the shrine-stones. I need to see the stone itself."

"Why?"

"Because," Elodie Whitlock said, with the particular calm that Osk had learned preceded either great scholarship or great catastrophe, "if the lock is answering prayers, then the lock has developed preferences. And if the lock has preferences—" she stood, pulling up her hood, "—then it can be *negotiated with*. And nobody negotiates with a door until they know which side of it they're standing on."

Osk drained his cup, gathered his ledgers, and followed her out into the cold—toward the Saltmarket, toward the shrine, toward whatever was waking beneath the city counting its grievances like coins.

He only hoped, as the snow began to fall, that they would arrive before the account settled.
