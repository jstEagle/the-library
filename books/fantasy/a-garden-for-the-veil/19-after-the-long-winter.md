## 19. After the Long Winter

Spring came to the city the way a drunk comes home—late, apologetic, and insisting it had been there all along.

Cassian stood at his window on the fourth morning of Thaw-Month and watched it happen. The snow didn't melt so much as surrender, in patches, like garrisons abandoning hopeless positions. A crocus had forced its way up through the cobbles outside the guildhouse door, which was against every rule of masonry and botany both, and he found himself absurdly proud of it.

*Wrong-footed and real*, Osk had called it, when Cassian mentioned the crocus. "The old gods are doing the seasons by hand now, you understand. No more clockwork winter. They're learning. We're learning. Everyone's fumbling." He'd said it with the cheer of a man who had spent twenty years being defrocked for saying true things and was finally being paid for it.

Cassian pulled on his coat—which was no longer an enforcer's coat, because there were no enforcers anymore, only *liaisons*, a word chosen by committee and hated by everyone—and went down to see what the world had broken overnight.

* * *

The guildhouse was gutted. That was the honest word for it. Half the eastern stacks had been torn open to air out three centuries of secrets, and the smell of old paper and older lies drifted through the corridors like incense at a funeral nobody had wanted to hold.

Osk ran the place now, or rather refused to run it while running it entirely, in the manner of a man who insisted he was merely holding the tiller until someone better came along and who had quietly drowned all the candidates. The council chamber had been renamed the Negotiation Hall, the chairs had been arranged in a circle instead of a crescent—"so no one sits at the head, Cassian, it's *symbolic*"—and the charter itself hung framed on the wall with amendments scrawled across it in red ink like wounds that were healing.

"Read me the morning list," Osk said, not looking up from a table buried under treaty drafts. He was a small man made mostly of ink stains and grievance, and he had never once in Cassian's presence used anyone's title, including his own.

"The Riverfolk want the flood-god's blessing formalized before planting season. The hearth-keepers in Ashen Row are petitioning for a standing arrangement with whatever entity kept their fires lit through the winter—they don't know her name, they just call her the Warm One. And the delegation from the glassmakers' guild arrived at dawn demanding to know why their kilns have been producing perfect crystal without fuel."

"Ah." Osk looked up. "That'll be Serrin. God of small craft. Terrible show-off. Tell them it's a gift and gifts come with obligations—they'll want to leave an offering. Something beautiful, not something expensive. He can tell the difference."

"You've met him?"

"I've met four of them this month." Osk rubbed his eyes with an ink-stained thumb. "They keep coming to the *door*, Cassian. Knocking. Do you understand what that means? Three hundred years we built walls, and now they knock, and they bring complaints, like neighbors. Yesterday the rain-god sent a formal objection about drainage in the lower terraces. An *objection*. With a seal."

"What did you do?"

"I apologized," Osk said, with the expression of a man still astonished to be alive, "and fixed the drainage."

Cassian allowed himself the smallest smile. It was strange, how much of the new world was just this: paperwork conducted politely with powers that could unmake cities. The first god-treaty—the *Accord of Thresholds*, named, over Elodie's—over *someone's* objections, after the goddess herself—was two months old and already thicker than a bible and twice as argued-over. Every miracle now had a price, posted openly. Every prayer had a channel. Every god had a name, a jurisdiction, and a representative who could be written to, which the citizens did, constantly, at length, about everything.

It was working. That was the unbearable part. It was actually working.

"There's one more thing," Cassian said. "The ledger."

Osk's pen stopped moving.

* * *

The rewritten ledger lived in what had been the Founder's vault and was now, per another committee decision, the Public Record Room, though the public had not yet been permitted to record anything in it. Three hundred years of entries had been transcribed, amended, annotated. Where the old ledgers had recorded miracles stolen and prayers suppressed, the new one recorded agreements made and prices paid.

Except for one page.

Cassian had found it six weeks ago, on the night the transcription finished, and he had told no one, and he had come back eleven times since.

The page sat between the entry for the Unbinding—itself a masterpiece of bureaucratic understatement, reading in full: *"Binding renegotiated. Terms attached. Cost: see attached"*—and the first entry of the new era. It was blank. It should have been blank. Every other trace of the woman who had redrawn the chains had been erased from the world as thoroughly as chalk from a wet slate. The guild's memory-workings had taken her out of the records, out of the rolls, out of the minds of everyone who had ever known her. Aurelian himself, when they'd dragged him in front of the reformed council, had been asked if he remembered the cartographer Whitlock, and had answered, honestly, "Should I?"—and that answer had done more damage to the old regime than any trial.

But this page had writing on it. Faint, in a hand Cassian knew better than his own heartbeat, though the ink sat strangely on the vellum, as if it had been written from underneath.

*A map fragment.* Coordinates he didn't recognize. A little cartographic flourish—a curl of line like a vine, like a gate, like a garden trellis—and beneath it, in letters so small he had to hold the page to the light:

*tend well*

He had shown it to no one. Not Osk, who would have convened a committee. Not the archivists, who would have scraped it or sealed it or explained it. Because here was the thing Cassian had worked out alone, in the dark, eleven visits running: the erasure had taken her from everyone. From the ledgers, from the memories, from the very grammar of the guild's history. But the workings had been built to erase her *from others*. They had never accounted for someone choosing, deliberately, stubbornly, against all sense, to remember anyway.

Memory, it seemed, could be kept like a fire is kept—fed, tended, defended against the wind.

He touched the edge of the page, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping thing you're afraid to wake.

"Tend well," he said aloud, to the empty room. His voice sounded foolish in it. He said it anyway.

* * *

He walked home through the city, because walking was the only part of the day that still made sense.

Spring was doing its wrong-footed work everywhere. In Tanner's Square, the fountain had frozen solid in the shade while the sunny half ran free, so that the water seemed to pour directly into ice—a trick that delighted children and infuriated engineers. A flower-seller was arguing cheerfully with a patch of air about whether tulips constituted a sufficient offering for the local field-spirit; the tulips, Cassian noted, were blooming faster as she haggled, which suggested the spirit was flattered. Two doors down, a baker had left a loaf on his windowsill labeled FOR THE WINTER THAT WAS, THANKS AND NO HARD FEELINGS, and by the time Cassian passed, the loaf was gone and the crust crumbs had arranged themselves, briefly and unmistakably, into a smile.

The city had learned to pray the way it learned everything: badly, loudly, with tremendous confidence. There were prayer-boxes on corners now, official ones, with posted rates. There were also, inevitably, unofficial ones—chalk circles behind taverns where you could petition minor spirits for love or luck at cards, quality variable, refunds impossible. Osk's reformers had given up shutting them down and started licensing them.

Cassian passed the shrine on Candle Lane and stopped, as he always stopped, though there was nothing to see.

It was a plain alcove, whitewashed, with a single candle stub and a stone bench worn smooth by sitting. Before the Unbinding it had been a dead shrine, one of hundreds in the lower city, dedicated to gods whose names had been scrubbed so long ago even the scrubbing was forgotten. Now it belonged to Veyra, goddess of thresholds, first signatory of the Accord, and people came to sit on the bench when they were between things—between jobs, between griefs, between lives—and ask for safe passage across.

There was no marker for anyone else. No plaque. No name.

He knew, in the abstract, that he had loved someone. That was the shape the erasure had left in him—not a wound but a *socket*, a place where something had fit so perfectly that its absence had a presence of its own. He caught himself buying two of things. He woke some mornings with a word on his lips—*Liora*, though he didn't know why, it meant nothing, it was nobody. He walked certain streets and his chest tightened around nothing, grief with no object, mourning with no grave, love with no beloved, and the whole city moved around him, warm and spring-struck and utterly innocent of what it had cost.

Once, drunk on cheap wine after the Accord's first signing, he had asked Veyra herself—she had attended in the form of a woman made of doorways, which was exactly as disorienting as it sounds—whether she remembered the one who unmade the knot.

The goddess had looked at him for a long time. Doors had opened and closed in her face, and in each one he had glimpsed a different weather.

"She asked me not to say," Veyra had replied, gently, which was worse than no.

* * *

The liaison offices occupied what had been the enforcers' wing, and the irony was not lost on anyone. Cassian shared a room with a former smuggler named Petra who had joined the reform during the amnesty and who possessed, in his professional opinion, an unhealthy enthusiasm for procedure.

"Three items," she said, when he came in. She counted on fingers scarred from rope-burn. "One: the Riverfolk delegation is back, they've brought a lawyer, gods help us all. Two: the glassmakers accepted the offering terms and Serrin has apparently blessed their entire quarter, their windows are now self-cleaning, the neighboring quarters are furious. Three—" she hesitated, and something in her hesitation made him look up, "—there's a woman downstairs asking for you. Won't give a name. Says she'll only talk to the liaison who handled the Candle Lane shrine file."

His heart did something complicated and unauthorized.

"Describe her."

"That's the odd bit. Nobody can. I sent a clerk down and he came back saying he'd spoken to a very helpful person but couldn't recall a single detail. Juno swears it's an old woman. The porter says a child. Whoever she is, she's wearing the Veil's old tricks like a coat." Petra frowned. "Cassian? You've gone the color of treaty vellum."

"I'll take it," he said, and was already moving.

* * *

She was waiting in the ground-floor corridor, in the niche by the window where the light came through old glass the color of weak tea, and she was—of course—impossible to describe. Tall, short. Young, old. His eyes slid off her the way rain slides off oiled cloth, and yet underneath the sliding, underneath the impossibility, something in him recognized her with a violence that made his hands shake.

Frost-lashes. He didn't know why those words came. Frost on dark lashes, a voice calling a name that wasn't hers across a frozen field, a palm with a word inked on it—

"Hello, Cassian," the woman said.

"You know my name."

"I know rather a lot of your names. The one you use. The one you'll use later. The one you almost used, on a night in the stacks, and didn't." She smiled, and the smile was kind and ancient and slightly sad. "I'm told I owe you a debt. I disagree. I think I owe you a *thank-you*, which is different, and rarer, and worth more."

"Who are you?"

"A friend of the gardener," she said.

The word went through him like a hook through cloth. *Gardener.* He had never heard it before and it landed in that socket in his chest and fit, perfectly, horribly, the way a key fits a lock that has been waiting.

"I don't—" he started.

"No," she agreed. "You don't. That was the price, and she paid it gladly, and the terms were fair, and I honor fair terms above all things—I signed a treaty about it, you may have heard." She stepped closer, and the light through the tea-colored glass bent toward her as if listening. "But treaties have clauses, Cassian, and bargains have seams, and seams can be worked loose by patient hands. She is not gone. She is *elsewhere*—somewhere the erasure couldn't reach, because she wrote the erasure herself and left a door in it. She was always good at doors."

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere above, the glassmakers' blessed windows gleamed, and the river ran unfrozen, and the crocus by the front door held its impossible ground.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the treaty needs tending," the woman said, "and tending requires gardeners, and gardeners require—" she paused, savoring it, an immortal enjoying a punchline three centuries in the making, "—*visitors*. And because you have been keeping a page alive in that ledger for eleven weeks through sheer bloody-minded refusal to forget, and in my professional opinion, that deserves a reward."

She pressed something into his hand—small, folded, warm as a sunlit stone despite the chill of the corridor—and when he looked down at his palm, she was gone, and the clerk at the end of the hall blinked and said, "Were you talking to someone, sir?"

Cassian unfolded the paper with fingers that would not quite obey.

A map fragment. Coordinates he didn't recognize. A curl of line like a vine, like a gate, like a trellis waiting for something to grow. And beneath it, in a hand he knew better than his own heartbeat:

*tend well*

He read it twice. Then he laughed—one rough, cracked sound, the first real laugh since the winter—and wiped his eyes with the back of his hand like a man arguing with the weather, and went to find his coat.

Petra caught him at the stairs. "Where are you going? The Riverfolk lawyer—"

"Reschedule it."

"It's the *flood-god's* counsel, Cassian, you can't just—"

"Tell them," he said, taking the steps two at a time, the map burning in his pocket like a coal, like a promise, like a door, "that I've gone to tend something."
