## 20. The Blank Page

The rewritten ledger smelled of new vellum and old lies.

Cassian had been staring at it for the better part of an hour, which was, by his own accounting, fifty minutes longer than any sensible man should stare at a book. But then, he had never claimed to be sensible. Sensible men did not forge their way out of a guild that had erased them. Sensible men did not carry stolen atlas pages sewn into the lining of a coat that still bore the burn mark from the Council of Ash. Sensible men certainly did not sit in a rented room above a chandler's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, drinking tea gone cold because they kept forgetting to drink it, waiting for a miracle to make sense.

He turned the page again.

The ledger was Osk's doing—Osk and his reformers, who had spent the three months since the Unbinding prying open every sealed record in the guild like children cracking walnuts at midwinter. The old ledgers had been burned or, where burning seemed wasteful, copied out anew: clean pages, honest columns, entries written in ink that dried when it was supposed to. *Prayer received. Answer granted. Price paid.* The gods were answerable now. The arithmetic of miracles was public. Somewhere in the lower city, a woman who had prayed for her son's fever to break knew exactly what her hearth-fire would owe for it, and paid in warmth she could spare.

It was, Cassian admitted, a better world than the one he'd sworn to defend.

So why did the blank page bother him?

It was near the back of the volume, past all the tidy entries, past the treaty annotations in Osk's cramped hand. A single page left empty—not ruled off, not marked *reserved*, just blank, the way a page is blank when someone meant to write on it and then thought better of it. Except.

Except there was writing on it.

Faint. Faint as breath on glass. And in a hand Cassian knew better than his own heartbeat, because he had spent years forging guild seals and twice as long learning to read other people's intentions, and neither skill had ever felt like this.

*Elodie's hand.*

But Elodie Whitlock did not exist.

That was the trouble with what she had done. He understood it the way you understand a bridge after you've fallen off it—thoroughly, painfully, too late. She had redrawn the binding itself, stitched mortal consent into the chains, made the gods answerable. And the act had required a linchpin. Liora's soul-knot had held the old binding together; unmaking it meant unmaking her, root and record and memory alike. The guild's ledgers no longer contained her name. The registry of cartographers showed a gap where her apprenticeship should have been, a gap so smooth it looked intentional, the way a well-made lie looks true. Aurelian—who had survived the reformers' purge by the simple expedient of being useful and very sorry—had looked straight through Cassian the day after the Unbinding and asked him why he kept setting two places at breakfast.

"You eat alone," Aurelian had said, not unkindly. "You've always eaten alone."

"I know," Cassian had said, and had not believed a word of it.

He set down the cold tea and pulled the lamp closer. The faint marks on the blank page resolved as he tilted them into the light: lines. Contour lines. A hachure here, a benchmark symbol there—the private shorthand of the Guild of Impossible Things' mapping corps, the notation only a surveyor would know. And beneath the contours, small and slanted and unmistakably hers:

*A garden wants planting where the ground remembers. Follow the water that runs uphill.*

No name. No signature. Just that, and a map fragment—a fragment, he saw now, of the threshold coordinates, the door she had mapped into being and walked down with him at her side. Except the fragment didn't match the city maps anymore. After the Unbinding, the whole district around the chain-heart had shifted, streets rearranging themselves with the placid indifference of a cat settling into a new spot. Every map in the guild was wrong now. Osk's people were re-surveying from scratch, grumbling about it the way scribes grumble, which is to say constantly and at length.

Cassian traced the fragment with one finger. It led nowhere he knew. That was the point, he suspected. It led somewhere only she would know, and she had left it in the one place she could be sure he would look.

Because he was the only one who could see it.

He had tested this. Of course he had tested this—he had been an enforcer for eleven years; testing things until they broke was most of the job. He had shown the page to Osk, who had seen a blank leaf and suggested, gently, that grief took strange shapes and perhaps Cassian should sleep more. He had shown it to a junior scribe named Pell, who had a gift for spotting hidden text and who had held the page to candlelight, candlelight from behind, candlelight through wineglass (Pell's idea), and pronounced it "clean as a whistle, sir, though if you're seeing words on it I'd ease off the tea." He had even brought it to Veyra—or as close to Veyra as one could bring anything these days, which was to say he had stood at the garden gate on Wren Street where the goddess now kept a modest shrine shaped like a doorway, and asked the air whether she had done this.

The air had smelled, briefly, of frost and woodsmoke, and a voice like a bell rung underwater had said: *I did not. Nor would I need to. Some things are written where erasure cannot reach. You are holding proof, little enforcer. Be careful what you do with it.*

And then, after a pause, in a tone that was almost fond: *She always said you were stubborn enough to be useful.*

That had been six days ago. Since then Cassian had done what he always did with an impossible thing: he had catalogued it, cross-referenced it, and refused to let it go.

*Memory can be chosen rather than given.*

He wrote that in his notebook, underlined it twice, and then sat back and laughed at himself—a short, rusty sound, the laugh of a man who has forgotten how. Because that sentence wasn't philosophy. It was a confession. His memory of her was not given. Everyone else's had been taken, smoothed away like a thumbprint wiped from wet clay. His remained because—because of what? He hadn't done anything at the Unbinding except stand at the top of those stairs and hold the lantern while she worked, and argue, uselessly, beautifully, for mercy over justice, and watch her choose a third thing entirely. He hadn't cast a spell. He hadn't paid a price. Unless—

Unless choosing counted. Unless love, stubbornly maintained against the explicit mechanics of erasure, was itself a kind of craft. A cartography of one. A single map, drawn by a single hand, that the world's rewriting couldn't find because it wasn't stored anywhere the world could reach. It was stored in him.

"Stubborn enough to be useful," he told the empty room.

The room, being a room above a chandler's shop, declined to comment, but the tallow candles next door gave off a smell like honey and regret, and Cassian chose to take that as agreement.

* * *

He went to see Osk the next morning, because if he was going to do something foolish he preferred to do it with paperwork.

The reformer's office was everything the old guildmaster's chambers had not been: small, drafty, cluttered with petitions from citizens asking the gods perfectly reasonable questions (*Dear honored powers: is it permitted to pray for rain on my neighbor's field specifically, and if so what is the rate?*). Osk sat behind a desk built for a much larger man, looking like a sparrow nesting in a cathedral, and greeted Cassian with the wariness of a man who had learned that enforcers bearing questions were more dangerous than enforcers bearing warrants.

"You're not guild anymore," Osk reminded him, for perhaps the fortieth time. It had become almost affectionate.

"No," Cassian agreed. "I'm worse. I'm a private citizen with a grievance."

"Those we have a form for." Osk rummaged in a drawer, produced a form, and slid it across. "Name of aggrieved party?"

"Cassian Vael."

"Nature of grievance?"

"The world has lost something and won't admit it."

Osk stopped rummaging. He looked up, and for a moment the defrocked-scribe shrewdness fell away and Cassian saw the man underneath—the one who had run prayers past the Veil in bread carts because someone had to, consequences be damned. "This is about her," Osk said quietly. "The one from your report. The one who isn't in any record."

"There is no report. There's no her. Ask anyone."

"I'm asking you."

Cassian laid the ledger on the desk, open to the blank page. "Tell me what you see."

Osk bent over it. His lips moved slightly—he still mouthed words when reading, an old scribe habit he'd never bothered to kill—and then his brow creased, and then he said, with the flat certainty of a man reporting weather: "Blank. Ruled for entry, never used. Why?"

"And if I told you there's a map on it? In a dead woman's handwriting? Leading to coordinates that don't exist on any current survey?"

"I'd say you've either found the most important document in the guild's history or you've finally cracked like old glaze." Osk straightened. "Which is it?"

"That's the grievance," Cassian said. "I don't know. And I've decided I don't care. If it's nothing, I'll have taken a walk in the country. If it's something—" He tapped the fragment. "Then somebody erased a person from the world and left one thread hanging, and I intend to follow it. Not for the guild. For me."

Osk was quiet a moment. Then he took out a pen, filled in the grievance form in his neat, rebellious hand—*Citizen requests leave to pursue a matter of personal memory. Approved. No further action required by this office*—and stamped it with the new guild seal, the one with the door standing open instead of shut.

"For your files," he said. "In case anyone asks why a former enforcer is wandering the countryside muttering about uphill rivers." He glanced at the page once more, and something flickered across his face—hunger, maybe, the collector's itch—but he mastered it. "I won't ask to copy it. Whatever she left, she left for you. That's how these things work now, I think. Consent. Invitation." He almost smiled. "Go get lost, Vael. Report back if the world lets you."

* * *

The water that ran uphill was exactly where the fragment promised, which unsettled Cassian more than if it hadn't been there at all.

It was a stream on the northern moors, three days' walk from the city, and it ran uphill in a long, patient curve toward a fold in the hills, obeying some gradient of meaning rather than gravity. He followed it with the feeling of a man being expected. Twice he stopped, tested his resolve, considered turning back—and both times the stream seemed to wait for him, pooling politely, the way a host pauses in a doorway.

On the third evening the fold in the hills opened into a valley that was not on any map, because it had not existed to be mapped until recently. The ground remembered, the note had said. Well: the ground remembered green. Snow lay everywhere else on the moors, thin and early, the last residue of the long winter—but here the valley floor was bare earth, dark and soft, and along its edges grew things that had no business growing in late winter: crocuses, defiantly purple; a hedge of something flowering white; and in the center, a plot of turned soil, freshly dug, fenced with woven hazel, with a gate.

A gate. Of course a gate. She had been a cartographer of thresholds; she would hardly rest anywhere without one.

Cassian stood at the fence for a long time, doing the arithmetic of hope, which is the cruelest arithmetic there is. Someone had planted this. Someone with a surveyor's eye for contour and a gardener's patience. Someone whose handwriting appeared on blank pages only for him.

But the world had erased her. He had watched it happen. He had woken the morning after the Unbinding to a city that had never known her name, and he had carried that alone through a winter of treaties and purges and Aurelian's terrible, gentle blankness, and he had told himself that chosen memory was enough, that loving someone the world forgot was its own kind of vow.

He had not dared to hope the vow might be answered.

He opened the gate. It swung without a sound.

Beyond the planted plot, at the head of the valley where the uphill water began, there was a cottage—small, stone, smoke rising from its chimney in a thin gray line. And in the doorway, wrapped in a coat he recognized because he had bought it for her two birthdays ago from a peddler who swore it had belonged to a storm, stood a woman with ink-stained fingers and frost-pale eyes, watching him come with an expression caught between terror and joy, as if she had spent every day since the Unbinding telling herself he wouldn't come, and every night hoping she was wrong.

"You kept the page," Elodie said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Of all the entries in that ledger, you kept mine."

"It was the only one in my hand," Cassian said, walking toward her, faster now, dignity abandoned somewhere back at the gate. "Turns out I'm allowed to keep what I choose. Nobody told me the rule applied both ways."

"I didn't plan this." She was crying and laughing at once, and she had dirt on her cheek, and it was the best thing he had ever seen. "Veyra warned me the erasure might not hold against—against willful memory, but I thought—I hoped—you'd at least be angry first. You're supposed to be angry. You're an enforcer."

"Former," he said, and reached her, and took her face in his hands the way you handle a map you've crossed the world to find: carefully, disbelievingly, home. "And I was angry. For three months. Then I got curious, which is worse." He pressed his forehead to hers. "You're supposed to be gone. The records say you never existed."

"The records," Elodie said, "are going to have to learn to share."

Behind her, at the valley's edge where the snow refused to fall, the ground was already greening in long deliberate rows—beds laid out like a survey, a garden plotted over the old chain-heart, each furrow a boundary, each seed a signature. A garden for the Veil. A place where the answerable gods could come, by invitation, and be met.

And somewhere beyond the hills, Cassian would swear afterward, a bell rang underwater—once, approvingly—as if a very old goddess had checked her ledger, found the entry fair, and closed the book.

Not forever. Nothing was forever now; that was the whole point of the treaty. But for tonight, the page stayed written, the gate stayed open, and the man who had chosen to remember stood in a garden that shouldn't exist, holding the woman the world had tried to forget, while the last of the long winter melted around them like a lie giving up.

Tomorrow there would be explaining to do—to Osk, to the reformers, possibly to a goddess of thresholds with strong opinions about fencing. Tomorrow the world would want to know how a blank page had led a dead woman's lover up a river that ran the wrong way.

Tonight, the kettle was already on.
