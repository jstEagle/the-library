## 21. A Door Left Open

The first thing Elodie Whitlock noticed upon waking was that the ground was warm.

This was, by any reasonable accounting, wrong. She had last been conscious in the chain-heart beneath the Veil, where the cold had a texture and an opinion, where frost had grown up the walls like ivy with ambitions. She had redrawn a binding older than the guild's founding charter. She had unmade herself — or the part of herself that had been Liora's soul-knot, the linchpin, the lock wearing a person's shape — and she had done it expecting, if she was honest with herself, nothing at all on the other side. Annihilation did not usually come with grass.

And yet: warm ground. Grass, actual grass, tickling the backs of her hands. A sky overhead the color of a robin's egg left out in the sun too long, streaked with clouds that looked less like weather and more like someone had painted them while thinking about something else.

She lay still for a long moment and took inventory, because inventory was what she did. Fingers: ten, cold at the tips but hers. The ache behind her eyes that had lived there since the night Veyra first spoke her name: gone. The strange double-weight of memory — Elodie's life stacked atop Liora's centuries, two women occupying one skull like tenants who'd never signed the same lease — quieted. Not erased. Settled. Like sediment finally allowed to sink.

She sat up.

She was sitting on a hilltop she knew intimately and had never once visited. The threshold coordinates. The place every instrument in her kit had refused to map, the place that had drawn its own door through her hands. Below her, the city spread out in the early light, chimneys smoking, bells ringing the hour — and the hour, she noted with a cartographer's professional satisfaction, was actually the hour. For months the bells had rung late, as though time itself were walking through deep snow.

Spring had come. Wrong-footed and real, as Cassian would have said, if Cassian —

She stopped. Reached for the thought. Found it slippery, like a name on the tip of the tongue that retreats when you turn to look at it directly.

There was someone. She was almost certain there was someone. A shape in her memory with warmth attached to it, the way a bed retains the impression of a body after its occupant rises. Someone who argued with her about loyalty. Someone whose hand she could feel, ghost-faint, in her right one, as though they had been holding on very tightly right up until the moment she —

Elodie pressed her palm flat against the warm grass and breathed until the ache passed through her and out the other side.

"All right," she said aloud, to no one. "All right. Let's see what's left."

* * *

Her satchel was beside her. That was the first miracle of the morning, and she catalogued it accordingly, because old habits die hard and hers had died hard recently and come back anyway. Inside: her instruments, unbroken. Her compass, which now pointed not north but slightly west of true, toward the hill itself, as though the hill had become the thing everything else arranged around. And a single sheet of paper she had no memory of writing, in handwriting that was unmistakably her own:

*You are unnamed in every record but your own. This is not a punishment. It is a door.*

Below that, in smaller script, as if the writer had hesitated:

*He kept a page. I don't know why I'm telling you this. Yes I do. Tend well. — V.*

Elodie read it three times. Then she folded it with the precise creases she used for maps she intended to keep, and put it in the inner pocket, over her heart, where important documents went.

"He," she said to the hillside. "Wonderful. Very helpful. A pronoun and a riddle."

The hillside declined to elaborate. But the air did something — a shimmer, a warmth, the faintest scent of woodsmoke and myrrh, which was a smell she now associated with a goddess of thresholds trying to be subtle and failing in the way only immortal beings can fail at subtlety.

"Veyra." Elodie got to her feet, brushed off her skirts, and addressed the empty air with the tone she reserved for guild clerks who filed things incorrectly. "If you're going to haunt me, you could at least do it face to face."

The air rippled. And then, between one breath and the next, Veyra was there — or mostly there, the way heat is there above a road in summer. A tall figure woven out of doorway-light and the moment just before you decide to step through. Her edges kept moving. Her eyes, when Elodie managed to hold them, were ancient and furious and, today, doing a poor job of hiding something softer underneath.

"You're alive," Veyra said. It didn't sound like a greeting. It sounded like an accusation she'd been rehearsing for three hundred years and hadn't expected to need.

"So it would appear." Elodie flexed her fingers. "I'll admit, I had questions about that myself."

"I felt the knot come undone." The goddess drifted closer, her feet not quite troubling the grass. "I felt *her* come undone. Liora. Three hundred years I carried her inside the chains, and then she was — and then you were —" She stopped. The light of her flickered, guttered, steadied. "I don't have a word for what you are now. Do you understand? I am a god of thresholds. Naming things is *what I do*. And you stand there with no name in any ledger but your own, and I cannot find the word."

"Try 'cartographer,'" Elodie suggested. "It's worked well enough so far."

Veyra made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter had been invented yet when Veyra was young. "You always were insufferable. Both of you. It's very confusing, grieving two people who are standing in front of me complaining about my paperwork."

"I'm not complaining about your paperwork. I'm complaining about your *note-taking*." Elodie pulled out the folded page. "'He kept a page.' Who is he, Veyra? What did I lose?"

The goddess was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere below, the city went on waking — a dog barking, a cart rattling over cobblestones, someone singing badly and cheerfully about spring. The ordinary world, resuming. Elodie held onto the sound of it like a railing.

"A man who loved you," Veyra said at last. "Who chose, knowing the cost, to remember you when the world forgot. I watched him walk out of the ruins of the guild carrying a piece of map in his hand like it was the only true thing left." Her voice did something complicated. "I don't know where he is now. Thresholds are my domain, and even I can't see past the edge of a man's grief. He may find you. He may not. That door is his to open, not mine to unlock."

Elodie nodded slowly. The ghost-warmth in her right hand made sense now. So did the ache that had no object. Grief without a name is a strange country; she had mapped stranger, but never from inside it.

"And the others?" she asked. "The gods. The treaty. Did it hold?"

"It holds." Veyra turned, and Elodie followed her gaze down the hill, and saw what she should have seen immediately: the fields beyond the city, green where green had no business being in what should have been the death-months of winter. Smoke rising from hearths. And here and there, scattered across the landscape like lamps, small gatherings of people — at wells, at crossroads, at doorways — talking, gesturing, sometimes arguing loudly enough that the argument carried all the way up the hill.

"The treaty holds," Veyra repeated, "because mortals insist on holding it. They argue with us, Elodie. Daily. Hourly. A woman in the river district told Thane-Marrow, god of debts, exactly what he could do with his interest rates. To his face. He *listened*. I haven't seen him listen since before your guild existed." Something like wonder cracked through the goddess's ancient fury. "It's undignified. It's chaotic. Half of my siblings are outraged and the other half are delighted and none of them know how to proceed."

"That sounds like governance," Elodie said. "Welcome to it."

"Mm." Veyra's light dimmed a shade, and when she turned back, the softness was gone and the warning had arrived, the way storms arrive over mountains — visibly, and all at once. "Now hear the rest of it, because you've earned the truth and I'm going to give it to you whether you want it or not. Treaties are not stones. They are gardens. They hold only as long as someone tends them — pulls the weeds, mends the fence, notices when something begins to rot. Your founder built a cage because cages need no tending. You built a living thing instead, and living things require *gardeners*."

"I'm listening."

"There are gods under that Veil — what's left of it — who agreed to the treaty the way a starving man agrees to terms. Nodding, smiling, counting the exits. And there are mortals — some of them formerly of your guild, if the rumors that reach even me are true — who believe the old silence was safer and mean to restore it by any means necessary. And between those two forces stands a document written in map-notation that perhaps four people alive can read." Veyra's eyes fixed on her, and Elodie felt the weight of them like a hand on her shoulder. "Three, probably. Your mother is dead. Osk, I'm told, has other talents. Which leaves you."

Elodie looked down at her own hands. Cartographer's hands, ink-stained, steady. Hands that had drawn a door into being without meaning to, and redrawn the architecture of heaven, and were currently trembling very slightly, which she decided to attribute to hunger.

"You're asking me to be a gardener," she said.

"I'm telling you that you already are. The garden grew over the place where you unbound us. Whether you plant anything there or not, things will grow — and untended, Elodie, what grows wild in that soil will be *thorns*." Veyra stepped closer, and for a moment her edges stilled, and Elodie saw her plainly: not the monster of the borrowed memories, not the voice in the frost, but something tired and hopeful and terribly old, standing in a doorway she had guarded alone for three hundred years. "I came to say thank you. That was the whole errand. Thank you, and I'm sorry, and the word I couldn't find for what you are —"

"Yes?"

"*Tender.* One who tends." The goddess smiled, and it transformed her face entirely, the way dawn transforms a landscape everyone thought they knew. "It's a good word. Use it carefully."

And she was gone — not dramatically, not in a rush of light, but the way a host leaves a room: quietly, trusting you to find your own way out.

* * *

Elodie stood on the hilltop for a while longer, doing what she did best.

She surveyed.

Not with instruments — though she checked the compass twice more and noted, in her little field book, that it still insisted the center of the world was wherever she happened to be standing, which was either a malfunction or a compliment. She surveyed with her eyes and her training and the new, strange sense that had taken root in her somewhere between dying and not-dying: the sense of thresholds. She could feel them now. Doorways, boundaries, the seams where one thing became another. The city below was stitched together with thousands of them, and most were healthy, and a handful were fraying, and one — down in the low quarter, near where a shrine had healed a dying child a lifetime ago — was fraying badly, and getting worse.

She wrote that down too. *Low quarter. Seam failing. Investigate.* Then, after a moment, she added: *Bring bread. The shrine-keeper there owes me a conversation and I owe everyone an explanation I don't have yet.*

Because that was the other thing. She was unnamed in every record but her own. No guild ledger listed her. No census. No debt record, no property roll, no witness roster. As far as the world's paperwork was concerned, Elodie Whitlock had never existed — which meant, she realized with a slow and slightly unhinged delight, that she owed no taxes, held no license, and could not technically be arrested. The Guild of Impossible Things had spent three hundred years buying invisibility with the freedom of gods. She had gotten hers for free.

The humor of it caught her mid-note and she laughed — really laughed, alone on a hilltop, startling a nearby sheep — until the laugh turned wet at the edges and she had to sit back down in the grass for a moment.

*A page,* Veyra had said. *He kept a page.*

A map fragment leading nowhere he knew. She didn't remember it — couldn't, it wasn't hers anymore — but she knew Cassian, or the shape of him, the way you know a house you grew up in even after it's been sold. He would follow it. He was an enforcer; following things was his entire profession. And the fragment led nowhere he knew.

But she knew it. She was sitting on it. Because she had drawn the garden into the binding — had written, in her mother's notation, in her own careful hand, a clause nobody else would ever think to look for: *the tender shall have a gate, and the gate shall be findable by those who choose to seek it.*

Not leading nowhere. Leading *here*. Leading to a hilltop above a healing city, where a woman with no name and a great deal of work ahead of her was going to need help pulling weeds.

Elodie closed her field book, shouldered her satchel, and started down the hill toward the city — toward bread, and fraying seams, and a shrine-keeper who deserved answers, and a long list of small repairs that would take the rest of a life, which struck her as exactly the right length for the job.

Behind her, at the crest of the hill, in the place where the chain-heart had been, the grass was growing greener than anywhere else for miles.

Something was already taking root there. She intended to find out what — and she had a strong suspicion, walking down into the morning, that whatever it was, she would not be tending it alone for long.
