## 22. A Garden for the Veil

### I. The Gardener

The ground where the chains had been did not want grass.

Elodie learned this in the first week, on her knees in the thawing mud of the old chain-heart, pressing seeds into soil that seemed to flinch from them. Barley sulked. Clover came up gray and apologetic. Even the nettles she'd transplanted out of spite—*something should grow here*, she'd thought—turned their leaves away as though embarrassed to be seen.

"You're doing it wrong," said Veyra, who appeared most evenings at the edge of the plot like a woman arriving late to a dinner party she'd technically been hosting for three thousand years.

"I'm following the almanac."

"The almanac was written by people who never had soil under their nails." The goddess crouched, skirts of dusk pooling around her ankles—she wore whatever hour it happened to be, which made her difficult to buy clothes for. She pressed one finger into the earth, and Elodie felt it through her own knees: a shiver, deep down, like a sleeper rolling over. "This ground spent three centuries holding a god's weight. It doesn't want to forget all at once. It wants to remember *gently*. Plant things that forgive."

"Such as?"

"Roses," Veyra said, with the shameless enthusiasm of an immortal who had opinions about horticulture. "And thyme, for thresholds. And beans, because even gods must eat."

"Gods eat beans?"

"Answerable gods do. That was rather the point of your treaty, Liora."

"My name is Elodie."

"So you insist." But the goddess smiled when she said it—the small, private smile of someone who had watched a soul refuse its old shape and found the refusal *delicious*.

Elodie planted roses. She planted thyme along every path, so that walking anywhere in the garden meant crossing a threshold, over and over, until the whole place hummed faintly with invitation. She planted beans because she'd promised, and because the first crop came up absurdly abundant, as if the soil were showing off now that it had permission to live.

Word got around the city, the way word does. A widow came asking whether the garden took prayers. Elodie said the garden took visitors; prayers were between them and whoever listened. The widow sat by the rose bed for an hour and left lighter. A boy brought a jar containing what he swore was last summer's sunlight—he'd heard the Guild of Impossible Things traded such things once—and asked what it was worth.

"That depends," Elodie said gravely, "on how much of it you can spare."

He gave it gladly. She buried it at the garden's heart, where the linchpin's knot had been, and by midsummer something grew there that no almanac named: a plant with leaves like stained glass, which caught the light and gave it back warmer than it arrived. Children called it the sunflower before anyone could stop them, and so a sunflower it became, taxonomy being no match for a determined eight-year-old.

That was the nature of the new treaty, Elodie reflected, weeding in the gold light. Nothing was imposed anymore. Everything was *tended*. Gods answered by consent, miracles were priced fairly—a hearth-blessing cost a kindness repaid within the season; rain over one field cost a day of cloud over another, agreed upon in advance by farmers who were getting shockingly good at negotiating—and the Veil itself had softened from wall to curtain, drawn aside whenever someone knocked honestly.

It was not victory. Victory, she suspected, would have been simpler. This was gardening: endless, unglamorous, and only survivable if you loved it.

She was on her hands and knees in the bean rows, arguing with a slug about boundaries, when she felt the threshold at the gate hum.

Not a visitor. Not a pilgrim or a farmer come to negotiate weather. This hum was older, lower, familiar in the way a voice is familiar before you place it—the way your own name is familiar when spoken by someone you haven't seen in a year.

Elodie sat back on her heels. Her heart performed a maneuver she chose not to dignify with analysis.

"Well?" said Veyra, materializing beside the bean pole with the subtlety of a cathedral. "Are you going to answer the door, or shall I? Because I will, and I've been told my hospitality is *overwhelming*."

"It's probably a merchant," Elodie said. "Selling turnip seeds."

"Liora."

"*Elodie.*"

"Elodie," the goddess conceded, wickedly patient, "who rewrote the chains of heaven with a fountain pen and a grudge. Go open your gate."

### II. The Map

Cassian had carried the map fragment for two hundred and eleven days.

He knew the count precisely, because knowing it precisely was the only dignity he had left. He'd been stripped of rank, stripped of quarters, briefly stripped of a name in the guild rolls—Osk's reformers had restored that much, at least, once they'd finished gutting the old council—but no reorganization of the Guild of Impossible Things could restore the thing he'd actually lost, which was a person whom the world had agreed, unanimously and without his consent, to forget.

Everyone else remembered the Unbinding as an act of anonymous cartography. *A Whitlock*, some said—there had been Whitlocks, surely? mapmakers ran in families?—but no records survived, no signature, no grave, no marginalia. Even her mother's annotations in the old atlas had faded to ghost-ink, legible only as intention. The guild's new archivists called the treaty's author *the Cartographer*, capitalized, and argued pleasantly over tea about whether the Cartographer had been one person or several or possibly a very organized cat.

Cassian knew better. Cassian remembered deliberately, every morning, the way other men said their prayers.

He remembered her arguing with slugs. He remembered her mapping the Veil's edge with instruments she didn't entirely trust, and trusting herself instead. He remembered candlelight and the argument they'd had about loyalty versus truth, and how they'd both been wrong, and how he would give a great deal to have that argument again and lose it properly this time.

And he remembered the map fragment—the single entry that persisted in his hand alone, in the rewritten ledger, leading nowhere he knew. For months he'd assumed it led nowhere at all. Grief makes cartographers of us all; we keep drawing the country where we lost someone.

Then, in the two hundred and eleventh day, Osk—new-minted First Scribe, still defrocked in spirit, gloriously unshaven—had spread the fragment across his desk and gone very quiet.

"This isn't nowhere," Osk had said. "These are threshold coordinates. Old ones. Pre-treaty." He'd tapped the parchment. "Someone drew you a map to the one place in the world that doesn't exist on any other map. Which means either the Cartographer left you a message—" he'd looked up, and his eyes had been careful, kind, and unbearably hopeful— "or the universe is playing a very cruel joke, in which case I'd like a word with the universe."

So Cassian walked. Out past the miracle districts, where hearth-fires burned at fair prices and neighbors negotiated rainfall over fence posts with the ferocity of professional diplomats. Past the frost-scarred quarter, rebuilt crooked and proud, its survivors having developed strong opinions about gratitude and stronger ones about interest rates. Through streets where children played a game called *Thresholds*, in which the rules involved knocking on doors and being let in, and nobody quite remembered inventing it.

The coordinates ended at a lane that smelled of thyme.

There was a gate. Of course there was a gate—it was that kind of ground now, ground that insisted on doors. And beyond the gate, a garden: bean rows, rose beds, a path lined with low herbs that hummed faintly against his boots like a struck string, and at its heart a plant with leaves like stained glass, throwing warm light across a woman kneeling in the dirt, arguing with a slug.

Cassian stood at the threshold for a long moment. Long enough that the humming under his feet grew pointed, almost impatient, as if the ground itself had opinions about cowardice.

He knocked.

The slug argument ceased. The woman in the bean rows went very still—not frozen, he noted; *listening*, the way she used to listen to maps, head tilted, entire attention given.

Then she stood, turned, and looked at him across a garden she had grown out of the bones of a prison.

"Cassian," she said. Not *do I know you?* Not *can I help you?* His name, plain and certain, in a voice that had no right to know it and clearly did.

"They erased you," he said. His voice came out rougher than he'd planned. "Everything. Every record, every memory. There's an archive full of very nice people who think the treaty wrote itself."

"It did, more or less. I just held the pen." She came toward him along the thymed path, unhurried, mud on her hands, and stopped an arm's length away—close enough that the threshold-hum wrapped them both like a held breath. "You kept the map."

"I kept it on purpose." Two hundred and eleven days, and this was what he said. "Memory can be chosen rather than given. Someone taught me that. I don't remember who."

Something crossed her face—grief and delight fighting to a draw—and then she laughed, wet-eyed and furious about it, and reached up to touch his jaw as if confirming he was real, or perhaps that she was.

"You absolute stubborn man," Elodie Whitlock—who-was-not-in-any-record-Elodie-Whitlock said. "I erased myself from the ledgers of heaven, and you simply *declined*."

"It seemed rude to comply."

"I rewrote the binding of the gods." 

"And I'm told I'm moderately competent at following instructions, when the instructions are worth following."

Behind them, discreetly failing to be discreet, a goddess in a dress made of early evening cleared her throat. "I hate to interrupt," Veyra announced, interrupting enormously, "but there are beans that need picking, a treaty clause due for review at moonrise—the rain delegation from the eastern farms sends its *regards*, Cassian, you'll love them, they negotiate like wolves—and frankly, you two have earned the right to be embarrassing somewhere I'm not standing."

"Veyra," Elodie said, without turning, "this is Cassian."

"Oh, I know who he is." The goddess smiled, ancient and fond and slightly terrifying. "I remember him too. He abstained at the vote to seal the Veil. Do you know how rarely gods get to watch a mortal choose mercy over law in front of witnesses? We keep those moments. They're currency where I'm from." She swept past them both toward the bean rows, trailing dusk. "Also he owes me a debt from the chain-heart. Thresholds keep accounts, enforcer. We'll discuss terms after supper."

### III. Tending

They ate at a table under the sunflower-tree, whose light made everything look like the best possible version of itself. Beans, bread, honey from hives the bees had established on their own initiative—the garden, it turned out, recruited. Veyra held forth on the eastern rain delegation ("they wanted a week of cloud for a river's blessing; I got them down to four days and a festival") while Elodie laughed in a register Cassian hadn't heard since before the winter, and Cassian mostly watched her, cataloguing her again the way he'd once catalogued a shrine wall: her mark, in handwriting he didn't recognize, meaning everything.

Later, when the goddess had withdrawn to wherever gods withdrew—she claimed she slept in the space between doorways, which explained a great deal—they walked the paths together, slow, her hand in his, the thyme announcing each step like a heartbeat.

"The guild sent a letter," she said. "Officially requesting the Cartographer attend the first anniversary of the treaty. They don't know who I am. They just want 'the author.' Osk signed it. He added a postscript in cipher—old guild cipher, the kind we used for lunch orders—that says, and I quote, *'Whoever you are: the beans are a good sign. —O.'*"

"Will you go?"

"I *am* the treaty's chief tender. If I show up, half the council will realize the Cartographer has been selling them honey." She squeezed his hand. "Let them have their anniversary. I'll send a map. Maps say more."

They reached the gate. Beyond it the lane ran dark toward the city, where ten thousand hearth-fires burned at prices their owners had set themselves, and somewhere a child was playing Thresholds, knocking on doors and being let in.

"Do you ever regret it?" Cassian asked quietly. "Being unwritten. Everyone forgot you, Elodie. The whole world."

"Not everyone." She leaned into him. "One man read a blank ledger and chose to fill it. That's not forgetting. That's—" she searched for the word, and being who she was, drew it instead: traced on his palm, in cartographer's script, the sigil from the shrine wall, from the ledgers, from the beginning. Her mark. Chosen twice now: once by her, once by him.

"Tending," he supplied.

"Tending," she agreed.

Above the garden, the Veil hung soft as a drawn curtain, and behind it the old gods waited—not caged, not free exactly, but *answerable*, which was its own strange freedom for everyone involved. Somewhere beneath the roses, the ground remembered gently. Somewhere in the east, the rain delegation drafted its demands. Somewhere in the guild's rewritten ledgers, an entry persisted in one man's hand alone, leading to a gate that hummed when he approached.

And on the garden's southern border, where the soil was newest, Elodie had marked out a bed she hadn't planted yet. She'd left it blank on purpose. A cartographer's habit: always leave room for the country you haven't mapped.

Tomorrow, she thought, watching the sunflower-light breathe, she would sow it. She didn't yet know with what. Something forgiving, Veyra would say. Something that remembers gently.

But tonight—for the first time since a dying child had been healed in the lower city and the impossible had begun answering back—the long winter was over, the chains were gardens, the door was open, and the woman who had rewritten heaven fell asleep with mud under her nails and a man's deliberate, chosen memory keeping her name alive in exactly one ledger, which had always been enough.

In the morning there would be beans to pick, clauses to review, a blank bed waiting for whatever came next.

In the morning, the garden would need tending.

It always would. That was the treaty. That was the joy.

That was theirs.
