## 1. Charms and Small Mercies

The night market of Corvale smelled of fried onions, tallow smoke, and the particular desperation of people who wanted very much to believe in luck.

Ada Villalobos had learned to read that smell the way a sailor reads weather. On good nights it was mostly onions. On bad nights—nights when the mills had laid off hands, or the river had flooded the tanneries, or some alderman had said *austerity* three times in one speech—the desperation came through thick enough to chew, and her little stall of charms sold like bread before a fast day.

Tonight was an austerity night.

"Three coppers for the knot-charm," she said to the millwright's wife, who was turning over a braided cord of red wool with fingers stained blue from dye-vats. "Tie it around your husband's left wrist on a Thursday morning and don't tell him what it's for. The not-telling is half the magic."

"And the other half?"

"The other half," Ada said, "is that he'll think of you every time his sleeve catches on it."

The woman laughed—a real laugh, rusty at the hinges—and paid the three coppers without haggling, which told Ada more about the state of the marriage than any charm could have. She watched the woman go, red cord tucked safe in her apron pocket, and felt the small warm ache she always felt: the ache of selling people hope and knowing, honestly, that hope was worth at least three coppers.

Because here was the truth Ada kept folded up beneath her tongue like a coin: the charms did nothing.

Or rather—they did nothing *magically*. They were well-made things, knotted and beaded and painted by her own two law-abiding hands, and they worked the way all small objects work: they gave worry somewhere to sit. A woman who believed her husband carried her luck would look at him gentler. A man who wore a painted acorn against bad harvests would take one more risk than he otherwise might, and sometimes that risk was the whole difference. Ada had never once lied about this. She simply didn't go out of her way to correct anyone either. In Corvale, where magic had been outlawed since before her grandmother lost her teeth, a charm-seller who admitted her wares were only wood and wool and paint was a charm-seller who starved.

And a charm-seller who admitted her *real* gift was a charm-seller who burned.

So Ada kept the other thing folded away, and sold knots, and got by.

Her stall sat at the crook of Tanner's Row where the night market bent around the old pump, wedged between a pie-cart run by a widow named Bess Halloway and a knife-grinder who sharpened blades so badly that Ada privately suspected him of being a genius of repeat business. She'd held the spot for six years. Six years of the same striped awning, the same hand-lettered sign—*VILLALOBOS CHARMS, LUCK MENDED WHILE YOU WAIT*—the same jokes for the same customers. It was a small life. She had built it the way you build a wall around something precious: stone by careful stone, each one unremarkable, none of them admitting what they protected.

She was restringing a bracelet of river-glass when the horse began to scream.

* * *

It came from the cart-lane behind the pie-cart, and it was not the scream of pain, exactly. Ada knew pain-screams. This was worse and quieter in its way: the high, sawing terror of an animal that has decided the world has ended and no one has told it why.

The crowd barely looked up. Market folk were used to cart-horses having opinions. But Ada's hands went still on the river-glass, because she could hear what the horse was actually saying, underneath the screaming, the way you can pick one voice out of a crowd:

*Too dark. Too dark too dark too dark, the chains are singing again, the ground hums, I want my stable, I want the mare with the white sock, I want—*

Ada closed her eyes for exactly one breath. One breath was all she allowed herself, ever, to be sorry for herself.

Then she set down the bracelet, called "watch the stall, Bess!" without waiting for an answer, and slipped into the cart-lane.

The horse was a big gray gelding, lathered and rolling his eyes, backed up against a wall with a delivery cart slewed sideways behind him. His driver—a boy of maybe fifteen, all elbows and panic—was hauling on the bit, which was rather like trying to argue with a landslide.

"Don't yank him," Ada said. "You're telling him the world's ending and he believes you."

"He spooked at nothing! There's nothing there!"

"There's always something there." Ada approached the gelding slowly, at an angle, the way she'd approach a skittish dog or a nervous lord. Up close the horse's fear poured off him like heat off a forge. *Ground hums. Chains sing. Dark coming under the city.* She let her hand hover near his muzzle—not touching, just present—and did the thing she had done perhaps ten thousand times in her life and never once in front of a witness.

She listened properly, and answered.

*I know,* she said, in the wordless way that was less language than warmth. *I know the ground hums. I hear it too. But the humming is old, older than your stable, older than your dam, and it has never once come up to bite you. You are safe. Your driver is frightened, not angry. Nobody here will hurt you.*

The gelding's ears, pinned flat, rose halfway. His breathing slowed by degrees, like a kettle taken off the fire. He lowered his great head and bumped his nose against Ada's palm, damp and velvet-soft, and sighed the enormous sigh of a creature deciding to trust the world for one more hour.

"There," Ada said aloud, stroking his neck. "He just wanted someone to agree that it's frightening. That's all most of us want."

The boy stared at her. "How'd you—that's not—I've had Dapple since he was two and he's never—"

"He's tired," Ada said smoothly, "and the market's loud, and you were pulling on his mouth. Walk him home the long way, past the chandlery. The light's better there." She fished a knotted red cord from her apron pocket and pressed it into the boy's hand. "For the cart axle. Luck. No charge."

It wasn't entirely a lie. The cord would do nothing to the axle. But the boy would hold it, and remember that a stranger had been kind, and kindness travels through hands into reins whether you mean it to or not.

She watched them go—boy and horse, both calmer than they'd been—and stood a moment longer in the dark of the cart-lane, listening to the ground.

It did hum. Faintly. It always had, this deep in the city, this close to the mountain's roots. Most people couldn't feel it. Ada could, the way she could hear horses and sparrows and once, memorably and terrifyingly, a magistrate's cat, who had spent four full minutes explaining exactly which servant was stealing the silver. The gift had come to her young, unasked-for, the way gifts do. Her mother had—

Her mother had.

Ada found, as she often did, that the thought slid sideways before it finished forming, like a fish slipping a net. There was a shape where her childhood should have been, and the shape had a hole in it, worn smooth from touching. She knew her mother had existed. She knew it the way you know a room exists behind a locked door. She could not, no matter how she pressed, remember her face.

She pressed now, briefly, out of habit. The hole stayed smooth. She let it go, the way you let go of a tooth that isn't ready to come out, and went back to her stall.

* * *

Bess Halloway had sold four pies in her absence and eaten the profits of one, and greeted Ada with the unbothered honesty of a woman who had buried two husbands and considered surprise a waste of good mourning. "Your neighbor's boy came by. Tam. Bought a luck-knot with his own money, if you please. Said he's going to teach it to sing."

"You can't teach a knot to sing, Bess."

"That's what I said. He said—" Bess shrugged, wiping grease on her apron. "He said everything sings if you listen right. Odd child. Sweet, though. Like his da."

Tam. Eight years old, gap-toothed, forever in trouble for whistling during prayers. He'd bought the knot with coins earned running errands, and he was going to make it sing, and Ada smiled at the thought of it all the way through the next hour of customers—a drunkard wanting a charm against sobriety, a bride wanting one against mothers-in-law, a clerk who bought a painted acorn every single week and never seemed to notice he already owned forty of them.

It was nearly midnight when the drums started.

Everyone heard those. The market didn't so much quiet as flinch, all at once, like a herd. Down the length of Tanner's Row came torchlight, and in the torchlight came six men in crown livery walking in step, and ahead of them walked the herald—a thin man on a tall horse, which Ada noted with distant professional sympathy was a combination the horse had strong feelings about.

The herald dismounted. Unrolled a scroll with a snap like a breaking bone. And read.

"By order of His Majesty's Chancellor, and sealed under the Act of Ash: Let it be known throughout Corvale and its holdings that the Hunt is renewed. The gifted—those who traffic with beasts, bend flame, whisper to the dead, or carry any art forbidden since the Dragon War—are declared enemies of the peace. Any citizen harboring, concealing, or failing to report such persons shall share their forfeit. Rewards are offered for true names. The Hunt begins at dawn."

He nailed the proclamation to the notice-post beside the old pump. Four nails. Each hammer-strike rang through the square like a bell tolling wrong.

The market stood very still. Then, being Corvale, it did what Corvale always did: it pretended nothing had happened. Pies were sold. Charms were packed. Conversations resumed at slightly higher volume, the way people talk over a grave they're standing in.

But Ada saw the glances start. Quick, sidelong, measuring. The glance of the knife-grinder at the pie-widow. Of strangers at strangers. Six years of stone-by-stone wall-building, and she could feel the mortar going soft already, because this was how it always began—not with arrests but with *looking*, everyone checking everyone, hunting for the flicker of something forbidden.

She packed her stall with steady hands. Knots in the basket. River-glass wrapped in flannel. The painted acorns back in their crate, all forty-odd of them, useless and beloved.

*Gifted.* The word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. She had been gifted since she was small enough to ride in a market basket herself. Every kind word she'd ever murmured to a frightened animal, every secret conversation with sparrows and stray dogs and one unforgettable magpie who had tried to blackmail her—every one of them, under this new ink, was a crime with a reward attached.

At the edge of the square, the herald remounted his tall, put-upon horse. As he turned, the horse swung its head around and fixed one dark eye on Ada across the whole crowded square.

*They're coming for the ones like you,* the horse said, in the flat, simple way horses say terrible things. *I heard them in the barracks. They have a list.*

Then the torchlight swept away down Tanner's Row, and the drums faded toward the river gates, and Ada Villalobos stood alone in the emptying market holding a basket of harmless knots, listening to the ground hum beneath her feet, and thinking of a gap-toothed boy who believed everything sings if you listen right.

Dawn was five hours away.

The Hunt began at first light.
