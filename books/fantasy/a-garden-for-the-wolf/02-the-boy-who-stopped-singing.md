## 2. The Boy Who Stopped Singing

The trouble with living above a charm-seller, Tam's mother liked to say, was that you started believing in luck.

The trouble with being a charm-seller, Ada thought now, watching the boy through her shutters, was that you learned exactly what luck looked like when it left a person. It looked like Tam Voss sitting on his doorstep in the gray of morning, perfectly still, with his hands folded in his lap like a little lord at prayer. Ten years old and motionless as furniture. The sparrows used to land on his shoulders while he sang — he sang *constantly*, badly, with tremendous conviction, songs about eels mostly — and they had not landed on him in three days.

He hadn't sung in three days either.

Ada pulled her shawl over her nightdress and went down into the cold. The night market's debris still littered the lane: cabbage leaves, a broken lantern, the trampled ghost of someone's spilled cinnamon wine. Her own stall stood shuttered and dark, its charms sleeping under canvas. She crossed to the Vosses' step and crouched down until she was level with the boy.

"Morning, Tam."

Tam's eyes came around to her. That was the wrongness of it, Ada thought. His eyes moved. They tracked her face, blinked when dust blew, watered in the wind. Everything about him worked except the part that made him *Tam* — the fidgeting, the grinning, the relentless eel-based music of him. What sat on the step was a well-kept house with nobody home.

She held out her hand anyway, palm up, the way you offered anything to anything hoping for trust. "Brought you nothing at all. Best gift I give."

Nothing. Not even the flicker he'd have given any stranger.

Behind her, wings. A small brown shape dropped from the gutter and hopped along the step's edge, head cocked, fixing her with one black eye. Tam's sparrow. It had been haunting the Voss windowsill since the boy went quiet, and it had been screaming at Ada since yesterday morning — not ordinary sparrow complaint, but the sharp repeated *chee-chee-chee* that meant *come, come, come, something is wrong.*

"I know," she murmured, so low only the bird could hear. "I'm coming. Keep your feathers on."

The sparrow cocked its head the other way, deeply unimpressed by her pace, which was fair.

"Ada." Marta Voss stood in the doorway, and Ada saw at once why the whole lane had stopped asking after Tam. Marta had the look of a woman who had been crying in a room where crying wasn't allowed: eyes scrubbed pink, smile nailed on, hands busy with a basket of mending that had clearly not progressed past the first sock. "You're up early."

"So are you."

"We're all up early these days." The joke landed dead between them. Marta glanced at her son, then away, quick as a woman touching a hot pan. "He's resting. Doctor Bram says some children go quiet. Grows out of it, he says."

"Doctor Bram also charges two bits to tell you to drink water," Ada said gently. "Marta. Three days."

"What do you want me to say?" And there it was, cracking through — not anger, something worse, something pleading. "That my son sits there like a coat on a hook and I don't know why? That his father won't—" She stopped. Pressed the sock to her mouth for a moment. "His father's working nights. He'll sort it. He said he'd sort it."

"Sort what?"

But Marta had already stepped back inside, and the door closed with the soft finality of a woman who could not afford one more honest sentence.

The sparrow cheeped twice, meaningfully.

"Yes, yes," Ada sighed. "Everyone's a critic."

* * *

She took the bird up to her rooms because a screaming sparrow in a public lane was a risk she couldn't afford, and because — though she'd never have admitted this to anyone, there being no one to admit it to — she wanted to know what it knew before she decided what she'd do. There was a difference, she'd learned, between hearing an animal and listening to one. Hearing was the gift, the cursed illegal thing she'd been born with, the reason she sold luck-charms instead of using the one talent the crown would burn her for. Listening was the work. Animals lied too, if you let them; they dramatized; a cat would tell you the world ended daily. You had to sit with what they gave you and find the true grain of it.

So she sat on her floor with a heel of bread and the sparrow perched on her knee, and she listened.

What came was not words. It never was. It was a tumble of impressions, feathered and frantic: *the small one, the singing one, warm shoulder, gone, cold, the tall one weeping into cloth, the man going down-down-down beneath the stones, coins changing hands, a door of iron, a smell like candles burning wrong, the small one's song taken out of him like a thread pulled from cloth—*

Ada set down the bread. Her hands were not steady.

"Beneath the stones," she whispered. "Say that again."

The sparrow said it again, because sparrows are generous that way, and this time she caught more of the shape of it: the father, Wynn Voss, going *down*, down stairways cut into the mountain's root, into a place that smelled of tallow and old iron, carrying something wrapped in his coat. Carrying *nothing anyone could see*. And men in fine wool taking it, and counting coins into his shaking hand, and the boy on the surface going quiet mid-song, like a lamp pinched out.

There were stories, of course. Every market rat knew the stories. The court beneath the mountain, where the chancellor kept his ledgers. Souls bought and sold, they said, though everyone said it the way you said the moon was made of cheese — a joke with a nervous edge, because the Proclamation had gone up on the market post just last week, the herald's brass voice ringing off the tiles: *the Hunt for the Gifted is open, informers rewarded, concealment punished.* Ada had read it three times and then gone home and counted her charms and told herself, as she always did, *keep small, keep dull, keep invisible.* The hunt was for fire-starters and storm-callers, for the dramatic ones. Not for a woman who soothed cart-horses. Not for children who talked to sparrows.

Except apparently it was exactly for children who talked to sparrows, or at least children whose fathers were desperate enough to sell whatever the buyers beneath the mountain wanted. And what they wanted, the sparrow's memory made horribly clear, was the singing itself. The bright thread of him. Pulled out and wound onto a ledger somewhere in the dark.

Ada looked at the sparrow. The sparrow looked at Ada, and its small body was trembling, and she understood that it had flown to every warm shoulder in the lane looking for its boy, and found none of him anywhere, and she was the only creature in Corvale who had even tried to understand what it was saying.

"All right," she said softly. "All right, little one. I hear you."

It was a terrible plan forming. She could feel it forming, the way you feel weather change. She was one woman with a stall of painted acorns and knotted string. The people beneath the mountain bought souls and the people above it hunted gifts, and between those millstones, a sensible person kept very, very still.

But Tam had once spent an entire summer leaving dead beetles on her stall because he'd decided she was lonely and beetles were treasure. He was ten. He sang about eels. He had a gap in his teeth he was proud of and a mother who cried into socks pretending she wasn't, and his soul was wound on a ledger in the dark like spare thread, and the whole city was going to walk past his doorstep and call it *resting*.

Ada got dressed. She put on the plain brown dress, the dull shawl, the face of a woman who sold luck and believed in nothing. Then she paused at the shelf above her hearth, where she kept the things that mattered: her mother's ring, too big for any of her fingers. A pressed flower she no longer remembered receiving. The small tin box she did not open.

*Keep small,* she told herself. *Keep dull.*

The sparrow cheeped once, sharply, from the windowsill. It sounded almost exactly like *coward.*

"You're a bad influence," Ada told it, and reached for her cloak.

* * *

Wynn Voss came home at dawn, as the sparrow had promised, coming up out of the ground near the old quarry road with coal dust in the creases of his knuckles and new coin in a purse that had been flat for a year. Ada was waiting by the water pump, ostensibly filling a jug, actually positioned like the ambush predator she absolutely was not.

"Wynn."

He flinched like she'd struck him. That told her most of it before a word passed. The rest she read in his face as he turned: the guilt already calcifying into defensiveness, the eyes that couldn't quite land on hers.

"Ada. Early."

"Everyone keeps saying that. Is it catching?" She kept her voice light, kept the jug in her hands, kept herself small. "How's work?"

"Good. Steady." The lie sat between them, enormous.

"That's wonderful," she said. "Steady work. Funny — I heard the mines under the hill haven't hired in years. I heard the only thing that pays beneath the mountain is the court, and the court doesn't buy labor." She watched his throat move. "I heard it buys other things."

"You don't know what you're talking about."

"No," she agreed quietly. "I don't. So tell me. Because your son hasn't sung in three days, Wynn, and I've known that boy since he was small enough to fit in a soup pot, and children who stop singing aren't *resting*."

"It's done." His voice cracked on it. "It can't be undone, so there's no use—" He stopped himself, jaw working. When he spoke again it was barely above the pump's drip. "You think I wanted this? They came to *me*. After the herald posted the Hunt. Said they knew about my boy. Knew what he was. Said I could hand him to the executioner's cart in a month, or I could bring them the... the bright part of him, and take coin, and he'd live. He'd live, Ada. Gray-eyed and quiet, but alive, and no one would ever prove what he was, because there'd be nothing left to prove." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd ever heard from him. "They called it mercy. They had a *price list*."

Ada felt sick. She felt, underneath the sickness, a cold clear fury settling into place like a keel. "And you paid it."

"He's *alive*."

"He's a coat on a hook!" The words tore out of her before she could dress them, and Wynn recoiled, and she lowered her voice to a whisper that shook. "Your son is sitting on your step with nothing behind his eyes, and you walk past him every day, and you call that a life? Wynn. Where is he? Where did they take it?"

"You can't get it back. No one gets it back." But he said it like a man reciting something he'd been given to say, and his eyes — finally, finally — met hers, and in them was the thing she'd been waiting for: not defiance. A question. A drowning man's *is there any way.*

"The court beneath the mountain," she said. "Yes or no."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. He looked at the ground, and that was yes enough, and Ada nodded slowly, committing the geography of his shame to memory.

"If anyone asks," Wynn said hoarsely, "you heard nothing from me."

"You're right," Ada said. "I heard nothing. Nothing at all." She picked up her jug and turned away, and then stopped, because fairness demanded it even now. "For what it's worth — I think you believed you were saving him. That's the trap they built. It's a good trap. It caught you clean."

She walked home through the waking lane, past Tam on his step, past the shuttered stalls, past the market post where the Proclamation fluttered in the morning wind. *THE HUNT FOR THE GIFTED IS OPEN,* it said, in letters tall enough to read from a galloping horse. *INFORMERS REWARDED.*

Ada stood before it for a long moment. Behind her eyes, the sparrow's memory turned and turned: *down-down-down beneath the stones, a door of iron, candles burning wrong.*

Keep small. Keep dull. Keep invisible. She had built her whole life on those three commands, and they had kept her breathing, and breathing was not nothing.

But a child's soul was wound on a ledger in the dark, and the first coil of the hunt was already tightening around her street, and there was a limit, Ada discovered, to how small a person could make herself before she disappeared entirely.

She went home, opened her stall, and began, methodically, to pack.

She was cinching the last strap when she noticed the shadow across her awning — boots, a long coat, the particular stance of someone trained to stand. A woman in royal livery stood at the mouth of the lane, watching her stall with the patient attention of a hunter who has scented something.

She wore the badge of the Crown Hunt.
