## 3. The Huntswoman's Debt

Maren Vos had been a royal huntswoman for eleven years, and in that time she had learned three things about her trade. First, that the gifted never looked like the woodcuts on the warrant posters—no wild eyes, no crooked fingers, just tired people with tired hands. Second, that the best way to find a liar was to stop talking yourself. And third, that she hated the new writ in her breast pocket the way other women hated a stone in their boot: constantly, and with a low simmering fury that made her heel ache.

The writ was signed by Chancellor Corvane himself. *By order of the Crown, all persons suspected of unlawful communion with beasts shall be taken to the court beneath the mountain for examination.* Examination. Maren had been to the court beneath the mountain once, to deliver a prisoner, and she still woke some nights with the memory of the sound the vault doors made when they closed—a sound like a book shutting on a finger.

She had been given a list of names. Seven names, gathered by informers whose faces she didn't trust and whose coin she suspected came from somewhere deeper than the treasury. The fourth name on the list was *Ada Villalobos, charm-seller, Night Market.*

Maren read that name four times before she let herself feel anything about it.

* * *

She remembered the fire the way you remember a scar—vividly, and only when you touched it.

Six years ago. The tannery row, a blaze that had eaten three buildings and would have eaten a fourth if the wind had turned. Maren had been younger then, still believing her rank meant something, and she had gone into the smoke after a scream that turned out to be a cat, because that was the kind of fool she'd been. The ceiling came down on her left shoulder and pinned her to the floor of a burning dye-house, and the last thing she saw before her eyes scalded shut was a girl—sixteen, maybe seventeen, dark braids coming loose, standing in the doorway with a horse behind her.

The horse had dragged her out. Maren remembered that much clearly: the enormous, terrified bay mare, harnessed to a cart, being *coaxed* through fire by a girl who was talking to it in a low, urgent voice, like a mother talking a child across a frozen river. Horses did not walk into burning buildings. Horses did not stand in a doorway with their ears flat and their whole body trembling and *wait* for a stranger to be loaded onto the cart.

The girl had looked at Maren through the smoke. Maren had looked back. Neither of them had said a word, and both of them had understood everything.

The girl was Ada Villalobos. Maren had confirmed it a year later, quietly, from a distance, and had then done the most cowardly and most decent thing of her career: nothing. She had filed no report. She had let a debt sit unpaid, the way debts do, gathering interest.

Now the crown had put the name on a list and handed her the list and said, *bring them in.*

* * *

The Night Market at dusk smelled of fried onions and cheap tallow and, underneath it all, the river. Maren went in plainclothes—a gray cloak, no badge, hair braided back like any dockworker's wife. She was good at this part. She had spent years learning to be furniture.

Ada's stall was where the informer said it would be: a crooked little booth of green-painted wood near the fountain, hung with luck-charms on red string. Knotted horsehair for safe travel. Dried rowan for fever. Little clay wolves for—Maren squinted at the hand-lettered sign—*courage, or at least the appearance of it.*

She almost smiled. Almost.

Ada was mid-sale, wrapping a rowan bundle for a nervous-looking apprentice clerk, and Maren watched her work. She was good. Warm without being oily, funny without being desperate. "Now, if the fever comes back, you boil it, don't steep it. Boiling's for sickness, steeping's for heartbreak, and the two get offended if you mix them up."

The clerk paid and drifted off, and then Ada's eyes found Maren's, and Maren watched the warmth bank itself like a fire damped for the night. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. *A stranger who is looking at me too carefully.*

"Luck-charm, mistress?" Ada said. "You strike me as a woman who makes her own luck, but I sell to the ambitious too."

"I'm looking for someone," Maren said.

"Terrible place to look. Everyone here is no one."

"I'm looking for a woman who was at the tannery fire. Six years back. The one with the horse."

The silence lasted exactly as long as it needed to and no longer. Ada set down the charm she'd been untangling. "You'd better come round the back," she said. "The fountain's got ears. Don't ask me how I know. It just has that look."

* * *

The back of the stall was a lean-to of tarps and crates, one candle, two stools, and a cat of enormous self-regard who took one look at Maren and sat down between them like a magistrate.

"You were the one in the dye-house," Ada said. It wasn't a question. "I wondered for years if you'd made it. You didn't come back."

"I didn't come back because coming back would have meant questions. From you, from my superiors, from myself." Maren took off her hood. "You saved my life with that horse. I've known what you are for six years and I've said nothing, and I'm not proud of how long I sat on it, but there it is."

Ada's hand had drifted, very casually, to a boning knife on the crate beside her. Maren noted it without comment. She'd have done the same.

"And now?" Ada said. "You've come to collect the debt the other way? Turn me in and feel square?"

"No." Maren pulled the writ from her breast pocket and laid it on the crate between them, facedown, like a card she didn't want to look at either. "Now I've come because the game changed. There's a hunt on, a real one. Not the usual sweep-and-fine. The chancellor's men are taking the gifted to the court beneath the mountain, and they're not coming back out. There's a boy on my list. Tam. The cooper's son, off Tanner's Lane."

The color went out of Ada's face so fast and so completely that Maren's chest hurt with it. "Tam's not gifted," Ada said. "Tam's just a boy. He feeds the sparrows. He—"

"His name's on the list. Fourth from the top. I don't know why. I don't know what they want him for." Maren turned the writ over. *By order of the Crown.* The seal gleamed, fat and red as a wound. "I was given this writ and seven names and told to be thorough. And I've spent three days being thorough, and what I've learned is that thorough, right now, means complicit."

The cat put one enormous paw on the writ, delicately, as if claiming it.

"So here's what I know about you, Ada Villalobos," Maren said. "You pull strangers out of fires. You've been hiding a gift that could hang you since before you were grown, and you've done it to sell fever-charms to people who can barely pay. I don't know what they want with a child who isn't gifted, but I know you're the only person on my list who might find out, and I know I owe you a life, and I know—" her voice did something she hated, caught slightly, "—I know what the vault doors sound like when they close. I'm not handing anyone else to that sound."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. The candle guttered. Somewhere beyond the tarps, the market was packing up for the night, the ordinary sounds of ordinary law-abiding life.

"You understand what you're doing," Ada said at last. "If they catch you warning me, that's not a fine. That's the vault for you too."

"I've been doing the arithmetic since the writ landed in my hands." Maren stood. "There's a patrol sweep on Tanner's Lane at dawn. Cooper's house first. If the boy's going to be anywhere else, he needs to be anywhere else tonight. I can get you out of the city—there's an ash-gate on the river side the watch doesn't check because it smells too bad to check. After that, I can't help you. I'm a huntswoman. I know how to find people. I have never once in my life known how to hide them."

"Then it's lucky," Ada said, "that you're about to learn, because I'm not going without him, and you're not staying behind, because I've seen what happens to people who stand in doorways when I'm leaving, and I don't like the odds."

Maren stared at her. "That's not— I just said I'd get you to the gate."

"And I'm saying the gate's not far enough." Ada blew out the candle. In the sudden dark her voice was calm and absolutely certain. "You've decided to be a criminal, Huntswoman. Congratulations. The hours are terrible and the pay is nothing, but the company improves."

* * *

The writ was still on the crate. Maren picked it up, and found she couldn't put it back in her pocket—her pocket belonged to a woman who delivered people to vaults. Instead she folded it small and held it in her fist.

"I'll need to take something," she said. "A writ, unsigned, from the chancellery. Proof of what's coming, in case we find someone with the power to care. There's a courier satchel in the hunt office with tomorrow's orders in it. I can lift it before the night watch changes."

"You're going to steal from the crown," Ada said, "to help a charm-seller and a cooper's kid."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It is insane. It's also the first decent thing the crown's hunt has done all year, so try not to get caught." Ada was already moving, pulling a canvas sack from under the crates, sweeping her charm-strings into it with the brisk economy of a woman who had always known, somewhere, that she'd have to pack fast one day. The cat leapt onto her shoulder as if this had been arranged.

Maren looked at the writ in her hand one last time. Eleven years of service, folded up small enough to burn.

"Dawn sweep," she said. "Tanner's Lane. Don't be late, Villalobos."

"Wouldn't dream of it," said Ada, and behind her, in the dark of the lean-to, the cat's eyes caught the last of the market-light and glowed green as the gate Maren was about to betray everything to reach.
