## 4. What the Jailer Buried

Osk Brandt had spent forty years learning the weight of doors, and he could tell you this: every door in Corvale had a voice, if you knew how to listen. The great oak ones groaned like old men complaining about their knees. The iron ones in the mountain court said nothing at all, which was worse. But the little door behind the tannery on Sallow Row—this one sighed. A long, wet, unhappy sigh, like a man settling into a confession.

Osk put his shoulder to it and gave the sigh back.

"Rust," he told the three figures huddled in the alley shadow behind him. "Not me. I'm too old to sigh like that."

The huntswoman, Maren, didn't smile. She hadn't smiled since the tannery district began, and Osk supposed he couldn't blame her; she was a crown officer walking into the undercity with a stolen writ in her coat and treason in her future, and smiling at that would take a kind of courage Osk had never managed. The fox trotted at Ada's heel with its burned ear flat, eyes catching the lamplight like two coins dropped down a well. And Ada herself—Ada watched him the way she'd been watching him since he'd knocked on her stall that morning and said the words *I know where they'll take the boy*.

She watched him like a woman deciding whether to trust a lock or break it.

"It opens," Osk said, "if you know the trick. Everything down here opens if you know the trick. That's the whole problem with Corvale."

"What's the trick?" Maren asked.

"Knowing someone who owed me money." He lifted the latch, turned it backward—the door had been hung by a drunkard forty years ago and never fixed—and pushed through into darkness that smelled of tallow smoke and old river.

* * *

The undercity of Corvale was not so much built as accumulated, the way lint accumulates in a pocket. Generations of smugglers, debtors, and people who simply wished not to be found had tunneled and bricked and shored until the space beneath Sallow Row resembled the inside of some enormous, apologetic animal. Osk walked it with a candle stub and a lifetime of certainty, his boots finding the dry path without looking, because looking took time and time was the one thing they didn't have.

Tam was in the executioner's cart now. Had been since dawn. They were taking him up the mountain road tonight, Osk had heard it from a turnkey who still owed him for covering a dice debt, and once the boy passed the mountain gate, no one came back down. Not alive. Not whole.

"So you guarded them," Ada said, somewhere behind him. Her voice echoed strangely off the low brick. "The courts beneath the mountain. You were jailer there."

"I was." Osk kept walking. "Thirty-one years. Started when I was younger than you, ended when my knees did."

"And now you're helping us break into it."

"Now I'm helping you break into it," he agreed. "You can say the next part, girl. Everyone's been thinking it since the tannery. Go on."

There was a pause. Then, quietly: "Why?"

Osk stopped. Ahead of them, the passage forked around a fallen pillar some ancestor had decided was easier to build over than remove, and the candlelight made two shadows of everything, including him. He stood between his own selves for a moment before answering.

"Because there's a bench down here," he said, "where I used to sit and eat my lunch. And because I'm going to make you all sit on it while I tell you something, and I'd rather do it sitting down, because what I have to say is heavy, and I'm old."

He led them into a side chamber—a vaulted room, half-collapsed, where somebody's grandmother had once tried to grow mushrooms and given up. There was indeed a bench, stone, cold as a magistrate's heart. Osk sat. The others sat. The fox sat on Ada's feet, which seemed to be its way of claiming territory.

"My daughter's name is Liesel," Osk said. "She's twenty-six now. She has her mother's laugh and my stubbornness, God help everyone. And when she was nineteen, she got sick. Not the ordinary sick. The kind of sick that costs money the way a fire costs a forest."

He turned the candle stub in his fingers.

"I was a jailer. Do you know what a jailer earns? Enough to be honest and nothing else. And there came a man to my door—not the chancellor himself, don't flatter the story—a man *for* the chancellor, soft-voiced, well-gloved. And he said: Master Brandt, we understand your girl needs a physician from the capital. We understand what those cost. And we understand that you see names, down there in your ledgers. Names of the gifted. People whose neighbors whisper about them. People nobody will miss."

Ada had gone very still. Even the fox had stopped moving.

"He didn't ask me to arrest anyone," Osk said. "That's the thing I want you to understand, because it's the thing I hid behind for six years. He asked me to *write*. Just write. A name here, a name there. A street. A description. 'The cooper's daughter hums to the rats and the rats come.' That's all. Ink on paper. I told myself I wasn't selling anyone—I was just writing things that were already true."

His hands weren't steady anymore. He let them be unsteady. It seemed dishonest to hold them still.

"Fourteen names," he said. "I wrote fourteen names, and every month after, the gloves came back with coin, and Liesel got well, and I told myself the story a jailer tells best, which is that the door was already locked and I only ever held the keys. But I'll tell you what I know now, sitting here, that I didn't know then. Tam's father wrote one name too. One. His own son's, because the court doesn't take coin—it takes souls, and it makes you pay in whatever currency breaks you worst. My currency was Liesel. His was Tam. And mine bought medicine, and his bought bread, and neither of us gets to feel cleaner than the other, though I've spent six years trying to feel cleaner than him, because that's what guilt does. It looks for company and calls it comparison."

The silence afterward had weight, like snow on a roof.

It was Maren who spoke first, and her voice was rougher than Osk had yet heard it. "The writ I stole. The hunt orders. They're written in a hand I recognized. Chancellor Vess's own clerk." She looked at Osk. "Your fourteen names are probably in the same ledger as Tam."

"They're in the ledger," Osk said. "I know they're in the ledger, because I've dreamed about that ledger every night for six years, and in the dream it's very tidy, and the ink is very black, and my handwriting is very neat." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Neat handwriting. Forty years of service, and that's my monument. Neat handwriting in a book of stolen children."

Ada leaned forward. In the candlelight her face was young and old at once, the way faces get when they've decided something.

"The sparrow told me about Tam," she said. "Before he went gray-eyed. The birds talk, and I listen, and it's illegal, and I've hidden it my whole life. So I won't pretend I don't understand hiding." She paused. "But I need to ask you one thing, Osk Brandt, and I need the true answer, not the comfortable one. Can you get us into the court? Not near it. Into it."

Osk looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached inside his coat and drew out something he had carried against his chest for thirty-one years and six years both: a ring of keys, iron gone brown with age, each one worn smooth by his own hand.

"A jailer keeps his keys," he said. "It's the last law of the trade, older than any proclamation. They never asked for mine back, because they never imagined an old man would walk back up the mountain. Nobody imagines anything about old men. It's our one advantage." He weighed the ring in his palm. "These open the servants' gate below the ash-roads. From there—" he nodded toward the fox, whose burned ear twitched— "from there I'm told we have a guide who knows the ways better than I do. Though I'd take it kindly if someone asked the fox directly. I'm too old to spend another hour pretending I can't hear it judging me."

Ada blinked. "You knew?"

"Girl, I guarded the gifted for thirty-one years. You think I don't know what I'm looking at?" He heaved himself up off the bench, knees announcing their objections to the congregation. "Come on. The cart goes up the mountain road at moonrise, and moonrise waits for no one, not even the guilty."

They fell in behind him, single file down the dark passage, and Osk took the lead with his candle stub and his keys and his fourteen names rattling around inside him like stones in a tin cup.

At the mouth of the tunnel, where the first gray breath of the ash-roads showed ahead, Maren caught his arm.

"Brandt. What you did—fourteen names. If we get them out, the ledger burns with everything else. Does that buy you peace?"

Osk considered it honestly, because the woman deserved honesty.

"No," he said. "Peace isn't for sale down here. But maybe service is. Maybe that's the closest thing left." He patted her hand off his arm, gently. "Ask me again when the book's ash. If I'm alive, I'll give you a better answer. If I'm dead, lie to yourself on my behalf. It's the least a friend can do."

Ahead of them, the fox slipped out into the gray, and stopped, and stood very still, ears forward, staring up the slope toward the mountain—toward the faint, guttering red glow that bled from its peak like a coal refusing to die.

And Osk, who had listened to doors for forty years, heard the fox make a sound he had never heard a fox make.

It sounded almost like a name.
