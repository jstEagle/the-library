## 5. The Fox with the Burned Ear

The ash-roads began where Corvale's cobbles ended, and they were exactly as pleasant as they sounded.

Ada had imagined, in the fevered hours after Maren's defection, that fleeing the crown would involve more dramatic gestures and less walking. Instead there was Osk, wheezing ahead of them like a bellows with opinions; Maren, moving through the gray scrubland with the silent economy of a woman who had spent twenty years being paid to find people and was now spending it not being found; Tam, hollow-eyed and mute, riding on Ada's back because his legs forgot how to be legs when the mountain wasn't looking at him; and a fox.

The fox had simply appeared on the second morning, trotting at the edge of their little procession like an escort who hadn't been hired but intended to invoice them anyway. It was red once, Ada guessed. Now its coat was patched with scar tissue, smooth and hairless, and its left ear was a crumpled crescent of melted cartilage — burned, badly, by something that had kept burning. It watched them with amber eyes that did not blink enough for a fox.

"Shoo," Ada had tried, on principle.

The fox had sat down and begun washing its face with the air of someone declining to be shooed until the end of time.

Now, three days out from the city, it still hadn't left, and Ada was beginning to have thoughts about it she didn't like.

"Your friend's back," Maren said, falling into step beside her. She nodded toward the fox, which was pacing them along a ridge of slag heaped up from some old smelting works. The ash-roads were littered with such things — the bones of the Dragon War's industry, kilns and foundries abandoned when the magic died out of them, or was outlawed out of them, which amounted to the same thing in law if not in fact.

"It's not my friend."

"You talk to it."

"I talk to *everything*. It's a character flaw." Ada shifted Tam's weight on her back. The boy was lighter than he should have been, the way bread goes light when something's been eaten out of the middle of it. "Besides, it doesn't answer."

"Doesn't it?"

Ada looked at her. Maren had that huntswoman's stillness, the kind that made you want to fill the silence just to prove you could. "It's a fox, Maren."

"Mm," said Maren, who had once tracked a smuggler for nine days through a snowstorm on the strength of a bent grass stem, and who was therefore constitutionally incapable of letting a thing go. "Foxes don't usually follow five strangers across open country. Foxes don't usually sit down and wait for stragglers. And foxes—" she nodded again, "—don't usually walk around fires."

Ada frowned. "What?"

"Watch."

She didn't have to watch long. Within the hour they came to one of the ash-roads' hazards: a seam of old coal waste that still smoldered underground, breathing thin ribbons of smoke up through the gray dirt. Osk detoured wide around it, coughing. Tam, on Ada's back, made a small sound — the first sound he'd made in days, a thin whine, and he pressed his face into her shoulder until the smoke was behind them.

The fox walked straight through the middle of it. Through the smoke, over the warm ground, unhurried, unbothered, its burned ear swiveled forward like a hand cupped to listen.

"Huh," said Ada.

"Mm," said Maren.

* * *

That night they camped in the shell of a foundry, under a roof of ribs and rust that let the stars in through the holes. Osk built a fire small enough to hide in a teacup, out of habit, and they ate barley porridge that tasted mostly of the pot. Tam sat close to the flames. He always sat close to the flames now, Ada had noticed. He held his hands out to the heat and stared into it with those terrible gray eyes, and sometimes his lips moved, shaping words that had no sound in them anymore, as though the fire remembered his voice even if nothing else did.

The fox came to sit near him. Not beside — near, the way you sit near a stranger at a sickbed.

And Tam reached out and put his hand on the fox's head, right on the ruined ear, and the fox let him, and closed its eyes, and something passed between them that Ada felt in her own chest like a plucked string.

Her gift woke before she decided anything. That was how it always worked — the charm-seller's smile, the market patter, the careful silence of years, all of it swept aside by the simple animal fact of a creature hurting nearby. She heard the fire's small crackling gossip. She heard Osk's bad knee complaining to him in its creaky old voice. And she heard the fox.

Not barking. Not yipping. *Words.*

*—cold, it's cold, why is it cold, I made it warm, I made everything warm, where is my—*

Ada stopped breathing.

The voice was a girl's voice. Young. Fractured, looping, snagging on itself like a torn hem — but underneath the brokenness, unmistakably, a child's mind, wandering the corridors of itself and finding the doors locked. The fox opened its eyes and looked directly at Ada, and its ears went flat, and the thought cut off mid-word like a snuffed candle.

"No," Ada said softly. Out loud. In front of everyone. She didn't notice she'd done it. "No, no, sweetheart—"

The fox scrambled backward, hackles up, a growl rattling out of it that was half animal terror and half something else, something that wanted very badly to say *you can hear me* and couldn't find the way.

"Ada?" Maren was on her feet, hand on her knife, scanning the dark beyond the foundry walls. "What is it? What did you see?"

"Not saw." Ada got up slowly, hands open and empty, the way you'd approach a spooked horse. Her heart was hammering against her ribs hard enough to bruise. "Maren. The fox. Listen to me. The fox is *saying things.*"

Osk lowered himself back onto his crate with the groan of a man whose knees had heard everything twice. "Aye. They do that, some of 'em."

"*What?*"

"The court keeps records, girl. I read 'em, back when I guarded the vault doors. Names come in, names go out, and some of the ones that go out—" He rubbed his face. "Gifted children, sold young. The mountain takes the soul and the body does what bodies do when nobody's home. Runs wild. Goes four-legged. There were pages about it. Nobody reads the appendices."

Ada sank slowly back down. The fox had retreated to the far wall, a smear of shadow and scar-tissue, watching her with those too-human eyes.

"Sefi," Ada said, guessing, reaching. "Is that your name? Sefi?"

The growl stopped.

Encouraged, Ada kept her voice low and warm, the voice she used on frightened horses. "Sefi. You're Sefi. Were you from Corvale? The river quarter? Did you—" She cast about. "Did you like the bells? Children like the bells."

The fox crept forward one cautious step. Then another. Then it did something no ordinary fox would ever do: it came and put its head under Ada's hand, deliberately, pressing the ruined ear into her palm, and the thought that spilled out was so raw and so simple that Ada's throat closed.

*nobody has said my name in six years*

"Oh," Ada managed. "Oh, sweetheart. Well. Sefi, then. Sefi it is. Sefi, we're going to get you back whatever they took, or die of trying, and I mean that literally, I've done the sums."

Sefi made a sound that was half bark and half sob and pushed her whole head into Ada's lap, and Tam leaned over and put his arms around the fox's neck, and Osk blew his nose loudly into a rag, claiming it was the ash, and Maren stood watch at the broken wall and pretended very hard that her eyes weren't shining.

Found family, Ada thought, looking around at all of them — the jailer, the huntswoman, the hollow boy, the lost girl. Assembled out of wreckage and debt and stubbornness. A garden planted in cracks.

It lasted until dawn.

* * *

They broke camp early, and it was Sefi who led them off the road. She trotted ahead, doubled back, insisted — insistent in the way of thoughts pushed hard against the inside of a locked door — *this way, this way, I know these roads, I ran them for years, the patrols don't come here* — and took them down into a dry culvert that wound between two ridges of slag.

"She says the patrol routes bend around this gully," Ada reported, translating without thinking about it, the way you'd translate for a foreign guest. "She's used it herself. Says the hunting's good here because nobody else comes."

"Fascinating," said Maren. "Ask her what she eats."

"Sefi, what do you— actually, never mind, I don't want to know."

*Rabbits,* said Sefi, with dignity. *Mostly rabbits. Once a goose, and I regret nothing.*

"She regrets nothing," Ada relayed solemnly, "regarding a goose."

It was almost — almost — easy. For half a morning, walking single file through the culvert with the sun coming gray-gold through the smoke haze, Ada nearly forgot what they were running from. Tam rode on Osk's shoulders and patted the old man's bald head like a drum. Sefi ranged ahead and brought back news of the road in a stream of fox-thoughts, and Ada translated, and Maren listened with her head tilted like a woman hearing music she'd been told didn't exist.

Which was, of course, precisely the problem.

Because Maren heard it too. Not the words — but Ada's half of the conversation, spoken aloud, soft and constant, in a voice that had no listener visible. Anyone watching would see a woman murmuring endearments and route reports to empty air, and anyone watching who wore the crown's colors would know exactly what that meant.

The patrol came out of the smoke at noon.

Three riders, blue-and-silver, hawks on their surcoats, materializing on the ridge above the culvert mouth the way patrols do — suddenly, and all at once, and far too late to run. Maren's knife was out before Ada finished turning. Osk pulled Tam down behind a slab of fallen furnace-wall. And Ada froze, dead center in the open, with Sefi's last thought still hanging in the air between them:

*run, they're here, RUN—*

"Hold!" called the lead rider, a young lieutenant with a voice full of promotion. "In the name of the Proclamation! You there — woman! Stand and be counted!"

Ada stood. Her mind raced through the old choreography — the charm-seller's shrug, the harmless grin, *just talking to myself, sir, bad habit, grief does funny things* — and found, to her horror, that she didn't want to use it. Because Sefi had pressed herself flat against Ada's leg, trembling, and Tam was peeking around the furnace slab with his gray eyes wide, and lying to the patrol meant leaving them all standing in the open with a huntswoman's knife drawn and a fugitive child and a fox that didn't run from fire.

"Sir," Ada called back, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "You've caught us fair. We're travelers."

"Travelers don't walk the ash-roads." The lieutenant nudged his horse down the slope. Behind him, one of the other riders lifted a horn to his lips — the calling-horn, the one that would bring every patrol within six miles down on their heads like a dropped ceiling. "And travelers don't talk to nobody, which is what our scout here says you were doing. Talking to nobody." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was worse. "Now. I'm going to ask you once, nicely. Are you gifted?"

The horn-blower drew breath.

Sefi looked up at Ada. And Ada looked down at Sefi, at the melted ear, at the girl locked inside the fox, and understood with perfect clarity that the moment had arrived — the moment she'd spent her whole life dodging, the crack her fragile life had been balanced on since the night market, since the herald's proclamation, since Tam stopped singing.

She could lie. Or she could spend the truth like coin, here, now, and buy them all a fighting chance.

Ada Villalobos took a breath, put her hand on the fox's burned ear, and said, clearly, for everyone on that ridge to hear:

"Yes. And if your man blows that horn, my friend the huntswoman is going to introduce your kneecaps to her knife, and *I* am going to tell the birds to follow you home."

The horn-blower hesitated.

On the ridge above, all three riders turned — slowly — to look at each other.

And somewhere far to the north, beneath the mountain, something ancient stirred in its sleep, and guttered, and waited.
