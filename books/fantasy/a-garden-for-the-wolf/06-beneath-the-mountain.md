## 6. Beneath the Mountain

The door in the mountain's flank had not been opened, by Osk's own admission, in eleven years.

"It opens," he said again, for the fourth time since dawn, patting the stone as though it were a mule that might spook. "It opens. It's just particular."

"Particular," Maren repeated. She shifted her pack and looked at Ada, who was crouched at the treeline with the fox-girl's thin fingers laced through hers, both of them watching the mountain the way one watches a sleeping dog of uncertain temper. "Osk. You were a jailer here. Jailer doors are famously unparticular. They open for anyone with a key or a grudge."

"This isn't a jailer's door." The old man's knuckles were white around his lantern. "This is the court's back stair. And the court—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter. "The court knows who I am."

Above them, the mountain called Corve rose black against a bruised morning sky, its crown lost in cloud. Somewhere inside it, beneath the granite and the old ceremonial halls, was a vault full of names. One of those names belonged to a boy of nine who had stopped singing in the spring.

Maren had spent six years as a royal huntswoman learning to read mountains. This one read wrong. Mountains breathed—snowmelt, wind through scree, the small industry of birds. Corve did none of that. It held its breath. And beneath the stillness, so faint she might have imagined it, something pulsed. Slow. Labored. Like a heart that had been beating too long on too little air.

*Like a fire guttering,* she thought, and did not let herself finish the sentence.

"All right," she said. "Everyone behind me. Sefi—" the fox-girl's ears swiveled at the name, which was progress; three days ago she had answered to nothing but a whistle "—if anything down there smells of iron and fear, you pull my sleeve. Not run. Pull. We stay together."

Sefi looked up at her with amber eyes that were far too human for comfort, and nodded once.

Ada stood last, brushing pine needles from her skirt, and gave Maren a look that Maren had learned to dread: the look of a woman about to say something reasonable in an unreasonable situation.

"You don't have to come," Ada said. "You've got a stolen writ and a face every patrol in the province knows. There's still a road south."

"There is," Maren agreed. "And there's a boy in there who hummed while he swept my neighbor's step, and a woman who pulled me out of the Tanner's Row fire when everyone else ran, and a debt I've been carrying like a stone in my boot for eight years." She rolled her shoulders. "Besides. If I leave now, you'll all die down here doing something stupid, and I'll have to haunt myself."

Ada smiled—the crooked, tired smile that had been undoing Maren's better judgment since the night market—and touched her shoulder once, briefly, before turning to the door.

Osk set his palm flat against the stone and said, in a voice gone rusty with disuse, "Hartwell. Oskar Hartwell. Warden, third watch. I'm coming home."

For a long moment, nothing. Then the mountain exhaled—a groan of old hinges somewhere deep in the rock—and a seam of darkness split the cliff face, wide enough for one person, breathing out cold air that smelled of dust and tallow and, underneath it all, faintly, impossibly, of smoke and roses.

Maren went first, because that was what she was for.

* * *

The back stair went down farther than any stair had a right to. Three hundred steps, then four hundred, the walls sweating cold, Osk's lantern throwing their shadows huge and wavering against the stone. The fox-girl pressed close to Ada's side. Even Tam's sparrow, riding in Ada's hood where it had refused to leave since the city, had gone silent and puffed, feathers flat.

"No guards?" Ada whispered.

"There were never guards," Osk said. "That's the thing nobody understood. The court doesn't need guards. It needs *clerks*." His laugh came out wrong. "I stood at a door for twenty years holding a key nobody ever asked for, because the thing inside never tried to leave. Why would it? Where would it go? It can't fit through a door."

"What is it?" Maren asked, though her skin had already begun whispering the answer.

"You'll see it," Osk said. "I won't spoil it. Spoiling it feels like a mercy, and I've given this place enough mercies."

At the bottom of the stair, the passage opened without warning into a gallery so vast that the lantern light simply gave up, swallowed by dark above and dark below. Maren heard water—no. Not water. A slow, liquid dripping, but each drop rang, faintly, like a struck bell. And everywhere, filling the space the way weather fills a valley, was heat. Dry, enormous, patient heat, the kind that comes off a forge or a fevered body.

Then the light found it, and Maren forgot how to be a huntswoman.

It lay coiled in the vault's heart like a hill that had decided to breathe: scales the color of banked coals, dull now, ash-grey along the ridges, wings folded tight against flanks that rose and fell with the slow pulse she'd felt from outside. Its head rested on the stone, eyes half-lidded, and from its nostrils curled a thread of flame no brighter than a candle—that same smell of smoke and roses, stronger here, almost sweet.

A dragon. The last dragon. Smaller than the war-banners promised, older than the law that outlawed its kind, and dying by inches in a room built to hold it.

"Oh," Ada breathed. Beside her, Sefi made a sound Maren had never heard from her—not a fox's whine, but something between a word and a sob.

And the dragon's eye opened.

It was the size of a cart wheel and gold as lamplight, and it moved—not the head, not the body, only the eye—to find them across the dark. Maren's hand went to her knife out of pure animal habit, and she was ashamed of it before the blade cleared leather.

"Huntswoman," said a voice, and it came from everywhere, from the stone itself, dry as pages turning. It sounded tired the way old men sound tired, the way rivers sound tired at their mouths. "You carry a writ. I can smell the seal. Come closer, little wolf-cloak. It has been eleven years since anyone brought me anything but silence."

* * *

They crossed the gallery floor in single file, and Maren saw now what the dark had hidden: shelves. Racks upon racks of them, rising into the black overhead, and on every shelf, rows of small glass vessels glowing with a light like dawn through fog. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one labeled in a clerk's careful hand.

Each one flickering in time with the dragon's breath.

When the great lungs drew in, the vessels brightened. When they exhaled, they dimmed. All together. All in rhythm. An entire harvest of souls kept alive—or kept *something*, kept *lit*—by the guttering fire of a chained and dying creature.

"Tam," Ada whispered. She was already moving down the rows, fingers flying over labels, lips shaping names. "Villalobos—no—Tamasin, Torvald—here. Here!" She lifted a vessel from its cradle with both hands, the way one lifts a sleeping child. Inside, the light steadied, and Maren would have sworn—would testify before any court, though there were no courts left she trusted—that the light leaned toward Ada's warmth like a plant toward a window.

"That's him?" Maren asked quietly.

"That's him." Ada's voice cracked clean through. "He's so *small*. He was always small, but—he's smaller in there. Like the glass is pressing him down."

"The ledgers do that," Osk said hoarsely. He had stopped a respectful distance from the dragon, hat in hand, an old man come to judgment. "A soul in a ledger keeps its shape. Take it out, keep it out, and it starts to remember being free. That's why they need the fire. The fire holds the shape. Without it—" He gestured at the racks, and for once the old jailer had nothing tidy to say.

"Without it, they wake up," said the dragon, "and then they grieve, and grief burns hotter than any flame I have left. So I hold them gently. It is the only kindness permitted me." The vast eye turned to Osk. "Warden. You sold me forty-one names. Do you remember them?"

"I remember every one," Osk whispered.

"Good. Then you may begin paying." But there was no cruelty in it—only a weariness so profound it circled back around to gentleness. The eye slid on, to Maren. "And you. Huntswoman. You came armed into my house. Tell me why I should mind."

Maren found her voice somewhere near her boots. "Because I'm going to help empty your shelves," she said. "Every name. Starting with the boy."

Something moved through the great coils—a shiver, a settling, the closest a dying mountain could come to laughter. "Ambitious. The chancellor restocks faster than you can carry. And the hunt patrols the upper halls with silver nets and worse." The nostril-flame guttered, brightened. "But you are the first in eleven years to say *every* name instead of *one* name. So I will tell you what I told no warden, no clerk, no chancellor." The dragon's head lifted, slow as a season changing, and the candle-flame of its breath rose with it, and in that light Maren saw the truth stamped into the great sad face:

"They call me the jailer. I am the prisoner. The chancellor holds my chain, and the chain is a bargain older than your law: my fire keeps his harvest alive, and his harvest keeps me alive, and neither of us has been asked whether we want the arrangement." The gold eye fixed on Ada, on the small glowing vessel in her hands. "Break the bargain, girl-with-the-beast-gift, and we both burn free. Or fail, and we both burn anyway. There is no third door."

Behind them, far up the back stair, iron rang against stone.

Once. Twice. Then a horn, thin and echoing—the hunt signal Maren had blown herself a hundred times, and knew the meaning of by heart.

*Found them.*

Sefi's claws pricked Maren's wrist, pulling her sleeve, pointing down the gallery's far dark—where, faint as fireflies, a second line of lights was kindling. Not souls. Lanterns. Patrol lanterns, descending from the front gates.

The dragon watched them all with its terrible patient eye, and said, almost kindly:

"Now, little wolves. Choose quickly. I haven't much fire left for either ending."

Maren drew her knife, turned to face the lanterns, and heard Ada behind her take a breath that meant a bargain was about to be struck.
