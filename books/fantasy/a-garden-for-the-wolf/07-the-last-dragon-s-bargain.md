## 7. The Last Dragon's Bargain

The dragon was smaller than Ada had imagined.

She had spent the whole descent building a monster in her head—wings like ship-sails, a voice like a landslide, the kind of creature that filled a cavern the way a king fills a throne room. What she found instead, at the bottom of the court's deepest vault, was a dragon curled up like an old dog by a dying hearth. Her scales were the color of banked coals, dull at the edges, bright only along the ridge of her spine where something still smoldered. She was perhaps the size of a plow horse. Perhaps smaller. The great cavern around her was black with soot and silence, and in the center of it, above the dragon, hung a single flame no bigger than a candle.

That flame was the only warm thing in the mountain.

"Close the door," the dragon said. Her voice was dry as autumn leaves, but it carried, the way a whisper carries in a church. "The drafts bother me. Everything bothers me, lately. That is what dying is, I have learned. Everything bothers you, and then nothing does."

Ada stood very still. Behind her, the vault door Osk had unbarred hung open on its ancient hinges, and beyond it the dark throat of the passage where Maren and the fox-girl waited. Osk stood at her shoulder, breathing like a man climbing stairs.

"You speak," Ada said, and immediately felt foolish.

"And you listen. To more than words, I think." The dragon's eye opened—a slit of molten gold, filmed at the edge like an old man's eye. "I smelled you coming down three turnings ago. Horse-speech. Sparrow-speech. Fox." The golden eye found her, and Ada felt it the way she felt the cart-horse's fear in the market: as a touch, gentle and enormous. "You are the one who talks to beasts. They told me a listener was coming. The rats are terrible gossips."

"The rats told you?"

"The rats tell everyone everything. It's why nobody trusts them." The dragon sighed, and the little flame above her guttered, and somewhere far up the mountain Ada heard—a feeling more than a sound—hundreds of small lights flicker with it. "Sit, charm-seller. My knees are gone. I will not rise to threaten you, and you will not run, because you have come too far to run. We may as well be honest with each other. It is a rare meal these days. I intend to enjoy it."

Ada sat. The stone was cold through her skirt. Osk remained standing, wringing his cap in his hands like a washerwoman, until the dragon said, "You too, jailer. Sit. You sold names to keep your daughter, and you have hated yourself every day since, and sitting will not make it worse."

Osk sat.

* * *

"Ember-that-Was," the dragon said, when Ada asked her name. "That is what they called me. Now they call me the Furnace, or the Engine, or the Asset, depending on which clerk is writing. I was the last to sign the Accord after the Dragon War. Did you know that? The others flew east, over the sea, and I stayed because someone had to hold the flame. They said it would be for a hundred years. It has been a hundred and forty, and the chancellors keep coming with their ledgers, and I keep burning, and the flame keeps getting smaller."

She lifted one claw—a movement like a drawbridge deciding against it—and gestured at the candle-flame hovering above her.

"You have seen the ledgers. You have seen the names."

"Tam," Ada said. "A boy from my street. His father sold him."

"His father sold him, the chancellor bought him, and I keep him." The dragon's voice did not change, but the flame dipped. "That is the arrangement, charm-seller. The gifted are harvested—their gifts, their souls, the bright part of them—and the bright part is stored here, in my fire, because it is the only fire in the world that can hold a soul without consuming it. Every soul in those racks is a coal I am keeping lit. There are three hundred and eleven. I know each name. I say them every night, so they do not forget themselves."

Ada's throat was tight. "And when you die?"

"Then the flame goes out, and three hundred and eleven souls go out with it. Unless—" the golden eye gleamed, and there was something in it that was almost, terribly, like humor— "unless a bargain is struck. There is always a bargain. This mountain runs on bargains. It is the one law that survived the war."

"Name it."

"One soul freed for one memory surrendered." The dragon said it the way a merchant quotes a price she has quoted ten thousand times, and Ada heard in her voice how much she hated the price, and how powerless she was to change it. "A true memory. Whole. Given up so completely that you will not know you ever had it. You will feel the shape of the hole where it was, the way an amputee feels the hand. But you will not know what was in it. That is the trade. It is not a kind trade. It is the only trade I have."

"Why memories?" Ada whispered. "Why not years, or blood, or—"

"Because memory is the soul's firewood, little listener. A soul is only the stories a life has made. When I free one of the kept, I must feed the flame something of equal weight to fill the space they leave, or the whole rack gutters. And I cannot give my own memories, because I gave those up a hundred years ago to keep the flame lit at all." She said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "I am mostly smoke now, charm-seller. What is left of me is duty and one bargain's worth of power. So. Whom have you come to buy?"

"Tam," Ada said, without hesitation, because there was no hesitation to have. "The boy. Tam Wicker. His soul was sold three weeks ago and he stopped singing, and he is nine years old, and no one—"

"Tam Wicker." The dragon closed her eye. Her lips moved, a silent recitation, an old woman counting her beads. "Yes. He is near the front of the rack. The new ones always are. He sings in his sleep, you know. Even hollowed, the part I keep still sings. It is the only comfort in this place, hearing him." She opened her eye. "Then it is done. One memory, freely given. Which shall it be?"

And that was when Ada made the mistake of hesitating.

* * *

It was a small hesitation. A breath. But the dragon had been making bargains for a hundred and forty years, and she caught it the way a fisherman catches a twitch of the line.

"You have another name in your heart," the dragon said softly. "I can smell it. Grief has a scent, you know. Old grief smells of dust. Fresh grief smells of rain. Yours—" the nostrils flared, the great head tilting— "yours smells of rain that never fell. A name you have carried a long time, hoping."

Ada's hands had gone cold in her lap.

"Who?" the dragon asked.

And Ada heard herself say it, in the smallest voice she had used since childhood: "My mother."

The silence that followed was long, and full of the sound of the flame burning, which was not a sound at all but a feeling, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Her name," said the dragon.

"Lucía Villalobos." The words came out of Ada like teeth. "She was taken when I was seven. They said she ran away. They said—everyone said. My father said it until the day he died, because it was easier than the truth, and I believed him because I was seven, and believing him was easier too." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Is she here? Is she in your racks?"

The dragon was quiet for a long moment. Then, gently, in the voice one uses for a wound: "Say the year."

Ada said the year.

The dragon's eye closed. "Lucía Villalobos. The listener. Yes." A pause, and Ada understood that the pause was mercy, and braced herself anyway. "She is here. Two hundred and six. She has been here twenty-three years, charm-seller. I say her name every night with the others."

The world went very simple and very far away. Ada was aware of Osk making a small wounded sound beside her. She was aware of the cold stone, the candle-flame, the smell of soot and old smoke. Mostly she was aware of a single thought, enormous and terrible and warm:

*She is alive. My mother is alive. She has been alive all along, twenty minutes' walk from the market stall where I sold luck to strangers.*

"I can buy her back," Ada said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "The trade. One soul for one memory. I could—Tam has a father. Tam has a street full of people who know his name. My mother has no one but me, and I didn't even know she—"

"Stop," said the dragon.

"You don't understand. Twenty-three years. I could have her back. I could hear her voice. I don't even remember it anymore, that's the— I was seven, and the memories are worn through like old coins, and I could have her back and she could tell me—"

"Charm-seller." The dragon's voice was iron wrapped in wool. "Look at me."

Ada looked. The golden eye held her, and in it she saw her own face, small and wet and lit by the little flame.

"Tam Wicker is nine," the dragon said, "and his father sold him, and no one is coming for him but you. Lucía Villalobos has been kept for twenty-three years, and she will be kept tomorrow, and the day after, and she is not in pain—the kept do not suffer, they sleep, they dream, they sing—and the man who will be chancellor after this one, and the one after that, will come down here with new ledgers whether you buy her back or not. But if you walk out of this mountain with your mother's hand in yours and Tam still hollow, you will spend the rest of your life listening to him not sing. And you will know, every day, in the shape of the hole where your memory was, that you had a choice, and you chose the face you loved over the child who needed you."

"That's cruel."

"Yes," said the dragon. "I have had a hundred and forty years to become cruel. It is the one gift this court gave me freely." The great head lowered, until the dragon's breath stirred Ada's hair, warm and smelling of hearth-smoke and, faintly, impossibly, of roses. "But I will tell you what I tell no one, because you are a listener, and listeners deserve one true thing. Your mother, when she was brought here, asked me for a favor. It cost me nothing—she was already kept, her wish was already her own—and so I granted it. Do you know what she asked?"

Ada could not speak. She shook her head.

"She asked me to remember her daughter's name. Ada. She said it once, and I have said it every night for twenty-three years, along with my three hundred and eleven. *Ada,*" the dragon said, and the name in that dry old voice was like a hand on the back of the neck, gentle and unescapable. "*Ada Villalobos. The listener. She talks to sparrows. She will be all right, because she is brave, and because someone once told her that brave is just frightened with its boots on.*"

Ada made a sound that was not a word.

"Her last free thought was of you," the dragon said. "And her first kept thought, every night since, is of you. If you buy her back, you buy her back into a city where the hunt still runs, where the chancellor still harvests, where a woman who speaks to beasts will last a week before she is racked beside her daughter. Or—" and here the golden eye glinted, and there was that terrible humor again, and beneath it something that might have been hope, ancient and starved— "or you do what your mother would have done, which is burn the whole rotten ledger to the ground, and free them all, and never have to choose between two names ever again."

"I can't free them all. The trade is one for one. I have one memory to give. Maybe two. Not three hundred and eleven."

"No," the dragon agreed. "You don't." She settled back, and the flame above her guttered, and far above them Ada felt the racks shiver. "But the flame is mine, charm-seller, and I am dying anyway, and a dying fire can be spent all at once or a little at a time. There is a man in this mountain who sold his own harvest to buy his power. His name is written in his own hand in the first ledger. And there is a huntswoman at your back with a stolen writ, and a jailer who knows which walls are thin, and a fox who was a girl, and you—who talks to beasts, and beasts, little listener, remember what humans burn."

The dragon's eye held hers.

"So. The bargain stands as I named it. One soul for one memory. Choose your name, spend your memory, and walk out with what you bought." A pause, long as a held breath. "Or tell me you want them *all*, and we will find a larger bargain, and it will cost more than you have, and we will pay it anyway."

Above them, the little flame burned on. Somewhere in its light, three hundred and eleven souls slept and dreamed, and one of them, two hundred and six, was dreaming of a girl with her boots on.

Ada wiped her face with the heel of her hand. When she spoke, her voice was rough but steady, and it did not sound small anymore.

"Tell me about the larger bargain."

The dragon smiled. It was not a comforting sight. It was the best thing Ada had ever seen.

"First," said Ember-that-Was, "you will need to steal a flame. And I am afraid, charm-seller, that the only hands it will not burn are hands that have already given something away. So we begin with your memory after all—and we begin with the one you least want to lose."

* * *

They talked until Ada's knees ached and the candle-flame above the dragon had burned low as a dying match, and what they built between them was terrible and simple and might, if every piece held, unmake the court of Corvale. When it was done, the dragon said, "Go. Your huntswoman is holding the door against six men with crossbows, and your jailer is crying, and your fox is setting fire to things, which I approve of enormously. And charm-seller—" the golden eye caught her at the threshold, one last glint of ember in the dark— "when you give me your memory, and you feel the hole where it was: do not go looking for what filled it. Some things are given so that others may be kept. That is not a tragedy. That is a garden."

Ada climbed the dark stair with the bargain folded in her chest like a coal, and above her, muffled by stone, she could hear Maren shouting, and the fox-girl snarling, and the unmistakable crackle of ledgers beginning, at last, to burn.
