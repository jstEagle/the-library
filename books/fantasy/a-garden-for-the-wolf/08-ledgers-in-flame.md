## 8. Ledgers in Flame

Osk had spent thirty years learning how doors worked.

Not the way a carpenter knows them—hinges and grain and the honest weight of oak—but the way a jailer knows them: what they were afraid of, what they wanted, which of them could be flattered and which must be bullied. The vault doors beneath the mountain court were the proudest doors he had ever met. They were iron-faced and rune-locked, and they believed, with the deep conviction of things built by clever men for cruel purposes, that they would never open again.

"Come now," Osk murmured to them, his palms flat against the cold. "You remember me."

The runes flickered, sullen as old coals.

He remembered them too. He had stood on this side of these very doors twenty-two years ago, holding a lantern while men in chancellor's gray carried out ledgers that weighed more than ledgers should, and he had told himself it was none of his business, and he had been paid well enough to believe it. His daughter had eaten that winter. He had told himself that too.

The runes brightened reluctantly, one by one, like an old man being woken from a nap he'd earned.

"That's it," Osk said. "You're not betraying anyone. The man who built you is dead, and the man who uses you is a thief, and I—" His voice caught on something rusty. "I'm just an old jailer doing the last job he should have done first."

Behind him, down the corridor of the deep vaults, Ada's voice rose and fell in the dragon's chamber—a bargaining voice, low and steady, though he could hear the crack in it the way you can hear a crack in a bell if you've rung enough bells. Osk did not let himself listen. There was nothing he could do about bargains. Doors, though. Doors he understood.

The great bolts drew back one at a time, each with a groan like a confession.

* * *

Maren counted six of them coming up the ash-road, and knew there would be more behind.

They moved the way she used to move—lanterns hooded, boots wrapped, spread in a line across the narrow place where the mountain's throat narrowed to a walkway barely wide enough for two. Captain Vess was among them. Of course she was. Vess had never once trusted Maren, not in eleven years of shared rations and shared watches, and tonight Vess's distrust was going to be the most useful thing about her.

Because Maren was already standing in the middle of the walkway, between the patrol and the vault door, and she had put her stolen writ on a stone where the lantern-light could find it, and beside the writ she had laid her sword.

"You're under arrest," Vess said, without much surprise. "Or you're mad. I've honestly never ruled it out."

"I'm standing here," Maren said. "That's the whole of my plan so far. I'm hoping the rest occurs to me."

Vess came forward alone, as protocol demanded, and stopped just outside arm's reach, which was also protocol, and looked at the writ, and then at Maren, and then past her shoulder at the vault door, which was, at that precise moment, finishing its long iron sigh and swinging open onto darkness.

"Maren," Vess said quietly. "What's behind that door?"

"Names," Maren said. "About four hundred of them. Children, mostly." She watched Vess's face do the thing that every face in Corvale did when someone said the word *children* in connection with the court—the small flinch, the quick look away, the arithmetic of a career measured against a conscience. She'd done the same arithmetic herself for years. It never came out even. That was rather the point.

"The hunt orders say the gifted are harboring fugitives," Vess said. "It doesn't say anything about—"

"No," Maren agreed. "It doesn't. Chancellor Wrenn writes his orders very carefully. You should ask yourself why."

Behind Vess, the five other hunters shifted. Maren knew three of them by name and all of them by habit. They were not monsters. That was the tragedy of the whole rotten machine—it ran on ordinary people who were tired, and cold, and wanted to go home to their suppers.

Then the fox came out of the dark.

* * *

Osk heard the shouting start above and did not stop working.

There were nine inner doors between the vault entrance and the ledger hall, and each one was a smaller, meaner cousin of the great gate—locks he had oiled, wards he had fed passwords to, back when feeding passwords to things was a jailer's daily bread and nobody thought twice about what the passwords unlocked. His hands remembered everything. That was the horror of it. Twenty-two years of complicity, stored in his knuckles, ready to serve.

"Third door's a liar," he muttered to himself, working the false catch that looked real and ignoring the real catch that looked decorative. "Always was. Built her myself, gods forgive me."

The fourth door opened onto the ledger hall, and Osk stopped.

He had carried ledgers out of this room once, by lantern-light, and told himself they were tax records. Now he saw them whole: rack upon rack of bound volumes rising to the ceiling, thousands of names in chancellor's ink, and over it all a warmth that had no business being warm. The dragon's fire came through vents in the far wall, guttering, amber, barely alive—and every tongue of flame bent toward the racks like a mother bending toward cradles. The souls in those books were alive because the fire refused to let them be otherwise. The fire was dying, and it was spending its last strength keeping stolen children breathing.

"Well," Osk said, and his voice broke in the middle, and he let it. "Well, then."

He took out the crowbar he'd brought for exactly this purpose and felt suddenly ridiculous, an old man with an iron bar against four hundred years of court magic. Then he thought of Tam's gray eyes, and his daughter's face the winter he'd sold his first name, and he swung it into the nearest rack's mounting bracket with thirty years of guilt behind it.

The bracket shrieked. Somewhere above, a woman shouted Maren's name in a voice that meant blood.

"Hold on, girl," Osk told the empty air, and swung again.

* * *

The fox hit the patrol line like a thrown torch.

Maren had seen the girl-thing in the fox only in glimpses—a posture too human, eyes that tracked faces instead of movement—and she had wondered, idly, what Sefi would do in a fight. The answer was: everything at once. The fox went through the lantern-line in a streak of red and scar, and two hooded lights went spinning off the walkway into the dark, and the patrol's neat formation became five people very suddenly concerned with not falling off a mountain.

"Don't hurt them!" Maren shouted, because apparently she was giving tactical advice to a fox now, and because the fox had paused with her teeth an inch from a young hunter's throat, and because the young hunter was maybe nineteen and crying.

The fox looked at Maren. Something passed between them—not words, exactly, but the shape of a question: *how much?*

"As much as it takes," Maren said. "No more."

Vess chose that moment to draw steel, which settled the question of whether this would be a conversation. Maren scooped up her sword and met her, and the ring of it went down the ash-road like a bell tolling.

Vess was good. Maren had trained half this patrol herself, and it stung to discover how well. They fought along the narrow way, boot-edges over the drop, breath smoking in the cold, and Maren took a cut along her forearm she barely felt and gave back a pommel-blow to Vess's jaw that she definitely felt. The captain staggered, spat blood, and grinned with red teeth.

"Eleven years," Vess said, "and I finally get to know what you actually are."

"Tired," Maren said, and hit her again.

But there were more lanterns coming up the road below—she could see them bobbing, eight, ten, a dozen—and her arm was bleeding freely now, and the vault door behind her stood open like a mouth, and somewhere inside it an old man was breaking brackets and a fox was about to learn what fire really was.

Maren planted herself in the gap, back to the door, sword up, and understood with perfect clarity that she was going to hold this spot until her body stopped agreeing to the arrangement.

"All right," she told the approaching lanterns. "Line up. I'll do this all night if I have to. I've got nowhere better to be."

* * *

The seventh rack came down with a crash that Osk felt in his teeth, and the fox arrived.

She flowed through the door he'd opened, and she stopped in the middle of the ledger hall, and Osk saw her see it—the racks, the names, the guttering fire bending toward its captives—and he saw the girl rise up inside the animal like a sun coming over a hill. Her fur lifted. The burn-scarred ear stood straight up for the first time since he'd known her.

"Sefi," Osk said gently. "Careful, little one. That fire's a friend. It's the paper we want burning, not the—"

She was already moving.

She didn't breathe fire—she wasn't a dragon, whatever she'd been before some clerk wrote her name in a book. What she did was worse, or better: she *pulled*. Osk watched her dart along the racks and touch each one with her nose, quick as a kiss, and where she touched, the dragon-fire leapt. It came through the vents in answering tongues, eager, starving for permission, and the ledgers began to burn—not with destruction, Osk realized, but with release. Each page as it caught flared a color that had no name, and each flare was a sound like a held breath let go, and the names were not being destroyed. They were being *read aloud*, at last, by fire.

A volume near Osk's head burst open of its own accord. He caught one glimpse of the page—a child's name, a date, a price—and then light took it, and the light went up through the ceiling cracks toward the world above like seeds on wind.

"Gods keep them," Osk whispered, and pulled down the eighth rack with his bare hands, crowbar forgotten, splinters and all.

Above, faintly, he heard Maren shout something defiant, and heard many boots answer, and understood that the door behind her was the only door that mattered now. Everything they were doing in this room was worth exactly as much as the time she bought them.

He worked faster.

* * *

Maren counted her wounds the way she counted everything: without sentiment. Forearm, deep. Thigh, shallow. Ribs, probably cracked, definitely complaining. Four hunters down or fled. Vess on one knee, spitting blood, no longer grinning.

And still the lanterns kept coming up the road.

"Maren." Vess's voice was hoarse. "Whatever's in there—whatever they told you—it isn't worth dying on a mountainside for."

Maren laughed, and it cost her a rib. "That's the funny part, Vess. Nobody told me anything. A horse told me once. And a boy's sparrow. And a fox." She rolled her shoulder, set her feet. "Turns out the animals are the only ones in this city telling the truth."

The lead lanterns were close enough now that she could see faces—young faces, tired faces, faces she'd eaten supper with—and she raised her sword, and behind her, from deep in the mountain, came a sound she would remember for the rest of her life:

Singing.

Hundreds of voices, thin and high and unmistakably children's, pouring up through the open vault door and out into the night air for the first time in years. The hunters froze. Every lantern on the ash-road stopped moving.

Maren lowered her sword half an inch, and smiled at all of them with bloody teeth.

"There," she said. "Now you've heard it too. So the only question left is—which side of that door are you standing on?"

And down in the dark, in the chamber where a dying dragon kept its last promise, Ada Villalobos began to speak the words of a bargain that would cost her everything she had.
