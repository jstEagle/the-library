## 9. The Chancellor's Harvest

The dragon's flame was the color of a candle about to go out.

Ada stood in the vault beneath the mountain, in a room that was not a room so much as a throat—the whole court of Corvale had been built above it like a lid on a pot, and here at the bottom of everything sat Ember-that-Was, chained not with iron but with arithmetic. Racks of ledgers rose around the beast in tiers, thousands of them, each spine inked with a name, and from every open page a thin silver thread ran upward into the dark, and all the threads gathered at the dragon's breast like rain finding a gutter.

The dragon's fire burned low and steady in a brazier of its own ribs—no, Ada corrected herself, watching closer: the fire *was* the dragon now, or what remained. The great body was stone-colored, half-shadow, wings folded like a beggar's coat. Only the eyes still moved, and they were kind, which was somehow the worst thing in the whole vault.

"You are the one who speaks," Ember-that-Was said. Its voice came not through air but through the threads, humming up through the ledger pages, through the floor, through the bones of her feet. "I have felt you coming for three days. You talk to horses."

"I talk to anything that will put up with me," Ada said. Her voice cracked on the joke, which ruined it, which was probably honest.

Behind her, Osk stood in the doorway he had spent forty years learning to open, his big hands hanging empty at his sides. Maren was somewhere above, holding the stair with a stolen writ and a crossbow and one working arm. And Sefi—the fox-girl, the girl who had forgotten she was a girl—crouched at the edge of the firelight with her ears flat, growling softly at the ledgers as if they were a pack of rival dogs.

"Tam," Ada said. "His name is Tam Wrenlow. He's nine. He used to sing while he swept his father's step, badly, on purpose, because it made the old man laugh. His soul is in one of your books."

"He is in many of my books," the dragon said gently. "They copy everything. It is how the chancellor keeps count." A plume of smoke curled from its nostrils, gray as old wool. "You wish to buy him out."

"I don't have money."

"No one has money down here. We trade in heavier coin." The dragon's gaze settled on her, patient as winter. "One soul freed for one memory surrendered. Yours must be true. Yours must be yours. That is the only law left in this place that I still enforce."

Ada had known this since the vault doors opened. She had walked down six hundred steps rehearsing the bargain the way you rehearse a confession. But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different countries.

"My mother," she said, before she could stop herself. "Osk told me her name might be in the kept. Marisol Villalobos. She was taken when I was seven. I have—" Her hands did something without her permission, twisting together. "I have four memories of her. Five if you count smells. Bread and river-smoke and the way she hummed off-key and how her sleeves smelled of chamomile and one more thing I've never told anyone. That's all I have. That's my whole inheritance."

"Yes," said the dragon. "She is here. Page nine thousand and some. She has been comfortable, as much as any kept soul is comfortable. She dreams of a garden."

Ada's knees nearly went. She heard herself make a small sound, and Sefi pressed warm against her leg, and even that kindness hurt, because Sefi had lost her own name to these racks and knew exactly what was being weighed.

"So I could buy her back," Ada whispered. "One memory for one soul. I could give you the memory of Tam sweeping, or the memory of Maren laughing, or—no. No, they'd want mine. My mother-memories. One of them. I could trade one and keep four, and walk out of here with my mother's hand in mine, and Tam would stay gray-eyed forever, singing nothing, sweeping nothing."

The vault was quiet except for the gutter of the flame and the far-off sound of Maren's crossbow being cocked above them, once, twice—a metronome of someone buying time.

"The chancellor is coming," the dragon said. "He comes every seventh night to feed. He will be here within the hour, and he will bring the court's scribes, and if the ledgers burn before he arrives, everything he has stolen goes with them. If they do not burn, they never will. My fire lasts perhaps one hour more. After that I am only a shape, and the souls in these books go out like lamps." The ancient eyes blinked, slow. "So you see, child, you cannot save everyone. You can only choose what burns first."

"That's a horrible choice."

"It is the only kind worth making. The easy ones are just habits."

Ada looked at the racks. Somewhere in those tiers was her mother, dreaming of a garden she'd never planted. Somewhere else was Tam, hollowed out, his sparrow waiting on a windowsill in Corvale for a boy who no longer whistled at it. And somewhere in her own chest were five memories, small as seeds, all she had.

Her mother had been taken by this machine. Her mother would tear the machine apart with her bare hands if she had hands to tear with. And her mother—this was the thought that undid her, that came not from memory but from somewhere deeper than memory, from the marrow where mothers live even after they're gone—her mother would rather be ash than be the reason a child stayed hollow.

"Bread and river-smoke," Ada said aloud, naming them, holding them up to the light of the dying fire like coins. "Chamomile sleeves. The off-key humming." She swallowed. "And the fifth one. Keep the fifth one last. Let me say goodbye to it properly."

* * *

The fifth memory was this: rain, and a doorway, and her mother kneeling to button Ada's coat, and saying—not *I love you*, which anyone might say—but "Look at you. Look at *you*." With such astonishment, such plain delight, as though Ada were a miracle that had happened overnight and her mother was only now being told.

Ada gave it away slowly, the way you let go of a rope hand over hand over black water.

The dragon received it the way the earth receives rain. The silver thread from page nine-thousand-and-some brightened; somewhere in the dark above, Ada felt—she would always feel it, even after—a door opening, a woman waking, a garden dreamed of becoming a garden longed for. Freed. Her mother was free, and Ada was already forgetting why her chest ached, only that it ached, only that there had been something there, bread and river-smoke and—

"What did I lose?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away.

"A coat," said the dragon softly. "Buttons in the rain. You will not miss the shape of it. You will only miss that there was a shape."

"And Tam?"

"Take the ledger. Tear the page yourself. It must be a living hand."

Ada found the book—Wrenlow, T., and her fingers shook so badly Osk had to steady her wrist—and she tore the page out, and the thread snapped with a sound like a harp string breaking, and high above them, in a cell in Corvale, a boy gasped like a swimmer surfacing and began, immediately, tunelessly, gloriously, to sing.

Ada laughed and sobbed in the same breath. Then the vault doors boomed.

* * *

The chancellor came down his own throat like a man descending into his pantry.

He was smaller than Ada had imagined, Chancellor Vessin, a neat gray man with neat gray hands, and yet the air bent around him the way air bends over a forge. She could see it now—now that she stood in the dragon's light—see the stolen glow threaded through him like wire through wax. Dozens of gifts. Hundreds. The speech-of-beasts she recognized with a sick lurch, because it moved his mouth the way it moved hers. He had harvested speakers and seers and fire-hands and dream-walkers, and worn them all at once, a coat made of people.

Behind him came the scribes, and behind the scribes, filling the doorway, the court itself—lords and ladies drawn down by the noise, by rumor, by the smell of burning, their faces candle-pale in the dragon-light.

"Kill the beast," Vessin said mildly, "and arrest the rest."

"Your writs are in the fire already," Maren's voice called down the stair, ragged but steady. She appeared at the top landing, arm bleeding through a bandage, crossbow leveled. "I checked. Very flammable, bureaucracy."

Vessin didn't even look at her. He looked at Ada, and smiled the smile of a man who has read every ledger twice. "The charm-seller. I wondered whose gift kept slipping my nets. Give me the page you're holding, girl, and I'll only take the fox."

Sefi's hackles went up. And Ada—Ada who had spent her whole life making herself small, selling luck in thimbles, whispering comfort to cart-horses where no one could see—stood up straight.

"You want to know what I am?" she said. "Ask him. Ask him what *he* is. Go on. Look closely, my lords and ladies. Look at the chancellor's shadow."

And because curiosity is the one law stronger than fear, they looked.

Ember-that-Was understood then, Ada was sure of it. Understood that this—this assembled court, this audience of comfortable, complicit, candle-pale faces—was the last and greatest use of a fire that had maybe minutes left. The dragon drew breath for the first time in a hundred years, not to warm a brazier but to *burn*, and Ada opened her gift wide, wider than she had ever dared, and spoke—not to a horse, not to a fox, but to a room full of human beings:

"*Look at him.*"

Dragon-fire does not destroy. Everyone believes it destroys, but what it truly does is reveal—it burns away everything that is borrowed, everything that is stolen, everything that is not truly yours, until only the truth stands in the ashes. The flame leapt from the brazier, caught the ledger racks, and the racks went up like autumn fields, ten thousand names blazing silver-bright, and the light poured over Chancellor Vessin and showed the court exactly what their tidy gray man was wearing.

They saw the gifts. All of them. They saw children's voices stitched into his hands, a huntswoman's courage sewn under his skin, an old woman's second sight glinting in his eyes like glass buttons. They saw him the way the hunted had always seen him. And the stolen magic, feeling its owners' names turn to free ash above it, simply *left*—pouring out of him like water from a cut sack, rising, scattering, going home to wherever home was now.

Vessin stood in the middle of the vault, suddenly ordinary. Small. Gray. Empty-handed.

The court said nothing. The fire roared. And Ada, who could not remember her mother's face, who was trying even now to hold on to *bread, river-smoke, chamomile* and finding the words going smooth and strange like stones in a stream, laughed until she cried, because justice, it turned out, smelled like burning paper and sounded like a boy singing badly two miles above her head.

The last thing the dragon-fire touched was the chancellor himself—or rather, the last thing it *offered* to touch. It circled him once, politely, the way a dog circles a stranger, and waited.

The court looked at Vessin. Vessin looked at the floor.

"Well," said Maren from the stairs, spitting blood, "someone fetch a rope. Or don't. I'm told the city's learning to look away from certain things."

But Ada wasn't listening anymore. Because through the smoke, up the long dark throat of the mountain, she could hear footsteps—many, quick, coming down—and a voice calling her name that she almost, almost recognized, and a small gray-eyed boy bursting ahead of everyone with a sparrow on his shoulder, shouting,

"ADA! ADA, I REMEMBER EVERYTHING!"

She turned toward the light of the torches, her heart doing something complicated and new, as the first of them stepped into the vault—

and stopped dead, because the woman at the front of the torchlight had her mother's laugh lines, and Ada had no idea why that made her want to weep.
