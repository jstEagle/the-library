## 10. A Garden for the Wolf

The dragon died on the first morning of spring.

Ada felt it before anyone told her—a warmth going out of the world somewhere beneath the mountain, like a hearth banked at last after a hundred years of burning. She was standing in the ruins of the night market with a spade in her hands when it happened, and she stopped, and pressed her palm flat against her breastbone, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that Ember-that-Was would have wanted to hear.

*Free,* he had told her, in the last hour of his long life, when his fire was down to a single coal and his eyes were the color of dusk over water. *Little speaker. I have been a jailer and a prisoner and a furnace, and I am tired of all three. Burn my name off the last ledger, and I will call it a good death.*

She had burned it. She had burned them all—every name in every rack, every soul sold and kept and fed upon, until the vaults filled with light like a sunrise happening underground. And when the ledgers were ash and the chancellor's stolen magic had guttered out of him like a snuffed candle, leaving him small and gray and ordinary in front of the assembled court, the dragon had folded himself around what remained of his flame the way an old man folds his hands, and closed his eyes.

They said afterward that the mountain breathed out smoke for three days, and that on the third day it was only mist, and then not even that.

Ada dug her spade into the cobblestones where her charm-stall had stood.

* * *

The stones came up easier than she expected. Corvale's cobbles were old and crooked, laid by masons who had quarreled with their rulers about something no one remembered, and half of them were loose anyway. Osk helped, of course. The old jailer could not seem to stop helping; it was as if his hands had made a private treaty with his guilt and now worked double shifts to honor it.

"You know," he said, levering up a stone with a grunt, "in forty years of guarding that mountain, I never once saw the sun come up over it. You go down in the dark, you come up in the dark. A man forgets there's a morning side of things."

"There's a morning side," Ada said. "You're leaning on it."

He laughed—the rusty laugh he was still learning to trust—and set the stone aside. His daughter Wren came by most days now, bringing bread and pointed remarks about her father's back, and Ada had learned that Wren laughed exactly the way Osk did, which meant that somewhere under all those years of selling names, the man had been worth saving too. Whether he believed it yet was another matter. Atonement, Ada had decided, was less a door you walked through than a garden you kept weeding.

Maren arrived at midmorning, walking stiffly, her arm still bound from the vault doors, and stood at the edge of the digging with her huntswoman's posture softening by degrees, like ice deciding to be water.

"The writ's been torn up," she reported. "Officially. The Proclamation of the Hunt is rescinded." She paused. "Unofficially, the chancellor's clerks are burning papers so fast the river's gone gray. Nobody wants their names found in anything."

"And the gifted?"

"Still illegal." Maren shrugged one shoulder. "Magic's against the law, same as ever. But the law's got no teeth left, and everyone knows it. There's a difference," she added, "between a law and a habit. Corvale is learning the difference."

That was the phrase Ada would carry through the years that followed: *a law and a habit.* The city had spent a generation practicing fear, and practice did not unmake itself in a season. But people were already looking away—not the cold looking-away of the hunt years, but a warmer kind, the deliberate blindness of neighbors deciding that what they didn't see couldn't be reported. A woman three stalls down from the garden plot had a rooster who answered questions. Everyone knew. No one cared. The rooster, for his part, was insufferable about it.

* * *

Tam woke singing.

Not immediately—his soul came back to him slowly, the way feeling returns to a frozen hand, prickling and strange. For two days he lay in Ada's spare room, gray-eyed but breathing, while Sefi curled at the foot of his bed like a sentry and the sparrow sat on the windowsill and scolded him back toward the world.

Then, on the third morning, Ada heard it through the floor: a song, thin and cracked and absolutely tuneless, about a sparrow, a fox, and a lady who talked to horses. Tam had always sung badly. It was one of the things his father had hated about him, right up until the day his father sold him, and it was one of the things Ada loved most, and hearing it again—awful, alive, unmistakably *Tam*—she had to sit down on the stairs and let herself cry for the first time since the vaults.

He came downstairs singing. He stopped when he saw her face, and grinned, and sang louder.

"Your voice is a crime," Maren told him.

"So arrest me," said Tam, and kept singing, and Maren—who had once held a patrol at bay with a broken arm and a stolen writ—laughed until she had to hold her ribs.

His father came to the door that evening. He stood in the street with his hat in his hands, gray-faced, unable to cross the threshold, and Tam looked at him a long time through the window. In the end the boy went out, spoke to him briefly—no one ever learned what was said—and came back inside alone. He did not sing for the rest of that night. But in the morning he was singing again, and Ada understood that whatever forgiveness was, it wasn't hers to arrange. Some gardens grow back wild or not at all.

* * *

Sefi got her name on a Tuesday.

It happened the way these things happen when they are allowed to happen at all: quietly, at the edge of ordinary life. The fox-girl had been growing more girl and less fox every week since the ledgers burned—her fire-scarred ear staying stubbornly vulpine even as her hands became hands, her speech arriving word by word like spring arriving northward, unevenly, in patches. She had lived in Ada's spare room with Tam, the two of them communicating mostly in glances and stolen biscuits, two children hollowed by the same machinery, filling back in together.

On the Tuesday, Ada was planting peas—she had read that peas forgive poor soil, which seemed wise counsel for a garden planted in a city's conscience—and Sefi crouched beside her, watching the seeds go into the ground with an intensity usually reserved for rabbits.

"What do I put in the row?" Sefi asked suddenly. "For me. When they ask."

Ada sat back on her heels. "When who asks?"

"When someone asks my name." The girl's ears—one round, one scarred and pointed—twitched. "I don't remember having one. Before. I think I was little. I think I liked—" She frowned, reaching. "Rivers. I think I liked rivers."

"Then we'll pick one that sounds like rivers," Ada said. "Or we'll wait, if you'd rather. Names keep."

Sefi considered this with the gravity of a fox considering a henhouse. Then she shook her head. "I want one today. Today I'm real enough."

So they tried some out. *Brook* was rejected as "too wet." *Maren* was rejected on the grounds that there was already a Maren and one was plenty. *Sefi*—which Ada offered almost at random, a syllable that moved the way water moves—made the girl go very still, and then very bright, the way a person looks when a word fits a shape they'd carried all along without knowing it.

"Sefi," she repeated. She said it twice more, tasting it. Then she grabbed Ada's muddy hand and shook it solemnly, like a merchant closing a deal. "I'm Sefi. I live here. I'm keeping the fox ear."

"Keep it," Ada said. "It suits you."

That night Sefi slept in the garden, in the fresh-turned earth between the pea rows, wrapped in a blanket, and no one had the heart to make her come inside. Some children need walls. Some need sky.

* * *

The garden grew the way found families grow: unevenly, stubbornly, in cracks.

Ada had expected the city to be suspicious of it—a plot of open soil in the middle of the market quarter, run by a known beast-speaker, tended by a disgraced huntswoman, a confessed jailer, and three children of varying legality. She had braced for complaints, for inspectors, for the cold shoulder.

Instead, the strays began to arrive.

A cart-horse with a lame leg whose driver could no longer afford to feed him—he grazed by the fence and told Ada, at length and in great detail, about every injustice of his working life. An old woman who had once been able to coax storms apart with her hands and hadn't dared try in thirty years; she came to weed, and her fingers remembered. Two brothers who could whistle birds down out of trees. A clerk from the chancellery itself, pale and twitchy, who turned out to have a minor gift for finding lost things and had spent a decade pretending not to notice how useful he was. He found Ada's mother's ring in the second week.

She had not known she'd lost it. That was the shape of the thing now: her memories of her mother were a room in her own house with the door sealed, and she knew the room existed the way you know weather behind a wall. She knew she had loved her. She knew her mother's soul had been among the kept, and that freeing it had cost the dragon nothing and Ada everything, and that somewhere—freed, dispersed, become part of the ordinary warm world—her mother was finally beyond the reach of ledgers. But her face was gone. Her voice was gone. The lullaby Ada must have been sung was gone, and the smell of whatever bread she must have baked, and whether her hands were rough or soft.

The ring fit Ada's finger. That was all she had. She wore it anyway, every day, and when the old storm-woman asked about it, Ada said, "It was my mother's," and found that the sentence still held warmth even though everything behind it had been spent. Love, it turned out, left residue. Even bought and paid away, it left residue.

"I'm sorry," the storm-woman said gently.

"Don't be." Ada meant it, mostly. "I'd pay it again. There was a boy who needed it more than I needed remembering." She paused, turning the ring. "Though I'll tell you what nobody warns you about grief. It doesn't check its receipts. I mourn her just the same. I just don't know what I'm mourning *with*."

The old woman nodded as if this were the wisest thing she'd heard in thirty years of storms, and pulled weeds, and said no more, which was its own kindness.

* * *

By midsummer, the garden had a fence (Osk), a gate with a bell (Wren's idea), a scarecrow wearing Maren's old hunt-livery ("so the pigeons know the war is over," Tam explained solemnly), and a sign that Sefi painted herself, letters wobbling with pride:

**A GARDEN FOR THE WOLF — ALL STRAYS WELCOME — BRING YOUR OWN SEEDS**

"Why wolf?" Maren asked, when she first saw it. "There's no wolf."

"There's you," said Tam.

"I'm not a wolf."

"You arrested people for a living and then bit the hand that fed you. That's basically a wolf."

Maren opened her mouth, closed it, and looked at Ada, who was doing a very poor job of not smiling. "I hate all of you," Maren announced, and stayed for supper, which was how she expressed affection.

The city learned to look away, and then, gradually, it learned something better: to look *at*. To see the horse talking to the charm-woman and simply nod, the way you nod at rain. Magic remained illegal—there was still a line in a ledger somewhere, in a building full of clerks who had discovered that paperwork survives revolutions better than chancellors do—but the line grew quieter every month, like a law being spoken in a language fewer and fewer people bothered to translate. Justice, Ada reflected, did not always arrive as a verdict. Sometimes it arrived as a garden, and a fence with a gap in it, and a city full of people deciding, one neighbor at a time, that some laws were habits, and habits could be broken gentle.

* * *

On the eve of autumn, Ada walked out past the walls at dusk, to the low hill where the whole city could be seen laid out below—rooftops and chimney smoke and the mountain standing quiet at its back, no longer smoking, no longer hungry.

She was not alone. Sefi trotted at her heel, fox-eared and barefoot. Tam brought his terrible singing. Maren and Osk argued amiably about fence posts. The sparrow rode on Tam's shoulder, and the lame horse had insisted on coming, and somewhere behind them the garden bell rang in the evening wind, calling in whoever needed calling.

Below, lamps were being lit, one window at a time. Somewhere in that sprawl, Ada knew, there were other hidden ones—children going gray-eyed, gifts being buried, bargains being whispered. The ledgers were ash, but hunger was patient, and cities forget.

"We'll have to plant more than peas next year," she said aloud.

Sefi tilted her head, one ear round and one scarred. "What else?"

Ada watched the lamplight spread across Corvale like a slow, stubborn dawn, and smiled, and reached down to scratch the fox-girl's ears.

"Everything," she said. "We're going to need a bigger garden."
