## 2. What the Rats Remember

The rats came to her at dusk, as they always did, but on the third night after the impossible prayer they came wrong.

Petra was banking the coals behind her stall when she felt them first—a pressure at the edge of her gift, like fingers testing a latch. Twenty years of practice had taught her to keep the beast-sense folded small inside her, a coin palmed in a closed fist, and most nights the city's animals were no more to her than weather: present, ambient, unremarkable. But this was different. This was a crowd pressing against a door.

She set down the poker. *Come,* she thought, not opening herself so much as cracking the shutter of herself one finger's width.

They poured out of the drainage grate by the tanner's wall in a gray ribbon—dozens of them, then scores, moving with none of their usual scatter and greed. They arranged themselves in the alley's mouth in a rough crescent, whiskers twitching, eyes catching the last light like wet river stones. The largest sat forward, an old male with a torn ear and the scarred confidence of a creature that had survived every cat in Vellamarch twice over.

*Speaker.* His voice arrived in her mind the way all beast-speech did—not words exactly, but meaning wearing words like borrowed clothes. *We bring you what we saw.*

"You usually bring me complaints," Petra said aloud, low, glancing up the alley both ways. "A stolen egg. A rude cat. Last month it was the whole question of the baker's dog and whether his tail is an insult."

*This is not complaint.* The old rat's claws clicked once against the cobbles, a sound like a tiny key turning. *This is warning. There is a second sun under the world. It burns beneath the broken cathedral, where the bells fell. We went into the dark places to escape the cold of winter, and we found it burning there, though nothing feeds it. It does not warm. It looks at you.*

"Rats don't say *looks at you*."

*Rats do now.* He groomed his whiskers, a gesture she'd learned long ago meant he was steadying himself. *Others of us went closer. They do not come back. Not dead—dead leaves bodies, and bodies feed us, and there is a grim honesty in that. These simply stop. One moment my sister was beside me, and the next there was only her shape walking toward the light, and when I called her name she had no name anymore to answer to.*

The coal-smell, the familiar rot of the alley, the distant clang of the night-watch bell—all of it suddenly seemed very thin, a painted cloth hung over something vast. Petra crouched, bringing herself level with the crescent of watching eyes. "You're saying something under the cathedral ruins is eating rats."

*It is eating names,* the old rat said. *And it has begun to eat louder things than ours.*

---

She didn't sleep. She told herself it was the ordinary insomnia of a woman with rent due and a shipment of beeswax coming from the coast, and for perhaps an hour she believed it. Then she lay listening to the city through the thin wall of her back room, listening the way she'd taught herself never to listen, and heard what the rats had meant.

Vellamarch's beasts had always been a murmur to her—the constant low tide of animal being, dogs dreaming in yards, horses stamping in the livery, pigeons turning their small ambitions over on the rooftops. That murmur was the sound of her whole hidden life. Tonight it stuttered.

By the hour before dawn she could map it without trying: silence spreading through the city like ink through water. The pigeons on Cooper's Row had gone quiet—not roosting-quiet, but the silence of things that had forgotten how to be birds. The livery horses stood awake in their stalls, staring at walls. And somewhere south, near the river, a dog was screaming in a voice that had stopped being a dog's voice somewhere in the middle of the scream.

Petra got up, dressed in the dark, and stood a long time with her hand on the door.

Every instinct she owned said *stay*. Her mother's voice, twenty years dead and still the loudest thing in her head, said it too: *Small, Petra. Be small. A candle flame doesn't argue with the wind; it just stays lit until the wind gets bored.* She had built her entire existence on that advice. She sold charms that did nothing because doing nothing was safe. She spoke to beasts only in whispers, only at night, only ever in the grammar of gossip and comfort. She had watched the Wardens burn a hedge-witch in Foundling Square when she was nine years old, and she had decided, with the terrible clarity available to children, that she would rather be nothing than be seen.

But the prayer at her stall three days ago would not leave her. Old Marta, hands knotted with the arthritis of forty years' washing, had whispered *please* over a two-copper charm—and her hands had straightened. Not eased. *Straightened*, smooth as a girl's, while her face went slack and strange for the space of four heartbeats, and when it returned she could not remember her son's name, and she laughed about it, laughed and laughed, and bought another charm besides.

Something was answering prayers in Vellamarch. And everything Petra knew about the old covenant—the little her mother had let slip, the less Anselm's banned pamphlets had confirmed—said that nothing should be answering at all. The gods had gone silent a generation ago. That silence was the law's whole foundation. Magic outlawed, speakers burned, all of it justified by a heaven that had stopped speaking first.

If the silence had ended, then the world's rules were being rewritten, and Petra Whitlock could either be rewritten with them or find out who held the pen.

She opened the door and went out into the dark, following the silence south.

* * *

The ruined cathedral district was fenced, mostly, and patrolled, occasionally, and avoided, absolutely. Twenty years ago the great bells had come down—one of them, the deep-voiced Vellamourn, had cracked clean in half, and half-fallen through three floors of collapsing nave—and the Church, already dying since the gods went quiet, had simply walked away from the wreckage rather than pay to raise it. Now it was a hill of pale stone and black girders, weeds growing from the eye sockets of saints, and the city's poor picked it for lead and firewood the way carrion birds pick anything.

Petra came at it from the river side, through the tannery sludge-flats where even Wardens didn't like to walk. The dawn was a bruise-colored rumor behind the eastern hills. Frost furred the reeds. She kept her gift folded tight—if the beasts here had gone strange, she didn't want to know by invitation—but the silence pressed against her shuttered senses anyway, the way cold presses through a coat.

Near the fence line she found the first evidence that stopped her breath.

Birds. Dozens of them—starlings, sparrows, a crow—sitting in the frost-killed grass in a perfect spiral, each bird precisely a wingspan from the next, all facing inward toward the cathedral mound. Alive. Breathing. Their eyes tracked her as she approached, all of them together, a single movement like a page turning.

"Go home," she whispered, and hated how her voice shook. She cracked her gift open just enough to touch them.

There was nothing to touch. Not emptiness—emptiness she knew, the plain animal blankness of a sleeping sparrow. This was worse. They were *full*. Full of something that was not bird, something patient and vast and attentive, using their small warm bodies the way a man uses a glove. The crow turned its head further than a crow's neck should allow and looked at her with an expression she recognized, with a lurch, from her own mirror: the careful blankness of someone hiding something enormous behind a shopfront face.

*Not yet,* the birds said, in one voice, in no voice at all. *But soon, daughter of Marisol.*

Then they rose as one body, wheeled once over the ruins, and scattered into the gray sky, leaving the spiral of bent grass behind like a signature.

Petra stood very still in the frost. Her heart was doing something complicated and painful against her ribs.

Her mother's name. Marisol Whitlock, dead twenty years, buried in the paupers' row at Saint Ferren's with a wooden marker that had rotted to anonymity. A charm-seller before her, a small careful woman, a woman who had taught her daughter to be a candle flame. Whatever waited under the cathedral knew things that no living person should know—and it had chosen, out of everything it might have said, to say *that*.

Against every instinct, Petra climbed the fence.

* * *

Inside, the ruins were a country of fallen stone. She picked her way along the old processional path, now a canyon between collapsed vaults, past saints lying face-down in nettles with their hands worn smooth by generations of vanished pilgrims. The silence deepened with every step. By the time she reached the lip of the nave crater, where the half-bell Vellamourn hung wedged in its own destruction like a great bronze seed, her ears were ringing with the absence of sound. No rats here. No insects. Even the wind moved through the weeds without whispering.

Below, where the crypt stairs had been exposed by the collapse, there was light.

It was wrong in every particular. It didn't flicker. It cast no shadows—or rather, it cast shadows that leaned the wrong way, falling toward the light instead of away from it. It was gold, but a gold with no warmth in it, the gold of a coin, of a judge's seal, of a closed eye seen through a keyhole. It rose from the stairwell in a column and pooled against the underside of the ruined roof, and the half-bell above it hummed faintly, resonating like a struck glass, though nothing touched it.

Petra should have run. She catalogued, distantly, all the reasons: the Wardens patrolled this district at dawn; the light itself was evidence enough to hang her; whatever had spoken through the birds had invited her, and invitations from such things were traps with better manners than most. She stood at the top of those stairs and recited her mother's catechism—*be small, be small*—and found, to her horror, that grief had made her reckless. Because if something down there wore her mother's voice, then some part of her mother had not finished dying. And Petra had spent twenty years being small in front of a grave marker she couldn't even read anymore.

She went down the stairs.

The crypt had been a chapel once—you could still see where the altar rail had been pried out—and the false light filled it like water fills a bowl. At its center, where the high altar must have stood, someone had built a shrine. Crude, recent, reverent: candles whose flames burned without heat, offerings of bread and coins and children's shoes, and above them, scratched into the fallen slab of the altar itself, a name.

Petra knew the name. Every child in Vellamarch knew the old names the way they knew nursery rhymes—Haleth who keeps the gates, Maren of the deep water, the Shepherd-of-Small-Things. This was none of those. This was a name from the oldest layer of the liturgy, a name the Church had struck from its books even before the silence: **Vessia**, who was called the First Listener. The god whose whole office had been to hear.

The light gathered around the name like breath around a window.

And it spoke.

*"Petra."*

Her mother's voice. Not similar to—*identical to*, down to the slight catch on the second syllable, the way Marisol Whitlock had always said her daughter's name as if setting down something fragile. Petra's knees nearly went. She gripped the cold wall. Twenty years of discipline screamed at her to close her gift, shut it out, be a candle flame—but the voice wasn't in her gift. It was in her ears. It was in the room. It needed no magic of hers to reach her, which meant it wanted her plain human self, the daughter, not the speaker.

"Don't," Petra said. Her voice came out cracked. "Don't you use her voice. Whatever you are."

*"I use many voices, little merchant. The washerwoman's. The child's. The drowned man's. I wear what is given me, as any honest priest wears vestments."* The light pulsed gently, tenderly. *"But hers I keep closest. She gave it to me willingly. Did you know that? Your mother talked to me for eleven years before the Hollowing. Longer than she talked to anyone. Longer, I think, than she talked to you."*

"That's a lie." It came out flat and certain and Petra had no idea if it was true. Eleven years before the Hollowing would put her mother's dealings with this thing back before Petra's birth. Before the theft, if Anselm's banned pamphlets could be believed—before the chorus was severed and the gods went silent and the world learned to make do without miracles. Her mother had been a thief. Her mother had been a conspirator. And her mother had been, apparently, a *congregant*.

*"Is it?"* The voice was gentle, so gentle, Marisol soothing a nightmare. *"Ask yourself what she was doing in the deep places, all those years, while you slept. Ask why a woman with no gift worth naming spent her life among stones and rites. Ask why she taught you to hide, Petra—to be small, to be nothing—when hiding is the one skill a guilty conscience needs."*

"Stop."

*"I loved her,"* said the thing in the light, and the awful thing was that it sounded like it meant it. *"I love what remains of her. She is in me, little one. All of her that the world let go. Come closer and I will give her back to you—I will let you hear her laugh again, hear her scold you, hear her say the thing she never said before the end. You have wondered, all these years, whether she died proud of you or afraid of you. I can tell you. I am the only thing left that can."*

Petra became aware that she was crying, and that her feet had moved—two steps, three—down into the light, which received her the way still water receives a dropped stone. The candles guttered toward her. In the shine of the false gold she could almost see shapes, faces, a woman's silhouette with her mother's narrow shoulders and her mother's way of standing with her weight on one hip—

The crow landed on the broken altar rail.

Its presence broke the spell like ice water. Petra gasped, staggered back, and the silhouette dissolved; the light dimmed by a degree, and the voice, when it came again, had set her mother aside like a folded garment.

*"Soon, then,"* it said, in a voice that was now everyone's and no one's. *"You will want more than a glimpse, daughter of Marisol. You have your mother's blood, and your mother's debts. When you are ready to inherit them fully, come back. Or—" and here it did something unforgivable, something that would keep her awake for the rest of her life—"*or ask the priest. Brother Anselm remembers more than he has written down. Ask him what your mother carried out of this place, twenty years ago, in her hands.*"

The light went out. Not faded—*out*, all at once, and the crypt was suddenly, blessedly ordinary: damp stone, rotten offerings, cold. The crow ruffled its feathers and looked at her with its own eyes now, small and black and merely animal, and Petra realized she was alone in the dark with the sound of her own breathing and the knowledge that the thing below had just handed her a name.

Anselm. Brother Anselm, the disgraced archivist, the priest who recorded the covenant rites and was stripped of his orders for writing them down. She'd seen him once, years ago, buying turnips at market—a big soft man with ink-stained fingers and the defeated posture of someone carrying a library no one was allowed to read.

He knew her mother. He knew what she'd carried.

Petra climbed the stairs on legs that barely answered, past the humming half-bell, out into a morning that had arrived without her noticing—gray, cold, ordinary, full of the distant clatter of carts and the cries of fishwives and ten thousand people praying to a heaven that had finally, horribly, begun to answer.

Behind her, deep under the ruins, in the dark where the light had been, something that loved her mother began, very softly, to sing.
