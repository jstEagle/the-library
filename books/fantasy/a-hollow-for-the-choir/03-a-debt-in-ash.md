## 3. A Debt in Ash

The priest lived above a tannery, which Petra took as a kind of answer before she'd even asked the question. No man of the old faith would choose to breathe that reek—rendered fat and lime and the sweet rot of hides—unless he had stopped believing he deserved anything sweeter.

She climbed the outside stair with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, telling herself the whole way up that this was a mistake. The rats had given her a name two nights ago, hissed from the mouths of a dozen sewer-mouths at once while she'd crouched over a grate like a madwoman: *the one who wrote it down, the one who wrote it down.* Beasts did not lie, exactly. But they saw the world slantwise, through hunger and territory, and what they remembered of men was mostly teeth.

The dog had insisted. That was the strange part. Bramble—the stray who owned no one and deigned to share her evenings—had sat on her doorstep that morning and said, in the flat gray way of dogs, *Go to him. He smells of ink and endings.* And then he had looked at her with those amber eyes until she'd put on her boots.

So here she was, climbing toward a smell of endings, because a dog told her to.

The door at the top was oak gone soft with damp, and it opened before she knocked.

Brother Anselm was not what she expected, though she couldn't have said what she'd expected instead. He was small and dry as a stick of old kindling, with a scribe's stoop and eyes the color of weak tea, and he looked at her the way a man looks at weather he has been predicting for years.

"You're late," he said. "I've been expecting you since the rats started talking."

Petra's hand went to the charm-bag at her belt out of pure reflex—a habit, a shield, a piece of theater—and Anselm watched the gesture with something too tired to be contempt.

"Come in," he said. "Mind the floorboards. They've opinions."

* * *

The room inside was not a room so much as an argument between a library and a ruin. Books stood in towers against every wall, some stacked flat, some propped open face-down like sleeping birds. Papers covered the table, the bed, the single chair. The window overlooked the tannery yards, and its shutters were sealed with wax, which explained why the room smelled less of hides than of dust and lamp oil and something older underneath—incense, maybe, burned long ago by someone who still remembered how.

Anselm cleared a chair by lifting an entire stratum of parchment and setting it on the bed. "Sit. Or stand. Standing is honest."

"I'm not—" She stopped. Started again. "I sell charms. Little ones. Wart-charms, luck-knots. Nothing that works."

"No," Anselm agreed pleasantly. "Nothing that works." He lowered himself into the room's other chair, the one with a broken arm mended by wire, and folded his hands. "And yet three nights ago, a fishwife prayed to Saint Vessa for her son's fever, and the fever left him so completely that it took his memory of her with it. Two days ago, a lamplighter asked the old names for rain and got a flood that drowned only his debts—every ledger in the countinghouse, ruined, nothing else touched. Precise. Courteous. Wrong." His tea-colored eyes settled on her. "You felt it. I can see you felt it. The air changed key. People like us hear the change even when we can't name the note."

"People like us," Petra repeated carefully.

"Beast-speakers." He said it the way other men said *good morning*. "Don't bother denying it. You flinched when the gulls argued over the harbor on your way up the hill. You walk on the outside of the street, next to the gutters, where the rats run. Twenty years I've watched you do it, girl, since you were a child trailing your mother through this market, and you never once stepped on a shadow that moved."

The bottom dropped out of her stomach. "You knew my mother."

"I recorded her," Anselm said. "There's a difference. Sit down, Whitlock. This is going to take longer than your courage will."

* * *

He talked, and Petra let the words come the way she'd learned to let beast-speech come—not grasping, not flinching, just letting them land where they landed.

"There was a covenant," he began. "Not a treaty. A song. That's the first thing everyone gets wrong, because treaties can be renegotiated and songs cannot—they can only be sung or stopped. Three realms: human, beast, divine. Each realm kept a stone, a choir-stone, and each stone held one voice of the harmony. When the stones sounded together, the gods could hear us and we could hear them, and the beasts stood between as the bridge-voices, the translators of need. Prayer worked because it was *answered*—not by miracle, but by agreement. Do you understand? The rain came because the sky agreed to send it. The sick were healed because flesh agreed to mend. Everything cost something, everything was paid for, and nothing was stolen."

"And now?"

"Now the chorus is severed. Twenty years ago—give or take a season—the gods went silent all at once, everywhere, mid-word. Not dead. Silent. There is a difference there too, though it took me fifteen years to believe it." He rose, joints crackling like green wood, and went to a shelf where a single book lay wrapped in oilcloth. He unwrapped it with the care of a man uncovering a wound. "I was the recording secretary of the Rite of Maintenance. Every year, the covenant was renewed—three voices, three stones, one song. I wrote down what was sung. And then one year, the singers didn't come, and the stones went dark, and everything I had written became either scripture or nonsense, depending on who won the argument."

"The Wardens won," Petra said.

"The Wardens won," Anselm agreed. "Magic outlawed. Speakers burned or scattered. And the city congratulated itself on curing a disease by cutting off the limb." He set the book on the table between them. It was bound in gray leather gone brittle, its spine stitched with thread that glimmered faintly, wrongnessly, like frost on wool. "But here is what the Wardens never learned, because I never told them, because telling them would have meant admitting the silence wasn't natural. The chorus wasn't broken, Petra. It was *severed*. Cut clean, at a single point, by someone who knew the rite. Someone who stood inside it."

The room seemed to lean closer. Somewhere below, the tannery vats turned over with a wet, industrial sigh.

"The covenant needed three stones," Anselm continued. "Human, beast, divine. The human stone was kept in the cathedral—public, ceremonial, guarded by ceremony rather than locks, because who steals a god's voice? The night of the last rite, twenty years ago, the human stone vanished from its cradle between one verse and the next. No break-in. No blood. Just absence, discovered too late, and by then the severance was already done—the theft *was* the cut. Take the human voice out of the harmony and the whole chord collapses. The gods couldn't hear us anymore. We couldn't hear them. And the beasts—" his voice caught, just slightly, a hairline fracture in dry stone, "—the beasts lost their bridge. Half of them went quiet forever. The rest went mad, or learned to hide, like you hide."

Petra's mouth was dry. "Who took it?"

"That," said Anselm, "is the question I have spent twenty years failing to answer. Three people stood inside the rite that night. The cantor. The warden of relics. And the recorder." He tapped his own sunken chest. "Me. And I swear to you on whatever is left to swear on—I did not take it. Which leaves two. The cantor died the following spring. Fell from the cathedral tower the week the proscriptions began, and the Wardens called it penance and I have always called it murder. Which leaves the warden of relics."

He looked at her then, fully, without mercy and without cruelty, and Petra understood what was coming the way beasts understand weather—one heartbeat before the thunder.

"Her name," Anselm said gently, "was Maren Whitlock."

* * *

The room did not spin. That was the terrible part. It stayed perfectly, insultingly still—the books, the papers, the sealed window, the little man watching her—and only Petra moved, only Petra fell, inward, down a well she hadn't known was under her feet her whole life.

"My mother," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "My mother sold charms. My mother taught me which mushrooms kill and which ones cure and how to keep my mouth shut around anything with ears. My mother was—" *ordinary*, she almost said, and heard the lie die in her throat, because her mother had never once been ordinary; she had been careful, careful the way only guilty things are careful, careful the way Petra herself had learned to be careful, and she had never once said why.

"She was the warden of relics," Anselm said. "Keeper of the human choir-stone. And when it vanished, she vanished with it—resurfaced eight months later in the market district with a child and a stall full of harmless charms, and lived the rest of her life as quietly as a woman can live while sitting on the largest crime in the history of the covenant."

"You're lying." It came out flat, automatic, a door slammed against wind. "You're a disgraced priest with a grudge and a room full of paper, and you'll say anything that makes the silence somebody's fault instead of nobody's."

"Yes," Anselm said. "All true, except the lying." He opened the gray book to a page near the end, and turned it toward her. "Read it yourself. I copied the relic-ledger the morning after the theft, before the Wardens sealed the archives. The stone's removal was logged. Signed."

Petra read it. The letters swam and then steadied, and they were her mother's letters—she knew them instantly, helplessly, from shopping lists and recipe cards and the forged provenance papers that had made her own forged charms legal. *Removed for cleansing prior to final verse. M. Whitlock, warden of relics.* Dated the night of the last rite. Her mother's handwriting, steady as a surgeon's, signing away the voice of God.

"She believed she was saving the world," Anselm said quietly. "That's the part I pieced together later, from what she wouldn't say rather than what she did. The gods' demands had been growing heavier—the rite cost more each year, the payments stranger. I think she found something in the old records, something about what the covenant was *for*, and it frightened her enough to stop the song rather than pay the next verse. I don't know if she was right. I don't know if she was the bravest woman in Vellamarch or the one who damned it. I only know that the stone went with her, and the stone has never been found, and now something is answering prayers in the gods' names, and it is answering them *wrong*."

"What does that mean?" Petra whispered. "Answering wrong."

"It means a chorus missing its human voice doesn't simply stop." Anselm closed the book. "It means something else fills the silence. Something that knows the old names because it heard them sung for a thousand years, but never understood the music—only the notes. It gathers worshipers the way floodwater gathers debris. And it is looking for the stone, Petra. Whatever wears the gods' faces under the cathedral ruins, it wants what your mother hid, because with the human stone in its grip, it wouldn't be an echo anymore. It would be the whole song."

Outside, far below, a cart rattled past, and every rat along the gutter wall lifted its head at once and stared upward—at the tannery, at the sealed window, at her. Petra felt their attention like a cold finger drawn down her spine. *The second sun,* they had said. *It grows. It grows.*

"Why tell me?" she asked. "Why me, of all people? You could have gone to anyone. You could have gone to no one."

"Because you're the heir," Anselm said simply. "To the crime, to the debt, and possibly to the hiding place—blood answers blood, and stones that hold voices answer the bloodlines that sang them. And because," and here his dry voice roughened into something almost tender, "because your mother came to me, three days before she died, and asked me to watch over the market stalls. All of them. She wouldn't say why. She paid me in charms that didn't work, and I took them, because I understood they weren't the payment. The watching was."

Petra stood. Her legs held, which surprised her. The grief hadn't finished arriving yet—it was still out on the road somewhere, walking toward her—but its advance guard had reached the walls, and she knew that tonight, alone above her shop, she would come apart entirely.

"The stone," she said. "If I wanted to find it. If I wanted to—" She couldn't finish the sentence. *Undo it. Pay the debt. Hear my mother explain herself across twenty years of silence.*

"Then you'd need to think like a warden of relics," Anselm said. "Which means thinking like a woman who expects to be hunted. Your mother didn't bury that stone, girl. Buried things are found. She *drowned* it—in water deep enough to swallow prayer, moving enough to scatter any voice trying to sing through it. The old cisterns beneath the Tanner's Quarter. The flooded ones. The ones even the rats won't enter."

Bramble, she thought absurdly. *He smells of ink and endings.* The dog had known. The dog had sent her here knowing what she'd leave carrying.

"One more thing," Anselm said, as she reached the door. She turned. He was holding the gray book against his chest like a shield, or a wound. "Whatever you find down there—and you will find it, because you are your mother's daughter and neither of you ever learned how to leave a locked thing locked—when it sings, it will know your name. Stones remember their keepers' blood. And the thing wearing the gods' faces will know it too, the moment you touch what belongs to you."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's an inheritance," said Brother Anselm. "They look the same, from the inside."

Petra went down the stairs into the stink of the tannery, into the gray afternoon, into a city that had been silent for twenty years and was learning, wrongly, terribly, to speak. Behind her she heard the priest begin, softly, to write—as if recording her departure were the same sacred work as recording the fall of heaven.

By nightfall, she had packed rope, oilcloth, and a lantern whose flame burned the color of drowned things.

By midnight, the rats had stopped talking altogether.

And by dawn, Petra Whitlock stood at the mouth of the flooded cisterns, listening to the water below breathe in a rhythm that was almost—but not quite—a song, and understanding at last that she had inherited not her mother's guilt, nor her mother's secret, but her mother's war.
