## 4. The Warden's Question

The report arrived at the ninth hour, folded twice and stained with tallow, the way all reports from the Saltrow parish arrived—carried by a boy who would not come inside the Wardenhouse and would not say why.

Ilsa Corvane read it standing at her window, because sitting made reports feel like they could wait, and this one could not. *Charm of mending, sold at market, functioning as described. Woman's crooked finger straightened in three days. Vendor unknown. Witness unreliable (weeping).*

She read it again. Then she set it on the sill, weighted it with the brass lamp, and looked out over Vellamarch as if the city might confess to her directly.

Below, the morning fog was burning off the canals in long gray ribbons. Barges moved. Gulls argued over fish guts on the quay. A bell rang somewhere in the Templeward—not a god-bell, those had been silent for twenty years, but the civic bell that marked the hours now, because even a city that had murdered its heaven needed something to count time by. The sound reached her window flat and toneless, like a coin dropped on stone.

*Functioning as described.*

There were four thousand licensed charms in Vellamarch. Ilsa knew this because she had personally signed off on the inventory ledger for six years running. Every one of them was a lie you could hold in your palm: knotted threads said to ease childbirth, river-stones said to calm horses, copper coins bored through and strung on red cord said to draw fever from a child's brow. None of them worked. That was the entire point. They were permitted to exist precisely because they were empty—a pressure valve for the human need to believe, sanctioned by the Wardenship, audited annually, and worth exactly nothing. A charm that worked was not a charm. It was evidence.

And evidence, in the twenty years since the Hollowing, had been rare enough that she remembered every piece of it like faces.

She took her coat from its hook. Gray wool, gray braid, the silver question-mark of the Wardenship pinned at the collar—the sigil people called *the Hook* behind her back, because it caught things and did not let go. She pinned it on without looking. Twenty years of practice.

"Vess," she said to the outer room.

Her junior appeared so fast he must have been waiting with his ear against the door. He was twenty-three, earnest, and still believed the Wardenship was a clean instrument. She had not yet decided whether to correct this.

"Saddle two horses. And pull the parish rolls for Saltrow—all of them, five years back."

"The market district?" Vess blinked. "That's Corvane's territory, isn't it? The charm-sellers' row?"

"It is." She buckled her sword belt. The weight of it sat familiar as her own spine. "Which is why no one has looked there properly in twenty years. Everyone assumes the Wardens already have."

* * *

The ride down into the low city took an hour, and Ilsa spent it watching.

This was the part of the work no one understood—the part her superiors thought was wasted motion, the part that had earned her the nickname *the Hook*. Anyone could kick down a door when someone screamed about witchlight. The screaming cases were easy; they solved themselves with fire and paperwork. What Ilsa hunted was quieter. It lived in the pause before a shopkeeper answered a question. In a child's drawing of a dog with too many teeth. In the way a street went silent one door too early as she rode through it.

The market district of Corvane's Row unfolded below her in its usual morning chaos: awnings, smoke, the wet gleam of fish on ice, a hundred small economies conducted in shouts. Charm-sellers' row ran along its southern edge, a lane of stalls where the licensed vendors hung their wares like dead birds—rows of knots and coins and carved bone, each tagged with the little lead seal of the Wardenship that said, in effect, *this does nothing, and we know it, and you know it, and here we all are together.*

She dismounted at the head of the lane and walked it slowly, Vess trailing with the ledger.

"Count the seals," she said quietly.

He counted. "Forty-one stalls. Forty-one seals. All current year."

"Yes. Now tell me what's wrong with that."

Vess frowned at the lane, at the vendors pretending not to watch them, at the customers haggling over trinkets whose only magic was the shared pretense. Then his face changed. "It's too neat. If a seal lapsed or a stall changed hands, there'd be gaps. There are never any gaps."

"There are never any gaps," Ilsa agreed, "because the woman who runs the licensing audit for this lane has run it for eleven years, and she is very good, and she has never once flagged an irregularity." She stopped walking. "No one is that good, Vess. Not for eleven years. Either she's blind, or she's protecting something, or—" she resumed walking, "—she understands better than anyone that the safest place to hide is inside a system everyone trusts to be boring."

"You think there's a working charm here. On this lane."

"I think a woman in Saltrow straightened a broken finger in three days, and the charm that did it came from somewhere, and every road out of Saltrow passes through this market." She lifted a knotted thread from a stall's display, turned it over with two fingers, and set it down exactly where it had been. "And I think whoever sold it is either a fool or the most careful person in Vellamarch, and careful people are the only ones worth hunting."

* * *

The licensing auditor's name was Mistress Odile Fenn, and she received them in a back room that smelled of ink and old paper, surrounded by eleven years of flawless ledgers.

Ilsa liked her immediately, which was inconvenient.

"Warden Corvane." Fenn was sixty-odd, sharp-faced, with ink stains to the second knuckle and the unbothered air of a woman who had been audited by scarier things than Wardens. "To what do I owe the honor? We're up to date. You can check."

"I checked on the ride down. You're always up to date. That's why I'm here."

Something flickered behind Fenn's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "I don't follow."

"No," Ilsa said pleasantly, "you don't. You're very good at not following. Eleven years of not following." She drew out the Saltrow report and laid it on the desk between them, open to the relevant line. *Charm of mending, functioning as described.* "Tell me about this."

Fenn read it the way Ilsa had seen clerks read death warrants: completely, once, without moving. "A customer complaint?"

"A miracle. Which is worse." Ilsa watched her. "Someone sold a working charm in your jurisdiction, Mistress Fenn. Under your seals. So either you sold it, you missed it, or you saw it and said nothing. Those are the options. I'd take almost any of them over the fourth one."

"Which is?"

"That you didn't see it because you weren't meant to, and neither was I, and we're both being handled by someone very patient."

The silence stretched. Outside, the market roared on, oblivious. Fenn's hands stayed flat on her ledgers, and Ilsa noted that they were perfectly steady, and noted also the faintest tremor in the throat when she finally spoke.

"If I had seen such a thing," Fenn said carefully, "and I'm not saying I have—I'd want to know what the Wardenship intends to do about it. Before I decided anything. Because eleven years ago, Warden, the answer to a working charm was a pyre in Tanner's Square, and the last one they burned was a girl of nineteen whose crime was a horse that loved her too obviously." Her voice did not rise at all. "I've filed my seals honestly for eleven years under that regime. If the regime has changed, someone should tell me. If it hasn't, then you'll forgive me for remembering that the last hunter who asked me these questions used my answers to hang a family."

Ilsa held her gaze for a long moment.

Then she stood, and took the report back, and said, "The regime hasn't changed. But I have a question I'm asking for myself, off the record, and you can refuse to answer it."

Fenn waited.

"When the gods went silent—when the Hollowing happened—you were what, forty? Old enough to remember the bells." Ilsa paused at the door. "Did anything actually change? In the charms? Or did we just start calling the same silence honesty?"

For the first time, Mistress Odile Fenn smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and it was not aimed at Ilsa but at some private joke twenty years old.

"Come back when you've decided which side of that question you're on, Warden," she said. "Then I'll answer it."

* * *

They found the charm itself by evening, and it nearly broke her certainty in half.

Not through brilliance—through the dull, grinding work of canvassing Saltrow door to door, the work her superiors sneered at and she had built her reputation on. The woman with the healed finger was a laundress named Berta Cull, fifty-eight, hands like rope from thirty years of washboards. She produced the charm readily enough: a small disc of pale wood, bored through, strung on green cord, bearing a lead seal that was genuine, current, and registered to a stall on Corvane's Row.

Stall fourteen. The Whitlock stall.

"It worked," Berta said, and showed Ilsa the finger—straight, whole, the knuckle smooth as a girl's. "I know what you're going to say. I know they're not supposed to work. My mother bought one of these same charms when I was young and it did nothing and she wore it anyway till she died, God rest her, because hoping is free." She closed her hand around the disc. "But this one worked. Three days. I'd have sworn on anything you like it wouldn't. I bought it as a joke, Warden. A joke for my own foolishness. And then God—or something—answered me for the first time in twenty years, and I don't know whether to be grateful or terrified, and I'd like very much for you to tell me which."

*I don't know either,* Ilsa did not say.

She examined the charm instead. Pale wood—willow, maybe. Cleanly bored. The seal was authentic; she knew the die-marks the way other women knew their lovers' faces. Nothing about it was wrong except the single impossible fact that it had done what it claimed.

Twenty years ago, a working charm would have meant a sorcerer, a cell, a pyre. Ilsa had written three of those reports herself in her first years, before she learned to read between the lines of her own orders. She remembered the girl from Tanner's Square—everyone in the Wardenship pretended not to remember her—and the horse that had screamed for three days outside the prison until they'd had it destroyed too, and how the crowd had cheered, and how Ilsa had cheered with them, twenty-two years old and certain, and how the certainty had died somewhere along the way without ever announcing its funeral.

"Where did you buy it?" she asked. "Exactly. The stall, the seller, the hour."

Berta told her. Stall fourteen, south end of the row, past the fishmongers. Bought at dusk from a woman—middle-aged, plain-dressed, brown hair, forgettable face. Paid four coppers. The woman had apologized for the price.

Four coppers. An apology. A forgettable face.

Ilsa stood in the doorway of Berta Cull's washing-house as the light failed, and felt the shape of the hunt shift beneath her, the way a floor shifts when you realize the house is built over water.

Because here was the thing she could not put in any report: a sorcerer flaunting power would have charged gold and worn robes. A desperate amateur would have left traces everywhere. This was neither. This was someone who had sold a working charm for pocket change, sealed it correctly, apologized for the cost, and made herself impossible to describe—which meant she knew exactly what she was doing, and had known it for a long time, and had slipped, just once, just enough.

Or something had slipped *through* her. Ilsa turned the thought over. The Hollowing had cut the realms apart; every schoolchild knew the catechism. The gods were silent, magic was dead, and the Wardens kept the peace over the corpse. But the rats in the Wardenhouse cellar had gone strange lately—her clerk complained of it, traps untouched, gnawed holes appearing in the archive wall, as if they were digging toward something or away from it. And the gulls had abandoned the cathedral roof this autumn, all at once, the way birds leave a forest before a fire.

Animals knew things. They had always known things. The old rites said the covenant had three voices—human, beast, divine—and that the world was a song sung in harmony across all three. When the gods fell silent, the scholars said the song simply ended.

But what if it hadn't ended? What if it had been *severed*—cut, deliberately, by human hands—and everything since had been the bleeding stump of it, and now, twenty years on, something on the far side of the cut had begun to press its mouth against the wound?

"Vess," she said.

Her junior looked up from the horse's bridle.

"Pull the archives tomorrow. Everything on the Hollowing—not the public catechism, the primary records. The rite transcripts, the witness statements, whatever survived the purges." She mounted. "And find out who authorized the purges while you're at it. I want names."

Vess hesitated. "Warden—with respect. The Hollowing records are sealed under the First Charter. Directly under the Lord Protector's office. If we request them—"

"I know what we're requesting." The fog was coming back off the canal, swallowing the lamps one by one, and somewhere across the darkening city a dog began to bark—not the idle bark of a yard animal but a long, urgent, rising cry, and then another dog answering it, and another, until the whole southern quarter sounded like a country she didn't recognize. "I'm asking you to help me steal from my own government, Vess. Formally. With paperwork. Are you with me?"

He stared at her. Then, slowly, he grinned like a man stepping off a cliff. "Yes, Warden."

"Good." She turned the horse toward home, toward the barking, toward the sealed doors of a history no one wanted opened. "Because either I'm hunting a witch," Ilsa Corvane said quietly, to herself more than to him, "or I'm hunting the truth about what we did twenty years ago—and I'm beginning to suspect that whichever it is, the person I should be arresting is already dead, and the person who gave the order is still signing my pay."

Above the rooftops, the civic bell rang the tenth hour, flat and toneless as a coin dropped on stone.

And beneath it—so faint she might have imagined it, so faint she would spend the rest of this case failing to prove she hadn't heard it—something else rang back.
