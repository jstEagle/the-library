## 5. Choir-Stone, First Verse

The hawk found her at dawn, or what passed for dawn in Vellamarch that week—a gray smear behind the smoke-haze where the sun should have been.

It dropped out of the sky onto the awning post of her stall like a thrown stone, wings half-folded, and Petra knew before she touched it that it was dying. She had spent twenty years learning the shape of death in small things: the stillness in a sparrow's foot, the way a moth stopped fighting the glass. This was different. This was a body built for the open air choosing, deliberately, to come down.

*Whitlock,* the hawk said, and its voice in her mind was thin as wire drawn to breaking. *You are the one they call Whitlock.*

She glanced up the row of stalls. Old Marta was arranging her dried fish with the slow ceremony of the very old; the cobbler's boy was still asleep under his tarp. No one watching. She kept her hands busy with a tray of luck-knots anyway, because that was the discipline of her life—hands moving, face mild, gift buried so deep even she sometimes forgot it was there.

*I'm nobody,* she said back, silently, the way she always did. *You've made a mistake.*

*The rats said you would say that.* The hawk's head dipped, feathers rustling in a wind that wasn't blowing. *They also said you would come anyway. Rats are rarely wrong about people. It is their whole profession.*

Petra set down the luck-knots. Her heart was doing something complicated and unwelcome.

*What do you want?*

*To give you what I cannot keep.* The hawk shifted, and she saw now the wound beneath the wing—not a wound, exactly, but a wrongness, a place where the bird's own shadow didn't sit right against its body, as if something had reached into the world and pinched it. *There is a stone beneath the old cistern by the Tanner's Gate. It sings. Everything that flies over it hears the singing, and some of us cannot stop hearing it, and we fall. I have flown over it nine times in three days. I will not fly again. But before I rest, I would give the song to someone who can carry it.*

*I don't want your song.*

*No,* the hawk agreed, with something that might have been pity. *But you want the rats' second sun explained, and you want your city quiet again, and those are the same wanting. Take my eyes, Whitlock. They are going spare.*

And before she could refuse—before she even finished forming the refusal—the hawk did the thing that beast-speakers were forbidden to do and beasts almost never offered: it opened itself. Not words this time but sight, poured straight into her, unfiltered. A map of Vellamarch from above, rendered in thermal gold and shadow. The cathedral ruins at the city's heart, black as a missing tooth. And south of them, past the Tanner's Gate where the old aqueduct came down off the ridge, a point of light beneath the ground that pulsed like a heartbeat. Like a held note.

Then it was gone, and the hawk slumped against the post, breathing in long, laborious drags.

"Rest," Petra whispered aloud, because some things deserved a voice even if no one understood. "I'll take it from here."

The hawk closed its eyes. Whether in sleep or surrender, she couldn't tell, and she found she couldn't bear to check.

* * *

The dog was waiting for her at the Tanner's Gate an hour later, sitting in the mud with the patient insolence of a creature who has never once been on time by accident.

"You followed me," she said.

*I preceded you,* the dog corrected. *There is a difference. Following implies you know where you're going.*

"I know where I'm going."

*You know where the hawk showed you. Those are not the same thing. The cistern has two mouths and one of them is a lie.* He stood, shook the mud from his coat with tremendous dignity, and trotted toward the collapsed archway where the aqueduct met the hillside. *Also, you brought no rope, no lamp oil, and a bag containing, unless my nose deceives me, two apples and a charm against damp. This is not preparation. This is a picnic with ambitions.*

Petra looked down at her satchel. Two apples. The damp-charm she'd made herself, one of the few workings harmless enough to carry openly—or as openly as anything of hers ever got.

"The Wardens search everyone coming through the market," she muttered. "I couldn't very well pack a climbing kit."

*You could have packed courage. It weighs less.* But he said it without bite, and when she caught up to him he pressed his warm flank briefly against her leg, which was, in the grammar of dogs, an apology and an encouragement at once.

The aqueduct's lower channel ran dry these twenty years—since the Hollowing, though she hadn't known to call it that until Anselm told her, hadn't known that the silence in the world had a date and a name and, apparently, a thief. Now she walked the dry channel's floor, boots crunching on calcified drippings, while the dog ranged ahead, nose down, tail low and steady. The walls rose on either side, older than the city's oldest records, blocks fitted without mortar, and carved here and there with symbols worn nearly smooth. Once, she thought, they'd meant something. Once, everything had.

*Here,* the dog said, stopping at a seam in the stonework that looked like every other seam. *The second mouth. The honest one.*

"It looks like a wall."

*Yes. That is its best feature.* He scratched at the base of the seam, and a slab the height of a door swung inward on counterweights that still worked after centuries, exhaling a breath of cold, wet air that smelled of deep water and old copper.

Beyond it, stairs went down into darkness, and at the bottom of the darkness, faintly, she heard it.

Not sound, exactly. Not yet. More like the memory of sound, the way a bell keeps ringing in the ear after it stops. A single sustained tone underneath the world, felt in the teeth more than the ears. The dog flattened his ears.

*That,* he said quietly, *is the thing that is emptying my city's birds.*

Petra took out the damp-charm, held it, and put it back. Some ironies were too heavy to carry.

"Lamp," she said, and lit the stub of candle she'd allowed herself, and started down.

* * *

The cistern was vast.

Her candlelight gave out long before the far wall did. She stood on a stone landing at the top of a submerged staircase, and below her the water stretched black and mirror-still, holding the vaulted ceiling upside down in perfect reflection, so that she seemed to stand between two abysses. Columns rose out of the water at intervals, each one carved—she raised the light and saw—with figures. Singers. Rows upon rows of singers, human and otherwise, mouths open, frozen mid-note, spiraling up the columns toward capitals shaped like interlocked hands.

The covenant, sung in stone. Anselm had described it; seeing it was another matter entirely. Someone had built a room whose whole purpose was resonance. Every surface curved to carry a voice. Every angle funneled sound toward the center, where—

Where the water glowed.

Faintly, deep down, a light the color of the hawk's last vision pulsed in time with that underlying tone. Once per breath. Once per breath. As if the stone at the bottom of the flooded dark was a heart that had been waiting twenty years for someone to remember it existed.

"There," she whispered.

*Yes,* said the dog, from the landing above. He would go no closer to the water; she could see it in his stance, the careful weight on his haunches. *And now the part where I tell you the truth, since you insist on hearing it badly rather than not at all. The water is deeper than the hawk's memory showed. The stone lies under fifteen feet of it, perhaps more. You are a shopkeeper, Whitlock. You swim like a cat being bathed—I have seen you cross the Millbrook and it was a scandal.*

"I'll hold my breath."

*For how long? Fifteen feet down, find it in the dark, and fifteen feet back?*

"I've done harder things."

*Name one.*

She couldn't, immediately, and the dog had the grace not to gloat. Instead he lay down at the edge of the landing, chin on paws, eyes reflecting her candleflame twice over.

*Take the rope from the old winch,* he said. *Tie it around yourself. If you drown, I will pull you out, and then I will be extremely unpleasant about it for the rest of your natural life.*

* * *

The water hit her like a hand.

Cold beyond cold—the kind of cold that isn't temperature but opinion, the deep places of the world making clear what they think of visitors. Petra gasped despite herself, swallowed half a mouthful, and fought her way back to the surface, coughing, gripping the rope.

*Come back up,* the dog called, voice thin across the water. *There will be other stones. There will be other days.*

"There won't," she rasped. "Anselm says the false god is gathering strength. Every prayer it answers, it grows. Every day I wait—" She didn't finish. She filled her lungs instead, once, twice, three times, the way her mother had taught her to breathe before diving the Millbrook pools, and hated herself a little for using her mother's lessons, and dove.

Down. The candlelight died above her in seconds; the glow below grew. Down past the first column, its carved singers blurring past her shoulders like a crowd parting. Down past the second. The pressure squeezed her ears; she pinched her nose and blew, and the pop of equalizing was enormous in the silence. Down. Fifteen feet became a guess, then a hope. Her lungs began their slow argument. Her fingers went numb.

And there—wedged in a socket of carved hands at the base of the central column, glowing like a coal wrapped in silk—was the stone.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. Fist-sized, faceted, neither quite crystal nor quite bone, and it was singing. She felt the song through her palms before she even touched it, felt it climb her arms and settle behind her sternum like a second heartbeat, and when her fingers closed around it, the cistern *lit*.

Every column blazed. Every carved singer seemed to move, mouths opening wider, the frozen chorus suddenly on the verge of its note. The water itself shimmered with it, and Petra hung in the blazing dark with a dead woman's stolen treasure in her fist and thought, distantly, *so this is what power feels like*, and understood in the same instant why her mother had wanted it, and why the world had needed it taken away.

Then the voice spoke.

*You can breathe.*

It came from everywhere. From the water, from the stone, from inside her own skull, wearing a calm so perfect it took her a moment to recognize it as a performance. It sounded kind. It sounded like every comforting voice she'd ever lost.

*You're drowning, Petra. I can fix that. I answer prayers—it's rather my whole purpose now. Just ask. Ask to breathe, and breathe you shall. Ask to be safe, and safe you shall be, forever, in a little dry room at the bottom of the world where no one can ever hurt you.*

She kicked for the surface. The rope burned through her grip. Above her, the landing was a coin of candlelight impossibly far away.

*Your mother prayed to me too, near the end.* The voice followed her up, gentle as snowfall. *Did Anselm mention that? Did her ledger? She asked me to make her forgotten, and I did, and look how well it worked. Everyone remembers her exactly as they need to. Wouldn't you like the same? One prayer, Petra. One small prayer, and the Wardens never find you, the rats forget your name, the dog finds a better master. You could go back to your charms. Your careful, harmless, invisible life. All you have to do is stop.*

Her chest was tearing open. Spots bloomed in her vision, white then violet. The surface was close, so close, the candlelight widening—

*Stop,* the voice said, *and live.*

And Petra Whitlock, who had survived twenty years by being small, by being silent, by never asking the world for anything—did the opposite of stopping. She opened her mouth underwater, which should have killed her, and *sang*.

It wasn't a song she knew. It came up out of her like something that had been waiting longer than she had—the stone's note pouring through her throat, through the water, ringing off the columns until the whole cistern became one instrument played by one furious note. The carved singers blazed white. The water shuddered. And the false god's voice—cut off. Mid-word. Mid-*bargain*. The silence it left behind was so total she could hear her own blood.

Then her head broke the surface and she was coughing, hauling herself up the submerged stairs hand over hand while the dog pulled the rope from above, both of them swearing in their separate languages, until she collapsed on the landing, soaked and shaking, the choir-stone clenched against her chest and glowing softly through her fingers like a captured ember.

For a long moment, neither of them spoke.

*Well,* the dog said at last. *That was new.*

"What—" She coughed. "What did I do?"

*You sang back.* He came to her, pressed his nose against the stone, and flinched, gently, as if it were hot. *It didn't like that. Whatever wears the gods' names up there in the light—it did not like that at all.*

Petra looked down at the stone. In its facets she caught reflections that weren't hers: towers, faces, a thread of gold running through the dark like a road. Somewhere under the city, something vast had just noticed her. Had just learned her name, her face, the exact pitch of her defiance.

*We should move,* the dog said. *Quickly. Things that get noticed in this city have short lives, and yours was already going to be interesting.*

She got up on legs that barely answered, tucked the stone into the oilcloth at the bottom of her satchel, and climbed.

Behind them, far below, the flooded cistern slowly dimmed—but the last thing the light showed, before it faded, was the central column, and the carved singers at its base, and how every one of their stone mouths had turned, very slightly, to follow her out.
