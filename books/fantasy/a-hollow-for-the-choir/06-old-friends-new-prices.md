## 6. Old Friends, New Prices

The Lamplighter's Rest smelled of tallow smoke and old arguments, and by the time Petra pushed through its door, the evening crowd had settled into the low murmur of people drinking away the day's small failures. She kept her hood up out of habit more than need. Nobody looked at a charm-seller twice. That was the whole architecture of her life: a woman built to be overlooked.

Tam had claimed his usual table in the corner—the one with the wobbly leg he'd never let anyone fix, because, he said, a fence should always have an excuse to lean. He sat with his back to the wall, as always, a thin man folding himself around a cup of wine like a letter someone was trying not to read.

He saw her come in. Of course he did. Tam Vess missed nothing that happened in the market district except, apparently, when it mattered.

"Whitlock." He lifted two fingers off his cup in greeting. "You look like a woman who's been talking to animals again."

"I look like a woman who's been working."

"Same thing." He kicked the chair opposite him out from under the table. "Sit. You're making the doorway nervous."

She sat. Up close she could see what the lamplight was trying to be kind about: the hollows under his eyes had gone from shadow to trench, and his rings—Tam wore cheap rings the way other men wore confidence—were gone. All of them. Even the tin one shaped like a fish that he'd sworn he'd be buried in.

"You sold your rings," she said.

"I *invested* my rings."

"Tam."

"In opportunities," he amended, and drank.

* * *

They had known each other twenty-two years, which in Vellamarch meant they had survived each other. They'd been children together in the Tanner's Rows before the fire took the Rows and half its people, two orphans of the same bad luck who'd learned the same lesson in different alphabets: Petra learned to hide, and Tam learned to sell. He'd started with buttons and worked his way up through lockets, letters, confessions, and once—a story he told only when very drunk—a bishop's seal that had gotten three men hanged and paid for Tam's first real coat.

He was the only person alive who knew what she was. Not all of it—he didn't know about the rats, or the hawk, or the shrine beneath the cathedral ruins—but he knew she spoke to beasts, because when they were nineteen and stupid she'd told him, laughing, certain he'd laugh too.

He hadn't laughed. He'd gone pale, gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise, and said, *"Never say that where walls can hear you,"* and then he'd bought her dinner and never mentioned it again. Twenty years of silence on the subject. Twenty years of him quietly steering customers with sick cats and haunted cellars toward her stall, and never once asking what she charged them.

That was the shape of their friendship: a secret held between them like a hot coal, passed back and forth so neither had to carry it alone.

Tonight he wasn't carrying it well.

"How much do you owe?" she asked.

"That's a rude question."

"How much?"

He turned his cup a slow circle on the wood. "Enough that I've stopped counting in coppers. The Kettle Street boys want their interest by week's end, and Marlow—" he said the name carefully, the way you'd handle something with teeth, "—Marlow wants it by tomorrow."

Petra felt the floor tilt slightly under her chair. Marlow Vane ran half the gray trade east of the canal, and the things people owed Marlow were rarely measured in coin.

"What did you borrow against?"

"My charm," Tam said, with the ghost of his old grin. "My considerable personal charm."

"Tam."

"And a promise of future earnings." The grin died. "I told him I had information coming. Something valuable. Something a certain kind of buyer would pay well for."

The room's noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Petra became aware of her own heartbeat, steady and traitorous.

"What kind of information, Tam?"

He wouldn't look at her. He studied his cup, the table, the grain of the wood, with the thoroughness of a man cataloguing the last view of a country he was about to flee.

"There's talk," he said finally. "In the market. About a stall that sells charms that work."

* * *

She made herself breathe. In through the nose, out slow, the way she calmed frightened animals—and, tonight, herself.

"Charms that work," she repeated. "Imagine. A charm-seller whose charms function. Someone alert the Wardens."

"Don't." His voice cracked on the word, and he leaned across the table, close enough that she could smell the wine and the fear underneath it. "Don't do that. Don't make it small. You know what I'm telling you."

She did. She'd known since the dog first spoke to her of the second sun, since the hawk fell burning out of a clean sky, since a dead god's name answered her in her mother's voice. The world was cracking open, and cracks were exactly the kind of thing men like Marlow Vane sold by the pound.

"Who's asking?" she said.

"Marlow's new buyer. Someone with deep pockets and a Warden's posture. They came through three nights ago asking after anomalies—charms that outperform their craft, animals behaving strange near the market, that sort of thing. And Marlow remembered I grew up with a charm-witch." Tam's hands were flat on the table now, pressing down as if the wood might buckle. "He gave me until tomorrow to bring him something worth selling. A name. A location. Anything."

"And you're telling me instead." Her voice came out flat. "Why? So I can run? Or so you can tell yourself you warned me before you sold me?"

The words landed. She watched them land, watched him flinch like a struck animal, and hated herself for the accuracy even as some cold, frightened part of her stood apart and noted it: *good. Make it hurt. Hurt keeps you honest.*

"Both," Tam whispered. "God help me, both."

* * *

Here was the arithmetic of fear, done quickly and without ceremony:

If Tam sold her name to Marlow, and Marlow sold it to a buyer with a Warden's posture, Petra Whitlock would be dead within the month. The law was simple and old: magic was outlawed, speakers were executed, and the pyres in Covenant Square were not a metaphor. Twenty years she had built her smallness brick by brick—harmless charms, honest prices, a face nobody remembered—and one desperate friend could burn the whole structure in an afternoon.

And here was the other column, the one that made her throat ache:

Tam had kept her secret for twenty years. Through debts and beatings and at least two occasions she knew of where men had held him upside-down over the canal asking questions, he had never once traded her name. He was selling her now the way a drowning man sells anything—with no pleasure in it and no alternative he could see.

Fear makes liars of us all, her mother used to say. It was one of the few things Maren Whitlock had said that Petra still believed.

"I'll give you the money," Petra said.

Tam's head came up. "What?"

"For Marlow. Whatever clears the debt. I've got savings—"

"No." He said it fast, and then slower, shaking his head. "No, Petra. It's not just the debt anymore. Once Marlow knows there's a buyer paying for this kind of news, I'm worth more to him as a source than as a debtor. Paying him doesn't end it. It *confirms* it. It tells him the witch in the market is real and rich and scared." He laughed, a terrible dry sound. "You'd be buying me a month and signing your own—"

He stopped. He couldn't finish it. Neither of them needed him to.

"So that's it," Petra said. "Tomorrow you walk into Marlow's and you give him whatever you've got."

"I give him something," Tam said. "That's different."

She stared at him. "Different how?"

"Because I've been thinking," he said, and here something of the old Tam surfaced—the boy who'd talked his way past a bailiff at fourteen, the fence who could sell a lie so smoothly it arrived home believing itself true. "A buyer like that doesn't want a name. Names are cheap; names get checked. What they want is a *story*. A trail. And a trail, my dear, is something a professional can manufacture."

* * *

They talked for two hours, heads together over the table like conspirators, which was precisely what they were becoming.

The plan was Tam's, mostly. He would go to Marlow tomorrow with a story built of ninety percent truth, because lies collapsed under inspection but truths arranged crooked could bear any weight. Yes, there was a charm-seller in the market district whose work exceeded her craft. Yes, she was cautious, solitary, careful. But—here was the crooked arrangement—she was already gone. She'd packed up three nights ago, right after the trouble at the cathedral ruins, right after the Wardens started sniffing. He'd tried to follow the lead himself, he'd say, chased it out along the river road toward Thornharrow, lost her in the rain. Dead end. Sorry. Worthless news, really, and he'd take the beating rather than sell worthless news twice.

"It has to cost you something," Petra said. "Or he won't believe it."

"Oh, it'll cost me." Tam smiled without humor. "Marlow doesn't forgive bad merchandise. I'll take a beating for it. Maybe worse. But a beaten man who brought nothing is a closed account. A beaten man who brought a *name* is an asset, and assets don't get to retire." He met her eyes. "I'd rather be worthless than useful. To him."

"Tam—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't thank me yet. Thank me when I can still count my teeth."

She wanted to argue. She wanted to drag him out of the city tonight, put him on a barge, buy him a new life in some town where nobody knew his debts. But she had seen what happened to people who fled Vellamarch with Marlow Vane's ledger open against them—they washed up eventually, in canals or in chains, and their running only confirmed the value of what they'd carried.

So instead she reached into her coat and set a purse on the table—not for Marlow, but for after. For the doctor, if it came to that. For the road, if he ever got to take one.

"For the teeth," she said.

He looked at the purse for a long moment, and then he pushed it back across the wood, gentle and firm, the way you'd return something sacred.

"Not yet. If I take your money and then your story goes wrong anyway, I'll have sold you twice." His voice went quiet. "Just—get out of the market for a while, Petra. However long this takes. Go be harmless somewhere else for a season."

* * *

They parted at the door of the Lamplighter's Rest, and Petra did something she almost never did: she hugged him, briefly, fiercely, feeling how thin he'd gotten, how his ribs pressed through his shirt like the staves of a barrel.

"Twenty years," she said into his shoulder. "You held it twenty years."

"Held what?" Tam said, pulling back, grinning his ruined grin. "I have no idea what you mean, and if anyone asks, neither do you."

She walked home through streets gone blue with dusk, past shuttered stalls and the first lamps being lit, and she lied to herself the whole way—that Tam would be fine, that Marlow would believe the story, that the buyer with the Warden's posture would chase a ghost down the river road and lose interest.

But lying to herself was a craft like any other, and she knew her own work was flawed. Because she had seen Tam's face when he said *a Warden's posture*, and she had heard the specific weight he'd put on the word *anomalies*, and she understood, with the cold clarity that had kept her alive for twenty years, that the hunt had already found her scent. Tam's story might buy days. It would not buy safety.

Which meant the time for hiding was over, whether she liked it or not.

At her lodgings above the shop, she lit one candle and took out the things she'd gathered: the rat's directions scratched into memory, the hawk's dying image of water and stone, the dog's stubborn certainty that something sang beneath the cathedral ruins. A flooded cistern, the beasts said. Old stone, older water, and a note of music that made even the rats afraid.

Somewhere down there, if her mother's sins had left anything behind, lay the first choir-stone.

Petra looked at the candle flame until her eyes watered, then pinched it out and began, in the dark, to pack.
