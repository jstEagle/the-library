## 7. The God That Answers Wrong

Brother Anselm had kept a ledger of miracles for eleven years, and in all that time it had held exactly nothing.

He kept it anyway. Habit, his superiors at the dissolved seminary would have called it—though they were scattered now, the seminary a granary, its library sold by the shelf-weight. The ledger was a slim thing bound in grey calf, ruled in faded ink, one page for each year since the gods went quiet. Most pages carried only a date and the words *No response recorded.* He wrote them anyway, every year on the Feast of the Severed Chorus, because a scholar's first duty was to record absence as faithfully as presence. Absence was data too. Absence was the shape left behind when something true departed.

On the third morning of the ninth month, in the forty-first year of the Silence, Anselm sat in his rented room above a chandler's shop and wrote:

*Response recorded.*

His hand shook so badly the ink blotted.

* * *

It began, as these things did, with a child.

The girl's name was Merrit Hale, eight years old, and she had been born with her lungs full of water—a wasting sickness the physicians called "the drowning," because no other name fit. Anselm knew her family distantly; her grandfather had once been a lay brother at the seminary, back when there were such things. The Hales lived in the Tanner's Reach, where the river smell got into everything and children died young more often than not.

Anselm heard about the healing from the chandler's wife, who heard it from her sister, who bought tallow from a woman whose nephew delivered soap to the Hale household. By the time the story reached him it had already grown a halo. But Anselm had spent thirty years learning to strip halos off stories, so he took his notebook and walked to the Reach himself.

Merrit Hale answered the door herself.

She was pink-cheeked. She was breathing easily. She was, by every visible measure, a healthy child—and she looked at Anselm with an expression he could not at first place, until he realized he had seen it before, on the faces of very old monks: a stillness too complete for her years. A room swept clean.

"Grandfather says I'm not to talk about it," she said pleasantly.

"I understand. May I ask you one question? Just one."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "One."

"When you pray now—to whom do you pray?"

The stillness rippled, like the surface of a well when a stone has not yet finished falling. "I don't," she said. "There isn't any need anymore." And she smiled at him, warm and vacant as noon, and closed the door.

Anselm stood in the alley between tanneries for a long while, writing. His notes from that day survive in his hand, though he crossed them out twice:

*Subject healed completely. Subject reports no further prayer—not refusal but irrelevance. Eyes clear, affect flat, memory (provisional) intact. She has the look of a vessel after decanting. Something was poured out to make room for the health. Must find what.*

He found out three days later, from Merrit's grandmother, weeping over her washboard. The girl had forgotten her mother's face. Not lost it—the way grief loses things—but misplaced it entirely, cheerfully, the way one misplaces a key to a door one no longer intends to open. When shown a portrait she'd drawn herself the previous spring, she said, "That's a nice lady," and went back to her games.

The grandmother gripped Anselm's sleeve with wet hands. "Was it worth it?" she asked. Not accusingly. Genuinely asking, the way people ask priests, even disgraced ones, even defrocked ones, even ones who hadn't worn the collar in twenty years. "Her life for—whatever it was she gave up. Was it worth it?"

And Anselm, who had spent his whole life preparing answers to questions like that, found he had none. "I don't know yet," he said, which was the most honest thing he'd said aloud in a decade.

* * *

By the end of the month he had nine cases.

He worked methodically, the way he'd been trained: sources triangulated, claims verified against physical evidence, dates cross-checked. He mapped them. He pinned slips of paper to the wall of his room above the chandler's shop, and the chandler's wife, bringing up his supper, remarked that it looked like a constellation.

"It is one," Anselm said. "Or the beginning of one."

The pattern, once seen, could not be unseen. Nine healings, nine blessings, nine answered prayers across Vellamarch—and every single one of them wrong in the same direction.

Not wrong the way sorcery was wrong, all fire and appetite. Wrong the way a counterfeit coin is wrong: perfect weight, perfect ring, perfect shine, and something underneath that made the teeth ache. A lame carter healed of his bad hip—who could no longer remember his wedding day, or why he'd loved the woman in the portrait above his bed. A widow's prayer for rain answered within the hour, a soft warm rain that ended the drought on her street—and the widow woke the next morning unable to recall her husband's voice, or her daughter's name, or twenty years of mornings, gone like fog off glass. A fevered boy saved. A debt forgiven—literally: the moneylender woke with no memory of the debt, or of lending anything to anyone, his ledgers blank in neat rows.

Always the same exchange. Health, luck, mercy—given freely, eagerly, almost desperately. And always the payment taken from the same account: memory. Selfhood. The small hoarded treasury of a life.

"They're not being robbed," Anselm murmured to his wall of paper, late one night. "They're paying willingly. They don't even know they've paid."

He began attending the gatherings.

They met in a disused wool warehouse near the Salt Gate, and they called themselves nothing grander than *the Answered*. There were perhaps sixty of them when Anselm first slipped in among them, standing at the back among the tallow-smoke and the wet wool smell. They sang no hymns. They recited no creed. They simply told their stories, one after another, in voices gone smooth and calm as river stones, and the others listened with shining faces, and at the end they prayed together to—

To what? That was the question that kept Anselm awake. They used the old names. Vessa-of-the-Deep-Well. Corm the Shepherd. The Mother Under Hill. Names out of the liturgies Anselm had spent his youth transcribing, names that had hung silent for two decades like bells with their clappers removed.

But the names rang wrong. He felt it in his spine before he could articulate it, the way a musician hears a false note. He spent a week in the ruined seminary library—now open to anyone with coin for the archivist's fee—pulling the old concordances, checking each name against its original usage. And there it was, small and terrible, in a marginal note by some long-dead canon:

*The Name is not the god. The Name is the door. Guard the doors.*

Someone—or something—was knocking on the old doors. And whatever stood on the other side answering, it was not what had once come through them.

* * *

The twelfth case broke him.

Her name was Odile Ferrant, a laundress of fifty-three years, and Anselm had known her slightly—she washed linens for the chandler's household, and she had once, unprompted, brought his shirts back folded with sprigs of lavender tucked in the collars, because she remembered that his mother had done the same. She was one of those people who remember everything: birthdays, debts of kindness, the exact words someone said to them twenty years ago on a bridge. Her memory was famous on her street. It was, in a city that had lost its gods, a kind of holiness. People brought her disputes to settle. *Ask Odile; she'll know.*

In the sixth month she had prayed—for her grandson, sick with the marsh-fever. The boy recovered. Odile forgot him.

Not the fact of him. Everything of him. She stood in her own kitchen looking at a child's cup on the shelf and asked her neighbor, politely, whose it was. The neighbor told her, and Odile laughed, and said, "Well, whoever he belongs to, I hope he's well," and turned back to her washing, and the neighbor swore afterward that the laugh was the worst part. Warm. Untroubled. A laugh with nobody home behind it.

Anselm went to see her. He brought the lavender, absurdly, as if the scent might serve as a key.

She received him kindly. She did not know him either, but she was courteous to strangers; that much of her remained, the shell of her manners, the habit of her hands. He sat in her kitchen and asked her gentle questions and she answered them all pleasantly, and every answer was a little emptier than the last, and at the end she leaned forward and said—eagerly, confidingly, the way the others did when they spoke of their blessing:

"You should pray too, Brother. You've got such a worried face. It answers, you know. It *answers.*"

"What does it cost?" Anselm asked. "When it answers. What do you give?"

Odile frowned, genuinely puzzled, searching a self that had holes in it like a moth-eaten coat. "Nothing," she said at last, brightly. "It asks for nothing. It only wants us to be happy." Then, softer, with something flickering far down beneath the brightness—something that might have been the last drowned light of the woman who remembered everything: "Doesn't it?"

Anselm walked home through streets gone dark, and he did not sleep, and at dawn he sat down at his desk and did not write in the ledger. Instead he pulled out fresh paper and began a second document, one he had hoped never to write: a report. Addressed to no authority that existed anymore, because the authorities that had once handled such matters were the ones who had outlawed the very subject. Addressed, in the end, to whoever might someday find it.

*Findings,* he wrote. *First: prayers are being answered in Vellamarch, in quantity, under the old Names. Second: the entity answering is not the deity those Names belong to. Third: its blessings are transactions disguised as gifts; the currency is memory, identity, selfhood. Fourth: it does not select the strong. It selects the desperate—the sick, the grieving, the indebted—and it builds its congregation out of what remains after payment. Fifth:*

He stopped. Dipped the pen. His hand was steady now; horror, past a certain depth, goes very calm.

*Fifth: it is not consuming them. Consumption leaves waste. This is husbandry. It is keeping them. Fed, healthy, content, empty. A congregation is not a meal. A congregation is a harvest that walks itself in.*

He thought of Merrit Hale's swept-clean eyes. Of the carter who kissed a stranger's portrait goodnight. Of Odile asking, *doesn't it want anything?* with the ghost of her whole self flickering underneath.

Twenty years ago, the chorus of heaven had been severed mid-note. Anselm had documented the Silence itself, in his early days—had interviewed the last witnesses, the ones who claimed to have felt it go, a sound ending that had never begun again. The official histories called it the Withdrawal, the gods' inscrutable departure, and the Wardens had built a regime on the certainty that magic died with them.

But what if the chorus hadn't broken?

What if it had been *cut*—and cut by human hands, from the human side—and what waited beyond the old Names now was not a returning god but something that had slipped through the wound? Something wearing the doors like masks.

If that were true, then somewhere in this city were the hands that held the knife. Twenty years uncaught, unaged perhaps, unnamed certainly. And somewhere—this was the thought that made him set down the pen and press his palms against his eyes—somewhere there might be a record. Conspirators keep records. Thieves keep ledgers. Whoever had cut the covenant would have needed to write down what they'd done, if only to trust their own memory of it.

He thought of the old covenant rites, the ones he had spent his youth copying out in a scriptorium that smelled of ink and beeswax. Three realms, three voices, three stones—one for the speech of men, one for the speech of beasts, one for the speech of the divine. The rite required all three, sung together, to bind the worlds in their long conversation. If the covenant had been cut rather than broken, then the stones—

The stones would still exist.

Anselm rose so quickly his chair went over backward. He caught it, steadied himself, and stood in his narrow room surrounded by his constellation of the damned, feeling something he had not felt since his ordination: purpose with edges on it.

He was an old man, disgraced, unfrocked, tolerated by his landlady and ignored by his city. He had no gift, no magic, no standing. He had a notebook, a fair hand, and thirty years of scholarship in a subject everyone else pretended was dead.

But he knew the rites. He knew the Names' true shapes, and could tell the false knock from the true. And he knew—if the stones survived, if they could be gathered, if the covenant could be sung again—exactly what the rite demanded: a voice for each realm. Human. Beast. Divine.

He did not yet know who the other voices would be. But he knew where he would begin looking: with the families of the ones who had cut the covenant. Traitors keep their secrets close, and secrets pass down like eye color.

He blew out his candle, and in the dark he spoke aloud—the first prayer he had offered in twenty years, addressed carefully, precisely, to no Name at all, just to the silence itself, in case anything true was still listening:

*I am coming. Hold on.*

Somewhere across the city, in a charm-seller's stall in the market district, a woman he had never met was about to find her mother's handwriting on the inside of the world's wound. The ledger of the Whitlocks waited, sealed and patient, and it was going to change both their lives.

But that morning, Brother Anselm knew none of it. He only knew that the god answering wrong had counted sixty souls, and was counting still—and that counting was a thing one could interrupt, if one moved quickly, and did not stop to be afraid.

He packed his notebook, and went out into the grey dawn to be afraid later.
