## 8. Mother's Ledger

The seal on my mother's effects had been broken for three days before I worked up the courage to look inside.

It was a wax stamp of the Wardens' office, pressed into grey tallow, and it should have been intact—should have stayed sealed until the state decided what a dead witch-collaborator's belongings were worth as evidence. That it wasn't told me one of two things: either someone in the evidence vault had sticky fingers and poor taste in loot, or the Wardens had already been through everything worth taking and left the rest as dross.

Both possibilities frightened me equally. Both meant eyes.

I waited until the shop was shuttered and the lamp turned low, until the evening had settled into that particular Vellamarch quiet where the bells stop counting hours and the city starts listening to itself instead. The dog came to the back door as he always did, a brindled street-thing with one torn ear who had never given me his name because, he said once, *names are collars you put on yourselves*. He curled up under the workbench with a sigh like a bellows settling, and I took the box down from the high shelf where I'd hidden it behind the jars of dried lavender no one ever bought.

*You smell of fear,* the dog observed, without opening his eyes. *Not the good kind. Not the running kind. The other kind.*

"There isn't another kind."

*There is,* he said. *The kind where you're afraid of a thing you already know.*

I didn't answer him, because there was nothing honest to say, and lying to a dog is worse than lying to a person. They hear the shape of the lie underneath.

The box was small. Pine boards, iron corners gone brown with rust, and heavier than its size suggested—not with objects but with consequence; I felt it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. Inside, packed in wood shavings long since gone grey: her good shawl, smelling faintly of smoke even after twenty years. A hairbrush with three silver strands still caught in it. A ring I'd never seen before, plain band, worn smooth. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed cord, a book.

Not a grimoire. Nothing so dramatic. It was a ledger—the kind merchants keep, ruled columns, leather spine cracked from use—and my hands shook so badly unwrapping it that the oilcloth whispered like something breathing.

"Mother," I said aloud, though she'd been twenty years dead and had never answered me once, not even through the beasts, who loved her too—I'd always assumed that was just how it went, that animals grieved for everyone who fed them. "What did you do?"

*Read it,* said the dog, and opened both eyes. *And read it fast. Fear goes stale. Better to choke it down fresh.*

---

The first pages were innocent. Accounts. My mother had kept books for the charm-stall the way I keep them now—ink neat, sums correct, margins annotated in a small slanted hand I recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in a stranger's face. *Lavender, 2 bundles. Tallow, 6 lb. Paid the widow Sarn for eggs.* Twenty-two years ago. Before.

Then, eleven pages in, the handwriting changed.

Not gradually. One line it was my mother's careful script; the next line was the same hand moving faster, harder, the pen digging grooves into the paper, letters leaning like people running. She had written whatever came next in a single sitting—I could tell by the ink, all the same brown-black, none of it drying unevenly—and she had written it in the form she knew best, because it was the only form she trusted:

She had recorded it as an account.

*Received:* the entry began. *One choir-stone, the First Verse, removed from the Undercroft of the Cathedral of Vellamarch by consent of those present. Condition: whole. Song within: dormant, per agreement.*

My skin went cold in the warm room. Anselm had used those words—the Hollowing, the severed chorus—but hearing them in my mother's voice, in her own accounting hand, was different. Anselm described history. This was a receipt.

I kept reading. The lamp guttered. The dog had come out from under the bench and sat close against my leg, his warmth pressing through my skirts, and I don't think either of us moved for a long time.

There were three names. She wrote them plainly, no codes, no false humility of initials, as if she wanted whoever found this page to have no room for mercy in their understanding.

*Merrin Whitlock.* My mother. *Keeper of the stone; author of the severance rite.*

*Cassian Vale.* The name meant nothing to me, and then it did—a flicker, a memory of my mother going very still once when a man of that name passed our stall, years before the fall. She had gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise and smiled at him like a neighbor. *Warden-Captain at time of covenant. Holder of the second seal.*

And third: *Sister Ondine Marrow, called the Listener. Keeper of the rites; keeper of the vessel.*

Vessel. The word sat wrong in me, a splinter working sideways, and I set it aside because there was more, so much more, each line a step deeper into water over my head.

They had not broken the covenant. That was the thing the ledger made brutally clear, the thing Anselm had only half-guessed. The gods had not fallen silent on their own, nor abandoned us, nor died. My mother and two others had *cut them off*—severed the chorus at its root, between realms, using a rite of my mother's own composition—and they had done it deliberately, carefully, and with what my mother's own margin notes called, in a phrase that will follow me to my grave, *"merciful necessity."*

Because the gods had begun to demand.

I read her justification four times, hating myself a little more each pass. In the years before the Hollowing, the true gods' answers to prayer had grown hungry. Healing asked for years of the healed one's life. Blessing asked for memory, for love, for children unborn. The old covenant had been fraying, she wrote—or rather, the *gods'* restraint had been fraying, their need growing as the worlds wore thin—and the prayers of the desperate were being answered with interest rates no mortal could pay. My mother, who could speak to beasts and therefore to the divine current beneath all living things, had heard the gods themselves begin to call the covenant a debt owed *to them*, and she had concluded that the price of keeping faith would be the slow eating of the world.

So she stopped their mouths.

*We do not kill them,* her ledger said, in the hardest, steadiest ink. *We cannot, and we would not. We silence them as you silence a mad dog—not from cruelty but from love, and with grief. The stones hold their song safe until such time as wiser hearts may restore it. Three verses, three stones, three keepers, so that no one hand may ever undo what conscience required. Let the record show we acted alone, uncommanded, and accept whatever judgment follows.*

Let the record show. As if any record would survive to judge her. As if judgment weren't precisely what I was now, sitting in the lamplight with her crimes balanced on my knees.

---

*I knew,* the dog said.

I looked down at him. His head was tilted, one torn ear forward, and his dark eyes held me with the terrible patience animals have, the patience of creatures who watch us make the same mistakes across ten thousand years and never learn to look away.

"You couldn't have."

*Rats talk,* he said simply. *Old rats. There is a rat under this district older than your mother's stall, and rats remember what humans burn. We knew the chorus was cut. We did not know whose knife.* A pause, and then, gently, in the way he is gentle, which is not gentle at all: *Now we do. You are her daughter. The blood remembers. That is why the shrine spoke in her voice. That is why the stones sing when you come near. It was never your gift alone, Petra. It is the gift and the crime, wound together, and both of them live in you.*

"I didn't ask for any of it."

*No one asks for inheritance,* he said. *That is what makes it inheritance and not a purchase.*

I laughed. It came out broken, one note, and I pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes until I saw sparks. Because here was the truth I had been circling for days, the truth that had been circling me: I had spent twenty years hiding my gift, hiding my smallness, hiding behind harmless charms and a harmless life, telling myself the story that I was hunted because magic was outlawed, because the regime was cruel, because the world was unfair to people like me.

But the Wardens weren't hunting me because of what I was.

They were hunting me—if they ever truly found me—for what my mother *did*. And worse: the false god answering prayers in the streets, the hollow healings, the second sun beneath the cathedral ruins—that thing wore the old gods' names because it was what my mother's rite had *made*. A chorus cut in half does not die. It goes mad. Somewhere in her ledger's later pages—blurred, water-damaged, frantic—she seemed to know it too. The final legible entries were no longer accounts. They were apologies, or attempts at them, the same sentence written and struck through, written and struck through, until the last surviving line:

*If you are reading this, Merrin's child, then the insurance has failed and the debt is yours. I am sorry. I chose, and choosing was the only power I had, and I chose wrongly, or rightly, and no one will ever be able to tell me which. Finish it or forgive me. I could not do both.*

The insurance. One stone kept back—one verse withheld from the distribution—"as insurance," the ledger said, against the day the severance failed or needed undoing. My mother had kept the First Verse herself.

The stone in the flooded cistern. The stone I had pulled from the drowning dark while something wearing a god's voice offered me safety, offered me anonymity, offered me my mother—

It hadn't been offering me anything.

It had been offering its *keeper* a chance to come home.

---

I sat until the candle burned down to a thumb's width, and I thought about my mother's hands.

Everyone remembers their mother's hands. Mine had been strong and chapped from market work, quick with knots, gentle with injured birds. I remembered them cupping a fledgling sparrow, murmuring to it in the beast-speech I wasn't allowed to learn yet, and I remembered thinking: *she loves them more than she loves the customers.* I built my whole childhood on that. My mother the soft one, my mother the kind one, in a city full of hard people.

She had cut the throat of heaven with those hands. Gently. Carefully. With proper notation and clean sums and a margin note about merciful necessity. And then she had come home and fed sparrows and sold lavender and raised a daughter she taught to hide—to hide so well that when the reckoning came, the reckoning would land on the child who inherited the crime without ever having been shown the weapon.

Or had she? Was the hiding protection, or was it training? Had she been teaching me to survive the Wardens, or teaching me to survive until I was ready to finish what she started?

*Finish it or forgive me.*

"I can't do either," I said aloud, and my voice sounded young and stupid in the empty shop.

*Yes you can,* the dog said. *You can finish it AND forgive her. Humans think forgiveness means saying a thing was right. It doesn't. Forgiveness is carrying the weight and loving the one who handed it to you anyway.* He stood, stretched, shook himself nose to tail. *Beasts understand this better than you do. Every animal forgives the winter every spring.*

"And if finishing it kills me?"

*Then it kills you,* he said, walking to the door. *Everything does eventually. The question is never whether. Only what for.* He nosed the door open, then paused, silhouetted against the lamplit fog of the alley. *One more thing, speaker. Your friend the fence—Tam. He came sniffing round the back of the stall yesterday while you were at the cistern. Looking at the locks. Counting the windows. Rats saw him talking to a woman in a grey coat with a red cord at her collar.*

The cold came back all at once, total, like the cistern water closing over my head.

*A Warden,* the dog said. *He is selling you, Petra. Small men sell what they love when they're afraid enough. Forgive him if you can. But bar the door tonight.*

He slipped into the fog, and I sat alone with my mother's ledger open on my knees, the Whitlock name burning on every line, and somewhere out in the sleeping city I heard—faint, imagined, or perhaps not—boots on cobbles, and a knock beginning somewhere far away, coming closer, house by house by house.
