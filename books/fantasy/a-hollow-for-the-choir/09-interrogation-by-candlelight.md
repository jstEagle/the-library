## 9. Interrogation by Candlelight

The cell smelled of wet stone and old tallow, and Warden Ilsa Corvane had chosen it deliberately.

There were better rooms in the Bastion of the Vigil—rooms with iron chairs and drains in the floor, rooms where the light could be made to behave however the questioner wished. She had signed requisitions for them often enough. But tonight she wanted a room that looked like nothing at all: a table, two stools, one candle stub burning low in a dish of blackened tin. A room that said *this is only a conversation*.

She had learned, over eleven years of hunting the unlicensed, that fear made liars of everyone, but comfort made artists of a certain kind of man. Tam Voss was going to be that kind. The informants' ledger already told her so: three prior interviews, all voluntary, all cooperative, all useless. A man who talked that much and said that little had practiced.

She sat across from him now and watched him watch the candle.

He was thinner than his file photograph suggested—the charcoal sketch, rather; the Wardens did not waste silverplate on fences—and he had arranged himself on the stool with the studied looseness of a man pretending to be at ease. His hands were folded on the table like a merchant's. There was ink on his fingers. There was always ink on the fingers of men who kept accounts.

"You're not what I expected," she said.

"No?" He smiled without warmth. "What did you expect?"

"Bigger," she said. "The witch of the market district should have bigger friends."

Something moved behind his eyes—not flinch exactly. Recalculation. She noted it and let it go. Pushing there would cost more than it bought.

"I'm not anyone's friend," Tam said. "I'm a businessman. You arrested a businessman."

"At the third bell this morning. In the Salt Lane culvert, with four crowns of untaxed silver and a ledger written partly in cipher." She opened her own book—hers was bound in gray leather, official, its pages ruled by the Bastion's scriptorium—and turned it as if consulting it, though every line was already printed behind her eyes. "Your ledger is interesting, Master Voss. Not the cipher part. Anyone can cipher. What's interesting is what you *didn't* write down. You're careful about people. Names get initials. Descriptions get abbreviations. Except one entry, nine days ago." She read aloud, flat as a clerk reading freight manifests: "'W—paid double for the candle-wax inquiry. Nervous. Kept looking at the charm-stall.'"

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved.

"Charm-stall," Tam repeated pleasantly. "There are forty charm-sellers in the market district. It's a market. That's rather the point of markets."

"There are thirty-one," Ilsa said. "Two retired last month. And of the remaining twenty-nine, only one has been selling charms that work."

* * *

She let the sentence sit between them like a blade laid flat on the cloth—not brandished, just present, just visible.

Tam's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was itself an answer. Amateurs lost their smiles. Professionals held theirs, and holding a smile under that sentence required either innocence or extraordinary discipline, and no fence in Vellamarch had ever been accused of innocence.

"Works," he said. "You mean sells well."

"I mean works. A love-charm that produced actual affection in the buyer's intended—a cooper's daughter on Cooper's Row, three weeks ago. Her father filed no complaint because he was grateful. The girl has since been married." Ilsa turned a page she hadn't read. "A fever-break sold to the widow Hask, on Tanner Street, that ended a wasting sickness overnight. A luck-piece carried by a carter who walked away from a wreck that killed his mule." She looked up. "These are not charms, Master Voss. These are prayers with price tags. And someone in your market is answering them."

"And you think it's me."

"I think you're the man who knows where everything flows. Silver, information, favors—it all runs through the same gutters, and you've got your boots in all of them." She closed her book. "I don't actually need you to confess to anything, you understand. The silver and the cipher are enough for a conviction. Smuggling, tax evasion, conspiracy. Ten years in the quarry pits at Coldharbour, maybe twelve with the cipher counted as treason-adjacent. You'll be fifty when you come out, if your lungs last."

The candle flame leaned and righted itself. Somewhere above them, water dripped through old mortar with the patience of stone.

"So," Tam said quietly. "What do you need?"

That was the pivot. She'd felt it turn, smooth as a lock giving. Good. Now the delicate part—because the delicate part was not getting him to talk. Any competent interrogator could make a frightened man talk. The delicate part was making sure that what he gave her was true, and men lied most fluently when they were buying their way out of pits.

"Tell me about the charm-stall," she said.

"Which one?"

"The one that works."

He laughed—a short, genuine bark of sound that surprised them both. He pressed his knuckles briefly to his mouth, composing himself. "You know what's funny? Everyone thinks Wardens are terrifying. And then I sit across from one and she says 'the one that works' like we're two fishwives arguing over herring, and honestly—" he spread his hands, "—you're worse. Terror ends. This just goes on."

"Master Voss."

"Tam."

"Master Voss. The quarry pits have no candles."

The humor went out of him the way heat leaves a stone at dusk. He looked at the little flame in the tin dish as though apologizing to it, and then he looked at her, and Ilsa saw something she had seen perhaps five times in eleven years: a man deciding, consciously and with full knowledge of the cost, to lie to her well.

"All right," he said. "Here's what I know. Which is what everyone knows. There's a woman—could be any age between twenty-five and forty-five, brown hair, keeps her hood up, plain face, forgettable. Sells charms off a stall near the fountain square. Mid-market row, third or fourth stall depending on the day. People say her pieces are lucky. People always say somebody's pieces are lucky. Half of them are wrong and the other half are lying." He shrugged. "I couldn't pick her out of a crowd. Nobody could. That's the whole trick of her, isn't it?"

It was beautiful, really. Ilsa sat very still and admired it the way a locksmith admires a rival's work. Every word of it was probably false and none of it was provably so. Brown hair, hooded, plain, forgettable—that description fit half the women in Vellamarch and a fair number of the men, if you allowed for ambition. Third or fourth stall depending on the day—he had even built in the deniability of movement. And the closing flourish, *nobody could pick her out of a crowd*, was a masterstroke, because it pre-poisoned every witness she might gather afterward. If she brought ten market vendors in here and they described ten different women, she would look the fool, and Tam Voss would look like a helpful citizen who simply didn't know anything.

"He's good," she thought. "Whoever she is, she's lucky to have him. Or unlucky. Depending on how this goes."

"That's a remarkable amount of detail," she said, "for a man who can't pick her out of a crowd."

"I'm a detailed man. It's my failing." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the table, back and forth, a small nervous metronome he probably didn't know he kept. "Look—Corvane. Can I call you Corvane? Warden feels like it comes with shackles attached." He didn't wait for permission. "Suppose I knew something. Suppose I even knew *her*. What happens to me if I give you a name? Have you thought about that? Really thought? Because I have. I've done the arithmetic. If I name her, I walk out of here, and then either your Wardens protect me forever—which they won't, I've seen what your protection looks like, it looks like a locked door and a guard who falls asleep—or her friends find me. And she has friends. Everyone in the market likes her. Do you understand? They *like* her. When the fever took the Kess family's youngest last winter and the priest's prayers did nothing and her tea did something—people remember that. You'd be arresting the only person in Vellamarch who ever answered anybody."

"She's breaking the law."

"Yes," Tam agreed mildly. "So were the priests. So were the gods, some said, before they went quiet. Laws change. Pits don't." He lifted his manacled hands slightly, an odd little gesture, half apology, half demonstration. "I'm telling you my honest position, Corvane. Not my innocent position—my honest one. I will sell anyone anything. That's my trade and my sin and I've never pretended otherwise. But there's a difference between selling a thing and spending a person, and I don't spend people. It's bad for business." A pause. "And other reasons. Unbusinesslike ones. I decline to enumerate them."

Ilsa studied him for a long moment.

Eleven years ago, newly sworn, she had believed interrogation was extraction: pressure applied, truth released, like oil from a press. It had taken her years to learn that interrogation was closer to cartography. You mapped a man. You charted where his loyalties ran like rivers, where his fears lay like marshland, where the bedrock was and where the ground would not hold weight no matter how hard you marched across it. And the best maps—the only ones worth having—were drawn in honesty. A prisoner who lied to you was telling you where his walls were. A prisoner who told you the truth about his walls was, whether he knew it or not, handing you the map entire.

*There's a difference between selling a thing and spending a person.*

There it was. Bedrock. She marked it and did not march on it, because marching on bedrock gained nothing and broke your soldiers' feet.

Instead she said, quietly, "Her mother was named Whitlock."

It was a guess dressed as a statement, and she watched it land. Tam's thumb stopped moving on the table's edge. One heartbeat. Two. Then it resumed, steady as before—but she had it, she had the stutter of it, the small mechanical failure of a man whose pulse had jumped and whose body had briefly forgotten its own performance.

"I don't know that name," Tam said.

"Mm," said Ilsa. "No one does. That's rather the point of it."

She rose, collected her gray book, and paused at the door with her hand on the iron ring.

"Here's what happens now, Master Voss. Tomorrow morning you pay a fine that will hurt—one hundred crowns, which I know you can raise, because I know what you cleared on the Salt Lane run. Your cipher ledger stays with us. And you go home." She heard him shift behind her, startled; he had been braced for the pits, and mercy disoriented him far more than threat ever would. "And every month, for the next year, you will come to the Bastion and sit across a table from me, and we will have a conversation exactly like this one. About the weather. About herring prices. About whatever I like. And each time, I will learn a little more about where your walls are."

"And if I skip a month?"

"Then the arithmetic changes, and you'll wish it hadn't."

She opened the door. The corridor's cold air slid in and made the candle dance.

"One more thing," she said, without turning around. "The entry in your ledger. Nine days ago. 'Kept looking at the charm-stall.' You wrote that because you were already watching her yourself. Not for profit—for worry. Someone had asked you about her, or you'd noticed someone else watching, and you wrote it down the way careful men write down things they hope they'll never need." Now she turned. Tam had gone very still on his stool, and in the candlelight his face had lost its merchant's polish entirely; underneath it he was simply tired, and fond of someone, and afraid. "Someone is hunting her, Master Voss. And it isn't only us."

She left him with that, and with the candle, and signed the release order at the duty desk without meeting the night-captain's eye.

* * *

She did not sleep. She rarely slept after interrogations; other people's courage kept her awake the way other people's snoring kept ordinary wives awake.

In her narrow quarters above the armory, Ilsa sat by the window with her case-notes and the city laid out below her in rooflines and lamplight, and she turned the evening over the way she turned everything: looking for the flaw in the stonework.

The flaw, of course, was herself.

She had gone into that cell intending to break a fence and come out having extended credit to one. One hundred crowns and monthly conversations—her superiors would call it lenience bordering on corruption, and they would not be entirely wrong. She had traded a conviction for a map she wasn't sure she believed in. Because here was the trouble, the trouble that had been growing in her for months now, patient as mold: the evidence was *wrong*.

Charms that worked were impossible. That was the first law of her profession and the load-bearing wall of the entire regime: magic died with the gods, the gods died twenty years ago, and everything since that called itself power was fraud, madness, or treason. She had built her life on that wall. She had pulled down smugglers and hedge-witches and one poor deluded boy who'd convinced himself he could hear his dead dog, and she had never once doubted the ground beneath her boots.

But the cooper's daughter's affection was real—she had interviewed the husband; the man adored her with a simplicity that no philter could fake. The widow Hask's fevers had broken in a single night and never returned. And today, an hour before she'd gone down to the cells, a report had crossed her desk from the wall-garrisons: birds. Thousands of them, roosting silent on the battlements at dusk, facing inward, toward the cathedral ruins, like a congregation waiting for a sermon. Garrisons reported it as a nuisance. Ilsa had read it four times, and each time the same cold thought came back:

*Nuisances don't kneel.*

She looked at her own reflection in the dark glass—gray-eyed, close-cropped, the scar through her brow that recruits whispered about and she never explained—and asked it the question she had been avoiding since the charm reports began.

*If the wall is wrong, what am I standing on?*

Below her window, the lamplight of the market district glittered like spilled coin. Somewhere down there was a stall that shouldn't exist, selling answers that shouldn't be possible, protected by a liar with kind eyes who had given her a description that could have been half the city—and had, in doing so, told her the truest thing anyone had said to her in a year: *she's the only person in Vellamarch who ever answered anybody.*

And someone else was hunting her. Tam hadn't known that—Ilsa was nearly certain he hadn't known she knew it—but she had watched his face when she said it, and fear had moved through him like wind through wheat. Real fear. Not of her.

Ilsa opened her gray book to a fresh page and, after a long moment, wrote three lines in her private shorthand, the cipher she used for thoughts too dangerous to write plainly:

*Subject: unknown female, market district. Gift: confirmed, nature unknown.*
*Tam Voss: loyal. Leverage: none. Respect: —*

She stopped at the dash. Then, because no one would ever read it and because a map that lies to its maker is worthless, she finished the line:

*Respect: considerable.*

She closed the book. Below, the silent birds wheeled once over the ruined cathedral, a pale spiral against the stars, and settled again to wait.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would walk the market herself. No escort, no uniform. Just a gray-cloaked woman among thousands, listening to the noise of a city that had begun, very softly, to pray.

And if she found the stall—if she found the woman with the forgettable face and the working charms—she genuinely did not know what she would do.

That, more than anything, frightened her.
