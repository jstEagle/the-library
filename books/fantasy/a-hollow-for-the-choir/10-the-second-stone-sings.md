## 10. The Second Stone Sings

The dog led her there.

Not by barking, not by any gesture a stranger would have marked as guidance—just a slow, deliberate walking, pausing at corners until Petra caught up, choosing left when she would have gone right, as though it had been given an address and intended to honor it. She had learned to read the animal's silences over the years, and this silence had a shape to it: purposeful, unhurried, faintly mournful.

"Where are we going?" she asked under her breath, the way she always did, words pitched below the hearing of anyone human.

*Where you must,* the dog answered, which was no answer at all, and was also, Petra was beginning to understand, the only kind of answer the beasts ever gave freely.

They went down through the Weft—the tangle of lanes behind the dyers' yards where the buildings leaned together like conspirators—and out through a gap in the old city wall that Petra had known about since she was nine and had never once used for anything braver than avoiding a toll. Beyond it lay the burial grounds. Vellamarch's dead had been sorted, in the plague years, into two cities: the honored above ground in their marble rows, and the poor below, in crypts cut into the hillside like swallows' nests, sealed with lime and iron and the particular silence of people who died too fast to be wept over properly.

The dog stopped at the third arch along the lowest row.

Petra stood before it a long moment. The stone above the arch had been carved with something—a name, a blessing, a warning; time and lichen had eaten it to suggestion—but she could feel the cold coming off the dark inside like water off ice. Not grave-cold. Something else. A cold with a hum in it, very low, felt more in her teeth than her ears.

"This is where the rats said," she murmured. "Under the singing ground."

The dog sat. It did not offer to come with her. That, more than anything, told her what waited below.

She bought light from her own stall's stock—one of the glow-charms she sold to sailors who wanted to read charts without attracting harbor fees—and cracked its seal with her thumbnail. Soft amber bloomed in her palm. She took a breath that tasted of lime dust and old rain, and stepped through the arch.

* * *

The crypt was not large. Rows of niches climbed the walls, most still holding their bundles of wrapped bone, and the floor was a crust of fallen plaster through which the occasional skull showed pale as a mushroom. Someone had been here before her—long ago, or recently; the tracks in the dust were boot-shaped and half-erased. But it wasn't the tracks that made her stop three steps in.

It was the sound.

A hum. Faint, patient, rising from somewhere beneath the floor itself, and it was doing something to the air that Petra had no honest word for except that it *rhymed*. Every small noise she made—her breath, the shift of her boot, the tick of the charm cooling in her hand—came back to her slightly altered, slightly harmonized, as though the room were learning her voice and practicing it.

"The rats called this the singing ground," she whispered, and heard her whisper come back to her in thirds.

She found the seam in the floor near the far wall, beneath the niche whose occupant had been buried with a child's clay whistle tucked against its ribs—an offering she noticed only because her gift snagged on it, the way her gift snagged on anything that had once been loved. The seam was a slab, and the slab had an iron ring set flush into it, and the ring came up easier than it should have, as though someone had oiled it within the year.

Below: steps. Below the steps: a chamber that predated the plague-crypt entirely, because the crypt had been built *around* it, the way a tree grows around a nail.

She knew before she reached the bottom. Her whole body knew. The hum had become a chord, and the chord had become almost unbearably sweet, and sweetness like that—she'd felt its cousin once, years ago, when she'd held a dying hawk against her chest and let its fading spirit pour through her—sweetness like that meant power. Meant song. Meant stone.

The chamber was round, no bigger than a chapel, its walls raw-hewn and wet. In the center, on a plinth of black basalt, sat a fist-sized crystal that drank her charm-light and gave back something better: a slow pulse, gold-white, gold-white, steady as a heart.

The second choir-stone.

It was smaller than she had imagined. All this dread, all these weeks of hunting, and the thing itself could have fit in a bread roll. But when she crossed the threshold of the inner circle scratched into the floor around the plinth, the pulse changed. It quickened. It turned toward her—not physically, stones don't turn, but the way a face turns toward you across a crowded room when it knows your name.

And then it sang to her bloodline, and everything she thought she understood broke open.

* * *

It began gently. Warmth spreading up her arm from her fingertips, the sensation of being recognized, of a lock finding its key after twenty years in a drawer. Then the warmth became pressure, and the pressure became light behind her eyes, and then—

She was standing in a room that no longer existed.

Her mother's workroom, above the old shop, before the fire, before the arrest, before the name Whitlock became something spoken only in whispers and warrants. She knew every detail even though she had been six when she last saw it: the jars of dried vervain on the sill, the cracked mortar and pestle, the window with its view of the cathedral spire. And in the center of the memory, at the table, sat her mother—younger than Petra was now, hands unlined, hair still brown—weeping.

Not quietly. Not the composed grief of a woman at a funeral. This was the crying of someone who has passed through despair and come out the other side into a terrible clarity, tears running freely while her voice stayed level, because the level voice was the one doing the work.

"—and if I refuse," her mother was saying, to someone just outside the frame of the memory, "then they keep taking. You understand? It never stops. The tithe grows every generation. First the firstborn's health, then the firstborn outright, then—" Her breath hitched. "I have held a mother while the god's price drained her daughter down to a husk. I have *seen* what they ask. And everyone says it's holy. Everyone says the price is right."

The unseen listener said something Petra couldn't catch, and her mother laughed—a broken, awful sound.

"Right. Yes. Of course. If I do this, I'm the thief. Twenty years of covenant and I'll be the one who stole it." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way Petra herself wiped away tears, and Petra felt that gesture go through her like a blade, because it was *hers*, it had always been hers, and now she knew where she'd gotten it. "Let them call me that. Let them hunt me. Better a world with a wound than a world that lets itself be eaten politely."

Then the memory shifted, the way dreams shift, and Petra was elsewhere—in a place of white light and vast, patient presences, the covenant-chamber beneath the ruined cathedral, though it looked different then, whole, alive, hung with bells of living wood. She watched her mother stand before the assembled chorus of the gods—she couldn't see them clearly, only feel them, a pressure of attention like standing in a flock of ten thousand birds—and she watched her mother lift the first stone from its cradle at the heart of the rite.

Watched her carry it away.

Watched the light behind her gutter. Watched the gods' voices thin, fray, fall silent one by one, each silence described in the memory not as sound but as *absence*, a hole opening in the world's fabric, and with each hole her mother flinched as if struck, and kept walking, and wept harder, and kept walking.

"Why?" Petra said aloud, into the memory, knowing the dead cannot answer. "Why would you—"

But the memory answered anyway, because the stone remembered her mother's reasons the way amber remembers the fly. Her mother's thoughts poured through the vision, unfiltered, in her own voice:

*Because they were going to take her. They were going to take my daughter. The tithe comes due next spring and the chorus voted and the vote was yes and no one would tell me what form it would take—health, mind, life, they wouldn't say, they said the god decides and the god's decision is good. I begged. I knelt. I offered myself instead and they said the covenant doesn't trade upward, only down.* A pause, and then, quieter, rawer: *So I will break the machine. I will steal the note that holds the whole chord together, and the song will stop, and whatever comes after will be worse but it will be OURS, it will be human, and my daughter will grow up owing nothing to anyone's heaven.*

*I am saving the world from the gods.*

*I am saving my daughter.*

*I know what this costs. I accept it. God forgive me—I mean that literally, I hope some part of them survives to forgive me—*

And then the memory ended, snapped shut like a book, and Petra was on her knees on the cold floor of the plague-crypt with her palms flat against the wet stone, sobbing.

* * *

She didn't know how long she stayed there. The charm-light had died; the stone's own pulse lit the chamber now, gold-white, gold-white, patient as a heartbeat that was not hers. When she finally found her voice, it came out wrecked and small.

"You let them believe you were cruel," she said to the stone, to her mother through the stone, to the empty air—it hardly mattered. "You let me believe it. Twenty years I've hated you for breaking the world, and you *let me*."

The stone pulsed. Once. Slowly. Whether it was answer or merely rhythm, she couldn't tell, and she was too tired to pretend she could.

Because here was the trap her own heart had sprung: the anger hadn't dissolved. It had *complicated*. Her mother had done it to save her—that truth sat in Petra's chest like swallowed glass, warm-edged, impossible to spit out. And her mother had done it wrong. However merciful the motive, the severing had not stopped the taking. It had only opened the door for something worse to walk through wearing the gods' names, something that took memories now, took whole minds, built its congregation out of the desperate and hollowed them smiling. Her mother had smashed the lock to protect one child, and the whole house had been left open ever since.

*You saved me,* Petra thought, pressing her forehead to the cold floor. *And look what walked in through the door you broke.*

And underneath that, quieter, the question she was afraid to ask: *Would I have done differently? If it were my child? If something vast and holy named its price and the price was you—*

She didn't finish the thought. She was afraid of the answer, and the stone, gentle as it had been, seemed to understand that some questions should stay unfinished.

She sat back on her heels. Wiped her face with the heel of her hand—her mother's gesture, inherited twice over now, once in blood and once in memory—and looked at the stone properly for the first time since the vision released her.

It was still pulsing. Still waiting. And she understood, with the plain clarity that had been following her all week like the dog at her heels, that it had not shown her the past out of kindness. It had shown her so that she would *take it*. So that she would pick it up and carry it, bloodline to bloodline, debt to heir, and bring it where it needed to go.

One stone recovered. One still hidden—no. Two. The first, from the cistern, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her floorboards. This one, here, in her hand if she dared. And the third, wherever it slept, guarded by whoever remained of her mother's conspiracy—or carried, perhaps, in flesh.

She reached out.

The moment her fingers closed around the crystal, the hum rose through her arm and settled into her sternum, and it felt—there was no other word—like being *claimed*. Not painfully. Like a name being written into a family register. Like the difference between visiting a country and belonging to it. The stone warmed against her palm and went quiet, satisfied, its light dimming to a coal-glow, and Petra understood that it would sing again only when all three were together, and that until then it trusted her to keep it.

"I'm not my mother," she told it, hoarsely, uselessly, needing to say it to something older than herself.

The stone pulsed once, and for a heartbeat—half a heartbeat—she could have sworn it felt like forgiveness.

Or like pity. With the old gods' kind, she was learning, those wore the same face.

* * *

Climbing out was harder than climbing down. Grief makes limbs heavy. At the top of the steps she paused, looking back at the emptied cradle, the little chamber that had kept its secret through plague and purge and twenty silent years, and she did something she couldn't have explained: she bowed to it. To the dead in their niches, who had unknowingly stood guard over a fragment of heaven. To the child with the clay whistle. To all of it.

Outside, the evening had gone strange.

She noticed it before she could name it—before the dog, waiting faithfully at the arch, stood up and pointed its whole body toward the city walls. The birds. The rooftops of the Weft, usually loud at dusk with sparrows settling, were nearly bare. And along the ridge of the western wall, where the lamplighters were just making their rounds, things moved. Four-legged shapes. Dozens. More than dozens. Standing in the long grass beyond the ditch, facing inward, utterly still, in the way of animals gathered by something they could not refuse.

The dogs of the city had begun, softly, all across the districts, to howl.

Not the frantic howling of alarm. A low, sustained, coordinated keening, rising and falling together, district answering district, like a choir tuning before a requiem. And threaded under it, from the direction of the cathedral ruins, came that other sound she had hoped never to hear again at full strength: the false god's hum, swelling, hungry, *gathering*.

The dog pressed against her leg. Its thought arrived in her mind stripped of all its usual restraint, urgent enough to shake:

*They are calling the beasts to the walls. Whatever sleeps under the cathedral wants them there. Petra—something is coming, and the Wardens will meet it with arrows, and the animals do not know they are marching to a slaughter.*

Petra stood in the dark with two choir-stones against her heart and listened to the city's dogs sing the dead god's name, and understood that whatever she chose next, she would choose it in front of everyone.

She started running for the market district.
