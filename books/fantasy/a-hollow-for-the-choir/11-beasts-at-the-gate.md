## 11. Beasts at the Gate

The dogs began it, three nights before the walls filled.

Petra heard them from her cot above the stall—not barking, which was ordinary, but a long, low keening that rose from every quarter of Vellamarch at once, as if the city had grown a throat and found it full of grief. She sat up in the dark. Beside her window, the stray she'd named Crumb lifted his head from the floorboards, ears pricked, and his voice arrived in her mind the way it always did: not words, exactly, but shapes of meaning that her gift translated as it pleased.

*The ground is wrong,* he said. *The ground has been wrong for nine nights. We dig and it closes. We run and the streets run with us.*

"What does that mean?" she whispered.

*It means the second sun is climbing.*

She thought of the rats' report from the cathedral ruins, the shrine where her mother's voice had answered out of the dark, and she pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars that were not stars. Nine nights. Nine nights since she'd pulled the first choir-stone from the flooded cistern, since the thing wearing the old gods' names had whispered *leave it* through the water like a lover's breath against her ear.

She had taken it anyway. And the city's beasts had begun to march.

* * *

By dawn of the third day, the market had emptied of anything with four legs.

The fishmongers complained first—no cats to keep the gulls off their tables, and the gulls themselves gone strange, wheeling in perfect circles above the cathedral district without ever crying. Then the carters found their horses standing at their stall-doors, saddled or unsaddled, facing east, trembling and refusing to eat. The pigeons left the eaves in a single body on the second morning, a grey river pouring over the rooftops toward the old walls, and did not come back.

Petra walked the market with her basket held too tight and listened with every sense she had spent twenty years strangling.

*They are calling us,* said a rat beneath the baker's steps, its voice thin and urgent as a struck wire. *The loud one below the stones. It calls in the shape of a bell. We hear it in our teeth. The dogs hear it in their blood. The birds hear it in the hollow of the sky. We are all being pulled by a hook no one can see.*

"Can you refuse it?" Petra murmured, crouching to fuss with her bootlaces as cover.

*Some. The old ones. The ones who remember the true bells.* The rat's whiskers brushed her knuckle, deliberate, a touch like a signature. *The young ones are already halfway to the walls. Speaker, they are not going to fight. They are going to lie down. It has told them that lying down at the gate is how the world gets mended.*

A cold walked up Petra's spine and put its hands on her shoulders. She knew that voice's trick now. She had heard it in the cistern, offering safety, offering quiet, offering *rest*. The false god did not command the way tyrants command. It soothed. It made horror sound like a lullaby, and the beasts of Vellamarch—hungry, beaten, kicked and caged and worked to death by the city above them—were hearing, for the first time in their hard lives, a voice that promised them their suffering meant something.

*They will go to the gate and they will not fight,* the rat said. *And the men in grey will see ten thousand beasts at the wall and they will not ask why.*

The men in grey. The Wardens.

Petra straightened, her basket forgotten, and looked east toward the old walls where the city met the marshlands, and she understood, with the terrible clarity that had been following her since the cistern, that she had perhaps two days before the thing beneath the cathedral ruins spent the animals of Vellamarch like coins.

* * *

She went to the walls herself on the second evening, against every instinct that had kept her alive for twenty years, because Crumb refused to stay behind and she refused to let him go alone.

The old wall of Vellamarch was a grey shoulder of granite running the city's eastern edge, built when the gods still spoke and mortals still built things meant to outlast their grandchildren. Petra climbed the watch-stair in the shadow of the Salt Gate, keeping to the dark, and looked out.

The marshland beyond was moving.

Not water. Not wind in the reeds. *Animals.* Dogs in packs and dogs alone, foxes, badgers, deer come down from the northern hills, cattle that had broken from their pens, horses, rats in rivers, cats walking shoulder to shoulder with things cats should have fled, and above it all the birds—crows, gulls, hawks, sparrows—turning in a vast slow wheel that covered the sky from the wall to the horizon. They were not charging. That was the horror of it. They were *arriving*. Settling. Lying down in the wet grass by the thousands, facing the city, patient as stones, as congregants.

As a congregation.

"Oh," Petra breathed. "Oh, you absolute *monster*."

*It is building a picture,* Crumb said beside her. His voice had gone flat, the way it did when he was afraid past the reach of fear. *A picture the men in grey cannot ignore. Beasts at the gate, in their thousands, silent. It wants them to be seen as a plague. It wants the grey men to open the animals' throats. And when the killing starts—* he paused, and Petra felt him searching for the shape of the thing he'd heard beneath the ruins, *—it will tell the dying that the city did this. That the city has always meant to do this. And every beast in Vellamarch will hear it, and believe it, and remember.*

The covenant. She saw it now, whole and obscene. The false god could not break the peace between the realms while the covenant's remnant held—but it could *poison* it. It could make the beasts believe humanity had declared war, make humanity believe the beasts had, and let the two worlds tear at each other until nothing remained that a covenant could bind. The Hollowing had cut the chorus. This would salt the earth where it might have regrown.

Below her, torches were moving along the wall-top. Wardens, dozens of them, and behind them the heavier silhouettes of the crossbow teams from the Citadel garrison. She heard a captain's voice, clipped and carrying: "—orders from Corvane herself. No loosing until she's seen it. But have the oil cauldrons readied at the Salt Gate and the Ash Gate both. If it turns, we turn it back. If it doesn't—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

Petra's hands found the wall's cold stone and gripped it. *Ilsa Corvane.* The name went through her like a fishhook. The Warden's shadow had been lengthening across the market for weeks—Tam's frightened eyes, the questions, the charms that worked when they shouldn't. If Ilsa Corvane stood on this wall tonight and watched a massacre, Petra would lose the beasts, the city, and quite possibly the war, all in the space of a single order given.

And if she stepped out of the shadow and used her gift in front of the Wardens of Vellamarch—

Twenty years. Twenty years of smallness, of harmless charms, of being nobody, of keeping her mother's crime and her own nature both buried so deep that even she sometimes forgot they were there. Every instinct she owned was screaming the old litany: *hide, hide, hide, the gift is death, the gift is the pyre.*

Below the wall, in the dark of the marsh, ten thousand throats waited in silence for a voice.

*Speaker,* said Crumb quietly. *They will hear you if you speak. But you cannot speak for them. Not tonight. Not for this. Do you understand what I am telling you?*

She didn't. Not yet. But the night was about to teach her.

* * *

Ilsa Corvane arrived at the Salt Gate an hour past midnight, and Petra knew her by the way the other Wardens straightened—not with fear, exactly, but with the particular attention soldiers give a blade that has never once been dull.

She was smaller than her reputation, grey-cloaked, unhurried. She walked the wall-top to the parapet above the gate and stood looking out at the marsh for a long, long moment. The torchlight caught her face: composed, pale, unreadable. Then she said, to the captain, in a voice like a ledger being opened:

"How many?"

"Count's impossible, Warden. Ten thousand. Fifteen. They keep coming. And they won't scatter—fired a warning volley at the west flank at dusk and they didn't so much as lift their heads."

"They're not here to fight." Ilsa's voice was flat. "Look at them. They're here to be killed."

"Warden?"

"Beasts that mean violence come in a wave, Captain. They come fast and loud and they break when they break. This—" her gloved hand traced a slow arc across the silent marsh, "—this is an audience. Someone has arranged them. And I want to know who before we give them the massacre they've been seated for."

*She sees it.* Petra, flattened in the shadow of the watch-stair, felt something crack open in her chest—hope, maybe, or its dangerous twin. *She sees it.*

But the captain's jaw was set. "Begging pardon, Warden. The Citadel's orders—"

"The Citadel's orders were written by men who think a wolf is a wolf." Ilsa turned from the parapet. "Wake the oil crews at both gates. On my word only. And send a rider—"

She never finished. Because that was the moment the false god stopped being subtle.

It came out of the marsh like a tide of cold, a pressure Petra felt in her back teeth, and every beast on the plain lifted its head at once—ten thousand heads, one motion—and *sang*. Not howled. Not cried. Sang: a single vast note, wordless, harmonized across a thousand species, beautiful the way an avalanche is beautiful, and in the note Petra heard the shape of the thing beneath the ruins wearing its borrowed names, heard it say to every creature on that plain: *they are coming to kill you, little ones, and here is the proof, and here is the grief, and grief is a door, and I am through the door—*

The wheel of birds above the marsh tightened. The packs rose to their feet. Somewhere in the dark, the first dog began to run at the wall, and then a hundred, and the note climbed into something that was no longer a lullaby but a war-hymn, and the captain was shouting for the oil, and Ilsa Corvane's hand had closed on his arm with a grip that would bruise, and Petra Whitlock stood up out of the shadow and walked to the parapet in full view of every Warden on the wall.

Later, she would never be able to say where the courage came from. She suspected it had never been courage at all—only arithmetic. Twenty years of hiding against ten thousand deaths, and the sum had finally come due.

"Wait," she said, and her voice was small against the hymn, and then she stopped using her voice at all.

She opened the door inside herself that she had kept barred for two decades, and she let the beasts *in*.

It was not like speaking to Crumb. It was not like anything. It was the cistern's whisper a thousandfold, it was drowning in voices—but these voices were *true*, muddy-pawed and feathered and furious and frightened, and they poured through her like a flood through a broken dam, and she did not speak *for* them, could not, there was no *for* left; she simply stood at the parapet with her arms spread and became the place where their voices met the air.

What came out of her throat was not one sound. It was the marsh entire—the dogs' grief and the rats' wire-thin warning and the hawks' cold arithmetic and the old ox lowing in the dark about the fields it had plowed for men it had loved—and it rolled down over the wall and across the plain, and every beast on that marsh heard its own voice coming back to it *from the city*, and the war-hymn stuttered.

The false god's note wavered.

*There you are,* it said, directly into Petra's skull, sweet as rot. *The little speaker. The daughter of the thief. I wondered when you'd show them what you are. Do it, then. Show them. They burn your kind on the same pyres as us.*

"I know," Petra said aloud, and her voice was her own again, ragged, human, shaking. She looked at Ilsa Corvane—looked her full in the face across ten feet of torchlit stone—and said, "Warden, I need you to hear this, because I can only say it once."

And she let the voices come again, but this time she shaped them, gathered them the way her mother must once have gathered them, and gave them words—not her words, *their* words, the beasts' own testimony, channeled through her and spoken plain:

*We were called by a voice that is not a god. It wears the old names. It promised us our pain had meaning. It brought us here to die so that you would be blamed. We do not come against the wall. We come because we are afraid, and because the one who called us has taken the voices of the true gods, and we are the only witnesses left who heard them sing.*

The plain had gone silent. Ten thousand beasts, heads raised, listening to themselves.

And on the wall, forty Wardens stood with their hands frozen on crossbow stocks, staring at a market charm-seller wreathed in the voices of every animal in Vellamarch, and the captain's mouth was working around words that wouldn't come, and Ilsa Corvane—

Ilsa Corvane was not looking at the marsh. She was looking at Petra, with an expression that Petra would remember for the rest of her life: not triumph, not horror, but the terrible stillness of a woman watching the foundation of everything she served develop a crack from crown to keel.

"Stand down the oil," Ilsa said quietly. "All gates. That's an order."

"Warden, the Citadel—"

"*That is an order.*"

The hymn in the dark surged once more, furious now, and Petra felt the false god gather itself beneath the ruins for a second attempt, felt the beasts on the plain begin to lean toward it like grass toward wind. She was so tired. The channeling had opened her like a wound; blood was running warm from her nose, her knees were water. She could not hold the gate against another tide.

*Speaker,* Crumb said, pressing against her leg, his heart hammering through his fur into her bone. *Hold one more minute. The old ones are coming. The ones who remember the true bells. They heard you. They are coming to stand with you.*

And out of the dark marsh, walking slowly through the sea of lying-down bodies, came the elders of the beast-world: a scarred grey wolf with frost on its muzzle, an ox white with age, a raven older than any bird had a right to be, a marsh-pony, a dog with one eye—and behind them, hundreds more, the ones who had refused the lullaby, coming to stand between the city and the called, and as they came, they sang, and this time the song had no false god in it at all.

Petra stood at the parapet, bleeding and burning, and held the gate open, and let the true voices through.

The false note broke. The marsh exhaled. And in the sudden, enormous quiet, Petra heard boots on the watch-stair behind her—two pairs, unhurried, deliberate—and knew, with a calm that surprised her, that whatever happened next, the invisible life of Petra Whitlock had ended on this wall, tonight, in front of witnesses.

She turned. Ilsa Corvane stood an arm's length away, and beside her, hood back and face pale in the torchlight, was Brother Anselm.

"You'd better come with me," Ilsa said. "Both of us have questions. And you—" her voice caught, just barely, on something she would deny later, "—you have until sunrise to convince me not to do my duty."
