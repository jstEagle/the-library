## 12. Witch of the Market

Ilsa Corvane had made four hundred and eleven arrests in her nineteen years as a Warden, and she had never once hesitated on the four hundred and eleventh.

She was thinking about that number now, standing on the marsh wall with her hand resting on her cuff-irons and not moving, watching the witch in the market district's shawl turn to face her with the calm of a woman who had already grieved for herself and gotten it over with.

The beasts were still leaving. That was the thing Ilsa could not stop looking at. Thousands of them—dogs and cattle and rats in silver rivers, crows peeling off the rooftops like burnt paper, the great antlered shapes of the marsh deer wading back into the reeds—and they were going *quietly*. Not driven, not fleeing. Dismissed. The way a congregation leaves after the benediction.

She had seen what they showed her. She would spend the rest of her life seeing it.

"Corvane." The witch's voice was low, roughened from whatever she'd done with it. "You heard them too."

"I heard an unlawful working," Ilsa said. Her voice came out level. It always came out level; it was the one discipline she had never had to practice. "I saw a class-four manifestation performed in open air, in front of forty witnesses, by an unregistered practitioner. I saw you speak with animals, which is a capital art under the Edict of Severance, article one."

"Yes," the witch said.

"And you're not running."

The woman—Whitlock, the charm-seller, the stall with the honest little charms that Ilsa had walked past a hundred times without a second glance—shrugged. Behind her, Brother Anselm stood with his hands folded like a man at a funeral, though whose funeral he did not seem sure.

"Where would I run?" Whitlock said. "You're the best hunter the Wardens have. Everyone says so. Even the rats say so, and the rats hate everyone."

*Even the rats say so.* The words went through Ilsa like a splinter finding old wood, because three nights ago, in a cellar off Tanner's Row, a rat had spoken to her too—or rather, through the witch, or *with* her, she still didn't have the grammar of it—and it had known things about her that no rat should know. That she kept her mother's ring on a cord under her shirt. That she had stopped praying at fourteen and had never told anyone why.

That she doubted.

---

Four hours earlier, Ilsa had come to the wall expecting a cull.

The order had been signed by Warden-Marshal Greve himself: the massed beasts were to be driven from the walls with fire and pike, and where fire failed, crossbow. Two hundred beasts at the Salt Gate alone. Ilsa had read the order twice, signed her countersignature beneath his, and felt nothing—which was itself a kind of alarm, because there had been a time when signing such orders had felt like something. Justice, maybe. Or at least the shape of justice, which in Vellamarch had always been close enough.

Then she had climbed the wall, and the beasts had turned to look at her.

Not the individual animals. All of them. At once. A thousand pairs of eyes swinging toward her like weathervanes in a single wind, and in that wind she had heard—not sound exactly, but pressure, meaning arriving whole without bothering to become words—

*You cut us out.*

And then the Hollowing had opened inside her skull like a door she hadn't known her house contained.

She saw it the way the beasts remembered it: the world when every throat was a prayer. Cattle lowing benedictions over the fields. Sparrows carrying gossip between the realms like couriers. The great slow voices underneath everything—the whale-song of the deep covenant, the root-choir, the stone-hum—that had held the gods and men in one long conversation. And then the severance: a knife made of silence, descending, and the chorus tearing along a line someone had drawn in advance. She saw three figures standing in the wreckage of the song, small as dolls, holding a piece of it that sang and sang and would not stop singing, and she understood, with the beasts' terrible simplicity, that this was not a crime of malice.

It was a crime of rescue. Someone had decided the conversation between realms was killing the world, and had amputated it, and the wound had never closed, and for twenty years both sides of the severed line had been bleeding into each other through the gap—and something had grown in the gap. Something that answered prayers wrong. Something wearing the old names like stolen coats.

When the vision released her, Ilsa was on her knees on the wall-stones and her countersigned order was lying at her feet, and the witch was already walking among the beasts with her arms slightly out from her body, singing—not a spell, just a voice, a human voice offered up like a hand—and the cull was dissolving around her.

Ilsa had watched a woman commit the highest crime in the legal code of Vellamarch, and had thought, with perfect clarity:

*She is the only person here doing the right thing.*

---

Now the torches burned lower. Anselm had said almost nothing, which was unlike him; Ilsa had questioned him twice before, years ago, when his name first appeared in the margins of confiscated texts, and he had talked then the way scholars talk, in generous spirals, offering context nobody asked for. Tonight he stood like a man waiting for a verdict he already knew.

"You understand my position," Ilsa said.

"I understand it better than you do," Whitlock said. "You've got me dead to rights. Class-four working, public, unregistered. Under the Edict you don't even need the Marshal's sign-off. You could put me in irons right now and be a hero by breakfast."

"Yes."

"So why are we talking?"

Because, Ilsa thought, because I knelt on this wall in front of two hundred witnesses and my own signature, because I have spent nineteen years enforcing a law whose origin I just saw in an animal's memory, because the law says magic was outlawed because it was *dangerous*, and what I saw tonight was dangerous the way surgery is dangerous, the way truth is dangerous—

What she said was: "Walk with me."

They walked the length of the wall, away from the last of the Warden detail, who were busy being confused about why they hadn't fired. Below, the marsh lay black and breathing. Somewhere out in the reeds, a heron called once—the ordinary kind of call, animal to animal, no meaning in it but hunger—and Ilsa found her eyes stinging at the ordinariness of it, which was absurd, and which she did not permit herself to examine.

"The Marshal expects a report by noon," she said. "Two hundred beasts at the Salt Gate. He'll want numbers killed."

"He'll get none," Whitlock said. "There'll be carcasses from the crush at the gate—a few cattle, some dogs. The rest dispersed."

"Dispersed by you."

"By all of us. They weren't coming to attack, Corvane. They were coming to *ask*. And nobody in this city has listened to them in twenty years, so they got loud. That's all a riot is, most times. A question asked at volume."

Ilsa stopped walking. The torch behind them threw their shadows long across the stones, three figures stitched together at the feet.

"Do you know what happens if I file a report that says *dispersed*?" she said. "Greve sends someone else. Someone who wasn't on this wall. Someone who fires. And then he asks why I didn't, and I have an answer or I don't, and either way, Whitlock, either way, your name goes in a ledger, and ledgers are how people like me find people like you. I have been reading ledgers since I was sixteen years old. I know exactly how a name dies in one."

"Then don't write my name."

"It's already written. Tam Voss gave it to us."

Something moved through the witch's face—grief, and fury, and under them both a stubborn tenderness that Ilsa recognized against her will, because it was the same expression her mother had worn whenever Ilsa came home having done exactly what she was told.

"He gave you a description," Whitlock said. "Not a name."

"A description that fits half the market district. Yes. I know what he did." Ilsa looked out at the dark water. "He sat in my questioning room with his hands shaking and gave me a woman of medium height with brown hair, and he knew—I could see him knowing—that he was spending his one coin to buy nothing. He did it anyway. Do you know why?"

"To survive."

"No. Surviving was selling you properly. He'd priced it, I think—he knew what a true name was worth, to the cult, to us, to anyone. He gave me a lie instead, and lied badly enough that I'd know it was a lie, and he did it while looking me in the eye." She turned. "Your friend chose you over his own skin, Whitlock. Whatever else is true tonight, that's true."

The witch looked away, jaw tight, blinking hard at the marsh. When she spoke again her voice had been sanded flat. "Is he alive?"

"For now. I released him at dawn with a warning. If the cult learns what he withheld—" Ilsa left it there. Some sentences finished themselves.

* * *

They ended up, absurdly, in the questioning room.

Ilsa told herself it was procedure: statement of witness, statement of suspect, containment of narrative before Greve's people started pulling threads. But she knew the room's other uses too. It was a small room, candlelit, with one table and three chairs, and it had a quality she had never managed to name—something about its size made large truths easier to say in it, the way confessionals did, though she'd have broken her own arm before admitting the comparison.

Anselm sat with his records stacked in front of him like a breastplate. Whitlock sat with her hands flat on the table, a woman deliberately not fidgeting. Ilsa stood, because sitting felt like surrendering the last inch of authority she had.

"Tell me about the Hollowing," she said. "Not what the beasts showed me. What *you* know. Names."

Anselm and Whitlock exchanged a glance with a whole conversation folded inside it. Then Anselm opened the top ledger, and Ilsa saw that the pages were copied fair, annotated, indexed—a priest's hand, meticulous, terrified.

"Twenty-two years ago," Anselm said, "three conspirators entered the cathedral undercroft during the Rite of Continuance. The covenant between realms was maintained by a chorus—seven choir-stones, each holding a verse of the binding, each requiring a living voice to keep it tuned. The conspirators severed the chorus at its first verse and removed the first stone. Without the opening verse, the remaining six fell silent one by one over the following months. The gods did not leave, Corvane. They were *unheard*. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire history of our city's suffering."

"Names," Ilsa said again.

Anselm looked at Whitlock. Whitlock looked at the candle flame.

"My mother," the witch said finally, "was one of them."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Ilsa found she had sat down without deciding to.

"Your mother," she repeated. "Maren Whitlock. Charm-seller. Deceased eleven years. Flagged twice in the early years for unlicensed craft, cleared both times, file closed by—" she stopped, because she was reciting it from memory, the file she had pulled three days ago on instinct, the file that had seemed like a dead end, "—file closed by internal review."

"Closed by the second conspirator," Anselm said quietly. "Who held high office in the Wardens' predecessor court. Who is, I believe, the reason the Edict of Severance reads the way it does—written not to prevent another theft, but to prevent anyone *looking* for the first one."

The room got very small. Ilsa heard her own breathing.

"Give me the third name," she said.

"We don't have it," Whitlock said. "The ledger gives initials. And the third conspirator took the second stone and vanished. My mother kept the first stone hidden—until someone found her hiding place, eleven years ago, the same month she died." The witch's hands had curled slowly into fists on the table. "Which means whoever killed her has been holding a verse of the covenant for eleven years. And I think—I *think*—that's what's answering prayers now. Or feeding on it. Or wearing it."

"That's speculation," Ilsa said.

"That's the shape of the hole," Whitlock said. "You're a hunter. You know holes have shapes."

She did know. Nineteen years of reading crime scenes as absences—the missing coin, the missing hour, the missing motive—and every absence shaped like the thing that filled it. She thought of the answered prayers Anselm had documented: the healed man from Cooper's Lane who had lost twenty years of memory in exchange for his lungs; the widow blessed with her husband's voice in the chimney, who now could not remember his face. Payment taken in the coin of the self. A god that hollowed its congregation.

A god built out of a hole.

"If any part of this reaches Greve," Ilsa said slowly, "it reaches the court, and the court reaches whatever remains of the second conspirator's protection. This doesn't die with a sealed file, Whitlock. Sealed files are how it survived the first time."

"So what does it do instead?" Anselm asked. Not challenging—asking, genuinely, the way he must once have asked questions of the rite itself. "You have evidence of a capital crime. You also have evidence that the law itself was authored as a lid over that crime. What does the law do when it learns it is the accomplice?"

Ilsa looked at the candle. In its small light she saw, quite clearly, her first year as a Warden, age seventeen, dragging a hedge-witch out of a burning cottage in the Fenward while the girl's charms—little things, weather-charms, milk-charms—crackled uselessly around her, outlawed and therefore unpracticed and therefore useless, and Ilsa had carried the girl's body out and told herself the law was the law, and had been telling herself that for nineteen years, and the telling had worked right up until tonight.

"The law," she said, "does what it can bear."

* * *

Near dawn she walked them out through the Warden-yard, past sleeping duty-officers and the smell of cold ash from the night's unfired pyres, and stopped at the gate.

"Here's what happens," she said. "My report states that the massing dispersed naturally upon the arrival of Warden forces, that casualties were minimal, and that rumors of a practitioner present are unsubstantiated. Unsubstantiated. I'm choosing that word carefully—it lets me reopen the inquiry later without perjuring myself about tonight."

"And me?" Whitlock said.

"You're a rumor. Stay one. Don't work in the open again—if the beasts move, you send word through channels I'll arrange, and you stay behind walls. Because hear me clearly: I let you walk tonight. That makes me a criminal too, by the letter of the Edict, and I have decided to be able to live with that, and the price of my living with it is that you do not make me watch you die for nothing. If you go back to that wall, I will arrest you. If you die on it, I will have done this for nothing. Don't waste it."

The witch studied her for a long moment. "Why?" she said simply. "You've spent your whole life on the other side of this."

Had she? Ilsa turned the question over and found it rusted through. "I spent my whole life," she said, "on the side of the law. I'm still there. I've just learned the law has a basement." She nodded past them, toward the grey edge of morning. "Go. Both of you. And Whitlock—"

The witch paused.

"When this ends, however it ends, I'll need your testimony. Real testimony, on record, with your real name attached. The city can't heal from a rumor." She held the witch's eyes. "So don't get yourself killed before you can be *useful*."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Petra Whitlock's face. "That's the coldest act of mercy I've ever received, Warden."

"I'm told I'm good at those."

She watched them go—witch and priest, down the empty street, two small figures in a city that had outlawed exactly what they carried—and then she went back inside and sat at her desk and began to write the report, and found her pen stopping at the first line.

Because there was a thread she hadn't said aloud. One detail from the beasts' memory that she had held back even from herself, the way you hold your tongue over a wound. In the vision of the severance, the three conspirators had stood in a triangle, and the third figure—the one who took the second stone and vanished—had turned at the threshold of the vision, and the beasts' memory had shown Ilsa its face for half a heartbeat.

Young. Round-faced. Ordinary. A face she had seen recently, in a questioning room, shaking hands giving her a false description.

Tam Voss was fifty-one years old and had been a fence in the Tallowmarket for thirty years. The face in the memory belonged to a man of perhaps twenty-five.

But faces could be worn. Gods wore names like stolen coats. And the third conspirator had taken a choir-stone and disappeared—and what better place to disappear than into a life so small, so failed, so magnificently irrelevant, that no hunter in Vellamarch would ever look twice?

Ilsa sat very still in the grey light, listening to the yard wake up around her, and thought about the fence who had lied to her badly on purpose, and wondered whether she had just released the most dangerous man in the city—or whether she had just watched a coward choose, for one shining moment, to be better than his own history.

Either way, she thought, reaching for a fresh sheet, either way, someone needs to find out which.

She wrote a single line at the top of the page, and underlined it twice:

*Find Tam Voss. Watch him. Trust nothing he says—and note everything he chooses not to.*
