## 13. The Third Stone Is a Person

The first stone hummed against Petra's ribs like a second heartbeat. The second, wrapped in oilcloth in her satchel, sang a lower note—something between a bell and a held breath. Together they made a chord that the rats of Vellamarch had begun to follow her for, sitting in little attentive rows along the gutters as she crossed the city at dusk, their whiskers trembling toward her like flowers toward light.

She hated it. She hated being followed by anything.

But she went to Anselm's rooms anyway, because the stones had left her no choice. She'd laid them on her workbench the night before, side by side, and watched them lean toward each other across the wood like two people straining to hear over a crowd. And in the space between them—where a third note should have been—there was nothing. A silence shaped exactly like a missing thing.

*Two verses,* the dog had said that morning, watching her pack. *A song needs three.*

*I know,* she'd said.

*You don't want to know where the third is.*

*Tell me anyway.*

The dog had looked at her with those amber eyes, and for once it had seemed almost sorry. *You already know. You've known since the shrine. You just haven't let yourself say it.*

Now she stood outside Anselm's door in the Candlewick district, where the lamplighters were making their rounds and the smell of tallow and old paper drifted from every window, and she let herself say it.

*It's him. It was always going to be him.*

Her knuckles hovered over the door. Behind it she could hear the scratch of his pen—he wrote everything, that man, recorded the world like he was afraid it would forget itself—and beneath the scratch, faint but unmistakable now that she knew what to listen for, a third voice. A stone's voice. Slow and deep and patient, singing under his heartbeat like a river under ice.

He answered before she knocked. "Petra. The door's unlatched."

Inside, the room was exactly as she remembered: books stacked in leaning towers, candle stubs hoarded like treasure, the great ledger open on his desk with its columns of answered prayers and their terrible prices. He sat behind it all, thin as a candle himself, ink on his fingers, spectacles pushed up into gray hair. He smiled when he saw her, and something about the smile—the simple, unguarded gladness of it—made what she'd come to say feel like murder.

"You look," he said, "like a woman carrying bad news in both hands."

"I found the second stone." She set the satchel on his desk, unwrapped it. The plague-crypt's damp still clung to the oilcloth. The stone sat there among the folds, dark green shot through with veins of silver, and it *sang*—she saw him hear it, saw his whole body go still the way a hare goes still.

"Mother's memory," he whispered. "In the crypt."

"She cut the covenant believing she was saving us. I saw it. I felt it." Petra pulled out the chair across from him but didn't sit. Sitting would make this a conversation. Standing kept it at the distance of an accusation or a eulogy, and she hadn't decided which. "Anselm, the stones aren't three objects. They're three voices. Human, beast, divine—that's how the covenant was sung the first time. That's why they resonate with bloodlines, with living things. They were never meant to be locked away. They were meant to be *carried*."

"Yes." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a scholar's stalling gesture. "I've theorized as much. The rite requires a vessel for each verse. Which means somewhere in this city there are—or were—three bearers. Your mother was one. The others..."

"The divine verse was stolen. That's the false god. The severed half." She made herself breathe. "The beast verse was my mother's line. Me."

"And the human verse?" He put his spectacles back on and looked at her over the rims, patient, kind, utterly unprepared. "Someone must have carried it into hiding after the Hollowing. Someone the conspirators trusted absolutely. Someone who couldn't be tortured, couldn't be bought, couldn't even be *found*, because they didn't know themselves what they carried."

The third voice under his heartbeat sang on, low and steady, and Petra understood suddenly why the stones leaned toward each other on her workbench and fell silent in his presence. They weren't calling to a missing thing.

They were calling to him.

"Anselm." Her voice came out wrong, scraped. She started again. "Twenty years ago, you were young. A novice. You told me—you told me you were sick that winter, the winter the gods went silent. Fevered for weeks. You don't remember most of it."

Something shifted in his face. Not understanding—not yet—but the animal awareness that comes before it, the way beasts go quiet before an earthquake.

"There was a surgery," Petra said. "My mother's records are very precise. Three conspirators. One stone too dangerous to hide anywhere findable. So they hid it where no Warden would ever cut, where no thief would ever dig. They hid it in a faithful man. In a man whose whole life would become the lock around it." She heard her own mother's words coming out of her mouth, the ledger's dry lines given flesh: *the vessel must believe his devotion is his own. The best cage is one the prisoner loves.*

"No," Anselm said. It wasn't refusal. It was the sound a man makes when the floor gives way.

"The fever was a story. Or maybe you really were ill—I don't know. But they opened you, Anselm. They put the first verse of the covenant inside your chest, and then they sealed you back up and let you spend your life serving the gods you were built to bury." Her eyes were burning. She blinked hard and refused to wipe them. "The third stone isn't hidden. It was never hidden. It's been singing under your heart for twenty years."

The candle flames didn't move. The whole room seemed to hold its breath with him.

Then he laughed—one short, cracked syllable—and reached for his pen, because of course he did, because recording things was how he survived the world. His hand shook so badly the nib chattered against the page and left a row of black dots like a wound.

"That's why my prayers were never answered," he said. "All those years, when the other novices felt the gods' warmth and I felt nothing—I thought I was unworthy. I thought the silence was a judgment on me specifically." He set the pen down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a knife. "And all the while I was *full*. I was the reason the silence existed."

"You couldn't have known."

"I could have *felt* it." He pressed his palm flat against his sternum, and Petra saw him listening—really listening—for the first time, and saw the moment he found it: the slow deep note beneath his own pulse. His face did something complicated. Grief and wonder fighting for the same ground. "It's beautiful," he said, horrified. "Why is it *beautiful*? I should feel robbed. I feel like a cathedral that never knew it had bells."

"Because it's not a tumor, Anselm. It's a voice. It's been keeping you alive, maybe. Keeping you *kind*." She finally sat, because her legs wouldn't hold the argument any longer. "Which brings me to the part I've been walking past your door for two days trying not to say."

He looked at her. He was not a fool; he had been a priest of a silent god for twenty years, which required more faith than most men ever need, and faith had taught him to see the shape of a sentence before it was spoken.

"The rite needs three voices," Petra said. "Human, beast, divine. Sung together, the covenant reforges, the false god returns to wholeness, the realms stop bleeding into each other. But the human verse has to come out of its vessel to sing. And the vessel—" Her throat closed. She forced through it. "The records are clear. The stone was implanted with a severing rite. Removing it isn't surgery. It's a *singing*. And the vessel doesn't survive the song."

The silence that followed was the longest of her life.

Anselm removed his spectacles again. Folded them. Set them on the ledger with a small click, and looked out the window at the lamplighter moving down the street, lighting wicks one by one, a small mortal ritual performed by a small mortal man who would do it again tomorrow and the day after, unless—

"I see," he said.

"That's it? *I see?*"

"What would you prefer? Screaming?" He turned back to her, and his eyes were wet but his voice was level, and Petra realized with a lurch that she was watching a man do deliberately, consciously, what she had spent twenty years doing without meaning to: make himself small. Make himself safe. Wrap the unbearable in ordinary cloth. "Petra. I have spent my entire life feeling like scaffolding. Like the building was the church and I was merely the poles holding it up until something real arrived. Do you know what a relief it is to learn I was right?"

"That's not—"

"It's exactly it." He stood, and crossed to the window, and stood looking at the lamps. "Every choice I ever made—staying in the order after the fall, keeping the records, refusing the Wardens' offers, living alone in this room with my candles—all of it, I told myself, was devotion. And some of it was. But underneath, I think I always knew I was waiting. Keeping something safe. I just thought the something was my faith." He touched his chest again, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "It was this. It was always this. My whole life was a reliquary and I am only now meeting the relic."

"You're not *only* anything." Petra was on her feet too, though she didn't remember standing. "You're the man who taught me the old rites. You're the man who fed me soup when my mother died and didn't ask why I wouldn't cry. You're the only person in this city who believed me about the rats. Don't you dare reduce yourself to a box."

"Then help me not to." He faced her fully, and there it was—the thing under the calm, blazing now, the scholar's fire, the believer's fire. "Help me spend what's left properly. How long? If we begin the rite soon. Days? Weeks?"

"I don't know. The records say the severing can't wait too long once the other verses gather. The stones pull at each other. The false god will feel it." She swallowed. "Weeks. Maybe less."

"Weeks." He nodded slowly, and then, absurdly, unbearably, he began to tidy his desk. Squaring the ledger. Capping the ink. As if there were time for tidiness. "Then I have conditions."

"Anselm—"

"*Conditions,* Petra." His voice cracked like a whip, and for one moment she saw the priest he must have been before the silence, the man who had once held a congregation in his palm. "I will carry this stone to your cathedral of roots and I will sing myself apart if that's the price of the world continuing. Gladly. But my records survive me. All of them. Every prayer answered wrongly, every price extracted, every name of everyone the false god emptied—they go to someone who will use them. The next time magic returns to this city, someone must know what it cost last time. Promise me."

"I promise."

"And—" He stopped. The fire guttered. What came next cost him visibly, word by word. "And you tell no one else what I am. Not the Warden, not the fence, not the beasts. If Ilsa Corvane learns the third stone lives in a disgraced priest, she won't see a man. She'll see an asset to be seized, or evidence to be burned. This stays between us until the night it can't."

"All right."

"And Petra." He came around the desk and took her hands, and his grip was stronger than his frame suggested, warm and ink-stained and trembling only slightly. "Thank you for telling me the cruel way. Straight, with no softening. If you had lied to me, or waited, or tried to protect me—I'd have died a lie. Now I get to die a truth. There are worse fates. I've recorded most of them."

She broke then. She hadn't cried at her mother's funeral—hadn't been able to, hadn't known how to mourn a woman who was both hero and traitor—but she cried now, ugly and silent, and Anselm held her the way he must once have held grieving parishioners, competently, kindly, one hand patting her shoulder blade in a slow rhythm that was almost, she realized, almost, the rhythm of the stone's song.

* * *

They worked until dawn.

That was his answer to death, she learned: work. He pulled twenty years of ledgers from under his bed and began copying out the essential pages—the rite fragments, the names of the emptied, the map of answered prayers spreading through Vellamarch like frost through glass. Petra read aloud from her mother's records while he transcribed, and twice she had to stop because the handwriting on the page was her mother's and the words in her mouth were a confession, and the two of them sat in the candlelight letting the grief pass through like weather.

Near the fourth hour, he asked the question she'd been dreading.

"Do you think she knew? About me?"

Petra turned the ledger page slowly. "Yes."

"She was there. At the surgery." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Three conspirators. She was one of the three."

"She was the one who insisted the vessel be someone willing. Someone who'd consent without knowing what he consented to." Petra met his eyes. "I used to think that made it better. Now I think it might make it worse."

Anselm was quiet a long moment. Then: "Your mother saved my life once, you know. Before all this. I'd fallen through the ice on the Mirch as a boy—a stupid novice stunt—and a woman pulled me out and warmed me at her stall fire and told me the river takes what it's owed and I'd owe it a debt someday." He smiled faintly. "I thought it was a folk saying. I thought she was being colorful."

"Oh, Anselm."

"She chose me because I owed her. Because a debt makes a man agreeable." He shook his head, but there was no bitterness in it—only the weary clarity of a scholar annotating his own tragedy. "Or perhaps she chose me because I was the only soul in Vellamarch who'd have said yes. Who *wanted* to be of use to the gods so badly he'd have agreed to anything." He looked down at his chest. "Both things can be true. I'm learning that everything true comes in pairs lately."

Outside, the sky was going gray at the edges. Somewhere across the city, a bell rang the hour, and beneath the bell—faint, distant, but growing—Petra heard the false god's chorus answering wrong, wearing the bell's voice like a mask.

Anselm heard it too. He went still, head tilted, listening the way she listened, and when he spoke his voice had dropped to nearly nothing.

"It knows," he said. "Doesn't it. It can feel the stones gathering. It's starting to sing back."

The third voice under his heartbeat rose slightly—not afraid, Petra thought. *Ready.* A note tuning itself for a final performance.

"Yes," she said. "We're running out of nights, Anselm. We need the Warden. We need Ilsa Corvane—and we need her to choose our side before the Wardens choose theirs."

He picked up his pen. Dipped it. And above the ledger, in his careful, record-keeping hand, he wrote the date, and beneath it: *Day one of the last verse.*

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we go hunting a hunter."
