## 14. Anselm's Choice

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow when Anselm finally set down his pen.

He had been writing for six hours. His hand ached in the old way, the way it had ached through seminary and scriptorium and the long gray years after, when the Church of the Silent Choir still kept its records and its dignity and he still believed both were the same thing. The page before him was dense with his small, careful hand—the hand of a man who had spent forty years making himself legible to God, only to learn that God had been made illegible by men.

*The third stone is not lost,* he had written. *It was never hidden. It was given legs.*

He looked at his own hands now, turning them over in the candlelight as though they belonged to a stranger whose biography he happened to have memorized. Knuckles scarred from a childhood on a farm outside Vellamarch that he could barely recall. A burn mark on the left wrist from the night the cathedral burned—no. Not from the burning. From before it. From the rite itself, though he had never known that until three days ago, when Petra Whitlock stood in his doorway with rain in her hair and her mother's ledger under her arm and said, in a voice carefully stripped of pity: *Brother Anselm. I think you should sit down.*

He had sat. He had listened. And then he had done what he always did with unbearable things: he had written them down.

*Conspirators: three. Maren Whitlock, archivist. Doss Corvane, warden-captain. And one whose name is struck from every record but whose role is recorded in function: the Vessel. A man chosen for his faith, his obscurity, and his health. A stone of the covenant, cut free during the Hollowing, was sealed into his body so that no power—not god, not king, not thief—could reclaim it without his consent or his death.*

His name was not in the ledger. But the date of the sealing was. The ninth of Frostwane, twenty years past. He remembered that date for other reasons: it was the day he had taken his final vows. The day he had lain feverish for a week afterward and been told it was a bad winter cold. The day, he now understood, when three people he trusted had opened him like a letter and posted something inside him that was never meant for human hands.

Anselm rose, joints cracking, and went to the window of his rooms above the defunct chapel where he kept his archive. Below, Vellamarch lay under a sky too clear for the season. The false god's influence showed everywhere now if you knew how to look: candles burning in windows that had held no faith for twenty years; processions of the hollow-eyed moving toward the cathedral ruins at all hours; a baker on the corner who had forgotten his own daughter's face but could recite the new prayers with perfect devotion. The city was filling up with people who had traded pieces of themselves for miracles, and the pieces were being worn, somewhere beneath the ruins, like a coat assembled from stolen cloth.

And inside him, quiet as a swallowed bell, sat the third stone.

He had tried to feel it since learning of it. He pressed his palm flat against his sternum in the dark and waited for resonance, for warmth, for anything. There was nothing. Only his heartbeat, which had suddenly become a stranger's drumbeat, keeping time around an object he could not feel and could not refuse.

That was the cruelty of it, he thought. Not the violation—he had made his peace with violations; the gods themselves had been violated, and the world went on eating and sleeping around the wound. The cruelty was that the stone had changed nothing. He had prayed every day of his life into what he now knew was a vessel holding a fragment of the divine chorus, and he had heard nothing back, because the stone did not answer prayer. It only waited. It was a seed planted in him by gardeners who never intended to see it bloom—who intended, in fact, that it never bloom at all, that it sit in the dark of a faithful man's chest until the faithful man died of ordinary causes and the stone passed out of reach forever.

He had been scaffolding. That was the word Petra had used, gently, and it was the right one. Twenty years of sermons and fasts and vigils and careful scholarship—all of it load-bearing structure erected around a thing that did not care about him, would not notice him, would remain exactly as it was whether he lived a saint or died a heretic tomorrow.

He laughed—a dry sound with nothing behind it—and the laugh turned into something else halfway through, and he let it, because there was no one to hear.

* * *

In the morning he went down to the archive and began, methodically, to prepare for his death.

Not in the manner of a man despairing. In the manner of a librarian expecting fire. He had watched the old order die once already; he knew how records were lost—not dramatically, mostly, but through neglect, dispersal, the slow erosion of things left to people who did not understand them. If the rite succeeded—if Petra Whitlock reforged the covenant and the true chorus sang again—then everything he had documented about the Hollowing would matter more than any book in Vellamarch. And if the rite failed, the record would be the only testimony that anyone had ever known the truth and tried.

So he worked. He copied the ledger pages onto fresh vellum in duplicate. He cross-referenced his twenty years of notes on answered prayers—the healings that hollowed, the blessings that took memory as tithe, the pattern of consumption that he had charted month by month as the entity beneath the ruins grew bolder and hungrier. He wrote out the full text of the covenant rite as Maren Whitlock had preserved it, including the parts that had made his hands shake to transcribe: the requirement of three voices, one for each realm. Human. Beast. Divine.

The beast-voice would be Petra. He had seen her speak with the market dogs; he had heard the rats' testimony secondhand through her pale, reluctant mouth. She was the voice, whether she wanted it or not.

The divine voice would be the stones—two recovered, one sleeping in his chest.

And the human voice—

He stopped writing. The candle guttered. Outside, a procession passed singing the new hymns, their voices sweet and wrong, like honey poured over gravel.

The human voice, the rite said plainly, must be freely offered and fully spent. The covenant was not a contract signed but a song sung, and a song required a singer who gave the whole of himself to the note. The original rite had used a choir of nine. They had sung themselves into the stones, becoming the bridge between realms, and the bridge had held for a thousand years.

There were not nine of them. There were three, and a dog, and whatever remained of a god.

Anselm did the arithmetic the way he did all arithmetic: without flinching, because flinching was how errors crept in. Three voices for three realms. One singer per realm. The stone in his chest was the divine voice—but a stone did not sing. A stone resonated. Something had to strike it. Something had to give it breath and intention and the willingness to be spent.

He thought of Petra's face when she had told him. She had rehearsed the telling—he could see that now, the way she had gripped the doorframe, the way she'd rushed the middle of it—and she had stopped short of the final implication, or perhaps she had not yet reached it herself. *Telling you means telling you that you're a vessel built to die when the covenant is remade.* No. She hadn't said that. She had said the stone must be present at the rite. She had said it might be dangerous. She had looked at him with those careful gray eyes and offered him every exit except the true one, and he had loved her a little for it, the way one loves a child who lies badly about breaking a cup.

But Anselm had read the rite. Anselm had spent forty years reading rites. He knew what *fully spent* meant in liturgical language, and he knew that the conspirators had chosen a vessel who would never know, because a vessel who knew might refuse—and a refusal at the moment of the rite would shatter the covenant forever and leave the false god fat upon two worlds.

They had built him to be willing without knowing why. And the terrible joke, the joke he suspected even Maren Whitlock had not foreseen, was that they had succeeded beyond their design. Because now he knew. Now he had the choice they never intended to give him.

And he found, sitting alone in his archive with the dust settling around him like a congregation, that he did not want to refuse.

* * *

He spent the afternoon walking the city, which was a thing he did rarely and had missed more than he admitted. He walked because his body wanted to move while his mind worked, and because he wanted to look at what he was deciding about.

Vellamarch in the last light of a failing autumn. The market district first, where Petra's stall stood shuttered—she was in hiding now, hunted by Warden Corvane, though the warden's pursuit had gone strangely quiet since the night of the beasts at the gate. He touched the counter of the empty stall, worn smooth by twenty years of harmless charms sold by a woman hiding a dangerous gift, and he thought: *her mother did this to her too.* The whole Whitlock inheritance was a wound dressed as a duty.

Then the river quarter, where the plague-crypts yawned under moss-eaten stones and the second choir-stone had sung to Petra of her mother weeping as she cut the covenant. He had read Petra's account of it twice. *She believed she was saving the world from the gods' demands.* The old gods had asked much—that was in the oldest records, the ones even he had only glimpsed. Tithes of memory. Generations of service. The covenant had been a heavy yoke, and three people had decided the oxen should simply be unyoked mid-field, consequences damned.

They had been wrong. But they had not been evil. That distinction mattered to him. Evil was easy to condemn from a warm room; wrongness required understanding, and understanding required the humility to admit that he might have helped them. Would he have refused, twenty years ago, if Maren Whitlock had come to him—young Brother Anselm, devout and obscure and healthy—and said, *we need a vessel, someone the stone can trust, someone whose faith will keep it quiet*? Would he have said no?

He knew the answer. He had spent the whole walk arriving at it, and the answer sat in him with the weight of the stone itself.

He would have said yes. He would have said yes because it was asked in the name of protecting the world, and because he was a man who had organized his entire life around the principle that love means carrying what others cannot. They had not corrupted him. They had recognized him.

That was the revelation he carried home through the darkening streets, past windows full of false candles and doors marked with the chalk-sigils of the new cult: that his whole faithful life had not been wasted or stolen or rendered meaningless. It had been preparation—not the preparation the conspirators intended, but a truer one. Forty years of devotion had not been scaffolding around a stone. It had been practice. Every prayer he had ever spoken into silence had been a rehearsal for the one prayer that would finally be answered, the one that would cost everything he had.

A man could spend his whole life learning a song he would sing only once.

* * *

Petra came at dusk, hooded, with the stray dog ghosting at her heel like a shadow that had learned loyalty. She looked worse than the last time he'd seen her—thinner, shadowed, the sleeplessness of a woman carrying two stones and a dead mother's sins. The dog settled by the cold hearth and watched Anselm with amber eyes that he now understood were watching, in some sense, on Petra's behalf.

"You've been copying everything," she said, looking at the stacks of vellum. "Even the parts about—" She stopped.

"The parts about me," he said. "Yes. Especially those."

"Brother Anselm—"

"I've read the rite, Petra. All of it. The original text, not your summary." He kept his voice level, gentle, the voice he had used for deathbed confessions. "I know what *freely offered and fully spent* means. I've known since the morning after you told me. You've been trying for three days to find a kinder way to say it, and I'm telling you now so you can stop carrying it."

The color left her face. The dog lifted its head.

"There may be another way," she said. "I've been studying the resonance patterns. If the stones can harmonize without a—without a human anchor, if we position them correctly, if—"

"If my grandmother's chicken had teeth," he said mildly, "it would bite." He crossed to her and took her hands, which were cold and ink-stained and trembling slightly. "Child. Sit. Please."

She sat. He poured the last of his decent wine—saved, he realized now, for exactly such an occasion without knowing it—and gave it to her, and drank water himself, because his head needed to stay clear for what came next.

"Here is what I want on the record," he said, "and I want it witnessed, so let your friend listen too." The dog's ears pricked. "Twenty years ago, three people committed a crime against the world, and they hid the instrument of their crime inside a young priest because he was faithful enough to keep it silent. For twenty years I prayed beside that silence and called it God's absence, and I was wrong, and I am not angry." He paused, surprised to find the truth of it as he spoke it. "I am not angry, Petra. Do you understand? They stole my ignorance, but they could not steal my faith, because my faith was never about being answered. It was about speaking anyway. And now, at the end, I get to do both."

"Anselm." Her voice cracked on his name. "You're talking about dying."

"I'm talking about being useful, which I have wanted to be since I was six years old and buried the first of my father's sheep with full honors." He smiled at her stricken face. "Every priest talks about dying, child. We just usually lie about wanting anything else. I have read every account of the original rite. Nine singers, each one spent entirely, each one recorded by name in the founding chronicles—and do you know what the chronicles say of them? Not *they died*. They say *they were heard*. A thousand years, Petra. A thousand years of beasts speaking and gods listening and the two worlds breathing together, bought with nine voices that were glad to pay. I have one voice instead of nine, and one stone instead of a chorus, and I am asking you—" and here his composure finally bent, and he let it bend, because some things should cost the speaker visibly— "I am asking you to let me carry it to the rite. To let me sing. To write my name in your record not as the conspirators' vessel but as a volunteer. That is the only thing I have ever asked of God that I intend to insist upon."

Silence. The candle flame leaned and steadied. The dog rose, crossed the room, and laid its great head on Anselm's knee—an act, he understood, of witness and assent, the beast-voice concurring in the only language it had.

Petra was crying silently, the way people cry who learned early that tears were dangerous. "Your records," she said at last. "You'll want—"

"My records survive me. All of them. The ledger copies, the prayer-patterns, the names of the hollowed—I've listed every soul I could identify who paid the false god, so that whoever comes after can try to give them back what was taken. Bury me under the archive floor if you must, but the books go to someone who will read them." He wiped her cheek with a thumb, an old man's gesture, unhurried. "That is my entire estate. Paper and truth. See it distributed."

"I promise," she whispered.

"Good." He nodded, and felt something in his chest settle—not the stone, which slept on indifferent, but something of his own, some knot he had carried since the ninth of Frostwane twenty years ago, loosening at last. "Then we have work to do. The rite requires the cathedral of roots, and the false god knows we're coming, and I suspect Warden Corvane knows more than she has admitted. Tell me everything about the third stone's resonance. I should like to know my tenant before I evict it."

She laughed through her tears—a broken, grateful sound—and drew her chair close to his, and they worked late into the night, two scholars preparing a funeral and a wedding in the same breath.

Near midnight, alone again, Anselm knelt at his bedside and prayed for the first time in twenty years without asking for anything. He simply spoke into his own chest, into the dark and the silence and the sleeping shard of a broken chorus, the way he had spoken ten thousand times before:

*I am here. I am willing. When you are ready, I will sing.*

And deep beneath the ruined cathedral, something vast and hungry turned its attention toward the sound of a faithful heart, and began, for the first time, to be afraid.
