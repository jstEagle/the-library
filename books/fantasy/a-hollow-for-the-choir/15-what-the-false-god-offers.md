## 15. What the False God Offers

The dream began the way her mother's kitchen used to smell: bread rising, woodsmoke, rain on a slate roof that no longer existed anywhere in Vellamarch.

Petra stood in a house she had not entered in twenty years. Not the tenement where she'd grown up after—the first house, the one before the arrests, before the name Whitlock became something whispered and spat. Sunlight came through windows that should have been broken. The table had its scarred leg. The kettle was on.

Her mother stood at the counter with flour to her elbows, humming.

Petra knew it was a trap. She knew it the way you know a floor is rotten even when the boards look sound—some animal part of her registering the wrongness beneath the warmth. She had slept with the second choir-stone wrapped in wool against her sternum for six nights now, and every night the dreams had come closer to this: more detailed, more patient, more *kind*.

"You're dead," Petra said aloud. Her voice sounded young in the old kitchen. "You've been dead since I was nine."

Maren Whitlock turned, wiping her hands on her apron, and smiled the smile from before everything—the one that had no guilt folded into its corners yet. "Am I? That doesn't seem right. I'm holding bread."

*It's wearing her,* Petra told herself. *It wore the god's names. It can wear anything.*

But her feet crossed the floor anyway. Twenty years of discipline, twenty years of keeping herself small and quiet and unremarkable, and all of it dissolved at the sight of flour on her mother's forearms like it was nothing, like it was Tuesday.

"You look tired, little wren," Maren said. "Sit down. It's almost ready."

"I'm not hungry."

"You never were, when you were frightened." Her mother set the loaf on the sill to cool and looked at her with those gray eyes Petra saw in her own reflection every morning. "Frightened, and pretending to be clever instead. You get that from me, I'm afraid."

* * *

She woke once, briefly, in the safehouse above the tannery, heart slamming, hand pressed flat over the stone's faint warmth through her shirt. The dog—Bracken, though he'd only let her call him that recently—lifted his head from the doorway and regarded her with one amber eye.

*Dreaming again,* he said. His voice in her mind was dry leaves and river stones. *The wrong-smelling one.*

"Yes."

*Wake me next time. I'll bite it.*

"It's a dream. What will you bite?"

*Whatever is there.* He resettled his chin on his paws. *Things that wear faces you love are still things. Bite them early.*

She almost laughed. Almost. Then she lay back down and stared at the rafters until dawn, and hated how much of her wanted to go back under.

Because here was the thing she could not say to Bracken, or to Anselm with his careful notes, or to anyone: the dream had been *good*. Not seductive in the way the cistern whispers had been, all honeyed menace. Good the way ordinary life was good. Bread. Rain. Her mother humming off-key. The false god had stopped trying to frighten or flatter her and had simply begun offering her the thing she'd been starving for since she was nine years old, wrapped in the most innocent packaging imaginable.

And some traitorous chamber of her heart kept whispering: *what if stopping wouldn't be so terrible?*

* * *

The second night, the kitchen had a garden beyond the window.

"You found the second stone," her mother said, shelling peas into a bowl. The sound was exactly right. The small wet clicks of them. "I felt it wake. I'm proud of you. I always was, even when I couldn't say so."

"Don't." Petra stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, gripping her own elbows hard enough to bruise. "Don't do that. Don't be proud of me. You cut the covenant. You stole the first stone. Anselm has the ledger. Your name is on every line, Mother. Every line."

"I know." Maren didn't flinch. That was the cruelest part—she didn't flinch, didn't deny, just went on shelling peas as if confession were a household chore. "I've had twenty years in the dark to know it. Longer, really, depending on how you count."

"How would you count?"

Her mother looked up, and there was something ancient behind the gray eyes now, something using her mother's face like a mask held slightly away from the skin. "However you need me to," it said gently. "That's rather the point."

Petra turned to leave—and the door was gone. Just wall, warm plaster, a hook where her coat used to hang.

"Stay," the thing said, in her mother's voice, and then, softer, in its own—a voice like a choir heard through a closed door, many mouths moving as one. "Stay and listen. Not to temptations. To terms. You've earned terms by now, haven't you? You've taken two of my siblings' stones out of the dark. You've bled for it. A negotiation deserves better than nightmares."

"What are you?" Petra asked, because she had never actually asked, and asking seemed suddenly important. "Anselm thinks you're the severed half. The part they cut away when they took the first stone. Is that true?"

The kitchen flickered—candlelight guttering—and for half a heartbeat Petra saw what stood underneath: a vast hollow shape, a cathedral of absence, filled edge to edge with faces. Faces she recognized from the market, from the prayer-lines outside the shrine of Saint Ovelia, from the healing queues. People who had prayed and been answered. People whose eyes had gone smooth and quiet afterward.

Then it was her mother's kitchen again, and her mother's face, kind and tired.

"Does it matter?" it asked. "What I was before? You want to know what I am *now*. I am every prayer this city has made in twenty years with nowhere to go. I am the echo that learned to answer back. And I am, whatever your priest says, half of something holy. Half of a covenant is still a covenant, Petra. Ask yourself why the true gods have said nothing while I have answered thousands."

* * *

On the third night, it stopped being the kitchen.

It was the market—her market, her stall, but washed in evening light that made everything gentle. The charms hung in their rows: knotwork against headache, braided thread for safe travel, painted river-stones for luck. All harmless. All lies, in their way, because the real charm was Petra herself, standing behind them looking dull and trustworthy while the actual magic stayed locked behind her teeth.

Except in the dream, the stall was busy. Bustling. Customers three deep, laughing, and among them—beasts. A fox sitting polite as a clerk at the end of the counter. Crows lined along the awning. Bracken beside her stool, and no one staring, no one reaching for a Warden's whistle, because in this dream there was no law against her gift. There was no fear at all.

"You could have this," said a woman at the front of the queue, and when Petra looked up it was her mother's face on a stranger's body, and then it wasn't a stranger's body at all, it was simply Maren, alive, buying a luck-charm she didn't need. "Not through victory. Through stopping. That's my offer, little wren, and I'll make it only once, so attend."

Petra's hands had gone cold. "I'm listening," she said, hating herself for it.

"Stop." The word landed soft as snowfall. "Stop gathering the stones. Stop helping Anselm. Stop planning whatever desperate rite you're planning in whatever hole you're planning it. Give me the two you carry—or don't, simply bury them, keep them from me forever, I only ask that they never sing again. And in exchange—"

"In exchange I keep my head. The Wardens never find me. I live out my life selling knots and painted stones."

"No." Her mother set the luck-charm down on the counter and covered Petra's hand with her own, and the touch was warm, was flour-soft, was *exactly right*, and Petra's eyes burned without her permission. "In exchange, you keep everything you're afraid of losing. The Wardens have a description of a witch in the market—I can make that description die. Corvane's suspicions, her threads, her little file with your name in it—I can unravel it. One answered prayer. She'll wake tomorrow certain she was chasing smoke."

"And my mother's crimes? The ledger? The Wardens would—"

"—find nothing, because I will show them nothing. Or better: I'll show them a different thief. Dead men make excellent conspirators. No one will ever connect the name Whitlock to the Hollowing again. You will be nobody's daughter and nobody's target. Anonymous. Safe. Small, the way you've made yourself. Only—" the smile tilted, tender, terrible, "—you won't have to pretend anymore. Keep the gift. Speak with the beasts all you like, quietly, in your rooms, on your walks. I'll ward you myself. No hunter will ever smell it on you. You can have the voices *and* the hiding place. Isn't that what you've wanted since you were nine years old? Both halves of your life, whole?"

The market hummed around them. Somewhere a kettle whistled. Bracken leaned warm against her leg.

"And the price," Petra said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything shaking in her chest. "There's always a price. Your healings hollow people. Your blessings eat memories. What does this one cost?"

"Others," her mother said simply. "Not you. Never you. But the last true god dies unheard—dies having been unheard for twenty years already, poor starved thing, so what does it truly lose? And the beasts..." She paused, and for a moment the mask slipped and something vast and sad looked out. "The beasts stay yours alone. Their voices never return to them. They remain what they have always been under your covenant-less sky: creatures who think and grieve and plan and cannot say so. You will hear them. They will never hear each other. Is that so high a price? They've paid it for twenty years without knowing there was an alternative."

"That's not—you make it sound like nothing."

"I make it sound like the world as it is," her mother said. "The world you have survived in beautifully for twenty years. I am not offering to break anything, Petra. I am offering to let you stop breaking yourself."

* * *

She woke weeping, which was the worst part.

Not weeping from grief or terror—from *relief*, some deep animal relief, the way a hunted thing feels when it imagines the hunt ending. She lay in the dark above the tannery with tears running into her ears and Bracken's warm bulk pressed against her side where he had evidently climbed onto the pallet sometime in the night, and she thought: *I almost said yes. Last night, in the dream, my mouth opened, and I almost said yes.*

*What did you answer?* Bracken asked. He was learning to speak into her silence now, unprompted, and she wondered distantly whether that was the stone's doing or his own stubbornness.

"Nothing. I woke up."

*Coward,* he said, without heat. *Or wisdom. With two-legs it is often both.*

She laughed, wet and cracked. "Whose side are you on?"

*The side of the pack that is still a pack.* He rested his chin on her ribs, heavy as a blessing. *The hollow one offered me a dream too. Did you know that? Before you came to the cistern. It offered me the pack I lost. Every scent, every voice, every winter den. All I had to do was lead you astray by one street, one night, one wrong turn.* A long exhale through his nose. *I bit it.*

"You bit a dream."

*I bit the thing wearing the dream. There is a difference.* He lifted his head and looked at her, amber eye catching the lamplight from the yard below. *Here is what I know, speaker, and I know it the way you know rain: the hollow one cannot offer what it does not have. It showed you your mother. Where did it get her face? From your memory. From your hunger. It has never held her. If it had your mother—truly had her, soul or shade or memory beyond death—it would not need to bargain. It would simply open its hand and show you.*

Petra stared at him. "You think it's bluffing."

*I think a thing that must bargain is a thing that fears. And I think—* he hesitated, choosing words, which he rarely did, *—I think you were not the only one it visited. The rats say the mad ones in the walls have gone quiet lately. Quiet, and listening. Whatever it wants from you, it wants badly enough to spend its congregation's dreams on getting it. Ask yourself what a starving thing fears most.*

"The rite," she said slowly. "The covenant remade. It doesn't just want me to stop. It needs me to stop."

*There,* Bracken said, satisfied, laying his head back down. *Now you are thinking like something with a nose.*

* * *

But knowing the trap was a trap did not spring her from it. That was the horror of the following days—that understanding changed nothing about the weight on her chest.

She went through the motions. Met Anselm in the scriptorium's ruined undercroft, where he spread his documentation across a broken altar like a physician's charts: forty-one hollowed congregants now, each one catalogued, each one a little less present behind the eyes. Discussed the third stone. Watched his hands tremble when the conversation circled too near what the third stone might require of its vessel, and said nothing, and carried that silence like a stone of her own.

All the while, the offer sat in her like swallowed glass.

Because it hadn't lied, exactly. That was what she couldn't purge. Go through the offer line by line and there was no lie in it—only weighting, only emphasis, only the careful omission of everything the beasts would lose. Her mother's face had been stolen from memory, yes. But the comfort had been real. The warmth of that hand had been drawn from somewhere true inside Petra herself, and the false god had merely held up the mirror, and the mirror had shown her exactly what she wanted, and she had *wanted* it, and wanting it had felt like coming home.

On the fifth night she dreamed the kitchen again, and this time she sat down at the table, and her mother put bread and butter in front of her, and Petra ate it, and it tasted like every good day of her childhood distilled, and neither of them spoke, and it was the kindest cruelty anyone had ever done her.

"I miss you," Petra said finally, to the woman who was not her mother. "That's the truth of it. Not the ledger-you, not the conspirator. I miss the woman who hummed off-key. Whoever she was before she decided the world needed saving from its gods. I miss her so much I'd take her secondhand, from a thing that eats prayers, if it meant hearing that humming again."

"I know," the thing said gently, in her mother's voice. "That's why I chose it."

"And if I said yes. If I brought you the stones and walked away. Would you give me this? This kitchen? This exact evening, over and over?"

The false god was silent for a long moment. When it answered, it dropped her mother's voice entirely, and spoke in its own—the chorus-through-a-door, the many-mouthed sound, and somehow that honesty was worse than the disguise.

"I would give you this kitchen until you forgot it was a gift. Until it was simply your life. That is what I gave the healed, at first—they forget too. That is what I am, Petra Whitlock. I am forgetting, offered warmly. I am the mercy that ends in emptiness. And I am asking you, daughter of my thieves, to choose it with your eyes open, because you of everyone in this city deserve to choose with your eyes open."

"Stop that," Petra whispered. "Stop making sense."

"I was made from your gods' sense," it said. "Half of it, anyway."

She woke with the taste of bread still in her mouth and the stone burning cold against her sternum, and she lay there in the gray pre-dawn and admitted it to the rafters, to Bracken, to whatever true god might still be listening through twenty years of silence:

"I almost said yes. Tonight I might say yes. I don't know how much longer I can carry this."

Bracken said nothing. Outside, far off toward the cathedral ruins, something began to sing—one thin, wavering note, like a bell underwater, like a throat that had nearly forgotten how. It rose, held, and broke off.

And in the silence after it, every rat in the walls of the tannery went still at once, and Bracken raised his head, hackles lifting, and said:

*Speaker. It knows where we sleep.*
