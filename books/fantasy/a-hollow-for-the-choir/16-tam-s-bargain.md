## 16. Tam's Bargain

The knife was called Mercy, and Tam Voss had bought it three years ago from a widow in the Salt Rows who swore it had never been drawn in anger. He kept it wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard with his mother's ring and the deed to a house that had burned down before he was born. He thought about it now the way a man thinks about a rope when he is not yet ready to hang himself: as an option. A last resort. Something he could still put down.

He sat in the back room of his own establishment—a fence's den disguised as a dealer in salvaged brass—and counted the ways he might die this week. It was a habit he'd developed since selling Ilsa Corvane a description of the witch in the market, since watching her walk out of his shop with his words folded into her coat like a blade. Counting deaths was calming, in its way. Most of them never came to collect.

This one, though. This one felt punctual.

* * *

They had come to him two nights ago, the ones who smelled of candle smoke and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft. Three of them, hooded, polite in the way of people who have already decided how you'll be useful. They'd found him through the same thread everyone found him through: he knew things, and he sold them, and lately what he knew was that a woman in the market district moved beasts with her voice.

"We only want to help her," the tall one had said, and Tam had looked into the hood and seen nothing—no face, no shadow where a face should be, just a smooth dark like the inside of a bell—and he had understood, with the clarity that comes to small men at the worst moments of their lives, that he was talking to something that wore people the way he wore his dead father's coat.

He'd given them a name anyway. Not Petra's. He'd given them the name of a herbwife in Tanner's Lane who deserved it about as much as anyone deserved anything, which was to say not at all, and he'd hated himself with a thoroughness that surprised him, because he'd thought himself long past the capacity for fresh self-hatred. Apparently there were always new rooms in that house.

Now they'd come back. The tall one stood in his doorway at dusk, and behind it the street lamps of Vellamarch guttered in a wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else.

"You told us Tanner's Lane," the thing said. Its voice was a chorus pretending to be one throat. "There was no one there."

"Shops move," Tam said. His hands were steady on the counter. He'd practiced. "People move faster."

"The witch," said the thing, "is real. We can smell her on you. Old debt." It tilted its head, and though there was nothing inside the hood, Tam felt it looking at him the way a fish feels the hook looking at the worm. "You will tell us where she will be. Or we will take your knowing out of you piece by piece, and keep the pieces."

And here was the thing—the terrible, clarifying thing—that Tam would carry to whatever grave he got: some part of him wanted to say yes. Not from fear, or not only. From relief. He had spent twenty years being a man who survived by bending, and here at last was a force that did not want him bent but broken, and there was a filthy restfulness in that. No more choices. Just the pieces.

Then he thought of Petra Whitlock at fourteen, sitting on the roof of his mother's tenement, teaching him to whistle so badly that the pigeons left out of pity. He thought of her face when she'd come to him last month and lied to him gently, kindly, the way you lie to someone you've decided to protect, and how he had taken her kindness and sold a version of her to the Wardens within the week.

Two betrayals. He'd been keeping score without realizing it, and now the ledger balanced against him on both sides, and there was exactly one way left to make the columns meet.

"I know where she'll be," Tam said.

* * *

He spent the next day preparing the lie the way he'd once prepared his best merchandise: lovingly, obsessively, turning it over until every seam held.

The truth, as far as he could stitch it together from a fence's worth of overheard fragments, was this: the witch, the priest, and the Warden were going under the old cathedral. Everyone in the deep market who traded in whispers knew the ruins above were watched, and anyone smart went below. But below was a maze of cisterns and plague-crypts and foundations older than the city's name, and the cult didn't need the truth. The cult needed a place, a time, and enough plausibility to spend lives on.

So Tam built them the Salt Chapel.

It was real, the Salt Chapel—a collapsed granary chapel in the Floodrows, half-swallowed by the old reservoir, reachable only through a smuggler's tunnel that Tam alone still had the key to. It had everything a liar could want: remoteness, atmosphere, a single defensible entrance. If the cult went there, they would find stone and water and silence, and they would wait, and while they waited beneath the ruined cathedral across the city, three people would be doing something Tam didn't understand and didn't need to. That was the whole architecture of the bargain: his life purchased against their hours.

He salted it well. He let slip to a cutpurse who owed him that the witch had been asking after the Floodrows tunnels. He paid a beggar-child to be seen near the smuggler's door at dawn, looking frightened, which cost him more than it should have—not coin, but the look on the child's face, which he suspected he'd be seeing again in better light someday. He wrote a note in his own hand, because the cult trusted handwriting the way dogs trusted scent, and burned two drafts before he could make the third one shaky enough to be honest.

*False god,* he thought, sealing it. *You answer prayers wrong. Here's mine answered right: may you choke on my lie.*

* * *

They came for it at midnight, all three hoods, filling his back room like smoke fills a chimney flue—from the bottom up, without effort.

"The Floodrows," Tam said. He laid out the key, the note, the map he'd drawn in ink thinned with water so it would look hurried. "There's a chapel under the reservoir. She's gathering things for a rite. Stones—I don't know what kind, white, singing, the priest carries one inside him if you believe the talk. Three nights from now, moonset. After that she goes to ground somewhere even I can't find."

The tall one took the map. The hood turned toward him, and Tam felt that bell-dark attention pass over him like a hand checking a horse's teeth.

"You are afraid," it said. Not accusing. Almost pleased. "Good. Fear is honest. The others lie to us and their hearts hammer anyway, but you—you lie with your whole body, little fence. It is why we chose you." A pause, and then, softly, in a voice that was suddenly, horribly familiar—suddenly, horribly *hers*: "Your mother prayed to us once, you know. At the end. We heard her."

Tam's breath went out of him. His mother had died raving of voices in the walls, twenty years ago, in the year the gods went quiet, and he had buried her grief-drunk and unshriven and had never once, in all the years since, wondered who—if anyone—had been listening.

"She asked us to keep you safe," the thing said, wearing her voice like a borrowed shawl. "We are offering to honor her prayer. Stand aside entirely. Forget the witch. Live."

Every prayer gets answered wrong, Anselm's notes said—that was the rumor moving through the market, that blessings took memories as payment, that healings hollowed the healed. And Tam understood, standing in his own back room with his mother's voice in a hooded mouth, that this was the payment being offered to him: not his memories, but his one chance to stop being the man his mother had begged the dark to protect.

"No," Tam said.

The hood tilted.

"I'll sell you the place and the hour," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle and he let it, because a lie with no cracks was a lie nobody bought, "and that's the whole of the goods. You want me gone afterward? Fine. Gone. But I'm not forgetting her, and I'm not forgetting the witch, and if you're going to wear my mother's voice to buy me, you should know it doesn't fit. She never once in her life said my name that gently."

Silence. The lamp flame leaned sideways though the air was still.

Then the tall one laughed—or something passed through the shape of laughter, the way wind passes through a dead tree—and set a purse of coin on the counter that Tam neither touched nor refused, because refusing was also a kind of information.

"Three nights," the thing said. "Moonset. If she is not there, little fence, we will come back for the pieces."

The hoods emptied out into the night, and the street lamps steadied, and Tam Voss sat down on his own floor among his salvaged brass and shook until dawn.

* * *

On the second day he did three things.

First, he went to the shrine of the Quiet Saint in the Tanner's Lane wall—a niche no bigger than a bread oven, where the old god's name had been chiseled off during the Hollowing and replaced with a blank, smooth stone. He had nothing to pray with and less to pray to, but he pressed his palm against the blankness and said, out loud, like a fool, like a son: "She was good to me. Whatever else. She was good to me." Whether the silence heard him, he couldn't say. But the silence, at least, didn't answer wrong.

Second, he wrote two letters. One went to the Warden's garrison, unsigned, naming the Salt Chapel and warning of armed cultists, because if his lie failed he wanted the Wardens bleeding the cult in the Floodrows instead of hunting a witch in the market. The other letter he addressed to P. Whitlock, care of the charm stall, and it said only: *Salt Chapel is a trap I built. Don't be in it. Don't trust any message that sounds like me being brave. —T.* He paid a runner triple rate and watched the boy disappear into the market crowd, and only then let himself consider the flaw in his design: that the cult read letters too, that hooded things could intercept runners, that his whole beautiful lie might arrive at Petra's stall wearing his own handwriting like a mask.

There was nothing to be done about it. That was the nature of bargains made by small men: you built the bridge as best you could and then you walked onto it and stopped looking down.

Third, he opened the floorboard and took out the knife called Mercy, and unwrapped it, and held it a long time. He was not a fighting man. He had never been a fighting man; he was a man who knew which doors had loose hinges and which guards drank on shift. But if they came for the pieces—if the lie failed and the hoods came back—he intended to make them work for it, and to do it somewhere far from the market district, far from her stall, so that whatever they found of him afterward would point nowhere but the Floodrows.

He wrapped the knife back up. He did not put it under the floorboard. He put it in his coat, where a decision lives once it has been made.

* * *

The third night came down over Vellamarch like a lid.

Tam walked to the Floodrows through streets gone strange with answered prayers—a baker weeping over bread that had risen into the shape of a face, a woman scrubbing at a healed scar like it was filth, children chanting a new rhyme about the second sun under the cathedral. The city was filling up with the blessed and the emptied, and everywhere the bells hung silent in their towers because no one dared ring them anymore, unsure what might answer.

At the mouth of the smuggler's tunnel he paused, one hand on the wet stone, and allowed himself the inventory of a lifetime.

He was forty-one years old. He had never married, never owned land, never done a single thing that would outlast him except debts and rumors. He had informed on a witch to save his own skin and informed on a cult to save his soul, and both times for reasons that would have made his mother laugh and then cry, and then laugh again, harder. He was a small man. He had made peace with that long ago, or told himself he had. But peace, he understood now, standing at the threshold of the trap he had built with his own careful hands, was not the same as mattering—and tonight, for once, the two had gotten tangled together, and he could no longer tell which one he was walking toward.

He lit the lantern the cult had given him—so they could watch, so they'd know he was where he promised to be—and stepped down into the dark.

Behind him, far across the city, faint as a memory, something began to sing beneath the ruined cathedral. And in the tunnel ahead, in the black throat of the Floodrows, three hoods detached themselves from the shadows where they had been waiting all along, and the tallest one said, in a voice like a bell with no clapper:

"You came. Good. Now show us where *she* really is."

The lantern flame stood perfectly still. Tam's hand found the knife through his coat, and he smiled the smile of a man whose last merchandise had just walked into the room, and said:

"Right this way."
