## 17. The Cathedral of Roots

The rain had stopped an hour before midnight, and the city smelled of wet ash.

Petra came down through the Rookeries with the dog at her heel and the second choir-stone burning cold against her sternum where she'd bound it beneath her coat. It did not warm. She had expected it to warm — in the cistern it had sung against her skin like a struck bell — but here, this close to the ruin, it only grew colder, the way a hand grows cold not from death but from gripping something too long.

"You don't have to come," she said to the dog for the fourth time.

The dog looked up at her with its one clouded eye and said nothing, which was answer enough. Since the beasts had gone strange — restless, half-mad, some simply *gone silent* like snuffed candles — his voice had thinned to a thread. But he walked beside her anyway, and when the streets narrowed into the old pilgrim lanes that no one swept anymore, he pressed close against her leg, and she understood that he was afraid, and that he was coming regardless.

That, more than anything, made her afraid too.

The ruined cathedral rose out of the ground like something that had never been built at all but merely grown — a ribcage of black stone half-swallowed by roots. When the gods fell silent twenty years ago, the Wardens had burned the interior, toppled the spire, salted the foundations, and declared the site condemned. The city had obeyed in the way cities do: by looking elsewhere. In two decades the roots had done what fire could not finish. They had come up through the crypts and the broken nave, thick as ship's masts, braided around pillars, shouldering apart flagstones, until the whole ruin wore a crown of living wood that no axe had been permitted to touch.

Petra stopped at the edge of the pilgrims' square and looked up at it.

"It's bigger than I remembered," Anselm said behind her.

She hadn't heard him approach. The old priest moved quietly these days, quieter than a man his size had any right to; since she'd told him what lived inside him, he seemed to be practicing, deliberately, for the day he would be nothing but quiet. He stood with his satchel of records strapped across his chest like a bandolier, rain-dark, gray-faced, his breath fogging in the cold.

"Memories shrink things," Petra said.

"No." He shook his head slowly. "It's grown. Look at the roots — they're all leaning inward. Toward the undercroft. Everything in this square is leaning toward it, like grass toward a lamp." He paused. "I recorded rites here as a young man. Before the Hollowing. There were seven entrances to the undercroft then. I count four now."

"The roots ate the rest."

"The roots *are* the rest," Anselm said softly. "Come. Before we lose our nerve, or whatever remains of it."

* * *

They went down through the western entrance, the one the rats had shown her in fragments — *down-down-down, where the second sun burns* — a stairwell of cracked granite swallowed halfway by root-wood so old it had gone smooth as polished bone. Petra went first with a shuttered lantern, the dog pressed to her ankle, Anselm's breathing ragged behind her, and the second stone against her chest growing colder with every step until it ached like a tooth.

At the bottom, the stair opened into the undercroft, and Petra lifted the lantern shutter, and forgot how to breathe.

The chamber beneath the cathedral was vast — a hollowed seed-vault of the old faith, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor a mosaic worn nearly blank. And the roots had come down here too. They filled the room the way veins fill a body: descending from the ceiling in great pale columns, spreading along the walls, crossing the floor in ridges you'd have to climb over like fallen timber. They were white. That was the wrong thing, the first wrong thing. Roots are brown, are earth-colored, are humble. These were white as candle-tallow, faintly luminous, and they pulsed — slowly, gently, in a rhythm Petra recognized from her mother's ledger diagrams before her mind refused to recognize it.

A heartbeat's rhythm.

"Oh," Anselm whispered. "Oh, it's been *growing* it. All this time. Twenty years of answered prayers—" His voice caught. "Every blessing fed it. Every healing. We thought it was drinking faith. It was planting itself."

The dog growled, low and continuous, a sound like a millstone turning.

And then the faces began.

They came out of the dark at the chamber's far end, and Petra's first mad thought was that they were ghosts, because they moved without walking — drifting, swaying, upright, in a slow congregation that gathered beneath the largest root-column like moths at a door. Then the lantern light reached them and she saw they were people. Living people. Dozens of them. Men and women in nightclothes and work clothes, barefoot on the frozen mosaic, their eyes open and fixed on nothing, their faces smooth with a peace so complete it looked flayed.

She knew some of them.

"That's the cooper's wife," she breathed. "From Tanner's Row. Her son fell from the roof in autumn — broken back, fevered — and then one morning he was walking, and everyone said it was a miracle, and she—"

"She gave thanks," Anselm finished. His voice had gone very flat, the way voices go when feeling would otherwise destroy them. "Look at her hands, Petra."

Petra looked. The woman's hands hung open at her sides, and they were empty — not of objects, but of *her*. Petra had spent twenty years reading people the way she read animals, in the small honest language of the body, and the cooper's wife's body was saying nothing at all. No tension in the jaw. No fear in the shoulders. No grief anywhere, though her son had nearly died and her god had eaten the payment from somewhere. She was a house with the furniture removed.

"They're not dead," Anselm said. "That's the horror of it. The healings worked. The prayers were answered. But the price wasn't taken all at once — it was taken in installments. A memory here. A want there. A name. And what's left—" he gestured at the drifting congregation "—is a vessel with nothing left to hold. Empty enough to be *worn*."

"Worn by what?"

As if in answer, the heartbeat in the roots quickened.

* * *

Ilsa Corvane came down the eastern stair with her sword drawn and her warrant unsigned, which was, she reflected with the cold clarity that had carried her through eleven years of service, the most honest document she'd ever carried.

She had left the Warden garrison at the ninth bell with six sworn hunters and orders from Provost Marshal Dune himself: *secure the condemned cathedral, find the witch, burn whatever is found.* Six hunters. She had sent four of them to seal the surface entrances and told them, in words that would cost her everything if ever repeated, to wait for her signal and to come down after no signal at all. Two she had dismissed entirely. What remained below the cathedral was not a crime scene. She had understood that the moment she'd seen the first root — white, pulsing, *breathing* — and felt her own oath turn to lead in her mouth.

Her boots found the mosaic floor. Her lantern found the congregation.

She had seen the emptied before — the Hollowed, the street called them, the ones who gave coin to the whispering priests of the Answering and came home lighter. She had filed reports on forty-one of them. She had believed, in the way duty requires belief, that they were frauds' victims or fanatics. Standing among them now, watching a tanner drift past her shoulder close enough to touch, his chest rising and falling in time with the roots, Ilsa understood that she had spent eleven years sharpening herself into a weapon pointed at the wrong enemy.

One of the drifted figures turned its head toward her lantern.

It was a girl of perhaps fifteen, and for one instant — one flickering, unbearable instant — her empty face *focused*, and Ilsa saw what was underneath the peace: terror. Vast, drowned, screaming terror, like a face pressed against the underside of ice. Then the peace closed over it again, smooth as water, and the girl turned away, and Ilsa Corvane, who had not prayed in nineteen years, heard herself whisper the opening words of a rite she barely remembered.

Somewhere across the chamber, a lantern answered hers. Three lights now, converging: the witch, the disgraced priest, and a dog whose growl she could hear even at thirty paces.

Ilsa lowered her sword — actually lowered it, point to the floor, a gesture that would have ended her career if anyone from the garrison saw it — and walked toward them through the congregation of the hollowed.

* * *

"I brought your hunter," Anselm murmured, as Ilsa's light joined theirs. "Or she brought me. I've honestly lost track of who is arresting whom."

"Neither of you is under arrest," Ilsa said. Her voice was steady. Her sword-hand was not. "Officially, none of us is here."

"That's the most religious thing anyone's said to me in twenty years," Petra said.

For a moment — absurdly, horribly — the three of them almost smiled. Then the roots pulsed, hard, once, and the smile died in all three throats together.

"We need the stones placed before dawn," Anselm said, unshouldering his satchel with hands that trembled only a little. "The rite requires a voice for each realm. Human—" he touched his own chest, where the third stone slept inside him like a swallowed sun. "Beast." He nodded to the dog. "Divine." He nodded, last and longest, at the far end of the chamber, where the largest root-column descended from the unseen ceiling into a knot of pale wood the size of a chapel altar.

And set into that knot, cradled in living tallow-white grain, was a hollow.

A choir-stone-shaped hollow. Waiting. Empty.

"The first stone," Petra whispered. "It doesn't have it. It's been *waiting* for it."

"It can't sing alone," Anselm said. "That's what my records say — that's what the covenant *was*. Not one voice. A chord. Whatever severed the chorus twenty years ago became something that can only ever hum. It has spent two decades building itself a congregation, a body of roots, a throat—" his hand rose, unwilling, toward the drifting people "—but it cannot make the chord. It needs the first stone. It needs *us* to bring it."

"Then why does the place feel like a trap?" Ilsa asked.

No one answered, because the answer was obvious, and because saying it aloud would make it true: a trap does not need to be clever when the prey must walk into it willingly.

Petra stepped forward. The dog pressed harder against her leg — a weight, a warmth, a small stubborn *no* — and she knelt and put her hand on his head, and for a moment, through the thinning thread of his voice, she felt what he felt: the chamber's pulse in his bones, the hollow's hunger like an open mouth, and beneath it all, faint as a star through smoke, something else. Something old. Something *true*, buried deep beneath the false heartbeat, so faint she might have imagined it.

A voice. Very far down. Very tired.

Not singing. Listening.

"It's still here," Petra breathed. "Underneath. The true god — it's not gone, it's *buried*. The roots grew over it. Around it. Like scar tissue over a wound." She looked up at Anselm, and her eyes were wet and furious. "We're not walking into its throat. We're walking into a sickroom. The thing wearing the gods' names isn't a conqueror — it's the severed half, the part that got cut off and left in the dark, and it has been *alone* down here for twenty years, filling itself with borrowed faces because it couldn't bear the silence—"

"Petra." Anselm's voice was gentle and terrible. "Understanding it will not save us. Pity might even feed it. My records are clear on one point above all others: the covenant must be remade *whole*. Both halves. Sung back together or not at all." He drew a slow breath. "Which means when we stand at that hollow and begin the rite, we will not be killing a monster. We will be performing surgery on a god, in its own body, while it begs."

The congregation stirred.

All of them at once — dozens of hollowed faces turning together with a sound like wind through wheat — and the cooper's wife began to speak, in a voice that was hers and yet arranged by something else, pitched sweet and reasonable and terribly, terribly tired:

"*You came.*"

The roots brightened. The heartbeat quickened. And in the drifting crowd, here and there, faces flickered — the flash of drowned terror beneath the ice — as the thing that wore the old gods' names looked out through every emptied eye at once, and smiled.

"*Bring me my other half,*" it said, in her mother's voice, "*and I will give you back everyone you have ever lost.*"

Behind its voice, deep beneath the roots, something true and buried and exhausted began — very softly, very faintly — to sing.

The rite could begin. Or the trap could spring. At the threshold of the hollow, with the first stone heavy in her satchel and dawn less than three hours away, Petra Whitlock took one step forward, and the roots closed overhead like a hand.
