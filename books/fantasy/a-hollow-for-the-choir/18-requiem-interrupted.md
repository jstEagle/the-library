## 18. Requiem, Interrupted

The stairs beneath the ruined cathedral had not been built by human hands.

Petra knew this the way she knew rain before it fell—through her skin, through the low constant murmur of stone that only beasts and beast-speakers could hear. The steps spiraled down past roots that had grown *inward*, thick as a man's waist, pale as bone where they crossed the walls. They were warm. Everything down here was warm, and that was the worst part. She had expected cold from the thing wearing her mother's voice. Instead it offered warmth the way a fever offers it: without permission, without end.

Behind her, Anselm's breathing came in short, careful pulls. He carried the third choir-stone inside his own chest, and she could hear it now—had been able to hear it since the crypt—a thin silver note beneath his heartbeat, like a finger held against a wet glass rim. He had not spoken much on the walk down. What was there to say? *I am a vessel built to die when the covenant is remade* was not a sentence you repeated for comfort.

Ilsa came last, torch in one hand, sword loose in its scabbard. Above them, muffled by forty feet of earth and root, Vellamarch was tearing itself apart.

They had heard it begin at dusk. The Wardens had come to the cathedral ruins in force—Ilsa's own former colleagues, three hundred strong, because Tam's false information had placed the rite there and the cult had believed it, and the Wardens had believed the cult believed it, and so every desperate soul in the city who wanted a miracle had come too, drawn like moths to a lie that glowed. Petra had stood at the mouth of the true stair—the hidden one, the one the rats remembered—and listened to the crowd above singing hymns to something that ate memories, while the Wardens formed ranks with censure-rods and fire-pots, and understood that whatever happened below, above them the city would burn a little tonight.

"Stop here," Ilsa said.

The stair opened into the Cathedral of Roots.

It was not a cathedral anymore. It was what a cathedral became when it was swallowed and digested and dreamed about. The vaulted ceiling remained, but the roots had taken it, braiding through the ribs of stone until architecture and tree were one body. Where the altar had been, there was a hollow—a wound-shaped absence in the floor, wide as a well, ringed by two shallow basins of dark water. The first choir-stone sat in the left basin, humming. The second sat in the right. Between them, the hollow waited for the third.

And around the hollow, kneeling in concentric rings, were the emptied ones.

Dozens of them. Men and women in the gray robes of the Answered, faces smooth and placid as eggs, eyes open and reflecting nothing. Petra counted forty, fifty, more in the dark beyond the torchlight. Each one had prayed to the false god and been blessed, and each blessing had taken something—a memory, a name, a grief, a love—and what remained knelt here now, arranged like candles, like kindling.

"They're the chorus," Anselm whispered. His voice cracked on the word. "The entity doesn't just wear their faces. It *sings* with them. Every voice it empties becomes a note it can use."

"Then we're late," Ilsa said flatly. "There are already enough notes."

"No." Petra stepped forward, and the warmth pressed against her like a tide. "Not enough. It needs all three realms. That's why it hasn't finished. Human voices—" she gestured at the kneeling gray shapes— "it has those. But the covenant was sung by three. A voice for each realm. Human. Beast. Divine."

"And the divine voice," Anselm said quietly, "is the half of itself it cut away. Us."

The false god did not announce itself. It simply arrived, the way understanding arrives—not entering the space but becoming the space. The warmth deepened. The roots overhead shivered, shedding a fine pale dust that drifted down like snow that had forgotten which direction snow falls. And out of the hollow rose a figure made of assembled light and absence, tall as two men, wearing—

Wearing everyone.

Faces moved across its surface the way reflections move across water. A baker woman Petra had sold charms to. A child. A Warden she didn't recognize. And then, settling over the whole of it like a mask finding its fit, her mother's face—younger than Petra remembered, unlined, gentle, terrible.

*You brought them,* it said, and the voice was every voice at once, layered, harmonized, wrong. *My singer. My priest. My little law.*

"We brought the stones," Petra said. Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. Fear was honest; she'd spent twenty years being honest with beasts and lying to people, and tonight she intended the reverse. "That's not the same as bringing them *to you*."

*Everything comes to me eventually.* The face smiled with her mother's mouth. *Even obedience. Even defiance—it arrives, and I wear it.* The great head tilted toward Anselm. *But you know this. You've known since the crypt. Come closer, vessel. Let me show you what you carry.*

"Don't listen," Petra said sharply.

"I have to hear it," Anselm said. "To refuse a thing properly, you must understand what it's offering." He limped forward, one hand pressed to his chest, and his voice took on the cadence of the scholar he had been before disgrace, before the archives, before any of it. "State your offer plainly, severed one. For the record."

*For the record,* the god echoed, amused. *Very well.*

Above them, distantly, the first screams began.

* * *

Ilsa Corvane had arrested two hundred people in her career, and she had never once heard anything like the sound coming up through the floor.

She stood at the edge of the root-cathedral with her back to a pillar, torch raised, cataloguing exits the way she catalogued everything—because cataloguing was how she kept the doubt quiet. Three exits: the stair they'd come down, a crawl-space behind the eastern roots where air moved, and the hollow itself, which was not an exit but felt like one, the way an open grave feels like one.

The screaming above sharpened. Then came a sound she knew intimately: the crack of censure-rods discharging, dozens at once, followed by the deeper roar of fire-pots. The Wardens were firing into a crowd of worshippers. Her Wardens. People she had trained.

*Hold the line,* she thought at them, uselessly, across forty feet of earth. *They're civilians. They're frightened civilians. Hold.*

But the cultists among the crowd would not be holding. Tam's misinformation had put the rite here, and the cult had come armed with relics and certainty, and certainty plus fear plus fire made arithmetic Ilsa had seen before in border towns. There would be dead above. Many dead. And none of it—not one drop of it—would matter if the thing below finished its work.

*Ilsa Corvane.* The god turned its assembled face toward her, and for one vertiginous moment it wore the face of Warden-Commander Doss, her old instructor, dead six years. *You doubt. I can taste it. Doubt is just faith pointed the wrong way. I could point it back for you. I could give you back your certainty—wouldn't that be a mercy?*

"That's not mercy," Ilsa said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "That's theft with extra steps."

*All ownership is theft. All law is theft with paperwork.* The god's attention slid off her like oil off glass, dismissing her, returning to the priest. *Anselm. Let us conclude our negotiation.*

Ilsa looked at Petra. Petra looked back, and between them passed the whole unsaid ledger of their acquaintance—the arrest she hadn't made, the beasts she'd seen, the truth neither of them had said aloud: that they were allies now, and neither had agreed to it, and both would die before admitting it mattered.

Then Anselm said, "No."

* * *

"No," Anselm said again, louder, and the word rang strangely in the root-vaulted dark, as though the cathedral itself had been waiting twenty years for someone to say it here.

He stood at the rim of the hollow, and the third stone burned under his ribs like a swallowed star. He had spent his whole life believing he was a man who had failed—failed the church, failed the rites, failed the gods whose silence he had documented page after patient page. Now he knew he had never been a man at all, not entirely. He was scaffolding. He was a lock built around a treasure, and the locksmiths were traitors, and one of them had sung him lullabies.

But a lock, he reflected, still got to choose what it locked.

"You want the stone," he said to the god. "You cannot take it. Your conspirators saw to that—it's bound to my life, and my life is bound to my will, and my will is the one thing in this world you cannot bless or empty, because I have already given it away." He spread his arms. "I gave it to the record. To the truth. Take my memories if you like—they're written down. Kill me if you must—the pages survive. You are negotiating with a footnote, severed one, and footnotes do not bargain."

For the first time, the god's harmony faltered. A single note went flat somewhere in the vast chord of it, and forty kneeling bodies flinched in unison.

*Then I will take the song another way,* it said.

The hollow began to sing.

It was not music. It was the memory of music, played backward, a requiem unwriting itself note by note. The two choir-stones in their basins flared—one gold, one blue—and answered, and the sound they made was so beautiful that Petra's knees nearly buckled, because it was the sound of the world before the silence, the sound her mother must have heard every day of her childhood, the sound of a covenant holding.

And then the false god reached into Anselm.

Petra saw it happen. Saw the god's light pour into the old priest like water into sand—and saw what it took. Not blood. Not breath. It took *him*, from the edges inward. She watched his eyes go briefly, horribly blank—the look of a man standing in a house where all the furniture has vanished—and then return, but changed.

"My name," Anselm said. He frowned. "Brother... Brother..."

"Anselm," Petra said, crossing to him fast. "Your name is Anselm. You recorded the covenant rites. You kept the archives. You are the bravest scholar I have ever known."

"Anselm." He clung to it. "Yes. Yes, that's—" His face cleared, then clouded again. "There was a garden. Why is there a garden? Who planted a garden in my head?"

"It's taking his memories," Ilsa said, sword out now, though against *that* a sword was a joke told badly. "From the oldest first. It's eating him backward."

"The rite," Petra breathed. "Oh, gods. Oh, no. The rite isn't just a song. It's a *record*. The covenant remade requires the original words—all three verses—and the third verse lives in *him*. If it unmakes his memories before we sing—"

"—there is no third verse," Ilsa finished. "No divine voice. No covenant."

Above them, the ceiling shook. Dust and pale root-fiber rained down. Through the cracks in the stone, firelight flickered, and with it came voices—hundreds of them, screaming hymns and curses and each other's names—and beneath it all, faintly, impossibly, a new sound.

Barking. Howling. The scream of hawks and the low bellows of oxen and the chittering fury of ten thousand rats.

The beasts of Vellamarch had arrived at the battle.

* * *

Petra felt them before she understood them.

It came up through her feet, through the warm stone, a pressure like a second heartbeat: the animals at the walls above, driven mad by the false god's hunger, meeting the Warden lines, and dying, and pressing forward anyway. She had spent her whole life speaking *for* them, translating their small angers and large griefs into human words. Now their terror poured into her untranslated, vast and undiluted, and she staggered under it.

*Petra.* The dog's voice—her dog, the stray, the first friend of her hidden life—cut through the flood like a fin through water. *We came. We shouldn't have. The song-madness pulled us. The Wardens are killing us, Petra, they're killing everything with fur and feathers, and we can't stop running toward the noise—*

"Hold," she whispered. "Hold, all of you. Please."

*We can't. It sings and we run toward it. That's what it built the noise for.*

The false god turned its mother-face toward her, and smiled, and Petra understood with sick clarity that this, too, was part of the design. The beasts massing above weren't chaos. They were the second realm, arriving exactly where the god needed them—drawn by the rite's resonance, corralled by the Wardens' blades, dying in numbers that would leave the beast-realm voiceless anyway. Win or lose, the god fed.

Unless.

*The rite needs three voices,* it had said. *Human. Beast. Divine.* It had assumed it would take them. It had assumed the beast-voice would be hers, seized, worn like everything else it wore.

It had not considered that she might give them freely.

"Anselm." She gripped his shoulders. His eyes were fogging again; she could see decades draining out of them like silt from a broken jar. "Listen to me. The third verse. Give it to me *now*, while you still have it. Sing it into me. I'll hold it."

"I don't—" He blinked. "Who are you?"

"Petra Whitlock. Your friend. The daughter of the woman who betrayed you both." Her voice broke and she let it. "Please. Before the garden goes."

Something surfaced in his drowning eyes—recognition, or its ghost. His lips began to move. A melody, thin and wavering, older than the city, older than the silence. The third verse. The divine voice, the god-half's own lament, the song the severed half had been singing to itself in the dark for twenty years without knowing it.

Petra opened herself to hold it.

And that was when everything unraveled at once.

The ceiling burst. Not collapsed—*burst*, roots and stone exploding outward as the battle above punched through, and down into the Cathedral of Roots tumbled fire and rubble and the first of the beasts, a hawk with a broken wing trailing blood, followed by a cascade of rats, followed by light—torchlight, censelight, the orange bloom of a fire-pot detonating against the far wall. Wardens rappelled down through the breach on ropes, censure-rods blazing. Cultists poured in behind them through the western crawl-space, gray robes flying, relics raised. The kneeling emptied ones did not move, did not flinch, even as the war arrived in their laps.

Ilsa shouted something—her own name, her rank, an order that no one living had the authority to obey—and was lost in the smoke.

The false god rose to its full height, and its harmony swelled to fill the shattered cathedral, and every fighting human below froze mid-swing, transfixed, as it sang the first bars of a stolen requiem.

Petra stood in the center of it—in the smoke and the falling fire and the flood of beast-terror and the fading echo of Anselm's verse—with two stones humming in their basins, a third verse burning in her chest, a priest dissolving beside her, a hunter shouting orders to no one, and every animal in Vellamarch screaming her name.

The god reached for her.

And Petra Whitlock, who had spent twenty years making herself small, decided to stop.

She did not speak *for* the beasts.

She opened the door, and stepped aside, and let them all in.

* * *

What happened next, no one below ever managed to describe the same way twice—but that is the next chapter's burden, not this one's.
