## 19. Every Voice at Once

The Cathedral of Roots did not look like a ruin from below. It looked like a throat.

Petra stood on ground that had once been the cathedral's nave, now buried under forty feet of collapsed stone and two decades of root-growth, and looked up at a vault of living wood so dense it had become its own sky. The roots of the old oaks that ringed the ruins above had grown down through the broken roof like fingers closing over something precious—or something stolen. Pale light filtered through them, greenish, underwater. It fell in long columns across the floor, and where it touched, the old mosaic tiles still showed: a circle of figures, human and beast and something else, all facing inward toward a center that had been pried out of the floor. A hollow, exactly the size of a choir-stone.

Three stones sat in her satchel against her hip. They were warm. They had been warm since the cistern, since the crypt, since Anselm had pressed his hand to his own chest and said, *I have carried this longer than I have carried my faith.*

"Petra." Anselm's voice came from behind her, thin as paper. "It's starting."

She turned. He was kneeling at the edge of the hollow, arranging his records—the covenant rites he'd spent twenty years transcribing from memory, from fragments, from the margins of books the Wardens hadn't burned fast enough to catch. His hands shook. Not with fear. She had seen him afraid in the plague-crypt, in the flooded dark, and this was not that. This was the shaking of a man whose mind was being read by something vast.

"It's taking the words," he said. "As I remember them. It's—Petra, it's eating them out of me."

Above them, muffled through forty feet of stone and root, the city was screaming.

* * *

They had come down through the ossuary passage Tam's information had bought them—the false route, the one Tam had sold to the cult knowing they would flood it behind their own vanguard. It had worked. Half the cultists who should have been here were sealed in a dead-end tunnel under the tannery quarter, and the other half were fighting the Wardens on the surface, because Ilsa Corvane had done the last thing anyone expected of her: she had marched the Third Vigil to the cathedral ruins and declared it under Warden protection, in open defiance of an order that had come down from the Convocation itself.

Petra had heard the first clash begin as they descended. Steel on steel, and worse—the wet, wrong sound of cultists who no longer felt pain, who had traded their exhaustion and their grief for blessings that hollowed them. She had heard Ilsa's voice cutting through it, level as a surveyor's line: *Hold the perimeter. Nothing comes down.*

Now the sounds above had changed. Slower. Fewer. And underneath them, rising through the roots like sap, a hum.

The false god was singing.

It didn't use a voice. That was the horror of it. It used theirs—every person it had ever blessed, every emptied vessel walking the streets of Vellamarch, all of them humming the same note at once, wherever they stood, whatever they were doing. Petra had heard it growing for three days, a drone beneath the city like a struck bell that refused to fade. Here, at the center of it, the note had a shape. It was almost the covenant. Almost the chorus Anselm had reconstructed from ash and memory. But where the true rite braided three voices together—human, beast, divine—this song had only one voice, repeated endlessly, a choir made of a single throat cut open and stretched across a city.

"It isn't trying to stop us," Anselm whispered. He had gone very pale. His fingers hovered over his pages without touching them. "It's waiting for us to start. Don't you see? If we sing the covenant here, in the hollow where it was severed, the rite will need somewhere to put the severed half. It wants to be the fourth voice. It has wanted that for twenty years."

"Then we don't sing," Petra said.

"We must sing." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet, and calm, and terrible. "Every hour it grows. Every blessing it gives takes more of the world into it. There is no version of tomorrow in which we do not sing, Petra. There is only the question of who holds the fourth voice when the song ends."

The hum deepened. In the columns of green light, dust began to move—not falling, but drifting upward, drawn toward the vault of roots. Toward the thing wearing the old gods' names that lived in the wood and the hollow and the half-remembered prayers of ten thousand desperate people.

And then, from the darkness of the ossuary tunnel behind them, the beasts arrived.

* * *

She had called them. That was the sin she would carry, whatever happened next—she had opened herself on the walk down, in the dark, and sent out the only prayer she had ever meant: *Come. Please. I cannot do this alone.* She had not commanded. Command was the old way, the way of speakers standing apart from the herd, translating, deciding. She had simply asked, the way you ask anything that might refuse you.

They came anyway.

Rats first, pouring along the tunnel walls in a gray river, thousands of them, and among them the cats who had hunted them for generations and did not hunt them now. Dogs from every yard and alley in Vellamarch, the strays and the chained and the beloved, some still dragging broken leads. Crows in their dozens filling the tunnel mouth like a black weather. The mare from the livery who had once told Petra about the taste of rain coming. A blind mole that had never seen the sun and came anyway. And at the front, limping, grizzled, one ear torn, the dog. Her dog. The stray she had spoken with each evening for six years, who had led her to the cistern and the crypt and every stone, and who now sat down at the edge of the green light and looked at her with eyes that held no worship at all—only recognition, only company.

*You asked,* the dog said. *So we came. That is what voices are for.*

Anselm had gone still. Twenty years studying the covenant, and he had never heard it plain, never heard a beast speak to a human as an equal, and Petra watched him understand in real time what the world had lost when the Hollowing cut the chorus—that this was not magic. This was conversation. This was the ordinary miracle the world had been built on.

"The rite needs three voices," Anselm said hoarsely. "Human. Beast. Divine. I am the third stone—I am the divine voice, or I hold it, or—" his hand pressed his chest again, "—it holds me. You are the human voice, Petra. Your mother's bloodline took that burden when they took the first stone. But the beast voice—" He stopped. Looked at the animals crowding the tunnel mouth. Looked at her. "There is no beast-speaker left. The gift died with the covenant. No one can speak *for* them."

"No," Petra said.

Her heart was very loud. Louder than the hum. Because she had known this moment was coming since the hawk died in her hands above the cistern, since the dying bird had shown her the shape of the choice, and she had been pretending she hadn't.

"But there is a speaker *among* them," she said. "If she stops being anything else."

* * *

The false god understood before Anselm did.

The hum broke. For one instant the cathedral of roots went silent as a held breath, and in that silence Petra felt the entity turn its whole attention upon her—a weight like an ocean noticing a single swimmer. It spoke then, finally, directly, in the voice it had worn in her dreams, the voice that was almost her mother's:

*You will unmake yourself. You know this. Open fully and there will be no Petra left to close. You will be a door, and doors do not get to choose what passes through them, little thief's daughter. I can give you back your smallness. Your stall. Your evenings with your dog. I can give you her—*

"Don't," Petra said aloud, to it, to herself. "Don't wear her. Not here."

*I wore her name for twenty years and no one corrected me,* the thing said, and there was something in it that was not malice. Something older and sadder. *Someone cut me out of the song and left me with nothing but names to steal. Do you know what that is, child? To be half a covenant? To be the verse that was removed and left humming in the dark?*

And Petra, to her lasting astonishment, pitied it.

That was the trap, maybe. Or the truth. Anselm's records said the conspirators had severed the chorus to save the world from the gods' demands—and the severed half had not died. It had waited. Hungry. Answering prayers wrongly because wrongness was the only grammar it had left. It was not an invader. It was an amputation that had learned to walk.

"You're not going to win," she told it. "But you might be going home."

She knelt in the hollow beside Anselm. She set the three stones around them in the mosaic circle, in the sockets the old tiles still marked, and the stones sang—one clear tone, one low, one like a bell heard through water—and the hum of the false god rose to meet them, discordant, yearning.

"Anselm," she said. "When I open, I won't be able to hold the human voice. Someone has to sing it while I'm—elsewhere."

"I know." He smiled. It was the first whole smile she had ever seen on him. "Twenty years of transcription, Petra. I know every verse by heart. It's the only thing in my head it hasn't managed to eat, because I keep giving it away faster than it can take." He laid his hand flat on his pages. "My records survive me. That was the bargain. Sing."

Above them, distantly, Ilsa Corvane was holding a line of Wardens against a tide of the blessed, buying minutes with law and steel, and Petra sent one thought up through the roots like a thrown rope—*hold, just hold*—and did not wait to learn if it landed.

She opened.

* * *

There is no honest way to describe surrender of self except badly, so: it was like setting down a heavy bag she had forgotten she was carrying, and discovering the bag had been her spine.

Petra Whitlock came apart like a knot coming loose. Her name went first. Then her fear, which had been her closest companion for twenty years and turned out to weigh almost nothing after all. Then the careful smallness, the shopfront, the charm-seller's hunched shoulders—all the architecture of hiding, dissolving, because a door does not hide; a door stands open.

And through her, everything poured.

The rats came through first, ten thousand small hearts, and their voice was the voice of walls and sewers and the underside of every city, patient and unsentimental: *We remember the second sun. We remember the taste of the old prayers in the mortar. We were here before the gods were named and we will be here after.* The crows came through carrying the memory of every gallows in Vellamarch, sharp-eyed, unforgiving: *We saw the executions. We saw the burnings. We do not forget, and we do not forgive, but we are here.* The horses, the oxen, the gulls on the river, the fish in the cistern where she had nearly drowned, the moles in their blind country, the cats with their ancient indifference, the dogs—

The dogs came through last, and their voice was the largest and the simplest and it broke her open entirely, because it was: *We stayed. When the gods went silent and the humans stopped hearing us, we stayed beside you anyway. We slept at your feet through twenty years of silence. We never left.*

Not one of them spoke *for* themselves. That was the forbidden thing, the thing the old speakers had never dared: Petra did not translate, did not channel, did not stand between. She simply ceased to be a wall. Every voice passed through her at its own pitch, its own grief, its own joy, and the human voice—Anselm's voice, cracked and steady, singing the covenant verses from memory as the false god gnawed at the edges of him—took them all and braided them.

Three voices became a chord. The chord became a chorus. The chorus rose through the roots of the Cathedral of Roots and out into the night air of Vellamarch, and the city heard it.

Later, people would say the bells rang by themselves. People would say the river ran backward for a minute. People would say their dead spoke to them once, kindly, and were gone. All of it was true and none of it mattered, because what mattered was what happened in the hollow:

The true god answered.

It did not descend. It did not appear. It had never been anywhere else—it had been the silence itself, the listening, the space between notes that makes music possible, and it had been dying unheard for twenty years, and now it was heard. Petra felt it arrive the way you feel dawn: not as an event but as a change in the quality of everything. Warmth without temperature. Presence without shape. The fourth socket in the mosaic lit with a light that was not light, and the covenant reached for its missing half—

And the false god screamed.

*No. No, you will sing me out, you will dissolve me, you will end me—*

"We were never going to destroy you," Anselm sang, tears streaming, blood beginning to seep from his nose, his ears, the price of holding a divine voice in a mortal throat. "Read my records. Read them. The covenant was never a wall against the gods—it was a *harmony*. You are not the enemy. You are the verse they cut. Come home."

*It hurts—*

"Yes," said every beast at once, through Petra, with terrible gentleness. "Coming home hurts. We know. Come anyway."

The false god—the severed half, the hungry amputation, the thing that had worn her mother's voice because it was the only voice it had ever been given—hesitated at the threshold of its own wholeness. And in that hesitation Petra, spread thin as light across a thousand throats, felt one last flicker of her self, her small saved self, reach out—not with power, not with command, but with the oldest gesture there is.

She forgave it. Her mother too. Both thieves, both mourners, both wrong.

The severed half came home.

The sound it made when the covenant closed around it was not a scream. It was a chord resolving—the ugliest, most yearning dissonance of twenty years sliding at last into consonance, and the whole buried cathedral shuddered, and the roots overhead bloomed. Out of season, out of reason, white flowers burst from forty feet of living wood and sifted down through the green light like snow falling upward reversed, and the mosaic blazed, and the stones sang one final note that was not an ending but a *turning*, a key change, the world modulating into a new key that contained, at last, all of its voices.

Petra felt Anselm let go.

He did it gently, in the last bar of the song, the way a singer releases a final note—his mortal throat giving out the divine voice it had carried for thirty years, his heart finishing its measure and resting. She felt him go the way you feel a hand slip from yours in a crowd: suddenly, irrevocably, and with a strange rightness, as if he had simply walked through a door into the part of the song written for him. His last breath was not a word. It was a hum, on pitch.

The flowers kept falling. The chorus faded, voice by voice, each beast withdrawing through her like guests leaving through a doorway, pausing, some of them, to touch her on the way out—the mare's velvet nose, a crow's feather-brush, the rat-river slowing to a trickle of small warm goodbyes.

The dog stayed.

The dog lay down beside Anselm's body and put its grizzled head on the old man's silent chest, and stayed.

And Petra Whitlock came back into herself—or into what was left, which was a woman kneeling in a hollow full of light and petals, empty in a way she had never been, unable to hear a single voice but her own.

She reached, out of habit, out of twenty years of instinct, for the dog's mind.

Silence. Vast, ordinary, animal silence. The dog looked at her with brown eyes that held no words at all.

She had done it. She had sung the covenant whole, and the price was exactly what she had known it would be, and she found, kneeling in the blooming dark with a dead man and a mute dog, that she could not yet tell whether she had lost everything or finally, at last, been given something.

Above her, through the flowering roots, the sounds of battle had stopped.

Footsteps were coming down the ossuary stairs. Heavy boots, measured pace, the tread of someone who had held a line all night and was now descending into a place no law had ever imagined.

A lantern-light swayed on the tunnel wall. A voice she knew called down into the flower-fall, rough with smoke and disbelief:

"Whitlock. Whatever you've done down here—you'd better pray I'm the first one who finds it."
