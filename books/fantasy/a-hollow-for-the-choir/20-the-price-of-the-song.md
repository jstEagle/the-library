## 20. The Price of the Song

The singing did not stop so much as settle, the way a great bell's note sinks into stone and stays there long after the bronze has gone still. Petra knelt on the root-woven floor of the cathedral beneath Vellamarch and listened to it fade through her bones, and for a long while she could not tell whether she was weeping or whether the weeping belonged to someone else—some voice that had passed through her and left its grief behind like water in a channel.

The false god—the severed half, the hollow twin, whatever name the scholars would one day give it—was gone. Not destroyed. *Sung whole.* She had felt it happen: the moment when the chorus she carried met the thing wearing ten thousand emptied faces, and instead of recoiling, opened wider. The faces had softened. The hollowed congregation above, the cultists and the blessed and the broken, had drawn breath all at once, and some of them were still breathing now, sitting in the ruins overhead, blinking at the dawn light with expressions like people waking from a fever they'd mistaken for faith.

And Anselm—

Petra turned.

He lay where the rite had set him, at the center of the knot of roots, his hands folded over the ledger he had begged her to preserve. His face was peaceful in a way it had never been in life. He had spent twenty years writing down what everyone else wanted forgotten, hunched over candle stubs in a disgraced priest's cell, and now the record-keeping was done and the man who had been scaffolding around a choir-stone was simply—a man again. Empty in the gentlest sense. The stone that had lived behind his sternum sat in her palm, warm as a sleeping bird, humming its last low note before it sank into the new covenant and became, at last, only itself.

"You asked me to make sure your records survived you," she said to him. Her voice came out cracked, half of it still resonant with voices not her own. "I will. Every page. Even the ones where your handwriting goes crooked because you were crying. *Especially* those."

She closed his eyes with two fingers. It seemed important that someone do it by hand, without magic, without song. Just a woman and an old dead scholar and the ordinary decency of touch.

* * *

Coming up out of the cathedral of roots was like being born slowly into a world that had changed shape overnight.

The ruined cathedral proper stood open to the sky, its shattered nave filled with morning. Wardens and cultists lay down their weapons among the rubble with no memory of why they had raised them. Some wept. Some laughed, horribly, helplessly, the laughter of people whose memories had been paid out as tithes to a god that no longer needed them. A woman in fine robes sat cradling a boot, turning it over and over, mouthing a name she could no longer attach to a face.

Ilsa Corvane found Petra at the top of the stairs.

The Warden looked worse than Petra felt, which was a sentence Petra would never have believed possible three days ago. Ilsa's coat was scorched along one side, her braid half-collapsed, and there was a cut above her eye that she had clearly decided was beneath her attention. In her hand, held loosely, almost embarrassedly, was her writ of office—the black-and-silver seal of the Inquisition of Unlicensed Arts.

"They'll want my badge by noon," Ilsa said. No greeting. No accusation. "A Warden who stood witness to the rite and did nothing. A Warden who let a confessed witch walk. A Warden who—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "They showed me things, Whitlock. Down there. The Hollowing. Your mother. What they took from the world to save it, or thought they were saving." She looked at the seal in her hand the way one looks at a knife that has cut oneself. "Twenty years I enforced a law built on a theft. I can't un-enforce it. But I can't keep doing it either."

"I'm sorry," Petra said, and meant it, which surprised her. This woman had hunted her, had arrested Tam, had come within a signature of the pyre—and had also, in the dark under the city, chosen truth over law at the cost of everything she was.

"Don't be." Isla tucked the seal away, and something in her posture hardened back into the shape Petra knew—not the hunter now, but something older and wearier underneath it. "Grief is cheap. Duty is expensive. I've paid both." She glanced past Petra, into the depths of the stairwell, and her voice dropped. "The priest?"

"Didn't make it."

Ilsa nodded once, slow. "His records. Are they safe?"

"Every page."

"Then he outlived the men who used him." She said it like a verdict, like a sentence handed down in Petra's favor, and then she walked away across the rubble, straight-backed, toward a future with no uniform in it, and Petra watched her go and understood that this too was part of the price—not death but dismantling, the careful taking-apart of everything a person had believed held them up.

* * *

The days after were strange in their ordinariness.

Vellamarch did not know yet what had happened beneath its streets. It knew only that the answered prayers had stopped—that the healing shrines went quiet, that the desperate who had traded memories for miracles woke with their losses intact and their gains unchanged, that the thing wearing the old gods' names no longer wore anything at all. Rumor moved through the market like weather. The Wardens' cordon around the cathedral district thinned and then dissolved, because there was nothing left to cordon off except rubble and a handful of hollowed survivors learning, gently, with hired helpers, how to live inside their own smaller lives.

Petra reopened her stall on the fourth day.

She told herself it was practicality. Rent did not stop for revelations. But her hands shook as she laid out the charms—honest ones now, or as honest as charms could be, bits of copper and ribbon and dried lavender that promised exactly nothing—and every time a customer paused at her table she braced for the word *witch*, for the Warden's hand on her shoulder, for the pyre that had been waiting for her since childhood.

It didn't come. Word traveled slower than rumor, apparently, or perhaps Ilsa's resignation had taken the sharpest questions with it. People bought love-knots and headache sachets and left. The world, indifferent, continued.

What did not continue was the voices.

She noticed it first on the fifth evening, walking home along the canal. For twenty years the city had murmured to her—rats reporting grain stores, gulls complaining about fish offal, horses remarking on cruel drivers, sparrows conducting their tiny politics in the eaves. It had been noise, mostly, a tide she'd learned to sleep inside. Now the canal banks were silent. The pigeons in the square regarded her with flat black eyes and said nothing at all.

Because the gift was gone. That was the price, and she had known it going in—had sung the covenant knowing the last verse required her to give back what she had borrowed. Every voice she had ever channeled, every beast-word she had ever spoken *for* another creature, returned to its owner. The beasts of Vellamarch were whole now, complete, sovereign in their own tongues. And she was just a woman standing near them.

She had expected loneliness. What she hadn't expected was the awe.

Because they were *choosing*. She saw it in small ways, in the weeks that followed. The stray dog that had been her evening companion for years—the grey-muzzled bitch with the torn ear, the first creature Petra had ever spoken with—came to the stall less often, and when she came, she came differently. Not drawn by the secret thread of speech. Drawn the way any dog comes to any person: by smell, by habit, by whatever inscrutable calculus dogs perform. Sometimes she stayed. Sometimes she didn't. Once she brought a rat—actually escorted it, trotting solemnly with the fat brown rat trotting beside her—and the two of them sat in front of Petra's table for a full minute, regarding her, before departing together down the alley like a delegation concluding an inspection.

"They're deciding," Tam said, when she told him about it. He was thinner than he'd been before the arrest, and jumpier, and he flinched at loud footsteps now, but he was alive, and he was trying, and she had forgiven him fully even though he couldn't quite believe it yet. "That's the thing, isn't it? Before, they talked to you because you had the—whatever it was. The key. Now they don't need a key, so if they come, it's because they *want* to." He shrugged, uncomfortable with sincerity, retreating into a grin. "Course, I'd want something in return too. You ever see me visit anyone for free?"

"You visited my mother's grave every year for a decade," Petra said quietly. "You told me you didn't remember why."

Tam's grin died. He looked at his hands. "Yeah," he said. "Well." And neither of them said anything else about it, because some debts are paid in silence.

* * *

She buried Anselm on a grey morning in the potter's field beyond the north wall, because he had asked for no ceremony and gotten, in life, far less than he deserved.

Tam dug. Petra read from the ledger—the passage where Anselm, decades younger, had first recorded the covenant rites in a hand so careful it looked printed, each word placed like a stone in a wall. She read until her voice failed, and then she put the book back in its oilcloth and laid it in the grave beside him, because he had asked that his records survive him, and she had realized, somewhere between the cathedral and the digging, that the surest way for words to survive was to be buried with the man who wrote them, in ground that would hold them longer than any archive, any regime, any law.

"He was the third stone," she said to the small gathering—Tam, herself, and, unexpectedly, a dozen crows in the bare tree overhead, watching with bright attention. "He never knew. They built him around it. And when it mattered, he carried it anyway." Her throat closed. She finished anyway. "I hope wherever he is, someone is finally listening to him."

The crows lifted off the tree all at once, a black exhalation against the grey sky, and circled the grave three times—three, precisely, deliberately—and flew west.

Petra stood very still.

It might have been wind. It might have been nothing. Twenty years of speaking for beasts had taught her every pattern of their movement, and this had not been a pattern of wind. This had been *intent*, shaped and offered, the way one offers a bow.

Or it might have been grief, seeing what it wanted to see.

She walked home with the question lodged in her chest like a splinter, and she did not try to pull it out.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and gentler than the almanacs promised, though the almanacs had always been written by people who assumed the gods were silent.

Petra kept the stall through the cold months because keeping it was easier than deciding what else to be. She sold honest nothing to honest strangers. She fed the strays that came by—not speaking to them, just feeding them, the way anyone might. She learned the new grammar of animals: the tilt of a head, the length of a pause, the difference between a dog that passes and a dog that lingers. It was poverty and riches at once. She had spent her whole life fluent in a language, and now she was illiterate in it, reduced to guessing, hoping, misreading—and somehow it was more honest than fluency had ever been. Every kindness a beast showed her now was real coin, minted by choice.

Some evenings she caught herself talking aloud to the empty alley behind the shop. Telling the dark about her day. Feeling foolish. Doing it anyway.

On the last night of the year, snow falling soft and thick, she closed the stall late and found the grey dog waiting by the door.

Not lingering. Waiting. Sitting in the snowfall with the patience of a creature that has made a decision and is prepared to defend it, her torn ear frosted white, her breath steaming. Petra froze with her key half-turned in the lock.

The dog rose. Crossed the snow. Stopped close enough that Petra could feel the warmth radiating off her flank, and looked up—not with the flat animal regard Petra had grown used to these past months, but with something older, something that made the hair rise on her arms, something that reached back across twenty years of shared evenings and stolen conversations and the long silence since.

And then the dog spoke.

Not in the old way—not through the channel Petra had once been, not a voice borrowed and relayed. This voice came from the dog herself, whole and hers, rough as gravel and warm as a hearth, and it said:

"*Petra.*"

Just the name. Nothing else. A name spoken freely, by a free creature, to a woman with no gift left at all.

Petra slid down against the doorframe into the snow, laughing and crying in the same breath, and the dog pressed her broad head against Petra's chest and let her, and somewhere above them the bells of Vellamarch began to ring out the old year—not the false god's answering chime, not the hollow echo of stolen prayers, but plain bronze rung by human hands, calling across the rooftops to anything in the world that cared to listen.

Something did. Far off in the dark, a fox sang back.
