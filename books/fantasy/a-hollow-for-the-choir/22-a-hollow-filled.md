## 22. A Hollow, Filled

The stall reopened on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were market days and the world had decided, without consulting anyone, to keep turning.

Petra arrived before dawn with a handcart and a crate of honest nothing: river stones worn smooth, bundles of dried lavender that smelled only of lavender, copper bells cast by old Ferro down the lane who had never once been accused of sorcery. She set them out in careful rows on the same scarred boards where she had spent twenty years selling charms that were mostly suggestion and small kindness. The difference now was that she did not have to pretend they were anything more.

A woman stopped, turned one of the stones over in her palm, and asked if it was lucky.

"It's a stone," Petra said. "It's very good at being a stone."

The woman bought three.

She should have felt lighter than this. Everyone said she would. The city was still limping through its new arrangement—magic licensed now, tested, stamped into ledgers by clerks who had spent their whole careers burning ledgers—but there was air in the streets that hadn't been there before. People prayed openly again, carefully, the way you'd approach a horse you'd once been kicked by. And the answers came back quiet. Small. *Right.* A fever broke. A lost child wandered home. Nothing hollowed. Nothing taken.

The covenant held. She had sung it herself, or rather she had stood at the center of it while every voice in Vellamarch—feathered, furred, scaled, and human—sang through her, and something vast and patient on the far side of the thin place had answered, and the thing wearing the gods' names had been gathered back into the whole it had always been meant to be. Not destroyed. *Completed.* Her mother's crime undone not with fire but with harmony.

And her gift—

Her gift was gone.

That was the price no one had warned her about, though perhaps someone had and she hadn't listened. In the last note of the requiem, when the chorus swelled past what any single throat could hold, the covenant had reached into her and taken the thread that connected her to the beasts' voices—not as punishment, but as *return*. It had never truly been hers. It had been borrowed, held in trust, passed down through a family of thieves like stolen silverware. Now it belonged to them again. All of them. Every rat and raven and stray dog in Vellamarch could speak, if they chose, to whom they chose.

They had not chosen her.

* * *

Three weeks after the reopening, she still caught herself pausing at dusk behind the stall, waiting for the dog.

He had found her in her first winter in the city—a lean, gray-muzzled thing with one torn ear and an opinion about everything. For twenty years he had come to the alley behind her shop each evening, and for twenty years they had talked: about the weather, about the butcher's stinginess, about the particular quality of loneliness that belongs to creatures who must hide what they are. He had guided her to the flooded cistern when drowning waited below. He had stood beside her at the cathedral of roots when the false god wore a thousand emptied faces. He had never told her his name, because dogs, he said, keep their names the way rivers keep their courses—you can describe them but you cannot own them.

Now he could say whatever he liked, to whomever he liked, and he had not come.

She understood. That was the worst part. She understood completely, the way you understand why a bird leaves an open cage. For twenty years his voice had lived inside her like a prisoner in a walled garden, and she had loved him, and love is not the same as freedom. Somewhere out there he was learning what his voice sounded like in open air, and she was one door among many, and doors that kept you locked are not the ones you run back to.

Still. She paused at dusk. She scratched the flagstone where he used to sit. She left the heel of a meat pie on the wall, and every morning it was gone, which meant nothing and everything, and she refused to decide which.

* * *

Anselm's records survived him. That had been his only request, and Ilsa had seen it honored with the same iron thoroughness she brought to everything—the cartloads of vellum carried from the ruined cathedral to the scriptorium of Saint Verene's, catalogued page by page by scribes who wept as they worked, though most of them had never met him. Petra went once, to look at his handwriting. It was small and exact, the letters of a man who had believed that precision was a form of prayer. On the last completed page, dated the morning of the rite, he had written:

*If I do not finish this entry, let it be known that I was not scaffolding. Scaffolding holds up a building and is discarded. I held up a song, and the song holds me still.*

She had stood in the scriptorium until the light failed, one hand flat on the glass case, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that he had not already said better.

Ilsa came by the stall sometimes. Not often. She had resigned her wardenship in a letter so brief it was almost rude—*I find I can no longer enforce laws I helped make obsolete; consider this my notice*—and now she did something vague and dangerous-sounding for the new Office of Covenants, which everyone pretended was different from the Wardens and no one believed was different, except that its first act had been to burn the execution warrants instead of the witches. When Ilsa visited she bought things she didn't need: a bell, a bundle of lavender, a stone that was very good at being a stone. They drank tea and spoke of weather and neither of them mentioned the cathedral, except once, when Ilsa set down her cup and said, "The beasts showed me what the Hollowing was. Did they show you too?"

"They showed everyone," Petra said. "That was rather the point."

"I spent twenty years hunting people like you," Ilsa said, "because I thought magic was the thing that took my brother's mind, when he was blessed by the false god. It wasn't the magic. It was the theft." She looked at her hands, a hunter's hands with nothing left to hunt. "I don't know how to be sorry enough for that."

"You enforced a law," Petra said. "My mother *wrote* it." She refilled Ilsa's cup. "Between us, I think you're owed more forgiveness than I am."

"That's not how guilt works."

"No," Petra agreed. "It isn't."

Tam's grave was on the hillside above the tanneries, under a rowan tree he would have complained about—"all those berries, do you know what rowan berries do to a man's luck"—and Ilsa visited it too, though Petra didn't know that yet, wouldn't know until much later, when grief made strange companions of them all. Tam had died the way he had lived: badly timed, badly paid, and somehow on the right side after all. The cultists had discovered his false information was false two days after the rite, when they stormed an empty granary in the Salt Quarter and found nothing but Ilsa's waiting arrest warrant nailed to the door like a taunt. They had found Tam four hours later.

He had left her a note, because of course he had. It read: *You always were a terrible liar, Pet. Good thing you're such a good forgiver. Don't waste it on me—I'd only spend it.—T.*

She had laughed and cried in the same breath, which was, she suspected, exactly what he'd intended.

* * *

Spring came late that year and then all at once, the way forgiveness does.

The market changed around her. Beasts walked the lanes openly now—not speaking to humans, not usually, but present in a way they had never permitted themselves before. A magpie ran a gossip exchange from the eaves of the chandlery, trading rumors for scraps of bacon at rates everyone agreed were extortionate. The cathedral cats had formed something between a council and a court. Children learned beast-tongue in school alongside their letters, stumbling through sparrow greetings and the long, rolling courtesies of cattle, and the old people wept to hear it, because some of them remembered, dimly, from before the Hollowing, when the world had been loud this way.

Petra sold her honest nothing and listened. Not with the gift—that was gone, and the silence where it had lived was a shape she was slowly learning to inhabit rather than mourn—but with her ears, like any person. She heard the magpie's prices drop when a widow couldn't pay. She heard the cats deliberate, gravely, on whether a certain dog was well.

"A certain dog," she repeated, one morning, to the tabby who had come to inspect her stall.

The cat looked at her with the magnificent indifference of its kind. "All dogs are certain," it said. "Certainty is the defining feature of dogs." And it stalked off, having said both everything and nothing, as cats do.

She began, after that, to watch the alleys.

* * *

He came on a morning of thin gold light, six weeks after the reopening, when the frost was melting off the awning in bright drops and the market was filling with the smell of bread and wet wool.

She looked up from arranging her stones and he was simply there, sitting at the edge of her stall as if he had never left—as if twenty years of evenings and one great singing had been a single unbroken conversation, merely paused between sentences. Grayer now. Slower in the haunches. One ear still torn. His eyes were the same dark amber, and they held hers with an expression she had spent a lifetime learning to read and was suddenly afraid to misread.

"Hello," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

He did not answer. He watched her lay out the lavender. He watched a customer buy two bells and haggle badly. He watched the light move across the boards. And Petra sat with the silence between them, that familiar old silence, and found—to her surprise—that it did not hurt the way it used to. It was just quiet. Two creatures sharing a morning, needing no words, which had always been the deepest layer of what they had anyway, beneath all the talk.

When the crowd thinned, he stood, stretched until his spine curved, and looked at her again.

"Petra."

One word. Her name. His voice was rougher than she remembered, gravel and smoke and the cold of long streets, and it came from his own throat, chosen, unhurried, belonging to no one but him.

Not called. Not commanded. Not drawn up through the old buried thread of her gift, because that thread was gone, cut free, returned. There was nothing in her reaching for him. There was nothing in him compelled. He had a voice the way she had a voice, and a choice the way she had a choice, and out of all the doors in all the city—all the children who fed sparrows, all the widows who talked to cats, all the open hands of the new loud world—he had walked back to hers.

"Why?" she asked. And then, because it was a bad question, badly shaped: "There are so many voices now. You could talk to anyone."

"I know," he said.

He came closer and laid his graying muzzle on the scarred boards of her stall, next to the river stones and the honest nothing, and closed his eyes, and let the morning happen to both of them.

"The others sent messages, you know," he said, after a while, drowsy in the sun. "The rats remember you. The hawk's grandchildren remember. The cats pretend not to, which is how cats remember." One amber eye opened. "But I didn't come for them. I came because the garden missed its gardener. Because the door was never the prison, Petra. You were the window. Twenty years, you were the only window some of us had, and windows are rare, and I am old, and I would rather spend what's left looking out of yours than any other view in this city."

Petra Whitlock, who had hidden for twenty years, who had drowned in a cistern and sung down a god and given away the only magic she had ever owned, put her hand on the broad gray skull of her oldest friend and wept without shame, in front of the whole market, in front of God and everybody—and nobody looked away, and nobody cared, because half of them were talking to their dogs.

"Stay, then," she said.

"I intend to," he said. "Someone has to supervise the stones."

She laughed, wet and cracked, and moved a stone aside to make room for him, and he settled onto the warm boards of her stall like a creature coming home to exactly the shape of himself.

Above the awning, the magpie called the morning's gossip in a dozen tongues. Down the lane, children stumbled through sparrow-greetings, laughing. Somewhere far off, beneath the roots of the world, the true god hummed its long restored chord, patient as stone, listening.

And in a stall that sold honest nothing, a woman and a dog sat together in the noise of the world—two voices among many, neither above the other, both entirely free—

when the magpie dropped onto the counter, feathers slick with river water, eyes wild, and said:

"Speaker. There's something waking in the deep places. Something the song didn't reach. And it knows your mother's name."
