## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There were three rules to surviving as a king's taster, and Orsen Ashgrove had learned all of them the hard way, which was the only way anyone learned anything in Veylan's court.

Rule one: never let them see you enjoy the food. Enjoyment suggested appetite, and appetite suggested a man with plans for a long life, and men with plans made other men nervous.

Rule two: never be the first to arrive or the last to leave. Invisibility lived in the middle of things, in the comfortable gray of the ordinary.

Rule three—the rule that had kept him breathing through six years and one hundred and forty-one deliberate poisonings—was this: know your own tongue better than you knew your own name.

His tongue was, in its way, the most educated part of him. It could distinguish wolfsbane from hellebore the way a musician distinguished two notes a hair apart. It knew the coppery signature of arsenic dissolved in wine, the almond ghost of cyanide in marzipan, the bitter green lie of foxglove folded into a custard. He had been dosed so methodically, so patiently, by the palace physicians under the late King Aldric's orders—and then, after the coup, under Veylan's—that his body had become something like a ledger. Every entry written in nausea and survived. A ledger of survival, his old instructor had called it, back when Orsen was fourteen and vomiting into a bucket while a bored physician took notes.

"Again," the physician had said. "The body remembers what the mind forgives."

Orsen thought about that every morning, standing in the cold antechamber off the royal kitchens while the cooks pretended he wasn't there. The body remembers what the mind forgives. His mind had forgiven a great deal. Court life required it. You could not stand in a room where a man had usurped a throne and watch servants bow to him with genuine warmth unless you were very good at forgiving—or very good at forgetting.

Orsen was excellent at both.

* * *

The morning began, as mornings did, with bread.

Simple bread, from the palace ovens, torn by the head baker's own hands because everyone knew the first assassination attempt on any monarch would come through the staff, and everyone also knew the staff hated being suspected, and so the bread arrived each day slightly stale out of spite. Orsen chewed it, swallowed, waited the prescribed count of sixty heartbeats, and nodded to the steward.

Then the porridge, sweetened with honey from the royal apiaries. Then smoked river trout. Then a small mountain of dishes carried up from the kitchens on silver trays, each one tasted, each one survived, each one forgotten before the tray reached the door.

It was Tuesday. Orsen knew this because Tuesdays meant quail, and quail meant Master Ferren, the sous-chef, would be in a temper about the larding needles, and there was a certain comfort in the predictability of other people's tempers when your own inner weather had to stay flat as a millpond.

"The fourth course," announced the steward, a reedy man named Pell who pronounced *course* as though it were an accusation.

The fourth course was not quail.

It was a soup—a pale, silvery thing served in a bowl so white it seemed to glow against the dark tablecloth. Orsen frowned at it. Not because frowning was allowed, but because his tongue was already leaning forward inside his mouth like a dog scenting something wrong on the wind.

"What is it?" he asked.

"Velvetroot bisque," said Pell. "A new recipe. His Majesty requested novelty."

Veylan requesting novelty was itself novel. The king ate the way he ruled: heavily, traditionally, and with an eye toward what his grandfather would have approved of. But Orsen had long ago stopped asking questions whose answers wouldn't change anything. He picked up the spoon.

The first taste was unremarkable. Cream, root vegetable, a whisper of thyme. Smooth, faintly sweet, entirely innocent. He held it on his tongue for the full count, let it sit, swallowed.

Nothing.

He tried again. Nothing. No bitterness, no numbness, no heat, no metallic edge. His tongue reported, in its dry professional way, *clean*.

And yet.

There was something else. Something underneath the cream and the thyme that his training had no name for—not a flavor exactly, but a pressure, the way you can feel someone standing behind you without hearing them. It sat at the back of his throat like a word he'd forgotten and couldn't stop trying to remember.

Orsen set down the spoon.

Pell watched him with the flat patience of a man whose entire job was watching other people's mouths. "Well?"

"It's fine," Orsen said.

It was not fine. As the swallow went down, the pressure spread—not pain, nothing so honest as pain—but a kind of opening, as though a door somewhere behind his breastbone had swung inward on well-oiled hinges. And through that door came a voice.

Not aloud. Not in his ears. Somewhere deeper, in the place where his own thoughts lived, a voice that was not his own said:

*—and the crown was heavy, heavier than they tell you, heavier than gold has any right to be—*

Orsen's hand found the table's edge.

*—they tell you it is an honor. They do not tell you it is a hunger. They do not tell you that crowns eat their kings slowly, from the inside, and leave the smiling parts for the portraits—*

"Master Ashgrove." Pell's voice came from very far away, then very close. "You've gone pale."

"I'm fine," Orsen said again, and heard how thin it sounded, and corrected it. "Rich cream. Early stomach. I'll take some air."

He did not take air. He walked, with the unhurried gait of a man with nowhere urgent to be, into the corridor, around two corners, and into the alcove where the palace kept its supply of candles and its single sad potted fern, and there he put both hands on the stone windowsill and breathed until the voice faded.

It took a long time to fade. Longer than any poison he'd ever survived. Poisons had arcs—they rose, crested, broke. This didn't break. It receded the way a tide does, reluctantly, leaving things behind on the sand of his mind: an impression of great halls hung with banners he didn't recognize, a weight on his brow that wasn't there, a grief so old it had fossilized into something like architecture.

When it was gone, Orsen laughed. Quietly, once, the way you laugh when the alternative is screaming.

He had been poisoned one hundred and forty-two times now. And for the first time, the poison hadn't wanted to kill him.

It had wanted to *talk*.

* * *

By afternoon he had constructed three explanations and dismantled all of them.

Explanation one: ergot, or some fungal contaminant in the velvetroot, causing hallucination. Dismantled by the fact that the voice had been coherent, specific, and interested in crowns—hallucinations, in Orsen's limited experience, tended toward shapes and fears, not editorial commentary on monarchy.

Explanation two: he was going mad. This one lingered longest, mostly because it was tidy. Tasters went mad; it was practically a pension plan. Years of swallowing death in small doses would crack anyone. But madness, he suspected, didn't wait politely for you to finish chewing.

Explanation three: the soup had been exactly what it claimed to be, and the voice had come from somewhere else entirely. He dismantled this one fastest, because it required believing in things that did not exist in the Ashen Valley of his childhood, where magic was a rumor from far-off places, like snow, or mercy.

Except.

Except that the voice had known about crowns. Had spoken of them with the intimacy of long suffering. And Orsen, who had spent six years making himself furniture, felt—with a dread that settled into his bones like damp—for the first time in years, *visible*. As though something had turned over in its sleep beneath the floor of the world and noticed him standing on it.

He finished the day's tastings mechanically. Quail, eventually—it had simply been delayed, which explained Ferren's temper. Roasted pears. A cheese that fought back. Each dish clean, each dish silent, each dish merely food. Whatever had ridden the velvetroot bisque had come alone.

At the kitchen door, on his way out, Master Ferren caught his sleeve.

"You looked peaky at the fourth course," the old man said. He was flour-dusted and scowling, which was his version of tenderness. "That bisque. I don't know where the recipe came from. Some peddler sold the root to the provisioner, said it grew wild in the western hills. Velvetroot." He snorted. "Never heard of it in thirty years of cooking. Have you?"

"No," Orsen said truthfully.

"Hm." Ferren squinted at him with eyes red-rimmed from oven smoke. "If it happens again—if anything happens again—you'll tell me? I've fed four kings from these kitchens, boy. I'd rather die than poison a fifth, even that one." He jerked his chin vaguely upward, in the direction of the throne room, and by extension the usurper.

"I'll tell you," Orsen said, and meant it, and knew he wouldn't.

Because what would he say? *Your mystery root whispered to me about the weight of crowns, Master Ferren, in a voice three hundred years out of fashion.* The physicians would have him on a table by nightfall, and the inquisitors would have him by morning, and Inquisitor Corveth did not release people once he'd finished finding out what they were.

Dain Corveth. Even thinking the name made Orsen's shoulders drop half an inch, the way they did when passing under a low beam. Corveth had interviewed him once, two years ago, after a minor incident involving a nervous under-cook and a vial of something in the salt cellar. The interview had lasted four hours and consisted almost entirely of Corveth asking questions he already knew the answers to, watching Orsen answer them anyway, and taking notes in a small, terrible book. At the end, Corveth had smiled—which was worse than not smiling—and said, "You have a remarkable memory, Master Ashgrove. I hope it stays remarkable. Unremarkable, rather. You understand me."

Orsen had understood him perfectly. Understand me: I am watching. Understand me: I forget nothing. Understand me: the safest thing you can be in this palace is boring.

So Orsen walked home through the evening streets of the capital, past the shrine-sellers dousing their candle-stalls for the night, past the walls where Veylan's proclamations fluttered, past the beggars and the guards and the ordinary machinery of a city pretending everything was normal, and he told himself the story he was best at telling:

*Nothing happened. You ate bad soup. You are a gray man with a gray job and a poisoned tongue, and tomorrow you will taste breakfast, and it will be eggs, and the eggs will say nothing.*

He believed it the way you believe a roof will hold: not because you've inspected it lately, but because the alternative is unbearable.

* * *

Sleep did not come easily. Sleep, that night, came like a guest who knocks softly and then lets himself in.

He dreamed of a hall. Not the throne room—he knew the throne room, had memorized its exits the way other men memorized prayers—and yet it was the throne room, older, taller, its windows full of colored light that fell across a floor of black stone. Banners hung from the rafters, deep green and silver, stitched with a tree whose roots and branches met in a circle. On the throne sat a shape made of shadow and gold, and the shape wore the crown, and the crown was—

*hollow*, said the voice, gentle now, almost kind, the way you speak to a child afraid of the dark. *Do you see it now? Do you see what we carried?*

"We," Orsen said, in the dream, in a voice that was his and not his. "Who is *we*?"

The shadow on the throne leaned forward. Where a face should have been there was a doorway, and beyond the doorway, a valley—the Ashen Valley, gray and quiet and safe, magic-proof, memory-proof, the place his mother was buried—and standing in the valley, small and clear as a figure seen through the wrong end of a spyglass, was a woman with his mother's hands, holding out a book.

*You are the last page,* said the voice. *We wrote everything else.*

Orsen woke with dawn graying the window and his heart going like a smith's hammer, and lay still, and listened to the city wake below—the carts, the gulls, the shrine-bells ringing for morning prayer.

One hundred and forty-two poisons, and his body had shrugged off every one. He had always assumed that was the whole of his talent: a ledger of survivals, nothing more.

But the physician's old saying turned over in him now, wearing new clothes. *The body remembers what the mind forgives.*

What else might a body remember? What might six generations of keepers write into the blood of a family from a valley where magic cannot follow—hide it there, the one vault no spell could reach?

He got up. He dressed. He sharpened his routine like a knife and went to work, because work was the shape his courage took.

In the antechamber, Pell handed him the morning's first tray with his usual funereal air. Bread, butter, preserved plums.

Orsen tasted each one. Clean. Silent. Safe.

He was halfway through exhaling when the second tray arrived, and on it, pale as a drowned moon, was velvetroot bisque.

"His Majesty's favorite," said Pell. "He's asked for it daily."

The voice, when it came, sounded pleased.

*Good morning,* it said. *Shall we begin?*
