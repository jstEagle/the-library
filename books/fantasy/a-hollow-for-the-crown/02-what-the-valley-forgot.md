## 2. What the Valley Forgot

The mule was named Turnip, and Orsen had decided within the first hour that he hated her.

She stopped at every stream to drink too long, refused every bridge on principle, and had once, memorably, eaten his mother's remembrance shawl out of the saddlebag while he slept. He'd spent the last mile of the mountain road holding the replacement—borrowed from a weaver in Lowmark who charged him triple because he looked like a man who could afford it, which was the funniest thing anyone had said to him all year.

"You're a thief," he told her.

Turnip flicked one ear, unbothered, and kept walking.

He let her. That was the trouble with him, his aunt always said. He let things walk all over him and then apologized for the mud they tracked in. Nine years as a food-taster in Veylan's palace had not cured it. If anything, the court had refined it into something almost professional: Orsen Ashgrove, the man who swallowed everything without complaint and thanked you for the privilege.

The road bent around the shoulder of Grey Crag, and there it was—the Ashen Valley, laid out below like something forgotten on purpose. Grey fields. Grey stone fences. The river running grey-green through the middle of it all, slow as syrup. Smoke rising straight up from a dozen chimneys because the valley air never moved unless it wanted to.

No color anywhere, except what people brought in with them. That was the joke travelers told, anyway. The truth was stranger: nothing *grew* colorful here. Flowers came up white or pale as bone. Birds were brown. Even the sunset seemed embarrassed, bleeding out in thin grays and pewters before it finished.

Magic cannot follow you here, his mother used to say. She said it the way other mothers said *mind your manners*—a rule so old it had worn smooth.

Orsen reined Turnip down the switchback, and somewhere around the third turn he noticed the silence.

Not the ordinary silence of empty road. A silence *inside* him. He'd been carrying it for two weeks now—that whisper, that thread of something murmuring under his skin since the night of the poisoned quail. It had followed him through the palace corridors, through the streets of the capital, through nine days of hard riding north and west. He'd stopped fighting it somewhere around the fourth day. It wasn't hostile. It was more like a cat that had decided his chest was furniture.

It was gone.

He pulled Turnip to a halt in the middle of the switchback and sat very still, listening to his own blood the way he listened to sauces—one wrong note and you spat, one right note and you swallowed. Nothing. Just his heart, doing its dull honest work, and the wind coming off the crag smelling of stone and cold water.

"Good," he said aloud.

His voice sounded wrong in the open air. Too loud. He hadn't realized how quietly he'd been speaking lately, as if the whisper might overhear.

*Good,* he told himself again, and clicked his tongue at Turnip, and did not believe himself even slightly.

* * *

His aunt Maren heard the mule before she saw it. Orsen knew this because when he came through the gate she was already standing on her doorstep with flour to her elbows, squinting down the lane like a woman expecting bad news and disappointed it was taking so long.

"You're late," she called.

"The mule ate my schedule."

"Mules don't eat schedules."

"This one would if you buttered them."

Maren Ashgrove was sixty-one years old and built like the doorframe she stood in—broad, load-bearing, unimpressed by weather. She crossed the yard in six strides, took his face in both floured hands, and studied him the way she studied bread dough: checking whether it had risen properly or gone sour somewhere in the middle.

Whatever she found, she didn't say. She just pulled him against her shoulder hard enough to rearrange his ribs, held on for exactly as long as dignity allowed, then shoved him back and smacked flour off his coat.

"You look terrible."

"I always look terrible."

"You've gotten *good* at it." She turned to the mule, who had begun investigating the fence post with criminal intent. "And you must be the famous Turnip. My nephew writes me letters about you. Three pages, once. About a mule."

"She exaggerates," Orsen said.

"The mule doesn't write the letters, love."

Inside, the house smelled the way it had smelled his whole childhood: woodsmoke, rosemary, and the particular mineral smell of Ashen Valley water, which everyone swore you stopped noticing after a week and nobody ever actually did. His mother's chair still sat by the hearth. Nobody sat in it. It wasn't sacred, exactly—it was just that sitting in it required a conversation nobody wanted to have, and the Ashgroves had always been better at silence than speech.

"Remembrance is tomorrow at dusk," Maren said, kneading without looking at him. Her hands worked dough the way other people wrung cloth. "Priestess from Lowmark is coming. Old gods rite, plain version. Your mother hated fuss."

"She hated priests too."

"She hated *fussy* priests. There's a difference. Sit down. You're thinner than the last time, which I'm putting on the palace's account, along with everything else."

He sat. He watched her work. And the strange interior silence pressed against him like weather.

"Aunt Maren." He kept his voice light, which was a skill, and aimed it casually, which was an art. "Do you ever get headaches? Up here. In the valley."

"All the time. I have your uncle's ghost for a husband and your mother's ghost for a sister-in-law and you for a nephew. Headaches are structural."

"Not those kind." He turned his cup in its saucer. "A sort of... pressure. Behind the eyes. Like a word you can't remember."

Her hands slowed. Not stopped—Maren never stopped—but slowed, the way a river slows over a submerged rock.

"No," she said. "We don't get that here."

"We?"

"People in the valley." She flipped the dough, thumped it down. "Why do you ask?"

Because for two weeks something has been whispering to me in a dead queen's voice, he did not say. Because I dreamed last night that I was standing in a coronation three hundred years old and everyone turned to look at me and their faces were all my mother's face. Because I am a food-taster whose body keeps a ledger of poisons and lately the ledger has started writing back.

"Palace headaches," he said. "Too many candles. I hoped the country air would fix me."

"Mm," said Maren Ashgrove, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer she did not open in company.

* * *

That night he dreamed of the kitchen again—or what he thought of as the kitchen, though no kitchen in any living house had ceilings that high or fires that burned blue.

In the dream he was small. Six, maybe seven. His mother was teaching him the tasting game, which was not a game at all, which was the whole of his inheritance dressed up in sugar.

"Bitter first," she said, laying out little dishes on the scarred table. "Bitterness is the liar's flavor. It arrives loud so you won't notice what's underneath it. Now—what's underneath this one?"

He'd licked the spoon. Almond, sweet almond, the pretty poison, the one that smelled of weddings. Underneath it, something green and cold, like a cellar in winter.

"Hemlock?" he'd guessed.

"Clever boy." But her voice had done a thing it sometimes did—a hitch, a catch, as if the praise cost her something. She'd knelt and taken his chin in her hand, and her eyes had been gray as the valley, gray as everything here, and she'd said, quiet enough that even then he understood it was not meant for the room: "One day you'll taste something that isn't poison at all, Orsen. Something old. When that happens, you come home. You come home and you put your feet on our ground and you listen to nothing, do you understand? Nothing at all. The silence will tell you what you are."

He'd nodded, six years old and sticky, understanding nothing.

In the dream—as in memory—he asked the obvious question. "What am I, Mama?"

And she smiled the way she smiled when the priestess came, polite and closed as a shut gate, and said, "Hungry. Wash your hands."

Orsen woke in his narrow childhood bed with his heart going like a drum in a procession, and lay in the dark listening to the silence inside him, and for the first time in two weeks found it terrifying rather than peaceful.

*Nothing at all. The silence will tell you what you are.*

What kind of mother taught her son to read emptiness?

* * *

Remembrance day came up grey and clear, the way all valley days did, and the whole of the Ashgrove plot turned out—which was to say eleven people and a dog, the dog being the only attendee with strong opinions about the priestess.

They buried nothing, because there was nothing left to bury; remembrance in the valley meant walking the boundary of the family land at dusk with a candle apiece, saying the dead person's name at each corner, so that whatever remained of them would know the shape of home one more year. Old gods rite, plain version. No incense, no chanting, no gold. His mother would have approved. His mother had approved, once, loudly, of the general principle and specifically of the wine afterward.

Orsen walked the eastern boundary with his candle guttering in the windless air—it shouldn't have guttered, there was no wind, but grief makes its own weather—and spoke her name at the corner stone. *Liss Ashgrove.* The words went out of him and did not echo, because nothing echoed in the valley, and he felt the loss of her the way he felt it every year: not sharper, just heavier, like a coat that gained a pound each winter.

At the southern corner, Aunt Maren fell into step beside him.

"You dreamed about her," Maren said. Not a question.

"How would you know that?"

"Because you walk like her when you've dreamed about her. Shoulders up. Listening." Maren snorted. "You've walked like that since you got off that dreadful mule. So either you dreamed about her, or the palace finally taught you to be afraid, and I know which one I'm betting on."

Orsen watched his candle flame bend toward the stone. "I dreamed about the tasting game," he said. "And something else. A hall. High ceiling. Everyone in old-fashioned clothes, the kind on tapestries. Someone put a crown on someone's head and the whole room—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The whole room *sighed*. Like a bellows. Like the building itself breathed."

Maren said nothing. The dog, trotting ahead, looked back at them with an expression of canine embarrassment at the family drama.

"There's a word for what I've been feeling," Orsen continued, quieter now. "Since the quail. Since the poisoning that wasn't a poisoning. It's like being a house where someone else has moved in and is very polite about it. And the moment I crossed the pass into the valley, it stopped. Completely. Like crossing a line drawn on the world." He turned to look at her. "You knew. Didn't you. You knew what I was before I did."

Maren watched the flame of her own candle with the flat calm of a woman who had been waiting nine years for this conversation and resented every one of them.

"Your grandmother made me swear," she said at last. "Your grandmother, mind, not your mother. Your mother couldn't say it out loud even at the end. Made me promise that if the whispers started, I'd tell you true." She paused. "I was hoping to wait until after the wine."

"Maren."

"All right. All right." She set her candle down on the corner stone, carefully, as if it were an argument she was setting aside. "You know the family story. That we were cooks. That we came up from the south generations back because the valley soil suited root vegetables and contrarians."

"That's the story."

"It's even mostly true. We *were* cooks. But we weren't cooks for farms, Orsen. We were cooks for the crown. The old crown. The Hollow Crown, before anyone called it hollow." Her voice dropped, though there was no one for three miles in any direction except the dog and the dead. "Every royal kitchen had tasters. Most of them died young and were replaced like spoons. But one family lasted. One family learned to make the body into a ledger—not just surviving the poisons, but *remembering* them. Every toxin, every antidote, every recipe that ever touched a king's plate, written into the blood and passed down. Mother to child. Taste to taste."

Orsen's mouth had gone dry. "Memory keepers."

"Keepers of the crown's *memory*, yes. Not the history-book kind. The living kind. The crown itself—there was a crown, a real one, before Veylan melted half of it and hid the rest—it held more than gold, love. It held everything the dynasty ever knew. Every secret, every recipe, every sin. And it needed somewhere to keep the overflow. Somewhere magic couldn't reach and meddle. Somewhere quiet." She gestured, one floured-sleeved arm taking in the whole grey valley, the grey fields, the grey sky. "Somewhere like this. You weren't born here by accident, Orsen. You were *planted* here. Like a bulb. Like a seed in a cellar, waiting to see if the world would ever need what's written in you."

The candle flames along the boundary had all gone still at once, every one of them, eleven flames standing straight as soldiers, and the dog whined and pressed against Orsen's leg.

"And the whispering," he said. His voice came out sanded flat. "The thing that follows me outside the valley. That's the—"

"That's the crown remembering it has a keeper," Maren said. "Yes."

"Why now? Why after three hundred years? Why *me*?"

And here his aunt did something he had never seen her do in his entire life. She hesitated. Maren Ashgrove, who answered questions before they were finished, who had opinions about the weather's character, looked down at her candle and was silent long enough that the dog sat down in protest.

"Because the prayers are being answered," she said finally. "You wouldn't have heard, up in the capital. Veylan burns preachers. But out here, in the poor parishes—candles burning blue. Prayers getting replies. The old gods waking up after three centuries of silence." She looked at him then, and her eyes were gray as his mother's, gray as the valley, gray as everything the magic could not touch. "And the priests are saying the same thing in every parish, Orsen. They're saying the gods went quiet because the crown went hollow. And they're saying the gods aren't answering prayers anymore at all."

"What are they answering, then?"

"They're answering *him*," Maren said. "Whoever carries the crown's memory. Whatever speaks through the vessel." She picked her candle back up, and her hand, for the first time Orsen could recall, was not entirely steady. "They're answering you, love. Whether you will it or no."

Far off across the grey fields, at the far boundary of the Ashgrove land, a single candle flame leaned sideways—against the windless air—and went out.

Then another. Then another, walking the perimeter toward them, corner by corner, like footsteps.

Maren stared at it, and Orsen watched the color drain out of her face until she matched the valley perfectly.

"Get your things," she said. "You leave tonight."
