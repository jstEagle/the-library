## 3. Prayers Answered in the Dark

The candle had been burning blue for three days before Sera told anyone.

She would like to say this was prudence. It was not. It was terror, plain and simple, the kind that sits in the chest like a swallowed stone and makes a person very good at pretending everything is fine, thank you, lovely weather for the harvest festival, isn't it.

On the first morning she had assumed the wax was tainted. On the second morning she had trimmed the wick, said the prayer again—*Hearthmother, keep my mother's hands from pain*—and watched the flame turn the color of deep water, steady and bright and utterly wrong. Candles in the Shrine of the Ninefold Hearth burned gold or they burned out. That was the whole of the arrangement. Gold meant the gods heard you the way a wall hears you knocking: something on the other side, perhaps, but nothing coming through.

Blue meant something else entirely. Blue was in none of the texts. Blue was not supposed to be possible.

By the third morning Sera had stopped sleeping and started counting.

* * *

"You're doing it again," said Maren, without looking up from her mending.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you've seen something holy and you're deciding whether to tell me or bury it in the herb garden with the rest of them." Maren bit her thread and squinted at the sock in her lap. She was the shrine's eldest keeper, sixty if she was a day, with forearms like a dockworker and a faith that had survived forty years of Veylan's edicts mostly by refusing to be interesting. "Well? Out with it."

Sera knelt across from her, in the little back room where the shrine kept its honest things—chipped cups, a leaking roof, the good tea hidden behind the dried lavender because anything nice got confiscated as idolatry eventually. Outside, dawn came gray over Copperlane, and somewhere below a fishwife was already arguing with the world at large.

"It's the candles," Sera said. "They're answering."

Maren's needle paused. "Candles answer all the time. That's what prayer is. You speak, and later, sometimes, life rearranges itself in a manner you choose to credit to the gods. Very tidy system. I invented half of it myself."

"Not—" Sera pressed her palms flat against her knees. "Not *answering*. Burning. The flames go blue when I pray. Every candle I light. And last night I went down to the river shrine, the one nobody tends since the confiscations, and I prayed there too, and the old votive lamps—the ones that haven't been lit in six years—they lit *themselves*, Maren. Blue. All nine of them. In order. Eldest first."

The needle stayed paused. Maren looked at her for a long moment, and Sera watched forty years of careful unremarkability war behind her eyes.

"In order," Maren repeated.

"Hearthmother, then Tidefather, then the Twins. Down the whole family. Like someone walking through a house turning on lamps."

Maren set down the sock. She set it down very carefully, the way one sets down a thing that might be a bomb, which in Copperlane was a reasonable instinct for most objects. "Have you told the Chapterhouse?"

"And say what? 'Forgive me, Reverend Mother, but the gods have broken their two-hundred-year silence and they're doing it in *my parish*, via *wax*?' They'd have me in seclusion by noon. Or worse—they'd believe me, and then the inquisitors would believe me, and then we'd both be a cautionary tale."

"That," said Maren, "is the most sensible heresy I've heard all year." She rubbed her jaw. "And you're sure it's your prayers specifically. Not the candles. Not the room. You haven't, say, bought new wax off a charming stranger with suspiciously good prices?"

"Maren."

"I'm asking liturgically."

"I prayed over a *turnip* last night. To test. I asked the Hearthmother to bless it." Sera held up her hands. "It has not gone blue, but when I touched it, it was warm. Warm as fresh bread. In a cold pantry. At midnight."

Maren closed her eyes. "You warmed a turnip."

"I warmed a turnip."

"We are so deeply cursed," Maren said, with feeling, and then, because she was Maren and therefore incapable of leaving a problem unattended, "Right. If the gods have decided to start talking after two centuries of dignified silence, they're saying something. And whatever they're saying, they're saying it *here*, which means either we are extraordinarily blessed or somebody up the hill has done something extraordinarily stupid. Possibly both. Those often travel together." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling. "We're going to find out where else it's happening. Get your cloak. And Sera—if anyone asks, we're collecting alms."

"We're always collecting alms."

"It's the perfect crime. Nobody donates."

* * *

They spent the day walking Copperlane, and Sera learned quickly that the city had been whispering for days already; it had simply been whispering in the language of small miracles, which no one had thought to translate.

The baker on Tanner's Row swore his oven hadn't needed wood since Tuesday. The midwife on Bell Street kept a candle now that "never gutters, don't ask me why, it just sits there being smug." A child showed them a puddle that reflected stars despite the overcast sky, and when Sera crouched to look, her own reflection wore a faint crown of blue light that the actual sky above did not provide. The child thought this was delightful. Sera laughed along and felt the stone in her chest grow heavier.

Because here was the thing she didn't say aloud, the thing she turned over in the dark like a coin she wasn't sure wasn't cursed: every miracle was small. Gentle. A warm loaf. A lamp that wouldn't die. A grandmother's cough easing the moment her granddaughter prayed over her tea. The old gods of the hearth and the tide and the threshold had never been gods of thunder. Their power had always been domestic, patient, woven into soup and childbirth and the lighting of lamps—and so their return was the same. It should have been comforting.

It was not comforting, because Sera had read the confiscated texts Maren hid inside the false bottom of the tea chest, and she knew what the old stories said about answered prayers. The gods did not spend themselves lightly. Every answered prayer was a payment made, and payments required a *source*. And in every old story, the source was the same terrible, beautiful thing:

A vessel. Someone the divine poured through, whether the someone consented or not.

"Here," Maren said, stopping so abruptly that Sera walked into her back. They stood at the mouth of Gull Street, where Copperlane's crooked houses gave way to the merchants' quarter, and beyond it, up the long gray spine of the Processional Way, the palace sat on its hill like a knife left standing in a table.

"Here what?" Sera said. "This is the third time you've stopped. My feet are filing a complaint."

"Count with me." Maren's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used for funerals. "The baker on Tanner's Row. His flour supplier delivers through the palace granaries—everyone knows it, it's how he gets around the grain tax. The midwife on Bell Street attends the household of a customs clerk who eats at the palace officers' mess twice a week. The woman with the star-puddle sells her fish to the palace kitchens every morning at dawn. And the child—" Maren turned, and her eyes were grim. "The child's father is a scullery boy. Palace kitchens. Has been since he was ten."

Sera felt the street tilt slightly beneath her.

"No," she said.

"Every answered prayer in this city, girl. Every single one I've heard about today. Draw lines between them on a map and tell me what shape they make."

"They make a *kitchen*," Sera whispered. "They make a kitchen in the palace."

"Aye." Maren looked up the Processional Way, at the banners of King Veylan hanging limp in the windless air. "Someone in that building is being used as a conduit. Someone down there in the dark among the pots, pouring the gods back into the world through bread and soup, and I'd wager my good knee they don't know it. Because here's the part I hate, Sera. Here's the part that keeps the needle still." She lowered her voice until it was barely breath. "Conduits burn. That's in the texts too. The vessel doesn't get a choice about any of it—not the pouring, not the burning. The gods aren't cruel, but they are *thirsty*, and they've been dry for two hundred years."

The fishwife's argument drifted up from the harbor, indomitable. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, ordinary as anything. Sera stared at the palace and tried to imagine a person inside it—a cook, a taster, a washerwoman—who had gone to sleep an ordinary soul and was waking, night by night, into something the heavens drank from.

"We have to warn them," she said.

"With what?" Maren snorted. "'Greetings, stranger, cease being holy immediately'? We don't know who it is. We don't know how to stop it. Half the Chapterhouse would call it a miracle and the other half would call it treason, and the inquisitors would call it *evidence*." She took Sera's arm, grip firm as a dock rope. "What we can do is watch. Learn who goes in and out of those kitchens. Pray carefully, sparingly, and never, ever in public. And if the gods want to talk to us badly enough—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Because at that moment, at the top of the Processional Way, in the gray afternoon light, every banner on the palace facade flickered—just once, just slightly, like cloth remembering it had once been flame—and for a heartbeat Sera could have sworn they burned blue.

Then they were only banners again, and the wind remembered itself, and the world resumed pretending to be ordinary.

"—then they'll find us," Maren finished quietly. "They usually do. Come along, girl. Alms to collect."

They walked home through streets full of small, impossible warmth, and Sera prayed the whole way—not for answers, but for whoever stood in the palace kitchens, unknowing, holding all that fire.

*Please,* she thought, toward the hill, toward the stranger. *Whoever you are. Hold on.*

In her pocket, forgotten, the stub of candle she'd carried since morning flared softly blue, like a held breath finally let out.
