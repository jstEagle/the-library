## 4. The Inquisitor's Invitation

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Orsen would later decide was the first insult.

Tuesdays were soup days. Soup days were the easiest days, the days when a taster could almost forget he was a man whose entire profession consisted of not dying in public. The kitchens of King Veylan's palace ran like a well-greased millwheel on Tuesdays—barley and smoked mutton, a leek broth for the queen's table, bread so fresh it steamed—and Orsen had a rhythm to it. Sip. Wait. Count to sixty. Note the flavor. Note the absence of flavor, which was often more telling. Move on.

He had survived eleven years this way, and he had learned that survival looked, from the outside, exactly like boredom.

So when a page in livery too clean for the kitchens appeared at his elbow and said, "Orsen Ashgrove? The Inquisitor Corveth will see you now," Orsen's first thought was that someone had finally poisoned the king properly, and he was about to be blamed for it.

His second thought was that his hands were steady, which was either very good or very bad. Eleven years of dosing himself with things that would stop a horse had left his body with a peculiar stillness. Fear arrived like everything else did—slowly, faintly, at the end of a long corridor of numbness.

"See him where?" Orsen asked.

The page blinked. "The White Tower."

Of course. Not the inquisitors' offices in the lower court, where they handled smugglers and tax-frauds and the occasional unlucky heretic. The White Tower, where the serious work happened. Where men went in with names and came out as entries in ledgers.

"Am I being arrested?" Orsen asked, because it seemed better to know.

"I don't think so," the page said, in the tone of someone who had been told to say exactly that.

* * *

The walk across the courtyard took four minutes. Orsen spent them doing what he always did when the world tilted: cataloguing.

The morning was cold enough to show breath. Two guards by the gatehouse, bored, one picking at a scab on his knuckle. A kitchen cart trundling toward the servants' entrance, driven by Marta, who waved at him with a ladle. Somewhere above, gulls fighting over the midden heaps. All of it ordinary. All of it continuing without him, which was, he supposed, the entire point of a life like his. He was a hinge nobody noticed until the door stopped swinging.

Except lately the door had started whispering.

It had been nine days since the dish that didn't kill him. Nine days since the saffron sauce had sat on his tongue and spoken—not in words, exactly, but in something older than words, a pressure behind the ears, a sense of vast attention turning toward him like a lighthouse beam. He'd reported the dish clean. He didn't know why. He still didn't know why. It was the first lie he'd ever told in the line of duty, and it had come out smooth as poured cream, which frightened him more than the whispering had.

Since then, the whispers came at odd hours. In the cellar among the wine casks. Over the wash-water. Once, horribly, during evening prayers in the servants' chapel, where Orsen attended purely for the warmth and had stood through the whole litany feeling like a struck bell.

And now the White Tower wanted to see him.

They took his knife at the door. They took his belt. A clerk with ink-stained fingers wrote his name in a book—Orsen watched the letters go down, *O-r-s-e-n*, and thought: *that's how it starts. First they write you down. Then they write you up.*

Then stairs, spiraling, lit by arrow-slits, until a door opened onto a round room at the top of the tower and Orsen stepped into the quietest room he had ever been in.

That was the thing about Inquisitor Dain Corveth's study that no one warned you about: it wasn't frightening. It was comfortable. There was a fire. There were books with cracked leather spines. There was a window with a view of the whole grey city, the river stitching through it, the palace roofs below like a second, smaller city built on top of the first. And there was Corveth himself, a broad, grey-templed man in plain black wool, pouring tea from a pot as if he had all afternoon and intended to enjoy it.

"Taster Ashgrove." Corveth gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit. You'll want the chair. This conversation is longer than it looks."

Orsen sat. "Inquisitor."

"Do you know why you're here?"

"Someone poisoned the king," Orsen said. "And I missed it."

Corveth smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "No. The king is perfectly well. I checked personally this morning. He ate three breakfasts and complained about all of them." He slid a cup of tea across the desk. "Drink that. It's chamomile. If I wanted to poison you, Taster, I promise you'd never taste anything at all."

Orsen looked at the tea. Looked at Corveth. Drank it, because refusing would have been its own kind of confession, and because it really was excellent chamomile.

"Now," Corveth said, settling back, folding his hands over his stomach like a man about to discuss weather. "Tell me about your dreams."

"My—" Orsen kept his face still. Eleven years of practice held. Mostly. "I don't sleep much. Occupational habit."

"Everyone dreams, Taster. Even the sleepless. Especially the sleepless." Corveth opened a ledger on his desk—not the one with Orsen's name in it; a different one, older, its pages soft at the corners. "Do you know what my office does? Most people think we hunt heretics. Burners of icons, whisperers against the crown. That's perhaps a tenth of the work. No—the greater part of what we do is listen. We listen to the realm. And lately, Taster Ashgrove, the realm has been saying strange things in its sleep."

"The realm dreams?"

"The realm reports." Corveth turned a page. "A laundress in the Salt Quarter dreamed of a coronation and woke speaking a dead dialect. A ferryman on the Graywater dreamed of a queen drowning and couldn't be persuaded she wasn't real. Three soldiers of the night watch deserted rather than keep dreaming of a battlefield none of them have ever seen. And in every case—" he looked up, mild as milk, "—the dreamers describe the same face. A young man. Dark-haired. Standing slightly apart from everyone else. Watching."

The fire popped. Orsen became aware of his own heartbeat, slow and heavy, a drum in an empty hall.

"That's very interesting," he said.

"It is, isn't it." Corveth closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a lid over something sleeping. "Here is what troubles me. In three hundred years, no vessel has ever been recorded inside the palace walls. The keepers were careful. Generations of careful breeding, careful placement, careful silence. And then, nine days ago, a taster in King Veylan's own kitchen tastes a dish laced with grave-pollen—a substance that has not been brewed since the fall of the old dynasty, incidentally, which raises questions of its own—and instead of dying, or reporting it, he says nothing. And the dreams begin."

The room was very quiet. Down in the city, a bell rang the hour, and the sound seemed to come from very far away.

"You've made a mistake," Orsen said.

"Have I?"

"I'm from the Ashen Valley. Nothing follows anyone out of the Ashen Valley. My mother was a cook. My father drove a lime cart. I count poisons for a living and go home to a rented room. That's the whole of me."

It was a good speech. He'd assembled it in the stairwell, and every word of it was true, which was the trick—he'd learned that from watching the court. Lies fail; true things arranged badly succeed.

Corveth listened to the whole speech with his head tipped, like a man enjoying music. Then he said, kindly:

"You lied just now. Twice."

Orsen said nothing.

"Your father didn't drive a lime cart. There are no lime carts in the Ashen Valley—the soil eats wood, everyone knows that. And you said 'my mother was a cook,' past tense, but your file says remembrance day, not funeral day, which means she's alive, and you misspoke because your mind was busy building the rest of the sentence." Corveth refilled his tea, unhurried. "You're a terrible liar, Taster. Genuinely dreadful. It's almost refreshing. Everyone else in this city lies beautifully and believes nothing. You believe everything and lie like a child with jam on his face."

Something in Orsen's chest went tight and small. "What happens now?"

"Now?" Corveth considered. "Now nothing. Now you go back to your soups and your counting. I simply wanted to meet you. To put a face to the ledger entry." He rose, and Orsen rose with him, knees stiff. At the door, Corveth paused with his hand on the latch.

"One piece of advice, freely given. When the memories come—and they will come, they always come—don't fight them in public. Find somewhere private. Somewhere safe." His eyes were pale grey and entirely without malice, which was somehow worse. "I would hate for anything to happen to you in front of witnesses. It complicates the paperwork."

* * *

Orsen walked back across the courtyard in a daze and got through the afternoon on muscle alone. Barley broth: clean. Leek soup: clean. Roast fowl for the steward's table: clean, though he counted to ninety instead of sixty, twice, because his hands had finally remembered how to shake.

Marta caught him staring at nothing over the wash-tub.

"You look like a man who's been asked a question he can't answer," she said.

"I've been asked a question I answered badly."

"Ah." She nodded sagely. "Those are the worst kind. My husband used to answer those honestly. Died of it eventually. Well—of a bad knee, but the honest answers didn't help."

He almost laughed. It came out as a huff, and Marta patted his arm with a wet hand and went back to her tubs, and Orsen stood there in the steam thinking: *she waved at me this morning with a ladle. She's waved at me for six years. What happens to Marta when the paperwork gets complicated?*

That was the terror, he realized. Not the Tower, not Corveth, not even the whispers. The terror was this: for thirty years he had been invisible, and invisibility had felt like safety, and now he could feel himself becoming visible—color rising into a sketch, edges sharpening, a figure stepping out of the background of other people's lives into the foreground of history. And visible things could be seized. Pointed at. Burned.

He finished his shift. He ate standing up. He walked home through streets gone blue with dusk, bought a meat pie from the corner stand out of pure stubbornness—it tasted of nothing, everything did lately—and climbed the three flights to his rented room under the eaves.

He barred the door. He lit the candle. He sat on the bed with his back to the wall, the way he'd slept as a boy, and he waited.

He didn't have to wait long.

It began in his teeth. A hum, low and deep, like a bell heard through stone. Then the candle flame stretched tall and thin and turned the color of deep water, and the walls of his room went soft as fog, and Orsen Ashgrove—who had trained his body to refuse death, who had counted sixty thousand times, who had made himself a ledger of survivals—felt the ledger torn open from the back cover, and the memory poured in.

*Snow on the steps of the High Temple. Banners the color of old blood, snapping in a wind off the mountains. A crowd so vast it made a single sound, the way the sea makes a single sound. And down the aisle, through the parting crowd, a young man walking—dark-haired, walking slightly apart from everyone else, watching—*

*No.*

*Not watching. Being crowned.*

*The crown coming down was not gold. It was hollow—a circlet of dark metal, open at the top, empty where a head should fill it—and when it touched the king's brow, Orsen felt what the crown felt, which was hunger. A great, patient, bottomless hunger, drinking the man's memories the way a root drinks rain. Every name he'd ever loved, flowing out of him and into the metal. His mother's voice. His first snowfall. The smell of his father's forge. The crown drank and drank and the king smiled and smiled, because the crown let him keep the shape of his memories while it swallowed their weight, and the crowd roared, and the priests sang, and high above, unseen, the old gods turned their faces away.*

*Three hundred years of kings. Three hundred years of drinking. And underneath it all, threaded through every coronation like wire through wax: a keeper family, generation after generation, standing slightly apart from the crowd, holding the overflow. Holding it in their blood. Holding it in their recipes, their lullabies, their soup pots—*

The vision broke.

Orsen came back to himself on the floor. He didn't remember falling. The candle had burned down to a stub; the meat pie sat untouched and cold on the table; outside the window, the sky had gone the grey before dawn. His cheeks were wet. He touched them, surprised.

He tried to stand and found his body wouldn't take instructions, so he stayed sitting against the bed, breathing, while the hum faded slowly out of his teeth.

*A coronation three hundred years dead,* he thought. *I was there. I was the crown.*

And beneath the terror, quieter, colder, a single practical thought surfaced, the kind of thought eleven years of tasting had trained into him:

*Corveth knows. Corveth has always known. And he told me to find somewhere safe—because he wants to watch what I do next.*

Orsen looked at the guttering candle, at the frost creeping up the window glass, at the city waking below him, unaware.

Tomorrow he would taste the king's breakfast. Tomorrow he would count to sixty. Tomorrow he would be invisible again, or try.

But tonight, for the first time in his life, Orsen Ashgrove understood that he had never been invisible at all. He had been hidden. And somewhere in the White Tower, a patient man was already writing the difference down.
