## 5. A Thief in the Pantry

There were three rules to stealing from the palace of King Veylan, and Wick had learned all of them the hard way, which was the only way anyone learned anything worth knowing.

Rule one: never steal anything anyone would miss immediately. Saffron was perfect for this—expensive enough to be worth the risk, but it lived in little clay jars on a high shelf in the spice pantry, and no one counted saffron the way they counted silver. Silver had a face. Saffron just smelled like a rich man's soup.

Rule two: never steal at the same hour twice. Tonight he'd come through the wall-gap behind the flour stores at the changing of the night watch, when the kitchen boys were snoring in their loft and the only soul awake was old Marta, who was deaf as a post and half-blind besides, and who owed Wick's mother a debt from before either of them could walk.

Rule three—and this was the one that kept you alive—never, ever let anyone see your face.

Which was why, when the lamp flared up behind him and a voice said, quite calmly, "You're standing in the nutmeg," Wick felt his whole short criminal career flash before his eyes like a dropped tray of dishes.

He turned slowly, hands raised, and found himself looking at the taster.

Everyone knew the taster. Or knew *of* him. Orsen Ashgrove, the quiet one from the valley where magic didn't work, the man who drank poison for a living and never seemed bothered by any of it. He stood there in his plain grey servant's coat with a candle in one hand and an expression that wasn't angry or alarmed or even particularly interested. He looked, if anything, mildly inconvenienced, the way a man looks when he finds a cat has gotten into the larder.

"I'm not—" Wick started.

"You are," said the taster. "In the nutmeg. It's imported from the Ember Isles and it costs more than you do." He tilted his head. "Well. Perhaps about the same."

Wick's mouth went dry. His hand drifted, entirely without his permission, toward the knife at his belt.

"Don't," said Orsen Ashgrove, in the same mild voice. "Not because I'll call the guard—I won't—but because you'd have to get past me, and I've spent six years building up an immunity to things that kill people, and I promise you the knife is the least interesting thing my body can survive."

That was so strange a thing to say that Wick actually laughed, one startled bark of a laugh, before clapping his own hand over his mouth. Above them, faintly, someone in the sleeping loft rolled over. The taster waited until the snoring resumed its rhythm.

"Right," Wick whispered. "So. You're going to turn me in."

"Am I?"

"That's generally what palace servants do with thieves."

"Mm." The taster set his candle down on the worktable, unhurried, and looked at the saffron jar tucked under Wick's arm. "How much were you taking?"

"...One jar."

"One jar sells for what a laborer earns in four months. You're not feeding a habit. Habits are cheaper." Those pale eyes came up and studied him, and Wick had the deeply unpleasant sensation of being tasted himself, weighed and catalogued like something on a spoon. "Who's sick?"

The question landed like a hook behind his ribs. Wick's face did what it always did, which was nothing—he'd trained it well—but apparently his silence said plenty, because the taster nodded slowly, as though Wick had answered at length.

"Someone's sick," Orsen said. "And saffron steeped in goat's milk brings down fevers that won't break on their own. My mother used it." Something flickered across his face then, quick as a fish under ice, gone before Wick could name it. "Take two jars. The second one's older stock; nobody will notice the difference, and fever doesn't care about vintage."

Wick stared at him. "Why?"

"Why what?"

"Why would you—" He gestured vaguely at the whole impossible scene: the thief, the jars, the calm. "You don't know me. I could be lying. I could be working for someone."

"Are you?"

"No!"

"Then it doesn't matter." The taster picked up his candle again. "Finish before the watch changes back. And step *out* of the nutmeg."

* * *

Wick did not sleep that night, which was normal, but he didn't sleep for the interesting reason, which was not.

He sat in the crawlspace above the tannery that served as his current lodgings—a space roughly the size of a coffin with ambitions—and turned the evening over in his mind like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. Nobody covered for a stranger. Nobody gave away two jars of saffron worth eight months of wages. Kindness in the capital was a currency, and currency always came with a rate of exchange written somewhere small.

Unless the taster wanted something. That was the obvious answer. Everyone wanted something. Wick's whole life was built on the certainty that everyone wanted something, and the trick to survival was finding out what before they collected.

But what could a food-taster possibly want from a smuggler? He had no money worth the name. No secrets—or rather, his secret was the most boring secret in the palace: *I drink poison and live.* No power, no friends, no—

No. Wait.

Wick sat up in the dark, banging his head lightly on a beam, and thought about the tunnels.

Because here was the thing nobody in the palace knew, or if they knew it they'd forgotten, and forgetting was the best security there was: the old palace had been built on top of an older palace, which had been built on top of ruins that predated Veylan's grandfather's grandfather. There were ways out of this city that didn't go through any gate. There were ways into it, too. Wick knew eleven of them personally and had heard rumors of others, and he had assumed—everyone assumed—that knowledge was worthless because nobody ever needed to leave the capital badly enough to pay what it cost.

But the taster had looked at him tonight with those flat grey eyes, and Wick had seen something underneath the calm. He'd spent his life reading faces across dark rooms, learning which merchants lied about weights and which guards took bribes and which doors had new locks, and he knew what fear looked like even when it was wearing its company clothes.

Orsen Ashgrove was afraid of something inside the palace. Not of it. *Inside* it.

"Well," Wick said aloud, to the rats, "that's interesting, isn't it."

The rats declined to comment. They'd seen him talk to himself before and had stopped finding it novel.

* * *

Three days later, Wick left a jar of honey on the spice shelf where the taster would find it, with a scrap of paper underneath: *For the fever. If it worked. — W.*

It was a test, obviously. If the taster reported the note, Wick would know he'd misread everything and would vanish into the river district by week's end. If the taster ignored it, fine, no harm. But if the taster answered—

The answer came the next morning, folded small and left in the same spot: *It worked. She slept through the night. Thank you. Why is there honey?*

*Saffron and honey,* Wick wrote back. *My gran's remedy. The milk carries the saffron, the honey carries the taste, because fevered children will spit out medicine that tastes like medicine.*

*I'll remember that,* the taster wrote, and then, after a visible hesitation in the ink, as though the pen had paused mid-thought: *Her name?*

*Pip. She's not mine—my sister's girl. Sister's got work that keeps her away.*

*I see. Tell Pip that honey helps.*

Wick read the note four times. Then he folded it up very carefully and put it in the lining of his coat, next to the other three things he owned that mattered, and told himself firmly that it meant nothing, that notes were notes, that kindness was a hook, that rule three existed for a reason.

He was aware, even as he told himself this, that he was already composing tomorrow's note in his head.

* * *

The trouble with leaving notes in a spice pantry was that eventually someone else read them.

Wick came through the wall-gap on the fifth night—not to steal, just to leave a reply about whether Pip's fever had stayed broken (it had)—and found the taster waiting for him, sitting on a flour sack in the dark like a ghost who'd taken up baking. He didn't have a candle this time. He was just sitting there in the blackness, which was somehow much worse.

"We need to talk," Orsen said.

"Those are famous last words," Wick whispered. "Also, how long have you been sitting in the dark?"

"An hour."

"An hour! What if I hadn't come?"

"Then I'd have sat another hour and gone to bed." A pause. "Someone died yesterday."

The words dropped into the dark between them and lay there. Wick lowered himself onto a sack opposite, keeping the gap between them—the gap was important, the gap was professional.

"A taster?" he asked quietly, because everyone in the palace below stairs knew about the tasters, the way sailors know about drownings.

"The newest one. Bennet. Nineteen years old. They gave him a plate at supper and he was dead before the plates were cleared." The taster's voice stayed level, but Wick heard the effort of the levelness, the way you hear the effort in a man carrying something heavy upstairs. "The poison was one my body shrugs off. I've had it twice. It shouldn't have killed him—it barely slows *me* down."

"So they weren't poisoning the king."

"No. They were poisoning me, using him as the proof." A long breath. "Someone knows what I am. Or part of it. And there's a priestess who's been asking questions, and an inquisitor named Corveth who asked me questions last week that had nothing to do with food, and I've started having dreams that aren't dreams, and I'm going to say something now and I need you to listen without laughing."

"All right," said Wick, who laughed at funerals and had once giggled through his own arrest.

"I think I'm going to need to leave this city. Soon. Possibly very soon. And I'm told—you're told—people say—that you know ways out that aren't gates."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, a beam settled with a creak, and the kitchen breathed around them, the banked ovens ticking as they cooled.

Here was the moment, Wick thought, where a sensible man negotiated. Set a price. Information like this was worth more than saffron, more than honey, more than Pip's whole street. Here was the moment where he said *what's in it for me* and started the haggling that governed every transaction in his life.

Instead he heard himself say: "Corveth asked you about *dreams*?"

"Yes."

"That's bad. Corveth doesn't ask about anything unless he already knows the answer. Asking's just how he checks if you'll lie about it." Wick rubbed his face. "Did you lie?"

"Badly."

"Oh, that's *worse*. Lying well gets you time. Lying badly gets you a list." He stood up and paced the length of the pantry, four steps each way, thinking aloud the way he did when the thinking got too big to keep inside. "Right. Tunnels. There's the drain route under the tannery row—foul, waist-deep in places, but it comes up outside the walls past the leper fields, and nobody searches a man who smells like that. There's the old wine cellars that connect to the smuggler's warren under Tanner's Gate. There's—" He stopped pacing. "Wait. Why am I telling you this for free?"

"Because Pip slept through the night," said the taster.

Wick opened his mouth. Closed it. Made a gesture that in a court of law would have been entered as evidence of surrender.

"That's cheating," he said.

"Is it?"

"You can't just—remind a man of a thing like that in the middle of a negotiation. There are rules. There's a whole *system*."

"I'm sorry. Should we start over? You tell me your price first."

"My price," said Wick, with dignity, "is that when you inevitably get us both killed, you admit it was your fault. And that you teach me the honey-saffron thing properly, measurements and all, because Gran's version uses too much honey and she'd haunt me if I let her recipe die wrong."

Something happened to the taster's face then. It took Wick a moment to recognize it, because it was so rare on palace faces, so rare on any face Wick dealt with professionally: it was a smile. Small, crooked, real.

"It's a deal," said Orsen Ashgrove.

They shook on it, there among the spices, a taster and a thief in the dark, and Wick noted with distant alarm that his hand didn't want to let go right away, and that this too was probably against some rule he'd forgotten to write down.

* * *

He was halfway back through the wall-gap when the taster spoke again, quietly, from the darkness behind him.

"Wick. The priestess. Sera. If she finds you first—she's not your enemy. I don't fully understand what she is yet. But she found me in the kitchens today, and she looked at me like I was a door she'd been searching for." A pause. "She called me 'vessel.' I don't know what it means yet. But I think the gods are answering prayers again, and I think they're answering through me, and I think that's why Bennet is dead."

Wick stood very still in the gap, cold stone against his chest, and considered all the sensible responses available to him. *Good luck with that. Find another smuggler. I steal saffron, not destinies.*

What he said was: "Tomorrow night. The drain route. Bring a change of clothes you don't like, because you're going to ruin them."

And what he thought, crawling home through the dark with his heart going like a drummer boy's, was that rule three had just developed a very large exception, and that he couldn't decide whether he'd just made the worst mistake of his life or the first honest trade he'd ever made—one secret for another, one frightened man for another, trust extended on credit in a city where credit was always called in eventually.

Behind him, in the sleeping palace, a nineteen-year-old's grave was fresh and an inquisitor was writing lists, and somewhere in the dark between the walls, the oldest god in the world had begun, very softly, to whisper through a kitchen servant's mouth.

Wick pulled his coat tight and walked faster.

Tomorrow night, then. Drain route. Clothes he didn't like.

And gods help them both, extra honey.
