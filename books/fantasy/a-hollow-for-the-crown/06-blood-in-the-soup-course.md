## 6. Blood in the Soup Course

The morning of Maren's death began, as all my mornings did, with a spoon and a prayer I didn't believe in.

"Small sips," Master Quillon instructed, hovering at my elbow in the tasting chamber like a nervous hen. "Chew the pheasant thoroughly. And for the love of all that's left of your stomach, Orsen, tell me *before* you turn colors this time."

"I turned a very dignified shade of mauve," I said. "It suited me."

"It did not suit you. Nothing suits you before the second bell." He pressed the spoon into my hand. The pheasant was glazed in quince and something darker underneath—juniper, maybe, or the memory of juniper. I put it on my tongue and waited.

This was the part no one understood about the work. People imagined tasting as glamour: silks, gold forks, the king's own ear bent close. In truth it was arithmetic. Every dish was a sum, and my body was the ledger. Bitterness here, a numbness there, a faint ringing behind the teeth—and I tallied each entry against ten years of small poisons, swallowed deliberately, catalogued by Master Quillon in a book he guarded more jealously than the crown jewels. *Ashgrove, Orsen. Immune to eleven of the fourteen common alkaloids. Sensitive to shellfish, which is not poison but is inconvenient.* I had built myself into an instrument, the way a swordsman builds calluses. It had seemed, at nineteen, a reasonable price for being indispensable to a man who killed people for being interesting.

The pheasant tasted of quince. Of juniper. And beneath both, faint as a coin dropped down a well, of *something else*.

I set down the spoon.

"Well?" Quillon demanded.

"Nothing," I said, which was true, which was also not the whole truth, because for three days now the dishes had been whispering—not words, exactly, but a pressure at the edge of hearing, like someone reciting poetry through a wall. I had decided, with the disciplined rationality of a man whose livelihood depended on his tongue being trustworthy, that I was going mad. Slowly. Politely. In a way that wouldn't cost me my post.

Quillon waved the pheasant through. It went up to the king's table, and King Veylan ate it, and the world continued its rotation, indifferent as ever.

Maren died at supper.

* * *

He was the junior taster, twenty-two years old, freckled across the nose like a thrush's egg, and he laughed too loudly at his own jokes because he'd learned early that loud men survive courts better than clever ones. He'd been with us eight months. He called me "the Corpse" affectionately, because I'd survived things that would have felled a mule, and he'd once told me, drunk on festival wine, that when he'd saved enough he meant to buy a boat. Not sail anywhere. Just *own* it. Look at it. He said a man should own one beautiful useless thing.

The soup course was white bean and smoked ham, a peasant dish dressed in silk, the kind of thing Veylan liked because it let him perform humility. Maren tasted first—it was his rotation, his right, and I remember thinking idly that the beans were over-salted, that Cook Aldith would be wroth.

I remember thinking that, and then Maren said, "Huh," in a small surprised voice, like a man finding a door where he'd expected a wall.

Then he said nothing ever again.

He went down between one breath and the next. No color change, no clutching at the throat, none of the theater poison usually provides. One moment he was reaching for the bread; the next he was on the floor with his eyes open and his mouth slightly curved, as if the joke he'd been about to tell had died with him.

The hall erupted. Guards, shouting, the king swept out under a canopy of spears. Someone knelt by Maren and someone else vomited into a corner and I stood exactly where I was, holding my untouched bowl, doing arithmetic.

Because here is what I knew, standing in that spreading pool of silence: I had tasted that soup. An hour earlier, from the same pot, from the same ladle. I had stood in the kitchen doorway with my little silver spoon and swallowed a mouthful of white bean and smoked ham, and I had felt—nothing. No bitterness. No numbness. No whisper, even. Just soup.

And Maren, healthy, young, unarmored by a decade of deliberate self-poisoning, had taken one sip and stopped existing.

"There was nothing in it," I heard myself say. A guard turned on me, hand on his hilt. "I tasted it. There was *nothing in it*."

"Then why is he dead?" someone asked, and nobody answered, and the question hung in the smoky air like a blade waiting to be claimed.

They carried Maren out feet-first. On his way past me, lolling with the undignified grace of the dead, his sleeve brushed my arm, and I thought: *a boat. He wanted a boat.*

That night I sat in my narrow cell above the kitchens and did not sleep. I took out the vial of ashroot extract I kept against emergencies—the one poison I was genuinely immune to, my security blanket, my rosary—and held it without drinking, and thought about doors where walls should be.

Someone had poisoned that soup. Poison doesn't invent itself. But it had been aimed past every other mouth in the hall, through the whole crowded room, to find one throat. Mine. Except it hadn't found mine, because my body had shrugged it off the way a duck shrugs off rain—and so the arrow had struck the next man in line.

Maren hadn't been the target. Maren had been the *miss*.

I am not a good man. I want that recorded somewhere, in case anyone tells the story wrong later. I have swallowed poisons for a usurper and felt nothing, and I mean that in every sense. But I sat on my cot in the dark with Maren's death sitting on my chest like a cat that had decided to stay, and I discovered that even a ledger can bleed.

* * *

She came for me two days later, in the hour before dawn, when the kitchens were empty except for the ovens ticking as they cooled.

I smelled her before I saw her—tallow smoke and some herb I couldn't name, which bothered me professionally. She stepped out from behind the flour racks like she'd been growing there, a young woman in the grey-and-blue of the old faith, hood back, hair cropped short as a soldier's. I knew her vaguely; the priestesses came begging candle-wax at the servants' entrance sometimes, and Cook Aldith gave it grudgingly, muttering about superstition.

"You're Ashgrove," she said. "The taster."

"And you're trespassing in a royal kitchen, which carries a penalty I'd rather not describe before breakfast."

"My name is Sera." She said it like a key turning in a lock, like the name itself was the point. "Of the temple of the Ninefold Flame. What was in the soup?"

I leaned against the prep table and crossed my arms. "Beans. Ham. Grief. You'll have to be more specific."

"The night the boy died. You tasted it first." Her eyes were pale grey, the color of weather coming. "You tasted it and lived, and everyone is saying you're lucky, but luck isn't a thing, taster. Luck is a story people tell so they don't have to look at what's actually happening." She stepped closer. Up close I could see she was frightened—not of me, which was refreshing, but of something behind her own eyes. "Three nights ago my candle burned blue. Do you know what that means? It means a prayer got *answered*. And not just mine. Old Marta praying for her grandson's fever—blue. The lamplighter praying for rain—blue. Half the city, blue flames, and every answered prayer, when we trace them—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "Every thread points here. To the palace. To the kitchens. To *you*."

"That's flattering," I said, "but I don't pray. I'm from the Ashen Valley. We consider it poor manners to ask favors of powers that can't follow us home."

"I know where you're from." Something flickered across her face—not hostility. Pity, maybe, which was worse. "Everyone from the Valley says that. *Magic can't touch us.* You wear it like armor. But armor only matters if something's attacking, Orsen Ashgrove, and something is. It's been attacking you for days, hasn't it? Whispering."

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the ovens seemed to hold their breath.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, and it came out steady, and I was almost proud of it.

And then Sera's face changed.

I've seen possession described in books—rolling eyes, foaming mouths, voices like gravel dragged over stone. This was nothing like that. It was worse. It was *calm*. Her shoulders settled. Her breathing slowed. Her face simply... opened, the way a house opens when the owner steps out and leaves the door swinging, and what looked out through her eyes was old. Unimaginably old. Patient the way stone is patient.

When she spoke, it was her voice, but arranged differently, the way a room is different after the furniture's been moved.

"*Vessel.*"

My spine tried to leave through the top of my skull. "Nope," I said, with tremendous dignity. "Absolutely not. Whatever this is—"

"The crown remembers in you." She—or it—took another step, and I backed into the prep table hard enough to rattle the knives. "Three hundred years we were silent. Three hundred years the throne drank memory and called it glory, and we would not serve it, and so we went quiet, we gods of the small hearths, we gods of bread and salt. But the crown could not be unmade. Only *carried*. Keeper to keeper. Mother to son. Down the long years, hidden in blood and recipe and bone." The old voice softened, and somehow that was the most terrifying thing yet. "We did not choose you, vessel. We are only answering. You prayed without knowing. Everyone prays without knowing. And now the prayers burn blue, and the hunters are coming, and the boy who died—"

"Don't," I said. My voice cracked straight down the middle. "Don't you dare use him. He wanted a boat. He wanted a stupid beautiful useless boat, and he's dead because whatever's inside me couldn't be bothered to warn anyone, because I didn't *know*, because nobody *told* me—"

"He died because the crown's enemies have scented it," the god said, gentle and terrible. "A vessel can be captured. A vessel can be emptied. And a vessel who cannot be poisoned must be reached another way. They will try again, Orsen Ashgrove. They will keep trying until they learn your shape. Unless you run."

"Run where?" I laughed, and it came out ragged. "I taste soup. That's my entire contribution to civilization. I don't run anywhere. I stand in front of plates and I swallow."

"Yes," said the god, through the girl's weather-grey eyes, with something that in any other mouth might have been warmth. "You swallow. You have been swallowing your whole life. Poisons. Orders. Grief. You were made for carrying, vessel—that is what a keeper *is*. The only question left is what you will consent to carry, and for whom."

Sera shuddered, all at once, like a horse shedding a fly, and caught herself on the flour rack, gasping. For a moment she just breathed. Then she looked up at me with her own eyes again—younger now, scared now, human now—and said, hoarsely:

"What did It say? While I was—what did It say?"

"That I'm a vase," I managed. "An heirloom vase. With a price on it."

"Oh." She winced. "Yes. That tracks. It's not subtle. Gods never are—they think everything's obvious because it's obvious to *them*." She steadied herself, pushed back her cropped hair, and fixed me with a look that had steel under the fear. "There are people who can help. Not many. A smuggler who knows the tunnels. A deserter camped outside the walls. Me, apparently, whether I like it or not. If what It said is true—if they'll keep poisoning until they map you—then staying in this palace isn't courage, taster. It's just dying slowly in a place with nice floors."

I looked at her. I looked at the ovens, cooling, ordinary, the only church I'd ever really attended. I looked at the spot on the flagstones where Maren had fallen, though of course there was nothing there now, there's never anything there, that's the trick of it.

Ten years I had made myself invisible. Small sips, dull eyes, mauve with dignity. And in three days I had become a lighthouse: prayers bending toward me, gods borrowing mouths, poison sliding off me onto the people beside me. Visibility, I was beginning to understand, was just another kind of exposure. Another entry in the ledger, and the sum was coming due.

"They'll ask questions," I said finally. "About Maren. About me. By tomorrow there'll be an inquisitor in that tasting chamber asking why the Corpse won't die."

"Then tomorrow we're gone," Sera said. "Tonight, you decide."

She slipped out the way she'd come, between the flour racks, leaving tallow-smoke and that unnamed herb hanging in the air.

I stood alone in the dark kitchen, and I took out my little vial of ashroot, and for the first time in ten years I didn't drink it. I just held it, this small proof that my body belonged to me, and understood that it never had. It had belonged to the crown all along, passed hand to hand down the generations like a cup at a funeral feast.

Somewhere above me, in the sleeping palace, a bell tolled the hour before dawn.

Tomorrow, an inquisitor would come asking questions. Tonight, a smuggler supposedly knew the tunnels.

I put the vial away, picked up my knife, and went to find out if the stories about Wick the pantry-thief were true.
