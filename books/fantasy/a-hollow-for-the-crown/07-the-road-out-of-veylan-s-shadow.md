## 7. The Road Out of Veylan's Shadow

### Orsen

The tunnel smelled of wet stone and old onions, and Orsen had never been so grateful for either.

"Mind the third step," Wick said, holding the lantern low. "It's a liar. Looks solid. Isn't."

Orsen tested it with his boot and felt the stone give like stale bread. He stepped over it and kept moving, one hand on the slick wall, the other full of the small bundle Sera had pressed on him before they'd left: bread, hard cheese, and a candle stub she claimed was blessed, though he suspected all candles were blessed if you believed hard enough.

Behind them, the palace kitchens would be waking. Someone would find the note he'd left on his own cot—*gone to my aunt's for the remembrance week, back by Moonday*—and someone else would notice that Inquisitor Corveth's summons sat unanswered on the same cot, and the two facts would sit together in some clever head like garlic and butter in a hot pan.

He tried not to think about that. Thinking about it made his hands shake, and shaking hands on a liar step was how men died in tunnels.

"You're doing the thing," Wick said.

"What thing?"

"The breathing-through-your-teeth thing. Cooks do it when the steward walks in."

"I'm not breathing through my teeth."

"You are. I've watched you do it for two years every time the King asked about the venison." Wick lifted the lantern so its light caught his grin—quick, crooked, entirely unrepentant. "Relax. Nobody's followed us. I checked twice."

"You checked twice because you expected to be followed."

"I checked twice because checking once is for amateurs." Wick ducked under a low arch of brick, and Orsen followed, and the tunnel bent away from the palace and toward the river and toward whatever came next.

That was when the memory took him.

It didn't ask. It never asked. One moment he was walking behind a smuggler with saffron still under his fingernails, and the next he was standing in a field of trampled rye at dusk, three hundred years dead, watching ten thousand men kneel in the mud before a boy-king on a white horse. The boy was crying. Nobody had told him the crown would weigh what it weighed. The crown's gold was cold against a brow that wasn't Orsen's, and the roar of the army went up like a wave, and underneath the roar—

Underneath the roar, Orsen heard the whispering again. *Remember us. Remember us. Remember.*

"—Orsen? Orsen!"

He came back to himself with his knees in the mud and Wick's hand fisted in his collar, lantern swinging wild shadows across the brick.

"You dropped," Wick said. "Like a sack of turnips. Just—dropped mid-step."

"I'm fine."

"You're kneeling in a puddle."

"I'm fine," Orsen repeated, and heard how unconvincing it was even as he said it. He let Wick haul him upright. His legs held. Mostly. "It happens sometimes. Lately."

"Lately meaning since the soup?"

Lately meaning since the dish that whispered. Lately meaning since the coronation dream, since Sera's god had looked out of her eyes at him across a kitchen table and called him *vessel*. "Since the soup," he agreed, because it was simpler.

Wick studied him for a long moment, lantern light flickering over a face that was too thin and too quick to be trusted and somehow trustworthy anyway. Then he shrugged. "Well. If you fall down again, fall forward. There's a grate ahead and I'm not carrying you."

It was such an absurdly practical kindness that Orsen laughed—a short, startled sound that echoed off the tunnel walls—and found that some knot in his chest had loosened by half a turn.

They walked on. Somewhere above them, dawn was coming to Veylan's city, and somewhere behind them, an inquisitor was probably reading a note about an aunt's remembrance week and not believing a word of it.

But down here there was only the dark, and the smell of old onions, and a thief who'd checked twice.

### Wick

Wick had stolen from nobles, priests, tax collectors, and one memorable circus strongman, and he could say with authority that none of them were as frightening as a food-taster having a religious experience in a drainage tunnel.

He kept talking because talking was what he did when things got bad. "So the grate opens into a cooper's yard, right, and the cooper owes me money, which is the best kind of neighbor to have. From there it's the tannery lane, then the river gate, and Bram's waiting past the gate with a cart and the personality of a wet fencepost, but he can drive anything on four wheels or two legs, so we tolerate him."

Silence behind him. That was worse than the teeth-breathing.

"He'll grow on you," Wick added. "Like moss."

Still silence. He turned the lantern.

Sera had caught up to them somewhere in the last hundred yards—he hadn't even heard her, which bothered him more than he let it—and she was crouched next to Orsen, who stood with both hands flat against the tunnel wall like a man listening for a knock.

"It's happening now?" she asked quietly.

"It's always happening now," Orsen said. His voice was strange. Layered. Like two people speaking in unison and only one of them knew it. "There was a battle here. Under the city. Before the city. They flooded the tunnels on purpose and drowned their own garrison rather than surrender the river gate." He blinked, and blinked again, and came back looking exhausted and twelve years older. "I don't think this is a tunnel. I think it's a tomb they built streets on top of."

Wick looked at the wall he'd leaned against a thousand times, at the old brick worn smooth by water, and decided very firmly not to think about the bones that might be under the mortar.

"Right," he said brightly. "New plan. We take the tannery lane instead."

"That doesn't help," Sera said.

"It helps *me*."

She almost smiled. It was a small, tired thing, but it counted. She'd been like that since the kitchens—since she'd grabbed his sleeve with a grip far stronger than her size suggested and said *you're coming with us, the god says so*, and Wick, who had planned to spend that evening selling Corveth a tidy report about a taster's habits, had heard himself say *fine* instead. He still wasn't sure how that had happened. He was less sure why he hadn't undone it.

Because of the brother, he told himself, the way he told himself every night. Because Rennick rots in a work camp until Corveth gets what he wants, and you already stopped giving it to him, and that's done, that's chosen, no taking it back.

Mostly he believed it.

"Cooper's yard," he said aloud, and pushed open the grate.

Dawn in the cooper's yard was gray and cold and smelled of oak shavings, and Bram was exactly where he'd promised to be, sitting on the cart bench with his arms crossed and his face arranged into its usual expression of a man being personally offended by the weather.

"You're late," Bram said.

"We're early," Wick said. "You're just grim."

"I'm punctual and grim. They're different crimes." Bram's eyes went past Wick to the priestess and the taster climbing out of a hole in the ground, and something in his jaw tightened—the jaw-tighten of a man recalculating odds he didn't like. "This is everyone? A priestess, a poison-eater, and you?"

"And your sunny disposition. Load up."

### Sera

Sera rode in the cart bed between a barrel of pickled herring and Orsen Ashgrove, and she prayed without words, because words felt too small for what was happening to him.

She had spent her whole novitiate praying into silence. That was the doctrine: the gods were distant, the gods were quiet, the gods had gone wherever gods go after the old war, and faith was the discipline of speaking anyway. She had been good at it. She had liked being good at it. And then, eleven days ago, her candle had burned blue, and the silence had ended, and every answered prayer in the city pointed like a compass needle at a man who tasted soup for a usurper.

Now she watched him sleep—or whatever passed for sleep in him—slumped against the herring barrel as the cart rattled north through farm country, and she watched the whispering move through him like wind through wheat.

His lips moved. Not words she could catch. His fingers twitched in patterns—drumming, counting, tracing lines on his knee as though writing in a script that predated any script she knew.

"Bram says he did that all night," Wick said, dropping onto the cart's tailboard beside her, keeping his voice low. "Talked in his sleep. Not our language. Bram thought it was a fever."

"It's not a fever."

"No," Wick said. "I got that." He was quiet a moment. For a thief, he had a remarkable talent for looking like a man carrying something heavy and pretending it was empty. "Is he going to be all right? Honestly. You're the god-expert."

Sera considered lying gently, and discarded it. Gentle lies were for people who hadn't watched a friend drown in someone else's memories. "I don't know. The crown chose him—or the gods chose him, or the crown and the gods are the same argument wearing different robes, and honestly the theology is above my pay grade. What I know is that a vessel holds until it pours. And he's filling fast."

"Filling with what?"

"Three hundred years of everything the crown ever saw. Every coronation. Every betrayal. Every—" She stopped, because the cart bed shifted.

Orsen's eyes were open. Not seeing them. Seeing elsewhere.

"There was a queen," he said, in that doubled voice, and Sera's whole body went cold, because she recognized the cadence now, had read it in the archive fragments her order kept hidden in a locked chest: the formal speech-patterns of the Hollow Court. "Maren, second of her name. She was dying in the winter palace and the loyalists wanted to move the crown east and she refused. She said—" His brow furrowed, reaching. "She said, *a crown is only heavy where it sits. Take it off and it weighs nothing. Let them fight over the chair.*"

And then, horribly, gently, in a voice that was entirely his own and cracked straight down the middle:

"She died alone. Everyone who loved her had already run. She died alone and she was *right*, and nobody listened for three hundred years—"

He shuddered once, all over, and came back to himself, blinking at the gray sky, at the herring barrel, at the two strangers staring at him from the tailboard.

"How long," he said.

"A minute," Sera said. "Maybe less."

"They're getting closer together." He said it flatly, the way he must have said *this dish is poisoned* a thousand times, cataloguing danger without flinching. "In the tunnel it was a battle. Now it's her. It's getting faster."

"Then we get faster," Wick said, and Sera heard it in his voice too—that new thing, that unpracticed, dangerous tenderness he probably thought he was hiding. "Bram! How far to the crossroads?"

"Twelve miles if the horse agrees," Bram called back, "and the horse and I are in negotiations."

### Bram

Bram Voss had deserted from the Fourth Border Regiment eleven months ago, and in that time he had learned three truths. One: guilt traveled lighter than armor but you carried it further. Two: every plan improved dramatically when Wick wasn't allowed to name it. Three: he was going to die for someone eventually, and he'd always assumed it would be for his company, stranded and starving in the hill country, waiting on an amnesty that would never come.

He had not assumed it would be for a kitchen servant who talked in dead queens' voices.

He drove the cart and watched the road and did not look back at the cart bed, where the priestess was feeding the taster bits of cheese like he was a convalescent sparrow, and where Wick was telling some story about a circus strongman that was at least forty percent true.

The plan was simple, which meant it was doomed. North through farm country, east along the old drover's road, into the hill country where the roads forgot themselves and so did the King's patrols. From there—well. From there, Bram had a letter in his breast pocket with a wax seal he'd been turning over in his mind for eleven days like a coin he couldn't stop flipping.

Because there was another way to save the Fourth Company. A faster way. A way that involved a certain inquisitor learning that a certain taster was traveling north with a deserter, a priestess, and a thief, in exchange for amnesty papers signed and sealed.

He flipped the coin. Heads: the letter. Tails: the road.

The taster—Orsen, he kept having to remind himself the man had a name—climbed up onto the bench beside him around noon, apparently tired of being fussed over. Up close he looked like what he was: a man who'd spent years making himself invisible and had recently and catastrophically failed.

"You drive well," Orsen said.

"I drive adequately. You don't have to be polite. I know what I am to this arrangement."

"What are you to this arrangement?"

"A pair of hands and a knowledge of back roads, hired at the going rate." Bram watched the road. "Don't make it strange."

To his surprise, the man laughed—not much, just a breath of it. "All right. Hands and back roads. I'm the cargo, then."

"You're the strangest cargo I've ever carried, and I once moved a bear."

"A bear?"

"A circus bear. Long story involving Wick, which means forty percent of it is true."

Orsen smiled at the road, and then the smile faded into something more careful. "The priestess thinks I should let the memories come. Stop fighting them. She says fighting makes it worse."

"Do you believe her?"

"I believe she prays harder than anyone I've ever met, and that her god has opinions about me, and that neither of those things makes her wrong." He flexed his hands on his knees. "But every time I stop fighting, I lose another hour of being myself. And I keep thinking—if I pour enough of myself out to make room, what's left at the end? Do you understand? I'm a ledger. I've spent my whole life keeping exact accounts of what goes into my body. And now something is writing entries in me that I never approved."

Bram drove in silence for a while. The horse clopped. The hills got slowly taller.

"My company," he said finally, because apparently today was a day for saying things. "Forty-one men. I left them at a pass in the border hills when I deserted—no. That's not honest. I didn't leave them. I was sent to scout an escape route and I took it. Just me. Just my legs, and the route, and the chance." He flipped the invisible coin. "Every day since, I've wondered if a man can owe a debt to forty-one people at once, or if it just spreads too thin and stops counting."

Orsen was quiet. Then, softly: "What were their names?"

Bram glanced over. "What?"

"The forty-one. You said it like a number. What were their names?"

And Bram opened his mouth to answer and found—to his genuine astonishment—that he could only summon nine. Ten, maybe, if he counted Dole who everyone called Dill. Eleven months of guilt, and guilt had worn the names smooth as river stones, and he sat there on the cart bench with his throat tight and his hands steady on the reins and understood that this stranger beside him, this poisoned, haunted, borrowed-memory man, had just asked the only question that mattered.

"Tomas," Bram said. "Corporal Tomas Reyes. And Ferro, who owed everyone money. And little Anselm, who was nineteen." The names came easier now, one pulling the next. "Hetta. Osk. The twins, Jory and Jem, who weren't actually twins. Captain Aldous Vane, who deserved better than me."

"See," Orsen said quietly. "Still counts."

Bram flipped the coin one more time—heads, tails, spinning—and put it, for today, back in his pocket.

They made the crossroads by late afternoon, and that was where the trouble was waiting, because trouble has excellent timing. A rider stood at the junction where the drover's road met the north highway, mounted on a gray courser, wearing travel leathers cut in the style of the Inquisition, and even at two hundred yards Bram knew the set of those shoulders from posters and pulpit warnings.

"That's him," Bram said quietly. "That's Corveth."

Behind them, in the cart bed, Orsen Ashgrove began to whisper in a language three hundred years dead—and every crow on the crossroads fence turned its head to look at the cart at once.
