## 9. The Queen's Last Recipe

The chest smelled like my mother.

That was the first thing—before the cedar and the dust, before the cold of the valley morning seeping through the shutters—it simply smelled like her: dried lavender and tallow soap and something underneath both, faintly metallic, like a coin held too long in the palm. I sat on the floor of my aunt Maren's back room with the lid open across my knees and let that smell do what it always did, which was to make a liar out of me. For three full breaths I was eleven years old again, and she was alive, and the worst thing in the world was that she'd burned the porridge.

Then the breath went out of me, and I was twenty-six, and I was a fugitive sitting in a house where magic could not follow, which meant the crown inside my skull had gone quiet as a held tongue.

I should have been grateful. I *had* been grateful, for two days—the first silence I'd known since the whispering started in Veylan's kitchens. But silence, I was learning, has its own weight. A room with no sound in it is not peaceful. It is just waiting for you to notice how loud your own heart is.

"Found anything yet?" Wick asked from the doorway, mouth already full. He'd found my aunt's plum preserves within an hour of arriving and had been conducting a thorough investigation of them ever since. "There's a shawl. There's some letters tied with string. There's a boot."

"A boot?"

"One boot. Just the one. Your family keeps secrets even from their own footwear."

"That's a left boot," I said, without looking up. "The right one's under the floorboard by the hearth. She hid things asymmetrically so no one would think to look for pairs."

Wick stopped chewing. "That is the most unsettling sentence anyone has ever said to me, and I once shared a cell with a man who talked to his own beard."

I reached past him into the chest and lifted out the letters—her handwriting, small and slanted, addressed to Aunt Maren mostly, ordinary things, prices and weather and *tell Orsen to eat more*. Beneath them lay the shawl, grey wool gone soft as moth-wing, and beneath the shawl—

A book.

Not a grand thing. A kitchen book, the kind every cook keeps, its cover stained with grease ghosts and its spine mended twice with different thread. My mother's name was inked inside the front cover in her careful hand: *Lira Ashgrove, who learned it from Dell Ashgrove, who learned it from Sela.* Three names. Three generations. And then, squeezed beneath Sela's name in a hand much older, much smaller, as if the writer had been afraid of being overheard even on paper:

*who kept it.*

I turned pages. Recipes. Only recipes—or so my eyes insisted at first. Honey cakes for a naming day. Barley soup for grief. A brine for pork that called for juniper and one measure of something written only as *the dark salt*. Notes in the margins, tiny, cramped, sometimes upside down. My mother's hand, my grandfather's hand, hands before them layered like sediment.

And threaded through all of it, in the margins and between the lines, marks I almost didn't recognize because I hadn't seen them since I was a child doodling in the ashes of the hearth.

Royal cipher. The old dynasty's cipher—the one they taught us didn't exist anymore, the one from before Veylan, before the war, before the Ashen Valley was a place people went to forget. I knew it the way you know a lullaby: not the words, but the shape of them. My mother had sung nonsense syllables over her cooking when she thought I wasn't listening. I had thought they were nonsense.

They were cipher. She'd been reading aloud.

"Orsen." Sera stood in the doorway now too, and behind her Bram filled most of the rest of it, arms crossed, the way he stood when he wanted to look like furniture. The deserter had a genius for making himself part of walls. "You've gone white as flour."

"It's her recipe book."

Sera came in and knelt beside me, careful of the chest, careful of me, in that way of hers that made me want to tell her everything and also never speak again. Her candle-prayers had stopped burning blue the moment we crossed the valley border—she'd checked, three times, standing at the boundary stones like a woman testing whether a river was still there—and I knew it frightened her more than the burning had. Gods going quiet around me was somehow worse than gods speaking through me.

"May I?" she asked, and touched the page when I nodded.

"*Barley soup for grief,*" she read. "That's lovely."

"It's not just soup." My voice came out strange. Flat. "Look at the measures. Look at the *words* she uses for them. 'One memory of salt.' 'A thumb's width of forgetting.' 'Stir widdershins until it remembers being water.'"

Bram shifted against the doorframe. "Kitchen talk gets strange in old families," he offered, in the tone of a man hoping very hard that this was true.

"No." I traced the marginal cipher with one finger. The marks woke something in me—not the crown, the crown was mute here, but something older and quieter, something that lived below the crown the way roots live below a tree. "This isn't strange kitchen talk. This is a hiding place. You hide things in plain sight by making them boring. Nobody steals a soup recipe. Nobody burns a soup recipe. Nobody interrogates a woman about barley." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Gods. She fed the whole valley off this book. She made me learn these dishes. She made me *cook* them, every remembrance day, every festival—I thought it was just—"

"Tradition," Sera said softly.

"I thought it was love."

"It is love," Sera said, with the mild ferocity she reserved for correcting theology. "It can be both. That's rather the point of everything, if you ask me."

* * *

We worked through the morning while Aunt Maren pretended not to hover and failed completely, bringing bread and cheese and tea in a rotation so relentless it could have provisioned a siege. She watched me decipher with an expression I couldn't read—grief and guilt braided together, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of a woman who has kept a secret for forty years and has just watched it walk in the front door wearing her nephew's face.

It was Wick, naturally, who broke it open. Not through scholarship. Through appetite.

"This one," he said, upside down over my shoulder, jabbing a finger at a page near the middle. "Make this one. 'Queen's honey-cakes, for courage before a hard road.' If I'm going to be hunted across half a kingdom I want baked goods involved."

"You can't bake queen's honey-cakes," Maren said sharply, from the doorway. Then, catching herself: "They're—difficult. Lira never managed them right."

"She managed them fine," I said slowly. Something cold was walking up my spine. "She made them once a year. On the anniversary of the fall of the old capital. She cried the whole time and told me it was the smoke from the oven." I looked at the recipe properly for the first time. Not skimming. Reading. The ingredients were ordinary—honey, flour, eggs, rosemary—but the *method*. The method was insane. Fold the batter nine times, speaking a name with each fold. Rest the batter facing north. Bake until the cake *answers*, whatever that meant. And in the margin, in cipher, running along the edge like a vine:

*The ninth name is the gate. The gate opens inward.*

"Sera," I said. "What happens if I say a name out loud? One of the old names. Here, in the valley."

Her eyes went wide. "Nothing. Nothing *should*. The valley is dead to—" She stopped. Recalculated. "But you're not nothing. You're a vessel. The valley silences magic coming *in*. It doesn't necessarily silence what's already *in* you. Oh." She put a hand flat on the table, steadying herself or the table, hard to say. "Oh, Orsen. The book isn't hiding memories *about* the crown. It's a key *to* them. Your family didn't just keep the vessel safe. They built him a library and gave him the catalogue in recipe form."

"The keeper line," I whispered. Chapter after chapter of it suddenly rearranging itself in my head: why the Ashgroves had lived in the one place magic couldn't reach, generation after generation—a vault, we'd been a *vault*—why my mother had dosed me with bitter teas all childhood, "for the constitution," why she'd taught me to taste, to distinguish, to hold a flavor in memory whole and perfect. Why she'd chosen, or been chosen, or been *assigned*, and had carried it alone, and had died carrying it, and had left it to me folded inside a book of soup.

Inheritance. I'd spent my whole life thinking I had none. Tasters inherit nothing; they inherit other people's poisons. And here it was, the whole estate, written in honey and barley and grief.

Burden and gift, in the same binding. I didn't know yet which one weighed more. I suspected I'd get to find out.

* * *

By afternoon the others had drifted to tasks—Bram chopping wood with the grim efficiency of a man who trusted axes more than conversations, Wick ostensibly keeping watch and actually eating the failed first batch of honey-cakes ("they don't answer, but they're honest")—and Sera and I bent over the book together, translating.

The cipher came easier the deeper we went, as though the book recognized me. Which, Sera pointed out, it might. Ink remembers hands. Most of the annotations were household wisdom dressed in royal grammar—remedies, warnings, a suspicious number of opinions about neighbors' goats—but near the end, in my mother's freshest hand, the entries changed. Shorter. Urgent. Written fast, the letters leaning like runners.

And one entry, dated eight years past, the year she sickened:

*For the one who comes after. When the whispers start, do not run to the free cities blind. They will not open their gates to a rumor. Go to Meridian, to the Saltcellar, and give the counter-sign: "The last course is served cold." They will take you down to the council beneath the harbor. Tell them the vessel carries the whole of it, and watch their faces. Some will kneel. Some will reach for knives. Both are telling you the truth.*

I read it three times. The words didn't change. The world did, a little.

"There," Sera breathed. "There's the council. The real one, under the harbor—we'd heard rumors, but rumors are smoke; this is a *door*." She gripped my arm. "Orsen. We know where to go. We know how to be believed. That's everything. That's the whole mission, sitting in a soup book."

"Mm," I said, because my throat had closed.

Because there was more on the page. Below the instructions, smaller, in the plain alphabet instead of cipher, in the voice she used only for the truest things:

*My darling boy. If you are reading this, then I am done, and the whispers have found you, and I am sorry for all of it—for the burden, and for the lie of our ordinariness, and for every bitter tea. But listen to me. I chose to raise you in the valley because I wanted you to have twenty years of being nobody, and nobody is the sweetest thing a person can be. Whatever they call you—vessel, prince, archive—you were mine first, and you were a boy who burned porridge, and no crown can unmake that unless you let it. Cook the soup. Feed people. It is the oldest magic there is, and it is yours by right, and it cannot be taken or crowned.*

I set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Sera, to her enormous credit, said nothing. She just stayed kneeling next to me in the dust and lamplight while I got control of my face, and after a while she put her hand over mine on the table, and we stayed like that long enough for the light to move.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when I noticed the boot.

The right one, under the hearth floorboard, exactly where I'd told Wick it would be—he'd fetched it delightedly hours ago and then gotten distracted by preserves. Inside the boot, wrapped in oilcloth, was a letter. One page. Not cipher. Plain hand, plain words, addressed simply: *To whoever comes asking after the Ashgrove boy.*

My aunt's handwriting.

I found her in the garden, pulling winter onions in the failing light, and I held the letter out without a word. She looked at it, then at me, and something in her shoulders resigned itself, the way a wall resigns itself to a breach.

"You weren't meant to find that until after I was dead," she said. "Your mother had a real talent for making everyone around her plan for her death. Sit down, Orsen."

I sat on the cold step. She pulled onions and talked.

"Three riders came through the valley two days ago. Wore merchants' colors. Didn't buy anything, didn't sell anything, asked at the crossroads inn about travelers heading south. One of them had a scar through his eyebrow, left side, and gloves he never took off." She dropped onions into her basket with little thuds. "I've seen inquisitors before, child. I married one's cousin, gods rest the fool. They're looking for you, and they're patient, and patience is the one thing this valley has plenty of."

"How long do we have?"

"A day. Two if we're lucky, and we raised you better than to believe in luck." She finally looked at me straight, and her eyes were my mother's eyes, which felt like theft. "You have the book?"

"I have the book."

"Then you have everything she had. Everything any of us had." She pressed the basket into my arms, onions and all, absurd and holy. "Now get your strange little family off my property before I cry, because I'm a very ugly crier and I won't have witnesses."

From the kitchen window, Wick's voice floated out, muffled and mournful: "Maren, do we have *any* more plums, this is a matter of morale—"

I stood there a moment longer, holding onions, holding a book full of queens, holding a name I hadn't earned yet and a family I hadn't chosen but would burn down kingdoms before I traded. Burden and gift. Same binding. Same boy.

Behind me, far off toward the crossroads, a lantern flickered along the valley road where no honest traveler would be walking at dusk.

Two days, my aunt had said.

It looked like we'd get one.
