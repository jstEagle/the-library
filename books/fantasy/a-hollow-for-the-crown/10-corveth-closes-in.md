## 10. Corveth Closes In

Dain Corveth had a theory about doors.

Most men saw a door and thought *barrier*. Dain saw a door and thought *confession*. A locked door told him its owner had something worth locking away. An unlocked door told him its owner believed no one would bother to look. And a door that stood carefully, deliberately ajar—well. That was the most interesting kind of all. That was a door belonging to someone who wanted to seem unafraid.

The door to the Ashgrove cottage in the foothills above the Ashen Valley stood exactly three fingers' width ajar when he arrived on the fourth morning of his ride from the capital. He noted it, admired it, and did not touch it. He knocked instead, once, softly, the way a man knocks when he has all the time in the world and wants you to know it.

"Come in or don't," said a woman's voice from within. "The wind's making my tea cold either way."

He entered. The cottage was small, immaculate, and smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke—the particular smell, he'd found over twenty years of this trade, of households that kept their floors swept out of discipline rather than joy. A woman sat at the table with her back straight as a plumb line, hands folded around a cup she was not drinking. Grey-streaked hair pinned without vanity. Eyes the color of river stones: pleasant at a glance, and underneath, worn smooth by something patient and old.

Maren Ashgrove. Aunt to the taster. Sister-in-law to the dead mother. Keeper of—well. He was here to learn how much she knew about what she kept.

"You'll be the king's inquisitor," she said. Not a question. "You have the look. Polite boots."

"Polite boots?"

"Boots that knock." She nodded at his feet. "Soldiers' boots come in without asking. Priests' boots ask forgiveness after. Yours asked permission first. That's the polite kind, and it's the dangerous kind, if you want an old woman's honest opinion."

"It's a good opinion," Dain said, removing his hat and taking the chair opposite her without being invited, because being invited was a formality that wasted both their time and they would both know it. "I've always thought so myself."

* * *

He began, as he always began, with nothing.

He talked about the road up from the lowlands. About the weather, which had turned wet two valleys over. About the price of wool, which he genuinely did know because knowing prices made merchants trust you and merchants heard everything. He talked for the better part of ten minutes while Maren Ashgrove watched him with the expression of a woman timing an egg.

Finally she said, "Are you going to ask me about my nephew sometime before supper, or shall I put another log on?"

Dain smiled. It was a real smile; he permitted himself very few false ones, because a man who smiled falsely all day lost track of the true ones, and the true ones were the only tools worth owning.

"Orsen Ashgrove," he said. "Taster to His Majesty. Nineteen years in service, eight of them at court. Survived forty-one documented poisonings. No ill effects. Ever."

"Lucky boy," said Maren.

"He's not lucky, Mistress Ashgrove. Luck doesn't hold a ledger. Forty-one entries, all survived—that's arithmetic. Someone taught him to dose himself. Deliberately. From childhood, I'd wager, given the tolerance he's built." He let that sit. "That's not luck. That's inheritance."

She drank her tea. Slowly. Setting the cup down with a small click, precise as a lock turning shut.

"My sister-in-law fed him well," she said. "Good bone broth. Bitter greens. Children in this valley eat what grows here, and what grows here is bitter. Builds a hardy constitution."

"Mm," said Dain. "Tell me about your brother's wife."

Something moved behind her eyes then—not fear, he noted with professional interest. Grief. Old grief, worn smooth like the rest of her, but grief nonetheless, and grief was a lever he disliked using and used anyway, because the alternative was worse.

"Elara," Maren said. "She came to the valley nineteen years ago. Married my brother Tam. Kept bees. Made the best plum preserves in three parishes. Died seven years ago, winter fever, same as took Tam the year before." Her voice didn't waver. It didn't need to; it was already flat as a pressed flower. "She was a good woman. She was afraid of something. And she taught my nephew to be afraid of it too, only quietly, the way you teach a child to swim—you don't tell them about drowning, you just put them in the water every day until floating is a habit."

"And what was she afraid of?"

"If I knew," Maren said, "I'd have asked her."

It was a good lie. Dain gave it full marks. It had the right texture—a lie built around a truth, the way masons build arches around a centering frame. But he had been taking apart lies for two decades, and he could hear the frame still inside this one, holding its shape.

* * *

He did not press. Pressing was for soldiers and priests. Instead he stood, thanked her for her hospitality, and asked—politely—if he might see the house.

"Why?" she said.

"Because you'll say yes," Dain said pleasantly, "and we'll both be spared the theater of pretending otherwise."

And there it was again: that flicker. Not fear. Respect, almost. He had seen it before in the eyes of people smarter than their circumstances, the rare few who understood that an inquisitor who knocked was infinitely more dangerous than one who kicked the door in, because the kicking kind announced everything he intended to find, and the knocking kind found what wasn't announced.

She walked him through the cottage. Two rooms and a lean-to kitchen. He looked at everything and touched almost nothing. He looked longest at the kitchen, because kitchens were archives—every household wrote its history in its pantry—and this kitchen was wrong in ways that would never occur to anyone who hadn't spent his life reading rooms.

There were too many jars, first of all. Preserves enough for a household of six in a house built for one. Second, they were labeled in a hand that was not Maren Ashgrove's—he had seen her handwriting on the parish ledger she kept for the village, a cramped practical scrawl—and the labels were not labels at all. *Plum & honey, third summer.* *Elderflower, the year of the long frost.* Dates that meant nothing, unless they meant something.

Third—and this was the detail he carried out of that cottage the way other men carry gold—there was a shelf, high up, holding eleven jars, each sealed with wax stamped by a signet ring rather than a thumb. And the dust on that shelf had been disturbed. Recently. Within the season.

Someone had taken a jar down. Read it, perhaps. Put it back—or not put it back, and replaced it with a different one, because there were twelve rings in the wax-drip pattern on the shelf board and only eleven jars standing.

Twelve jars. Eleven remaining.

One recipe had left this house. And Orsen Ashgrove had ridden down out of the valley in spring to visit his aunt on his mother's remembrance day, and he had left again carrying something more than grief, though grief was certainly part of the cargo.

"Your nephew visited at remembrance-tide," Dain said. It was not a question either. Nothing he said today would be a question; questions were concessions, and he conceded nothing until he had already won.

"He visits every year," Maren said. "He stands at the grave. He eats too much of my bread. He leaves."

"What did he take with him when he left?"

"The bread," she said. "And a coat, because he never dresses warm enough, that boy. And—" and here, for the first time, Maren Ashgrove hesitated, and Dain Corveth felt the particular cold satisfaction of a hunter hearing the trap close, "—a book. My sister-in-law's recipe book. Family thing. I'm too old to cook for a crowd anymore. He cooks for the king. Seemed right it should go to him."

"May I see it?"

"No," she said.

"May I see where it was kept?"

"No," she said, and then, with a dryness that nearly made him laugh despite himself: "You may note that I said no twice, inquisitor, and that I'm saying it the same way both times, which means you may stop asking in different words hoping I'll tire. I raised a boy who survives poison for a living. Do you imagine I tire?"

* * *

He rode down out of the hills that evening and made camp alone, as he preferred, and sat by the fire doing what he did best, which was thinking in order.

Here was the shape of it, laid out like instruments on a surgeon's bench:

Item the first: Elara Ashgrove, née unknown, arrived in the Ashen Valley nineteen years ago with a child of perhaps four and married a beekeeper within the year. Nineteen years ago. Which was to say: precisely as long as Orsen Ashgrove had been alive, give or take the years before memory begins.

Item the second: the Ashen Valley was the one place in nine kingdoms where magic could not follow. Every hedge-witch and runaway priest knew it. Whatever Elara had feared—and whatever she had been running from—it was something magic could do to a child, and she had brought the child to the one place it couldn't reach him.

Item the third: the child had been trained, patiently, secretly, in resistance to poison—not as a soldier is trained, all at once and brutally, but as a keeper trains a vessel. Slowly. Over years. With love, probably. Dain had read the old accounts; he was one of perhaps five living men who had read the old accounts, and Veylan paid him extremely well to be one of them. The royal lines of the old dynasty had done the same. Tasters. Keepers. Living ledgers, dosed from the cradle until their bodies became vaults no poison could crack and no ordinary death could breach.

Item the fourth: prayers were being answered across the realm. Blue candles. Voices in wells. Dreams that came true by morning. The old gods, silent for three centuries since the Hollow Crown fell silent, waking up all at once like birds before an earthquake. And the center of every answered prayer his agents had traced, from the capital to the coast roads, bent toward one place like iron filings toward a lodestone: the palace kitchens.

Item the fifth: a fellow taster named Pellom had died three weeks ago of a compound that had been in Orsen Ashgrove's own breakfast the same morning. Same dish. Same dose. One corpse, one survivor. Poison does not choose. Someone chooses for it—which meant someone knew which cup was whose, which meant Orsen was marked, which meant Orsen had run, because marked men with sense run, and Dain's sources confirmed he had run, through the smugglers' tunnels under the granary quarter, southbound with a priestess of the old gods and a deserter of the Third Foot and a saffron thief with a face like a fox and debts in every port.

Item the sixth, and the item that mattered: the crown itself.

Veylan understood power the way a miser understands coin—he counted it nightly and trusted none of it—but even a miser knows which coins are minted in heaven. The Hollow Crown was not jewelry. It was a repository. Three hundred years of a royal line's memory, poured into a single object through rites older than the kingdom that now pretended those rites had never existed. And a repository required a vessel, because memory that sits still rots, and memory that lives must live somewhere, and the somewhere had always been a keeper. A bloodline of keepers. Tasters. Ledgers of survival.

Which brought Dain to the question he turned over last, the way he saved the sweetest thing on the plate for the end, and the question was this: what happens when a vessel dies?

He knew the answer. He had extracted it from a dying archivist in a monastery cell seven years ago, and he had dreamed about it since, which annoyed him, because Dain Corveth did not approve of dreams. The answer was: nobody knew. The archive might scatter—dissolve into the world like smoke, unrecoverable, the dynasty's claim gone forever. Or it might ignite—all of it, three hundred years of kings and wars and the terrible weight of the crown's original purpose, released into the world at once through whatever channel happened to be open when the vessel failed.

Scatter, or ignite. Extinction, or conflagration. And no archivist, no priest, no scholar alive could say which.

Which was why Veylan wanted the taster alive.

Not dead. Dain had received that instruction four times now, each time more emphatic, each time accompanied by the king's peculiar whining insistence that Dain explain *again* why they couldn't simply cut the problem out of the world. Because a dead vessel was a dice-roll, Your Majesty. Because the gods are answering prayers through this man whether he wills it or not, and gods answering through a corpse are gods answering through an open door. Because the crown's memory in a living vessel can be managed, contained, studied, bled slowly into a form the throne can use. And a crown's memory loosed into ten thousand minds at once—

Well. Dain had seen riots start over a rumor. He did not care to learn what three hundred years of true memory would start.

* * *

Near midnight, his second horse—there was always a second horse; a hunter who loved his work kept remounts the way a duelist kept blades—shifted at its picket, and Dain looked up from his notes and realized he was smiling at the fire.

It troubled him. Not the smile itself; he knew his own cruelties and catalogued them honestly, the way he catalogued everything. What troubled him was the warmth underneath it. Somewhere between the polite boots and the shelf with twelve wax-rings and eleven jars, between the old woman's magnificent stubbornness and the image of a kitchen boy riding south with a dead queen's recipes in his pack, Dain Corveth had begun to enjoy this.

The boy interested him. That was the danger. Inquisitors who found their prey interesting started making allowances, and allowances were how hunters died. He had watched colleagues die of interest. He resolved, formally, in the ledger-keeping corner of his mind where he recorded his own poisons and doses, to kill his interest as soon as it stopped being useful.

But it was useful now. Because here was the thing the boy plainly did not understand—here was the thing Sera the priestess did not understand, and Wick the thief certainly did not, and Bram the deserter had probably never understood anything in his life: the prize they were carrying was not a secret. It was a fuse.

Every league they fled south, they were not escaping the crown. They were delivering it. To Meridian. To the free cities. To the council of merchants and magistrates who would smell a weapon in the boy and fight over him like gulls over a fish. Veylan wanted a vessel in a cage; the free cities would want a banner; the priests would want a prophet; and the crown itself, patient as rot, wanted only one thing, which was to be needed again.

Dain snuffed the fire and lay back with his hat over his eyes.

In the morning he would ride south. Alone, ahead of the army, ahead of the orders, because speed was a weapon and Veylan had never learned to use it. He knew the tunnel the smuggler would use—Wick's routes were practically published among the guilds; the boy sold passage to anyone with coin, bless him, bless his greedy little heart. He knew the shrine where the priestess's order kept safehouses along the coast road; he had burned three of them already and remembered the locations of the rest. He knew the deserter's company was stranded at Redford Ford with amnesty dangling in front of them like a carrot before a mule, and he knew exactly which one of them had a wife and a debt and a weakness for both.

He knew everything except one thing, and the one thing gnawed at him all night, so that near dawn he sat up, and spoke it aloud to the dark, because some thoughts need air to die:

What did the twelfth jar contain?

Eleven jars on the shelf. Twelve rings in the wax. One gone—one recipe walking south in a dead woman's cipher, inside the head of the last keeper, toward the free cities, gathering prayers like a storm gathers lightning.

Dain Corveth saddled in the grey light and rode.

Behind him, on a hillside above the Ashen Valley, an old woman stood in her doorway watching the polite boots diminish down the road, and went inside, and took from beneath her floorboards a sheet of paper covered in a cramped, practical hand, and began, with great deliberation, to write a letter addressed to a kitchen in Meridian.
