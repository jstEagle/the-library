## 11. Bram's Bargain

Bram Halloway had betrayed exactly one person in his life, and he'd been twelve years old at the time, and it had been his brother Tamsin, and the betrayal had consisted of eating the last honey pear and blaming a magpie. He still thought about that pear. He thought about it now, in fact, sitting on a cold stone in the Ashen Valley with a scrap of paper on his knee and a stub of charcoal in his hand, because apparently a man could go twenty-two years without betraying anyone and then find himself composing a letter to an inquisitor like it was nothing.

*To the Honored Inquisitor Dain Corveth,* he wrote.

He crossed it out. Too formal. Corveth would smell formality the way Bram's old sergeant had smelled fear, which was to say instantly and with visible enjoyment.

*Inquisitor.*

Better. Shorter. A man who wrote short letters was a man who wanted something simple. A man who wrote long letters was a man with a conscience, and consciences got people hanged.

*Inquisitor — I know where the taster is.*

There it was. The whole letter, really. Everything after that was just decoration, the way you put parsley on a plate so nobody noticed the meat was stringy.

Bram looked up from the paper. Across the little hollow where they'd made camp — if you could call three bedrolls, a cookfire, and Wick's collection of suspiciously acquired pots a camp — Orsen was showing Sera how to make flatbread without setting it on fire. This was going poorly for Sera, who was a priestess of gods older than kingdoms and could not, apparently, negotiate with dough. She was holding her third ruined attempt at arm's length like a dead thing she was too polite to name.

"You're pressing it too hard," Orsen said. "It's bread, not a confession."

"Everything I touch turns to ash lately," Sera said. "I used to light candles with a word."

"And now you can't even light a griddle. The gods are teaching you humility."

"The gods are *laughing* at me."

"They do that," Orsen said, and there was something in his voice when he said it — something easy and sad at once, the way a river is easy and deep at once — that made Bram look back down at his letter very quickly.

*The Ashen Valley,* he wrote next. *North fork, past the burned mill. There are four of us.*

Four. That was the part that mattered. That was the whole reason the letter existed, wasn't it? Not for Bram. Bram had been a deserter for two years; he'd made his peace with running, or at least he'd stopped apologizing to himself about it every morning, which amounted to the same thing. But Sergeant Denna and the others were still stranded at the border garrison at Coldmere, forty-one soldiers under a sergeant who'd refused Veylan's loyalty oath and been left there like furniture in a house nobody owned. No pay. No relief. Winter coming down the mountains like a debt collector.

Corveth had found Bram in Meridian's lower city six weeks ago — before any of this, before the valley, before everything — and made him an offer with the neatness of a man folding a knife back into its sleeve. Amnesty for the company at Coldmere. Full pardon, back pay, reassignment somewhere green and boring. All Bram had to do was find the taster and send word.

And Bram had said yes. Of course he'd said yes. Forty-one lives against one kitchen servant he hadn't met yet — that wasn't even arithmetic, that was just counting.

Except.

Except the kitchen servant turned out to be a thin, quiet man who flinched at loud noises and gave away food like it physically hurt him to see someone hungry. Except the kitchen servant had walked into the Ashen Valley — into the one place in the world where whatever lived inside him went silent — and stood at the border for a full hour, Bram had timed it, looking north toward Meridian like a man watching a door close. Except the kitchen servant had asked Bram, on the second night, what Coldmere was like, because Bram talked in his sleep, and then had listened to Bram describe forty-one freezing soldiers with an expression Bram couldn't read and hadn't stopped thinking about since.

Bram looked at the letter. *There are four of us.* He added, because honesty was a disease he'd caught out here:

*One of them is a priestess. One is a smuggler. One is me.*

*You don't need the fourth. He'll come quietly if you ask him. He's not what you think.*

He stared at that last line for a long time. It was true, and it was a lie, and both halves of it made him sick.

"Bram." Sera's voice. "You've been glaring at that rock for an hour. Did the rock insult you?"

"It's a personal matter between me and the rock."

"Is the rock winning?"

"The rock's been winning since I was nine."

She snorted and went back to murdering her flatbread, and Orsen glanced over — just a glance, quick, the glance of a man who'd spent his life being invisible and had gotten very good at noticing other people trying to be — and Bram felt the charcoal go heavy in his hand.

That was the thing nobody warned you about found families. They didn't come with instructions, but they came with witnesses. Every hour you spent beside them was an hour somebody would remember later, and remember you by.

* * *

Wick found him at dusk.

This was itself an achievement, because Wick moved through the world the way smoke moved through a house — present everywhere, accountable nowhere — and Bram had once watched him steal a pie off a windowsill directly behind a constable's back. Now Wick dropped onto the stone beside him without asking, which was how Wick announced friendship, and looked at the paper in Bram's hands with eyes that missed nothing and pretended to miss everything.

"Writing your memoirs?" Wick said. "*Deserter: My Story*, by a man who ran away. I'd buy it."

"It's a letter."

"To whom? Your mother? Because 'Dear Ma, sorry about the deserting' is a hard letter to write. I've drafted it eleven times myself."

"It's not for my mother."

Wick was quiet for a moment. Down in the hollow, Orsen had finally taken the griddle away from Sera, and the two of them were arguing about salt with the intensity of people discussing theology.

"I know what it is," Wick said, low. "Corveth's man found me in Meridian too. Six weeks back. Same offer, different currency — he has my brother in the Whitecells. I said yes too." He picked a pebble up and threw it into the dark. "Then I met him. The taster. And I started sending Corveth reports about a man who doesn't exist. Tall fellow. Red beard. Terrible with directions. You seen him?"

"No," Bram said. "Can't say I have."

"Pity. He sounds useful." Wick stood, brushed off his knees, and paused. "For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is nothing, coming from me — the longer you wait to decide, the more the deciding does itself. That's not wisdom. That's just how rot works."

He drifted off toward the fire, and Bram sat alone with his letter and the pebble-throwing dark, feeling like the last honest man in a country of liars, which was a bad position, because the honest man in a country of liars is always the one holding the knife wrong.

* * *

Orsen found him near midnight.

Bram heard him coming — Orsen was quiet, but he was quiet the way careful people are quiet, and Bram had spent enough years on watch to hear careful — and he had just enough time to fold the letter into his coat and arrange his face into something innocent, which fooled no one, least of all a professional poison-taster whose entire career was detecting things people hid.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Orsen said, sitting down.

"I sleep fine. I'm sitting here recreationally."

"Mm." Orsen looked out at the valley. The moon was up, silvering the dead grass, and somewhere far off a night bird was making its argument about territory. "You talk in your sleep. Did you know that?"

"So I've been told."

"You told me about Coldmere. About Denna. About a man named Ferro who could whistle three notes at once." Orsen smiled faintly. "And about the day you all refused the oath. You described it like a wedding. Everyone standing in rows, and Veylan's herald reading the names, and Denna saying no in a voice that carried across the yard."

Bram's throat went tight. "It did carry."

"Forty-one men and women. They followed you out, some of them. Some stayed and took the oath anyway, and you don't hate them for it."

"Hating them would mean I thought I was better than them. I'm not. I ran because I was scared and they stayed because they were tired, and those are nearly the same thing."

Orsen nodded slowly, and then he was quiet for a while, and Bram knew — the way you know weather coming — that something was gathering.

"There's something I should show you," Orsen said. "About what I am. What's inside me. Sera calls it the crown's memory. Three hundred years of kings and queens, stored up in—" he tapped his own chest, awkwardly, like a man pointing at someone else's luggage— "me. I can't always control it. Sometimes it comes whether I want it or not. But sometimes I can open a door and let a piece of it out. Share it. I've only done it twice, and both times it hurt the other person a little, so I want you to say yes first. Properly. Whatever's in your coat pocket can stay in your coat pocket. This isn't a trade."

Bram's heart was doing something unprofessional. "What would I see?"

"That depends on you. The crown keeps everything it ever touched. Every life that passed through the royal line, every soldier who served under their banner, every—" Orsen hesitated. "Every man who died at Coldmere's sister garrison at Harrow Ford, eight years ago, wearing the old king's colors. The crown remembers them. It remembers all of them."

The night bird stopped singing.

"Ferro," Bram said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like a road after frost. "Ferro died at Harrow Ford."

"I know," Orsen said gently. "I've been carrying him for weeks without knowing whose he was. He came up in a memory two nights ago and he wouldn't leave, and I finally understood why. He's yours. He was always going to be yours." He held out his hand. "Do you want to see him?"

Every sensible part of Bram said no. Sensible Bram — the Bram who had written a letter to an inquisitor, the Bram who had learned that survival meant keeping your hands empty and your debts paid — that Bram said no, absolutely not, you do not open doors in yourself for a stranger's magic, you especially do not do it the same night you've agreed to sell that stranger.

But Bram had also spent eight years not thinking about Ferro's laugh, which was a whistling laugh, three notes at once, because Ferro couldn't do anything simply, and he was so tired of not thinking about it.

"Yes," Bram said.

Orsen took his hand. His grip was cool and dry, a kitchen man's grip, and for a moment nothing happened at all, and Bram almost laughed with relief—

—and then the valley was gone.

He was standing in mud. Rain fell sideways, gray and endless, and the air tasted of iron and burnt hair, and around him stretched the wreckage of a ford crossing — broken wagons, drowned horses, banners soaked black. And there, wading through the shallows with a wounded man over his shoulders, laughing — actually *laughing*, three-note laugh, rain streaming down his face — was Ferro. Twenty-four years old forever. Alive in the way memory makes things alive, more alive than he'd ever been in life, every detail sharp as a blade: the gap in his teeth, the scar on his knuckles from the card game in Yearsend, the way he shouted, over the rain, *"Halloway! Tell my ma the dice were rigged!"*

"He never said that," Bram heard himself whisper, inside the memory, outside it, both at once. "He won fair. He just liked blaming the dice."

"He knows," Orsen's voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, gentle as banked coals. "He remembers you telling him that. Every time. He kept it."

And Ferro — the memory-Ferro, the true one, the one the crown had held safe for eight years like a coin in a closed fist — turned and looked straight at Bram, and grinned, and said, clear across the years and the rain:

*"Told you somebody'd keep score, Hal."*

Then it was gone. The mud, the rain, the boy. Bram was sitting on cold stone in the Ashen Valley with wet tracks on his face and Orsen's hand still wrapped around his, and the night bird had started up again, and the moon hadn't moved, and none of it mattered, because Ferro had looked at him. Ferro had *seen* him.

"He's in there," Bram said. His voice cracked down the middle. "He's really in there. All this time he's been — somebody's been keeping him."

"Not somebody," Orsen said. "Me. Without knowing it. That's what I am, Bram. I'm not a prince. I'm not a weapon. I'm a shelf. And everything on this shelf belongs to someone, and most of those someones are dead, and the ones who aren't—" he looked at Bram steadily— "deserve to get their people back. That's what I'm carrying to Meridian. Not a crown. A ledger of everyone the old world owes."

Bram sat with that. He sat with it for a long time, and the letter in his coat pressed against his ribs like a coal.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: forty-one living soldiers at Coldmere against one thin man on a stone who kept the dead. Here was Denna's chapped hands and the boys coughing in the barracks and winter coming. Here was Ferro, laughing in the rain, kept safe in a stranger's chest for eight years because the world was cruel and something in it was kind anyway.

And here was the thing Bram understood, sitting in the dark, that he should have understood in Meridian six weeks ago: Corveth didn't want to free the ledger. Corveth wanted to *own* it. Put it in a room, put a king's seal on the door, and let the dead serve the living throne forever. And the first thing a throne does with a ledger of the dead is burn the pages it doesn't like.

Bram took the letter out of his coat. He unfolded it, smoothed it on his knee, and read it once more — *I know where the taster is* — in his own handwriting, in his own hand, and it looked like a stranger's.

"You don't have to tell me what that is," Orsen said quietly. "I taste for a living. I know poison when it's in the room."

"I was going to sell you." Bram said it plainly, because Ferro had looked at him, and you didn't lie to a man after that. "Amnesty for my company. Forty-one of them, stranded at Coldmere because of me. It was good arithmetic. It was the best arithmetic I ever did." He fed the corner of the letter to the cookfire's embers, watching it catch, curl, blacken. "Turns out I'm bad at math."

Orsen said nothing for a moment. Then: "Your company. We can't leave them there."

"We can't *reach* them. Coldmere's a fortress."

"Wick steals things from fortresses for breakfast." Something had come into Orsen's face — not the frightened kitchen servant, not the vessel, but something harder and warmer underneath both. "If we're carrying a ledger of everyone the old world owes, we start paying today. Write me everything you know about Coldmere. Gates, rotations, which sergeant drinks. We'll get them out."

Bram stared at him. "You'd risk all of it — the mission, Meridian, everything — for forty-one deserters?"

"For Denna," Orsen said. "Whoever she is, she raised you right, and the world needs more of whatever that was."

Bram laughed — a rough, wet, surprised sound — and reached for the charcoal.

He was halfway through sketching Coldmere's south wall when Wick materialized out of the dark with a pot of tea nobody had asked for, took one look at the drawing, one look at Bram's face, and said, "Oh good, we're doing a heist. I'll get the priestess. She'll want to bless something and set it on fire."

But far to the south, though none of them knew it yet, a rider in gray was already climbing out of the lowlands with a report in his saddlebag — a false report, red-bearded and terrible with directions — and behind that rider, patient as winter, Inquisitor Dain Corveth was reading maps of the Ashen Valley by candlelight, and circling one small mark with one steady finger.
