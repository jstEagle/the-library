## 12. The Free Cities Gate

**Wick**

Wick had been thrown out of better gates than this one.

That was what he told himself as the Meridian gate-wardens looked over their little procession—two road-dusted travelers, a priestess with ash still in her hair from three days of prayer, and a deserter who walked like a man apologizing to the ground with every step—and pronounced them, collectively, uninteresting.

"No beggars inside the walls after dusk," said the nearer warden, a woman with a jaw like a dock cleat and the eyes of someone who had heard every story ever invented.

"We're not beggars," Wick said. "We're merchants."

"Of what?"

"Recipes," said Orsen.

The warden looked at him. Orsen looked back with that particular blankness he wore like armor, the one Wick had privately named the Kitchen Face—the face of a man who had tasted forty poisons and found none of them interesting. It worked on kings' servants. It did not work on dock-cleat jaws.

"Recipes," she repeated.

"Heirloom ones," Sera added brightly. "Very old family. Very old."

Bram made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

What saved them was not wit but coin—or rather, the absence of it, which made them beneath suspicion. The wardens waved them through the customs arch with the contempt reserved for the poor, and then they were inside Meridian, and Wick forgot to be nervous for a full ten seconds because the city rose up around him like a held breath finally let out.

He had smuggled through a dozen cities, but Meridian was different. It was built upward instead of outward, terraces stacked on terraces, white stone gone gold in the afternoon sun, canals cutting through everything so the whole place seemed to float on its own reflection. Banners hung from every balcony—not one king's colors, but hundreds of guild flags, house flags, temple flags, a riot of loyalties that canceled each other out into something like freedom.

"Well," Bram said quietly. "It's real."

"You thought we'd imagined it?" Sera asked.

"I thought the free cities were a story soldiers tell each other. Like the ones about mermaids." He squinted up at a terrace where a fishmonger was arguing cheerfully with three customers at once. "This is better than mermaids."

Orsen said nothing. That was normal now. Since the valley, since his mother's recipe book, since the memories had started arriving like weather, Orsen had taken to silence the way other men took to drink—steadily, and with visible relief. But Wick had learned to read the silences. This one had an edge to it.

"What is it?" he asked, low.

"The last time I stood in a city like this—" Orsen stopped. Closed his eyes briefly. "Not me. The crown. Queen Maren, before the war. She stood on a wall like this and watched banners, and she thought, *they will all burn if we are foolish.*" His voice came out flat, recitation-flat. "I don't know why it picks moments. It doesn't ask."

Sera touched his sleeve, light as a moth. "Because you're listening now. That's not the same as being used."

"It feels the same from the inside."

Wick clapped him on the shoulder, hard enough to make it a joke, soft enough to make it not one. "Come on, your majesty's memory. We've got a council to annoy."

* * *

**Sera**

Getting an audience with the Council of Meridian turned out to be harder than crossing half of Veylan's kingdom, which Sera found both absurd and deeply reassuring. A realm where bureaucracy could stop a prophet was a realm worth saving.

Three days of petitioning. Three days of waiting rooms that smelled of ink and old carpet. On the first day they were told the council sat only on even-numbered weeks. On the second day, an under-clerk informed them that petitions regarding "dynastic or thaumaturgic matters" required sponsorship by a guild member in good standing. On the third day, Wick vanished for four hours and returned smelling faintly of canal water and triumph, holding a letter of introduction signed by a spice merchant named Odo Ferran, whom Wick described as "a business associate" and whose seal Sera suspected had been borrowed rather than given.

"Wick," she said.

"I'll return it. Eventually. To someone. Possibly Odo." He grinned at her, and Sera felt her resolve soften exactly as much as she allowed it to, which was some, but not all.

She worried most about Orsen during those days. Not because of the memories—though she caught him twice standing very still in the middle of ordinary rooms, his lips moving around words three centuries dead—but because of how small he made himself in the council's antechambers. Shoulders drawn in. Hands folded like a servant awaiting dismissal. She wanted to shake him. She wanted to tell him: *you carried this across a kingdom; stand like it.*

But she remembered what awe felt like when it curdled, and she understood that being chosen does not feel like glory from the inside. It feels like being a cup that keeps being filled past the brim.

On the fourth morning, the doors opened.

* * *

**Bram**

Bram had faced cavalry charges with more enthusiasm than he faced the Council of Meridian.

There were nine of them seated in a crescent behind a marble balustrade, and every one of them looked like a person who had never once in their life been hungry by accident. Silk robes, oiled beards, rings worn openly—openly! In a city!—and expressions ranging from boredom to the particular alertness of merchants scenting either profit or danger.

The central figure, a broad woman with silver-shot hair introduced as First Speaker Ysolde, regarded their little band the way one regards a stain on good upholstery.

"A petition concerning the Hollow Crown," she read, from the clerk's summary. "Presented by a priestess of the old rite, a self-described merchant, a soldier of—ah." Her eyes flicked to Bram's missing rank-pins, the faded ghost of Veylan's blue on his collar where he'd picked the thread out. "A deserter. And."

"And me," said Orsen.

"Your trade?"

"I taste food. For King Veylan."

Silence. Somewhere in the crescent, a man in green actually laughed—one short bark, quickly smothered, but not quickly enough.

Ysolde leaned forward. "Child, do you understand where you are? Meridian has spent two hundred years making itself uninteresting to thrones. We do not involve ourselves in the quarrels of kings. We especially do not involve ourselves in the quarrels of *dead* kings." She set the paper down. "You bring us a story—a kitchen servant who carries the memory of a deposed dynasty. Do you know how many such stories reach us in a year? Pretenders, prophets, madmen with birthmarks shaped like crowns. Last spring a woman claimed to be Queen Maren reborn and demanded the treasury fund her fleet. She was very convincing. She also ate an entire roast goose in our presence without offering anyone else any, which settled the matter."

"Veylan's army is six weeks out," Sera said. Her voice rang against the marble, too loud, too clear. "His inquisitors ride ahead of it. When they take the coast cities, they will come here. You cannot buy distance from this war. It is already walking toward you."

"Every war walks toward us, priestess," said the man in green. "We have gotten quite good at not answering the door."

"Then answer *this*," Sera said, and stepped forward before fear could catch her hem. She had rehearsed arguments. Petitions. Proof by lineage, by cipher, by the recipe book wrapped oilcloth-tight in Orsen's pack. She had prepared all of it, and looking at those nine comfortable faces, she knew every word of it would land like rain on canvas.

So she did the thing the frightened part of her had been begging her not to do.

"My lords. My ladies. The old gods went silent three hundred years ago. Three nights ago, in your own harbor temple, my candle burned blue." She drew it from her satchel—stubby, wax-streaked, its flame burning that impossible cold color that made the nearest clerks flinch back. "They are silent no longer. And they are speaking through him." She gestured to Orsen. "I will show you. But I warn you: memory, once released, cannot be called back. What you see, you will carry."

"Is this a threat?" asked the woman in green silk beside the laughing man.

"It is a door," Sera said. "You may keep it shut. The war will open it anyway."

Ysolde's gaze moved to Orsen—measured, weighing, the look of a woman pricing a risk. "And you? What do you say, taster?"

Orsen came forward. Slowly. And to Bram's astonishment, the Kitchen Face fell away—not replaced by grandeur, nothing like that, just by a plain and terrible honesty.

"I say I didn't want any of this," Orsen said. "I say I would give it back if there were anyone to give it to. I say that whatever you decide, it will happen anyway, because it's happening whether councils agree or not. I'm not asking you to believe I'm a king." A pause. "Good thing. Because I'm not. I'm asking you to believe me about what's coming."

Another silence. Then the man in green murmured to his neighbor, "Well, he's honest, at least. That's novel."

Ysolde raised one hand—and Sera saw, with sinking clarity, that the hand was raised to dismiss, not to permit.

"Meridian thanks you for your journey. The council will deliberate on—"

"*No.*"

Sera hadn't meant to say it aloud. But it was out, ringing off the marble, and every face in the crescent turned to her, and she found—to her lasting surprise—that she wasn't afraid anymore. Fear had been her companion since the candles first burned blue. It had kept her careful. It had kept her alive.

It was not going to keep her silent.

"Forgive me, First Speaker. I have crossed a kingdom on the strength of your city's name. I have buried friends on the road. I have watched that man"—she pointed at Orsen, and her voice cracked and she let it crack, let them hear it—"carry things no one should carry, alone, because everyone who might help him was busy deliberating. So no. No more deliberation."

She took the candle and knelt, right there on the council floor, and began the Rite of Witnessing—an old, old rite, forbidden in two kingdoms, half-forgotten in the third. It required only flame and faith and a vessel willing to be seen. She looked up at Orsen.

He understood immediately. She watched him understand—watched the alarm cross his face, and then, terribly, the resignation, and then something steadier underneath both.

"You're sure?" he asked her quietly.

"No," she said honestly. "But the gods are. Apparently."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then he nodded once, and knelt across from her, and opened himself the way she had taught him on the road—not fighting the tide, but turning to meet it.

* * *

**Orsen**

It had never happened outside his skull before.

The memories had always been private weather—storms he endured alone, whisperings, flashes, a queen's dying breath in his own mouth at inconvenient hours. Even in the Ashen Valley, even with Sera's rites, the crown's remembering had stayed behind his eyes.

But the Rite of Witnessing was not built for privacy. It was built for congregations. For shared grief, shared joy, the old worship where a whole village remembered together so no one had to remember alone.

The candle flared blue-white. The chamber went dark at the edges, as though the marble itself were leaning in. And Orsen stopped holding the doors shut.

He felt it leave him like heat leaving an open oven: three hundred years pouring outward. The coronation of Aldric the First, the crown forged from a melted enemy sword. The long reigns, the marriages, the slow rot of certainty. Queen Maren on her wall, watching banners. The betrayal at Redford Bridge—General Corveth's ancestor, even then, kneeling with a smile and a knife. Famine. Feast tables. A child prince hiding in a kitchen, learning recipes from a cook who loved him more than his mother did. War. Always, at the end of every corridor of memory, war—the crown drinking deeper and deeper of the past until the living starved of it, until the gods fell silent because they refused to serve a throne that hoarded remembrance.

And he felt it land. In the council. In the guards at the doors. In the clerks frozen mid-note. Nine hundred years of dead memory settling into three hundred living minds at once, and the sound the chamber made—

The sound it made was weeping.

The man in green was on his feet, both hands pressed over his mouth. A clerk had fallen off her stool. Old Ysolde sat rigid, tears cutting clean lines down her composed face, staring at nothing—at everything—while somewhere inside her a child prince hid in a kitchen three centuries gone.

When it ebbed, when the candle guttered back to ordinary orange, the silence was total.

Then the woman beside Ysolde stood, shaking, and her voice came out with gravel in it:

"That was *propaganda*. Whatever that was—it is a weapon, and it points wherever *he* points it. You have brought a siege engine into this chamber and lit it, priestess. Half the realm will kneel to him within a season. The other half will burn everything to stop him. That is what you saw in there. That is what you gave us." Her finger trembled toward Orsen. "*War.* Dressed as a kitchen boy."

"That is what the crown always was," Sera answered, rising, weary to the bone. "I only removed the wrappings."

Ysolde lifted her hand again. This time the room waited on it properly.

"Meridian," she said slowly, "does not crown kings." A pause, and Orsen watched hope rise in Sera's face and begin to fall. "Nor does it refuse shelter to the hunted. You will have guest-quarters in the Temple Quarter. You will have bread, and beds, and the council's—consideration." Her eyes, when they met Orsen's, were not unkind. They were worse than unkind. They were afraid. "But hear me, taster. What I felt in that memory—the way it *wanted*, the way three hundred years of it reached for a throne—I will spend the rest of my life making sure nothing in this city hands it one. Deliberation continues."

The guards showed them out through a side door, gently, firmly, the way one shows out guests who might be dangerous.

In the corridor outside, Bram exhaled for what sounded like the first time in an hour. "So. That went well."

"They wept, Bram," Sera said hollowly. "Half of them wept, and half of them reached for swords, and not one of them said *yes*."

"They will," Orsen said.

They all looked at him. He was staring down the long marble hall toward the great doors, toward the city beyond, and his face had gone quiet in a new way—not the Kitchen Face, not the recitation-flatness of memory, but something resolved and calm and slightly terrible.

"How do you know?" Wick asked.

Orsen turned. And smiled, small and crooked and utterly unlike himself.

"I don't," he said. "But she does."

Behind them, at the far end of the hall, a woman in travel-stained grey was walking toward them at speed—a woman Orsen had never seen, carrying a sealed letter bearing the broken-sword sigil of House Corveth—and she was smiling too.
