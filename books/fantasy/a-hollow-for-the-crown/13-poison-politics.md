## 13. Poison Politics

The Council of Meridian had three ways of looking at a man, and I learned all of them in a single afternoon.

The first way was the way a merchant looks at grain—weighing, appraising, calculating what you'd fetch and when to sell. That was Chancellor Ovelin's look. He was a round, comfortable man with rings on every finger except the one that mattered, which he kept bare so people would remember he was a man of the people, or so he believed people believed. He smiled at me the way a man smiles at a very promising pig.

"Orsen Ashgrove," he said, as though tasting the name. "The Taster of Veylan. You understand, of course, that Meridian is a city of free citizens. We have no kings here."

"No," I said.

"But we do have *traditions*," he said, "and one of them is that when the old blood walks among us, we honor it. Publicly. Ceremonially." He leaned forward, rings clicking against the table like small, greedy teeth. "You would stand beside us at the equinox assembly. Say nothing—or say very little, we'll draft something suitably humble—and let the people see that the crown's heir stands with the free cities. Morale, you understand. The legions are coming, and frightened people want a banner."

"I'm not a banner," I said.

"Of course not," said Chancellor Ovelin, in the tone of a man agreeing that a horse is not, technically, a cart.

The second way of looking at a man belonged to Lady Vess of the Salt Quarter, who sat across the chamber with her hands folded and her eyes doing the work of a butcher counting cuts. She hadn't spoken yet. She just watched me drink my tea—which, I noted, a servant had poured from a fresh pot brought in after I arrived, from a direction no one had explained—and she watched me the way you watch a door you've rigged.

I'd been a food-taster for nine years. I knew what a rigged door looked like.

"You survived Corveth's inquisitors," she said at last. Her voice was dry as old rope. "You survived the crossing through Veylan's patrols. And they say—" she paused delicately, "—they say things about your tongue."

"They say many things," I said. "Most of them started with Wick, who exaggerates."

Across the chamber, Wick—who had insisted on attending because, quote, "someone has to watch these vultures and it might as well be a professional"—pressed a hand to his chest in wounded innocence. He was dressed in his best stolen finery, which is to say finery so well made no one could prove it was stolen, and he was eating the council's candied figs with the confidence of a man who had already checked them for poison by eating six.

"The third thing," Lady Vess said, rising, gathering her documents, "is that Meridian remembers what crowns cost. Whatever you are, Master Ashgrove—vessel, prince, kitchen boy—we will be watching what you become." She paused at the door. "We are all watching."

Which was, I reflected later, the most honest sentence anyone spoke to me that day.

* * *

Sera found me on the council hall steps afterward, sitting in the late sun with my head between my knees, which is not a posture they teach princes but is an excellent one for tasters.

"How bad?" she asked, sitting down beside me. She smelled of candle smoke and the particular soap the temple used, which smelled like someone had tried to make soap out of hope.

"The fat one wants me to be a flagpole," I said. "The scary one wants me dead or useful, and hasn't decided which order."

"That's politics," Sera said. "Bram told me about politics once. He said it's like a knife fight in a sack, except everyone insists it's a dance."

"Bram," I said, "has been surprisingly quotable since he stopped trying to sell us."

Sera laughed, then sobered, turning her cup of nothing in her hands. Below us, the lower city sprawled toward the harbor, all slate roofs and drying nets and the smell of fish and tar that Meridian wore like a perfume it had stopped apologizing for. Somewhere beyond the eastern hills, Veylan's legions were marching, and everyone in this city could feel it the way you feel weather in an old scar.

"They're going to use you," she said quietly. "Both factions. The ones who want you crowned and the ones who want you gone—they both need you to be a *thing*, Orsen. A symbol. Symbols don't get to be tired, or scared, or wrong."

"I know," I said. "I spent nine years being invisible. It turns out visibility is worse."

"It really is," said Wick, arriving with a napkin full of contraband figs. "I was visible once, in Kell's Landing. Never again." He sat down on my other side, completing some kind of formation, and offered me a fig. "Eat. Council figs are the only honest thing in that building."

I ate the fig. It was excellent. For a moment, the three of us just sat there in the sun, and I thought: *this*. This is the thing worth keeping. Not the crown, not the memory, not the name the streets were starting to whisper. Just this—two people who stayed.

Then a bell began ringing in the harbor district, the wrong kind of bell, the fast kind, and the moment went the way moments do.

* * *

The equinox assembly came anyway, because assemblies always do.

Chancellor Ovelin got his wish. There I stood on the marble dais of the Assembly Hall, dressed in clothes Bram's tailor friend had made—"humble but significant," the brief had read, which is a phrase that should appear on no garment ever—and beside me a herald read out a proclamation about unity and the old blood and the defense of the free cities, and I said the eleven words I'd been given to say, and the crowd in the galleries roared.

They roared for me. Not for the proclamation. Not for the council. For me—the kitchen boy with the dead king's memories rattling around in his skull like coins in a dropped purse.

I hated it. I want to be clear about that, because later, when the stories got told, people assumed I stood there glowing. I did not glow. I stood there sweating in humble-but-significant linen, scanning the hall out of nine years of habit, cataloguing exits, watching the servants' door, noting which cup-bearer's hands were steady and which weren't.

Because that was the other thing about being visible. When everyone looks at you, you can't see who's looking too hard.

It was the wine.

The toast was ceremonial—a single cup raised to the assembly, drunk before ten thousand witnesses, because nothing says *unity* like drinking from the same cup as a man you don't trust. The cup-bearer was a young woman with steady hands and a bored face, and the wine was Meridian red, sharp and dark, and I took it because refusing a toast is its own kind of proclamation and I'd had enough proclamations.

Nine years of training said: *swirl, smell, coat the tongue, wait.*

But ten thousand people were watching, and waiting was not on the program.

So I drank.

For three heartbeats, nothing. The wine was good—cherry and iron and something smoky—and then the something smoky bloomed, and my tongue went cold, and cold climbed the back of my throat like water running the wrong direction up a pipe.

*Hemlock-and-something,* said the ledger in my head, the one written in scars and survived doses. *Hemlock base, cut with something I don't know. Something new.*

Something new was bad. Something new meant someone had gone to trouble. Trouble meant intent, and intent meant—

The hall tilted. The roar of the crowd went strange and distant, like the sea heard through a wall. My knees informed me they were considering resignation.

And then the crown woke up.

I still don't have better words for it. The memory that lives in me—it stirred, the way a great animal stirs, and something old moved through my blood alongside the poison, and the cold stopped. Didn't fade. *Stopped*, met at the border of my heart by something older than any poison ever brewed in any kitchen, and turned back.

Three hundred years of kings had drunk from hostile cups. Apparently some of that had soaked in.

I didn't fall. I set the cup down on the dais rail—carefully, precisely, the way you do when falling would start a riot—and I stood there while my vision swam and cleared, and the hall went quiet in that peculiar rolling way silence does through a crowd, gallery by gallery, as ten thousand people realized simultaneously that something had happened and nobody knew what.

Lady Vess was on her feet. So was half the council. Ovelin was saying something about a fainting spell, a long day, the excitement—

"He's been poisoned," I said.

My voice carried. I don't know how; I'm not a loud man. But the hall was straining toward me like a field of wheat toward wind, and every word landed.

"The wine. Hemlock, and something under it—I don't know the under-thing. Whoever made it wanted it to look like hemlock failure. Like my body finally quit." I picked up the cup and held it up, and my hand, thank every god listening and several who weren't, was steady. "I know poisons the way you know your children's faces. This was meant for me."

The silence lasted four seconds. Then the hall detonated.

I will not give you the whole of what happened next, partly because much of it was shouting, and partly because some of it I spent doubled over behind the dais with Sera holding my shoulders and Wick crouched in front of me saying, "Don't die, don't die, I owe you money, dying debtors haunt people, it's known"—which, for the record, is not a real superstition, he invented it on the spot, and it worked anyway, because laughing and retching cannot occupy a body at the same time.

What matters is this: they tested the cup. They tested the cup, and then they tested the cup-bearer, who wept and swore she'd drawn the wine from the sealed cask assigned to the ceremony, a cask that had passed through the cellars of exactly one council faction on its way to the hall. Lady Vess's faction. Though, as Wick pointed out cheerfully that evening, "sealed casks pass through a lot of cellars if you pay cellar-men, and paying cellar-men is the second-oldest profession."

The point is not who did it. The point is what the crowd saw.

The crowd saw a kitchen boy raise a poisoned cup before the assembled city, drink, name the poison, and stand unbowed. The crowd did not see a terrified taster leaning on a dais rail doing arithmetic with his own heartbeat. Crowds never see that part. Crowds see the story they need.

By nightfall, they were calling me the Hollow Prince.

* * *

I heard the name first from a child.

We'd gone down to the harbor market two days later—Sera's idea, something about salt for the rites, though I suspected she mostly wanted me walking and breathing open air instead of pacing the safehouse like a cat in a box. Bram came along as escort, which meant he walked six feet behind us radiating the specific menace of a large man pretending to shop for turnips.

The child was selling roasted chestnuts near the fish stalls, maybe eight years old, gap-toothed, with her mother's shawl wrapped twice around her. She looked up at me as I passed, and her eyes went wide, and she grabbed her mother's sleeve and said—not whispered, *said*, at market volume:

"Mama! Mama, it's him! It's the Hollow Prince! He drank the poison!"

And the mother looked, and the fishmonger looked, and the net-mender looked, and within the space of ten heartbeats a quiet ring of people had formed around me, not touching, just *looking*, the way you look at a relic or a storm cloud, and an old woman pressed a dried flower into my hand and said, "Bless you, Highness, bless you for standing," and I stood there holding a dead flower and a title I had never asked for, smiling the smile I'd learned in Veylan's court, the one that means *please stop* in every language.

In the Ashen Valley, where magic cannot follow, I had been nobody's anything. In Veylan's palace, I had been furniture that tasted soup. Now I was a *prince*—a hollow one, whatever that meant to them, and I suspected it meant everything and nothing, which is the worst kind of meaning.

That night I sat on the safehouse roof with Sera, because the roof was where we talked, and because Bram had declared the stairs structurally suspicious and refused to come up, which left the roof to those of us with fewer survival instincts.

"Hollow Prince," I said. "Say it back to me. I want to hear how stupid it sounds out loud."

"It doesn't sound stupid," Sera said. "It sounds true. The crown is hollow—that's what you told the council, that's what the memory says, the throne that ate its own gods. And you're the one carrying it. Hollow Prince." She shrugged. "People are better at naming things than councils are."

"People are going to get me killed with better names than councils would have."

"Probably," she agreed, comfortably.

I pulled my knees up. Below us, Meridian glimmered—ten thousand lamps, ten thousand suppers, and somewhere out past the hills, torchlines that were Veylan's army getting closer by the mile. "Sera. What happens when a symbol stops doing what the crowd wants? What happens when the Hollow Prince says something true and inconvenient?"

She was quiet a moment. Candle-smoke and harbor wind.

"Symbols that disappoint get broken," she said finally. "Or worshipped harder. Usually both at once." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Good thing you're not actually a symbol. Good thing you're just Orsen, who burns the rice when he's worried."

"I do not burn the rice."

"Twice last week."

"Once was Wick's fault. He distracted me with a story about a bishop and a goat."

"It's a good story," said Wick's voice from below, muffled by the roof hatch. "Also, visitors. Downstairs. Council seal on the letter, and the messenger has that face people get when they've practiced being polite in a mirror."

I closed my eyes. Somewhere in my chest, the crown's memory shifted its weight, patient as tide.

"Tell them," I said, standing, "that the Hollow Prince will receive his correspondence after supper."

"And if they object?"

"Tell them the Hollow Prince gets *terrible* without supper. It's in all the stories."

But it wasn't a summons that waited downstairs. It was a warning—neat handwriting, no signature, delivered on council paper that anyone could buy: *They know the poison failed. They will try what does not fail. Watch the ones closest to you.*

I read it twice. Then I looked up at Wick, who was reading over my shoulder, and whose face had gone, for the first time since I'd known him, completely unreadable.

"Wick," I said slowly. "Is there something you want to tell me?"
