## 14. What Wick Carries

Wick had stolen a great many things in his life. Candlesticks, obviously. A bishop's boots, which had been a personal triumph. An entire shipment of candied plums that he'd eaten himself in a fit of spite and then reported stolen, which had required three days of committed lying and was, he still maintained, worth every minute of it.

He had never stolen anything like this. He had never carried anything like this.

It sat in his chest now like a stone swallowed whole, and it had been sitting there since Meridian, growing heavier by the day, and tonight—watching Orsen laugh at something Bram had said across the kitchen fire, actually laugh, that startled, unpracticed laugh of a man who'd never had much cause for it—Wick decided he couldn't carry it anymore.

He just hadn't expected the confession to ambush him in the middle of a card game.

"Your turn," Sera said, nudging him with her elbow. She was winning. Sera always won, which Wick suspected had less to do with skill than with the fact that she could tell when people were bluffing by some priestly sixth sense, which was frankly cheating.

Wick looked at his cards. He looked at Orsen, who was losing with the serene dignity of a man who'd survived worse than a bad hand. He looked at Bram, who was cheating, badly, and whom nobody had called out on it because Bram's cheating was the only thing keeping the game interesting.

"Actually," Wick said, "I need to tell you all something, and I'd rather do it before I win this hand, because if I win it after you'll think I was softening you up."

Sera set down her cards. "That's ominous."

"It's very ominous," Wick agreed. His mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, because he'd once delivered a speech to a room full of armed smugglers while bleeding from a head wound, and his mouth had been fine then. "It's about Orsen. And me. And Inquisitor Dain Corveth."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, Meridian's night bells rang the third hour, and a drunk sang two streets over with the confidence of a man who had never once been told he couldn't sing.

Orsen's face did the thing it always did—the stillness, the taster's stillness, the face he wore when a dish might be the dish. "Go on," he said quietly.

* * *

Here was the thing about Wick Fenn, and he'd told this story to himself so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone: he had a brother. Tam. Younger by two years, dumber by a wide margin, currently residing in the Saltcells beneath the Inquisitors' Hall in Veylan's capital on charges of smuggling, sedition, and—this was the one that made Wick's blood boil every time—*impertinence to a servant of the Crown*, which wasn't even a real crime, it was just a magistrate with a grudge and a quill.

Corveth had come to Wick's cell—not a cell, an interview room, Corveth always insisted on calling it an interview room, which was somehow worse—and laid it out with the terrible gentleness he used on everyone, like a man breaking bad news to a relative.

*Your brother hangs in forty days. Unless.*

*Unless what?*

*There's a taster in the palace. Ashgrove. I want to know what he eats, who he speaks to, what he dreams about when he sleeps. You have access to the kitchens. You'll have access to him.*

And Wick, who had never in his life been able to refuse a deal that kept someone he loved breathing, had said yes.

"That's the whole of it," Wick said now, to the fire, because he couldn't look at Orsen's face yet. "I scouted you. Before the tunnels, before the soup course, before any of it. Corveth wanted a report on the taster who wasn't dying, and I was the man who could get close to you. I watched you for three weeks before I ever 'accidentally' bumped into you in the pantry. The saffron theft was a performance. I'm not even that fond of saffron. It tastes like hay that's been to university."

Nobody laughed. Wick had been counting on somebody laughing. Bram, of all people, usually reliable for a snort.

"The first reports were true," Wick went on, because stopping now would be worse. "What you ate. Who you talked to. That you were quiet, and careful, and that you had a way of standing in a room like you were trying to be furniture. Corveth liked that. He said furniture was easy to move." He swallowed. "And then you covered for me. You'd known me a day, and you lied to the under-steward with your face perfectly smooth, and you walked me out through the herb garden, and you said—" his voice did something humiliating here, cracked, and he pushed through it— "you said, *nobody watches the man carrying something.* And I went back to my room and I wrote my report and I lied. I lied about everything. I told Corveth you were dull. I told him you were superstitious and greedy and that you watered the wine when the steward wasn't looking, which, for the record, you never did, you're the only honest man in any kitchen I've ever worked. And I've kept lying ever since. Every report. False. Every one."

He finally made himself look up.

Orsen was watching him with that stillness, but it was different now—not the taster's stillness. Something rawer underneath it. Sera had gone pale. Bram had stopped cheating, which was how you knew things were serious.

"The brother," Orsen said at last. "Tam. Is he—"

"Alive. Corveth kept him breathing because I kept reporting. It's the only reason he's alive. My lies bought his days, one at a time." Wick laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the joke of it, isn't it? The false reports are the only true service I've ever done. Corveth thinks I'm his best asset in Meridian. He thinks I'm sitting at the heart of your little group, feeding him everything. And I am sitting at the heart of it. I just haven't fed him anything worth a damn since the day we left the city."

* * *

The silence afterward was the bad kind. Wick knew silences the way a fisherman knew water, and this one had hooks in it.

It was Bram who broke it, because Bram had a talent for saying the simple thing at the worst moment. "So when we were in the tunnels, and you knew the guard rotations—"

"From Corveth's own briefings, yes."

"And the safehouse in the tannery district—"

"Corveth's list. I gave us the one he'd already burned."

Sera stood up. She didn't storm out; Sera didn't storm, she *withdrew*, which was worse, like watching a candle get carried out of a room. She paused at the edge of the firelight and turned back, and her voice was very quiet. "Did you ever consider telling us before tonight?"

"Every day since the soup course."

"And you chose tonight because—"

"Because tomorrow Corveth's riders reach the city, and if I'm going to be a liar, I'd rather be an honest one about it first." He winced. "That sounded better in my head."

"It usually does," Sera said, and she left, and the door closed softly, which was the worst possible way for a door to close.

Bram looked between them, gathered his cards with the dignity of a man retreating from a battlefield, and mumbled something about checking on the horses, though they had exactly one horse and Wick could see it from the window.

And then it was just the two of them, and the fire, and the stone in Wick's chest that had finally, finally cracked open, and he discovered that a cracked stone was somehow heavier than a whole one.

* * *

Orsen didn't speak for a long time. Wick had learned that about him—learned it, he realized now, during those three weeks of "scouting," which meant he'd learned it while betraying him, which put a poison in every fond memory he had of their early acquaintance. He kept doing that, kept picking up a warm thing and finding Corveth's fingerprints on it.

"You could have run," Orsen said finally. "After the soup course. After Sera. You knew what was coming for me. You knew what Corveth wanted. You could have walked out of Meridian with your reports finished and your brother's neck safe and never looked back."

"Tam would've looked back. He'd have haunted me specifically. He's very petty."

"Wick."

"I know." Wick rubbed his face. "I know. No. I couldn't have run. And I hate that I couldn't, because I'm a professional, Orsen, I have run from things my entire life, it's the one skill I've perfected, and you—" he gestured helplessly at all of him— "you ruined it. You handed a starving man saffron you couldn't afford to lose. Who *does* that? I spent three weeks building a profile of you for the most dangerous man in the kingdom and the entire profile was wrong. I wrote 'opportunistic' and you were generous. I wrote 'detached' and you covered for a stranger because it was the decent thing. I wrote 'no attachments' and then I watched you start collecting us. Bram. Sera. Me. Like a man picking up strays in a rainstorm. And every report I sent after that was me lying to Corveth about who you are, and the horrible thing, the truly horrible thing—" his voice went again, damn it— "is that lying to Corveth about you was the first time in my life I ever felt like a good man. You made lying feel like virtue. Do you understand how infuriating that is?"

Orsen was quiet a moment longer. Then, in the tone he used for tasting verdicts—flat, final, unadorned—he said, "You sold me."

"Yes."

"And then you spent months buying me back."

Wick blinked. "That's... one way to put it."

"It's the only way to put it. The ledger has to balance, Wick. You taught me that, actually. Back in the capital. 'Everything's a ledger,' you said, 'you just have to know which column you're in.'" Orsen turned his hands over, palms up, the way he did when he was looking at something invisible. "I've been carrying the crown's memory for months. I know what betrayal looks like from the inside. I've felt queens betrayed by brothers, kings by priests, whole armies sold for a river crossing. And none of it—" he stopped, and started again. "None of it feels like this. This feels like a man who got caught in something too big for him and chose his brother, and then chose us, and has been choking on it ever since. That's not a betrayal. That's a debt. And I've eaten worse things than debts."

"You have," Wick said weakly. "I've watched you."

That got a ghost of a smile out of him, and Wick grabbed it like a drowning man grabs a rope.

* * *

Sera came back near dawn. Wick heard the door and didn't turn around—he was sitting where he'd been sitting all night, next to Orsen, who had fallen asleep sitting up at some point, the way he did, like sleep was a dish he only ate when nobody was watching.

Sera stood over them. Wick braced.

"Tam," she said. "Your brother. What's his full name?"

"...Tammas Fenn. Why?"

"Because I light candles for people," Sera said. "It's what I do. I've been lighting one for a smuggler's brother in the Saltcells for two months without knowing his name, because you mentioned him once, drunk, in the tunnels, and I remember everything people say when they're hurting." She crouched down so they were eye to eye. "I'm still angry with you. I'm going to be angry with you for a while, and you're going to deserve it, and you're going to sit there and take it. But I'm not angry because you lied to us. I'm angry because you carried it alone when you didn't have to. That's the sin, Wick. Not the spying. The carrying."

She stood, and paused, and added, "Also you cheat at cards, which I've known for weeks, and I let you win sometimes out of pity, so we're even."

She went to her room. Wick sat there in the gray light, feeling like a man who'd been flayed and bandaged in the same motion.

Orsen, eyes still closed, said: "She let you win?"

"Every hand. I'm devastated."

"I never let you win."

"No," Wick said, and found he was grinning like an idiot, wet-eyed and grinning, "you really don't."

* * *

He wrote the report that morning. His last one, he decided—the last lie he'd ever send down the line to Dain Corveth. He sat at the kitchen table with the good paper, the paper reserved for important falsehoods, and he wrote:

*The vessel grows stronger. The council fears him. The priestess has bound herself to him completely. He will not run when your army comes. He will stand on the plain before Meridian and do something none of us expect. Come quickly. Come yourself. And when you see him, Inquisitor—look at his face, and remember that I told you first: you are not bringing a prisoner home. You are walking into a memory three hundred years deep, and it has been waiting for you.*

He sealed it, and sent it by the usual dead-drop, and came back to find Orsen at the stove, cooking, which he did when his head was loud.

"Feel lighter?" Orsen asked, not turning around.

"Like a man who's set down a stone and picked up a debt instead."

"Debts get paid," Orsen said. "Stones just sit there." He handed Wick a bowl of something with eggs and the last of the good cheese, and Wick ate it, and it was the best thing he'd ever tasted, and he was almost—almost—foolish enough to think the worst was behind them.

Then Bram burst through the door with his boots still on and his face the color of ash, and said the words that ended the quiet forever:

"Riders on the south road. Veylan's colors. And Wick—" he swallowed— "they're flying the Inquisitor's standard at the front. Corveth's not coming with the army. Corveth's coming *ahead* of it. He'll be at the gates by nightfall."
