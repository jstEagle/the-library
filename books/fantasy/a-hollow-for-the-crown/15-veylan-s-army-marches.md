## 15. Veylan's Army Marches

The pigeon arrived at dawn, and Wick knew before anyone opened the message tube that it was bad news, because good news never traveled that fast.

He was on the roof of the safehouse with a stolen cup of tea—he'd stopped pretending he hadn't stolen it; Orsen had started leaving cups out for him, which was either kindness or a very subtle insult—and he watched the courier bird spiral down onto the council spire like a stone dropped into still water. Within an hour, the ripples reached everywhere. By midday, Meridian's markets had doubled their prices for flour and tripled them for rope. Fear had a flavor, Wick thought. He'd grown up tasting it. It tasted like overpriced rope.

He found out the details from a harbor clerk who owed him money, which was Wick's preferred method of finding out anything.

"Three legions," the clerk said, not looking up from his ledger. "Crossed the Vell at Marrow Ford. That's what the bird said. Corveth's riding ahead of them with—"

"With how many?"

"Does it matter? One of him is enough."

Wick bought two apples with the clerk's own coin—lifted back off him during the handshake, a small art form—and went to find his people.

His people. When had they become that? Six months ago his only obligations were to himself, his brother in a Veylani cell, and the general principle that other people's property was simply property awaiting redistribution. Now he had a priestess who hummed while she prayed, a deserter who cried at songs, and a former food-taster who was slowly becoming either a king or a weapon or both, depending on which faction you asked, and everyone in Meridian was asking.

The walls were closing in. Wick could feel it the way he felt a pickpocket's hand in a crowd—not seeing it, just knowing.

* * *

Sera heard the news in the temple of the Ninefold Flame, where she had gone to argue with her superiors and instead found them already arguing about her.

"—cannot be permitted to perform another public rite," Mother Cassarine was saying as Sera came through the door. She didn't stop talking, which was its own insult. "The girl has made our order a political instrument. The council now believes the old gods have opinions on succession."

"The old gods *do* have opinions on succession," said Brother Elam mildly. "That is rather the point of gods."

"The old gods have been silent for three hundred years, and there was peace in that silence—"

"There was a usurper on a stolen throne, Mother. I would not call it peace. I'd call it a held breath."

Sera stood in the doorway with her hands folded and waited, because interrupting elders was a sin, but standing silently until they noticed you was merely annoying, and annoyance was practically a sacrament among the stubborn.

Mother Cassarine noticed her eventually. The temple went quiet in that particular way rooms go quiet around Sera lately—as if the Flame itself were leaning in to listen.

"You've heard," Cassarine said. Not a question.

"About the legions? Yes, Mother. About half the order wanting to crown Orsen Ashgrove and the other half wanting to cut his throat before he can be crowned?" Sera let that sit. "Also yes. You all talk loudly near windows."

Cassarine's jaw tightened. "There are those among us who believe the vessel should be brought to the Flame and *ended* gently, before the crown's memory can be used as a banner. A war fought under the Hollow Crown would not end this time. It would burn until nothing was left to remember anything."

"And there are those who believe he should be enthroned immediately, crowned in the old rite, so the realm has a true king when Veylan arrives." Sera took a breath. "And did anyone, in all these loud discussions near windows, ask the vessel what *he* wants?"

Brother Elam smiled faintly. "You have met the council, child. Asking people what they want is not how power works."

"Then perhaps," Sera said, and felt the candle-flame behind her flare blue at the edges, felt every head turn toward it, "it is time someone taught Meridian how gods work. Because mine has an opinion. And it is not silence, and it is not a knife. It is waiting. On him. On his choice. Which means if any of you touch him before he chooses—" she looked at Mother Cassarine, and her voice went gentle, which was worse than shouting, "—you will be answering to something considerably older than this order."

She left before the blue flame faded, because she'd learned that exits were best made while people were still afraid of you.

Outside, in the gray afternoon light, her hands were shaking. They always shook after the god used her mouth. She pressed them flat against her skirts and thought: *weeks. We have weeks.* And then, honestly, the way prayer demanded honesty:

*Please let it be enough.*

* * *

Dain Corveth read maps the way other men read scripture: completely, and without comfort.

The map spread across his campaign table showed the Vell crossing at Marrow Ford, the supply lines threading back south like veins, and Meridian sitting on its peninsula at the top of the page—fat, rich, confident behind walls that had never been breached. He moved a wooden marker three finger-widths north. Three legions. His lord's answer to one kitchen servant.

"He'll run," said Captain Vess from the tent flap. She was chewing something that had once been jerky. "The taster. Every report says he's soft. Kitchen-soft. Men like that run."

Corveth did not look up. "Every report also says he survived poisons that kill in a heartbeat, carried the memory of a dead dynasty across half a kingdom, and talked Bram Tallow—a man who once sold his own sergeant to save his skin—into loyalty. Does that sound like kitchen-soft to you?"

Vess chewed. "Sounds like luck."

"Luck is a story people tell afterward." Corveth set down the marker. "He won't run. Running requires somewhere to run *to*, and I have spent four months making sure he has nowhere. The free cities' council fears him more than they fear Veylan—that's not my doing, that's just cowardice doing its work. His priestess's order wants him dead or crowned, and either way divided. And his friends—" here Corveth paused, and something almost like respect crossed his face, "—his friends are the only wall around him I haven't built. Yet."

"You have a plan for the friends?"

"I have a plan for everything. That is why I ride ahead." He rolled the map closed. "Send word to the garrison at Saltmere. I want the eastern road watched—not blocked, watched. If a smuggler, a deserter, or a priestess leaves Meridian alone, let them go. If they leave together, follow."

"And if the taster comes out to meet us?"

Corveth gathered his seals, his letters, his instruments of patient ruin. "Then, Captain, we will finally have a conversation worth having."

* * *

Bram heard the news secondhand, thirdhand, and then firsthand from a fishwife who shouted it into the market square with the relish of a woman who had waited her whole life for genuinely terrible news to liven things up. Three legions. Weeks away. Maybe less, if the rains held off and the roads stayed dry.

He went straight to the safehouse, because that was what Bram did now. For eleven years he had gone wherever the army pointed him, and then for six months he had gone wherever Orsen pointed, and he had discovered—slowly, embarrassingly, like a scar fading—that the second kind of going was better.

Orsen was in the kitchen. Of course he was. The boy cooked when he was frightened, the way other men drank, and the safehouse smelled like onions and rosemary and something with saffron in it that Wick would pretend not to notice was missing from someone else's pantry.

"They're coming," Bram said, without preamble. He'd learned preamble was wasted on Orsen.

"I know." Orsen didn't look up from the pot. "Wick told me an hour ago. Then Sera told me again, louder. Then a neighbor's kid told me a third time, apparently for free."

"How are you—" Bram gestured vaguely at the soup, the calm, the whole impossible boy.

"Stirring." Orsen stirred. "It's what I know how to do. Everything else—the council, the crown, the legions—it's all too big to hold. But a pot is exactly the right size for two hands." He glanced up, and his smile was thin but real. "Sit down, Bram. There's bread."

And that was the thing about him, Bram thought, taking the stool by the window where he always sat, where he could see the street. Everyone in this city wanted to make Orsen into a king or a corpse, a symbol or a sacrifice, and meanwhile he just kept feeding people. Kept making the world exactly the right size for two hands.

Bram ate his bread and watched the empty street and thought about the letter hidden under his floorboard. The amnesty offer. The one he'd refused, weeks ago, after Orsen had shown him Tomas's face—Tomas, dead two years, laughing in a memory that belonged to a crown and now, somehow, to Bram.

He'd refused. He was sure he'd refused.

But the letter was still under the floorboard, wasn't it. He hadn't burned it. And tonight, with three legions on the road and the walls closing in, Bram caught himself thinking the worst thought a man could think: *if it's all going to fall anyway, wouldn't it be better if some of us got out?*

He hated himself for it. He finished his bread anyway. It was excellent bread. That was the cruelest part.

* * *

The council convened at dusk, and Orsen attended—which was to say, he stood in the chamber and was discussed.

He had insisted on attending. "If you're going to decide whether to crown me or hand me over," he'd told the herald, "the least I can do is stand there and hear it. I spent years tasting poison for a living. I can stomach politics."

He regretted the phrasing almost immediately, because Councilor Maren of the Trade Houses stood, spread her hands, and said, "Honored members—we must consider whether delivering the vessel to King Veylan might purchase Meridian's neutrality."

The chamber murmured. Orsen tasted something familiar: the particular flavor of being weighed. He knew it from the palace, from the moment a dish was set before him and forty eyes calculated whether his death was acceptable. Some things followed you across kingdoms.

"Deliver him," scoffed Councilor Ashe of the Harbor Guild. "And then what? Veylan takes the crown's memory out of his skull, crowns himself properly, and comes for us anyway with a legitimacy we handed him gift-wrapped. Maren, you're not buying neutrality. You're buying a receipt."

"Then we arm," said General Oduya, who had been saying *arm* for a month with the enthusiasm of a woman whose entire personality was hammers. "We call the levies, we stock the walls—"

"With what money?" Maren snapped. "With what grain? The harvest convoys were seized at Saltmere—"

"By *your* trading partners—"

"Gentle councilors." This from First Speaker Lys, an old man whose voice operated at the frequency of dust settling. "Might I remind the chamber that we have not yet exhausted diplomacy. An embassy. Terms. Perhaps the young man could be... relocated. Temporarily. Somewhere the legions need not concern themselves with him."

"Relocated," Sera repeated, from the gallery, in a voice that made several councilors flinch. She hadn't been invited, but Sera treated invitations as suggestions and galleries as pulpits. "You mean exiled. You mean *give him to the road and pray*. First Speaker, the last time your generation solved a problem by sending it away, it came back as three legions."

Lys blinked at her, ancient and unbothered. "Child, everything comes back as three legions eventually. The trick of governance is being elsewhere when it arrives."

Laughter—nervous, ugly laughter—from a quarter of the chamber. Orsen felt the crown stir inside him, cold and vast, the way it did when he was angry. Memories pressed at the edge of his mind: another council, three hundred years dead, laughing the same laugh while a queen begged them to see the war coming. He breathed. He let the memories pass through without holding them. Mostly.

"First Speaker," Orsen said, and the room quieted, because whatever else he was, he was the thing they were all afraid of, and fear buys attention. "I don't want your throne. I've said so. I don't want your gold, though Councilor Maren seems to think I'm priced to move. I want one thing." He looked around the chamber, at the silk and the silver hair and the cowardice wearing robes of state. "Decide. Crown me, exile me, sell me—I will even stand still for it, because I'm tired. But *decide*. Because Veylan's army does not dither, and Inquisitor Corveth does not dither, and every day you spend weighing me like a sack of flour, they get closer."

Silence.

Then Councilor Maren, sweetly: "Noted. The chamber will take the matter under advisement."

Under advisement. Orsen had heard those words in a palace kitchen once, right before a man died of fig poisoning. He walked out of the chamber with Sera falling into step beside him, and neither of them spoke until they reached the stairs.

"Under advisement," Sera said finally.

"Mm."

"Weeks, Orsen. We have *weeks*."

"I know."

"And they're going to advise."

"I know." He rubbed his eyes. "Sera, when I was a taster, there was a rule: when you taste something wrong, you don't panic. Panic makes you swallow. You hold it in your mouth, you breathe through your nose, and you wait for the moment you can spit it out politely." He looked at her, and beneath the exhaustion there was something steady, something that had survived worse than councils. "We're holding it in our mouths. All of us. The question is who spits first."

From the tower above them, a horn sounded—one long note, then two short. The signal for a rider at the gates.

They looked at each other.

"That's the eastern road watch," Sera said quietly. "Nothing comes from the east except—"

"Saltmere," Orsen said. "The harvest convoys. Or—"

"*Him,*" they said together, and ran for the stairs, and behind them the council chamber went on advising, and ahead of them the gate horns kept sounding, closer now, like a countdown no one had agreed to start.
