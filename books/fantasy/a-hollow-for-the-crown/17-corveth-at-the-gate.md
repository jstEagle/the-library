## 17. Corveth at the Gate

Dain Corveth had breached forty cities in his career as an inquisitor, and he had learned long ago that every breach was the same: a great deal of noise arranged around a very small silence.

The noise tonight was Meridian's lower city burning its own barricades. His sappers had taken the Saltwater Gate at dusk without losing a man, which Corveth considered less a military achievement than an accounting one. The city's defenders were brave and badly supplied, which was the most expensive combination in warfare. By midnight his riders held the granary, the customs house, and the long crooked street called the Tannery Row, where the poor of Meridian lived stacked like cordwood and died the same way.

The silence was in a kitchen.

Corveth sat in the back room of a chandlery that smelled of tallow and old rope, across a scarred table from a woman named Maren who ran messages for the council and, for the last three years, for him. She was good. She was frightened. Both facts were useful.

"He's not hiding," Maren said. "That's the strange part. He cooks. Every morning, dawn bell, he's at the ovens in the Temple Ward hospice. Bread and barley soup for anyone who comes. He's been doing it since the army was sighted."

"Feeding the city he's about to doom," Corveth said.

"Feeding the city that's about to be doomed *at*," she corrected, with the pedantry he paid her for. "There's a difference. Half of them think he's a saint. Half think he's bait. They all eat his soup."

Corveth unfolded the report he'd already read twice. *The Hollow Prince feeds the poor. The Hollow Prince survived the poison of the Silver Faction publicly. The Hollow Prince laughs at the smuggler's jokes.* He found the last line the most troubling. Kings did not laugh at smugglers' jokes. Vessels did not laugh at anything. A vessel was a jar, and jars were silent.

Three hundred years ago, when the crown still sat on living heads, the vessels of the royal memory had been kept in vaults beneath the old capital. Corveth had read the surviving ledgers. Fed on honeyed milk. Washed by attendants who were forbidden to speak to them. Kept empty of everything but the crown, because a vessel full of itself could not hold anything else.

And yet this one made soup. This one had a smuggler who'd turned traitor for him and a priestess who sang at his elbow and a deserter who stood guard at his door with the posture of a man guarding his own brother.

Corveth had spent eleven months hunting Orsen Ashgrove, and somewhere in the ninth month he had stopped thinking of him as a ledger entry and started thinking of him as a problem with a shape. The shape bothered him. Problems with shapes could be reasoned with, and reasoned-with problems had a way of refusing cages.

"You can get me into the hospice?" he asked.

"I can get you to the door. After that—" Maren spread her hands. "After that you're an inquisitor standing in front of the Hollow Prince while your army sits outside the walls. I'd want better odds."

"I don't need odds. I need a conversation."

She looked at him for a long moment, and something almost like pity crossed her sharp face. "You know what they say about you in the lower city? That you burned the archive at Callowmere. That you read a man's whole life out of his spice cabinet." She stood, pulling her hood up. "They also say you're the only honest man serving Veylan. Which is worse, I think."

When she was gone, Corveth sat alone with the tallow-smell and thought about honesty, which was not a thing he practiced so much as a tool he kept sharpened.

He had been honest with himself about one thing since Callowmere: he did not serve Veylan because he believed in Veylan. He served because order was the only wall between the realm and the fire, and someone had to be the mortar, and mortars do not get to choose their walls. It was not a noble philosophy. It was a load-bearing one.

But the crown—

The crown was not order. Corveth had read enough of the old records to know what the crown actually was: a hoard. Three centuries of dead kings' memories, packed into one skull after another, each vessel ground down a little finer than the last. The old gods had gone silent rather than serve it. The first war had not been fought over territory or succession; it had been fought over whether any single mind should be allowed to hold that much of the past. And the answer, written in the ashes of four cities, had been no — and then everyone had gone ahead and done it anyway.

Veylan wanted the vessel alive because a living vessel was a weapon that pointed wherever the hand on it pointed. Corveth understood that. What Corveth understood better, and what he had never said aloud in any room where Veylan's ears might reach, was the other possibility. A vessel under strain. A vessel pushed past holding. Eleven months of reports, and in all of them the same detail recurred: the boy's nose bled when the memories came hard. The boy went grey and shook. The boy was a jar, and jars break, and nobody — not Veylan, not the council, not the priestess — seemed to have asked what was *in* the jar when it broke.

Corveth had asked. He had spent three nights with the Callowmere fragments before he burned them, and what remained legible described the last vessel of the old line, a girl of nineteen, who at the end of her life "spoke in the voices of the dead and was heard by ten thousand." Ten thousand minds, given three hundred years of crowned memory at once. The record did not say what happened to the ten thousand. Records written by survivors rarely describe the survivors honestly.

So: the deal. Come quietly. Live comfortably. Be a jar in a gilded cage, and let Dain Corveth spend the rest of his career making sure the jar never cracked near a crowd. It was not mercy. It was containment with better upholstery. But it was the only move on the board that ended with everyone alive, and Corveth had built his whole gray life on finding those moves.

He checked his blade out of habit, decided against bringing it, and went to meet the Hollow Prince.

* * *

Orsen knew the inquisitor was coming before the knock came, because Sera's candles told him.

They'd been burning blue since sundown — not the soft answering blue he'd grown used to, but a hard, jittery blue, like a held breath. "He's close," Sera said for the fourth time, pacing the hospice kitchen with a hymnal clutched to her chest like a shield. "The god is *loud* tonight, Orsen. Why is the god loud?"

"Because a man who burns archives is walking toward a building full of paper?" Wick offered from his perch on the flour sacks. He was cleaning his nails with a fishhook, which was either nerves or style; with Wick it was always both. "Also possibly because your god has dramatic timing. No offense to your god."

"None taken. He does."

Bram said nothing. Bram had said nothing since the news of the breach, just stood by the door with his arms crossed, wearing the particular stillness of a soldier counting exits. There were two. Orsen had counted them himself weeks ago, back when counting exits had felt like paranoia instead of arithmetic.

The knock, when it came, was polite. Three measured raps. Somehow that was worse than battering.

Sera looked at Orsen. Bram looked at Orsen. Wick looked at the fishhook, then at Orsen, and gave him a small shrug that meant, in the private language they'd built over months of shared bread and stolen hours, *your funeral, but I'll be nearby.*

Orsen opened the door.

Dain Corveth was smaller than Orsen had imagined, and older, and tired in a way that no amount of tailoring could hide. He wore plain black with the inquisitor's silver chain folded away out of sight, and he stood in the doorway of a charity kitchen with his hands visible and empty, and he said, "Master Ashgrove. Thank you for seeing me."

"I didn't see you. I opened a door."

"A distinction I'll allow." His eyes moved past Orsen — cataloguing Sera, Bram, Wick, the ovens, the blue candles — with the speed of a man reading a page. "Might we speak privately? I promise you're safer alone with me than your friends are with their hands on those knives."

"They're spoons," said Wick.

"They're sharpened spoons," said Corveth. "I've read the requisition complaints."

Orsen heard himself say, "Five minutes," and heard Sera make a small outraged sound, and stepped out into the herb garden behind the hospice because it was the only room in the building with no one else in it. The night was cold and smelled of rosemary and smoke. Somewhere beyond the walls, the army waited like weather.

Corveth stopped a careful distance away and clasped his hands behind his back, a man deliberately making himself unthreatening and knowing exactly how much that gesture cost him.

"You're wondering why I haven't taken you," he said.

"The thought crossed my mind. You have a gate, an army, and a reputation."

"I have a reputation because I do my homework, so let me save us both the performance." He spoke evenly, without menace, which was somehow more frightening than menace would have been. "You are the last vessel of the Hollow Crown's memory. Your body carries the coronations, the wars, the betrayals, and the recipes of a dynasty three hundred years dead. King Veylan wants you delivered alive to the capital, where you will live in comfort for the rest of your natural life. Good food. A garden. Physicians. Anything you want except a door that opens."

"That's the offer?"

"That's the offer. I'm authorized to improve it. I won't insult you with gold."

"And if I refuse?"

Corveth was quiet for a moment. In the dark, his face did something complicated. "Then tomorrow my soldiers take this quarter house by house, and the council's militia dies in the streets defending a man who makes soup, and you watch it happen, and then you come with me anyway. I would prefer to skip that chapter. I've read it before. The soup-maker part is new."

Orsen looked at him. Really looked. And what he saw was not the butcher of Callowmere, not the reader-of-spice-cabinets, but a tired man in his fifties standing in a garden at midnight trying to buy a catastrophe at discount.

"Why do you care how I come?" Orsen asked. "You could drag me. Nobody in that army would question it."

"No," Corveth agreed. "Nobody would." He turned slightly, looking up at the walls of Meridian, where watch-fires glimmered. "May I tell you what I found at Callowmere, Master Ashgrove? Before I burned it."

"You burned it."

"I read it first. I always read things first. Burning unread paper is vandalism, and I am many unpleasant things, but I am not a vandal." His voice dropped, not to a whisper but to the register men use for true things. "There was a vessel there once. A girl. Nineteen years old. The records say that at the end, the crown filled her past any holding, and she spoke with the voices of every dead king at once, and ten thousand people heard it. The record calls it a miracle." He paused. "The record was written by a man who could no longer remember his mother's face. None of the ten thousand could. The crown poured out, and it took the room with it. Their own memories went with the king's. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Orsen's mouth had gone dry. "No."

"I think you do. I think you bleed from the nose when it comes too fast, and I think you've wondered, in the small hours, what happens if it doesn't stop coming." Corveth's eyes were steady and, horribly, kind. "I am not offering you a cage because I'm cruel. I'm offering you a cage because a jar that breaks in a garden kills one gardener, and a jar that breaks in a crowd kills the crowd. Veylan wants you as a weapon. The council wants you as a banner. The priestess wants you as a temple. I am the only man in this war asking you to be nothing at all, safely, forever. It's a terrible offer. It's also the only one that ends quietly."

For a moment — a real, treacherous moment — Orsen wanted it. A garden. No whispers. No bleeding. No council chamber full of men measuring him for a throne or a pyre. Just bread, and mornings, and the small warm weight of being nobody.

Then he thought of Sera's candles burning blue. Of Wick's false reports, bought back with confession. Of Bram standing guard over a man not his brother. Of his mother's recipe book, cipher-annotated by generations of keepers who had carried this same burden through kitchens and valleys and remembrance days, and had never once been offered a garden in exchange for surrendering it.

"You're right," Orsen said. "It's a terrible offer."

"Master Ashgrove—"

"But here's what your records got wrong, Inquisitor." His voice was steadier than he felt. "My family weren't jars. They were keepers. There's a difference. A jar holds. A keeper *gives*. And I'm not going to give the crown to Veylan, or to the council, or to a cage. I'm going to give it to everyone. All of it. Every king, every war, every name — poured out until there's no vessel left worth stealing, including me."

Something moved behind Corveth's eyes — not anger. Recognition, maybe, or dread. "You don't know what that costs."

"I know exactly what it costs. That's why it's mine to pay."

Corveth looked at him for a long time. Then he did the last thing Orsen expected: he laughed, short and bleak, like a man finding the punchline of a joke he'd been telling himself for thirty years.

"Do you know what I feared at Callowmere, reading that girl's record?" he said. "Not Veylan. Not the old gods. I feared *this*. I feared that someday I'd stand in front of the vessel and hear him say something sane." He straightened, and the tiredness in his face settled into something almost like respect, which was the worst gift he could have given. "Because a madman I could drag. A banner I could burn. But a man who knows the price and pays it anyway — that man makes a mortar like me wonder what his wall was holding up."

"Inquisitor—"

"Tomorrow at dawn, my engineers open the River Gate. The council will fight, and lose, slowly. You have until then." He turned to go, then paused at the garden gate, silhouetted against the fires on the wall. "One more thing, freely given, since you gave me truth. When you pour it out — and you will, I can see it's already decided — be somewhere high, and be certain of everyone standing near you. The crown takes as well as gives. Whatever you love best, hold it loosely that day."

He left through the herb beds without disturbing so much as a rosemary sprig, which somehow figured.

Orsen stood alone in the cold garden until Sera found him, candle in hand, its flame burning a blue so deep it was nearly white.

"What did he want?" she whispered.

Orsen watched the flame. Dawn was perhaps five hours off. The River Gate. The council. An army like weather.

"Sera," he said, "how fast can you gather every priest of the old gods in Meridian?"

Her breath caught. "Why?"

"Because I need witnesses," said Orsen, "to an ending."
