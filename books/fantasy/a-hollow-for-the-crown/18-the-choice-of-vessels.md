## 18. The Choice of Vessels

The council chamber of Meridian smelled of beeswax, old stone, and fear. Orsen had learned to identify fear by smell long ago—in the palace it had come off the courtiers like sweat off a fevered man—but here it was different. Here it was collective. It rose from twenty-three councilors the way steam rises from a pot of soup left too long on the fire, and it had the same effect: everything underneath was cooking whether anyone admitted it or not.

"Lord Ashgrove," said Councilor Vessia, and Orsen still hadn't gotten used to that. Lord. He'd been a kitchen servant eleven weeks ago. Before that he'd been a taster, which was a kitchen servant whose job description included dying professionally. "The legions of King Veylan will reach the Meridian plain in four days. Perhaps five, if the rains slow them."

"They won't," said Councilor Hask, who had fought somewhere once and never let anyone forget it. "Corveth rides ahead of them, and Corveth doesn't let weather do his thinking for him."

"Which is why," Vessia continued, with the smooth inevitability of a tide coming in, "the council has reached its decision. The Hollow Crown must be taken up. By you. You will be crowned before the army arrives, and you will lead us to victory."

Orsen looked at her. She was a broad woman with silver-streaked hair and the eyes of someone who had spent thirty years making other people's hard decisions feel like their own idea. He respected that, in the way one respects a well-made trap.

"I'm going to say no," he said.

A murmur went around the chamber like wind through wheat.

"You haven't heard the terms—"

"I don't need to hear the terms. I've been tasting other people's terms my whole life. They all have an aftertaste." He folded his hands on the table in front of him, which was something Sera did when she was being patient, and which he had stolen shamelessly because it made him look calmer than he felt. "You want me to put on a crown that ate three dynasties and started a war that burned a century. You want me to wear it into battle so that every soldier on that field fights for a ghost with my face. And then, presumably, after we win, I keep wearing it. Forever. Because crowns are very easy to put on and famously difficult to take off."

"The alternative," said Hask flatly, "is that Veylan takes the city, finds you, and does exactly what you just described—except he'll be holding the leash."

"That's a fair point," Orsen admitted. "I've thought about it. At length. Usually at three in the morning, which is when all the worst arguments sound best." He looked down the long table, at the faces turned toward him—hopeful, hungry, frightened. "But here's what none of you seem to understand about the crown. It isn't a symbol. It's a stomach. It digests memory. Three hundred years of kings poured themselves into it, and it's still hungry, and the moment it's on a living head again it starts eating. I know because I *am* it. I've been carrying its leavings since I was born without knowing why my dreams smelled of smoke and coronation wine. If I put it on—if I make myself whole with it—I stop being Orsen Ashgrove who tastes soup, and I become the crown wearing Orsen Ashgrove like a coat."

"And would that be so terrible?" asked a young councilor near the end of the table—Tallow, Orsen thought his name was. "If it meant winning?"

And there it was. The honest question, from the honest end of the table. Orsen almost liked him for it.

"Yes," he said simply. "Because I've seen what it wins. I have its memories, Tallow. All of them, or enough of them. Do you know how the war started? Not with an invasion. Not with an insult. It started because a king wanted to remember everything and share nothing, and memory hoarded curdles into power, and power hoarded curdles into war. That crown doesn't lead armies to victory. It leads them to the same field, over and over, century after century, and it calls each massacre a triumph." He paused. "I'd rather lose as myself than win as it."

Silence. Then Vessia leaned forward, and her voice went gentle, which was worse than anything she'd said so far.

"Master Ashgrove. Four days from now, there will be forty thousand soldiers on that plain. Most of them are conscripts who don't want to die any more than you do. If you refuse the crown, they die anyway, and their deaths buy nothing. Can you live with that arithmetic?"

*Yes,* said a cold practical voice in the back of his head, the voice trained by years of palace service, the voice that had watched men die across the table and kept chewing. *Arithmetic is arithmetic.*

"No," said the rest of him, out loud, before he could stop it. "I can't. But I can't live with the other sum either, and when both sums are unbearable, you look for a third column." He stood. "I don't have it yet. But I have four days, and I promise you this: I will not put the crown on, and I will not run away. Give me until then."

He walked out before they could answer, because he'd learned that from Wick: leave while leaving is still your idea.

* * *

Sera was waiting for him in the corridor, which meant she'd either bribed a doorkeeper or prayed at one until it gave up. Both were plausible. She fell into step beside him, her candle-blue eyes searching his face with the particular intensity of a woman who talked to gods and therefore had high standards for conversation.

"You told them no," she said.

"I told them *not yet*. There's a difference. It's the difference between a locked door and a door I'm still picking."

"Orsen." She caught his sleeve. In the corridor's lamplight she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep—the specific exhaustion of someone whose god had been talking through her mouth lately and hadn't always waited to be invited. "Thank you. For saying no. I know what it cost you in there."

"It cost me nothing yet. Ask me again in four days."

"You know what I mean." She lowered her voice. "When they put that thing on your head, you won't come back. I've felt it. When the god speaks through me, even for a breath, I come back *less*. A little less Sera each time. And the crown is a thousand times older and hungrier than anything that speaks to me. If you take it up whole—" Her hand tightened. "I didn't walk out of the Ashen Valley behind you to watch you get eaten by furniture."

Despite everything, Orsen laughed. "Eaten by furniture."

"You know what I mean."

"I do." He stopped walking. They stood in an alcove where a shrine to some forgotten hearth-god had been repurposed as a coat rack, which struck him as either blasphemy or efficiency. "Sera. Why did you follow me? Truly. Not the god's reason. Yours."

She was quiet a moment. "Because you covered for a thief over saffron," she said finally. "Bram told me. Before you knew what he was, before you knew what you were—you saw a boy stealing and your first instinct was to stand between him and trouble. A vessel of the Hollow Crown, archive of tyrants, and your soul's first motion is *protect the small dishonest person*. That's not what the crown would do. That's what your mother taught you, in a valley where magic can't follow. And I thought: whatever the gods want with this man, whatever the crown wants, *that* is worth following." She smiled, small and crooked. "Also you make excellent soup."

"That's the real reason, isn't it."

"It's load-bearing."

He wanted to tell her he'd already found his third column—that the shape of it had been sitting in his chest for days, half-formed, terrifying, beautiful. But it wasn't ready, and saying a thing too early was like pulling bread from the oven too early: it collapsed, and you couldn't uncollapse it. So instead he said, "Whatever happens on that plain, I need you to do one thing for me."

"Anything."

"If I start sounding like a king—formal, distant, certain—hit me. Hard. Preferably with something soft but heavy."

"A hymnal?"

"Perfect."

* * *

Wick found him that evening on the kitchen roof of the council house, which was where Orsen went to think, because kitchens were the only rooms in any building that ever told him the truth. Below, the city was lighting its lamps one by one, and beyond the walls, in the dark, an army was doing whatever armies did before they killed you. Cooking, probably. Armies were mostly cooking, in Orsen's experience. War was a great deal of logistics interrupted by moments of horror.

Wick climbed up with the ease of a man who had been climbing into places he shouldn't be since before his voice broke, and sat down close enough that their shoulders touched. He had a cloth bundle, which he opened to reveal two apples, a heel of bread, and a wedge of cheese that was definitely stolen from the council's own larder.

"Dinner," Wick said. "From the people trying to crown you. Seemed fitting."

"You stole their cheese."

"I *liberated* their cheese. There's a difference. It's the difference between theft and commentary." He handed Orsen an apple. For a while they ate in silence, watching the lamps.

"Bram's gone quiet," Wick said at last. "Since the thing with Corveth's letter. He keeps looking at you like he's checking you're still real."

"He offered to sell me once. He chose not to. That kind of choice leaves marks."

"Yeah, well." Wick tossed his apple core off the roof with a flick of the wrist, a small act of vandalism against the courtyard below. "Speaking of choices." He turned, and in the lamplight spilling up from the street below, his face was unusually serious—which on Wick was like a dog standing on its hind legs: notable, slightly alarming, and oddly moving. "Council's going to keep pushing. They'll vote, they'll pass a resolution, they might even try to crown you by force if it comes to that. So I want to say my piece now, while it's just us and the cheese."

"Wick—"

"Let me finish, I rehearsed this." He took a breath. "Whatever you choose—crown, no crown, some mad third thing I can't guess—they'll stand with you. Me, Bram, Sera. All of us. Not because you're the Hollow Prince or the vessel or whatever they're calling you this week. Because you're the bloke who stood between a stranger and trouble over *saffron*, which, for the record, is the stupidest possible thing to take a beating over, and I've never forgiven you for how much it made me like you." His voice cracked slightly on the last words, and he covered it by scowling at the horizon. "So. Whatever you choose. We're with you. That's all. That's the speech."

Orsen found that his throat had gone tight, which was inconvenient, because he had things to say and they needed a working throat.

"You know what's funny," he managed. "Everyone keeps telling me I'm not alone. The council says it like a threat. Sera says it like a prayer. You say it like a threat *and* a prayer." He bumped his shoulder against Wick's. "It's the only argument that's ever worked on me. Did you know that? Every version of the crown's memory I carry, every king in that archive—they all died alone at the center of a ring of people who served them. Not one of them had a friend on the roof sharing stolen cheese. That's the whole difference. That's the entire secret of three hundred years of history, and it fits on a rooftop."

Wick sniffed loudly. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me and I resent it deeply."

"You should frame it."

"I'm having it embroidered." He paused. "So. Third column. You've got one, haven't you? I can see it rattling around in there like a pea in a whistle."

Orsen looked out at the dark plain beyond the walls, where tomorrow's battlefield lay sleeping under tomorrow's rain. He thought about the crown's memories—the hoarding kings, the silent gods, the war that began as a refusal to share. He thought about what he'd done in Meridian's square, letting a sliver of memory loose in public, and how it had moved through the crowd like light through water. He thought about the release he was contemplating, the one he hadn't dared name even to himself until this week, and how it would require him to open *everything*—not a sliver, not a ceremony, but the whole archive, into every mind on that field.

And he thought about the price. He'd felt the edges of it already, in the small leaks: names gone soft, a face from the Ashen Valley that he knew he should know and couldn't quite—

No. Not that. Not yet. There would be time to count the cost after he decided to pay it.

"Maybe," he said. "Ask me tomorrow."

"You always say that."

"Because it's always true. Tomorrow me knows more than today me. It's the only advantage of tomorrow."

Wick grinned, and the seriousness slid off him like rain off slate. "Well, tomorrow you better be brilliant, because I've got money on you. Literally. I bet Tallow's clerk four silver you do something unprecedented."

"Wick."

"What? It's called *faith*."

* * *

He dreamed, that night, of the first king.

Not the crown's dream this time—or not only. It was his own dream wearing the king's face, the way dreams do. He stood in the old coronation hall from the memory that had seized him months ago, the one with the smoke-smell and the wine, and the Hollow Crown sat on a cushion before him, black iron worked into a circle of openwork, hollow at the center, hungry at the edges.

*Take me up,* it said, in the voice every king had used in every memory: reasonable, warm, absolutely certain. *Take me up and save them. What is a little more weight, when you already carry so much?*

And Orsen—who had spent his life putting unknown things in his mouth and waiting to see what they did—looked at the crown the way he looked at an unfamiliar dish, and gave it the only answer a taster ever gives.

"Not yet," he said. "I want to see the rest of the menu."

He woke before dawn with the decision whole in his chest at last, baked through, ready or not. Rain was starting against the window—the rains that wouldn't slow the legions—and somewhere below, Sera was singing the morning hymn in a voice that cracked on the high notes, and Bram was arguing with the stable-master, and Wick was probably stealing something on principle.

Four days. One plain. Forty thousand minds.

Orsen got up, dressed, and went downstairs to tell his family what he intended to do—and to ask them, though he already knew the answer, to help him pay for it.
