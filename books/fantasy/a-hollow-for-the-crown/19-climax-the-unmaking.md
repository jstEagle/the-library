## 19. Climax: The Unmaking

The plain before Meridian smelled like every battlefield the crown had ever shown me, which was to say: iron, horse sweat, and the particular sourness of ten thousand men who had eaten a bad breakfast and were about to regret everything.

I stood on the low rise at the center of the free cities' line, and I was wearing a crown I had sworn I would never wear.

Not the real one. The real Hollow Crown was a museum piece, a circlet of blackened silver that the council had dug out of a vault and polished with more reverence than skill. No—this one was worse. This one was made of expectation. It sat on my head the moment I'd put on the ceremonial half-armor Sera's order had insisted upon, and it grew heavier with every eye that turned toward me.

"Stop fidgeting," Wick murmured at my left shoulder. He was not in armor, because Wick had explained at length that armor slowed a man down when running away, which was, in Wick's professional opinion, the most important thing armor could do. "You look like a man about to be sick, and kings aren't supposed to look like that."

"I'm not a king."

"You're wearing the face of one. I've seen it all week. It's the face you make when the soup's bad and you can't say so."

"That's my taster face."

"It's your king face now. Congratulations. Terrible promotion."

At my right shoulder stood Bram, who had spent the morning being aggressively calm, which for Bram meant cleaning a sword that was already clean and humming a marching song from a company that no longer existed. He'd stopped humming now. The Veylan legions had come over the eastern ridge in their gray columns, banners snapping, and even Bram's humor had gone quiet in the face of that arithmetic.

And behind me, on the rise, the council of the free cities sat on folding chairs.

Folding chairs. Three hundred years of history, a war older than any of the kingdoms, and the fate of the realm, and the council had brought folding chairs and a canopy and a clerk who kept trying to hand me a proclamation to read aloud.

I had read it. It was very fine prose. It named me the Hollow Prince, heir of the deposed line, bearer of the crown's memory, and it called upon me to assume the Hollow Crown and lead the hosts of the free cities to victory, restoring the ancient dynasty and ushering in a new age of—there was a word I'd stumbled over—*beneficence.*

Every word of it was a lie, or close enough.

Because I had opened myself fully to the crown's memory, three nights ago, in a cellar that smelled of turnips, and I had seen the whole truth at last: the war had never been about territory or succession or honor. The war had begun because the crown hoarded memory itself. The first kings had built a vessel to hold the dynasty's remembering, and the vessel had grown hungry, and the gods—who are, whatever Sera's order says, made of memory the way rivers are made of water—had gone silent rather than be poured into it. Every atrocity since had been the crown protecting itself. Every heir. Every vessel. Every kitchen servant's family quietly keeping recipes that were not recipes at all.

Including my mother's.

I had not told the council this. The council did not want truth; the council wanted a flag. And now the flag had legs and was standing on a hill wondering how to end a war without starting one.

"You're doing the face again," Wick said.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be interesting. Something's happening."

Something was happening. A single rider had come out from the gray lines, unhurried, under a parley banner, and even at this distance I knew the set of his shoulders the way you know a footstep on your own stairs.

Dain Corveth.

* * *

They let him come up because the alternative was shooting an unarmed man under a parley banner, and the free cities were very fond of telling each other they were better than that.

He stopped ten paces out, which was correct protocol, and looked at me for a long moment with those pale, patient eyes. Corveth had a face like a locked drawer. I had spent one terrified evening in his office learning that everything about him was precise—the part in his hair, the angle of his hat, the way he asked questions he already knew the answers to, which was all of them.

"Orsen Ashgrove," he said. "Or should I say the Hollow Prince?"

"Orsen's fine."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. It might have been amusement. With Corveth you could never be sure it wasn't indigestion.

"King Veylan offers terms," he said, and unrolled a document with a crisp snap. "Your life, guaranteed, in comfort and honor. A residence in the capital. A stipend. You would be—consulted." He said the word the way a man says *caged.* "The king is not a fool. He knows a vessel's memory dies with the vessel, or worse. He would rather have you whole and idle than broken and scattered."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then there is a battle, which you will lose, and I will be required to take you from the field, which I would very much prefer not to do, because you have become, against every expectation I held, someone I would rather not break." He said it flatly, without warmth, which somehow made it worse. Or better. With Corveth, flatness was the only honesty available.

Behind me, I heard the council's clerk whisper, *terms are terms,* and I heard Sera, somewhere to my left, draw a breath that could have powered a bellows.

"May I ask you something first?" I said.

"You may."

"The crown. You've read everything about it. The archives, the inquisition's files, all of it. Did you ever find out why the gods went silent?"

The locked drawer of his face shifted, very slightly, like a drawer someone had tried to open. "That is not relevant to the terms."

"It's the only relevant thing. But you don't know. Nobody knows. Three hundred years, and nobody knows, because the crown made sure of it." I looked past him, at the gray columns waiting on the ridge, at all those soldiers who had marched because someone told them history required it. "I know, Corveth. I've seen it. And I'm about to do something with that knowledge, and I want you to know that it isn't a trick, and it isn't a weapon, and when it's done you're going to have to decide very quickly what kind of man you are. I'd appreciate it if you'd decide to be the one I think you might be."

"That," said Corveth, "is a great deal of dramatic preamble."

"Yes," I admitted. "I've been practicing. I'm told kings do it."

* * *

Sera caught my arm as the parley party withdrew. Her candle-blue eyes were wet and furious, which was her default state around me lately, and I had come to treasure it the way you treasure a fire in a cold room.

"You're going to do it," she whispered. "The thing you told us. You're actually—Orsen, do you understand what it will cost? You told me yourself. The crown's memory isn't a coat you can hang up. It's *woven into you.* Your mother's voice. The valley. The first time you tasted nightshade and didn't die. If you pour it all out—"

"I know what's in the ledger," I said. "I've been keeping it my whole life."

"That's not—"

"Sera." I took her hands. They were shaking. So were mine, but hers were shaking with fear for me, and mine were shaking with the specific terror of a man about to give away the only thing he's ever owned. "You prayed for years and got silence. You kept lighting candles in an empty room because it was right, not because it worked. And then the gods started answering through a food taster who never wanted them, and you know why? Because the crown was full, and something had to give." I smiled, and it came out crooked, the way my smiles do. "You told me once the gods chose me for a reason. You were wrong. There was no reason. There was just a vessel, and a hunger, and someone had to be the one to say *enough.* I'm the someone. I've always been the someone. It's the family business."

She laughed—a wet, broken sound—and gripped my hands hard. "If you do this, I will personally resurrect every one of the old gods to haunt you."

"Get in line. Wick's already promised to haunt me first, and he's very competitive."

"I heard that," said Wick.

* * *

The council's herald called my name. The armies settled. Somewhere a drum began, slow as a funeral, and I walked down from the rise into the flat ground between the two hosts, alone, as the proclamation demanded, wearing my crown made of expectation.

The Hollow Crown itself sat on a velvet cushion on a small table, because the council had insisted on props. I picked it up. It was lighter than I expected. Most terrible things are.

I held it up so both armies could see, and I heard the free cities' line roar, and I heard Veylan's gray ranks go quiet in the way of men who have been promised a spectacle.

"Lords and councilors," I said, and my voice carried farther than it should have—Sera's doing, a blessing she'd pressed into my throat that morning, the last prayer she'd ever light for me, though she didn't know it yet. "Soldiers of Veylan. People of Meridian. I am Orsen Ashgrove. I was born in the Ashen Valley, where magic cannot follow, and I have spent my life eating poison so that a usurper could eat dinner in peace. I am the last vessel of the Hollow Crown. Everything the old kings were, everything they did, everything they *hid*—I carry it. All of it."

I let the silence sit.

"And the council has asked me to put this crown on and lead you to glory. And I could. The memory in me knows every battle plan of the old dynasty. It knows where every vault is buried. It knows the true names of things that have been sleeping a very long time." I turned the crown in my hands. "It also knows why the gods went silent. It knows that this war never ended because the crown never let it end. It knows that every heir, every vessel, every keeper who kept recipes that were not recipes—we were not a dynasty. We were a *hoard.* And a hoard does not care who starves outside its walls."

The council's clerk was making strangled noises on the rise. Let him.

"So here is what I am going to do instead. I am going to give it away. All of it. To everyone. Every mind on this field, every mind in Meridian, every mind that ever wonders afterward—the whole of it, the coronations and the betrayals and the queen's last words and the first king's hunger. No vessel. No vault. No throne that can hold it, because it will belong to all of you and none of you, the way a song belongs to everyone who's ever sung it." I looked up at the ridge, at the gray ranks, and I found—because the crown's memory found him for me—the face of a boy soldier in the third column who was thinking about his mother's bread. "You will all remember being kings. You will all remember being the ones kings stepped on. And then you will get to decide, each of you, what to do with that. That's the only coronation I have to offer. It's the only one that was ever real."

I put the Hollow Crown on my head.

It was warm. It had always been warm; I'd just never had the courage to notice.

And I let go.

* * *

I don't have words for what it was like, and I was, at the time, the most qualified person in the world to have them.

It was not a flood. It was a *harvest*—three hundred years of stored remembering turning inside out all at once, and I stood in the middle of it like a man standing in a granary as every sack splits open, and the grain goes everywhere, into everything, into the ground and the wind and the ten thousand minds before me.

I felt them receive it. That was the terrible, wonderful thing—I felt all of them at once. The councilors on their folding chairs, gasping. Sera, weeping openly, her god's silence ending not in a voice but in a *chorus.* Bram, on the rise, suddenly on his knees, because somewhere in the pouring he had found his own dead comrades—not a memory of them, but *them*, the crown had kept them, kept every soldier who ever died under the old banners, and it was giving them back. Wick's mind touched mine for half a heartbeat, and I felt him find his brother in the flood, and I felt him laugh and swear at the same time.

And on the ridge, in the gray ranks, ten thousand soldiers of Veylan remembered, all at once, being kings and being conquered, being the ones who built the crown and the ones it ate. I felt Veylan himself, far back behind his banners, receive the whole of it—every illegitimate claim, every murder dressed as law—and I felt his certainty come apart like wet paper.

Dain Corveth, ten paces from me, sword drawn because he had drawn it when I crowned myself and had not yet decided what he was—Corveth received it standing. I watched him through the flood. I watched three hundred years go through him like a river through a door, and I watched him understand, in one long terrible moment, that the inquisition he had served his whole life had been a broom sweeping before a hoard, and that he had always, somewhere in the locked drawers of himself, known it.

His blade came down. Not in a strike. He drove it into the earth up to the hilt, and left it standing there, and sat down on the trampled grass like a man who had walked a very long way and had finally, finally arrived.

The gray ranks did not charge. The gray ranks *broke*—not in panic, but in the way a knot breaks, all at once, into ten thousand separate men who each remembered too much to be a column anymore. Some wept. Some laughed. Some, I knew, were already walking home.

And me? I stood in the middle of the plain with an empty crown on my head, lighter than air now, lighter than nothing, and I felt the last of the hoard pour out of me and I thought, with the last coherent corner of my mind: *good. Take it. Take all of it. It was never mine. It was never anyone's.*

I thought: *Mother, I'm giving it back.*

I thought—

I thought—

* * *

The grass was soft against my cheek. That was the first thing. The second thing was that the sky was very blue and I did not know why I was looking at it.

There were people around me. Many people. Some of them were crying, which seemed like an overreaction to a man lying in the grass, but I supposed I might have been trampled, or something. There was a woman with candle-blue eyes holding my hand and saying my name over and over—*Orsen, Orsen*—and the name fit me like a coat that almost, almost closed.

There was a thin man with quick eyes who was laughing and crying and swearing, all three at once, which seemed inefficient.

There was a big man with a soldier's bearing who had his hat off, which felt important, though I couldn't have said why.

"Welcome back," the thin man said, and his voice cracked clean down the middle. "Don't you dare—don't you *dare*—we've got so much to tell you. You have no idea. Well. You have no idea. That's rather the point."

The woman pressed my hand to her heart. "It's all right," she said, in the voice of someone who had decided it was going to be, by force, if necessary. "You did it. Whatever it cost—it's done. And we remember. Do you hear me? We *all* remember now. You'll never have to carry it alone again."

I looked up at their faces—kind faces, wet faces, faces that were looking at me the way you look at someone you love—and I felt warmth rise in me like bread, and grief like an old scar aching before rain, and I did not know their names.

I did not know their names.

But the woman was still holding my hand, and the thin man had begun, absurdly, to talk—fast, tumbling, *so it started when you were nine and you ate the wrong soup on purpose, and there was this priestess, absolutely insufferable, wonderful, you'll love her*—and somewhere in the flood of his voice I understood that I had given away a kingdom's worth of memory and kept, somehow, the one thing that mattered.

I just had to be told what it was.

Fortunately, it seemed, everyone around me had made it their business to know.
