## 20. The Cost of Forgetting

The first thing I knew was the taste of honey.

Not the memory of it—just the fact of it. Sweetness on my tongue, thick and slow, someone pressing a spoon between my lips with the careful patience of a person feeding a child or a fool. I swallowed because my body remembered how even when nothing else did.

"There," said a voice above me. A woman's voice, cracked down the middle like a dropped plate glued back together. "There, that's it. Again."

I opened my eyes.

Light came through a window I did not know, in a room I did not know, onto the face of a woman I did not know—and she was crying. Not quietly, either. She was crying the way people cry at funerals, with her whole chest, her hand pressed flat against my sternum as if she could hold my heart in place by force.

"You're awake," she said. "Oh, thank every silent god, you're awake."

I knew what I had done. That was the strange mercy of it, the one piece the leaving hadn't taken. I remembered the plain before Meridian, the two armies like a wound held open across the grass, the council's crown of black iron sitting on its cushion waiting for my head. I remembered choosing otherwise. I remembered opening myself the way you'd open a locked door in a flood—not gently, not bravely, just all at once, because there was no other way to keep the water from drowning everyone behind it.

The crown's memory had poured out of me into ten thousand minds. Kings' coronations. Queens' last words. Three hundred years of betrayals and recipes and small human kindnesses preserved like flies in amber. Every soldier on that field had woken up knowing the old dynasty from the inside, knowing Veylan's claim was built on a theft so old it had forgotten it was a theft. The war had ended not with a battle but with an understanding, sudden and total, like dawn arriving all at once instead of gradually.

And my own memories had gone out with it. Poured through the same door. The flood takes what's in the house along with what's in the cellar.

I looked up at the weeping stranger and felt nothing but courtesy.

"I'm sorry," I said. My voice sounded like gravel dragged over gravel. "Do I know you?"

Her face did something I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe. Grief and relief fighting for the same ground, neither winning, both losing. "It's me," she said. "Sera. Sera. We came out of the capital together, through Wick's tunnels, and Bram carried you half the way to Meridian when your legs gave out, and—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. Started again, slower, the way you'd talk to someone standing on a ledge. "You're Orsen Ashgrove. You were a food taster for Veylan. You hate the smell of cloves because they remind you of the inquisitor's office. Your mother's name was Maren, and she made a fish stew with too much pepper, and you loved it anyway."

She recited it like a liturgy. Like she'd been saying it for days.

"Those are lovely details," I said honestly. "I wish they were mine."

Sera made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite anything else, and pressed the back of her wrist against her mouth, and sat down hard on the edge of the bed.

* * *

They told me the shape of my life in pieces, over hours, over days. There was no other way to do it. A whole life is too heavy to lift at once; you have to carry it in buckets.

The room belonged to a cookshop near the harbor in Meridian—a real one, with a hearth and a courtyard where herbs grew in cracked pots. It had been given to us, or to them, by a city that didn't know what else to do with the man who had unmade a war. Wick appeared on the second day: a wiry young man with quick hands and quicker eyes who stopped dead in the doorway when he saw me sitting up, and then crossed the room in four strides and hugged me hard enough to crack a rib, if I'd still had ribs worth cracking.

"Careful," said the woman called Sera, automatically. "His body's been through—"

"He's fine," Wick said into my shoulder. "He's poisoned-proof, remember? Man drank things that would kill a horse. He can survive one hug." But his voice broke on *remember*, and he held on a moment longer than a hug needs, and when he pulled back his eyes were red-rimmed and furious about it.

I smiled at him the way you smile at a friendly dog in a market. Warmly. From a polite distance.

I watched him understand what he was seeing. Watched it land. He turned away fast, busied himself with the water jug, said something about how I'd missed the worst of the council's squabbling, which apparently continued even now that there was nothing left to squabble about.

Bram came on the fourth day. A big man, gray at the temples, moving carefully like a man carrying something breakable—which, I suppose, he was. He stood in the doorway a long time before coming in.

"They told you who I am?" he asked.

"Sera says you deserted from Veylan's army eleven years ago. That you lost men at Redford Ford. That one of them was named Callen, and that I once showed you—" I paused, checking the notes she'd written for me, the little book of facts she kept by my bed in her own careful hand. "That I once let you see Callen again. Through the crown's memory. His last morning, laughing about something."

Bram nodded. His jaw worked. "You cried," he said finally. "When you shared it with me. You said, *that's how he should be kept.*" He rubbed his face with one huge hand. "You don't remember any of that."

"No."

"Well." He lowered himself into the chair by the bed, the wood groaning under him. "Then I'll tell it to you again sometime. Properly. With the parts you'd want to hear." And he stayed the whole afternoon, talking about nothing—weather, the price of salt, the terrible state of Meridian's cobblestones—and it was the kindest thing anyone did for me in those first weeks, because he never once looked at me like a man looking at a grave.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about forgetting: the world does not become empty. It becomes full of strangers who love you.

Every morning Sera brought breakfast and sat with me while I ate, testing everything first out of habit—I learned later this had been her way since the beginning, this priestess who couldn't cook blessing food she wouldn't trust. She'd tell me one story each day. Just one. She said Dr. Ferrin—the physician the city had assigned, a harried woman with ink-stained fingers—warned against flooding me, as if my mind were a field and my past a spring planting.

Today's story was about a pantry.

"You caught me stealing saffron," Wick said—he'd taken over the storytelling that afternoon, sprawled sideways in a chair like the furniture owed him money. "Worst thief in the history of thievery. Knocked over a jar of dried plums on the way in. You heard the whole thing from three shelves away and pretended to be asleep until I had the saffron in my boot, and then you opened your eyes and said, 'Take the good jar, the one behind it is cut with marigold.'"

"That sounds like something a taster would know," I said.

"It's something only you would know." He grinned, and then the grin faltered, the way it always did, at the edge of the thing. "You covered for me. First day we met. Didn't know me from a hole in the wall, and you lied to a steward's face for me." He picked at a loose thread on his sleeve. "I've been trying to work out why ever since. Best theory I've got is you were just that tired of everyone in that palace being awful, and here was one person being honestly, competently crooked, and some part of you respected the craft."

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe I was already the kind of person who covers for thieves, and I still am, and the rest is just... inventory."

Wick looked at me then—really looked, the way he rarely let himself—and said, quietly, "Yeah. Maybe that's exactly it."

* * *

The dreams were the worst part, or the best, depending on the hour.

I dreamed of the Ashen Valley without ever having seen it. Gray hills, gray sky, a valley where magic pooled away from the land like water from an oiled cloth. I dreamed of a kitchen with a blue door and a woman humming off-key, and I woke reaching for her name and found only the shape where it had been—a mother-shaped absence, warm at the edges.

Sera found me awake after one of these, in the small hours, sitting up with the fact-book open on my knees.

"Maren," I said. "Your notes say her name was Maren. Tell me about her."

So she did. She told me about a woman who had annotated a recipe book in royal cipher, generation after generation of keepers hiding a kingdom's memory in instructions for bread and stew. She told me about remembrance days in the valley, and an aunt named Odile who talked too much when she was frightened, and a boy who dosed himself with poisons until his tongue became a ledger. She told it all in her low, steady voice, and when she finished I realized she was crying again, silently, the way she'd gotten better at.

"I'm sorry," I said. "This must be like mourning twice."

"No." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, impatient with them. "You want to know what it's actually like?" She thought about it. "It's like the gods answered my prayers after years of silence, and the answer was you, and now the gods are quiet again and I'm left holding—" She gestured at me, at the room, at all of it. "This. Which is still more than I had. So I'm not going to sit here crying about the shape of the gift."

"That's very pious of you."

"It's very *stubborn* of me. Don't confuse the two, everyone always does." She stood, straightened her robes, and paused at the door. "Tomorrow's story is about the road out of the capital. Bram fell off his horse. You'll like it."

"Did he get hurt?"

"No. That's what makes it funny."

* * *

On the ninth day, I cooked.

Nobody asked me to. I woke before dawn with my hands already itching, went down to the cookshop's kitchen, and found that my body remembered what my mind had surrendered. Knife work. Heat. The particular patience of onions. I stood at the counter cutting leeks into ribbons and understood that I had done this ten thousand times, that the knowledge lived below the waterline where the flood hadn't reached, and I wept into the cutting board like a fool, grieving and grateful in the same breath.

Wick found me there an hour later, six pots going, enough food for twenty people laid out in various stages.

"You're supposed to be resting," he said.

"I don't know how to do that. Apparently I never did."

He surveyed the spread, picked up a spoon, tasted the soup, and went very still. Then he tasted it again.

"What?" I asked.

"Nothing. It's just—" He set the spoon down with exaggerated care. "Maren's fish stew. Too much pepper. Sera described it to you once, weeks ago, one sentence." He looked at me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't have the history to read. "You made it from one sentence."

I looked down at the pot. The steam rose and curled and meant nothing to me, and everything to him.

"Huh," I said.

He laughed—wet, cracked, helpless laughter—and then he rolled up his sleeves, washed his hands, and said, "Right. If you're cooking, I'm cooking. You're not doing this alone. That's the rule now. New rule. Nobody cooks alone."

* * *

Weeks passed that way. Stories in the mornings, cooking in the afternoons, the city outside slowly learning to live without a war it had expected the way you expect winter. Corveth had surrendered his commission, I was told—had walked out of Meridian the day after the Unmaking without a word to anyone, and no one knew where he'd gone, and Sera watched the roads longer than she admitted. Veylan's army had dissolved like sugar in rain, ten thousand men suddenly remembering three centuries of stolen crowns. The council had declared a holiday, then argued about the holiday's name, which Sera said proved the world was healing.

And every evening, the three of them gathered around my bed or my table and grieved me. Openly, deliberately, the way you'd tend a fire. They laughed at stories of the man I'd been and wiped their eyes when they thought I wasn't watching, and I sat among them like a guest at a wake for someone who kept passing the bread.

One night, late, Sera stayed behind after Wick and Bram stumbled home through the fog. She banked the fire, then sat across from me, turning her blue candle stub over in her fingers—an old habit, I'd learned. The candle that had burned blue when prayers started getting answered. It didn't burn anything now. The gods had gone back to their silence, having used their one loud word.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. "What you chose. Knowing what it cost."

I considered it honestly, which was the only way I knew how to consider anything anymore.

"I know I saved everyone," I said. "I know it in the way you know arithmetic. But I don't feel the weight of what I gave up, because I can't. It's like being told I once owned a house that burned down. Everyone around me remembers the rooms. I just see smoke." I looked at her—at this stranger with kind eyes who had crossed a kingdom beside me, whose prayers had helped carry me, who loved a man who no longer entirely existed. "But here's what I do feel. When Wick laughs, something in my chest turns over. When Bram sits down heavy in that chair, I feel safe. When you sing those hymns off-key, I grieve, and I don't know why, and I've decided I don't need to." I met her eyes. "Whatever they were to me, I think I'm becoming them again. Slower. Worse handwriting. But becoming."

Sera pressed her lips together and nodded several times, blinking hard.

"The new book," she said finally. "The one we're writing. Wick's idea—you'd have hated how proud of himself he is about it. Recipes and stories, everything written down, everything shared, so no memory ever depends on one person again." She pulled a battered notebook from her robe and set it on the table between us. "We saved you a page. We weren't sure whether to give it to you yet."

"Give it now," I said.

She opened it to the marked page. At the top, in Wick's slanted scrawl, was a title: *Orsen's Fish Stew (Too Much Pepper — This Is Correct).* Below it, the recipe. And below that, in fresh ink, a single line:

*Day 1: He made it himself. He doesn't remember her. He will.*

I read it twice. Then I took the pen from Sera's hand, and beneath that line, in my own unfamiliar handwriting, I wrote:

*Day 2: Teach me her name again.*

Outside, the fog came off the harbor, and somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour, and tomorrow there would be another story, another page, another day of building a man out of love and repetition. It was not the life I had been promised. As far as I could tell, it might be better.
