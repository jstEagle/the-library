## 21. Recipes Without a Keeper

### Sera

The first morning after, Sera woke before dawn out of habit and lay in the narrow bed above the kitchen listening to the sounds of a house that had learned to grieve quietly.

Below her floorboards, someone was already working. That was Wick — Wick rose early the way thieves rise early, out of old instinct, as though daylight were something he might be caught stealing. She could hear him moving through the kitchen below, careful around the stove, careful around the table where Orsen slept sitting up because lying down frightened him now, though he would not say so.

She dressed, braided her hair, and said the dawn office to gods who were silent again. She found she did not mind. The silence of the gods, she had decided, was not absence. It was the silence of parents who have finally put down a burden they carried too long. Somewhere below, a kettle began to sing, and that was its own kind of prayer.

She came downstairs to find the ritual already beginning.

"Good morning," Wick was saying to Orsen, too loudly, the way people speak to the deaf and the way, Sera suspected, Wick spoke to everyone when he was nervous. "It's the fourth of Thawmonth. You're in Meridian, in the free city, in our kitchen. My name is Wick. I steal things. Professionally. Formerly. Formerly professionally."

"I remember," Orsen said mildly, from behind his cup.

"You remember some things," Wick corrected. "You remember what we tell you. Which is different, and which is why we're telling you again." He counted on his fingers. "Item one: you saved my life in a pantry over saffron. Item two: you are immune to every poison known to man, which makes you a terrible dinner guest and an excellent friend. Item three: Bram snores."

"I do not snore," said Bram from the doorway, carrying an armload of split wood. "I narrate my dreams. There's a difference, and it's dignified."

Orsen laughed — a small laugh, uncertain at its edges, like a man testing ice — but it was a laugh, and Sera saw Wick pocket it the way he pocketed anything valuable.

This was the liturgy now. They had not planned it; it had assembled itself out of necessity, the way families do. Every morning they told Orsen who he was. Every evening they told him what he'd done. In between, they cooked.

Because that was Sera's contribution, the part she had insisted on, the part that had made Wick roll his eyes and Bram go uncharacteristically still: the book.

She set it on the table now — a fat, blank ledger she'd bought from a stationer on Tallow Street, its cover stamped with nothing, belonging to no order, no crown, no god. Just paper.

"We're writing it all down," she told Orsen, for perhaps the fortieth time, and watched him nod with the patient expression of a man being told about weather. "Everything. Not just the recipes from your mother's cipher-book — those too, all of them, Bram's grandmother's stew, whatever disaster Wick calls cooking — but us. How we met. What we said. What we did. So that no one ever has to carry it alone again. So that no one can ever be taken from it."

"That's a lot of paper," Orsen observed.

"It's a lot of us," Sera said. "We're worth the paper."

Wick snorted. Bram, setting down his wood, said quietly, "Aye. We are." And then, because Bram could never let a tender moment stand without fortifying it: "Someone write down that I said that, so I can deny it later."

Sera wrote it down.

### Wick

Here was the thing nobody warned you about loving somebody with holes in their head: you started memorizing everything, compulsively, like a magpie preparing for a very long winter.

Wick had always been good at remembering. It was a professional skill. He could hold a guard rotation in his head for a week, recall which merchant's cellar had the loose brick, recite the exact words Corveth's clerk had used when Wick sold him his first lie. Memory had been currency. He'd spent it carefully and hoarded the rest.

Now he remembered for love instead of coin, and it turned out to be much harder work.

"Tell me about the road," Orsen said one afternoon, while Wick was supposed to be chopping onions and was instead watching him with the intensity of a man defusing a trap. "You always stop there. In the telling. You do the tunnels and the gate and then you get very interested in your knife."

Wick looked at his knife. His knife offered no escape.

"All right," he said. "The road. Fine. But I want it noted that this is the third time this week and you ask the same questions every time, which either means you're testing me or you genuinely enjoy hearing me talk, and honestly both options are flattering."

"Both," said Orsen serenely.

So Wick told it. The tunnel under the tannery, and how Orsen had gone first without hesitation even though he'd never seen it, because — Wick paused here, because this was the part that caught in his throat every time, the part he'd only admitted once, drunk, to Bram — because Orsen trusted him. Had trusted him from the pantry onward, before Wick deserved it, before Wick had confessed what he'd done, that he'd scouted him for Corveth, that his loyalty had been bought with his brother's freedom and then repaid with treason against the only employer who'd ever paid on time.

"And then," Wick said, recovering, "Bram got us lost. Spectacularly. We walked in a circle for four hours. A deserter of eleven years' service, lost in a wheat field."

"The field moved," Bram called from the yard, without shame.

"The field *did not move*, Bram, it's a *field*—"

"There was fog."

"There was *incompetence*—"

And Orsen laughed, really laughed, and Wick felt the day's labor pay itself out warm in his chest, and thought: *this*. This was why they did it daily, like kneading dough, like feeding a fire. Not because the story changed. Because *he* changed by telling it. Every retelling carved the grooves deeper so that the water of them — Orsen's attention, Orsen's laughter, Orsen's terrible puns about leeks — would run in familiar channels even if the source had run dry.

That night, by candlelight, Wick wrote his section of the book. He wrote badly, in a hand like spider legs in ink, and he wrote everything: the pantry, the saffron, the confession, the forgiveness he still didn't feel he'd earned and which Orsen, infuriatingly, seemed to consider already spent and forgotten.

At the bottom of the page he added a recipe, because Sera had ruled that every entry needed one, *so the book stays honest*, she said, *because food is how this family talks.*

He titled it: **Onions, When You Have Been Crying Over Them (Wick's Method)**.

*Step one: claim it's the onions.*

*Step two: see step one.*

He read it back, decided it was the truest thing he'd ever written, and went to bed.

### Bram

Bram had buried thirty-one soldiers. He knew the arithmetic of grief by heart: the weight of a body, the depth of a hole, the number of years it took for a name to become a sound and a sound to become nothing.

What he did not know was what to do with a grief that breathed. With a man who sat across the breakfast table wearing a dead friend's face and asking, politely, *who are you again?*

The first weeks, Bram had handled it the way he handled most unbearable things: by going off alone to chop wood that did not need chopping. The woodpile behind the kitchen had grown absurd. Neighbors had begun leaving notes. One had left a small axe-shaped cake, which Wick ate without shame.

But Sera had cornered him one evening, arms crossed, priestess to the bone. "He asks about you least," she said. "Do you know why? Because you flinch. He notices the flinch, and he protects you from it by staying away. Fix it."

So Bram fixed it the only way he knew how: he made it a duty, and duties he could keep.

Every evening, after supper, he took the last watch — not a real watch, there was nothing to watch for anymore, but Orsen liked the word, liked the shape of it, the idea that someone stood between him and the dark — and during that watch, Bram told him the stories. Not the big ones. Sera and Wick had the big ones. Bram told the small ones, the ones that lived nowhere else now except in his mouth.

He told him about the pass at Cold Ford, where Orsen — the Orsen who was, the one who'd carried the crown's whole memory like a keg of burning oil — had shared a memory with him deliberately. Had opened himself up and let Bram walk through a battlefield that wasn't his, and find, standing in it, Corporal Dennet Marsh, who'd died at Bram's side nine years ago and whose last words Bram had never managed to hear.

"He asked about your sister," Bram said now, on watch, in the dark, for the eleventh time. "Dennet did. In the memory. He said — he said, *tell Bram the letter got through.*"

Orsen was quiet a moment. "I don't remember saying that."

"No," Bram said. "But I do. And now you've heard it, and tomorrow you'll hear it again, and that's how it works. That's the whole trick of it. Nobody remembers alone."

He taught Orsen the stew on the twelfth evening. Bram's grandmother's recipe, the one thing he'd carried out of his old life besides a sword and a scar. He talked Orsen through it hand over hand — brown the meat properly, don't crowd the pot, patience, boy, patience — and Orsen followed along with the focused, grateful attention of a man being handed rope in deep water.

"It's good," Orsen said, tasting it, and frowned slightly, chasing something across his face. "It's — I know this. Not the recipe. The—" He pressed two fingers to his temple. "Warmth. Like walking past a door and smelling bread."

"That'll be the memory of eating it," Bram said gruffly, hiding the lurch in his chest. "Twelve evenings running. It soaks in."

"It soaks in," Orsen repeated, wondering, like a man learning rain.

Bram wrote the recipe in the book that night, in his square soldier's hand, and beneath it, after a long argument with himself, he added a line:

**Serves six. Leftovers keep. Some things, if you feed them often enough, stay fed.**

### Sera

By midsummer the book had grown fat enough to need a second ribbon for its place, and the kitchen had grown a rhythm that Sera recognized, with a start, as liturgy.

Morning: the telling of names and places. Midday: work, side by side, Orsen's hands relearning the knife, quickening week by week until Wick accused him of showing off. Evening: Bram's watch and the small stories. Night: the writing, all four of them bent over the table, passing the pen like a communion cup.

They argued over entries. Wick wanted the tunnel escape recorded with more heroics; Bram crossed out the heroics and inserted the wheat field; Sera adjudicated with the serene tyranny of a woman holding the pen. Orsen listened to these arguments with his chin on his hands and a smile that was becoming less borrowed and more his own each week, and sometimes he contributed a detail none of them had mentioned — the smell of the tannery tunnel, the exact blue of Sera's answered candles — and they all went quiet when he did, because those details came from somewhere none of them could follow, some sealed room inside him where the crown's released memory had left, impossibly, a few embers in the ash.

"He keeps things," Sera wrote in the book one night, when the others had gone up. "Not memories. Something older than memory. Callings. The way a bell remembers a note after it stops ringing."

She blew out the candle. Below the window, in the dark kitchen, she heard Orsen moving — he still slept poorly, still woke reaching for faces he couldn't name — and heard him stop at the shelf where the book lived, and heard the soft sound of pages turning in the dark.

Then, faintly, half to himself, in a voice full of something that was not quite memory and not quite hope:

"So that's what I taste like to them."

Sera lay in the dark and smiled, and did not sleep, and began composing tomorrow's telling — *it's the ninth of Highsummer, you're in Meridian, my name is Sera, I burn candles for gods who are resting* — because tomorrow it would need saying again, and the day after, and the day after that.

Love, she was learning, was not a thing you had. It was a thing you did, daily, with your whole hands.

And in the morning, when she came downstairs, she would find Orsen at the stove already, making tea for four, humming a hymn he had no reason to know — and she would stand in the doorway with her heart in her throat, and say, gently, as always:

"Good morning. Let me tell you who we are."
