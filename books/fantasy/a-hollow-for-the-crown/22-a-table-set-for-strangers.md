## 22. A Table Set for Strangers

The morning bread was proofing badly, and Orsen Ashgrove had never been happier about anything in his life.

He stood in the kitchen of the Salt & Cinder—his kitchen, though the deed said otherwise and Wick's name was on it somewhere in a forgery so competent that Orsen had decided not to ask questions—and watched the dough sit there like a sulking child. Too cold. The hearth had gone lazy overnight, the way hearths did in Meridian when the sea fog rolled in off the harbor and settled over the lower city like a damp wool blanket.

Once, he would have known exactly what to do. Once, his hands had moved through kitchens with the certainty of a man who had been trained since childhood by a mother whose recipes were written half in cipher. Now he watched the dough and felt something adjacent to knowledge, like hearing a conversation through a wall.

"Yeast is dead," said Wick from the doorway.

Orsen turned. Wick leaned against the frame with a crate of eggs balanced on one hip and an expression of profound satisfaction, as if the yeast's death were a personal triumph he had engineered himself.

"The yeast isn't dead."

"It's dead. Look at it. It's given up. I respect that, honestly. There are days I'd like to give up too, but instead I get up and steal eggs." He set the crate down with exaggerated care. "Stole these from a man who tried to shortchange Bram on rope last week, so technically they're reparations."

"You can't call it stealing if you've invented a moral framework for it."

"I can call it whatever I want. It's my city too. Well—" he glanced around the kitchen, at the copper pots Sera had haggled down to half price, at the long scarred table where strangers became regulars became something else, "—it's our city, I suppose. Which is worse, honestly. More people to disappoint."

Orsen smiled. And there it was again—that warmth, sudden and unbidden, rising in his chest like steam from a pot. It arrived without context, without memory attached to it, just pure feeling aimed at this thin, quick-eyed man who stole eggs and lied beautifully and had once sold him out and then spent every day since trying to earn his way back.

The warmth didn't know why it loved Wick. Orsen did not know either. But he let it stay. He had gotten good at letting things stay.

* * *

They opened at midmorning, because Meridian's lower city kept its own hours and nobody respectable came to the Salt & Cinder anyway. The first customer was old Maren, who ordered the same thing she always ordered—fish stew, extra pepper, no fish—and sat by the window complaining about her knees and the council and her knees again.

Bram came in an hour later, filling the doorway the way he filled every doorway, which was completely. He had a new scar along his forearm and refused to say where it came from, which meant it involved either a bar fight or a woman named Tessa, and Orsen had learned not to press.

"Bread's ruined," Wick announced before Bram could sit.

"It's not ruined," Orsen said.

"He's mourning it. Look at him. He's doing his grief face."

"I don't have a grief face."

"You have four faces," Bram said, settling onto the bench with the groan of a large man who had earned every ache. "Grief, suspicion, hunger, and the one where you're pretending you don't know something. You used to do the fourth one constantly. Still do, sometimes."

Orsen went still for half a heartbeat—the length of time it took for the words *used to* to land, for the shape of everything he couldn't remember to press briefly against the inside of his skull and then recede.

Bram saw it. Bram always saw it, and always pretended he hadn't, which was its own kindness. He picked up a spoon and studied it like it was fascinating.

"Sera's coming tonight," he said to the spoon. "Said she'd bring the hymnals. The new ones."

"New hymns?"

"Old hymns. New copies. Her order finally agreed to write them all down." Bram turned the spoon over. "She's been after them for months. Apparently there was a faction that thought writing them down would make the gods feel *catalogued*."

"And now?"

"Now they think the gods might prefer being remembered than being guessed at." He shrugged. "Times change. Somebody taught us that."

Somebody had. Orsen didn't remember who, or how, or what it had cost. But the whole city seemed to know the story in fragments—the taster who carried a crown, the battle on the plain, the light that had gone into everyone at once and come out of no one. They told it wrong in the taverns. They told it wrong in the temples too, which Sera found more offensive.

In the Salt & Cinder, they told it right, or as right as they could, which mostly meant they told it to Orsen, patiently, in pieces, whenever a piece surfaced that needed a home.

* * *

The afternoon rush came and went—or came and stayed, rather, because the lower city treated closing times as suggestions. A dockhand burned his mouth on the stew and declared it perfect. Two council clerks came in wearing plain clothes, pretending not to be council clerks, and ordered wine, and Wick served them with such elaborate courtesy that they left flustered and overtipped.

And through all of it, Orsen cooked.

His hands knew things. That was the strange mercy of it. He hadn't kept the memories—those had poured out of him into ten thousand minds on the plain before Meridian, and what remained was only the sediment, the silt of skill without story. His knife work was flawless and he could not have told you who taught him. He salted by instinct and could not have told you whose instinct. When he tasted a sauce and adjusted it, some ghost in his tongue approved, and the ghost had no name.

He had been a ledger once. Every poison catalogued, every survival recorded. Now he was a ledger with the pages torn out, and somehow the arithmetic still worked.

A child—Tamsin, the harbormaster's daughter, who appeared most days and paid in interesting rocks—sat at the counter watching him fold dumplings.

"How do you know how to do that?" she asked.

"Practice," Orsen said.

"But how do you know *how* to practice?"

He considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "I suppose somebody shows you. Or your hands remember even when you don't. Or both."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He slid her a dumpling. "It's the truest one I have."

She ate it, pronounced it acceptable, and left a rock on the counter as payment. It was a good rock. He kept it by the hearth with the others.

* * *

Evening brought Sera.

She came in with the smell of incense and rain, carrying a satchel of hymnals wrapped in oilcloth, and the room changed temperature—not literally, though Bram swore sometimes it did, but in the way rooms change when someone enters whom everyone has been quietly waiting for.

"Supper first," she said, before anyone could speak. "Then the singing. If we sing first we'll get hungry mid-hymn and it'll be disrespectful."

"To the gods?"

"To the hymns. The gods can wait. They're used to it." She set the satchel down and looked at Orsen, and her expression did the thing it always did—the brief flicker, there and gone, of grief held carefully behind welcome. "You look well."

"I look tired."

"You look well *and* tired. Both things are allowed." She hung up her cloak. "How was the dough?"

"Dead," said Wick.

"Recovering," said Orsen.

"We'll see who's right by morning," Sera said, and sat, and the table was full.

This was the liturgy now. He knew that word because Sera used it, gently, without apology: *we retell it daily, like a liturgy.* Supper at the Salt & Cinder, the four of them plus whoever drifted in, and between the courses someone would tell a piece of the story. Tonight it was Bram's turn, and Bram told the one about the tunnels under the capital, how Wick had led them through dark that smelled of centuries, how Orsen had seized in the middle of it with a memory of a battlefield three hundred years dead and woken speaking a language nobody knew.

"You bit me," Wick added helpfully.

"I did not."

"You absolutely did. My hand. Right here." He displayed his palm as evidence, though any actual mark had faded months ago. "I forgave you, obviously. Eventually. After therapy. The therapy was Bram telling me to stop whining."

"You were whining," Bram confirmed. "Loudly. In the dark. In a tunnel. While inquisitors hunted us."

"A *little* whining. Proportionate whining."

Orsen laughed—really laughed, from somewhere below his ribs—and the laughter brought the warmth again, fuller this time, spilling over. And underneath the warmth, faint as a bruise, grief. Grief with no object. Grief like weather.

He had learned to read it. The grief came when they told the stories, almost always, and it came strongest when Sera sang. He had asked her once, early on, whether it would be better if they stopped—if the stories hurt more than they healed.

She had taken his hands in hers, priestess-quick, and said: "The pain means it mattered. The forgetting doesn't undo the mattering. We'll carry it for you until you can carry it yourself—or forever, whichever comes first."

Tonight, after supper, she sang.

It was one of the old hymns, newly copied, the ink still sharp-smelling on the page. Her voice was not beautiful in the way trained voices are beautiful; it was beautiful in the way that a thing is beautiful when it is entirely sincere. And as she sang, the grief rose in Orsen like water finding its level, and tears came without permission, and he did not wipe them away, because everyone at the table understood, and no one made it strange.

Wick put a hand on his shoulder. Bram looked at the ceiling and blinked a lot and pretended the stew needed attending. Sera sang to him, and for him, and the hymn said something about a table set for strangers becoming a table set for family, and Orsen wept for a family he could not picture and felt their love anyway, arriving like heat from a fire in another room.

When she finished, he breathed, and wiped his eyes, and said, "That one's my favorite."

"You say that every time," Sera said.

"Is it true every time?"

"Yes," she said softly. "That's the miracle of it."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up to bed—Wick in the loft, Bram in the room that was technically a storage closet but had a bed in it now and therefore wasn't—Orsen stood alone in the kitchen and wrote.

The book lay open on the scarred table. It was Sera's idea originally, then Wick's project, then everyone's: a new recipe book, but not like his mother's. Nothing hidden in cipher. Nothing that required a bloodline to read. Every dish in the Salt & Cinder written out plainly, and beside each recipe, in different hands, the story of where it came from—who taught it, who ate it, who cried over it. So that no memory ever again lived in one skull, one vessel, one crown. Memory as a shared meal. Memory passed hand to hand like bread.

He wrote slowly, in careful letters: *Fish stew, extra pepper, no fish — Maren's order, three years running. She says the pepper keeps her young. She is wrong, but the stew is good.*

He paused, pen lifted, and looked at the page. Underneath the recipe, in the margin, his mother's handwriting had once lived in a book like this—so they told him. Ciphered secrets, royal lines, the whole buried architecture of a dynasty. He could not see it. He could not grieve it directly, the way you cannot look at the sun. But he could grieve it sideways, in fragments: the ache when Sera sang, the warmth when Wick lied, the way his chest tightened when Bram pretended not to watch him.

Fragments of feeling, surfacing without context. That was what remained. For a long time he had treated them like symptoms—evidence of a wound, echoes of a loss. Lately he had begun treating them differently.

He set down the pen and stood at the window. Below, the lower city murmured itself toward sleep. Somewhere a dog complained. Somewhere the harbor bells counted the hour, and the fog took the sound and softened it.

Here was the choice, the one he made fresh each morning and would make again tomorrow: he could spend his life mourning the archive he had been. He could chase the fragments, interrogate them, demand they resolve into the man he used to be. Some part of him wanted that. Some part of him would always stand at the border of the Ashen Valley, listening for whispers that no longer came.

But the fragments were not wounds. He was beginning to understand that. They were seeds. Warmth at Wick's laugh—let it mean *my friend*. Grief at Sera's hymns—let it mean *I am loved by people who refuse to let me be a stranger to myself*. Tightness at Bram's quiet watching—let it mean *someone once chose loyalty over survival, and I am living in the house that choice built.*

Let them mean what they mean now. Not what they meant before. Before was gone, poured out onto a battlefield so that no throne could ever gather it up again. What remained was this: a kitchen, a table, a book being written in many hands, and four people who retold him to himself daily, patiently, like a prayer that expected no answer because the answer was already sitting at the table eating stew.

He blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, tasting the air of his own life the way he had once tasted a king's food—carefully, attentively, alert to what might harm him.

Nothing harmed him. That was the strangest discovery of all. Immune to poison, yes—he still tested everything, out of habit, out of craft—but immune to belonging? No. Never that. Belonging got in under every defense he had. It sat down at his table uninvited and asked for seconds.

He banked the hearth, checked the dough—it had risen after all, stubborn, alive, refusing the verdict of the day's early pessimists—and climbed the stairs to bed.

* * *

Morning came gray-gold through the harbor fog. Orsen was up first, as always, hands moving through the opening rituals: banked coals raked live, water drawn, flour measured. He set the recovered dough to bake and stood in the doorway watching the street wake up.

Maren would come at midmorning. Tamsin would bring another rock. The clerks would pretend, the dockhands would burn their mouths, and the day would assemble itself out of small returns.

Footsteps on the stairs—two sets, mismatched, one heavy and one quick. Then a third, lighter, humming an old hymn slightly off-key on purpose, because Sera believed the gods enjoyed imperfection and Wick believed she was making that up.

Orsen turned to greet them, and the warmth arrived on schedule, and the grief arrived beside it like a quiet companion, and he welcomed them both to the table.

"Morning," he said. "Who wants to tell me a story today?"

Wick grinned, already reaching for the eggs he definitely stole. "Oh, I've got a *good* one. It involves a tunnel, a goat, and the single worst decision Bram ever made."

"It was not the worst—" Bram began.

"There was a goat, Bram. There was a *goat*, and you—"

And Orsen laughed, and cracked the eggs, and began the day's liturgy again—new pages, plain ink, nothing hidden, everything shared—a table set for strangers, fed by choice, kept by love, open every morning the hearth could be coaxed to life.

Which, in Meridian, in the fog, was every morning that mattered.
