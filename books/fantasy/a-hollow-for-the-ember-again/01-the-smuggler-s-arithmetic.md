## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

The wind came off the high pass at a slant, the way it always did in Thornfell, and Kestrel Vasquez read it the way other people read letters—quickly, and with an eye for what it was trying to hide.

*West-northwest, gusting to something ugly by midday. Snow above the treeline by dusk. Two hours of good light, maybe three.*

She crouched at the mouth of the pass with her palm flat against a boulder, feeling the stone's cold come up through her glove like a whisper. The mule train waited behind her, nine animals loaded with iron ingots packed under salted hides, and behind the mules stood four men who trusted her because she had never once given them reason not to. That trust had cost her seven years of careful living. She counted it among her assets.

"Two hours," she said, without turning around. "Maybe less if the weather turns honest."

Behind her, Doss—the one who paid for the cargo and therefore believed he owned the mountain too—spat into the scree. "Two hours for nine miles? My grandmother could crawl it faster."

"Your grandmother," Kestrel said, rising and brushing grit from her knee, "is dead. Which is what happens to people who argue with the arithmetic." She let that sit. She had learned long ago that the best way to win an argument with a smuggler was to make him feel stupid instead of defiant. Stupid men followed orders. Defiant men got everyone killed. "The patrol out of Greywatch changes at the third cairn at midday. We cross below it while they're eating. After that we're in the trees and it doesn't matter how loud your mules complain."

Doss muttered something about grandmothers again, but he waved the train forward, and the mules came on, bells muffled with wool, hooves wrapped in felt so their passage sounded like rain instead of traffic. Untaxed iron from the free cities, bound for smithies in the river valleys where the king's tariffs were treated as a rumor. It was good work. Honest work, almost, if you didn't count the crown's ledger—and Kestrel hadn't counted anything belonging to the crown in seven years.

She led them up through the first switchbacks with her eyes moving constantly: the ridgeline, the cloud deck, the dark seam of pine below, the scree slopes that would sing if anyone crossed them. This was the part of the work she was actually paid for—not the walking, but the *reading*. Every mountain spoke. Thornfell spoke loudest, and it lied more than most, but it never lied about wind or weight or the particular quality of silence that meant someone else was on the trail ahead.

That silence arrived an hour in.

Kestrel stopped so abruptly that the lead mule bumped her shoulder. She held up a fist, and behind her the whole train stilled, because they knew her signals better than they knew their own names. She tilted her head, listening past the wind's long vowel, past the creak of leather and pack-frame, down into the valley's throat.

There. Faint. A sound like a pebble worrying another pebble.

Boots on the old pilgrim stair, two switchbacks up and across.

Her heart did something complicated—a single hard knock against her ribs, then a strange, cold stillness, as if some door inside her swung open onto a room she kept locked. She closed the door. She was very good at closing doors.

*Patrol,* she thought. *Four men, maybe five, coming down the stair toward the cairn. Early. Which means either the schedule changed or they're hunting.*

Neither possibility was good. Both possibilities were survivable, if she spent the right coin.

She turned and moved back along the train, low-voiced, touching each man's arm as she passed—an old habit, borrowed from somewhere she didn't examine anymore. "Off the trail. There's a shelf below, fifty yards down-slope, big enough for the mules. Blankets over the packs. Nobody breathes loud."

"The iron—" Doss started.

"Weighs the same whether it's standing still or moving," Kestrel said. "Move it down."

They went. She watched them go, counting heads the way she counted everything, and when the last mule vanished over the lip of the slope she stood alone on the trail with the wind pulling at her hood and did the thing she always did before danger: she checked her hands.

They were ordinary hands. Brown, scarred across both palms in pale branching lines like frost ferns or river deltas, depending on who looked and how much they'd drunk. Smugglers assumed rope burns. Rope burns were a useful story. The truth—that storm-blood left its mark on the body the way fire leaves marks on a hearthstone, that those lines had been drawn by lightning passing *out* of her rather than into her—was a story she told no one, because the last time she had told it, a village had burned and eleven people had died and the crown had written her name into a book of names that were never spoken aloud.

Exile wasn't freedom. Exile was survival with better scenery. She had understood that by the second winter, and every year since she had gotten better at understanding it, the way you get better at any discipline: by practice, by repetition, by never letting yourself forget the price of the lesson.

The boots on the stair grew louder.

* * *

The shelf held them all, barely. Kestrel lay flat at its edge with a wool blanket half over her shoulders and half over the nearest ingot, watching the trail above through a gap between two boulders. Below her feet, six hundred feet of loose rock fell away into the grey throat of the valley. Somewhere under that rock, water ran; she could feel it, a thin cold thread of presence, the way you can feel a draft under a door. She ignored it. Feeling things was a habit she rationed like brandy.

The patrol came into view at midmorning.

Five men, as she'd guessed. Crown livery under grey travel cloaks, which meant Greywatch garrison, which meant they were real soldiers and not the worse thing she'd spent the night imagining. They moved well for men on a boring duty—spread out, weapons loose, eyes working. One of them led a dog.

The dog changed the arithmetic entirely.

Kestrel went very still, the way she'd taught herself to go still years ago, in another life, when stillness was the difference between being a person and being weather. Dogs smelled salt hides and mule sweat and fear. Dogs did not smell magic—magic wasn't a smell—but dogs smelled the *person*, and the person was the problem, because the person's pulse had just jumped and there was nothing in the world she could do about that except breathe slow and think about cold things.

Cold things. High passes. The weight of iron. The exact number of coins in her boot—eleven, plus the false one with the shaved edge she kept for luck, though she'd have denied the luck part under torture.

The patrol reached the point on the trail directly above the shelf. The dog stopped.

Kestrel's whole body became a held note.

The dog's head swung. Its nose worked the air, sorting the mountain's inventory—pine, snowmelt, mule musk drifting up faint from below, the sour ghost of the salted hides. It took one step toward the edge of the trail. Its handler said something bored and yanked the lead, and the dog, deciding that mule musk was yesterday's news, went on.

The boots faded down the stair. The pebble-worrying sound diminished. The wind filled the space behind them with its own indifferent noise.

Kestrel let out her breath in increments, the way she'd been taught—no, not taught. The way she'd taught herself, alone, in the first terrible year, when panic came on her like weather and she'd had no one to talk her down from any of it. In through the nose for four. Hold for seven. Out for eight. Repeat until your hands stop wanting to do something enormous.

Because that was the other thing, the thing beneath the thing. Her hands had wanted to do something enormous. For one black instant, lying under the blanket with a war-dog's nose pointed at her, some old furnace in her chest had stirred and offered itself up—*let me*, it said, in a voice like wind through a broken window, *I can make the dog lie down, I can make the men forget, I can make the whole mountainside sing*—and she had put her hand on that furnace door and held it shut with nothing but will and arithmetic.

Seven years ago, opening that door had killed eleven people. The wrong eleven people. The wrongness of it lived in her like a splinter too deep to draw—she had meant to strike a granary roof, a warning bolt, a sentence of smoke and terror, and the storm had read her rage instead of her aim and brought the lightning down on the ferry crossing where the tax collectors' families huddled under canvas waiting out the squall. She remembered the canvas. She remembered the color of it. Some nights she remembered the sounds, and those nights she walked out into whatever weather existed and stood in it until dawn, because standing in weather was the closest thing to penance the sky would sell her.

So: exile. So: smuggling. So: eleven coins in her boot and a name she never used and a magic she kept locked in a room at the bottom of herself, swept, unlit, visited only on nights like this to confirm it was still locked.

"Clear," she said finally, and her voice came out level, and she hated a little how easy level had become.

* * *

They made the trees by early afternoon, and the trees made everything easier—cover, quiet footing, the mules' felt-wrapped hooves sinking soft into needle-fall. Kestrel set a pace that ate distance without spending the mules, and the men settled into the rhythm of following her, which was the rhythm they liked best. Doss even managed a joke about his grandmother that was, structurally, an apology. She accepted it with a grunt. Grunts were currency too.

By the time they camped, in a hollow where a fallen pine made a natural windbreak, the snow had begun. It came down fine and dry, hissing through the branches, and Kestrel sat apart from the fire with her back against the deadfall, doing the evening's accounting.

Cargo: intact. Patrol: passed, though the schedule change bothered her. Garrisons didn't change schedules for boredom. Someone had paid for extra sweeps of the high passes, and paid recently, and she filed that away in the drawer of her mind marked *worry about later*, next to the other things she was saving for later like a miser saving coins she'd never spend.

Men: fed, warm, asleep within the hour, because smugglers slept the way stones fall—as soon as nothing was holding them up.

And Kestrel sat awake, as she always did, keeping the last watch of the day against no enemy in particular.

This was the hour she hated. Not the patrols, not the passes, not even the nights when the storm-memory came. This hour, when the work was done and done well and there was nothing left to be competent at, and the competence drained away like water through scree and left her sitting alone with the fact of herself. Twenty-nine years old. Seven years in the mountains. No name anyone here would recognize, no friend who knew her true one, no letter she dared write, no road home because home had been the first thing the storm destroyed.

She was good at this life. That was the cruelest part. She had built something skillful and self-sufficient out of the wreckage, and the skillfulness worked exactly as designed, and the design had a flaw at its center shaped exactly like her, and there was no fixing a flaw that was also the architect.

Snow ticked through the branches. The fire popped. Down the hollow, a mule sighed and shifted its weight.

Kestrel pulled her cloak tighter and did what she always did at this hour: she reviewed the exits. South pass, two days, if the snow held off. East ridge, dangerous in winter but passable alone. The free cities, where a woman with no name and eleven coins could disappear into a harbor crowd and start over as someone new, someone with no past at all, someone whose hands were only hands.

She never took the exits. But knowing they were there—that was a kind of warmth. A door you never opened could still keep a room from feeling like a cell.

She was halfway to sleep, sitting upright, when the feeling came.

It came the way weather comes—no, she corrected herself, drowsy and sharp at once. It came the way *pressure* comes, before weather. A leaning on the air. A faint wrongness in the weight of the night, as if the mountain had shifted a degree on its axis and everything now sat slightly askew. The fire's flame stretched tall and thin and leaned east, though there was no wind from the west.

Kestrel opened her eyes.

Above the tree line, far to the north, the clouds had begun to move. Not drift—*move*, purposeful, gathering, boiling up against the stars like ink dropped in water. And threaded through the pressure, faint as a word heard through a wall, came something that was not wind and not thunder and not any sound the Thornfell had ever made in the seven years she'd known it.

It sounded almost like a voice. It sounded almost like it was looking for something.

Every hair on her arms rose. The old furnace behind her ribs stirred once, gently, like a sleeper turning over, and she pressed her palm flat against her sternum as if she could hold the door shut through her own body.

"No," she whispered, to the fire, to the mountain, to whatever was listening. "Not me. Not anymore."

The clouds went on gathering in the north. The pressure eased—or seemed to ease, or perhaps simply turned its attention elsewhere, the way a searching eye moves on from one face to the next. The flame righted itself. The night resumed its ordinary weight.

Kestrel sat awake until the grey beginning of morning, watching the northern sky, telling herself it was a mountain storm, telling herself it was nothing, telling herself the story she was best at: that her buried life was buried, that the past was a country she'd renounced, that nothing was coming for her.

But she did not sleep. And when the others woke and asked about the weather, she looked north for a long moment before answering, and her answer was slower than usual, and her eyes were not on the pass at all.

"Storm coming," she said. "We move now."

And far below, in the valleys where the snow was already falling thicker than any early winter justified, something small was walking through it toward her door.
