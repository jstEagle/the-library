## 2. A Knock at the Door

The first snow of the season came down the mountain like a debt collector.

Kestrel had watched it build all day—gray light thickening over the eastern peaks, the pines going silent in that particular way they did before a storm, birds gone, wind holding its breath. By late afternoon the first flakes were falling fat and slow, and by dusk the world had been erased. Her cabin sat under a foot of new white and kept falling, and she stood at the window with a cup of barley tea going cold in her hands and counted her stores the way other people said their prayers.

Flour enough for three weeks. Salt pork, six slabs. Dried apples, half a sack. Turnips in the cellar pit. A wheel of cheese she'd traded two days' work for at the Halloway farm, wrapped in cloth against the mice. If the pass closed early—and it would; the snow was falling wrong, too heavy, too warm underneath, the kind that meant a real winter—she might not see another face until thaw.

She found, examining the thought honestly, that this did not distress her.

Seven years could teach a person to prefer their own company. Seven years of guiding smugglers through Thornfell's passes, of sleeping in way-stations with men who knew her only as *Vasquez* or *the guide*, of keeping her hands gloved even in summer because the scars on them asked questions she didn't answer—seven years had built her a life with walls so thick she sometimes forgot there had ever been anything on the other side. That was the trick of exile, she'd learned. Everyone thought it was punishment. It wasn't. It was amputation. You learned to stop reaching for the missing limb, and then you learned to stop noticing, and then one day you caught yourself being grateful for the wound because it had made you simple.

Simple was safe. Simple was alive.

She drank the cold tea anyway, washed the cup, set it on its shelf. Fed the fire one more split of pine. Checked the shutter latches out of habit rather than need—the cabin was hers, built with her own hands in the second year, and nothing got through those shutters that she didn't let in. Then she took down the whetstone and her skinning knife and settled into the chair by the hearth to do the evening's small work, because idle hands were the ones that remembered things.

Outside, the snow fell without sound. Inside, the blade whispered against stone. The candle on the table burned steady, tallow-smelling, ordinary.

That was when someone knocked.

Three knocks. Small ones. Fists the size of plums, if she'd had to guess, hitting the door low—waist height, maybe lower. Not the knock of a traveler lost in a storm; those came hard and desperate, hammering. This was polite. Almost apologetic. As though whoever stood on her step understood they were imposing and wanted her to know they'd be brief about it.

Kestrel did not move for a long moment. The knife rested flat on her knee. Snow ticked softly against the shutter.

No one knocked on Kestrel Vasquez's door. That was not an accident; that was architecture. She had placed the cabin a half-day from the smugglers' trail, off no map Odile Ferran had ever drawn—or rather, off no map anyone still living had ever drawn, because the woman who'd made the good maps had drunk herself out of the capital years ago. Hunters didn't come here. Trappers kept to the valley floor. And children—

Children especially did not come here. Children were carried away from the Thornfells by sensible parents who told them stories about the storm-witch of the high passes, and the stories worked, because fear was cheaper than fences.

Three more knocks. Softer now. The kind that expected nothing.

Kestrel rose, took the poker from beside the hearth, and opened the door with her left hand while her right kept the iron ready, and the cold poured in around a shape so small she nearly laughed.

A boy. Six years old, perhaps seven—thin as a winter branch, standing hip-deep in the drift that had piled against her step. He wore a coat that had been made for someone twice his size, sleeves rolled four times at each wrist, hem dragging in the snow, and beneath it layers of what looked like sacking. His hair was dark and crusted with ice. His lips had gone the gray-blue of old bruises, and his eyelashes were frozen into little white combs, and he was looking up at her with an expression she could not immediately name because she had never seen it directed at herself before.

It took her a moment to understand that it was trust. Plain, unguarded, total trust—as though he had walked a very long way through a killing storm to exactly this door, and having arrived, considered his journey complete.

"Are you lost?" she said.

He didn't answer. He just kept looking at her—at her face first, and then, deliberately, down to her right hand where it gripped the poker, bare because she'd forgotten the glove in her own kitchen, and the scars showed silver-pale in the candlelight: the Lichtenberg flowers lightning had bloomed up her forearm seven years ago, branching, ferning, the mark the storm had left on the flesh it had used.

Most people who saw those scars flinched. She'd watched grown soldiers cross themselves and back away. This child looked at them the way another child might look at a friend's freckles—with mild, unhurried interest, as if confirming something he'd already suspected.

Then he swayed.

Adrenaline made the decision for her. She dropped the poker, caught him under the arms before his knees finished folding, and hauled him inside—one arm hooked around his ribs, kicking the door shut behind her with her heel, the wind screaming briefly through the gap and dying as it latched. He weighed almost nothing. He weighed less than the wood she carried in every morning. Under the oversized coat his body was shaking in great shuddering waves, the deep muscle-tremor of cold that has gotten past all defending, and his boots—she felt this with a lurch—were soaked through, and had been for hours. Maybe a day.

"You idiot," she told him, laying him down on the hearthstones. "You absolute—" But there was no one to finish the sentence to. He was already half-conscious, eyes fluttering, and she was already moving: blanket from the chest, the good wool one, then the second blanket, then her own cloak over both. She knelt and chafed his hands, those small blue fingers, working blood back into them while the fire cracked and threw heat against her face.

Frostbite, both cheeks, both ears, the fingertips—caught early, she thought, caught before the black came. She'd seen frostbite take fingers on the passes. She knew the look of the border between hurt and ruined, and he was on the right side of it, barely. His feet worried her most. She peeled the wet boots off—no socks underneath, none, in this weather—and the skin beneath was waxy and white and painless, which was the bad sign, the very bad sign, and she rubbed them until he whimpered, which paradoxically relieved her, because pain meant living nerves.

He whimpered again when she pressed too hard on the left heel, and then his eyes opened, gray as slate, and fixed on her face with that same maddening calm.

"No frostbite in your mouth," she said. "So you can talk. Who sent you? Where are your people?"

Nothing. He looked at her, and blinked slowly, and looked at the fire instead, as though she'd asked him something tedious.

"Your parents. Your village. There are villages in the low valleys—Halloway, Brindle—did you come from Brindle? Someone must have—"

He turned his head and looked directly at the candle burning on the table across the room.

Kestrel stopped rubbing his feet.

Because the flame had moved. Not guttered—she knew guttering, knew drafts, knew every treacherous current of air in a cabin she'd built plank by plank—but *bent*, leaned toward the boy like a plant toward sun, stretched tall and thin and trembling, and held there. Held. The tallow flame stood a full finger-length taller than it had any right to stand, quivering, straining, and the light in the room changed with it, went strange and gold and faintly wrong, shadows leaning the opposite direction as though the dark itself were being pulled gently toward the hearth.

The boy watched the candle with an expression of mild satisfaction. Then he looked back at Kestrel, and something in his face said, plainly, in the wordless grammar of children: *there, you see.*

Her hands had gone still on his feet. Her heart was doing something complicated and painful behind her ribs, a stuttering arrhythmia of recognition and refusal, because she knew what moved fire. She knew what bent flame toward a living body the way her storms had once bent toward her, answering her moods before she gave them permission, and she had spent seven years burying that knowledge under arithmetic and altitude and the deliberate forgetting of everything she had been before the night the sky answered her grief and killed three people whose only crime was standing in the wrong place wearing the wrong colors.

"There is no magic in the Thornfells," she said aloud, to the boy, to the room, to herself. "There is nothing up here but rock and weather and me."

The candle straightened. Bent again. Held.

The boy opened his mouth.

She waited. Part of her—a large part, a sensible part—hoped he wouldn't speak at all, because whatever he said next was going to make this worse, and it was already so much worse than a lost child in the snow.

He said one word.

It was not a word she knew. It was older than any word she knew—she felt that immediately, the way you feel the age of a stone by the wear of it. Two syllables, low and round, with a weight in the middle of them like a coal settling in a grate, and when he spoke it the candle flame leapt upright and burned suddenly white at its heart, and every hair on Kestrel's arms rose beneath the scars, and the fire in the hearth sighed—a long, soft exhalation, almost human, almost contented—and the whole cabin went warm in a way that had nothing to do with pine splits.

The boy closed his mouth. He looked tired now, deeply tired, the tiredness of a long road finally ended, and his eyes were already drifting shut as the warmth pulled him under.

"What," Kestrel said. Her voice came out rough. "What did you say? Say it again."

But he was asleep. Genuinely asleep, breath slow and even, color creeping back into his lips, one small hand escaped from the blankets and resting palm-up on the hearthstones as though in offering. The candle had returned to its ordinary size and its ordinary color, and the room was only a room again, except that Kestrel's heart was still slamming and the word was still hanging in the air, dissolving slowly, and she could feel it the way you feel smoke after the fire's out.

She sat back on her heels. She looked at the child for a long time—the frost-nipped ears beginning to pink up, the ridiculous coat, the hollows under his eyes that spoke of more than one night's walking—and she ran the arithmetic she always ran, the smuggler's sums, risk against cost, and the numbers came out ugly.

A child appears at a hidden cabin on the first snow night. No footprints leading anywhere. No village close enough to walk from, not in this cold, not on those feet. He is mute, or near enough. He carries a coat that fits no one from any valley she knows. And the fire answers him.

Every instinct she owned said *turn him out at dawn*. Send him down to Halloway, pay the widow there to take him in, wash her hands, go back to being simple. Every instinct, honed by seven years and one catastrophe, screamed that things which arrive uninvited carrying old words are omens, and omens are the sky's way of telling you to run.

But he had looked at her scars without flinching.

And he had knocked politely. Three small knocks, at the bottom of the door, like someone who had been taught that big people don't hear small voices—and had walked through a killing storm to the one door in the mountains that belonged to a woman the world had thrown away, and had trusted her completely, instantly, as though he'd known.

As though someone had told him.

Kestrel stayed kneeling by the hearth long after the boy's breathing went deep and slow. The snow kept falling. The candle burned. At last she rose, stiffly, and fetched the basin, and the clean rags, and the salve she rendered from pine pitch and tallow each autumn, and she knelt again and began, carefully, to dress the frostbitten ears and cheeks and fingertips of a stranger's child, working by candlelight, her scarred hands moving with a gentleness she hadn't needed in seven years and had not, until tonight, known she still possessed.

Outside, the storm thickened toward midnight. Inside, the boy slept, and the fire held its warmth like a promise, and once—just once, near the deepest hour, when Kestrel had finally dozed in the chair with the whetstone still loose in her lap—the sleeping child murmured that same low word again, two syllables heavy as a banked coal, and every flame in the cabin leaned toward him in the dark like flowers turning to a sun only they could see.

*Ember,* said the dark of her mind, in a voice she did not know, in a language older than kingdoms. *His word means ember.*

Kestrel woke with a start, heart hammering, certain someone had called her name.

The cabin was silent. The boy slept on. And far to the south, past the passes, past the valleys, past the whole sleeping breadth of the usurper's kingdom, she could have sworn—for just one heartbeat before sense reasserted itself—that she felt something vast and buried turn over in its sleep, and begin, very slowly, to wake.
