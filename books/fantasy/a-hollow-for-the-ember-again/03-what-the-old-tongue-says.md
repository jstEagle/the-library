## 3. What the Old Tongue Says

Maren Ostwick arrived three days after the snow did, which was how Kestrel knew the boy had been sent to her on purpose.

The hedge-witch came down the mountain track with her herb-basket on her hip and her gray braid whipping in the wind, and she did not knock so much as let herself in, because that was what twenty years of trading poultices for smuggled salt bought you in a place like Thornfell. She stopped two steps inside the door. She looked at the child sitting on Kestrel's hearthstone eating porridge like he'd been born there.

"Well," Maren said. "That's new."

"He was on my doorstep," Kestrel said. "Frostbitten. Half-dead."

"And you kept him."

"I kept him alive. There's a difference." Kestrel stirred the pot harder than it needed stirring. "I'm sending him down to Vell when the pass clears. The sisters at the foundling house take—"

"Look at his hands, Kestrel."

Kestrel looked. She had been looking at them for three days without letting herself see them: small hands, chapped and healing, holding the spoon with a grip too careful for a child of six or seven. And when the firelight shifted, the faint tracery beneath the skin of his wrists—not veins. Something finer. Like frost-ferns drawn in old gold ink.

She had those same marks. Higher up, buried under scar tissue she never showed anyone, but she knew their shape the way you know your own heartbeat.

"Sit down, Maren," Kestrel said quietly.

* * *

The boy—Ash, because that was the only word he gave them, spoken once, flat as a stone dropped in a well—sat by the window while the two women talked in low voices across the table. He didn't seem to listen. He seemed to *wait*, the way a door waits.

"He doesn't speak the low tongue at all?" Maren asked.

"Not a word. Not high tongue either. I've tried trader's cant, coast-speech, the valley dialects." Kestrel turned her cup. "He understands me. I'd swear he understands me. But nothing comes out except—" She hesitated. She had not said it aloud yet, because saying it made it real, and real things had consequences, and consequences were the one currency Kestrel Vasquez could not afford to spend.

"Except?"

"Words I don't know. Same words, over and over." She set the cup down. "Four sounds. Maybe five. *Eska-ren-em-bra.* Like that. Always in that order."

Maren had gone very still. Maren was seventy years old and had buried two husbands and delivered half the babies in the foothills, and stillness did not come naturally to her, which meant that when it came, it meant something.

"Say it again," she said.

"*Eska-ren-em-bra.*"

"Slower."

"Maren—"

"*Slower.*"

Kestrel said it slowly. And Ash, at the window, turned his head. Not toward Kestrel. Toward the fire. The flames stood up straighter, all of them at once, like soldiers called to attention, and the room went warm—too warm, a warmth with weight to it, pressing against the skin like a hand laid flat over the sternum.

Then it passed. Ash looked back out the window at the snow.

Maren's hands were shaking. Kestrel watched her press them flat against the table until they stopped.

"That's not a language anyone speaks anymore," Maren said. "I heard it once. Once, sixty years ago, in a cellar under a tower that isn't there anymore, from an old woman who was older than kingdoms and who made me swear on my own grave-dirt never to repeat what she taught me." Her eyes came up. "It's binding-language, girl. Pre-dynastic. Older than Aldric's line, older than the line Aldric's line usurped. It wasn't built for talking. It was built for *holding things shut.*"

"Holding what shut?"

"You know what."

"I don't," Kestrel said, and it was almost true. "I know storms. I know iron weights and toll schedules and which passes freeze first. I don't know anything about—"

"*Ember,*" said Ash.

Both women turned. The boy was looking at them now, directly, and the word—the fifth sound, the one Kestrel hadn't been able to parse—came out clear and round and terribly certain. "*Embra.* Ember." He touched his own chest, then pointed at the hearth, then touched his chest again. Then, with the terrible patience of someone explaining something obvious to people refusing to understand, he pointed through the floor. Through the stone. South, where the capital lay, though he couldn't have known that. Through the whole spine of the world, to something underneath everything.

"Embra," he said again, softer. Almost gentle. The way you'd say the name of something sleeping.

The candles guttered. All six of them, in a room with no draft.

Kestrel stood up so fast her chair went over backward.

* * *

She spent the afternoon chopping wood she didn't need, because her hands needed work or they were going to do something she'd regret.

Seven years. Seven years since the storm off Cape Sorrow, since the lightning had answered her grief and struck the ferry that carried not the slavers she'd aimed it at but forty refugees fleeing the same slavers, since the tribunal had taken one look at her storm-scarred arms and pronounced the sentence everyone already knew: exile, north, Thornfell, let the mountains keep her. Seven years of teaching herself to be small. To be a guide, a calculator, a woman whose only magic was arithmetic—cargo weight against guard rotations, weather odds against payoff size. She had buried the other thing so deep she'd almost convinced herself it was dead. Almost. On nights like this one, with snow coming down like a curtain between her and every living soul, she could pretend the silence in her blood was peace instead of a held breath.

And now a mute child was sitting in her kitchen speaking the language of bindings, and the fire leaned toward him like a dog toward its master, and every hair on Kestrel's arms was standing up because some animal part of her recognized what was in that room. Recognized its *shape*. The same shape as the thing coiled under her own scars, the thing she'd spent seven years starving.

*Ember.*

She split the last log harder than necessary and told herself the plan again, the way she told herself cargo numbers: send him down to Vell with the spring caravan. Foundling house. Clean hands. Whatever he was, whatever was written into him, it wasn't hers. She had done her sentence. She owed the world nothing, and the world had repaid the favor.

The logic was flawless. It always was. That was the trouble with arithmetic—it never accounted for the way the boy had looked at her burned hands the first night, without flinching, without pity, just looking, like they were simply hands. Or the way he'd placed his small palm flat on her forearm yesterday while she was banking the fire, right over the worst of the scarring, and the pain that lived there year-round had gone quiet for a full minute. Like being forgiven by something that had no business forgiving anyone.

She came back inside at dusk and found Maren asleep in the good chair and Ash standing at the table, holding Kestrel's mending basket, frowning at the hole in the knee of his own coat with the intensity of a general studying a lost battle.

The coat was a ruin. Too big for him—someone else's, cut for a grown man, sleeves rolled four times—and burnt through in places, scorched along the hem in patterns that Kestrel's eye, trained by seven years of reading sign on trails, read all too well. Those weren't campfire burns. Those were deliberate. Ritual-shaped. Someone had put that coat on him after doing something to him, and the something had leaked heat like a forge.

He looked up at her. Held out the coat. Held out the needle. His expression said, plainly, in the universal grammar of children: *fix it.*

"I should send you away," Kestrel said.

He waited. He was very good at waiting.

"The pass is closed anyway," she heard herself say. "Nobody travels in this. It would be a death sentence, sending you down now. Even I wouldn't take that contract." She crossed the room, took the needle from his fingers, threaded it. "So it's not a choice. It's just arithmetic. You stay till the thaw. That's all. Don't make it mean anything."

Ash watched her take the first stitch with an attention most adults couldn't manage, and then, satisfied that the repair was underway, he climbed onto the bench beside her and simply sat, shoulder almost touching her arm, watching the snow come down.

Kestrel sewed. The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls, the cabin settled into itself for the night, and the warmth held, steady and deep, warmer than her stove alone had ever managed—as if the house itself had decided to lean toward the boy the way the flames did.

She hated how much she didn't hate it.

* * *

Maren woke near midnight and found them exactly like that: the exiled storm-caller stitching a ruined coat by candlelight, the mute vessel of something primordial dozing against her side, and the witch understood, with the weary clarity of the very old, that the decision had already been made somewhere below the level of words, and that arguing with it now would be like arguing with a river.

She got up quietly to bank the fire, and Ash opened his eyes and watched her, and she paused, ladle in hand, and said—in careful, rusty, sixty-year-old memory—the five sounds.

"*Eska-ren-em-bra.*"

The boy sat bolt upright. For the first time since the doorstep, his face moved past calm into something blazing and urgent, and he began to speak—fast, fluid, a whole torrent of that ancient tongue pouring out of him, hands flying, pointing at the fire, at the ground, south, at Maren, at himself, at Kestrel, *at Kestrel*, both hands extended toward her scars as if to say *you know, you KNOW*—

And the candles flared. Every one. Six feet of flame standing straight up, white-hot, and the warmth crashed through the room like a wave, and outside the window the snow stopped falling—all of it, every flake hanging motionless in the air for one impossible heartbeat, the whole mountainside holding its breath—

Then it let go. Snow fell. Candles sank back to ordinary light. Ash stood in the middle of the room, chest heaving, looking at Kestrel with an expression no child should have to wear: the look of someone carrying a message that will kill the person who receives it.

Maren set down the ladle. Her voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"It's waking up," she said. "Whatever they bound under the capital. It's been bound for three hundred years, and it's waking up, and this child is the key they made to open the door." She looked at Kestrel, and Kestrel saw that the old woman was afraid in a way that had nothing to do with herself. "And it sent him to *you*, Kestrel. Of all the doors in all the world, it knocked on yours."

Kestrel looked down at her hands. At the needle, still threaded, still warm from her grip. At the half-mended coat draped over her knees, and the small boy standing in the firelight wearing the face of a messenger who has finally delivered his message.

Outside, far to the south, past the frozen passes, past the sleeping valleys—a light flickered on the horizon that was not dawn, and did not fade.

"There's a rider coming up the pass," Kestrel said. "I can feel the horse."

Maren frowned. "Through the storm? Who rides in—"

"Not a patrol." Kestrel stood, and the old fear rose in her blood like water in a well, familiar and cold. "They ride black horses and they don't leave tracks. Maren—get your basket. We're leaving tonight."
