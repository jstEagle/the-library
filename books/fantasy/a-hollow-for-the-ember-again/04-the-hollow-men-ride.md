## 4. The Hollow Men Ride

Cold. Cold like teeth.

The boy wakes with the word already in his mouth and no word to say it. *Kalt.* No. Older. The old word for cold is a sound like wind through a crack, and he makes it into the dark of the loft, small, because making it makes the cold a thing that can be named, and named things are smaller than unnamed things. That is the first law he knows. He does not know where he learned it. He knows it the way he knows his own hands: without beginning.

Below him, the cabin breathes. Wood ticking as it cools. The banked fire's slow red pulse, like something sleeping under ash. And her—Kestrel—the storm-woman, turning over in her bed by the hearth, one scarred hand flung out of the blankets even in sleep, as if she is always reaching for lightning or holding it off.

He counts her breaths. Fourteen to the minute when she dreams quiet. Nine when she dreams bad. Tonight: nine.

*Bad dream,* he thinks, in the wordless way he thinks most things, pictures-without-words: a woman on a road, a door closing, hands full of rain. He does not know whose memory this is. It comes to him sometimes, other people's weather.

Then the dogs start.

Not her dogs—she has none—but the valley's dogs, far down the mountain, three farms away, then two, then one, a chain of barking lighting up the dark like a fuse running toward them. Ash sits up so fast the blanket slides. His ears do a thing they have always done: they open wider than ears should. He hears the barking stop at the nearest farm—not fading, *stopping*, cut off mid-note—and that silence is worse than any sound.

Boots. Many boots. Not marching. Riding, but wrong, the hoofbeats too even, too together, like one animal wearing many legs.

He is down the ladder before he decides to move, bare feet on cold boards, and Kestrel is already awake, already standing, and the look on her face is not surprise. That is the thing he will remember longest, later: she is not surprised. She looks like someone who has been waiting seven years for a knock she hoped would never come, and now that it has, there is almost relief in it, the way a wound feels relief when it finally opens.

"Cellar," she says. Her voice is low and flat and does not shake, and he hates that he can hear the shaking underneath it anyway, the way you can hear the sea under sand. "Now. Don't come out for anyone but me. Anyone. Even my voice, if it sounds wrong."

She takes his face in both hands. Her palms are ridged with old burn-scar, lightning-written, and they are warm, warmer than hands should be, warm like stones left too near a fire. For one heartbeat he feels it through her skin—a hum, a held breath, a storm on a leash made of will alone—and the hum leans toward him, or he toward it, he cannot tell—

She lets go like the touch burned her.

"Go," she says.

* * *

The cellar smells of earth and apples and old iron. Kestrel shoves him down behind the cistern, wedges a crate of winter turnips in front of him like a wall, pulls a sacking over his head until the world goes to brown weave and smell. Through the gaps he watches her climb back up and pull the hatch shut, and the last strip of lamplight narrows to a gold thread, then nothing.

Dark. Dark like a mouth.

In the dark, the boy does what he always does when he is afraid: he listens the world into pieces.

Wind, west, carrying snow-grit that ticks against the shutters like fingernails. The fire settling. His own heart, fast, a bird in a box. And beneath all of it, coming up through the ground itself, through stone and root and frozen water, a feeling he has no name for and which frightens him more than the hoofbeats:

Hollowness.

It rides toward the cabin inside the riders. He can feel it the way you feel a storm before the clouds arrive, except it is the opposite of a storm—it is an absence shaped like men, a hunger wearing armor, and every hoofbeat sounds like a bell with the tongue torn out. *Bong,* says nothing. *Bong.* Nothing again.

He presses his hands over his ears and it does not help, because the hollow is not a sound. It is a hole in sound. And some deep-buried part of him, older than his body, older than his silence, recognizes it the way a lock recognizes a key turned wrong.

*Vessel,* whispers the hole in the sound, in a voice like dust falling. *Where is the vessel.*

Above him, muffled by floorboards, someone knocks on the cabin door. Three knocks, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Voices come through the floor like water through a roof.

Ash cannot see. He makes pictures instead, and the pictures are worse than seeing would be.

The door opening. Snow blowing in. Lamplight swinging. And them—the Hollow Men—he knows them without being told, knows them from the hole in the sound of their breathing. They breathe through masks. He can hear the whistle of it, air pulled through carved bone or ceramic, in-out, in-out, all of them in rhythm, as if the masks share one set of lungs.

A voice asks questions. The voice is polite. That is the worst part; the politeness has no bottom to it, no warmth anywhere under it, politeness poured over emptiness like glaze over a cracked pot.

"—a child. Small. Dark-haired. May be unable to speak." A pause, the length of a lie being weighed. "He is property of the Crown. There is a reward for his return unmarked. There is a punishment for those who delay us."

Kestrel's voice answers, and Ash marvels at it even while terror eats his stomach. It is bored. Genuinely bored, or worn into boredom, the voice of a woman interrupted during a chore she resents. "No children come up here. Children don't like the mountain. Neither do I, most days. You want the shepherd track below the ridge—"

"We will look," says the polite voice, "nonetheless."

Floorboards groan under many boots. Boots cross above his head—one, two, five, eight, he counts them by weight, some heavy with iron, one lighter, almost floating, and that one stops directly overhead and stands there for so long that Ash forgets how to breathe.

Through the sacking, through the dark, he feels it looking down. Not with eyes. With the hollow. It pours downward through the floor like cold water finding a crack, and it touches the edge of what he is, and the thing inside him—the old thing, the word-thing, the ember-thing he has carried since before he can remember and which he has never once let wake—stirs.

*No,* he tells it, in the old tongue, the only prayer he owns. *Sleep. Sleep. Sleep.*

It sleeps. But it turns over first, like a sleeper disturbed, and its turning sends out one thread of warmth, one hair-fine line of heat through the frozen ground, up through the floorboards—

And the thing overhead inhales sharply through its mask.

"Something here," it says. Its voice is different from the others'—younger, or newer, less worn away. "Under the floor. Warmth. Fresh warmth."

Silence. Then Kestrel's voice, still bored, still flat, and underneath the boredom a wire pulled tight enough to sing: "That's my cellar. Root vegetables keep better above freezing. You'd know that if any of you had ever grown anything."

"Step aside, woman."

"I live here. You step aside."

What happens next, Ash hears rather than sees, and he spends years afterward assembling it into sense.

A boot heel turns on the boards. A hand—her hand—comes down flat on the table upstairs, and the slap of it is ordinary, a tired woman's gesture, exasperation given flesh. But the lamp flame leaps. Every candle in the cabin stands up straight at once, flames stretched tall and thin as needles, burning blue-white at their tips, and the air fills with a taste like the moment before a strike of summer lightning, copper and ozone and rain-that-hasn't-fallen-yet.

And outside, above the cabin, above the mountain, the sky answers.

There is no cloud. The stars are out, hard and cold in their thousands. But thunder rolls anyway—a single long roll, low, directionless, the sound of something enormous shifting its weight in its sleep—and every hair on Ash's arms rises, and the water in the cistern beside him trembles into rings without being touched.

The Hollow Man above him steps back. One step. Two.

"You," says the polite voice, and for the first time the politeness cracks, and what shows through the crack is fear, old fear, institutional fear, the fear of an organization that has met her kind before. "You are Vasquez."

"I'm nobody," says Kestrel, and the lamps sink slowly back to orange, and the thunder fades down the valley like a guest leaving, and her voice could not be more tired or more ordinary. "Storms happen on mountains. Ask any shepherd. Now get off my floor before you put your boot through my ceiling, because I'm not fixing it."

A long silence. The hollow-things confer in the language of silence, which they speak fluently.

"The vessel is not here," says the polite voice finally, and it is not a conclusion, it is a decision, and Ash understands with a chill that they are choosing not to believe what they felt, because believing it would mean reporting it, and reporting her means reporting the storm-blood, and some things even kings' men prefer not to carry in their mouths. "But we will return. We sweep this valley weekly now. If the vessel passes through these mountains, woman, it will be found. And anyone who has sheltered it—" the mask-whistle pauses, savoring, "—will be shown what the Crown does with harbors."

The door closes. The hooves begin, that too-even rhythm, receding down the track toward the next farm, the next door, the next polite question.

Ash stays in the dark a long time. Long after the last hoofbeat. Long after the hollow-feeling fades below the threshold of his skin. He stays until the hatch opens and lamplight pours down the ladder-steps and Kestrel's face appears above him, gray-lit, drawn, older than it was at sundown.

"It's me," she says. "Come up slow."

He climbs into her arms without meaning to. She holds him for exactly four seconds—he counts—and then sets him on the bench and crouches to look into his eyes, her scarred hands gripping his shoulders hard enough to leave marks she doesn't notice.

"They'll come back," she says. Not to him. To herself. To the room. "Weekly. Sweeping. Which means I have days, maybe a week, before they ride up here with a warrant and no patience." Her thumb moves against his shoulder, back and forth, back and forth, an unconscious rhythm, and he realizes she is doing it to calm herself, not him. "I should put you on the south road tonight. Walk you to the river, give you coin, wash my hands—"

The fire pops. Outside, far off, thunder mutters once, though the sky is still clear, still star-hard, still innocent.

Kestrel stops talking. Looks at the window. Looks at her own hands, spread open in the lamplight, the lightning-scars pale across the backs of them, and Ash sees her flex her fingers slowly, one at a time, like a woman checking whether a door she nailed shut seven years ago is still nailed.

"That wasn't permission," she whispers to her hands. "I didn't call it. I was afraid and it just—it answered."

The boy reaches out and covers one scarred hand with his small one. He has no words. He has never had words. But he has the old tongue, the one that lives under words, and he presses into her skin, through touch, the oldest word he knows—the one Maren will later translate, the one that started everything:

*Ember.*

Her head snaps around. Her eyes are wide and wet and terrified, and in them he sees the truth she will spend chapters denying: not that he is in danger from her past.

That her past has found its shape again, and it is small, and mute, and sitting in her kitchen warming its hands at her fire.

"We leave tomorrow," Kestrel says, to the boy, to the fire, to the thunder still grumbling somewhere over the peaks like a promise. "Both of us. There's a woman down-valley who reads dead languages and owes me nothing, which means I owe her everything by morning. Pack nothing you can't run with."

Outside, the snow begins again, soft and thick, filling in hoofprints, erasing the road, erasing everything except the way forward.

Ash falls asleep that night in the loft, listening to her pace below him, all night, nine breaths to the minute, and the ember-word glowing quietly in his chest like a coal someone forgot to bury.
