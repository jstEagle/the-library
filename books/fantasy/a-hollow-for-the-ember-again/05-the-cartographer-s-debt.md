## 5. The Cartographer's Debt

The town was called Bracken's Rest, which was a lie twice over—there had never been a bracken in sight of it, and no one who lived there rested. It sat where the Thornfell foothills finally gave up and flattened into the king's road, a smear of slate roofs and mud that existed for one purpose: to take money from travelers too tired to bargain.

Kestrel left Ash with Maren at the shrine two miles up the valley, wrapped in the hedge-witch's spare cloak, with instructions delivered in a whisper sharp enough to cut: *If anyone comes, you're her nephew's boy, mute since a fever, and you've never seen my face.* Ash had looked up at her with those dark, unblinking eyes and touched her scarred hand once—the way he did now, always, as if checking she was still solid—and she had walked away before the gesture could finish undoing her.

She told herself it was strategy. Leave the vessel hidden, scout the town alone, come back clean or not at all. She told herself a great many things these days, and most of them were lies she'd built so carefully they almost bore weight.

* * *

Bracken's Rest smelled of wet wool, tallow, and the particular sourness of beer kept too long in a cellar that flooded every spring. Kestrel came down the main street with her hood up and her stride arranged into the loose, aimless amble of a woman with nowhere important to be. She counted exits without meaning to—the cart track behind the smithy, the footpath over the millrace, the gap between the chandler's and the tithe barn where a person could walk sideways through if she were willing to sacrifice a coat. Seven years of guiding smugglers had taught her that every place was a map of ways out, and that the people who forgot this died in comfortable rooms.

She found Odile Ferran the way she'd known she would: by following the sound of an argument.

The Gilded Boar's common room was half-full of drovers and half-empty of hope, and in the corner farthest from the hearth sat a woman who had clearly been there long enough to establish sovereignty over her table. Three empty cups stood in formation before her. A fourth was under siege. She was perhaps fifty, gray shot through black hair cropped short as a soldier's, with ink stains on her fingers so old and deep they looked like bruises. Her coat had once been fine—you could tell by the buttons, real horn, and the cut of the shoulders—but it had been mended by someone who resented the work.

"—and I am telling you," she was saying to a drover who had made the mistake of sitting near her, "that your route through the Sable Pass is wrong. Not dangerous. Wrong. The pass moved in the quake of the old king's ninth year, and every map drawn since has been copied from maps drawn before. You will follow your pretty little line straight into a scree field, and when they dig you out, I want it noted that I tried."

"Maps don't move," the drover said.

"Mountains move," said Odile Ferran. "Maps just fail to admit it."

Kestrel bought a cup of the house's worst ale from a barman who didn't look at her twice—she'd paid extra for that skill years ago, in coin and in other currencies—and crossed the room and set the cup down on the cartographer's table like a toll.

"I'm looking for someone who mapped the palace at Carreth," she said quietly. "The lower levels."

Odile looked up. Her eyes were bloodshot and quick, and they went over Kestrel the way Kestrel went over mountain terrain: hood first, hands second, posture third, weighing all three against threat. They lingered on Kestrel's scars—of course they did; everyone's eyes did, the pale branching lightning-marks that ran from her right wrist to her collarbone like frost on glass—and then, notably, they did not flinch away.

"Interesting," Odile said. "Because the last person who asked me about the lower levels was a royal clerk, eleven years ago, and he asked me to confirm that no such mapping ever existed. Which was true by then, because his colleagues had burned it."

"I know what they burned."

"Do you." It wasn't a question. It was a door held very slightly ajar, and Kestrel could see the sentry standing behind it.

"I know it was forty-one sheets," Kestrel said. "Vellum, not paper, because you didn't trust paper underground. I know you drew them over four years, mostly at night, because the lower levels were forbidden to survey after the old queen's death and you did it anyway. And I know that when the new king's men came for your archive, you were in the provinces finishing a survey of the eastern watershed, and you came home to ash."

The common room noise seemed to recede, the way it does around a wound being opened.

Odile turned her cup slowly on the table. "Who told you all that?"

"A man named Corvo, who used to run iron past the Thornfell tolls, and who liked to talk about the one famous person he'd ever carried in his wagon. He said you rode with him for six days after the burning, and you didn't speak the whole time, and when you finally did, you asked him how much he'd charge to drive a wagon into the palace courtyard."

A sound escaped Odile—not quite a laugh, more the ghost of one, exhumed. "Corvo. Gods. He's dead, then?"

"Two winters back. His heart gave out on the high trail."

"Good," Odile said, and then, catching herself: "I mean—he'd have hated dying slow. He'd have wanted it quick and inconvenient." She pushed the opposite bench out with her boot. An invitation, grudging as a tax refund. "Sit down, storm-hands. You've spent something to find me, and nobody spends like that for trivia. So either you're going to rob me, recruit me, or ruin me, and I'd like to know which before I finish this cup."

Kestrel sat. "What if it's all three?"

"Then at least the evening won't be boring."

* * *

It took Kestrel the better part of an hour to decide how much truth to spend, and in the end she surprised herself by spending nearly all of it.

Not everything. Not the word *ember*, not yet—that word still felt too hot to handle, too much like handing a stranger a live coal and asking her to carry it in her bare hands. But she gave Odile the shape of it: a child who spoke a language older than the kingdom, a king whose men wore masks and asked questions in valleys they had no business visiting, a road south to the capital that had to be invisible. She watched Odile's face while she talked, and Odile's face did what faces do when they have been waiting years for a reason: it sharpened.

"The Hollow Men," Odile said, when Kestrel described the riders Maren had seen sweeping the valley. "They came through here last month. Bought horses, paid in new-minted crown silver, asked no questions and answered none. My landlord couldn't stop talking about their gloves. He said they never took them off. Even to eat."

"They don't take anything off," Kestrel said. "That's rather the point of them."

"And the child." Odile leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for the first time the drink fell away from her voice and something harder showed underneath—the mind that had memorized forbidden corridors by candlelight. "Mute. Old tongue. Unafraid of you." Her eyes flicked again to the scars. "You'll forgive me for noticing that children are usually afraid of you. Or of what you are. The stories out of the capital after the succession—"

"I know the stories."

"I helped write some of them," Odile said flatly. "Cartographers write more than coastlines, whatever we pretend. We choose what goes on the map and what stays off, and staying off is its own kind of story. Your storm-calling stayed off every official document for three years. Then the night the old king died, it got put on very loudly, and very badly, and by morning you were the reason the realm needed a strong young successor." She turned her cup again. "I drew the border of you, Kestrel Vasquez. Inset map, lower corner, *Disturbances in the Thornfell Provinces*. I was ordered to. I was also curious enough not to hate the order, and I've regretted both of those things for seven years."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind the bar, a keg was tapped and a small cheer went up for reasons that had nothing to do with any of this.

"You regret drawing me?" Kestrel asked.

"I regret believing the frame." Odile's voice dropped. "I was a good servant of the crown for twenty-two years. Good servants believe the frame, because the frame is what keeps the map flat and the world navigable. And then one day I surveyed the lower levels of the palace—which I was not supposed to do, which is why I did it—and I found corridors on the oldest foundations that don't appear in any plan the Office of Works holds. Older than the dynasty. Older than the *name* of the dynasty. Walls with channels cut into them, running down and down, converging on a chamber beneath the foundation stone. And every channel was scored inside, deep grooves, like—" she paused, hunting for the word, and Kestrel felt the word arrive in her own chest before Odile found it, "—like something had flowed through them. For a very long time."

"What did you tell the Office?"

"I told them nothing. I kept the sheets. Forty-one of them. I told myself I'd bring them forward when there was someone worth bringing them to." Her mouth twisted. "And then the old king died, and the young king rose, and within the year a clerk arrived with a warrant and a brazier, and I stood in my own archive and watched forty-one sheets curl. Do you know what vellum smells like when it burns? Like a living thing. Because it is one. I stood there and smelled my life's work die, and the officer in charge thanked me for my cooperation, and I signed a statement confirming that no such mapping had ever existed, and I have been drinking in places like this ever since, because places like this don't ask a woman what she used to be."

Kestrel sat with that. Outside, the wind had come up off the flats, worrying the shutters, and somewhere a dog had opinions about it.

"I buried something too," Kestrel said. It came out rougher than she intended. "Seven years ago. Not papers. Bigger. I buried it so deep I told myself it wasn't there anymore, and I was good at the telling, and then a child knocked on my door in the first snow and dug it up with one word." She turned her own cup, matching Odile's motion without deciding to. "So I'm not going to insult you by promising this ends well. I'm not even going to promise it ends. I'm going to ask you to draw me roads that don't exist, into a city that wants me dead, for a reason I can't fully give you yet. And I'm going to ask knowing exactly what it costs a person to be useful to a cause again after they've learned what causes do to useful people."

Silence. The drover who'd been lectured earlier had retreated to safer company. The barman watered beer and pretended deafness, which was the finest service the Gilded Boar offered.

"Why the child?" Odile asked at last. "Strip everything else away. Why does a smuggler's guide cross half a kingdom for a mute boy she's known a fortnight?"

Because he touched my hand like I was solid, Kestrel thought. Because when the candles guttered at his word, I felt less alone than I have in seven years, and that terrifies me more than the Hollow Men do. Because everyone I was ever responsible for died, and this time I would like, just once, to be early enough.

"Because he's carrying something," she said aloud, "and everyone who knows what it is wants to use him for it. And I have watched what happens to things the crown uses."

Something moved behind Odile's bloodshot eyes—recognition, the specific kind, exile meeting exile across a table of bad beer. Two women who had each, in different ways, been archived by the same regime: one burned, one banished, both filed under *no longer exists*.

"My wagon's in the stable yard," Odile said. "It doesn't look like much, but it has a false floor, a false roof, and a drawer under the driver's seat that opens only if you lift the left side first—a trick I learned from a lockmaker in Vell whose name I traded for a map of his cellar. I have inks, pens, calipers, and a memory that has never once lost a corridor. What I don't have—" and here her voice caught, just barely, on the edge of the sentence, and she drove through it the way she must once have driven a survey line through unmapped country, "—what I haven't had in eleven years is a single reason to use any of it."

"And now?"

"Now I have a child who speaks pre-dynastic binding-tongue heading for a chamber I found under the palace that the dynasty itself pretends isn't there." She drained her cup and set it down with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a decision. "The king's men burned my life's work, Kestrel Vasquez. Very well. I have decided to become a problem they cannot burn."

* * *

They settled terms in the way of professionals: quickly, without sentiment, and with one clause neither of them spoke aloud but both understood—if it went wrong, each would run in a different direction and neither would look back. Kestrel had made that clause before. She had never once honored it. She suspected Odile hadn't either.

She was at the door when Odile spoke again, quieter, the drink entirely gone from her voice now.

"The chamber under the foundation stone. There was one thing on the sheets I never told the clerk, because he'd have burned it twice." She was looking at her own ink-stained fingers. "Around the walls of that chamber, carved into the oldest stone, there was writing. I copied it faithfully—I copy everything, it's the disease of the profession. I never could read it. No one at the Office could. I showed a fragment once to an old linguist in Vell, and he went white, closed his shutters, and told me to take it to a priest or a pyre." She looked up. "He said it was older than the kingdom. He said it was the language they spoke *before* kings, when the land itself had to be spoken to directly, because it was listening."

Kestrel's hand found the doorframe. The wood was cold and ordinary and held.

"How large was the fragment?" she managed.

"Forty-one sheets' worth," said Odile Ferran, and reached for her coat.
