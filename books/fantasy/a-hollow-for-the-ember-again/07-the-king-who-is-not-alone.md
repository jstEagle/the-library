## 7. The King Who Is Not Alone

The crown was warm.

It was always warm now. Aldric Vane had worn it for thirty years and could not remember a single morning when it had been cold against his brow—not since the night in the under-crypt when he had pressed it down over his hair and spoken the words the old texts promised would make him more than a man. He had expected fire. He had expected glory. He had not expected this: a warmth like a hand cupped at the back of his skull, patient as a spider, whispering.

*Good morning, little king,* said the Ember.

Aldric opened his eyes. The ceiling of his bedchamber was painted with clouds—his grandfather's commission, a sky that never rained—and for three full seconds he did not know where he was. The panic rose in him like floodwater, and then the warmth at his skull pulsed once, gently, and the room assembled itself around him: the canopy, the salt-bell chime on the sill, the grey light of a capital morning. *You are Aldric Vane, third of your name, unifier of the Thornfell marches, beloved of the gods and the ledger-keepers alike.* The knowledge slid back into him like a key into a lock, and he exhaled.

That was how it worked now. He woke empty, and the Ember filled him.

He had stopped telling anyone about the empty part years ago.

* * *

The dressing took an hour because the kingdom required it. Two valets, a barber, a master of robes. Aldric sat before the tall silver mirror while they worked, and he studied his own face with the detached interest of a man reading someone else's letter.

Thirty. He looked thirty. He had looked thirty for eleven years.

His father had gone grey at fifty. His grandfather had been a ruin by sixty, hands spotted, voice cracked, carried up the coronation stairs on two men's shoulders. Aldric should have been greying at the temples by now, should have had lines enough to map a life. Instead the mirror gave him smooth cheeks and dark hair and eyes that were perhaps a shade too bright, a shade too steady, the way a lamp is steady when the oil has been replaced with something else.

*You are welcome,* the Ember murmured, amused.

"Your Majesty?" The barber had paused, razor hovering. "Did you speak?"

"No." Aldric watched his reflection smile. It was a good smile; he had practiced it for decades and it needed no practice anymore, which was somehow worse. "Continue."

The Ember did not speak aloud to anyone but him. That was the one mercy. In public it only hummed, low and content, whenever he stood near crowds—a vibration in his teeth, a sweetness at the base of his tongue, like sugar dissolving in hot water. It liked crowds. It liked worship. It drank attention the way other men drank wine, and the kingdom gave it oceans of the stuff: banners with his face, bells rung at his passing, children taught to sing his name in schoolrooms from Vell to the coast.

*They love you,* it whispered during audiences, while petitioners knelt and wept with gratitude. *They love us.*

*They love the story,* Aldric thought back, careful to keep the thought small and dull, a stone thrown into deep water. *Not me.*

*There is no difference anymore, little king. That is what I bought for you.*

And there it was—the thing he tried not to look at directly, the way one avoids looking at the sun. There was no difference anymore. He could no longer always tell which memories were his.

* * *

He held council in the Map Room, because the Map Room had windows and the Ember liked him to see the city while he ruled it. Six ministers, a chancellor, the Lord Marshal with his reports. Sunlight came through the glass in long amber bars and lay across the great table like something spilled.

"The Thornfell situation," the Lord Marshal began, and Aldric felt the warmth at his skull sharpen into attention, a cat lifting its head.

"Elaborate."

"A child, sire. Runaway from the foundling house at Greywater. The priests are—" the Marshal hesitated, choosing words the way a man chooses footing on scree, "—the priests believe the boy may be a vessel. Old blood. Pre-dynastic. There were signs at the orphanage. Candles behaving strangely. A language no one recognized."

Aldric kept his face still. Inside, the Ember went very quiet, and its quiet was louder than any shouting.

*A vessel,* it said at last, and the word came out of it slow and wondering, the way a starving man says *bread*. *They made another. After all these years, they dared to make another.*

"Who made it?" Aldric asked aloud. The ministers blinked; they had not heard the question's twin.

"We don't know, sire. Hedge-folk, possibly. The old rites survive in the mountains the way weeds survive in walls. But the boy fled east, toward the high passes. And there is—" the Marshal produced a paper, laid it flat, "—a complication. Storm-signs in the passes. Untimed, unnatural. The border garrisons report lightning out of clear sky, twice in one week. The old records say such weather follows storm-blooded lineages. If a caller lives out there—"

*Her,* said the Ember, and now its voice had teeth in it, and Aldric felt the warmth at his skull tighten like a fist closing in his hair. *The Vasquez girl. The one who burned. I remember her. I remember her blood singing across the valley like a struck bell. Do you remember her, little king?*

He did. That was the cruelty of it—the Ember ate his memories and left him the worst ones whole. Kestrel Vasquez, nineteen years old, standing in the ruins of the harvest festival with rain standing off her skin in halos, lightning crawling up her arms like jewelry, her face not triumphant but horrified, because the bolt she'd called had gone wide and taken the granary, and the granary had taken forty souls. He remembered signing her exile order himself. He remembered thinking, even then, even furious, even grieving: *she didn't mean it. She simply hadn't learned to hold it.*

He had exiled a frightened girl rather than teach her. Because teaching meant admitting the old blood existed outside the crown's control, and the crown could not afford that admission. The throne was built on the story that all magic flowed through Aldric Vane and nowhere else.

"Yes," Aldric said. "I remember her."

"Sire?"

"The Hollow Men," he said. "Send them both after. The vessel and the caller. Find them before they find each other—or after. Either way, bring me the boy alive." He paused. "The woman is expendable."

The ministers bowed. The orders went out within the hour, written in his name, sealed with his signet, and Aldric sat alone in the Map Room afterward watching the sunlight crawl across the table, and he counted.

This was the arithmetic no one else knew. Every command he issued through the Ember's will cost him. Not coin—something less renewable. He had noticed it first eight years ago: after ordering the suppression of the Salt Bells' rebellion, he had lost the taste of oranges. Not the memory of oranges—he knew they were sweet, knew their color, their season—but the taste itself, wiped clean off his tongue. After the purging of the cartographers' guild, he had lost his mother's laugh. He could recall that she had laughed often, could describe the circumstances, but the sound was gone, a room in his mind with the furniture removed.

Each use of the Ember's power took a year of himself. Sometimes a memory. Sometimes a sense. Once, horribly, a whole year of his childhood—age nine simply ceased to exist, a torn page, and he had woken weeping without knowing why.

And still he used it. Still he sent the Hollow Men riding. Because the alternative—

Because the alternative was the thing he did not think about, the black door in the cellar of his mind, behind which waited the truth he had learned slowly, over decades, the way one learns a lover is cruel: the Ember did not need him. It had never needed him. It had chosen him the way a man chooses a coat—because it fit, because it was available, because it would do until something better came along.

A vessel. They had made a vessel. A door built to the Ember's exact dimensions, and if the boy reached the binding site, or if any storm-blooded fool reached it and spoke the old words—

Then the coat comes off, Aldric thought. Then I am only a man again, and a man who looks thirty and is not, a man hollowed out and holding nothing, a king with no kingdom inside him.

*I would never leave you,* the Ember said softly, and it sounded almost kind. *We are married, you and I. Till death. And I do not permit death.*

"That," Aldric said aloud, to the empty room, "is exactly what frightens me."

* * *

He walked the walls at dusk, as he did every evening, because the people loved to see him and the Ember loved to be seen. The capital spread below him in the blue hour: ten thousand hearth-smokes rising straight in the windless air, the salt-bell towers catching the last light, the river running silver toward the sea. It was beautiful. He had made it beautiful, or the Ember had made it beautiful through him, and lately he could not separate the achievement from the theft.

A child on the wall-walk—one of the wardens' daughters, bold as brass—ran up and presented him with a posy of early crocuses, grown impossibly in the frozen month because the gardens near the palace had not known a true winter in eleven years. The ground stayed warm here. Everyone said it was a blessing.

Aldric looked at the crocuses and felt sick.

Everything green in this city grew out of him. The Ember leaked, the way all fires leak, warming the soil, quickening the seed—and every blossom was a receipt. He thought of the far provinces, the ones the warmth did not reach, the ones where the winters had grown longer and harder each year since his coronation, where the crops failed and the tax collectors came anyway and the peasants burned peat and cursed a king whose portrait showed a young man's face. The land was being drained to keep him radiant. He was a lamp burning the world for oil.

"Thank you," he told the child, and took the crocuses, and smiled his practiced smile, and the crowd on the wall cheered, and the Ember hummed in his teeth like a cat being stroked.

*See how they love us.*

*I see it.*

*Then why do you tremble?*

He looked down at his hand holding the flowers. It was trembling. Fine, constant, invisible to anyone not looking for it.

*Because I remember my father,* he thought, and let the memory come, one of the few he guarded jealously, hoarded like a miser's coin. His father, old and grey and ordinary, sitting with him by a fire, saying: *Aldric. Power that costs nothing is a loan. Read the terms before you spend it.* His father had died at sixty-three in his own bed, surrounded by family, having forgotten nothing worth keeping. A small life. A complete one.

*Sentiment,* the Ember said, with contempt so vast it was almost admiring. *Your father was dust before his time. You will outlive dynasties. I offer you forever, and you mourn a fire and an orange.*

"I mourn myself," Aldric whispered. "You just haven't finished eating him yet."

The Ember said nothing to that. It only tightened, very slightly, the way a hand tightens on something it intends to keep.

* * *

Night. The Hollow Men mustered in the outer yard by torchlight, and Aldric watched them from the balcony above, because he had learned it was wise to watch them. They were not men anymore, the ones who wore the hollow masks—grey-faced, silent, emptied of everything except obedience. He had made the first of them himself, eleven years ago, from criminals at first and then from malcontents and then, in his shame, from anyone the Ember marked as inconvenient. Each one made had cost him something too. He could no longer remember the sound of rain. He had traded it, he supposed, for the thing in the lead rider's mask, which turned its blank face upward toward the balcony as if it could smell him.

"Ride east," Aldric called down, and his voice carried the way royal voices are trained to carry, strong and clear and certain. "Into the Thornfell passes. You seek a child. Bring him to me living. You seek a storm-caller. Do with her as the law requires."

The masked faces turned toward him, thirty of them, and for a moment none moved, and the silence stretched so long that a horse stamped and a torch spat.

Then, as one, they bowed.

As one, they wheeled their horses.

As one, they rode out through the eastern gate into the dark, and the gates boomed shut behind them, and the sound rolled up through the stone of the palace and up through the bones of Aldric Vane, and he stood on his balcony in the cold with his fists on the railing and understood, with the clarity that came to him only at night, only when the Ember drowsed low in its warmth, what he had become.

Not a king. A larder. A stored provision, set aside against some future hunger, guarded jealously until the day it was needed.

And somewhere east, in the frozen passes, a child who was made to be a door was walking toward him through the snow—and for the first time in eleven years, King Aldric did not know whether he hoped the Hollow Men would reach the boy first, or fail.

Below him, in the palace's deepest vault, behind three doors and a wall of consecrated stone, something ancient stirred in its sleep and turned over, and the crocuses in his discarded posy, lying on the balcony floor, bloomed a fraction wider in the dark.

*Soon,* whispered the crown.
