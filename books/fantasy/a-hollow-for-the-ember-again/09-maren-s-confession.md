## 9. Maren's Confession

The fire Maren built was not a fire for cooking.

She had learned the difference at her grandmother's knee, before her knees had learned to ache: a cookfire was fed and forgotten, but a healing fire was *asked*. She asked it now with birch bark and rowan ash and three drops of melted snow from the eaves of Kestrel's cabin, and when it caught, it burned low and gold and steady, the color of broth simmering, of bread proving, of all the slow warm things that mend.

Ash lay on the pallet nearest the flames, wrapped in every blanket the cabin owned. The fever had come on him two days after the ford—two days after the storm, after the lightning, after the three men who would not be getting up—and it burned in him like something with intent. Not the wild heat of a child's illness. This was older. Deliberate. As if whatever slept in his blood had turned over in its sleep and set the bedclothes smoldering.

Maren sat beside him with her hands hovering over his brow, not quite touching, reading what rose off his skin. Heat, yes. But under the heat, a hum. A note held so long and so patiently it had become part of the silence around it.

*Ember,* he had said, back in her kitchen, weeks ago now. The only word she'd understood then. She understood a great deal more now, and most of it she wished she didn't.

Kestrel came in from the woodpile with an armload of split pine and snow melting in her hair. She moved quietly for a big woman; Maren had noticed that early. People who had spent years being hunted learned to move like weather coming in—present everywhere, visible nowhere.

"How is he?"

"Feverish. Restless." Maren kept her voice level. "The fever isn't the sickness. It's a symptom of the sickness underneath, which is that there is a primordial force using my patient as a hearthstone."

Kestrel crouched by the fire and fed it two logs without asking whether the fire wanted them. It took them anyway. Healing fires were polite that way.

"You've been quiet," Kestrel said. "Quieter than usual."

"I've been thinking."

"That's how I know something's wrong."

Maren almost smiled. Almost. Her mouth made the shape of it and then thought better of the expense. She looked down at Ash instead—at the frost-pale lashes, the small chest rising and falling, the lips that moved sometimes in sleep, shaping syllables from a language that had been dead longer than kingdoms had been alive.

"When I was nineteen," Maren said, "I was apprenticed to the Circle at Caer Dunan."

The logs crackled. Kestrel went very still, the way people do when they understand that a door is opening that has been shut a long time, and that what comes through it cannot be shoved back.

"There were nine of us apprentices that year. I was the youngest and the poorest and the loudest about how much smarter I was than everyone." Maren drew a slow breath. The smoke smelled of rowan and memory. "You have to understand what we believed. There had been sickness in the eastern provinces—blight in the fields, fevers in the villages, wells going bitter. The old king's healers called it a plague. The Circle called it a *leak*. Something beneath the capital, they said, seeping corruption up through the roots of the world. And the young prince Aldric—he was not king yet, just third-born, just a serious boy with serious eyes—he came to us and said he'd found where it leaked from. A chamber under the palace. Older than the palace. Older than anything. And the Circle said: seal it. Burn it out. Cauterize the wound."

"And you helped."

"I helped." The words came out flat as coins laid on a dead man's eyes. "I was nineteen and I could draw a binding circle truer than any of the masters, and I was so *proud* of that. Nine circles, one for each apprentice, set into the walls of that chamber while the masters chanted and Aldric stood in the center holding the vessel they'd prepared." She stopped. Her hands, hovering over Ash's brow, had begun to tremble, and she folded them in her lap like birds tucking their heads under wing. "We believed we were closing a wound. You don't ask a wound's permission. You don't wonder if it hurts. You sew it shut and you go home and you wash the blood off your hands and you feel *heroic*."

Kestrel said nothing. She was good at saying nothing. Maren had watched her do it through four hundred miles of hidden road, and she had come to understand it was not emptiness behind that silence but a kind of crowdedness—the whole past pressing against the inside of the skull, waiting its turn.

"What was the vessel?" Kestrel asked finally.

"A child." Maren's voice did not break, though she gave it every opportunity. "A foundling. Nobody's. The kind nobody asks after. They told us it was a construct. Clay and old magic, animated for the purpose, that it would feel nothing, that it was barely alive. We believed that because believing it was easier, and we were nineteen, and ease is the whole curriculum of youth." She looked down at Ash's sleeping face. "He has the same ears. I remember the ears. I told myself for fifty years that I remembered wrong."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind worried the eaves.

"Then Aldric bound it into himself," Kestrel said. "To take the throne."

"To take the throne. Yes. We were told the sealing required a royal anchor—that the binding would fail without blood of the old line to hold it. We thought it was sacrifice. We *cheered*, some of us. I cheered." Maren laughed, one dry note with no music in it. "Fifty years later I sit in a kitchen in Thornfell and a mute child walks out of a snowstorm speaking pre-dynastic binding-tongue, and I know exactly which circle I cut into which wall, and I know that the thing we sealed was never a plague, never a leak, never a wound. It was a *person*. Or the closest thing to one the old world ever made. And we caged it. And the cage needed a keeper. And the keeper has been eating our king alive for thirty years while the land starves for want of the warmth he swallowed."

Kestrel fed the fire another log she did not need. Maren recognized the gesture for what it was—a woman giving her hands work so her face wouldn't have to say everything.

"You knew the rite," Kestrel said. "All this time on the road. You knew how to unbind it."

"I knew the rite we used to bind it. Unbinding is the same knot tied backward. Yes. I knew." Maren met her eyes. "And I didn't say so, because I hoped—I prayed to every hedge-saint my grandmother ever swore by—that we'd find another answer before you asked me directly. Because here is what I also know, Kestrel Vasquez, and have known since Caer Dunan: the rite requires a vessel. Willing flesh, storm-blooded, to hold what we caged. Forever. That was always the shape of it. The Ember doesn't dissipate. It *transfers*. Fire moves; it does not forgive."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed down on the pallet, on the sleeping child, on the two women flanking him like badly built walls.

"So you let us walk all this way," Kestrel said at last, and her voice was not angry, which was worse, "toward a door you already knew opened onto a cliff."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because hope is a coward's arithmetic and I am a very old coward." Maren's hands had gone still in her lap, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake through the whole confession. "Because if I told you in Thornfell, you might have left the boy somewhere safe and gone back to your cabin and your smugglers and your buried lightning, and the king would have eaten himself hollow and taken the harvests with him, and that child would have grown up hunted by Hollow Men with no one who knew his own name for him. Because telling the truth early felt like murder and telling it late felt like merely... withholding the bill until the meal was finished." She shook her head. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. Forgiveness is a coin I stopped minting decades ago. I'm telling you because the boy is fevered and the road ahead ends in a binding chamber, and you deserve to walk into it knowing the full price. And because—" her voice caught, actually caught, an old hinge rusting mid-swing— "because I have carried this alone for fifty years and it turns out carrying things alone is the one spell I was never any good at."

*

Kestrel didn't answer for a long time.

Maren watched her—not the face, which was guarded as a border fortress, but the hands. Storm-scarred, the old burns silvered across the knuckles like lichen on stone. Those hands turned a stick of pine over and over, feeding it to the fire by inches, and Maren understood that Kestrel was doing with the stick what Maren had done with the truth: rationing it.

"You were nineteen," Kestrel said eventually.

"I was."

"They lied to you. All of them. The masters, the prince, the whole shining apparatus of the Circle—they fed a room full of children a lie dressed up as heroism and let you sew the seams shut."

"Yes."

"And then you spent fifty years—" Kestrel gestured at the cabin, the herbs hanging from the rafters, the little garden gone brown under snow, the whole small architecture of a hidden life— "doing *this*. Hedge-craft. Healing. Fixing what's broken in villages too poor to matter to anyone."

"Somebody has to."

"That's not penance, Maren. That's the opposite of penance. Penance is easy—you suffer, you're paid up, everyone goes home. What you did was spend half a century becoming useful to people, which is much harder, and much less forgivable, and—" Kestrel's jaw worked. "I know. Because I killed the wrong people with a storm seven years ago, and I've spent every day since trying to be worth the ones who died. And I will tell you what nobody told me: it doesn't balance. Nothing balances. You just keep the ledger open and keep working."

Maren found, to her astonishment, that her eyes were wet. She was seventy-one years old and her eyes were wet over a smuggler's terrible comfort, and she let them be wet because the healing fire was watching and healing fires knew the difference between grief and performance.

"That's forgiveness?" she managed. "It sounded like an indictment."

"It's both. Most true things are." Kestrel leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the scars. "I'm furious with you. I'll probably stay furious with you. And I'm not leaving, and neither are you, and tomorrow you're going to teach me every word of that unbinding rite so that when we get under the capital, nobody in this company is walking blind again. That's my forgiveness. Take it or leave it."

Maren took it. It sat in her chest like a stone warmed by a fire—still a stone, still heavy, but no longer cold.

"It's imperfect," she said.

"Everything worth having is."

*

The fever broke near dawn.

Maren felt it go the way you feel a knot loosen—a slow unwinding in the air above the pallet, the hum under Ash's skin settling from a shout back into a murmur. He surfaced from sleep in stages, blinking at the low gold fire, at Maren, at Kestrel asleep sitting up against the wall with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a gate even in dreams.

His eyes found Maren. And in them—clear, fever-washed, terribly awake—was the look of someone who had heard every word spoken over his sickbed. Children always heard. That was the first cruelty of children and the last kindness: they listened.

Maren's throat tightened. She reached for the words she'd prepared, the explanations, the apologies arranged in order of size.

Ash got there first. He lifted one small hand from the blankets and touched her wrist—just touched it, light as snowfall—and then, with the other hand, tapped his own chest twice, and pointed at her, and made a gesture she understood across every language and none: *yours too. Mine too. The same.*

"We'll see," Maren whispered, because she was a coward even now, even forgiven imperfectly, even at dawn.

But Ash smiled—the first smile she'd seen on him that wasn't borrowed from someone else's joke—and outside the window the sky was going the color of new iron, and far to the south, beyond the passes, beyond the salt bells of Vell, beneath a palace built over a prison, Maren felt something vast and patient turn its attention toward the north.

Toward the boy. Toward the road. Toward the last circle she had ever cut, waiting in the dark to be tied backward.

She looked at the child, and the child looked at the southern horizon, and neither of them said the word.

They didn't need to. It was already burning between them, small and bright and terrible:

*Ember.*
