## 10. The City of Salt Bells

The first thing you saw of Vell was the bells.

They hung from towers all across the city—bronze and iron and green-weathered copper, some as small as a fist, others big enough to swallow a cart whole—and when the wind came off the salt flats they rang without anyone touching them. The Vellish called it the city singing. Sailors called it bad luck. Odile Ferran had mapped the place twice in her former life, once for a trade delegation and once because she'd been young and arrogant and wanted to prove she could chart a city by sound alone. She'd gotten within a street's width of perfect.

That had been before. Before the palace archives. Before the fire that ate twenty years of her work in an afternoon while she stood in the courtyard and listened to her own maps crackle like a dying hearth.

Now she rode down the switchback road with three companions and no map at all except the one inside her skull, and the bells sang her into the city like a memory with teeth.

"Stop staring," she said to Ash, who had twisted around on the packhorse to look back at the towers for the fourth time. "You'll fall off."

He didn't stop staring. He never did what he was told, which Odile privately considered his best quality. The boy's face was turned up to the sound, mouth slightly open, and after a moment he raised one hand and moved it through the air—not conducting exactly, more like touching something. Tracing the shape of each note as it passed.

"He can *feel* them," Maren said quietly, riding close. The hedge-witch's voice had developed a particular carefulness around Ash over the past weeks, the way people spoke near sleeping animals or open wounds. "The harmonics. I've seen him do it with rivers too."

"Of course he can." Odile kept her eyes on the road. "He's a key. Keys know their locks."

"That's a grim way to put it."

"It's an accurate one. Grim is usually accurate." She shifted in the saddle, wincing at the ache in her hip—the old break from the palace stairs, the night she'd been escorted out of the only home her work had ever given her. "Besides, if we're going to trade passage on my last map, I'd rather we all understand what we're spending."

Kestrel rode ahead of them, as she always did, a lean dark shape against the grey of the salt flats. She hadn't spoken in two hours. That wasn't unusual—Kestrel could go half a day silent and call it conversation—but Odile had learned to read the quality of the silences. This one was tight. Wound. The silence of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Vell first," Kestrel said now, without turning. "Then the causeway south. We buy nothing we don't need, we talk to no one we don't have to, and nobody says the king's name out loud."

"You say it often enough," Odile observed.

"I'm allowed. I'm not from here."

*None of us are from anywhere anymore*, Odile thought, and did not say it, because sentiment on an empty road was a luxury and she'd spent her last luxury years ago.

* * *

The gate of Vell was carved salt, white as bone, wide enough for six carts abreast, and worn smooth by four centuries of hands and weather. The guards at its arch wore no livery—Vell was a free city, answerable to no crown, which was precisely why it existed and precisely why it would not exist forever—but they took one long look at four travel-worn riders leading a packhorse and held up a hand with the bored authority of men who had heard every story ever invented.

"Trade or pilgrimage?"

"Passage," Odile said, riding forward. "We're bound south along the coast road and we need the causeway tolls waived. In exchange—" she reached into her coat, slowly, letting them see the movement for what it was, "—I'm prepared to offer something better than coin."

The senior guard, a woman with a jaw like a dock cleat, folded her arms. "Better than coin. That'll be good."

Odile drew out the map.

It was the last one. Everything else had burned; this had survived by accident, rolled inside a brass tube she'd been using to carry her brushes, forgotten in a friend's cellar in a town whose name she still couldn't say without flinching. It showed the southern coast from Vell to the Saltreach delta: every channel, every sandbar, every safe anchorage and every drowned reef, surveyed at a scale no working pilot had access to since the royal survey was sealed. Ships died on those reefs every winter. Salvage crews grew rich on the bones.

The guard unrolled a corner of it, looked, and went very still in the way of someone recognizing value far above her pay grade.

"Where did you get this?"

"I made it," Odile said. "Before your king decided cartography was treason."

Something moved behind the guard's eyes—sympathy, maybe, or just the particular wariness of a border woman who knew better than to ask a second question. She rolled the map closed with unexpected gentleness and handed it back.

"Keep it," she said. "Tolls are waived. But there's a man at the Pilgrim's Rest who buys charts like that, and if you're selling, sell to him and not to the harbor brokers, because the brokers will cheat you and the crown's agents watch the brokers." She paused. "You didn't hear that from me."

"No," Odile agreed. "We certainly didn't."

They rode through the arch into the smell of brine and frying fish and tar, under towers hung with bells that rang and rang and rang.

* * *

Vell in late afternoon was a city performing prosperity, and Odile, who had spent her career reading landscapes the way priests read scripture, read it immediately and did not like what she found.

The market squares were full. That was the performance. But the stalls were full too—too full, piled high with goods nobody was buying, grain sacks stacked in pyramids that hadn't been disturbed in weeks. Prices chalked on slate boards had been erased and rewritten so many times the slates were grey with ghost-numbers. And everywhere, everywhere, the posters.

They papered the walls in layers, new over old over older: the radiant profile of King Aldric the Beloved, Aldric the Unifier, Aldric Who Brought the Long Peace. Crowned, serene, lit from within by a painter's trick of gold leaf that caught the sun and threw it back. THE KING'S LIGHT WARMS ALL LANDS, said the newest layer. Beneath it, half-scraped, an older poster promised bread at fixed prices. Beneath *that*, barely legible, something about open granaries.

Three promises deep. Each one contradicted by the empty purses of the people walking past.

"Look at the dates," Maren murmured, reining in beside a wall where the layers had peeled back like bark. She touched the oldest visible poster with one gnarled finger. "This one's from—gods, this is thirty years old. He looks the same in all of them."

Odile looked. She wished she hadn't.

She had seen Aldric once, from a distance, at a court function she'd attended because cartographers were expected to attend and be decorative. He'd been in his early thirties then—young for a king, hungry-looking, clever-eyed, the kind of handsome that comes from ambition rather than bone structure. That had been perhaps fifteen years ago. He should be nearing fifty now. Fifty, with the weight of rule on him, the sleepless nights, the gray creeping in at the temples the way it crept into every ruler's hair eventually, because ruling was a thing that aged you like salt ages iron.

The face on the posters was thirty. Not *looked* thirty—was thirty, down to the line of the jaw, the exact set of the mouth. The painters hadn't flattered him. They hadn't needed to. Whatever sat behind that face had stopped it moving forward in time the way a pressed flower stops being a flower.

"Ash," Kestrel said suddenly.

The boy had stopped his horse beneath the largest poster, the one covering the whole flank of the customs house, ten feet of gilded radiance. He was looking up at it with an expression Odile had never seen on him before—and Ash's face was usually a locked door, so any expression at all was an event—but this—

This was recognition. Cold, precise, adult recognition, arriving in a child's body like lightning down a wire too thin for it.

His hand rose. His fingers spread against the air between himself and the painted king, and though no one touched him, though the street noise went on around them, Odile felt the temperature drop. Just a degree. Just enough that her breath fogged.

On the poster, the gold leaf seemed to flicker. Or perhaps it was cloud shadow. Perhaps.

"Ash." Kestrel was off her horse and beside him in three strides, her scarred hand closing gently around his wrist, pulling his arm down. "Hey. Look at me. Not at it. At me."

He looked at her. The cold receded. But his mouth worked once, twice, silently, and then he said—one word, hoarse, in that language older than kingdoms:

"*Ember.*"

A bell somewhere overhead tolled, though the wind had gone still.

"We're leaving this square," Kestrel said, low and flat, to all of them. "Now."

* * *

The Pilgrim's Rest was a tavern built into the base of a decommissioned bell tower, which meant the whole building hummed faintly whenever the wind picked up, like living inside a struck glass. The man who bought charts turned out to be a retired pilot named Corvo with ink-stained fingers and a merchant's smile, and he paid Odile's price for the coastal chart without haggling, which told her he knew exactly what it was worth and possibly better than she did.

He also paid, unprompted, for their rooms and a meal, "because a woman who can draw like that shouldn't be eating tavern stew," and because, he added, examining her over his spectacles, "you've got the look of people heading somewhere the crown doesn't want them going. I was young once too."

"The causeway south," Odile said, testing. "What do you know about traffic on it?"

"Lighter than it used to be." Corvo turned his cup slowly on the table. "The crown posts riders on it now. Masked ones. They don't collect tolls—they collect *names*. Every traveler south gets asked questions. Where from, where to, who with." He glanced at Ash, sitting pale and quiet in the corner with Maren, and lowered his voice further. "And lately they're asking about children. Runaway child. Noble house, they claim. Distraught family." His mouth twisted, making clear what he thought of the claim. "There's a reward posted. Big enough to make honest men lie to themselves."

Kestrel's hand had gone still around her cup. "How big?"

"Big enough to buy a ship. Or a pardon." Corvo met her eyes. "I'm telling you because I like the boy's face, and because whoever burned this woman's maps—" a nod at Odile, "—burned things of mine too, years back. Charts I'd drawn fair and paid taxes on. Called them seditious instruments." He snorted. "A coastline. Seditious."

"They weren't wrong," Odile said quietly. "Maps are always seditious. A map says: this land belongs to itself, not to whoever claims it. Kings hate that."

Corvo laughed, short and real. Then his face sobered. "One more thing, since you're going south. There's a rumor out of the capital. Take it for what rumors are worth." He leaned in. "The king held court at midwinter. Public audience—first one in six years. A grain merchant's widow got past the guards, threw herself at the throne steps, begging relief for the famine districts. And the king—" Corvo's voice dropped to almost nothing. "The king looked at her and asked his chamberlain, in front of everyone, *who is that woman, and why does she keep changing?*"

The tavern hummed around them. Somewhere above, a bell stirred in the wind.

"Changing?"

"She'd been kneeling there a minute. He watched her age. Witnesses swear it—a minute, maybe two, and she went from forty to sixty in front of the whole court. And then the king smiled, and the court forgot it happened, the way crowds forget things. All but a few." Corvo sat back. "Those few are talking. Quietly. In cellars. But talking."

Nobody at the table said anything for a long moment. Odile found herself looking at her own hands—ink-stained now too, matching Corvo's, a life measured in lines drawn and lines crossed—and thinking about arithmetic. She was very good at arithmetic. It was, in its way, what maps were: the mathematics of distance and danger.

Here was the sum as she figured it: a king who did not age was a king who was aging *something else*. Something was paying his bill. The land's failing harvests, the dead rivers in the east Maren spoke of, the winters that came earlier every year—all of it, interest on a loan taken out in someone else's name. And the currency the Ember spent, apparently, was time itself. His time. Other people's time.

A woman had aged a lifetime in a minute at his feet.

And a child made of old magic was sitting in the corner of a tavern, learning to eat stew with a spoon held slightly wrong, and the crown had posted a reward for him large enough to corrupt any honest man between here and the capital.

"There's a causeway toll station at Halfway Reach," Corvo was saying. "If you must go south, go at dawn, when the shift changes and the masked ones hand over to the regulars. Regular soldiers can be bored. Bored is survivable." He pushed a scrap of paper across the table with a name and a mark on it. "Show this at the ferry if the toll station gives trouble. Old debt owed to me. Won't cover everything. Covers enough."

Odile took it. "Why?" she asked. "Truly. You owe us nothing."

Corvo looked past her, at Ash, at Maren's protective hover, at Kestrel's coiled stillness—at the whole improbable shape of them, four broken pieces that had somehow begun fitting together on the road.

"Because I'm old," he said, "and I remember when the kingdom breathed. And because somebody has to draw the next map." He smiled, tired and crooked. "Make it a good one."

* * *

Their room was under the eaves, one bed and two pallets, window facing the harbor. Odile took the window watch by unspoken agreement—she slept worst anyway, and lying awake staring at a ceiling helped no one.

Below, Vell glittered. Lantern-light on black water, the bells stirring their slow bronze music, and everywhere in the dark the pale rectangles of the king's face, glowing faintly even by night, gold leaf catching moonlight. Thirty years old. Forever thirty years old. A kingdom starving in the streets beneath a portrait of eternal youth, and loving it, because hope was the most addictive thing ever distilled and Aldric's face was pure distillate.

Odile heard the pallet creak and knew without looking that Kestrel had come to stand beside her at the window. They watched the city together in silence for a while.

"He's dying," Kestrel said at last. "Aldric. Whatever's left of him. The Ember's eating him alive and propping up the picture while it feeds." Her voice was steady, but Odile had learned to hear the storm under it, the pressure-drop before weather. "And it's eating everything else to keep him looking alive. The harvests. The rivers. That poor woman at the throne steps."

"Yes."

"And it wants Ash." Kestrel's jaw tightened. "Or the crown wants Ash. Both, probably. A vessel that won't burn out. A door that fits the lock." She exhaled slowly. "Every mile south, the price on his head buys more of the world. Every innkeeper, every ferryman, every bored soldier. We're carrying the most valuable child in the kingdom through a country full of desperate people, and we have one forged letter and a witch's luck."

"You forgot the mapmaker," Odile said.

Kestrel glanced at her.

"You forgot the mapmaker," Odile repeated, "who knows every smuggler's cut and drowned road between here and the capital, because she drew them all before they were illegal, and who has been waiting seven years for something worth spending herself on." She turned from the window and met Kestrel's eyes, and let the other woman see it plainly—the thing she'd been carrying since the ambush at the ford, since the firelit confession, since Ash had fallen asleep against her shoulder on the third night out and she had not moved her arm until morning. "I buried my life's work in a courtyard ash heap. I told myself I was done drawing roads for anyone. But this—" she nodded toward the corner where Ash slept, curled small, one hand loosely closed as if holding something invisible, "—this is worth a map. This is worth all of them."

Kestrel studied her for a long moment. Seven years of exile had taught her to distrust offers; Odile could see the distrust working, could see her deciding whether this was kindness or strategy.

"Odile," she said finally, roughly, "if the Hollow Men take you again—"

"They won't."

"If they do—"

"Then I'll stall them," Odile said, "the way I stall everyone. I'm extremely annoying. Ask anyone." And when Kestrel didn't smile: "Listen to me. We are four people the world threw away, and tomorrow we walk onto a causeway crawling with the king's hunters, carrying the one thing he cannot afford to lose. Fear is reasonable. I feel it. But dread—" she tapped the window glass, at the distant golden face glowing over the water, "—dread is what *he* wants us to feel. Dread keeps people home. Dread keeps mouths shut and heads down and kingdoms dying quietly. So we convert it. Tonight. Right now. Into route planning."

Kestrel huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You're terrifying when you're resolved."

"I'm a cartographer. Resolution is just fear with contour lines."

They woke Maren, and spread Corvo's borrowed ink and Odile's memory across the floorboards: the causeway, the toll stations, the shift-change at dawn, the ferry mark, the marsh cuts beyond Halfway Reach where the masked riders' horses would founder. Ash woke too, and sat cross-legged among them, watching the lines appear, and when Odile drew the capital and paused over the blank space beneath it—the space where the forbidden lower levels lived in her memory alone—he reached out and laid one finger on the parchment, directly below the city, as if pointing at something only he could see.

"That's where we're going, isn't it," Odile said softly.

Ash looked up at her. He didn't nod. But the candle flames all bent toward him at once, gently, like flowers toward sun, and outside, every bell in Vell rang one clear note together, though there was no wind at all.

Dawn was four hours away. Odile dipped her pen, and began to draw the road south.
