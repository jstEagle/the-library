## 11. Ash Speaks

The bells of Vell are made of salt.

Ash learns this on their first morning in the city, when Odile leads them down a street that smells of brine and hot metal and points up at the great tower rising from the harbor district like a finger accusing the sky. The bells hang there behind open arches, pale and translucent as frozen tears, and when they ring—for the changing of the watch, for the tide, for no reason anyone can name—the sound is not a sound so much as a weather. It moves through Ash's chest and settles there, humming, and the thing inside him hums back.

*Ember,* he thinks, in the old tongue that has lived in his mouth longer than any word of the common speech. *They ring for you too.*

He does not say this aloud. He has not said much of anything aloud since the night on the mountain, since the candles guttered and the woman with storm-scarred hands knelt in the snow and did not flinch from him. Words come to him more easily now—Maren's lessons have loosened something, like ice breaking off a riverbank in spring—but the important words, the true ones, still sit in his chest like stones at the bottom of a well. He can see them when he looks down into himself. He has not yet learned how to haul them up.

But they are closer to the surface every day.

* * *

Vell is not like anywhere Ash has been, which is nowhere, which is a life measured in cellars and mountain passes and the backs of strangers' carts. Vell is loud. Vell is *many*. The streets are canals of people, and the buildings lean over them like gossips, and everywhere there are colors—dyes and awnings and painted shutters—and everywhere there are faces of the young king, radiant and golden on banners and broadsheets and cheap tin medallions sold by street criers who shout his virtues between selling eels.

Kestrel buys one of the medallions without smiling. She turns it over in her scarred fingers as they walk, and her jaw does something complicated.

"He should be sixty," she says quietly, to Odile, but Ash hears. He hears everything now. That is another thing that has been loosening in him—not just words but *listening*, the deep kind, the kind that catches what people mean instead of what they say. "Sixty at least. I remember him crowned. I was—" Her hand closes around the tin face until the edges bite her palm. "I was eleven."

"You were eleven," Odile says, "and now you're smuggling children through mountain passes, and he's thirty forever. There's an arithmetic there I don't like."

"Most arithmetic isn't meant to be liked."

Ash walks between them, small enough that the crowd flows around him like water around a stone, and he looks up at the golden face on the banner above the fishmonger's stall, and he understands something the women do not say: the king is being eaten. He can feel it the way you can feel a fire in the next room through a wall—a warmth that is wrong, that takes instead of gives. The Ember is in the capital, coiled in a man who was once a boy of eleven's age, and it is drinking him, and through him it drinks the land, and the land is tired all the way down to its roots.

Ash knows because the Ember calls to him. Softly. Patiently. Like a mother calling a child home across a dark field.

*Door,* it says, in the old tongue. *Little door. Come and be opened.*

He holds Kestrel's sleeve tighter, and she looks down at him without breaking stride, and whatever she sees in his face makes her frown and put her hand on his head, briefly, like a blessing she doesn't know how to give.

* * *

The house Maren rents is above a chandler's shop, three rooms that smell of tallow and rosemary, and it is there, on the second evening, that the dreams change.

Until now Ash's dreams have been the old ones: snow, a white room, hands in gloves lifting him, a voice saying *carry him carefully, he is the last of the making.* But tonight the dream is the tower. The salt-bell tower. He stands beneath the open arches and the bells hang around him like enormous pale fruit, and they are ringing, ringing, and each note carves a word out of the air and lays it at his feet:

*DOOR.*

*MADE.*

*OPEN.*

*COST.*

He wakes with the old tongue filling his mouth like floodwater, whole sentences pressing against the backs of his teeth, and he lies in the narrow bed listening to the house breathe—Maren snoring softly in the next room, Odile turning over with a mapmaker's precision even in sleep—and he knows, with a clarity that feels like falling, that tomorrow he will speak.

Not a word. A sentence. Maybe more.

And the sentence will change everything, and he is afraid, and he is seven years old, or perhaps eight—he has never known—and he is also older than any kingdom, and both of these things are true at once, and only one person alive will understand when he says it.

* * *

It happens under the tower because the tower calls him.

He slips out at dawn, before Maren wakes, before the chandler begins his clattering below. The streets are empty and silvered with harbor fog, and his feet know the way the way rivers know downhill. The tower stands black against the paling sky, and the first bell rings the tide-change, and the sound goes through him like a key going through a lock.

The Ember answers from far away, east, beyond the mountains, beneath the capital. *Come,* it says. *Open.*

"No," Ash says aloud, to the air, to the thing in the distance, to himself. His voice cracks on it. He has said almost nothing for so long that the word comes out rusted. "No. Not yet."

"Not yet what?"

He spins. Kestrel stands at the mouth of the lane with her cloak over her nightclothes and her hair unbound, and her eyes go from him to the tower and back, and Ash understands that she followed him—that she wakes when he wakes, that she has been sleeping lightly as a bird ever since the Hollow Men rode the valley, that some part of her has been standing guard over him in the dark for weeks without either of them naming it.

"I heard the bells," he says. It is the longest string of words he has ever given her. He watches them land on her face one by one, each one a small astonishment. "In my dream. They were saying things."

"What things?"

He looks up at the pale bells hanging in their arches. The fog drifts through the sound of the fading note. And the old tongue rises in him—not pushing anymore, not flooding, but *rising*, calm as dawn water, as if it has only ever been waiting for him to stop being afraid of it.

"They were saying what I am," he says.

Kestrel comes closer. She does not kneel—she learned early that he hates being knelt to, that it makes him feel like a specimen—but she lowers herself onto the tower's stone base and sits, and pats the cold granite beside her, and waits. That is the thing about her that Ash loves most and cannot yet say: she waits. Everyone else in his short memory wanted things *from* him, wanted him opened or carried or delivered. Kestrel wants him *here*, and lets the wanting be enough.

He climbs up beside her. The stone is freezing through his trousers. Somewhere below, a fishwife begins to sing.

"The people who made me," Ash says, and the words come slowly, each one hauled up from the bottom of the well, gleaming wet, "they were the ones who bound the Ember the first time. Before the kingdom. Before Aldric." He has learned the king's name from the women's talk; it tastes like ash in the old tongue and like nothing in the new. "They couldn't kill it. Fire that old doesn't die, it only *waits*. So they made a door for it. A hollow. Something with blood that could hold it without burning." He looks at his own small hands, pale in the fog-light. "They tried many times. I'm the last of the trying."

Kestrel has gone very still. Stillness is her language; he reads it fluently now. This stillness is the kind that comes before storms.

"Ash," she says carefully, "what are you telling me?"

"That's why I talk the old way. Why the candles answer me. Why the Ember calls and I hear it." He takes a breath, and the fog goes into his lungs, cold and clean, and he says the sentence. The one that has been waiting at the bottom of him since the white room, since the gloved hands, since the beginning of everything he is.

"I was made to be the door."

The bells choose that moment to ring the sunrise. The sound pours down over them, salt-pale and immense, and Ash feels the truth of his own words vibrate in his chest like a second heartbeat, and the Ember, far away, stirs and whispers *yes, yes, little door, come home*—and he presses his lips shut against it.

When the last note fades, Kestrel still hasn't moved. But her breathing has changed. He knows her breathing the way sailors know weather.

"And the cost," she says at last, very quietly. "You said the bells said *cost*. What cost, Ash? Tell me what you know."

He could lie. He has discovered recently that lying is possible, that words can be arranged to hide things as easily as show them. But she waited for him. She sat in the freezing fog at dawn because he woke afraid. She mended his coat with thread the color of embers and never once asked him to be anything other than what he is.

"The binding needs a vessel," he says. "A willing one. With storm-blood." He watches her face do the thing it does when she is keeping herself from feeling something—assembling itself into calm, plank by plank, like a wall against weather. "That's why they made me. Storm-blood and willingness. Children are willing. Children haven't learned not to be."

"Stop." Her voice cracks like ice on a pond. "Stop talking like—"

"It doesn't hurt," he says quickly, because suddenly she looks like the night the riders came, like the cellar door closing, like fear wearing her face, and he cannot bear that he has done that. "Maren's books—I've looked at them when she sleeps—the old makers wrote it down. If the vessel is *right*, if it was made for it, the Ember settles. It doesn't devour. It dwells." He hesitates. "I think that's why it's eating the king. He took it by force. He wasn't made for it. He's a jar holding a river."

Kestrel laughs—one short, terrible syllable that isn't laughter at all.

"A jar holding a river," she repeats. "And you're the jug it was fired for." She puts her head in her hands. Her scars catch the first real light of morning, silver-pink against the grey. "You're eight years old."

"Seven. Or eight."

"Don't." The word comes out raw. "Don't make jokes. Not about this."

Ash looks at her bent head and feels something shift in him—the last stone coming loose from the well-bottom, the final word rising. He reaches out and touches her wrist, where the skin between the scars is soft.

"You called a storm at the ford," he says. "To save us. You killed three men and you cried about it after, when you thought we were asleep. You have storm-blood. You're willing—you've been willing since the night I knocked on your door and you let me in even though letting me in was the most dangerous thing you'd ever done." He says it gently, the way Maren taught him hard truths should be handled, like eggs. "I know what you're thinking. I've watched you think it since the ford. Since Maren started reading the old rite and going quiet in the middle of pages."

Her head comes up. Her eyes are wet and furious and terrified, and underneath all of it, unmistakably, is the thing he named: she has already offered. In the silence of her own heart, weeks ago, she has already stepped into the circle.

"No," Kestrel says.

"It should be me."

"*No.*" She grabs his shoulders—not hard, never hard, but firm as an anchor—and turns him to face her fully, and the fog swirls around them both, and somewhere overhead the salt bells begin to ring the hour. "Listen to me, Ash. Listen to me the way you listen to everything, because I know you do. You were *made* to be a door. Made—by people who are dust now, by people who decided what your life was for before you ever drew breath. And I spent seven years learning that the shape other people force on you is not the shape you have to stay in." Her voice shakes. Her hands don't. "I was exiled for killing the wrong people with a storm I didn't mean to call. Seven years I believed that was all I was—a weapon someone else aimed. It took a mute child knocking on my door to teach me different. So I will not stand here and let you believe that being *made* for something means you owe it your whole self. Do you understand me?"

Ash's throat is tight. "Then who?" he whispers. "If not me, then who holds it? The king is dying of it. The land is starving of it. Someone has to—"

"We," Kestrel says. "We figure it out. All of us. That's what none of the old makers understood—they worked alone, they made their doors alone, they built a child in a white room and called it mercy. We're not alone, Ash. That's the whole point of everything since the snow. Maren knows rites I haven't even seen. Odile knows passages under the capital that aren't on any living map. And I—" her grip tightens, gentle, fierce, "—I have storm-blood and I am *done* being willing to die quietly. If there's another way, we find it. And if there isn't—" her jaw sets, and Ash sees the storm gather behind her eyes like weather on a horizon, "—then we'll stand in front of that circle together, and no one steps into it alone. Not you. Not me. *No one.*"

The bells finish ringing. The fog is thinning; the harbor is emerging below them, boats waking, gulls wheeling and crying. Ash looks at her—at this scarred, stubborn, exiled woman who was given nothing and chose, anyway, to give everything—and the last word rises out of the well of him, the one deeper than *door*, deeper than *ember*, the oldest word in any tongue.

"Family," he says. Testing it. His first time saying it in the common speech.

Kestrel's breath catches. For a moment she is the one who cannot speak, and Ash finds, to his surprise, that he does not mind waiting. Turnabout, Maren would say, is fair.

"Yes," she manages finally, rough. "Family. Gods help us all."

She pulls him against her side, and they sit together on the cold stone while Vell wakes below them, and the Ember murmurs its patient hunger in the far east, and neither of them says what both of them are thinking: that Maren is awake right now in the chandler's house, hunched over the old makers' book with a candle stub and shaking hands—and that today, or very soon, she will reach the page that names the price in full.

Ash feels it coming the way he feels the bells: through the chest first, then the bones.

The rite is almost finished translating. And the book does not agree with him about what a door is for.
