## 13. Betrayal on the Causeway

The guide's name was Tam Broadwater, and Kestrel should have known better than to trust a man with kind eyes and clean boots.

Seven years in the Thornfells had taught her to read people the way she once read weather: by the small betrayals of surface detail. A smuggler's boots were cracked and re-stitched, patched with tar and prayer. A farmer's hands were calloused in patterns as legible as script. But Tam had come to them in the last village before the salt marshes with a smile that reached all the way up, and a story about his sister lost beyond the causeway, and a pair of boots that had never once touched mud.

Kestrel had noticed. She'd noted it the way she noted a cloud bank on the horizon—filed it, weighed it, and let it pass, because they needed someone who knew the causeway at low tide, and because Maren vouched for him, and because—she admitted this only to herself, in the dark hours before dawn—she was tired. Tired of trusting no one. Tired of the arithmetic where every kindness was a liability.

That was the cost of caring about people. Not that they betrayed you. That you let them.

* * *

The causeway ran three miles across the Greywash, a spine of ancient stone laid down before the dynasties, before even the old tongue Ash spoke had curdled into memory. At low tide it stood clear above the mudflats; at high tide the sea came back over it like a hand closing. They had timed their crossing for the ebb, walking in the blue hour before dawn, when the water pulled back from the flats and the whole world smelled of salt and rot and cold iron.

Ash rode on Kestrel's shoulders. He was getting too big for it—he was seven, maybe eight, all elbows now—but he still asked, in his way, tugging her collar and pointing, and she still said yes, because the boy had spent who knew how long being carried by no one at all.

He pointed now, east, where the sky was going the color of a bruise healing.

"I see it," Kestrel said. "Dawn. Very impressive."

Maren walked behind them, hood up against the wind, muttering the day's count under her breath the way she did—*one for the road, two for the tide*—an old hedge-charm that meant nothing and comforted everything. Odile brought up the rear with her satchel of salvaged maps held tight against her chest, the way she carried them everywhere now, since the crown had burned the rest. Her eyes kept moving. Left flat. Right flat. The broken watchtower on the near shore. The drowned chapel steeple rising from the mud like a black tooth.

"Something's wrong," Odile said quietly.

Kestrel stopped. Ash's weight shifted on her shoulders; his small hand closed in her hair, not pulling, just holding.

"What?"

"Tam." Odile nodded toward the head of their little column, where the guide had walked ahead these last ten minutes, further ahead than he needed to be. "He's been walking faster since we passed the second marker. And he keeps checking the sun." She paused. "People don't check the sun unless they're waiting for something to happen at a specific time."

Kestrel looked. Tam Broadwater stood perhaps sixty yards on, silhouetted against the brightening east, and he was doing exactly what Odile said: glancing at the horizon, then back along the causeway, then at the horizon again. His hand rested on the strap of his pack.

And there—Kestrel's stomach dropped through the stones beneath her feet—there was a lantern lit in the ruined watchtower on the far shore. One flash. Two. Three.

Signal.

"Off the causeway," Kestrel said. "Now. All of you."

"Off?" Maren's voice cracked high. "Into the flats? The tide—"

"The tide comes back in an hour. Hollow Men come back in ten minutes. Do the sums." She lifted Ash down, set him on his feet, and took his hand. "Flats, north side, there's a channel cut through the mud—old drainage, runs toward the steeple. We can wade it. It'll be filthy and freezing but it'll be cover."

"And if it isn't?" Odile asked.

"Then we're filthy and freezing when they find us, which is more than most people get."

They went over the low parapet on the north side, down onto the flats—and that was when Tam Broadwater turned around.

For one long moment the four of them looked at each other across forty yards of glistening mud, and Kestrel watched the man's face do its arithmetic. He wasn't cruel—that was the worst of it. He looked sick. He looked like a man who had sold something he couldn't buy back, and whose price had been his sister, or his debts, or simply his life, because the Hollow Men did not leave witnesses to refusal.

"I'm sorry," he called across the distance. His voice carried thin over the wind. "They have my girls. I'm sorry."

Kestrel didn't answer. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie or a cruelty, and she was out of time for both.

Because the riders were already coming.

* * *

They came off the far shore at a canter, six of them, black cloaks and pale masks catching the first true light of morning, hooves striking sparks from the ancient stones. Hollow Men moved wrong—Kestrel had seen it before, seven years ago, from the other side of a courtroom—and it was worse now, seeing it up close. Their horses obeyed without fear. Nothing that lived feared the Hollow Men, so the horses had learned not to bother.

"They'll ride the causeway and cut us off at the steeple," Odile said. She was breathing hard, mud to her knees, one arm clamped around her maps. "There's no other way across. If we go back—"

"We're not going back." Kestrel scanned the flats. The drainage channel was there, a dark seam in the grey mud, running crooked toward the drowned chapel. Waist-deep water at best. "Channel. Move."

They moved. Mud sucked at their legs, grabbed their boots, made every step a negotiation. Ash stumbled and Kestrel hauled him up without breaking stride, slung him half-carrying, half-dragging, his breath coming in sharp little gasps that tore at her more than any blade could have. Behind them, hoofbeats on the causeway—fast now, deliberate, the riders spreading out along the spine of stone like crows along a fence.

A voice rolled across the flats, amplified by some trick of hollow metal or hollow magic, flat and toneless as a struck bell:

"*Kestrel Vasquez. Deliver the vessel and walk free. This is the king's only offer.*"

"They always say that," Maren panted. "It's never true."

"It's true enough," Kestrel said grimly. "It's true for everyone except me and the boy. That's what makes it work."

The channel bent. The chapel steeple rose ahead of them, close now, its base ringed by a shelf of stone that broke the tide—a pocket of dry ground no bigger than a barn floor, hidden from the causeway by the angle of the ruins. Kestrel had found it years ago, guiding a cargo of untaxed brandy, and had sworn she'd never need it again.

She shoved Ash up onto the shelf. Maren next. Then Odile—

—and that was when the rider came off the causeway.

Not along it. *Off* it—the lead Hollow Man put his horse straight down the embankment and onto the flats, and the animal didn't slow, didn't founder, just ran across the mud as though the mud were road. The others followed. Four of them fanning wide to flank, two coming straight on, and Kestrel understood with cold clarity that the king's men had mapped this crossing too, had bought it from the same trembling man with the same clean boots, and that the channel was not cover at all.

It was a funnel.

"*Vasquez.*" The lead rider drew rein twenty feet away, mask tilted. Beneath the pale ceramic, nothing moved. No breath fogged the air. "*The vessel. The witch may live. The cartographer is wanted alive for treason of maps. You are wanted however you come.*"

Odile went very still beside Kestrel. Then, quietly, in the voice of a woman doing sums: "Treason of maps. They want my lower-level drawings. They think I memorized the binding site."

"Did you?" Kestrel breathed.

"A piece of it. Enough to get us in." Odile's jaw tightened. "Enough for them to want it out of my head."

The Hollow Men dismounted. Six of them, spread in a crescent, cutting off the channel mouth and the causeway both. Their movements had that terrible economy, no wasted motion, no hesitation—soldiers with the soldiering stripped out and only the obedience left. One of them produced iron manacles that Kestrel could feel from thirty feet away, a dead weight in the air, a silence pressing against her skin.

Storm-silence. Made to smother exactly what she was.

Her heart began to slam. Not fear—or not only fear. Something older, rising up through seven years of buried quiet like floodwater through a cellar floor. Her hands ached. Her scars ached. The air above the flats had gone strange and heavy, the way it did before weather that had no business existing.

*Not here,* she told herself. *Not with Ash right there. Not with Odile standing in the open.*

But the Hollow Man with the manacles stepped forward, toward Odile, and said in that bell-flat voice: "*The cartographer first.*"

"No," Kestrel said.

Lightning answered.

It came down out of a sky that had been clear five seconds earlier—not a bolt but a *column*, white and total, striking the mudflat between the riders and splitting the world in half. The thunder arrived inside her chest. Steam screamed up from the wet ground. Two horses went down screaming, and the men dragged them up with those awful unbothered hands, masks turning toward her in perfect unison, and Kestrel felt the storm gathering again, felt it *wanting*, felt seven years of exile crack open like river ice.

She could kill them. Some of them. Maybe all of them, if she stopped holding anything back—and the knowing of that, the sweet and terrible *knowing*, frightened her more than the masks did.

"*Take the vessel,*" the lead Hollow Man said, ignoring the storm entirely, and three of them started up the stone shelf toward Ash.

Everything after that happened fast, the way disasters do.

Maren threw herself between the riders and the boy, arms wide, shouting words that were half hedge-charm and half pure fury, and a ring of stinging nettles erupted from the stone around her, old wild magic, useless against iron but blinding and sudden. Kestrel got to Ash, got her body between him and the masked men, felt lightning crawling up her arms begging to be spent. Odile—Odile was moving the wrong way, away from the shelf, drawing the manacled one after her, waving her satchel overhead like bait.

"*You want the maps?*" she shouted. "*Come take them!*"

"*Odile—*" Kestrel lunged.

Two Hollow Men closed on the cartographer with terrible gentleness, the way you handle something valuable. One struck her legs from under her with a staff. The other wrapped her in a net weighted with iron rings, and Odile Ferran went down into the mud with her arms still locked around her satchel, still *protecting the maps*, and the last thing Kestrel saw clearly—before the storm in her own blood whited out the edges of the world—was Odile's face turning toward her through the netting.

Not afraid. Furious. And mouthing, with absolute clarity, three words:

"*Go. Get. Him.*"

Then the lightning came down again, and again, and Kestrel stopped choosing it—it chose her—and the flats turned to thunder and steam and the smell of scorched rain. When her vision cleared, two riders lay motionless in the mud. The others had withdrawn to the causeway, unhurried, unharmed where it mattered, because they had what they came for.

They were riding away with Odile Ferran slung across a horse like cargo, her satchel confiscated, her face turned west toward the capital she'd fled, and she did not struggle, because struggling would tell them the maps mattered, and Odile Ferran had never once told an enemy the truth for free.

* * *

Afterward there was only the tide coming in, and the three of them left on the stone shelf, and the arithmetic that could not be made to balance.

Maren sat with Ash pressed against her side, his small hands fisted in her coat, shaking without sound. Kestrel stood at the edge of the shelf watching the riders shrink along the causeway toward the far shore, toward the roads, toward the capital, and the storm still muttered in her bones, looking for something else to break.

"We go after her," Maren said.

"With what?" Kestrel's voice came out scraped raw. "Six became four. In the capital there are hundreds. We have a child, a witch with nettles, and—" she gestured at herself, at the smoking air, at everything she was—"this. Which they've clearly planned for. Those manacles weren't improvised, Maren. Someone told them exactly what I am. Again."

"So we follow anyway."

"To what end? Walk into the palace dungeons and die beside her?" The words tasted like ash and like cowardice and she couldn't tell which was which. "If we're taken, Ash is taken. Everything—everyone—every mile of this, for nothing. She knew that. You saw her mouth it. *Get him out.*"

"She's your friend."

"Yes." It came out of Kestrel like a splinter. "Yes, she is. That's what I'm telling you. That's the whole lesson of my entire miserable life, Maren—I called a storm to save people once, and it killed the wrong ones, and I learned that caring about people means handing the world a knife and showing it where you keep your heart. Seven years I kept my heart in a locked box in the Thornfells and nobody got hurt. Then I opened the box." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Look how that's going."

Ash pulled free of Maren. He crossed the stone shelf and took Kestrel's scarred hand in both of his small ones, and he looked up at her, and he did not sign *I'm sorry*, and he did not sign *it's not your fault*. He signed the old word instead, the one he'd said the first night, the one Maren had translated by firelight.

*Ember.*

Then he pointed—at Kestrel's chest, at the place behind her ribs where the storm lived—and then at himself, and then, with terrible gravity, at the east, where the capital lay and where Odile was being carried.

Kestrel knelt in the salt spray and held the boy's face between her hands. "We will get her back," she said. "I don't know how yet. But I know someone who drew the dungeons of that palace from memory, and I know she's in there right now counting guards and leaving us a trail, because that's who she is. So we're not going to abandon her. We're going to become the kind of problem the king can't solve." She stood. The storm had gone quiet in her at last, banked, waiting. "Under-city. Drowned catacombs. You said the old priests kept ways beneath the capital, Maren. Tell me everything you remember. Every door, every rite, every word."

Maren wiped her face with a muddy sleeve. "It'll take days to teach you."

"Then we start now." Kestrel looked east one last time, at the empty causeway, at the grey water closing over the hoofprints like a wound closing over a knife. "She bought us our lives with hers. The least we can do is make them worth stealing."

Behind them, faint across the water, the bells of some distant village began to ring the hour—and Kestrel thought of Odile hearing those same bells from a cell, and swore to the storm in her blood that she would hear them ring differently soon.

They turned north, toward the drowned places, and began to walk.
