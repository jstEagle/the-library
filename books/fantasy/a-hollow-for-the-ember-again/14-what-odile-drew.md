## 14. What Odile Drew

They took her boots first.

Odile Ferran noticed that the way she noticed everything—professionally, hungrily, cataloguing. Boots before belt, belt before knife, knife before the little brass compass she'd worn on a cord since she was nineteen. Someone in this palace had a system. Someone had done this before, many times, and had opinions about the order of things.

"Name," said the man across the table.

He was not what she'd expected. She'd imagined the Hollow Men's handlers as gray, faceless functionaries, but this one was fleshy and pink-cheeked, with ink-stained fingers and a clerk's stoop. He looked like a man who did his own correspondence. That frightened her more than a torturer would have. Torturers were theater; clerks were thorough.

"Odile Ferran," she said. "Royal Cartographer, Third Chair, retired."

"There is no Third Chair anymore." He smoothed a paper in front of him. "There is no Royal Cartographic Office anymore. You burned it yourself, I'm told. Or rather, you failed to stop it burning."

"I was in Vell when it happened."

"You were in Vell when you *heard* it happened." He let that sit between them like something with teeth. "You were seen on the causeway road with three companions. A woman. A witch. A child."

"A guide, a herbalist, and my ward," Odile said. "I'm told children travel poorly alone."

The clerk wrote something down. She watched his hand—the pen moved in small, economical strokes, left to right, top to bottom, and she caught herself mapping the motion the way she mapped everything: coastlines, riverbends, the sweep of a guard's patrol past a torch bracket. Twenty-two years of drawing the world had taught her that everything moved in patterns, and patterns could be memorized, and anything memorized could be drawn.

Even from inside a cell.

Especially from inside a cell.

* * *

The dungeons beneath the palace were older than the palace above them, which Odile found professionally offensive. The walls were pre-dynastic masonry, fitted without mortar, the kind of work you saw in ruins in the Thornfell foothills and in exactly one other place: the forbidden lower levels she had mapped at twenty-six, the only living person who had ever drawn them entire.

She'd been proud of those maps. She'd been proud of the commission itself—young Odile Ferran, third daughter of a wine merchant, elevated over two senior draftsmen because she could hold a corridor's dimensions in her head for six hours without a single error of scale. The king's steward had walked her down through seven locked doors into the dark under the throne, and she had measured by stride and knotted cord and memory, and she had come up into the daylight and drawn the under-city in ink so fine it looked like breath on glass.

Then Aldric took the throne, and the maps were confiscated, and the doors below changed their locks, and one morning the cartographic office smelled of smoke and her life's work was ash in a brazier, and the new regime thanked her for her service and suggested she might prefer retirement in the provinces.

She had spent eleven years telling herself she was over it.

Now she sat in a cell built from the same stone she'd once drawn, and the irony was so precise it was almost funny. Almost.

The cell held a pallet, a bucket, and a slot in the door through which meals arrived twice a day. The meals were better than prison food should be—bread with actual grain in it, a boiled egg, watered wine. Which meant someone upstairs considered her an asset, not a prisoner. Assets got redrawn. Prisoners got buried.

Good. She could work with assets.

* * *

They came for her on the second day, and this time they brought her confiscated satchel, and the pink-cheeked clerk set it on the table between them like a peace offering.

"You'll want your instruments," he said. "We're prepared to make you comfortable. Comfortable people do good work."

"What work?"

"The king requires certain documents restored." He opened the satchel with a curator's delicacy and lifted out her brass compass, turning it in the lamplight. "Your maps of the lower levels were destroyed. Regrettably. But we understand the original drafts exist here—" he tapped his own temple, "—in the memory of the woman who made them. The crown would be grateful for their reconstruction."

And there it was.

Odile kept her face still, but inside her chest something turned over like a key in a lock she'd forgotten she carried. They wanted the under-city. They wanted the binding site. Which meant Kestrel and the others were ahead of them—or behind them—and either way, every hour Odile spent hunched over parchment in this room was an hour the Hollow Men spent without a route into the dark.

She thought about Kestrel's hands, scarred silver like lichen on slate. About Maren's terrible tea. About Ash, who had taken Odile's spare pencil on the third night of travel and drawn, in careful strokes, a bird—not any real bird, a made-up one, with too many wings—and shown it to her with the grave pride of a craftsman. She had kept the drawing folded in her breast pocket until the Hollow Men took that pocket's contents on the causeway. She wondered if some clerk had already thrown it away.

People she'd known for weeks. Weeks. And here was her heart, doing something embarrassing and unconditional about it.

"All right," she heard herself say. "I'll need proper vellum. My own pens. A lamp that doesn't flicker—I won't be responsible for errors caused by bad light. And food at fixed hours. My hand shakes when I'm hungry, and shaky lines are worthless lines."

The clerk smiled like a man who has just been told the fish will jump into the boat. "You'll have all of it."

"One more thing." She leaned forward. "I draw from memory in layers. Structure first, then detail. If you want accuracy, you'll bring me back to the same table at the same hours each day. Cartography is ritual, Master—whatever your name is."

"Hale. Under-clerk to the Lord Inquisitor."

"Under-clerk Hale. Ritual."

* * *

The ritual began.

They gave her a cell converted to a workroom: a table bolted to the floor, a lamp with a glass chimney, good vellum, her own pens recovered from the satchel. Two guards outside the door, rotated on a schedule. And Odile bent over the blank page and began, slowly, lovingly, to lie.

Not entirely. That was the art of it. The outer galleries she drew true—the ones the Hollow Men already knew, the ones that cost her nothing. Stairways, antechambers, the flooded cistern level where the salt-bells' echo reached even here. Her hand moved with all its old precision, and she let herself feel the pleasure of it, because the pleasure was real and the guards watching through the grate needed to see a woman absorbed in her craft, not a woman counting.

Because she was counting.

Guard One: tall, left-handed, relieved at the sixth bell and again at the fourteenth. Guard Two: short, chews something constantly, checks the grate every quarter-bell, superstitious—touches the doorframe twice on leaving. The relief rotation came with a changeover of eight minutes during which the corridor lamp was trimmed and refilled, and during those eight minutes the far end of the passage went unwatched. She knew because on the fourth day she asked for water at the changeover, purely to test, and no one came for eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes. You could drown a man in less. You could also walk a child through a door in less, if you knew where the door was.

And there was the other thing, the thing she fed into her drawings the way a cook feeds bitter greens into a sweet dish, undetectable but present. Every map is an argument. Every line says *this way, not that way*. So she drew the true route to the binding site—but she drew it passing through a gallery that hadn't existed for thirty years, collapsed in the reign of the old king's grandfather, according to the settlement cracks she'd noted in the masonry herself at twenty-six. Let the Inquisitor's men march confidently into forty feet of solid stone. Let them bring crowbars.

It wasn't much. It was a delay, maybe a day, maybe two. But she had watched Kestrel navigate by instinct through passes that weren't on any map, and she had watched Ash put his palm flat against stone and *know*, and she had decided, somewhere on the causeway with a hood over her head, that if these were the last maps she ever drew, they would be maps that fought back.

* * *

The jailer came on the sixth day.

He was young—young enough that his uniform still fit properly—and he brought her supper tray himself instead of sliding it through the slot, because the slot had warped and the tray stuck. He muttered an apology. He had a farmer's accent, northern, the vowel shapes of the Thornfell valleys, and Odile's ears, trained on a hundred survey interviews in a hundred market towns, pricked up like a dog's.

"You're a long way north of home," she said.

He flinched. "Eat your supper, mistress."

"The valleys around Corren's Ford? No—farther up. Highvale, maybe. My sister married a man from Highvale." This was false. Odile had no sister. But the lie was warm and specific and it worked, because the boy's shoulders dropped half an inch, the way shoulders do when a stranger speaks the geography of your childhood.

"Near Highvale," he admitted. "Before the blight years."

"Before the blight years," she echoed softly. Everyone was from before the blight years now. The whole kingdom was a country of people from before. "And how does a Highvale boy come to guard the under-cells of a palace?"

"Debt," he said, and then seemed to hear himself, and straightened. "Supper, mistress."

But he came back. That was the thing about lonely boys from blighted valleys: they came back. On the eighth day he lingered to tell her the lamp oil was being cut with tallow and she should keep her wick trimmed short or it would smoke. On the ninth he mentioned, unprompted, that the Inquisitor's men had ridden out two days prior—a full company, south road—and hadn't returned.

South road. Toward the drowned catacombs. Toward Kestrel.

Odile waited until his footsteps faded, then sat very still on her pallet and let the information settle into the architecture of her mind, next to the guard rotations and the lamp-trimming interval and the eight-minute changeover gap. Pieces. She was assembling pieces. It was what she did. Give her fragments and she would give you back a world.

On the eleventh day, she gave the boy a fragment of her own.

"My eyes are failing," she said, holding a page up to the lamp and squinting with theatrical frustration. "These fine lines swim. Would you do me a kindness? There's a scrap in my satchel—yellow paper, folded twice. Bring it here and I'll show you what a real map looks like, before my hands forget how."

The yellow paper was nothing. A corner torn from a Vell innkeeper's bill, blank on both sides. But when the boy brought it to her, she spent a long minute holding it to the light, tracing invisible lines with one fingernail, murmuring measurements—and while she did, she spoke, low and quick, in the cadence of a woman reading aloud:

"Company rode south. Tell the one who comes for me: the floodgates under the third cistern still answer to the old wheel. And the boy must not touch the black door."

The boy stared at her. "Mistress, I don't—"

"You didn't hear anything," Odile said pleasantly, taking the blank paper back and folding it away. "You brought a lady a piece of scrap because her eyes are bad. That's all that happened tonight. But if a woman with storm-scarred hands ever asks you what the guards eat for supper—" she met his eyes, "—you'll remember that in Highvale, they look after their own."

He went pale. He went pale because everyone in this kingdom had heard the stories by now, the smugglers' tales that had traveled faster than any army: the storm-witch in the mountains, the child who spoke the old tongue, the king who was being eaten by his own crown. Stories were a currency too, and Odile had just spent some.

Three nights later, a scrap of yellow paper appeared under her supper tray, folded twice. On it, in a laboring, unpracticed hand:

*SUPPER IS STEW. THE SHORT ONE IS AFRAID OF THE FLOODED LEVEL. WONT GO PAST THE THIRD DOOR. THERE WAS A BELL TODAY ALL THE BELLS RANG AND NO ONE KNOWS WHY.*

Odile read it twice, then held it to the lamp until the paper curled and browned and came apart in her fingers, and she breathed the ash out through the grate.

All the bells rang. In the City of Salt Bells, where ringing was taxed and licensed and rare, all the bells had rung at once, unbidden.

Something had happened below. Something old had stirred, or something young had spoken, and the city's bones had answered.

*They're close,* she thought, and pressed her palm flat against the cold pre-dynastic wall of her cell, as if she could pass the warmth of the thought through the stone itself, down through the levels she alone had ever drawn entire, to a woman with lightning in her blood and a boy made to be a door.

*Hold on. I'm coming. I've always known the way.*

* * *

Under-clerk Hale came on the fourteenth day with the finished sheets stacked under his arm, and for the first time he did not smile.

"These are remarkable," he said. "The Lord Inquisitor is pleased. So pleased that he wonders whether your memory extends to other things. The rite, Mistress Ferran. The words the witch uses. The chamber where the Ember was first bound."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she rose, stiff from fourteen days at a drafting table, and walked to the cell's rear wall, and laid her hand against the ancient fitted stones.

"Do you know what this is?" she said. "Pre-dynastic. No mortar. The masons who built this wall are dust ten centuries gone, and their joints are still tighter than anything your palace masons can cut. I mapped rooms down there that aren't in any record. Rooms with channels cut in the floors. Rooms where the air is warm when it should be cold." She turned. "You don't need my memory of a rite, Master Hale. You need to ask why your king is sending companies of masked men into holes in the earth instead of letting the witch finish whatever she started. Ask who the Ember is eating *first*."

Hale's pen hovered over his paper and did not write. For one naked moment, the clerk's stoop looked less like servility than like a man carrying something heavy.

"They say the king looked old yesterday," he said quietly. "In council. Just for a moment. Then the crown was put on and he was young again."

"Did they," said Odile.

"And the bells rang today. All of them. Second time this week." His voice dropped further. "My mother rang bells. Before the blight. She said a bell is just a voice made of metal, and metal remembers what it's for."

Odile held his gaze. Somewhere above them, faint through fathoms of stone, a bell began to swing—no, not a bell. A tremor. A shiver in the floor, in the lamp flame, in the ink standing in her well. The whole under-palace humming like a struck string.

Hale looked down at his blank page. Then, deliberately, he tore it out, crumpled it, and put it in his sleeve.

"Mistress Ferran," he said, "the Lord Inquisitor will want you to verify the route personally. At the site. Tomorrow, at the change of the watch." And his eyes, when they lifted to hers, were the eyes of a boy from a blighted valley deciding, once and finally, whose side the metal remembered.

Odile Ferran smiled for the first time in fourteen days.

"Then fetch my boots," she said. "And this time, leave the compass."
