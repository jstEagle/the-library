## 15. Into the Under-City

The capital of Cindralon announced itself first by smell—woodsmoke and horse dung and the river's green rot—and then by bells. Salt bells, Odile had called them, though she wasn't with them to explain the name. Kestrel heard them tolling across the water as they came down off the last ridge in the blue hour before dawn: a deep, briny clangor that seemed to come up out of the ground as much as the towers.

Ash stopped on the ridge road and looked at the city the way other children looked at fires.

He was nine years old, maybe ten, small for either, and he stood with his hands loose at his sides and his chin lifted, and the wind coming off the lowlands moved his hair without touching him. Kestrel had noticed that over the weeks of travel—the way weather went around him like water around a stone. She'd told herself it was nothing. She'd gotten very good at telling herself things were nothing.

"That's where they took her," Maren said quietly beside her.

Kestrel didn't answer. She kept looking at the city too—at the palace rising above the terraced roofs like a black tooth, at the walls Aldric had raised higher every year of his reign, at the smoke of a thousand hearths smearing the dawn. Somewhere inside all of that, in a cell or a workshop or worse, Odile Ferran was drawing maps for men who would use them to hunt children. Every hour Kestrel spent standing here feeling useless was an hour Odile paid for.

"We go tonight," she said. "We sleep today. All three of us."

Maren made a sound that might have been agreement. Ash turned from the city at last, and when he looked at Kestrel there was something new in his face—not fear, not the flat watchfulness he'd worn since the ford, but a kind of terrible recognition, like a man seeing a house he'd dreamed about and never visited.

He touched two fingers to his own chest. Then he pointed at the palace.

"I know," Kestrel said. Her voice came out rougher than she meant it to. "I know what you are. That's why we're going."

* * *

They slept in a shepherd's bothy with a leaking roof, and Kestrel did not sleep so much as lie still with her eyes closed while her heart ran its arithmetic. Three Hollow Men dead at the ford. A guide bought and sold them for what she guessed was less than a year's wages. Odile taken because Kestrel had trusted a stranger's smile over her own seven years of instinct. The sums were ugly and they did not balance, and somewhere past midnight she gave up pretending to rest and sat by the cold hearth sharpening a knife that was already sharp.

Maren joined her without being asked. The old woman moved like a bundle of sticks wrapped in wool, but her hands were steady when she laid them on her knees.

"You're going to try to leave us behind," Maren said. Not a question.

"I'm going to try to get you killed slower, if I can manage it."

"Girl." Maren said it the way she said everything, dry as old paper. "I helped bind that thing into a living man forty years ago. Whatever's waiting under that city, I have more to answer for than you do. And the boy—" she glanced toward the corner where Ash slept curled against the wall, breathing slow, "—the boy will follow you into the dark whether you allow it or not. He always has. You just haven't noticed yet."

Kestrel looked at the child. In sleep his face lost its strangeness and became only a face—a thin, serious, frost-scarred face that had appeared at her door one snow night and refused, with the mute stubbornness of falling snow itself, to leave.

"When this is done," she said, "if any of us are alive, I'm going to teach him to fish. Something useless. Something no king wants."

Maren snorted. "Aim higher. Teach him to read."

* * *

They entered the city at dusk through the tannery district, where the stink covered their passage and the guards' lanterns burned behind shutters against the river fog. Kestrel had expected checkpoints, patrols, the nervous energy of a capital whose king was said to be radiant and young and everywhere at once. What she found instead was quieter and worse: emptiness. Streets swept clean. Doors barred early. And on the corners, standing motionless in the fog, figures in gray livery who did not shift their weight, did not talk, did not seem to breathe.

Hollow Men. Dozens of them. Not hunting anymore. Guarding.

"They know something's coming," Maren whispered as they pressed themselves into an alley between a chandler's and a shuttered shrine. "Or they know something's *here*."

Ash tugged Kestrel's sleeve. He pointed down—down through the cobbles, down through the earth—and then made a shape with his hands: a doorway, opening.

"The catacombs," she breathed. "You can feel them?"

He nodded once, impatient, and pulled.

* * *

The way in was exactly the kind of entrance Odile would have loved and Kestrel hated: a storm-drain grate at the base of the old sea wall, half-drowned at high tide, breathing cold salt air. They waited for the tide to turn in the shadow of a beached fishing hull, and when the water finally began its long withdrawal they pried the grate up and dropped into the dark.

The tunnel swallowed them to the waist in chill water. Kestrel went first with a hooded lamp turned low, Maren behind her reciting under her breath—not spells, Kestrel had learned, but memory, the old woman keeping herself anchored by naming things: *tide turns at the third bell, the sea wall is dynastic work, the drains predate the dynasty.* Ash came last, and Kestrel noticed that he barely splashed. The water seemed to part around his legs with a courtesy it showed no one else.

"How far?" she asked Maren.

"The binding site isn't under the palace." Maren's voice echoed strangely, doubled by stone and water. "Everyone assumes it is, because that's where Aldric sits. But the Ember was bound long before there was a palace. Before there was a Cindralon. The first binding was done in a temple beneath the oldest quarter—the salt-bell quarter—and the palace was built *above the approach*, like a lock built on top of a door." She paused. "Odile mapped those lower levels once. She told me the corridors down there don't follow any logic she could chart. They follow the Ember."

Kestrel walked on. The water deepened, shallowed, deepened again. Rats watched them from ledges with wet eyes and, oddly, did not flee. Somewhere far ahead—or perhaps far below; sound played tricks down here—something hummed.

Not machinery. Not water. A hum with warmth in it, like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing.

"Do you hear that?" she asked.

Maren had gone very still. "I haven't heard that since I was nineteen years old," she said. "Gods forgive me. I hoped I never would again."

* * *

An hour in, the tunnels changed.

It happened gradually enough that Kestrel might have missed it if her body hadn't been trained by seven years of mountains to notice gradients—the way a pass grows colder, the way air thins. Here the gradient was temperature, but backwards. The water warmed. The stone warmed. The fog of their breath shortened and then vanished altogether, and the lamp's flame stood up straight and tall as if ashamed of its own caution.

And things grew in the dark.

First moss, green and soft as velvet, carpeting the flooded floor where nothing should grow a hundred feet beneath a city. Then ferns, unfurling from cracks in the dynastic masonry. Then flowers—small white things Kestrel had no name for, blooming out of season, out of soil, out of sense, their petals luminous faintly, the way snow holds light after sunset.

"It's leaking," Maren whispered. Her voice had gone thin. "All these years, it's been leaking through the binding. Everything near it grows. Everything above it starves." She laughed, a horrible little sound. "We thought we were sealing away a plague. We were sealing away a *spring*. And we built a kingdom on its lid, and the kingdom drank and drank and never knew why it was thirsty."

Ash waded forward until he was beside Kestrel, close enough that she could feel the heat coming off him—actual heat, gentle as a hand held near a banked fire. His eyes had gone bright in the lamplight, and when he looked up at her she saw that he was afraid, truly afraid, for the first time since the snow night. Not of the dark. Of coming home.

She took his hand. It was the first time she'd initiated it. His fingers closed around hers with surprising strength.

"We can still turn back," she said, and hated herself for offering it, because they both knew it was a lie.

He shook his head. Then he pointed forward, down the tunnel where the white flowers thickened and the hum deepened into something almost like a voice, and made the doorway-shape with his free hand again.

*Open,* he meant. *It wants to open.*

"Not yet," Kestrel said softly. "Not until we choose the moment. Nothing opens tonight that we don't open ourselves."

The hum, impossibly, seemed to consider this.

* * *

They made camp in a side chamber where the floodwater had receded into a clear pool ringed with moss, and Kestrel allowed them four hours of sleep in shifts because exhaustion killed more smugglers than soldiers ever had. She took the last watch, sitting with her back to warm stone, knife across her knees, watching the luminous flowers pulse gently in the dark like a field of drowned stars.

Her magic stirred.

It had been doing that since the ford—rising unasked, answering fear, pressing against the walls she'd built around it the way roots press against a pot. Down here it rose easily, greedily, and she realized with a lurch that it wasn't feeding on her fear at all. It was feeding on the same warmth that fed the flowers. The Ember called to storm-blood the way a mother calls a child home, and her lightning lay against the inside of her skin and *purred*.

"Stop it," she whispered to herself. "You are a smuggler. You are nobody. You are a woman who carries iron for money and feels nothing."

The purring did not stop. But it gentled, the way a dog gentles when you speak to it, and she supposed that was the best bargain she'd get.

Ash woke near the end of her watch without being roused—he simply opened his eyes, the way he always did, arriving in wakefulness whole. He crawled over and sat against her side, and together they watched the flowers breathe light.

After a while he took her hand and traced something on her palm with one finger. Slowly, deliberately, so she could follow it.

A word. Letters in no alphabet she knew—but she'd watched Maren teach him to render the old tongue in chalk on their campfire stones, and she recognized the shapes now, the flowing pre-dynastic curves.

"What does it say?" she asked.

He frowned, searching for how to tell her. Then he pressed her palm flat against the warm stone beside them, held it there, and looked at her with those too-old eyes.

*The same as this,* he meant. *Warm.*

His name, she understood suddenly. Or the word he'd spoken the first night in her cabin, the one the candles had guttered to hear. *Ember.* He had introduced himself to her with the name of the thing he was made to hold.

"Oh, Ash," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she pulled him close against her shoulder and felt the impossible furnace-heat of him and thought: *I will burn the world down before I let it take you.*

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the salt bells began to toll—and not the measured hours. An alarm pattern. Fast, urgent, wrong.

Then, closer, much closer, from the tunnel they'd come by: boots. Many boots, moving through water, unhurried, in step.

The Hollow Men hadn't followed their trail.

They had simply known where the door was, and waited for the door to walk to them.
