## 16. The Crown Beneath

The Ember woke him at the hour when even dreams abandon kings.

Aldric sat up in a bed too large for one man and pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw constellations that were not there. The whispering had changed. For six years it had been a tide—slow, patient, licking at the shores of his mind, carrying away a memory here, a name there, the taste of his mother's bread, the color of his first horse, the face of the brother whose throne he had taken and whose grave he had never visited. A tide. He had learned to live with tides.

But tonight it was a scream.

*They are coming,* said the thing beneath his crown, in no language and every language, in the voice of his father, in the voice of the well from which he'd drunk as a boy, in the voice of himself at nine years old, before anything. *They are coming to open the door.*

He rose. His hands shook—not with fear, though fear lived in him now like a tenant who paid no rent, but with the other thing, the hunger that was not his. The Ember was agitated, and its agitation moved through him like fire through a dry field. He could feel it reaching down through stone, through the roots of the palace, through the old dark where it had been bound before he had been foolish enough, ambitious enough, desperate enough to draw it into himself.

*Foolish,* whispered the Ember, agreeably. *Ambitious. Desperate.* It liked to hand him back his own words. It liked to wear them.

He dressed without calling the valet. He had stopped calling servants in the small hours years ago, after the third one had found him standing in the corner of his chamber talking to someone who was not there, and after the fourth had simply vanished from the rolls, reassigned, forgotten—the forgetting done by Aldric himself, because remembering witnesses was a luxury the crown could not afford. The crown. That was the joke he no longer laughed at: everyone in the capital spoke of *the crown's will*, *the crown's justice*, and they thought they meant the man. They did not know how literal they were.

The crown was on its stand by the window, catching moonlight along its circlet of black iron set with a single stone the color of a coal that has never gone out. Sixty years old, the smiths said, though no smith alive had made it. Older than the dynasty it had crowned. Older than the word for king, perhaps. When he put it on, the whispering became conversation. When he took it off, the conversation became argument, and the argument became pain, and the pain became the certain knowledge that he would crawl across broken glass to have it back.

He did not put it on. Not yet. There were moments—he counted them now, hoarded them like a miser counts coins gone thin—when he wanted to be only Aldric Vane, second son, the one nobody had expected anything of. Those moments were shorter every year. Last winter he had managed an hour. This spring, minutes. Tonight he gave himself the walk down, unadorned, because what he was about to do deserved whatever was left of the man.

*You will go down anyway,* the Ember said, amused. *You always come down.*

"I know," Aldric said aloud, to the empty room, to the crown, to himself. "I know."

* * *

The palace had been built on top of something older, and everyone who built it had known, and everyone who knew had agreed, in the way of powerful men agreeing, to build anyway and call the lower levels by administrative names. Sub-vaults. Archive annexes. The Under-Registry. Odile Ferran had mapped them once—her charts had been the most honest things ever drawn of this place, which was why he had burned them, which was why she was drinking herself blind in some border town, which was one more debt on a ledger so long he had stopped reading it.

He descended alone, past the guards who did not look at him, past the sealed doors with their pre-dynastic script that no scholar had ever fully translated, down stairs that grew older as they went, the masonry shifting from dressed granite to fitted basalt to stone that seemed less carved than grown. The air warmed. That was the wrongness of it, the thing no visitor ever believed: it should have been cold down here, cold as graves are cold, but instead the deep air held a warmth like a body's warmth, like a sleeping animal's, like a hearth in a house you cannot find again in a dream.

The whispering swelled around him, delighted.

*Yes. Yes. Closer.*

At the last door—a slab of black iron banded in bronze, older than ironwork should be—two Hollow Men stood watch. Their masks were smooth white porcelain, featureless except for eye-holes, and behind the holes there was nothing you could call a face anymore. He had made them. That was the true obscenity of his reign, the one the ballads would never sing: not the taxes, not the burnings, not the disappeared, but the making. Each Hollow Man had been a person once, a soldier or a criminal or merely inconvenient, and the Ember had shown him how to hollow them—pour out the self, fill the shell with obedience. It cost him something each time. A year of himself, more or less. A season of memory. Once, a whole summer, gone between one dawn and the next, so that he had woken to autumn and to letters of condolence for a death he could not recall attending.

Thirty men hollowed. Thirty years, give or take. He had been fifty-four when he took the throne. The portraits still showed thirty. The portraits lied better than he did.

"Open it," he said.

The Hollow Men turned in perfect unison, drew the bolts in perfect unison, and swung the door inward on darkness that breathed.

* * *

The binding chamber had not been built. It had been *agreed upon*. That was Maren's word for it, decades ago, when she was a young apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a voice that shook as she explained the rite: *the chamber is where the world consented to hold it, Your Majesty. We do not make such places. We only find where they already are.* She had helped bind it, she and her masters, sealing away what they believed was a plague, a blight, a wild force that ate the land. They had not known—or had not told him—that they were caging something alive. Something that listened. Something that waited with the patience of geology for a door.

In the center of the chamber stood the well. Not a well of water; a well of absence, a circle of ancient stone perhaps three paces across, descending into a dark that lamplight refused to enter. Around its rim ran the old script, and above it, suspended by nothing, hung the original binding—a lattice of pale light like frost on glass, humming faintly, fraying at the edges.

It had been whole when he came down here six years ago with his ambition and his grief and his brother's crown fresh on his head. He had come to *look*, he told himself. To understand what he ruled. And the thing in the well had looked back, and it had said, in his dead father's voice, in his own voice, in a voice like warm honey poured over an open wound:

*You are tired. You are second-born. You were never enough, and you know it. Take me, and be enough forever.*

And he, being exactly the man the Ember had described, had reached out his hand.

Now the lattice above the well was threadbare as an old shawl, and the warmth in the chamber was fever-heat, and the walls sweated in the wrong season, and the Ember inside his chest leaned toward the well like a dog toward home, and Aldric understood—with the clarity that came only in these descents, these stolen minutes of being only a man—that he had not taken the Ember at all. He had been taken. He was not the vessel's master. He was the vessel's *stopper*, and stoppers rot.

"What is it?" he asked the dark. "What has it so loud tonight?"

The nearest Hollow Man tilted its blank mask. From somewhere in the porcelain came a voice like wind through a keyhole: *"Three travelers, Majesty. Taken at the causeway ford. One escaped us—one is held—and the vessel runs east with the storm-witch and the old woman."*

"The vessel." The word tasted of ash. "The child."

*"The child,"* the Hollow Man agreed.

Ash. He had seen the boy once, through a scrying bowl, months ago—a small figure in a snowfield, walking out of nowhere, and the Ember inside Aldric had risen like water in a flooded cellar, screaming through his skull in a hundred borrowed voices: *THE DOOR. THE DOOR IS WALKING. BRING ME MY DOOR.* He had sent riders. He had spent a year of himself on that order alone, felt it drain out of him like blood from a opened vein, and had done it anyway, because the alternative was to refuse, and he no longer knew if refusal was something he could still perform.

"They're coming here," he said. "Down. To the well."

*"Yes, Majesty."*

"And you will kill them."

*"If you command it, Majesty."*

He opened his mouth to command it. That was the liturgy; that was the shape of every day for six years; the words rose in him smooth as river stones, worn by repetition. *Kill them. Bring me the child. Burn the village if the village looks at them kindly.*

And then the lattice above the well flickered, and in its flicker he saw—briefly, horribly—his own reflection in the polished dark of the well's rim. Not the portrait-king. Not the radiant young face the propaganda painters gave him. A man of sixty-odd years wearing thirty years of stolen youth like a coat two sizes too small, seams straining, eyes sunk in bruised sockets, hair gone white at the temples where the crown sat. A man with his brother's murder folded up small inside him where he kept the things he could not afford to remember. A man who had, once, at nine years old, pulled a drowning puppy from the mill-race, and who could no longer remember the puppy's fur, only that he had been kind once, and that the kindness had weighed nothing then.

The words died in his throat.

"No," Aldric said.

The Hollow Men went very still. Stillness, in them, was not calm. It was attention.

"Not killed," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle like ice on a pond. "Brought to me. Alive. All of them. Do you—" He stopped. Started again. Somewhere far above, in the daylight world, there had been a man who could finish sentences. "Do you understand? Alive. I will speak with them myself. Alone."

*"Majesty,"* said the Hollow Man, and the tonelessness of it made the title sound like a diagnosis. *"The storm-witch is dangerous. The orders on record—"*

"The orders on record are mine to change." He heard the imperial snap come into his voice, felt the crown's cadence take his tongue, and hated it, and let it happen, because the alternative was to stand here trembling before furniture in a mask. "Go. Wait above. If they breach the under-city, you bring them to this chamber and you do not touch them. Go."

They went, two shadows folding themselves up the stair, and the door boomed shut behind them, and Aldric Vane, King of the Thornmark, Warden of the Salt Roads, Radiant Sovereign of the New Dawn, stood alone in a room with a god-shaped hole and finally, finally told the truth.

* * *

"You knew," he said to the well. "From the first night. You knew what I'd become."

The dark did not answer, but the warmth pulsed, pleased, and the whisper came gentle as a lullaby: *I knew you were lonely. I knew you were hungry. I am everything you were promised, Aldric. Eternity. Power. Never to be second again. Have I not delivered?*

"You've eaten my mother's face." His voice was steady. That surprised him. "I had her face, and now when I try to remember it, all I see is your dark. You've eaten my wedding day. You've eaten the name of the village where I learned to swim. You've eaten thirty people and made them into masks. You are not eternity. You are a *fire*, and I am the wood, and there is almost none of me left."

*There is enough,* said the Ember, and for the first time in six years, it sounded slightly less than certain. *There is enough of you to hold me. Hold me. Hold me. HOLD—*

The pressure came, sudden and vast, the old tidal pull become a riptide, dragging at everything he was, and Aldric dropped to his knees on stone worn smooth by worshippers older than any kingdom, and he gripped the well's rim, and he screamed—but quietly, the way kings scream, through closed teeth, because even alone in the dark some part of him was still performing.

When it passed, he knelt there shaking, and the lattice-light above the well guttered, and through its failing frost he watched sparks rise from the depths like the reverse of snowfall, and he thought: *it wants the child because the child is a better door, and when the door opens, what happens to the stopper?*

Nothing. Nothing happens to the stopper. That was the answer. The stopper is thrown away.

He laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a cough, and it echoed strangely in the chamber, and the echo sounded younger than he was, and older.

* * *

He did not know how long he knelt there. Time went strange in the deep places, pooled and stagnated. What brought him back to himself was a sound from the stair—the distant boom of the upper door, voices, the ring of steel-shod boots. The Hollow Men, reporting movement. Or the intruders already inside, already close, the storm-witch and the child and the old woman who had helped build this cage and must hate herself for it in ways he understood completely.

He should flee upward. He should summon guards, seal the levels, retreat behind the machinery of power and let it grind on his behalf, as he had done for six years.

Instead Aldric got to his feet, crossed the chamber, and sat down on the lowest step of the stair, like a servant waiting in a corridor, like a man waiting outside a sickroom. He took off nothing—there was nothing left to take off—but he bowed his head into his hands, and for a while he was simply a tired man in a warm dark place, listening to the thing below him breathe.

*They are coming,* the Ember sang, hungry, frightened—it could frighten too, he had learned that, it feared the door the way a prisoner fears both freedom and the gallows. *Stop them. Stop them. Stop—*

"No," Aldric said. "I think I'll wait."

*You cannot mean—*

"I mean to beg them." He said it aloud, to the dark, to the stairwell, to whatever was left of the nine-year-old at the mill-race. "That's what I'm doing here. Sitting where they'll see me. So they can't mistake me for an ambush. So the witch can see my hands empty." He looked at his hands—long, fine-boned, a fencer's hands, a signer-of-death-warrants' hands. "I'm going to ask her to end it. Whatever the rite costs. Whatever it takes. I'm going to get on my knees in front of a woman whose life I ruined for a crime she didn't commit, and I'm going to beg."

*You will not,* said the Ember, and the lullaby-gentleness was gone, and underneath it was something vast and cold and astonished. *You are mine. You reached for me. You said yes.*

"I said yes," Aldric agreed. His eyes were burning. When had he last wept? He genuinely could not remember—could not remember whether he had wept at his coronation, at his brother's funeral-that-was-not-a-funeral, at anything, ever, or whether the Ember had drunk those tears too, and the not-knowing was its own grief. "That's the hell of it. I said yes, and I've been saying yes for six years, and I am so *tired*, do you understand? Tired in the part of me you haven't eaten yet. There's a little room left in there. Small. Dirty. Mine. And I am going to sit in it, and I am going to wait for the woman with the storm in her blood, and I am going to say: *please.*"

The chamber went silent. Even the well's breathing seemed to pause.

Then, softly, in his father's voice, in his own voice, in the voice of every flatterer who had ever bent over his hand: *She will kill you, Aldric. The unbinding needs a vessel. Storm-blooded flesh, willing, forever. You know what she'll choose.*

"Yes," he said. "Probably me. Good."

*You don't want to die.*

"I don't want to *be*," Aldric said, and stood, and straightened his shoulders, and for one moment—for one clean, mortal, unrepeatable moment—he was not the crown's. He was just a man in a warm dark room, waiting to beg for his life by offering it. "There's a difference. You wouldn't understand. You've never been anything but yourself."

Above him, the stairwell rang with footsteps. Many feet. And among them—impossible, unmistakable, threading through the boot-beats like lightning through cloud—a low rolling murmur, a static hush, the sound the air makes just before a storm breaks.

The storm-witch. Coming down. With the child.

Aldric wiped his face with both hands, took a breath that hurt all the way down, and prepared to kneel.

Behind his eyes, patient as rot, the crown began to close.
