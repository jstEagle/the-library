## 22. Emberlight

The first thing I remember clearly is the color of Kestrel's laugh.

I know that sounds strange, that a sound could have a color, but when you have lived a year with a sun burning gently in your chest, you learn that everything has more colors than the eye was told about. Kestrel's laugh is copper and storm-green, like lightning seen through bottle glass, and for most of my life I did not hear it at all because I had no voice to ask for it.

I am Ash. I am nine years old, or I think I am; Maren says it doesn't matter, that time kept no ledger of me while I was being made. I live in a small stone house above a garden that never sleeps, on the shoulder of a hill beneath which an old, old door now stands open instead of shut. And inside me, coiled warm as a cat asleep by a hearth, is the Ember—the first fire of the world, the thing kings stole and choked on, the thing they said would devour whoever held it.

It has not devoured me. It hums.

Maren says that's the whole secret, the one the old binding-language carried all along, hidden in plain sight in every word Ash ever spoke before he could speak anything else: *a hollow made willing is not a cage.* The Ember ate Aldric because he took it with fists and fear. It nearly ate me too, I think—except that when it turned toward my heart and asked its wordless question, *will you hold me*, I was not holding it back. I was reaching out.

And I was not alone. That part matters more than all the rest. I have learned enough words now to say it properly, so I will say it once here and then get on with the story, because Odile says a story that lingers on its doorstep is a coward:

Survival was the beginning. Family is what you build afterward.

* * *

Morning comes up gold through the east window, and I wake before it does, which is new. For a long time after the binding I slept like something buried, ten hours, twelve, my body paying debts the Ember's keeping had run up against my bones. Now I wake early and lie still and listen to the hum.

It has moods. Nobody believes this but Kestrel, and she only believes it because she has storms and knows they do too. Some days the Ember hums low and thoughtful, like the ground under snow. Some days it runs bright and quick, wanting movement, wanting to be *used*. Today it is doing something I have come to think of as its kitchen-sound—a bubbling, contented warmth, like soup someone kind is stirring.

"You're awake," Kestrel says from the doorway. She says it the same way every morning, like she still can't quite believe it, like every dawn she checks that the boy who stepped into the circle is still a boy and not just a vessel wearing one. Her hair is shorter than it was a year ago—she cut it herself with a fish knife the week we moved in, declaring her old life "over, and badly styled besides." There's grey coming in at her temples now, real grey, not the hollowed-out white that grief and exile put there. She lets it grow. She says she's earned it.

"I'm awake," I say, and my voice cracks on the second word because I'm nine and growing, and Kestrel grins like I've performed a miracle.

She does that. Every ordinary thing I do—speaking, sulking, eating three helpings of porridge, losing at cards—is a small miracle to her, because for seven years her house held nothing but her own silence and mine. I used to think she was exaggerating. Now that Maren has taught me about the years Before, about the storm that killed the wrong people and the exile that killed everything else in Kestrel Vasquez except her competence, I understand: she is not exaggerating. She is counting. She counts every ordinary day like coins she never expected to have.

"Lessons," she says. "Maren's already downstairs threatening the alphabet."

"The alphabet isn't scared of her."

"The alphabet should be." She tosses my coat at me—it's the same coat, the one she mended that first night without admitting she'd decided to keep me, patches on patches now like a story told over and over—and I pull it on and go down into the smell of bread and ink and dried yarrow that means home.

* * *

Lessons happen at the long table by the south window, where the Emberlight reaches even in winter. That's what the village calls it now, the warmth that rises from below—Emberlight. The gardens up here bloom three weeks ahead of everyone else's. Root vegetables the size of fists. Tomatoes in the frost-months, which Maren calls "showing off" and the Ember presumably calls *being helpful*, because that is its whole personality now: enormous, ancient, primordial beyond the memory of gods, and desperate to be useful.

"Ash." Maren taps the slate. "Focus. Where were we?"

"*Water*," I say, in the old tongue. "*Vessel*." Then, because I am nine and cannot resist: "*Door*."

"Door," Maren agrees, and her eyes do the thing they do—not sad exactly, more like weather moving through. Maren is the only one of them who was there, at the first binding, seventy years ago, a young apprentice who thought she was sealing away a plague and found out too late she'd helped cage something alive. She confessed it around a healing fire on the road, voice shaking, hands shaking, and Kestrel had taken her hands and said, *you came back to fix it, that's worth more than clean,* and Maren had cried, and I had understood maybe half of it, and now I understand all of it. Guilt spoken aloud gets lighter. That's another lesson, though it's not in any book.

"Now," Maren says, "the sentence on the left."

The pre-dynastic script crawls across the slate like beetle tracks. A year ago these were the only words I had—fragments rising out of me, a mute boy leaking the language he was made in. Now I read them, write them, conjugate them, and the strangeness of that is not lost on me. I was the last speaker of a tongue older than kingdoms, and now I am its first student. Both things are true. Odile says most true things come in pairs like that.

"*The door opens from within,*" I translate slowly. "*Not from without. No hand may force it. No crown may claim it.*"

"And who wrote that?"

"The binders. The first ones." I look up. "They knew. They knew someone would have to choose it someday, so they wrote instructions into the language itself. Into *me*."

Maren sets down the chalk. "Do you want to stop?"

"No." I don't hesitate, and I feel her relax. We've had this conversation before, and we'll have it again, because Maren carries guilt the way other people carry knives—always, everywhere, checked nightly. But the truth doesn't hurt the way she fears. "They weren't cruel, whoever made me. They were desperate, and they were careful, and they wrote *from within* as big as they could. It's not a warning. It's a promise. Nobody will ever be forced again."

Maren blinks hard twice and says, "Well. Good. Mathematics after lunch," in a voice that has gone slightly gravelly, and I let her pretend that's why.

* * *

Odile arrives on Thursdays, riding up from Vell with saddlebags full of paper and complaints.

She is not technically disgraced anymore. When the crown went dark and Aldric collapsed into ordinary mortality—an old man of sixty who looked ninety, who wept in the binding chamber and then, mercifully, simply stopped mattering—the regency council that replaced him spent its first year undoing his crimes, and high on the list was Odile Ferran's name. They offered her back her post. Royal Cartographer. Rooms in the palace. Access to the archives.

She told them what she tells everyone who offers her anything official, which is a word I learned specifically from watching her say it.

Instead she founded the Ferran Atlas Works in Vell, three apprentices and a printing press, and she is redrawing every map in the kingdom—*accurately*, she thunders, *with roads that exist and borders that respect rivers and none of the lower levels blotted out because some king got squeamish*. The new maps are beautiful. I've seen the proofs. In the corner of each one, tiny, there's a little drawing of a flame, and under it: *Here be warmth.*

"How's my favorite geological formation?" she asks me, dropping her bags, accepting the tea Kestrel hands her without looking, because this is Thursday and this is what happens on Thursdays and some rituals do not need discussion.

"Humming."

"Good, good. Sit. Look." She unrolls fresh vellum across the table, shoving Maren's slates aside with total disregard for pre-dynastic scholarship. "The catacombs, redrawn. Properly this time. And—" her voice does its rare quieting— "the floodgates. Marked. With thanks."

We don't talk about that much, the causeway, the betrayal, the weeks Odile spent in the palace dungeons drawing maps with one hand and smuggling coded notes through a sympathetic jailer with the other. She escaped during the chaos of the rite, wading out through the very floodgates she'd opened, half-drowned and swearing in four languages, and arrived in the binding chamber just in time to see me step into the circle. She doesn't like to speak of it. But she maps the floodgates every time, marks them carefully, and once she said, not looking at anyone, *it's how I say thank you to architecture.*

"New commission," she announces now. "The council wants a map of the Emberlight district. Crop yields, garden boundaries, the works. Which means, my boy, surveying, and which means you're hired. Your fire can sense groundwater, yes? Excellent. You're worth three diviners and you work for bread."

"I work for *stories*," I correct. This is our running negotiation. Odile pays me in accounts of her dungeon escape, each telling slightly more heroic than the last, and both of us know the earlier versions are closer to the truth and neither of us cares.

"Stories. Fine. One story per mile surveyed." She's already sketching. "Kestrel, your boy drives a hard bargain."

Kestrel, mending a net by the window—she mends things constantly, nets and fences and coats, and I think it's her version of what Odile does with floodgates—looks up and smiles the copper-and-storm-green smile. "He learned from the best. All three of you drive ruinous bargains. He never had a chance."

* * *

But the festival. I meant to tell you about the festival.

It happens every year now on the anniversary of the unbinding, in the square below our hill, and it is the one night the whole valley climbs toward us. Last year there were lanterns and fiddles and a man selling candied apples who gave me two free for saving his crops, which I hadn't done on purpose, the warmth just does that sometimes. This year the council sent up an actual request, delivered by a breathless clerk: *would the young lord consider—*

I am not a young lord. I made them stop. I am Ash, guardian-in-training, apprentice cartographer, student of dead languages, owner of one patched coat. But the request itself was fine. What they wanted was lights.

Because here is the thing nobody expected, the thing that makes Maren rewrite her theories and Odile make little notes in the margins of hers: the Ember likes to play.

It was caged for seventy years and starved for a hundred before that, and now it lives in a boy who runs downhill too fast and laughs at bad jokes, and when I am happy it *pours*. Little arcs of gold off my fingertips. Warmth that makes bread rise twice as fast. And when Kestrel—storm-blooded Kestrel, whose magic woke again in a storm cellar one terrible night and then saved all our lives at a river ford, and who spent a year learning, patiently, with Maren's help, to call weather gently instead of catastrophically—when Kestrel raises her hands to the sky on festival night and calls down a storm that would have flattened armies—

—I catch it.

That's the trick. That's the whole show. She pulls the lightning out of the clouds and I take it as it falls, the Ember drinking the strike the way dry ground drinks rain, and then we throw it back up together, all of it, transformed, as a thousand slow golden sparks that drift over the valley like upward-falling stars.

The first time we tried it, in the yard behind the house, Kestrel was so frightened she shook. *If I hurt you* —she couldn't finish it. I took her hand and put it flat against my chest, over the hum, and said the first full sentence I ever spoke to her, the one I said under the salt-bell tower in Vell, and the one that is still true:

*I was made to be the door. Doors keep people safe. That's all a door is.*

So on festival night she stands beside me on the hilltop with her scarred hands raised, laughing—actually laughing, loud and unguarded, copper and storm-green—while the sky empties itself into my chest and I empty it back out in light, and below us the whole valley goes quiet and then roars, and Maren cries the way she always cries, pretending it's the cold, and Odile sketches fast and mutters *no one will believe the colors, no one will believe me,* and the Ember sings in its hollow, a song like *home, home, home.*

Afterward, sparks still settling in Kestrel's hair, she puts her arm around my shoulders and looks down at the fires of the festival, the dancing, the bells—Vell sent bells, salt-cast bronze, ringing freely now, no toll, no tax, no king to silence them.

"Seven years I did arithmetic," she says quietly. "Risks weighed like coin. Every road a calculation, every person a liability." Her thumb moves absently over the old burn-scars on her own wrist. "And then a knock at my door in the snow, and the whole ledger went to ash."

"You kept the coat," I point out.

"I kept the coat." She squeezes me. "Best trade I ever made."

Below, the music changes to something slow. Odile is arguing joyfully with a farmer about elevation readings. Maren has been captured by three grandmothers demanding to know if the Emberlight soil would suit their onions. The sparks drift down through the dark like patient golden snow.

I stand between them—all of them, my impossible, scavenged, found, *built* family—and I reach inward to the oldest language there is, the one that needs no words, and I ask the Ember how it fares tonight, in its hollow, in its boy, in its world that is learning to breathe.

It answers the way it always does. Not in pre-dynastic script, not in whispers or promises of eternity like the poison it fed a dying king. Just warmth, rising, sure as morning:

*Held,* it says. *Willing. Home.*

And far away, in the dark of the deep places where the first door once stood shut, the old stones lie open and quiet and cold, keeping only their inscription, waiting for no one—because the door opens from within, and everyone who needed through it has already come out into the light.
