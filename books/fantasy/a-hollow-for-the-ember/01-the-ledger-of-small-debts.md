## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The trouble with copying tithes for nine years was that Rafael Voss had become very good at it, and very good at a thing is a slow kind of trap.

She sat in the Emberhold scriptorium with her sleeves pinned back and her ink pot warming near the little brass brazier, working through the autumn tithe rolls of the valley parishes. Barley from Hollowmere. Wool from the Shepherd's Steps. Two casks of plum wine from the widow Ansel at Coldwater, who always sent wine instead of coin and always sent one cask more than she owed, because — as the margin note in last year's ledger put it, in Brother Odo's fussy hand — *the widow Ansel believes generosity is a form of accounting.* Rafael had smiled at that note every year for six years. It was, she suspected, the only joke Brother Odo had ever committed to paper.

"Voss." The under-cantor's voice drifted down the long room like something blown in on a draft. "You've smudged."

Rafael looked down. She hadn't smudged. But arguing with Under-Cantor Pellis was like arguing with weather; you could do it all morning and still end up wet. "I'll recopy the line," she said, and did, though there was nothing wrong with it, and felt the small familiar satisfaction of winning an argument nobody knew was happening.

The scriptorium occupied the east wing of the Emberhold, where the morning light came through windows of honey-colored glass and lay across forty desks in long amber stripes. Forty scribes, give or take the ones out sick or dead or promoted, bent over their work like a field of patient crops. The Emberhold kept the valley's records: births, deaths, tithes, debts, marriages, land transfers, the whole great slow river of ordinary life written down so that no one downstream could claim they'd never been told. It was holy work, nominally. Practically, it was work, and Rafael was grateful for it the way you're grateful for a roof during a storm — without ever quite forgetting what the storm was.

Because Rafael Voss had a secret, and the secret was this: when she touched things, sometimes they touched back.

Not everything. Not people — people were mercifully quiet, or perhaps simply too loud for her to hear over their own noise. But objects with history in them, objects that had been *held*, *used*, *loved* or *feared* — those carried residue. Old wax seals were the worst. Wax remembered being pressed; wax remembered the hand that pressed it, the warmth of it, the intention. Nine years of copying contracts and charters and tithe bonds had taught Rafael to handle seals by their edges, quickly, the way you'd pick up a hot pan. She had learned to keep her fingertips calloused and dry. She had learned to breathe through the flare when it came anyway — a warmth behind the sternum, a taste like struck flint, and then someone else's moment unfolding behind her eyes like a letter being read aloud by a stranger.

She had learned, above all, not to let anyone see.

The gift — she refused to think of it as a curse, mostly — had arrived at fourteen, uninvited, the way most gifts arrive in the Voss family. Their mother had gone quiet about it before that, watching Rafael's hands whenever Rafael handled anything old, and after it arrived she'd gone quieter still, until the winter she died and left them a carter's wage, a house with a leaking roof, and a set of instructions Rafael had followed ever since:

*Never touch what you don't have to. Never let them see your face change. And Rafe — if anyone asks, you're dull. Be dull so well they never look twice.*

Rafael had made a career of dullness. She was, by every outward measure, the least interesting scribe in the Emberhold. On time, unremarkable, competent in the flat way of furniture. Her handwriting was deliberately unlovely. She laughed at Brother Odo's jokes exactly once each, at the socially correct volume. If dullness were a craft, she'd have earned her master's marks years ago.

And yet.

She reached for the next document in her stack — a tithe bond from the miller at Stony Ford, sealed with the parish stamp — and her thumb found the seal's edge before her caution caught up with it. Warmth bloomed. Flint on the tongue. And then, briefly, unwanted: the miller's memory, three summers old, of standing in his own doorway watching his daughter ride away to service in the lowlands, his hand still raised in a wave he held too long, the road emptying, the doorframe rough under his other palm.

Rafael exhaled slowly and wrote *Stony Ford, 40 bushels milled grain, paid in full* in her careful, ugly hand. The miller's grief settled into her chest and stayed there, the way these things did, a pebble in the shoe of the heart. That was the part nobody warned you about, having a gift like hers. Not the seeing. The keeping. Every borrowed memory took up residence somewhere, and after nine years Rafael's interior was a crowded house full of other people's moments — weddings she hadn't attended, funerals she hadn't wept at, a shepherd's terror in a whiteout, a child's delight at a fair-day ribbon. She carried them all politely, like a woman holding coats at a party that never ended.

"You're doing the face again," murmured Wren from the next desk.

Wren was nineteen, freckled, chronically under-supervised, and the closest thing Rafael had to a friend inside the walls. Wren believed Rafael's occasional faraway expressions were piety. This misunderstanding had been carefully cultivated and Rafael intended to maintain it until one of them died.

"I'm doing *devotion*," Rafael said. "It's required."

"It's required at the altar, not at the tithe rolls. You look like you're listening to something."

"I'm listening to Pellis breathe. It's very spiritual."

Wren snorted, then ducked her head as Pellis's shadow crossed the aisle. He paused at their desks, a thin man with the posture of a plumb line and eyes that catalogued everything and approved of almost nothing.

"The registrar wants the bonded accounts separated out," he said. "Anything touching court debt goes in the second stack. Crown business now takes precedence." His mouth did something complicated around the word *crown*, like a man biting a lemon and deciding to be polite about it.

"Court debt?" Wren asked. "Out here? Who out here owes the *court*?"

"People owe everyone everything," Pellis said. "That is the nature of people. Second stack, Voss. By vespers."

He moved on. Rafael sorted her documents, setting aside two bonds that referenced crown levies, and tried not to notice how the words *crown business* had landed in the room like a stone dropped in a well — a small splash, then ripples moving outward through forty desks, everyone suddenly very busy, everyone suddenly very careful.

* * *

At midday the scribes ate in the refectory, where the bread was good and the conversation was rationed. Rafael took her usual spot by the wall, beside Wren and across from old Brother Fenwick, who had copied funeral registers for thirty years and claimed it had given him a cheerful disposition, since the dead, he said, never complained about his margins.

"They say riders came over the pass two days ago," Wren said, tearing her bread with the intensity of someone who needed something to tear. "White banner. Nobody carries a white banner except—"

"Hush," said Fenwick, but gently, the way you hush a fire you intend to keep.

"—except the king's hunt," Wren finished anyway, because she was nineteen and the world had not yet taught her better. "My cousin drove cart down from the Steps and saw them camped at the tollhouse. Six of them. Huntresses, real ones, with the grey cloaks."

Rafael kept her face pleasant and empty and felt the pebble in her chest turn to a stone.

The witch-hunters. Everyone knew of them the way you know of wolves — as a fact about the world rather than a fact about your life. They served the Office of Registration, which served the crown, which served, depending whom you asked, order, the gods, or its own appetite. Their work was simple to describe: the gifted were to be registered, bound, and taxed for the use of their gifts, or hunted if they declined. In the capital, they said, registration was nearly routine now. In the valleys, it was a story told to children. *Be dull,* her mother had said, nine years ago, when such stories were still stories. *Dullness is a wall, and walls hold.*

But walls were built for weather, not for wolves.

"Eat your bread," Fenwick said to Wren, "and leave the banner where it lies. Banners come and banners go. The barley still needs counting either way."

"That's very wise," Wren said.

"It's very tired," said Fenwick. "Wisdom is just tiredness that's been promoted."

Rafael laughed — once, at the correct volume — and meant none of it, and loved him for it anyway.

* * *

The afternoon brought the thing she would remember.

It came in innocently, the way trouble always did: as paperwork. A bonded account, flagged for the second stack — a debt of record against one Aldric Vane, carter, of the high roads, secured against goods seized at the tollhouse. Routine. The kind of entry Rafael had copied a hundred times. Attached to it was the evidence of payment: a single coin, sealed into a wax pocket on the bond itself, as the court's practice required for debts under ten crowns. Coin as receipt. Coin as proof.

Coin as memory.

She should have handled it by the paper. She told herself that afterward, many times, in the dark hours when memory keeps its own ledgers. But the wax pocket had cracked in transit, and the coin had shifted, and when she tilted the bond to read the endorsement the coin slid against her bare fingertip — a half-second of contact, less.

Flint on the tongue. Warmth behind the sternum. And then she was *elsewhere*.

A tollhouse at night. Rain on a plank roof. The interior of a smuggler's coat, close and dark, smelling of wet wool and pipe smoke — she was seeing from inside the coin's last holder, a man with big knuckles and a nervous habit of rubbing the coin with his thumb, which was why it remembered so much of him. Around him, voices. A fire. The particular clipped cadence of the grey-cloaked huntresses, and beneath it a voice reading aloud in the flat tone of law:

*"By edict of the crown, in the fourth year of the King's restatement: all persons gifted in soul-arts shall present themselves for registry within ninety days of the posting of this edict in their parish. The registered shall be assessed, bound, and levied according to the schedule of gifts. The unregistered, being taken, forfeit claim of mercy and are remanded to the court for judgment. There is no third path. There is no appeal. Signed under the white banner—"*

The man's thumb rubbed the coin faster. Someone nearby said, quietly, "Ninety days," and someone else said, "They posted it at Millbrook already," and the man thought — and the coin kept the thought the way wax keeps a seal — *Marta has the sight, Marta reads the weather in the sheep, they'll come for Marta, gods, they'll come for my sister's girl* — and then hands moved, and the coin changed pockets, and the memory tore away like a page turning.

Rafael came back to the scriptorium with her pen still moving, which was the only reason she didn't scream. The pen had written, in her careful ugly hand, *Aldric Vane, carter, debt of record, paid* — and stopped there, because her hand had begun to shake on the period.

Around her, forty desks scratched on. Honey light. Brother Odo's fussy margins. The widow Ansel's extra cask of wine. All of it continuing, indifferent, lovely.

*Ninety days.*

She looked at the coin in its cracked wax pocket. A little disc of base silver, worth maybe a copper honestly traded, carrying a smuggler's fear like a passenger. She thought of Marta, whoever Marta was — a girl who read weather in sheep, whose uncle rubbed a coin raw worrying for her. Somewhere out there, right now, a family was doing arithmetic with ninety days.

And somewhere out there, right now, her brother Tomas was driving cart on the high roads. Tomas, who couldn't lie to a customs man without his ears going red. Tomas, who carried goods along routes that existed precisely because the crown's roads had taxes and the mountains' roads had only weather. Tomas, who was not gifted — Rafael was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of your own heartbeat — but who was *associated*, who was *known*, who had hands and a name and a route, and who would stand in front of any danger that approached their door with his stubborn chin up and say something noble and idiotic like *I've done nothing wrong.*

That was the problem with Tomas. He believed the ledger balanced. He believed that debts paid were debts done, that honest men were safe men, that the world was a fair copyist. He had never spent nine years learning what Rafael had learned: that some readers only look for what they want to find, and write whatever suits them.

"Voss." Pellis, again, at her shoulder. She hadn't heard him come. Her stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "You've stopped."

"A difficult entry," she said. Her voice came out level. Dullness, expertly performed. "The endorsement is smudged."

He leaned over her desk, plumb-line straight, and examined the bond. The coin. The crack in the wax. For three seconds that lasted a season, Rafael watched him look at the exact object of her terror.

"Recopy it fresh," he said at last. "And Voss — the registrar wants the bonded accounts finished tonight. All of them. The court's auditors arrive within the fortnight, and everything will be checked twice." He straightened. Something flickered across his dry face that might, in a more expressive man, have been called unease. "Everything," he repeated, and moved on down the aisle, leaving the word hanging over forty desks like weather coming over a ridge.

Rafael recopied the entry. Her hand was steady. She had practiced steadiness for nine years and it held, the way a wall holds — against rain, against wind, against everything except the thing it was actually built for.

* * *

She walked home at dusk through streets going blue with early snowlight. Thornfell in late autumn was a town holding its breath: wood stacked, chimneys smoking, shutters going up, everyone racing the mountain to be ready. She passed the shrine at the crossroads where travelers left tokens, passed the tavern where someone was already singing too loudly about nothing, passed the smithy where the hammer-strokes rang slower now, evening-paced, winding down.

Home was the small house below the terraces, roof newly patched with Tomas's own hands, smoke rising from a chimney that worked because he'd rebuilt it twice. He was in the yard when she arrived, greasing an axle, sleeves rolled despite the cold, whistling. He whistled the same four bars of the same harvest song, always slightly off-key, and Rafael had spent nine years pretending it annoyed her.

"You're home late," he said.

"Pellis wants the bonded accounts by vespers. Court auditors coming." She hung her cloak. Watched him. "Tomas. Riders came over the pass. White banner."

The whistling stopped. He kept his hands on the axle a moment, then wiped them, unhurried, and looked at her with the expression he'd worn since he was twelve and she was nine and she'd broken the good bowl and he'd told their mother *I did it* without hesitation, because that was who Tomas was, a man constitutionally incapable of letting someone else carry weight.

"So I heard," he said. "Down at the yard, everyone's talking."

"Tomas—"

"I've paid my debts, Rafe." He said it lightly, but underneath it was bedrock. "Every toll, every tax, every fine. My book's clean. Honest men don't run from banners."

"Honest men don't get to decide what the banner counts as honest." She heard her own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "There's an edict. About the gifted. Registry or—" She stopped. Some words you didn't say indoors.

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. In the fading light his face did something careful, and Rafael realized — with a small cold shock — that he was choosing what to show her, the same way she chose what to show the world. When had her little brother learned to do that? Or had he always known, and simply never said?

"Ninety days is a long time," he said finally. "Winter's a longer one. Nothing moves over the passes till thaw anyway. We'll talk at solstice, when I'm back from the Stonebridge run." He smiled, and it was their mother's smile, warm and immovable. "Go eat. You get sharp when you're hungry, and you're plenty sharp already."

She wanted to grab him. To shake him. To make him understand that the ledger he trusted was kept by people who forged it. But nine years of dullness held her tongue as well as her face, and all she said was, "Come to solstice, then. Promise me the route, and come home."

"Promised," he said, easy as breathing, and went back to his axle, and began, softly, to whistle.

That night Rafael sat by her window with a candle and did what she always did when the house of her mind got too crowded: she counted the memories that weren't hers, the way a miser counts coins, taking inventory of everything she carried. The miller waving at the empty road. The smuggler's fear for Marta. A thousand small debts of attention, paid out to strangers who would never know.

Then she added a new entry, in the ledger only she kept:

*Riders at the tollhouse. Ninety days. My brother believes in clean books.*

Below it, after a long while, she wrote the thing she had been avoiding since noon, the true balance of the day's account:

*I am not registered. I have never been registered. And the white banner does not care what I believe I am owed.*

Outside, the first flakes of snow came down through the dark, silent and unhurried, beginning the long work of covering the valley — the roads, the roofs, the routes her brother would drive, and everything else that winter would find before spring did.
