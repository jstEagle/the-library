## 2. Snow Before the Bell

The first snow of the year came to Thornfell the way bad news always did: quietly at first, then all at once.

Rafael Voss saw it from the scriptorium window while grinding ink—a single flake drifting past the glass with the unhurried confidence of something that intended to be followed by ten thousand more. She watched it land on the sill, hold its shape for a heartbeat, and dissolve. By the time she had mixed her next batch of sepia, the sill was white and the valley beyond had gone soft-edged and silent, as though someone had laid a clean sheet over all the world's small messes.

"Snow," said Brother Aldous, without looking up from his column of tithes. He was eighty-one and had been announcing weather to the scriptorium for forty years, as if the rest of them might otherwise mistake it for something else. "Early this year."

"Early," Rafael agreed.

"Snow means roads close. Roads closing means carts stop. Carts stopping means—" he tapped his stylus against the ledger, "—tithes run short by Thawfeast, and Prior Maren writes me a letter about it in that hand of his, like a spider drowned in ink."

"You could write back less often," Rafael suggested. "Then he'd write you less."

"Aldous does not negotiate with spiders." He said it with such dignity that she had to turn back to her window to hide her smile.

But the smile didn't last, because she was already doing the arithmetic she'd been dreading since autumn: snow closed the high passes, yes—but it did not close them all at once. There were three weeks, perhaps four, when the low road through the valley stayed open and the smuggler cuts above it became the only way through. Three weeks when the crown's men, if they came, would come easily. And everyone in Thornfell knew they were coming. The edict had arrived a month ago, read aloud from the chapel steps in the prior's careful voice: *all persons gifted with the ember-sense shall present themselves for registration, or be taken.*

Rafael had copied that edict herself, twice—once into the temple record and once, in a moment of weakness she still hadn't confessed even to her own conscience, into her private book of things worth remembering. Her hand had not shaken either time. That was the strange part. Nine years of hiding what she was had taught her hands to be steady at exactly the moments they most wanted to tremble.

She looked down at the ledger open before her. Tithes of barley, tithes of wool, a tithe of candles from the chandler on Mill Street who always shorted the count by two and always would until the sun burned out. Ordinary wax, ordinary paper. She let her bare finger rest on the chandler's seal out of habit rather than intention—the little discipline she allowed herself, one touch a day, like a woman scratching an itch she couldn't admit to—and got nothing but the usual faint warmth of a man counting coins and feeling mildly guilty about the candles. Recent memory. Thin gruel. She withdrew her hand before it could deepen.

*Registration,* she thought. *Or be taken.*

And somewhere down in the valley, her brother was loading a cart.

* * *

Tomas Voss kept his mule and his cart at the yard behind the Drowned Bell, which was not a bell and had never drowned, but had once belonged to a man named Bell who had drowned, and Thornfell being Thornfell, the name stuck the way burrs stick to wool. Rafael found him there in the blue hour before full dark, checking the cart's iron rims by lantern light, his breath steaming, his big hands moving with the unhurried competence that made people trust him with their goods and their secrets in equal measure.

He heard her boots on the frozen mud and didn't turn around. "You walk heavy for a scribe."

"You hear well for a carter."

"I hear everything. It's the gift I got instead of whatever you got." He turned then, and grinned at her—that wide, easy grin that had gotten him forgiven for half the mischief of his childhood and most of the debts of his youth. Snow was settling on his shoulders and he hadn't noticed or didn't care. "Come to see the famous cart? New rim on the left wheel. Cost me a week's hauling."

"It's a very fine rim." She stopped a few feet away, suddenly unsure how to begin, which was absurd—he was her brother; she'd known him since before she knew anything. But some conversations you rehearse your whole life without knowing it, and then when they arrive you find you've rehearsed the wrong words entirely.

Tomas studied her face, and the grin faded by degrees, the way lamplight fades when the oil runs low. "Ah," he said. "It's that kind of visit."

"Tomas—"

"The snow came early. You counted the days on your fingers up there in your tower, and you did sums, and now you've walked all the way down here in the cold to tell me the answer." He set down his hammer. "So tell me the answer, Rafe."

"Go up the mountain," she said. "Tonight. Take the cart, take Bess, take the Cut above Coldwater—it'll hold a cart for another fortnight yet, and after that nobody's going anywhere anyway. Stay with the Ferrows at the high pasture. They owe you from the lambing. Vanish until thaw."

"And do what?" He spread his hands. "Sit in a hut eating Ferrow cheese and telling Ferrow jokes until spring?"

"Yes," Rafael said. "Exactly that. That is precisely my proposal."

"Rafe." He said her name gently, the way you'd set down something breakable. "I can't."

"You can. It's not complicated. You put one foot above the other and point them uphill."

"It's not a question of legs." He crouched to check the axle, buying himself a moment, and she recognized the gesture because it was hers too—the small useful task that lets you avoid looking at someone. "My debts are paid," he said to the axle. "Every one of them. You know how long it took. Six years, Rafe. Six years of hauling other people's freight over ground that wants to kill you, and I'm finally square. Nobody in this valley has a claim on me. Not the toll-gangs, not the chandler, not the temple, not the crown." He stood up. "I'm done running from things I don't owe."

"You're not running from a debt. You're running from a banner."

"Same thing, the way you tell it." He picked up the lantern and held it higher, and the light caught them both, and she saw that he wasn't as calm as his voice. His thumb was working at the callus on his palm, round and round, the way it had since he was nine years old and their mother was dying and nobody would say out loud what the debt-collectors had come about. "Tell me true, now. Have you seen something?"

And there it was—the question under the question. Because Tomas knew. Not everything, never everything, but enough. He'd known since they were children that his sister's hands knew things hands shouldn't know; he'd covered for her a dozen times before either of them had words for it, laughing loudly at the right moments, pulling her away from objects she'd gone too quiet around. He had never once asked her to explain it. He had only ever asked her to be careful.

So she told him the truth, because lying to Tomas was like lying to a wall.

"I touched a coin last month," she said. "A smuggler's coin, old, worn nearly blank. And there was a man in it—a memory of a man in a tavern in Lowmark, saying the witch-hunters have left the capital. White banners. A hundred strong, maybe more. And the edict's changed, Tomas. It's not register-or-fine anymore. The new writ says *taken*. Taken and assessed. And assessment isn't a priest asking questions." She swallowed. "Assessment is wax and iron. I've read what the court uses. Everyone they assess comes out of it smaller than they went in."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the lantern's tin roof. Somewhere across the yard, the Drowned Bell's dog began to bark at nothing, thought better of it, and stopped.

"They're coming for the gifted," he said at last. "Not for carters."

"They're coming for anyone whose paperwork looks wrong. And yours looks wrong, Tomas. A carter with no debts, no family registered in the parish rolls since Mother died, who hauls freight on roads that don't officially exist—you think a huntress with a ledger won't look twice at that? You think they need a reason? The edict doesn't ask for reasons anymore. It asks for names, and it fills in the rest itself."

"That's not law," he said. "That's just—"

"I know what it is."

They looked at each other across the lantern light, and Rafael felt the old fracture line—the one that had been forming between them for years, invisible, load-bearing—shift for the first time under actual weight. It frightened her more than the banners did. She could hide from witch-hunters. She could not hide from losing him.

"Come up the mountain with me, then," Tomas said suddenly. "Both of us. You first, actually—you should go tonight, I mean it, Rafe, don't give me that look, you're the one with the—"

"Don't say it out loud." Her voice came out sharper than she meant. "Not in this yard. Not anywhere."

"See, that." He gestured at her with the whole of one broad hand, helpless. "That's what I mean. You've spent nine years folded up small enough to fit inside a scriptorium, and it's making you see hunters behind every snowflake. Maybe they'll come through and find nothing worth taking. Maybe they'll read the rolls and move on. The valley's paid its tithes. We're not trouble, Thornfell. We've never been trouble."

"We're *quiet*, Tomas. That's different. Quiet lasts exactly as long as nobody looks."

"And when they look," he said, "they'll find a carter who owes nothing and a scribe who copies prettier than anyone in the province. What are they going to do, arrest us for being respectable?"

"Yes," Rafael said. "Yes, absolutely, that is literally the thing they do."

He laughed—she had to give him that, he laughed properly, head tipped back, breath steaming—and for a moment they were children again in the kitchen of the house that wasn't theirs anymore, laughing at some joke that was really just fear wearing a costume. Then the laugh ran out, and the cold came back into the space it left.

"Rafe." He stepped closer and took her hands, and his were warm and cracked and steady. "I love you. You know that. I'd walk into the Undercourt itself if you asked me to. But I will not run. Not because I'm brave—I'm not, you've seen me around bees—but because a man who's spent six years paying what he owed doesn't get to act like he still owes the world his absence. If they come, I'll stand in my own doorway and answer their questions, because I have honest answers. And when they're done finding nothing, I'll haul my cart, and in spring you'll laugh at me for worrying."

"And if they don't find nothing?"

"Then I'll be very polite," he said, "and extremely boring. I've been practicing boring my whole life. I'm very good at it."

"You're the least boring person in this valley."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment."

"I know." He squeezed her hands. "Go home, Rafe. It's snowing, and you've got ink to grind tomorrow, and Brother Aldous needs someone to argue with. I'll think about the mountain. I promise I'll think about it."

Which, from Tomas, meant he had already decided, and was giving her the courtesy of pretending otherwise. She knew him. She hated knowing him sometimes; it removed every comfortable illusion, including the useful one that she could win.

She pulled her hands free and did the last thing she had left. "Then take this." She worked the ring off her own finger—the plain pewter band she'd worn since she was sixteen, unremarkable, worthless, chosen precisely because it was both. "Wear it. Don't ask why."

Tomas looked at the ring, and then at her, and whatever lived behind his eyes did its arithmetic quickly, because he was not stupid, he had only ever been stubborn. He slid the ring onto his smallest finger, where it sat like a child's ring, and he didn't ask why, and that was its own kind of vow.

"If a huntress takes my hand," he said, "she'll feel a dead woman's wedding band and a lot of rope burns. Nothing else."

"Nothing else," Rafael agreed. "Because you're boring."

"Extremely." He smiled, and it almost reached his eyes. "Go on. Before the snow gets serious."

She went. At the edge of the yard she stopped and looked back once. He had taken up his hammer again, and the lantern threw his shadow huge against the stable wall, and he was working the iron rim the way he did everything—steadily, honestly, as if the world were a place where steadiness and honesty were the same as safe.

The snow was getting serious. It came down now in earnest, thick and silent, erasing her footprints behind her almost as fast as she made them, so that walking home felt like being unwritten.

* * *

The Emberhold's evening bell caught her halfway up the temple hill, tolling slow and bronze through the falling white. Below her, Thornfell lit its windows one by one, gold squares scattered across the darkening valley, each one somebody's supper, somebody's argument, somebody's ordinary life. Somewhere down there, a hundred armed strangers were making camp at the mouth of the pass—or would be by tomorrow, or the day after—under a banner white as the snow that was covering every road, every track, every escape.

Rafael stood in the bell-toll and the rising snow and admitted to herself what she had been refusing to admit since the coin: Tomas would not run. She had known it before she asked. She had asked anyway, because love makes you ask things you know the answers to, the way you knock on a door you can see is shut.

Which meant there was only one thing left to do, and it was not the thing she'd been trained for. Nine years of copying other people's accounts had taught her every rule of the ledger except the one she needed now. So she would have to learn a different trade, quickly, in the dark, alone: the reading of contracts. Of seals and signatures and smudged names. Of one contract in particular, older than the edict, older than her grief, signed in a hand she had spent her whole childhood trying to remember—

Their mother's hand.

Rafael turned up the hill toward the archive, into the wind, and the snow followed her all the way to the door.
