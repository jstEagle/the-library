## 3. What the Wax Remembers

The Emberhold's scriptorium smelled of tallow and cold stone and, faintly, of the honey cakes Brother Aldous smuggled in his sleeve like a man committing a crime he'd already confessed to twice.

Rafael Voss had spent nine years learning to love that smell. Nine years of copying tithes and grain tallies and the small, dull debts of a small, dull valley — and she had come, against all her younger self's expectations, to find a kind of peace in it. There was safety in tedium. Tedium asked nothing of her gift. A ledger of barley did not remember anything, and if it had, barley memories would surely be gentle: sun, rain, the patient arithmetic of roots.

She dipped her pen. She wrote *fourteen measures of winter wheat, received in good order* in the round, obedient hand the temple demanded, and she did not think about the edict.

She was thinking about it constantly, of course. It sat behind her ribs like a swallowed coal. *All persons of gifted blood to present themselves for registration within sixty days.* Sixty days, and then what? She knew what the registration rolls were for. Everyone knew. The rolls went south to the capital, and the capital sent back white banners.

"Your ink is going to pool," Brother Aldous said mildly, appearing at her elbow with the particular silence of a fat man who has spent thirty years moving among sleeping novices. "Page forty-one will be ruined. You'll have to copy it again. Tragedy."

"I'm fine," Rafael said.

"You're chewing your pen like it owes you money." He set a honey cake on the corner of her desk with the discretion of a man bribing a witness. "Eat. Frowning gives you wrinkles, and you're far too young to look as worried as you do."

"Aldous—"

"Hm?"

"Has anyone ever come here to register?" she asked. "With the new edict. Anyone from the valley."

He was quiet a moment, which was unlike him. Aldous filled silences the way other men filled cellars — with anything at hand.

"One family," he said finally. "Up past the mill. They walked to Thornfell themselves, before the snow. Polite people. The clerks gave them a receipt." He said the word *receipt* the way one might say *shroud*. "I don't know where they are now. I expect someone does. I expect it's written down somewhere."

*Somewhere.* Everything was written down somewhere. That was the whole architecture of Rafael's life: the temple kept its ledgers, the crown kept its lists, and between them, a person could be reduced to entries in other people's books. She had spent nine years making sure her own name appeared in none of them but the payroll.

She ate the honey cake. She copied page forty-one again. Outside the scriptorium's high windows, the first snow of the year was still falling, soft and indifferent, and somewhere under it Tomas was either hiding or being an idiot, and there was nothing she could do about either until the bell rang vespers.

* * *

It was the seals that got her, in the end. It was always the seals.

Every document that passed through the Emberhold carried wax — red for the temple, black for the crown, and once in a long while, a deep amber-red that Rafael had learned to dread without ever understanding why. Old wax held memory the way old wood held smoke. When her bare fingers pressed into it, when she forgot herself for half a second, the gift flared up through her skin like water finding a crack in a wall, and she would see — briefly, uselessly, unbearably — whatever the seal had last been pressed against with feeling.

A tithe seal might show her a farmer's relief or resentment. A tax seal might show her a clerk's boredom. But the amber ones, the old ones, the ones pressed by hands that had been afraid —

Those showed her other things.

She had learned to wear her gloves. She had learned to work the wax with a stylus, tilting documents without touching them, handling the temple's paper the way a midwife handles a stranger's child: competently, warmly, and never skin to skin. In nine years she had slipped exactly four times, and each time she had told herself *never again* with the sincere conviction of a woman who absolutely would do it again, because the gift was not a door she could lock. It was a draft under every door in the house.

On the evening after the snow began, near dark, a courier box arrived from the archive below the chapel — old contracts, recalled for recopying, because the new administration wanted everything entered in the modern hand. Brother Aldous sighed over the box like a man asked to translate a dead language for a living fool.

"Fifty years of paperwork," he grumbled, setting it on her desk because her desk was nearest and because punishment, in his theology, always fell on the competent. "Half of these are duplicates. Half the rest are for debts nobody remembers owing. You'll sort them, won't you? You have the patience of a saint and the handwriting of one too."

"And you have the work ethic of a cat," Rafael said.

"A well-fed cat," he agreed, and shuffled off to vespers, leaving her alone with fifty years of other people's obligations and a lamp burning low.

She sorted. Duplicates to the left, dead debts to the middle, live ones to the right. Her gloves stayed on. The snow ticked softly against the window. The scriptorium settled into its evening creaks, the building breathing around her like an old animal, and she was three-quarters through the box and thinking of nothing at all — which is how it always happened — when she came to a contract folded twice and sealed in amber wax.

Not temple red. Not crown black. Amber, dark as resin, dark as the light they said burned beneath Thornfell mountain, and something in her chest leaned toward it before her mind caught up.

She should have used the stylus.

She told herself she would use the stylus. She even picked it up. And then — because the contract was oddly heavy, because the wax had a strange depth to it, because nine years of discipline is a long time and a tired woman is a short distance from curiosity — she turned the fold back with her thumb, bare, gloved hand forgotten on the desk beside the lamp.

Her thumb sank into the wax.

The world went amber.

* * *

Memory is not like seeing. Rafael had tried to explain this to no one, ever, because explaining it would require admitting it existed. Memory, when it came through the gift, was like standing in a room where something important had happened — you saw the edges of it, felt the temperature of it, heard the parts that had been loud. The rest was shadow, and the shadows moved.

The room she stood in now was cold. A cellar, or a vaulted office built to feel like one. Stone walls. A table. A candle burned down to a stub, and two hands on the table across from each other, and between them a document — this document, she realized, this exact fold of paper, older than her whole life.

One of the pairs of hands was her mother's.

She knew them instantly, the way you know a voice in the dark. Broad-knuckled, ink-stained at the second finger, a faded burn scar across the left thumb from the bread oven. Hands that had braided her hair badly and carved her a wooden fox and held Tomas through the fever winter. Hands she had last seen folded on a bier eleven years ago, and had thought about every day since with an ache that had worn smooth, like a river stone.

In the memory, those hands were shaking.

The other pair of hands was still. Too still. They belonged to a figure whose face the memory refused to give her — the shadows above the table simply would not resolve, the way faces in dreams turn away — but she felt the weight of him anyway, a gravity, a patience like deep water. He wore rings. When he slid the document across the table, the rings clicked against the wood, and the sound went through Rafael like a needle.

Her mother read. Rafael felt her reading — felt the slow dread moving up through her mother's body the way cold moves up through boot soles. She heard her mother's breath catch at one line, and could not see which line it was, and hated the shadow for it.

Then her mother spoke, and the voice was the voice from Rafael's earliest years, the voice that sang off-key on purpose to make the children laugh.

"It says one soul," her mother said. "Whose name goes in the line?"

"The debtor's," said the still man, and his voice was pleasant, and pleasantness from that throat was worse than any snarl. "Or the debtor's kin, should the debtor predecease the term. The court is flexible. The court is always flexible. That is rather our reputation."

"My children are not part of this."

"Madam, your children are part of everything. That is what children *are*." A pause, and the rings clicked again. "But the line takes one name. One soul, one bond. Choose whose."

Silence. In the silence Rafael felt her mother's heart doing something terrible and steady, the way a heart does when it has already decided and is only catching up to itself. She wanted to shout into the memory — *don't sign, don't sign, walk out, we'd have been poor, we'd have been poor and together* — but the gift only let her stand in the room; it had never once let her touch anything in it.

Her mother took the pen.

And here the memory did something it had never done before. Every memory Rafael had ever read ended at its edges, frayed into shadow. This one narrowed instead — funneled, like a eye closing — down onto the signature line, down onto the descending stroke of a pen, and Rafael understood with a lurch that the wax remembered this moment most of all, that the seal had been pressed while her mother's hand was still trembling from writing, and the wax had drunk the trembling straight down into itself and kept it, fifty years, like a fly in amber.

The pen wrote a name.

The memory blurred at precisely the wrong instant — her mother's tears, perhaps, or the candle guttering, or the wax's own miserly hoarding of what it had seen — and the name smeared. Two letters legible, maybe three. A shape like a *V*, and then smudge, and then nothing.

The seal came down. The amber closed over everything. And the memory spat Rafael out into her own body, into the scriptorium, into the lamplit ordinary night, with her heart slamming and her bare thumb still resting in the cool wax as though it had never been anywhere at all.

* * *

She sat very still for a long time.

The lamp hissed. The snow ticked. Somewhere below, the chapel bell rang the quarter hour, indifferent as weather.

*One soul,* the contract had said. *One soul sold to the Undercourt, term of fifty years.*

Fifty years. Her mother had signed — when? The wax was old, the paper yellowed brown along its folds. Before Rafael was born. Perhaps before Tomas. Perhaps her mother had gone down into Thornfell with a debt on her back and a belly full of one of them, and had climbed back out believing it finished, paid, done — because that was what people believed about contracts. You signed, you served, you survived, and the paper went into a box.

But the still man's voice was still in her ears. *The debtor's kin, should the debtor predecease the term.*

Her mother had died eleven years ago. Eleven years into a fifty-year term.

Rafael looked down at the contract in her hands as though seeing it for the first time, which was true. She unfolded it fully, carefully, with the stylus this time, and read it the ordinary way, with eyes, the way honest people read. Most of it was formula — the court's clauses coiled around each other like snakes in a sack — but the relevant lines were plain enough for a scribe to parse:

*...one (1) soul, bonded in perpetuity of term, being fifty (50) years from date of sealing, transferable upon default, death, or failure of payment, unto the debtor's lawful issue, jointly and severally...*

Jointly and severally. Jointly — both of them. Severally — either of them. Both, or whichever the court chose to take.

The name line was smudged. Of course it was. But Rafael had spent nine years reading other people's handwriting, including the handwriting of drunks, dying men, and priests in a hurry, and she knew a *V* when the wax coughed one up. Voss. Her mother had signed her own name — or had meant to — and the court had let the rest blur, because a smudged name is a flexible name, and flexibility, the pleasant man had said, was rather their reputation.

They hadn't taken her mother's soul when she died. Rafael had stood at the graveside; she would have known; the ground would have felt different; something would have shown on her mother's face at the end, some terror, some accounting. There had been nothing. Only tiredness, and a strange gentleness in the last month, and once — Rafael remembered it now with a chill climbing her spine — once, near the end, her mother had gripped Rafael's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *Whatever they tell you we owed, we paid. Remember that. We paid.*

We paid. Not *I* paid.

Her mother had known. Had known the debt didn't die with her, had known it rolled downhill to her children like a barrel, and had spent her last strength trying to make Rafael believe otherwise — not to deceive her, Rafael realized, feeling sick, but to arm her. Because a lie you believe is armor. Because if Rafael truly believed the debt was paid, she would never go looking, never go asking, never go down under Thornfell with her name in her mouth.

*We paid. Remember that.*

"Oh, Mama," Rafael said aloud, to the empty scriptorium, and her voice cracked in the middle like a girl's.

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick with hands that were almost steady.

Here was the shape of it, plain and terrible: her mother had sold one soul to pay a debt of her own — Rafael would probably never learn what that original debt was; the court's appetite for secrets was the one appetite it never disguised — and the court had accepted, and waited, and counted, and when her mother died with thirty-nine years still on the term, the bond had done what bonds do. It had transferred. Jointly and severally. To her lawful issue.

To Rafael. To Tomas.

Tomas. Who was at this moment sitting in a cell in Thornfell under a white banner, charged with being *gifted*, of all the idiotic charges, when the truth was so much worse and so much simpler: he wasn't seized for what he was. He was seized for what he was *owed*. The witch-hunters hadn't found an ember on him because there wasn't one. There was a lien. Somewhere in the boxes below the chapel, in the archive below the chapel, in the court beneath the mountain, there was a ledger with the Voss name in it, and the white banner had come to collect.

And the temple — the temple that had employed her nine years, fed her, housed her, called her *daughter* in the casual way institutions call people things — the temple had handed her the proof herself, in a courier box, for recopying. Fifty years of paperwork. Half of it duplicates. Half the rest, debts nobody remembered owing.

Nobody remembered. That was the whole trick, wasn't it. Debts survived by not being remembered. Contracts slept in boxes for a generation, gathering wax and patience, and woke up hungry.

Rafael folded the contract along its old creases, precisely, the way her mother had taught her to fold letters so they traveled well. She did not put it back in the box. She was not entirely sure why not — only that her hands refused, the way a dog refuses to drop a bone it has finally caught. She slid it inside her tunic, against her ribs, where the swallowed coal of the edict already lived, and now the two of them lay together, edict and contract, kindling and spark.

Outside, the snow kept falling on Thornfell, filling the passes, filling the roads, filling the world with the soft white blankness that made everything seem forgiven. Down in the valley, in a cell that smelled of straw and iron, Tomas was waiting for a trial he couldn't lose and a sentence he couldn't understand.

And somewhere beneath all that snow, beneath the shrine-stones and the smuggler paths and the roots of the mountain itself, something amber-lit and patient had opened a ledger, licked its thumb, and turned to a page with a smudged name on it.

Rafael put out the lamp, pulled on her coat, and went to find out how one got into the archive below the chapel — and whether a sister was allowed to read what the dead had signed.

She was almost to the stair when she heard, far below the floor, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the sound of a bell ringing in a place where there was no bell.
