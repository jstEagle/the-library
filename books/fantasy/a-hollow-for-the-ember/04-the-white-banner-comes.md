## 4. The White Banner Comes

Sera Ilvane had read the Rites of Seizure so many times she could recite them in her sleep, and once—embarrassingly—had. Her tent-mate at the garrison swore Sera had sat bolt upright at midnight and intoned, *Let the named soul stand forth and be counted*, before lying back down and snoring like a landslide.

So it was with the particular weariness of a woman performing her own nightmare that she stood in the cart-yard of the Thornfell waystation, snow gathering on the shoulders of her white cloak, and began again.

"Tomas Voss," she said. "Carter. Of Thornfell parish. You are named in a writ of seizure under the Edict of Registration, issued by the Crown in its wisdom and sealed beneath the hand of the Lord Auditor of Debts. You are required to come with us. You are not required to confess. You are not permitted to flee. Do you understand these rights as they have been read to you?"

The man in front of her was broad as a barn door, with flour still dusting one sleeve—he'd been interrupted mid-errand, a sack of it abandoned by his wagon wheel—and he looked at her with an expression she could only describe as politely baffled.

"I understand you've got the wrong Voss," he said.

Sera had heard this before. It was, statistically, the most common response to a reading of rights, followed closely by running, weeping, and—in one memorable case in Dunmark—a goat being released into the ranks of the hunters with its horns wrapped in burning straw. She preferred the bafflement. Baffled men did not run, and goats were nobody's friend in winter.

"The writ names Tomas Voss," she said. "Son of Maren Voss, deceased."

Something moved behind his eyes at the name—not fear, exactly. Grief's older cousin. "That's me," he admitted. "But you've made a mistake all the same. I'm not gifted. Never been gifted. I can't even find my own keys half the time, and I've touched every object in that house."

"That," said Captain Drev Halloway from behind her, in the tone of a man explaining arithmetic to a child, "is not for you to determine."

Sera kept her face still. She had already done what procedure required: she had taken the man's hands in hers, palm to palm, the way the Rites prescribed, and she had felt—

Nothing.

That was the trouble. Not the absence of an ember, which would have been ordinary, unremarkable, the condition of nine souls in ten. She had felt the absence of *anything*. No ember, no residue, no scorch-mark of a gift burned out or buried. Tomas Voss's soul, as far as Sera Ilvane could read it—and she had been reading souls for eleven years, since she was nineteen and too earnest to know better—was as blank and honest as fresh paper.

She had said as much to Captain Halloway that morning, in the privacy of the command tent, while the writ lay open between them like a dead bird.

"He's clean," she'd said. "I'd stake my warrant on it. There's no ember in him at all. Whoever drew this writ didn't test him—they couldn't have. A testing would have shown nothing."

Halloway hadn't even looked up from his breakfast. He was a large, deliberate man who ate porridge the way other men conducted sieges. "The writ is sealed," he said. "Sealed by the Undercourt's own office. You know how it works, Ilvane. We don't judge the debt. We collect it."

"We're not collecting a debt," Sera said. "We're seizing a person. There's a difference."

"There's a form for both." He tapped the writ. "This is the second one."

* * *

The snow came down soft and thick over Thornfell, the kind of snow that made the whole valley feel muffled, apologetic, as if the sky were trying to hush itself. The white banner hung from the waystation's post, beaded with meltwater. Somewhere a dog was barking at it with great conviction and no success.

Tomas Voss had not run. That was the thing Sera kept turning over as her squad bound his wrists—not cruelly; she'd insisted on the soft cords, the ones used for witnesses rather than criminals, and Halloway had allowed it with the small shrug of a man conserving his objections for things that mattered. The carter could have run. The yard backed onto the smuggler roads—every child in these valleys knew where they went, even if no adult admitted it—and there had been a full minute, maybe more, when nothing stood between Tomas Voss and the treeline but Sera herself.

He'd stayed. He'd even helped, in a doomed, decent sort of way, holding out his own wrists and saying, "Left one's bad, mind the old break," as if he were instructing a nurse rather than submitting to arrest.

"Why?" she asked him now, quietly, while the others loaded the wagon. "You could have gone. Anyone with sense would have gone."

"Debts don't vanish because you do," he said. He nodded at the wagon, at the sack of flour someone had thoughtlessly left sitting in the slush. "If I run, they take the wagon. If they take the wagon, Widow Corren doesn't get her flour, and she's got two little ones and a bad winter coming. And then I owe *her*." He shrugged, enormous and mild. "I'd rather owe the crown. At least the crown only wants one thing off me, and I haven't got it."

"You haven't," Sera said. It came out flatter than she intended.

"No," he agreed, and looked at her—really looked, the way people rarely looked at a huntress, past the white cloak to whatever was underneath it. "You tested me. Back at the yard. Before your captain came out." His voice dropped. "You felt it, didn't you. That there's nothing to feel."

Sera said nothing. Procedure said nothing. Procedure, in fact, said quite specifically that a huntress did not discuss the results of a testing with the tested, and Sera had recited procedure so long it lived in her spine like a second set of bones.

But Tomas Voss smiled at her silence, small and sad, and said, "My sister could tell you. If you ever meet her. Rafael. She notices things." Then the guards walked him to the wagon, and the conversation was over, and Sera stood in the falling snow feeling as though she'd been handed something fragile and told not to look at it.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite shoulder called, with the poetry typical of surveyors, Rock. The prisoners' wagon was chained, the fire was lit, and Sera took first watch because taking first watch meant not eating with the squad, and not eating with the squad meant not listening to Brannoc explain, for the fourth time this campaign, why he was certain the gifted could smell a hunter's intentions through leather.

The truth—which Sera had never put into any report—was that most gifted folk could smell nothing of the kind. Most gifted folk were like Tomas Voss: tired, indebted, and carrying gifts so small the crown's own edict should never have troubled them. A woman in Dunmark who knew when bread was going to burn. A boy whose touch could tell true copper from false. An old ferryman who always, *always* knew which passenger wouldn't pay. The edict had been sold to the garrisons as a register of dangerous souls. What it had become, writ by writ, was a harvest of inconveniences.

And now a carter with no ember at all.

She heard Halloway's boots before she saw him. The captain had a walk like a filing cabinet: orderly, heavy, full of things in their places. He settled beside her on the watch-stone and offered her his flask, which she declined, which he expected, which was somehow part of the ritual.

"You tested him twice," Halloway said.

"Once."

"Once before the yard, once after. I count two." He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the fire, at the sparks climbing toward a sky too thick with snow to receive them. "You want to say something, Ilvane. You've been wanting to say it since morning. Say it."

Sera turned her warrant over in her mind—the stiff paper, the seal, the eleven years of her life folded inside it. Then she said it.

"The writ is wrong. He has no ember. No gift, no residue, nothing. A testing would have cleared him in a quarter hour. Either the court never tested him, or—" she stopped, because the next word was *forged*, and *forged* was a word that ended careers, usually the speaker's.

"Or," Halloway said comfortably, "the paperwork is sufficient."

"The paperwork says a man owes a soul-debt. The man doesn't owe a soul-debt. Which one of those is justice?"

Halloway took a long pull from his flask, unhurried, a man who had been asked versions of this question by younger versions of Sera and had outlived all of them. "Justice," he said, "is what the ledger says it is. That's the whole point of ledgers, girl. If every collector got to decide which debts were real, we'd be back to the old days—lords forgiving their friends and ruinous to everyone else. The record is the mercy. The record is the *only* mercy. It treats a miller's son the same as a duke's."

"And if the record lies?"

"Then the record lies for everyone equally." He said it without malice, without irony, with the terrible serenity of a man who had found a shape for the world and refused to notice it didn't fit. "Go to sleep, Ilvane. Tomorrow we cross the pass, and the Undercourt's auditors will do the weighing. That's above our cloth."

He rose and crunched away through the snow, and Sera sat on the cold stone and watched the fire and understood, with a clarity that felt less like a thought than like a hairline crack across a window, that she had just heard the law explain itself honestly for the first time in her career.

*The record is the only mercy.*

Then what was she? A woman with a warrant and a white cloak, delivering an innocent man to a cavern full of accountants, because a piece of paper said so. Eleven years she had believed she was the sharp edge of justice. Tonight, on a rock called Rock, in snow that fell without malice on the guilty and innocent alike, she considered the possibility that she was something else entirely: a courier. A very well-armed courier for a ledger that might be lying.

In the wagon, under canvas, Tomas Voss slept the sleep of a man with a clean conscience, which was either proof of his innocence or proof of how little consciences mattered here. Sera checked the chains anyway—procedure—and found herself lingering.

"Your sister," she said quietly, to the dark canvas. "Rafael. Does she know?"

No answer. Just the wind, and the creak of the wagon, and far below—so far below she told herself she imagined it, though she would remember it later, precisely, the way she remembered everything she failed to act upon—a sound like a bell ringing in a place where there was no bell.

She wrote the day's log by lantern light. *Prisoner secured. Conduct compliant. Testing results:* and there her pen stopped, hovering, for a long moment, over the space where the truth was supposed to go.

She wrote *inconclusive*.

It was the first lie she had ever entered in a hunter's ledger, and it was, she would think much later, the smallest possible crack—the kind you don't hear, the kind that only shows when the whole pane comes down.
