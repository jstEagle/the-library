## 5. Ash on the Register

The Emberhold smelled of cold stone and hot wax, and on the morning Rafael Voss went to beg for her brother's life, it smelled of both at once—incense from the dawn service drifting down the stairwells to mingle with the tallow vats in the sealing room below. Nine years she had walked these corridors without anyone looking twice at her. A scribe was furniture that could write. She had counted on that.

Today she wanted very much to be seen.

She had dressed for it, which was its own small comedy. Her good wool dress, the grey one with the collar that didn't itch. Her hair pinned back in the severe coil the temple preferred on women who were meant to be invisible. She had even polished her copy-stamp, the little brass wheel every scribe carried, until it shone like a coin worth having. Somewhere beneath her ribs, the ember-gift sat quiet and coiled, the way it always did when she was afraid—as if it, too, understood that today it must stay hidden.

Precentor Halvard received petitioners in the small audience chamber off the scriptorium gallery, a room designed, Rafael had always thought, to make supplicants feel like misfiled documents. There was one chair for the petitioner and one high desk for the precentor, and the light came down through a slot of window so narrow it might have been a ledger line itself.

Halvard was old, dry as August paper, with ink stains on three fingers and a voice like a drawer closing. He had taught Rafael her letterforms when she first came to the temple, orphaned and hungry and too clever by half. He had never once asked about her family. She had always assumed this was kindness.

"Voss." He did not look up from the document he was annotating. "You have an hour's leave from your slots. I trust it is for cause."

"My brother has been taken, Precentor. By the crown's hunters. Under the white banner."

That got his eyes up. Not with alarm—with assessment. The look of a man checking whether a page was worth binding.

"Tomas Voss," Rafael said. "A carter. He runs freight on the valley roads, he pays his tithes, he has never—" She stopped herself before she said *he has no gift*, because saying it aloud felt like confessing something else. "He has done nothing. They took him from his own yard in front of his horses."

"The edict permits seizure of the gifted pending registration or trial." Halvard said it the way he read out corrections: flatly, without appetite, without apology.

"He isn't gifted."

"Then the process will find him innocent." He returned to his annotations.

Rafael had rehearsed this conversation on the walk over. She had rehearsed it in the bath, at supper, lying awake listening to the snow creak against the shutters. In none of her rehearsals had Halvard been so quickly finished.

"Precentor." She made her voice steady. "I am asking the Emberhold to intercede. To send an advocate to the assize. To do what temples are for."

Something flickered across the old man's face—not anger, not contempt. Something wearier than either.

"You have copied ledgers here nine years, Voss. Tell me what the Emberhold protects."

"Its ledgers," she said. The answer came out of her before she could soften it, and she watched him hear it.

"Yes." He set down his pen at last and folded his hands, and for a moment he looked less like a drawer closing and more like a man standing in one. "The temple holds the tithe-records of four hundred households, the debt-registers of eleven guilds, the marriage lines and death lines of everyone born under Thornfell's shadow. When a harvest fails, it is our records that decide who eats. When a merchant dies, it is our seals that decide what his widow keeps. Do you know what happens, Voss, if the crown decides the Emberhold shelters gifted persons? Those records become evidence. Every household we have ever weighed becomes suspect. Four hundred families burn so that one carter might go free."

"One carter," Rafael said, "who is innocent."

"Innocence is a finding, not a fact. It requires a court. Courts require the crown's goodwill, and the crown's goodwill requires that we be exactly what we appear to be: a building full of paper that belongs to no one." He picked his pen back up. "I am sorry for your brother. Genuinely. But the Emberhold protects its ledgers, not its scribes. That is not cruelty, child. It is arithmetic."

*Arithmetic.* Rafael stood there a moment longer than dignity required, because some stubborn part of her believed that if she simply refused to leave, the world would have to make room. It was a belief she'd inherited from Tomas, who once stood in front of a tax-assessor's cart for half a day because the man had shorted their mother's estate by two crowns. Tomas had gotten the two crowns. Tomas had also gotten himself noticed, and being noticed, these days, was how men ended up in white-banner convoys.

"I'll file my petition formally, then," she said. "So it's in the record."

"Do." For the first time, Halvard looked genuinely at her, and there was something in his face she would turn over for days afterward—something that might have been regret, or might have been warning. "And Voss. Whatever you decide to do next—and I am an old man, I can see you deciding something—do it quietly. The temple cannot protect you. That is not the same as wishing you harm."

She bowed the way scribes bow, shallow and precise, and left him to his paper.

* * *

The register office occupied the ground floor of the west range, a long vaulted room where the temple kept its rolls of persons: births, deaths, employments, obligations. Every soul under the Emberhold's administration had a copy-slot—a pigeonhole of pale cedar bearing a lead seal with their name struck into it, holding the slips that summed them up. Rafael's own slot was number 311, third row, at eye height. She had dusted it faithfully for nine years. She knew the exact weight of the slips inside it: her indenture papers, her tithe waivers, the note excusing her from winter services the year her lungs went bad, a single commendation from Precentor Halvard dated six years back, for accuracy beyond requirement.

Accuracy beyond requirement. That was what they'd write on her, in the end. Not *she read the memories sealed in wax*. Not *she hid in plain sight for nearly a decade while the world decided people like her should be registered or hunted*. Just: accurate.

The night clerk was Brother Anselm, a round, kind, profoundly deaf-in-one-ear man who had once smuggled Rafael extra honey-bread during a fasting week and pretended afterward it had never happened. He was asleep at his desk when she came down after compline, chin on chest, a cooling lamp beside him and the great register book open to the day's entries.

Rafael stood in the doorway a long moment, watching him breathe.

Here was the thing about grief that nobody warned you: it did not feel like sorrow. Sorrow was soft. This was more like a fire burning down in a sealed room, consuming all the air of hesitation until what remained was very simple and very bright. Her brother was in a cage because their dead mother had signed something Rafael couldn't yet read. The temple would not help. The law would not help. And every record in this building said that Rafael Voss was a scribe, a fixture, a woman whose whole self fit comfortably into a cedar box three inches wide.

Well. Let the box be empty, then.

"Brother Anselm," she said gently, on his good side.

He started, blinked, and beamed at her with the uncomplicated pleasure of a man who liked everyone. "Sister Voss! Late hours. Trouble with your slots?"

"A correction," she said. "For my own register. You know how Halvard is about accuracy."

He chuckled, waved her toward the racks, and went back to his dozing. That was the mercy of being invisible: no one ever checked what the furniture was doing.

Slot 311 opened under her thumb with a small wooden click that sounded, in the silent room, enormous. She took the slips out one at a time and read each by the light of her hand-lamp, because it seemed important, somehow, to say goodbye properly.

The indenture. Signed at fourteen, her mark shaky where her mother's signature above it was sure. She touched the wax out of habit before she could stop herself—

—and got only the dregs, the way you always did with old personal seals: a smear of feeling, her mother's tiredness, a kitchen smell, bread and smoke and something bitter underneath. No words. Nine years ago that memory-wisp had frightened her half to death; now it just made her chest ache in a familiar place, like pressing a bruise to check it still hurt.

*One soul sold,* the contract in the temple archive had said. *Name smudged.* She'd spent three nights telling herself it might not mean what it plainly meant. Tonight she stopped telling herself that. Their mother had owed a debt, and the Undercourt beneath Thornfell took payment in souls, and somewhere in a smudged line of amber-lit contract-paper, Tomas's name—or hers—was already written down. The hunters hadn't taken Tomas at random. They'd come to collect.

The commendation slip she held longest. *Accuracy beyond requirement.* Then, with a steadiness that surprised her, she fed it to the lamp.

It caught at once, eager, curling like a living thing. She fed it the rest: the indenture, the tithe waivers, the sick-note, everything, letting each burn down to ash in the little brass dish the clerks used for sealing waste. The ember-gift stirred in her as the flames rose—her gift had always loved fire, or perhaps feared it, the way kin fear kin—and for one strange moment the flames showed her nothing at all, which was its own kind of peace. Fire remembered nothing. Fire kept no registers.

When the last slip was ash, she took out her copy-stamp—the brass wheel, polished that morning for a petition that had failed—and pressed it into the soft black residue, then rolled it across the inside back wall of slot 311, where no clerk would look unless he already suspected. A small spiral. The mark she'd invented at fourteen for *this is mine, this is true, I was here.*

Then she closed the empty slot, and Rafael Voss officially ceased to exist.

It should have felt terrifying. It felt instead like taking off boots after a long road. She stood there flexing her new freedom—no name, no slot, no obligations—and heard her own heartbeat, steady, almost amused. *Nine years hiding the gift,* she thought, *and erasing myself takes ten minutes.*

"Trouble with your slots?" Anselm mumbled, half-waking.

"All corrected, Brother," she said. "Thank you."

She banked the lamp, tucked the brass dish of ash into her satchel—she wasn't sure why, except that it seemed wrong to leave herself behind entirely—and climbed the back stair to her cell for the last time. There she packed the way smugglers pack, which Tomas had taught her one bored winter: everything that matters in one bundle, everything that doesn't left to be found and misread. Warm clothes. Her mother's kitchen knife. The rubbing she'd made of the smudged contract, wrapped in oilcloth. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, the thing she'd been building all evening in her head while her hands burned paper:

A plan.

The convoy would move Tomas within days—white-banner units never wintered in one town longer than the law required, and the law required almost nothing. They'd take the low road east, through the valley, because the passes were already closing with snow and hunters trusted roads the way Halvard trusted ledgers. Which meant the convoy would pass, sooner or later, along the smuggler's cuts above Coldwater—the narrow trails her brother had run since he was sixteen, trails with names instead of numbers, trails that belonged to people who paid their debts in silence and shelter rather than coin.

She didn't know yet what she'd do when she found them. She was one scribe with a kitchen knife and a stolen handful of ash. But she knew the mountain, and the mountain did not love the crown, and somewhere between those two facts there was room enough for a rescue—or at least for a reckoning.

Before she left, she did one last thing. At her desk, in the fair hand that had won her a commendation, she wrote a single line on a scrap of paper and weighted it under her inkwell where the morning clerks would find it:

*The Emberhold protects its ledgers. Ask them, someday, who protects the people in them.*

Then Rafael Voss—who was, as of tonight, no one at all—shouldered her bundle, blew out her lamp, and went out the postern gate into the falling snow, turning her face toward Coldwater and the sound, far off, of a valley holding its breath.

Behind her, in the dark register office, slot 311 stood open and empty, a small hollow shaped exactly like a life.

Above the passes, unseen, a prisoner convoy was already making ready to move.
