## 6. A Huntress Off the Road

*Rafael*

The smuggler's cut above Wolfpine was called the Needle's Eye, and it had three virtues: it was narrow, it was dark, and in nine years of copying other people's debts Rafael Voss had learned every loose stone in it.

She crouched behind a shelf of granite with her brother's spare carting cloak around her shoulders and a coil of tarred rope in her hands, and she tried very hard to feel like the sort of person who ambushed witch-hunters. She did not succeed. She felt like a scribe who had made a terrible arithmetic error and was about to be audited by the mountain itself.

*You burned yourself out of the register,* she reminded herself. *There is no Rafael Voss anymore. There is only a woman in a dead man's cloak holding rope.*

That was supposed to be comforting. It was not.

Below her, the prisoner convoy wound up the cut — six hunters on foot, two on mules, and between them Tomas, wrists bound, walking with that infuriating set to his shoulders he got whenever he was being brave, which was to say, stupid. His debts are paid, he'd told her. I owe no one flight. Well. Someone owed someone, and the ledger said so, and Rafael had read enough wax seals in her life to know that ledgers did not care what anyone thought they were owed.

The plan — if a plan could be said to exist when its entire architecture was one rope, one slope, and one terrified scribe — was simple. Loosen the scree above the switchback. Start a slide across the trail. In the confusion, cut Tomas free, shove him down the goat path, and both of them run until spring or death, whichever came first.

She had not planned for the snowmelt.

It happened as these things do: all at once, and wrong. The scree came down in a white roar, half again larger than she'd intended, and took out not just the trail but the mules, one hunter's tent-roll, and a good forty feet of the cut's eastern wall. Dust boiled up. Somewhere below, men shouted. A mule expressed its opinion at length.

Rafael scrambled down through the settling grit, knife out, heart hammering a rhythm she was fairly sure could be heard in Thornfell.

Tomas was gone from where he'd been walking. So were the hunters. And standing in the middle of the wreckage, sword drawn, coughing dust, alone — utterly alone — was the huntress.

Sera Ilvane. Rafael knew her by reputation even before she saw the grey-and-white badge: the one who read prisoners their rights word-perfect, who had once turned back a convoy because the writ was dated wrong. The one whose face, when Tomas was seized, had looked less like triumph than like a woman biting into bread and finding it stone.

The huntress saw her. Sword up. "Don't."

"I wasn't going to," Rafael said, which was true, mostly, in the way that things are true right up until they aren't.

"You brought down half the mountain."

"Only the part that needed bringing." Rafael edged left, angling toward where she'd last seen Tomas. "Where is he? Where are the others?"

"Swept off the trail. Your brother went over the lip with two of my men — alive, I heard them shouting. They'll have him at the ford by dusk if I don't signal." The huntress's eyes flicked upward, to the ridge, to the signal-horn hanging useless at her hip, buried somewhere under forty feet of newly relocated mountain. Her jaw tightened. "And I am separated from my unit. Which you know. Because you did it."

"I did some of it," Rafael said. "The snowmelt did the rest. We can apportion blame later, over drinks neither of us will pay for."

Something flickered across Sera Ilvane's face that might, in a different life, in different armor, have been the beginning of a laugh. It died quickly. The sword stayed up.

"You're the sister," she said. "The scribe. You signed yourself out of the Emberhold register in ash. We found the burn mark."

"Then you know I'm officially no one. You can't arrest a person who doesn't exist. It's in your own procedures — I copied them once, for practice. Section twelve."

For a long moment the huntress simply looked at her, dust sifting off her pauldrons, and Rafael had the strange sensation of being read — not the way she read things, with her hands, but the ordinary human way, eyes and judgment and something underneath both.

"The ford," Sera said at last, lowering the sword an inch. "If we go now, together, we might reach it before the column regroups."

"Why would I go anywhere with a witch-hunter?"

"Because alone, you're a woman with a knife and a bad plan. Alone, I'm a huntress with no unit and no proof of anything." The inch became two inches; the sword point settled toward the ground. "And because I've been reading contracts out of the Undercourt's registry for six years, Voss, and lately the ink has been lying to me. I want to know why. You want your brother. Those seem like reasons that might travel together for a while."

Rafael looked at her hand — the knife hand, steady enough — and then at the huntress, and thought about wax seals, and smudged names, and mothers who sold things they had no right to sell.

"All right," she said. "But if you blow that horn—"

"It's under a rockfall."

"Good. Then we understand each other perfectly."

They did not, of course. But it was a start.

* * *

*Sera*

Sera Ilvane had arrested forty-one gifted persons in her seven years of service, and she had never once been ambushed by a scribe.

It stung more than she expected. Not the rockslide itself — she'd survived worse in the passes during training, though never one so thoughtfully aimed — but the fact that she'd been beaten by paperwork. By a woman who smelled of lamp-oil and old vellum, wearing a carter's cloak two sizes too big, who negotiated like someone haggling over the price of candles.

They walked the goat path single file, Sera in front because she knew the route to the ford and Rafael behind because she didn't trust Sera's back and said so, frequently, in the tone of a person narrating their own suspicions for the record.

"You realize," Rafael said, hopping a stream, "that this is the part in every ballad where the huntress betrays the outlaw."

"I'm aware. I've heard the ballads. They get the procedure wrong in all of them."

"What do they get wrong?"

"There's no dramatic speech. You fill out a form first." Sera stepped over a fallen birch. "In triplicate."

"That's worse."

"It's much worse. I once watched Captain Dreev delay an arrest for two days because the county seal had smudged." She heard Rafael make a small sound behind her — not quite a laugh, more the ghost of one, quickly strangled. Interesting. The scribe could laugh. She simply chose not to, the way some people chose not to sing.

The truth Sera had not spoken aloud, would probably never speak aloud, was this: she had read Tomas Voss his rights and felt nothing under her palms. No ember. No gift-warmth, none of the faint prickling the trained ones learned to sense. The man was as unlit as a cold hearth, and she had said so — quietly, once, to Captain Dreev — and Dreev had shown her the contract. One soul, sold to the court beneath Thornfell, transferred by inheritance. The seal was genuine. The signature was genuine. Everything was genuine except, Sera was increasingly afraid, the truth.

"You touched him," Rafael said suddenly, as if reading her thoughts, which Sera sincerely hoped was not among her gifts. "When you seized him. Did you feel anything?"

Sera considered lying. It occurred to her that lying to this woman would be like lying to an accountant — technically possible, immediately expensive.

"No," she said. "He carries no ember. I reported it."

"And?"

"And I was told the paperwork was sufficient." The words tasted like the copper coins they paid informants with. "The contract says a soul was sold. The contract doesn't require the soul to glow, apparently. It requires it to be collectible."

Rafael had stopped walking. When Sera turned, the scribe was staring at her with an expression Sera had seen on witnesses before — the look of a person whose worst suspicion has just been confirmed by exactly the wrong mouth.

"So it isn't just us," Rafael said softly. "Your own law knows he's innocent, and it proceeds anyway."

"The law doesn't know anything. People do. People proceed anyway." Sera started walking again, faster, because standing still with those words in the air felt like standing in rising water. "Which is why I want the registry. If the contracts are forged — if the crown has been manufacturing soul-debts to fund the hunts — then everything I've done for seven years is—"

She stopped herself. Rafael, mercifully, did not finish the sentence for her. She just walked alongside, boots crunching, and after a while she said, in a completely different tone:

"Is it true there's a form for everything? Even apologies?"

"There's a reconciliation affidavit."

"And have you ever filed one?"

"No," Sera said. "But I've drafted several. At night. They're very well organized."

This time the laugh escaped fully, startled out of the scribe like a bird off a fence, and Sera found — to her considerable professional alarm — that she wanted to hear it again.

* * *

*Rafael*

They made the overlook above the ford as the light went amber, and Rafael's stomach performed a slow, sickening drop.

The convoy had reformed. Torches on the far bank, maybe twenty of them, and Tomas sitting bound against a supply crate with a blanket over his shoulders — alive, unharmed as far as she could tell, and glaring at his captors with the specific fury of a man who believed himself owed an apology and would wait a very long time for it.

"They'll cross at first light," Sera murmured beside her. "The ford's too high at night. They'll post watches, but the watch rotation is standard — four hours, two men, change at moonset."

"How do you know their rotation?"

"Because I wrote it." A pause. "Three years ago. Nobody's updated the manual since."

Rafael tore her gaze from her brother and studied the huntress's profile — the set jaw, the torchlight catching the pale scar through one eyebrow that Rafael had been politely not asking about. Here was the thing she kept circling and couldn't land: Sera Ilvane had every reason to signal her unit right now. One whistle, one flare, and the traitor scribe was taken, the huntress restored, the whole ugly business wrapped up with a ribbon. Instead the woman sat in the cold beside her enemy, sharing a heel of black bread, pointing out which sentries leaned on which leg when they were bored.

"Why?" Rafael asked finally. "Truly. Not the registry. Why help me?"

Sera was quiet long enough that a nightjar called twice in the pines below.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I reported my mentor for shielding a gifted child. It was correct procedure. Every box ticked." Her thumb moved along her sword hilt, back and forth, like a hand smoothing a page. "She died in custody before the trial. And afterward I kept waiting for the law to notice that something had gone wrong — some audit, some correction, some form, I don't know. The system notices everything, Voss. It noticed the child. It noticed my mentor. It has never once noticed that it eats people." She looked over, and her eyes in the torch-glow were steadier than her voice. "So either I'm wrong about all of it, in which case helping you costs me a career I deserve to lose. Or I'm right, in which case helping you is the first correct thing I've done in seven years. Either way, I sleep better than I have in a decade."

Rafael looked down at her own hands, at the faint ember-heat that lived in her palms the way warmth lives in stones after sundown — the thing she had hidden for nine years, copying tithes, flinching from every seal. This stranger had just handed her the most dangerous sentence Rafael had ever heard: *I doubt.*

People had been hunted for less. People had been saved by less.

"Here's my bargain, then," Rafael said. "You want proof the contracts are forged. I know where proof lives — in the wax, in the seals, in the memory of things touched. My mother's name is on one of those contracts, smudged, and I need to see it whole. That means going down. Into the Undercourt."

Sera went very still. "No one goes into the Undercourt. You sign yourself in as collateral and you don't sign out."

"I know how debt works. I've copied it for nine years." Rafael held out her hand — the ember hand, palm up, glowing faintly gold in the dark like a coal refusing to die. "Entry requires a debt of one's own. Fine. I have one. Myself. You get me to the gate and stand witness while I read the founding contracts, and I'll show you forgery older and deeper than anything the crown has filed. And when we come back up — if we come back up — my brother walks free, and you take your evidence to whoever in that rotten hierarchy still owns a conscience."

Sera stared at the small golden light in her palm. Most people, seeing the gift bare, reached for iron or prayer. This woman reached slowly, deliberately, and took Rafael's hand anyway — skin to skin, hunter to scribe — and Rafael braced for the flood of another's memories, the trespass she couldn't control.

Nothing came. Sera's mind, touching hers through the gift, was like a well-kept ledger room: ordered, austere, and — in the far corner, behind a locked drawer — one small, fierce, carefully hidden fire.

"Well," Sera said quietly, releasing her hand. "Now I know why they hunt you people. It's not the power. It's that you can't lie to each other."

"Occupational hazard." Rafael flexed her fingers, oddly reluctant to close them. "Do we have a bargain, huntress?"

"We have a bargain, scribe." Sera stood, shouldering her pack, and glanced down at the torches by the ford, at the small bound figure glaring beside the supply crate. "First light, they cross. Which means tonight—" she pointed up-trail, toward a smear of darkness that Rafael's smuggler's knowledge translated instantly as *the old shrine caves under Wolfpine* "—we move. And Voss?"

"Yes?"

"If you ambush me again, fill out the intent-to-ambush form first. I'll expect proper documentation."

Rafael grinned into the dark, and for the first time since the white banner came over the pass, the grin didn't feel like a mask.

"Lead the way," she said. "I'll bring the triplicate."

Above them, unseen, the first stars came out over Thornfell; below, the torches of the convoy burned on; and between the two lights, two women who should have been enemies started up the mountain together, carrying between them one stolen brother, one forged empire's worth of suspicion, and — though neither would have admitted it under torture, which was fortunate, as torture seemed increasingly likely — the first small, unfiled entry of something that was going to cost them both far more than a career.
