## 7. The Smuggler's Catechism

The pass above Thornfell had a name on no map, because the people who used it did not believe in giving the crown anything it could write down. Rafael called it the Needle, and she called the weather on it a liar, and by the second morning she had decided that Sera Ilvane was both of those things at once: narrow and sharp and impossible to trust, and yet somehow the only thing standing between Rafael and the wind.

"Rule one," Rafael said, breath steaming. She held up one gloved finger. "You don't ask where a road goes. You ask who keeps it."

Sera trudged behind her, mule in tow, looking like a woman who had been carved out of duty and then left out in the rain. "That's not a rule. That's a riddle."

"It's a rule. Everything on this mountain is a riddle if you're used to reading things off paper." Rafael turned up the switchback, boots finding the frozen scree without looking. Nine years of copying other people's debts had taught her that most truths were written twice—once for the record, once underneath. The mountain only kept the second copy. "Who keeps the road tells you whether there's shelter at the top, whether the toll is coin or silence, whether you'll be fed or merely not robbed."

"And how do I find out who keeps it?"

"You already know. The man who sold us cheese in Last Ford. The woman who watered our mule and wouldn't take payment."

"The innkeeper's wife? She refused my coin."

"She wasn't refusing your coin. She was telling you the next three shelters are hers to call in. You now owe her a favor of unknown size, redeemable whenever she likes, possibly years from now, possibly at an inconvenient hour." Rafael glanced back. Sera's expression was doing something complicated. "That's rule two. Never pay a debt early. It insults the ledger."

"I have *kept* ledgers," Sera said. "For the court. There are rules about—"

"There are rules about ink," Rafael said. "This is about bread."

* * *

They made the first shelter by late afternoon: a hollow beneath an overhang of gray stone, walled with stacked slates that some carter had fitted together generations ago, roofed with old wagon canvas gone stiff as bark. Someone had left split wood under a flat stone. Someone before that had left more. Rafael built the fire while Sera stood in the entrance with her hand on her sword hilt, watching the treeline as though the trees might file a report.

"No one will come," Rafael said.

"Hunters use these routes."

"Not these shelters. Rule three." She fed a branch to the flame. "A hunter who sleeps in a smuggler's shelter has surrendered her warrant at the door. It doesn't matter what the law says. The law stops at the valley floor. Up here, the only thing that outranks you is the person who built the walls, and they've been dead two hundred years."

"That's absurd."

"Yes," Rafael agreed warmly. "It's also why you're alive. Your captain's convoy took the low road. The low road has bridges, and bridges have tolls, and tolls have men who remember your face from the ambush at the cut." She looked up. Sera hadn't moved. "Sit down, huntress. The dead who own this shelter don't care about your rank, but I do, and I say you've earned the fire."

Sera sat. Slowly, the way a cat sits near a dog it hasn't finished evaluating. She unbuckled her sword belt and laid it across her knees rather than setting it aside, which Rafael chose to interpret as progress.

"You knew those men would be at the cut," Sera said. "At the tollgate. You walked us around them without telling me we were walking around them."

"I told you the route had scenery."

"You told me—" Sera stopped. Rafael watched her decide, visibly, not to finish the sentence. It was becoming a habit between them, this mutual act of not finishing sentences, and Rafael found she minded it less each day.

"Eat," Rafael said, and handed her the flatbread and hard cheese.

They ate. The wind worked at the canvas overhead with patient fingers. Somewhere below, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"My mentor taught me procedure," Sera said at last, to the fire rather than to Rafael. "Not law. Procedure. He said the law was the shape of justice the way a boot is the shape of a foot—made for someone else, worn anyway." She turned the cheese in her fingers. "I thought he was being clever. I thought cleverness was a kind of insubordination."

"What do you think now?"

Sera was quiet long enough that Rafael thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "I think a boot that fits nobody still gets worn. And somebody decides whose foot pays for it."

Rafael felt something turn over in her chest, slow and warm, like a banked coal remembering itself. She didn't say anything. Rule four, though she didn't voice it: never interrupt a person who is telling you the truth for the first time.

* * *

The second day brought them above the treeline, into a world of white and iron-gray rock where the sky seemed closer and less friendly. Rafael stopped at midday at a cairn no taller than her knee, crowned with a rusted iron bell no bigger than a fist, and waited.

Sera waited too, then lost patience. "What are we doing?"

"Paying the silence toll."

"There's no one here."

"There's everyone here." Rafael dug in her pack and produced a strip of dried apple, which she placed on the cairn's flat capstone. "Every traveler leaves something small and says nothing aloud within earshot of the bell. No names, no destinations, no business. The mountain carries sound strangely. A name spoken up here can travel further than you'd believe, and land somewhere you'd like it less."

"That's superstition."

"That's *infrastructure*." Rafael rang the bell once with a fingernail—a thin, bright note that fell away into the wind like a coin down a well. "Someone maintains this cairn. Someone replaces the bell when it cracks. Someone eats the apples, eventually, or the birds do, and either way the custom holds. You want to know who keeps this road? Everyone who's ever passed this stone and left something. That's the whole government. That's the entire treasury. One apple at a time."

Sera stared at the cairn. Then, with the air of a woman signing something she'd later deny, she took a copper bit from her purse and set it beside the apple.

"He won't take coin," Rafael said.

"Who's *he*?"

"Whoever comes next. Coin's no good up here—it's evidence. Leave something you'd actually want to find." Rafael nodded at Sera's belt. "The spare flint."

Sera's hand went to it reflexively. "That flint has lit every fire I've made for six years."

"Then whoever finds it will think very highly of you." Rafael smiled, and was surprised to find the smile was real. "That's the whole point of the toll. You leave a piece of what you are, and a stranger picks it up, and for one moment the road is generous instead of cruel. That's not superstition either. That's just... bookkeeping. The kind that balances."

Sera unclipped the flint. She held it a moment longer than necessary. Then she set it on the cairn, straightened, and rang the bell herself—one clean note, struck properly, like a woman who understood bells.

They walked on. Rafael did not look back, but she heard Sera do it, once, boots crunching, and pretended not to notice, and found that pretending was getting harder to do around this woman, and that she had stopped entirely minding that too.

* * *

The storm caught them at dusk on the third day, which Rafael had predicted at dawn, which Sera had noted with the sour precision of a woman keeping score. They took refuge in the shrine at Coldwater's upper reach—a waystone chapel hardly bigger than a hay barn, its door unlatched, its interior smelling of pine resin and old snowmelt and, faintly, of every traveler who had ever slept there, which is to say of humanity itself.

Inside, the walls were covered in marks. Not graffiti—records. Tallies, initials, dates scratched into the plaster in a hundred different hands across a hundred different years. *M. Kell, passed safe, 3rd thaw.* *Two brothers, going down, light of heart.* *She said yes.* Small drawings: a mule, a lopsided sun, a hand holding a hand.

"What is this?" Sera asked, and her voice had changed—lowered, the way people lower their voices in temples, except Rafael noticed she hadn't decided to do it.

"A register," Rafael said. "The only honest one I know of. No one writes here to be forgiven. They write so the next person knows the road was walked." She ran her fingers near—not quite touching—the wall of names, out of nine years' habit of caution. "Rule five. Every shelter keeps a record. Not because anyone demands it. Because leaving a mark is how you prove to yourself, later, that you were here and it mattered."

Sera read the wall for a long time. Her lantern threw her shadow tall against the plaster, over all those names, so that for a moment she seemed to stand among them rather than apart from them.

"My mother kept a household ledger," she said suddenly. "Incomes, outlays, everything balanced to the halfpenny. When she died I found a page in the back, hidden. Just names. People she'd helped—loans forgiven, sicknesses nursed, a roof mended—and no amounts at all. Just names, and little marks beside them. I thought she'd failed at bookkeeping." Sera touched one of the scratched initials on the shrine wall, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "I think I understand her better tonight than I did in twenty years before."

Rafael built the fire. When she looked up, Sera had taken out a knife and was cutting a small, careful line into the fresh plaster near the door: *S.I., passed safe.* She hesitated. Then, beneath it, in smaller letters: *learning.*

"There," Rafael said softly. "Now you exist up here. The crown can't grant that. Only the road can."

* * *

They traveled four more days, and Rafael taught the rest of the catechism in pieces, between ridgelines and cold camps. Rule six: share water upward—if someone's climbing toward you, they need it more. Rule seven: never lie to a person feeding you, even kindly. Rule eight: when the passes close, grudges close with them; winter makes cousins of enemies, and spring can sort it out again. Rule nine, delivered gravely over a fire while Sera tried not to laugh: never trust a mule's opinion of a stranger before dark, but always after.

"You're inventing half of these," Sera accused on the fifth evening.

"I'm *transmitting* half of these. There's a difference. Invention is what the court does. Transmission is what a road does." Rafael poked the fire. "My brother taught me most of them. Tomas could make friends with a landslide. He used to say the mountain doesn't care what you're owed—it cares what you carry, and whether you'll share it."

Sera was quiet. Then, unexpectedly: "Your brother. The one I arrested." She said it plainly, no armor on it, and Rafael respected the cost. "He asked me for water on the road to the hold. Not to drink—he had his own flask. He asked if *I* needed any. Climbing toward him, chained, and he offered me water first." She looked into the fire. "I wrote it up in my report as 'prisoner compliant.' It was the most dishonest entry I ever made."

Rafael's throat tightened. She thought of Tomas on the smuggler roads, stubborn as bedrock, paying his debts, owing no one flight. Of course he'd offered the huntress water. Of course he had.

"Rule ten," Rafael managed. "The last one."

"I'm listening."

"When someone shows you who they are on the road—believe them. Even if it costs you. Especially if it costs you." She met Sera's eyes across the fire. "You showed me who you are at the cut, when you put your sword down. I'm believing you. It's costing me. That's how I know it counts."

Sera held her gaze. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the high rocks with a sound like pages turning.

"Rafael." Sera said her name carefully, as though testing whether it would ring true, like the bell on the cairn. "When this is done—when we get your brother—I don't know what I am. I burned my oath. I can't go back down the mountain as what I was."

"Then don't," Rafael said. "Come back up it. The road keeps people. It's the one ledger that never calls in its debts—it just keeps adding names." She hesitated, then added, quieter, "Mine's on the wall at Coldwater. Has been since I was sixteen. *R.V., passed safe.* Under it, my mother wrote—"

She stopped. The words snagged, the way they always did.

Sera waited. She was learning the catechism faster than Rafael taught it.

"*Under it she wrote: carried,*" Rafael finished. "*Carried, not passing. My mother marked me as cargo. Something being moved along by others. I used to think it was pity.*" She shook her head slowly. "Now I think maybe she knew something was coming for me, someday, that I couldn't walk past alone. Maybe she was telling the road: whatever meets her, help carry her."

The wind rose outside the shrine, keening through the high stones. Sera reached across the fire and, without ceremony, took Rafael's hand—the first time she had touched her unprompted, ungloved, skin to skin. Her palm was warm and rough from the sword hilt.

"Then that's rule eleven," Sera said. "Nobody gets carried alone."

Rafael laughed—a startled, wet-edged sound—and did not let go.

It was near midnight when the bell at Coldwater's lower reach began to ring. Not the thin traveler's note of a single fingernail strike, but fast, repeated, urgent—the signal Rafael had named for Sera days ago without explaining it, and now saw understanding dawn on Sera's face in the firelight.

"Travelers in trouble?" Sera asked.

"Worse." Rafael was already pulling on her boots, her stomach dropping through the mountain's whole cold height. "That's the toll-gang's bell. Tollgate of Teeth. And they're ringing it because they know *my* name is on the road tonight."
