## 9. Tollgate of Teeth

The pass above Coldwater had a name on no map and a smell Rafael could have identified blindfolded: pine resin, cold stone, and the particular iron tang of the stream that cut through it. She'd run this route forty times in her smuggling years, back when her legs were younger and her conscience quieter, and she knew every place the mountain offered to hide a person or kill one.

She also knew the tollgate.

It wasn't much to look at—a shelf of rock where the trail narrowed to the width of two carts, a fallen pine laid across the gap like a bar across a door, and above it, the ledge where watchers could stand and count what came through. Every road in the high country had one. The mountains ran on them the way temples ran on tithes.

"They'll see us before we see them," she said, crouching at the bend where the trail dipped out of sight. "So we may as well be honest about it."

Sera settled beside her, huntress-quiet even off duty, her bow strung and slung but her hand never far from it. "Honest how?"

"We walk up with our hands visible and our mouths shut until they tell us the price." Rafael rubbed her gloved hands together against the morning chill. "That's the catechism. You pay the toll, you keep your name, you go on."

"And if we can't pay?"

Rafael winced. "Then we negotiate. And if negotiation fails—" She glanced sideways at Sera, whose expression suggested she was already calculating angles of fire. "—we don't fail."

"You've done this before."

"I've *paid* before. Many times. In coin, mostly." She hesitated. "Once in a very good knife I regretted for a year."

Sera's mouth twitched—the ghost of the smile she still seemed surprised to find herself wearing these days. "You regretted the knife."

"I regretted the knife. The man who took it was perfectly pleasant about it. That made it worse somehow."

They went up together, boots crunching on the crusted snow, and Rafael kept her hood down deliberately, because there were things worse than being recognized up here, and one of them was being recognized *wrong*.

The challenge came when they were thirty paces out—a whistle, sharp and doubled, from the ledge above the fallen pine. Then figures rose along the rocks like crows startled off a carcass: six of them, seven, eight, wrapped in grey wool and patchwork furs, bows drawn but not yet aimed with any real intent. Toll-gangs didn't shoot travelers who stopped. Shooting travelers who stopped was bad for business; word got around, and then nobody came through your pass at all, and you starved on a mountain full of nothing.

A big man climbed onto the fallen pine and stood balanced on it like it was a tavern bench. He had a beard that had clearly lost an argument with the wind and a scar running through one eyebrow, and when he looked down at Rafael, his face did something complicated.

"Well," he said. "Well, well, well."

"Hello, Brannic," Rafael said.

"*Little Voss.*" He said it the way other men said *ghost*. He hopped down from the log, landing heavy, snow spraying, and came toward her with his arms half-open, which was either a greeting or a trap depending on the day and the man. "Nine years. Nine years, girl, and not so much as a card."

"I sent word when the temple took me on."

"You sent a *boy* with a *note*. That's not word, that's a receipt." He stopped an arm's length away and looked her over, and she watched him cataloguing the changes—she'd been nineteen the last time he'd seen her, all elbows and secrets, and now she was twenty-eight and carrying different secrets in the same places. His eyes flicked past her shoulder to Sera, and narrowed. "And who's this, then? You traveling with a sword-arm now?"

"Huntress," Sera said flatly.

Brannic's eyebrows went up. Both of them, scarred and unscarred alike. "A huntress. On *my* road. Walking *openly*." He looked back at Rafael, and something in his face cooled by several degrees. "Raffie. Sweet Raffie. Tell me you haven't brought the law up my mountain."

"She's not the law anymore," Rafael said. "Not exactly. It's complicated."

"Nothing's complicated on my road. Things are paid or unpaid, passed or turned back. That's the whole beauty of it." Brannic sighed, and it was a theatrical sigh, the sigh of a man who enjoyed his own grievances. "Here's my trouble, little Voss. For six years you ran cargo through three passes—mine, Hesk's, and the Widow's—and for six years you never once gave up a route, a name, or a face. Not to the temple, not to the crown, not to anyone. Silence like that is worth money. Silence like that is worth *respect*." He spread his hands. "And now you come walking through my gate with a huntress at your back, and every man on this rock is thinking the same thing I'm thinking: how long before she talks about *us*?"

"I have never talked about you in my life," Rafael said. "I'm constitutionally incapable of it. Ask anyone."

"Can't. Anyone I'd ask is dead or scattered, thanks to the edict." Brannic scratched under his beard. "So here's the thing. The old price was coin. The new price—" He shrugged, apologetic and utterly unapologetic at once. "—is blood."

The word dropped into the cold air and sat there.

"Yours or ours?" Sera asked, in the tone of someone asking whether a shop took silver or copper.

Brannic grinned, showing teeth that had survived a lot of winters through sheer stubbornness. "Oh, I like her. That's unfortunate for everyone." He jerked his chin at the ledge. "Ours, if you make us take it. Yours, if you're sensible. A pint each, given freely—that's the old way, blood-toll, older than coin, older than the temples. You spill willing, you pass clean. Or—" and here his grin faded into something more careful, more tired, "—you turn around, little Voss, and you go the long way round, and we all pretend this morning was a dream. I'd rather that, honestly. I liked your mother. I don't want to bury her daughter over a pint of blood."

Behind him, Rafael counted the bows again. Eight men, maybe nine—one she couldn't see clearly behind the rock spur. Good positions. Relaxed draw. These weren't killers; they were toll-keepers, and toll-keepers fought like homeowners defending a kitchen—desperately, and only when pushed.

But pushed, they would push back.

"It's a fair ask," she heard herself say. "By the old law, it's a fair ask."

Sera turned her head slowly. "*Blood?*"

"A cup each. It's symbolic, mostly. It says: I acknowledge this road has a keeper, and I pay my passage in something that costs me." Rafael kept her voice low. "It's the oldest contract form there is. Older than wax. Older than ledgers."

"You want to bleed for them."

"I want to *pass*, and bleeding is cheaper than dying, and considerably cheaper than killing." She met Sera's eyes. "This is the catechism, Sera. Shelter debts, silence tolls. I told you the mountains run on debts. This is what one looks like."

Sera looked at her for a long moment, and Rafael watched her do the thing she'd been doing more and more lately—weighing the law she'd memorized against the law she was learning, and finding the second one heavier. Then she rolled her shoulders, unslung her pack, and said, loudly enough for the ledge to hear:

"Fine. But I go last, and if anyone's blade touches her while she's bleeding, I put arrows in the lot of you and explain it to your ghosts."

Brannic beamed. "See, this is why I don't allow huntresses on my road. They're all romance and threats."

* * *

The blood-toll was done at the shrine stone just past the gate—a flat slab of granite worn smooth by generations of palms and knives, with a shallow basin carved at its center where rainwater gathered and froze and thawed and gathered again. Rafael had paid here a dozen times in her running days. The stone remembered every one of them; she could feel the layered hum of it through her gloves, hundreds of small transactions, fear and relief braided together, and beneath it all something older and patient that she chose not to examine too closely, because examining things closely was how her whole life had come apart.

She bared her forearm. Brannic himself held the knife—an ugly, honest thing, honed thin—and when he opened the skin along the old white scar she already carried there, he did it gently, the way you'd treat a book you respected.

"Still hiding things under your sleeves, Raffie?" he murmured, low enough that only she heard.

"Always," she murmured back.

His blood went into the basin after hers, dark against the stone, steaming faintly in the cold. One of his men—a boy of maybe seventeen with a fuzz of beard and enormous earnest eyes—poured a measure of something from a clay jug, and Rafael smelled juniper and felt her eyebrows climb.

"You're cutting blood-toll with *spirit*?"

"Winter rules," Brannic said gravely. "Wounds close better if the blood's cheerful."

"That is not medicine."

"It's tradition."

"It's *poison* with ambition."

"It's *tradition*," the boy said, wounded, and Rafael decided she would die before she told him otherwise.

Sera paid last, as she'd insisted. She rolled up her sleeve without ceremony, set her arm on the stone, and looked at Brannic's knife with the expression of a woman inspecting a recruit's kit. When the blade touched her, she didn't flinch—of course she didn't flinch, Rafael thought, she probably had a regulation about flinching somewhere—but Rafael watched her anyway, watched the small involuntary tightening at the corner of her jaw, and found herself absurdly angry at the sight of it. Not at Brannic. At the world, generally, for making Sera Ilvane practice being hurt until it looked like nothing.

"There," Brannic said, when the basin held its dark measure. He pressed cloth to both their arms, brisk and oddly tender, a man who had bound a hundred wounds and liked none of them. "Paid clean. You're through, both of you, and anything of mine you meet between here and the Widow's pass will know it." He paused, then added, quieter: "You're going down, aren't you. Toward Thornfell way. Toward the court."

Rafael said nothing.

Brannic looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him sigh again, that same theatrical sigh, but softer at the edges now. "Your mother ran those roads too, you know. Before you. Same stubborn walk." He tapped the shrine stone once, twice, the way you'd knock on a friend's door. "Stone remembers, girl. Whatever you're going to do down there—do it so the stone's glad it remembered you."

They passed under the fallen pine, and Rafael did not look back, because looking back at tollgates was bad luck, and because if she looked back she'd see Sera's blood drying on the stone, and she was already carrying quite enough.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a smuggler's nest Rafael had used since she was sixteen—a dry pocket in the rock with a fire-ring of stacked stones and a cache hole where she'd once hidden a season's worth of contraband tea. The tea was long gone, taken or spoiled, but the hollow still held heat like a cupped hand.

Sera got the fire going while Rafael dug out the food: hard bread, smoked goat cheese, a jar of honey that had survived the journey by being wedged between socks. They ate in the comfortable silence that had grown between them over the weeks on the road—not empty silence, but furnished silence, the kind with chairs in it.

It was when Rafael changed the dressing on Sera's arm that the silence changed.

The cut was clean, shallow, already knitting—but Rafael had insisted on cleaning it properly, because she'd seen what mountain fevers did, and because, if she was honest, she wanted the excuse to hold Sera's wrist and be gentle at her for five uninterrupted minutes. She worked with boiled water and a strip of linen, and Sera sat very still and let her, watching the fire with an expression Rafael had learned to read as *thinking about something she isn't going to say*.

"You didn't have to fight for me today," Rafael said. "At the gate. You didn't have to say what you said."

"Yes I did."

"No. By every law you ever served, you should have stood aside and let them bleed me. I'm a fugitive's sister traveling with a deserter. We're the *paperwork problem*, Sera. You spent ten years learning to solve paperwork problems."

Sera was quiet a moment. "When I read Tomas his rights," she said finally, to the fire rather than to Rafael, "I knew the words were wrong. I read them anyway, because procedure. And it's been eating me since—reading wrong words correctly." She flexed her fingers as Rafael tied off the bandage. "Today I got to say right words. Loud ones. Threatening ones." A pause. "It was the first time in months I've felt like the law."

"The law," Rafael said softly, "as practiced, not decreed."

"Don't quote me back at me."

"You love it when I quote you back at you."

"I tolerate it," Sera said, with dignity, "under protest," and then, because the firelight was kind and the night was long and some truths travel better in the dark, she added, barely above the crackle of the flames: "When Brannic's boy reached for his knife too fast—at the basin—I moved before I decided to. Between you and him. I didn't think. There was no procedure. My body just... chose."

Rafael's hands went still on the knot she was tying.

"I know," she said. "I saw."

"I've never moved like that for anyone. Not in eleven years of service." Sera turned her head, and the fire caught the grey in her eyes and warmed it to something almost amber. "I don't entirely know what to do with that."

"You don't have to do anything with it," Rafael said. "Some things aren't debts. Some things are just... true. You can carry them without paying interest."

Sera laughed—a real laugh, short and startled out of her, the first one Rafael had ever heard her spend freely. "Did you just make a ledger joke at me."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them are accounting jokes. It's a hazard of nine years in a scriptorium."

"Gods help me," Sera said, and leaned, slightly, deliberately, until her shoulder rested against Rafael's, warm through the wool, and stayed there, and neither of them called attention to it, because calling attention to things was sometimes the surest way to break them.

They sat like that while the fire burned down, and the wind moved through the pines overhead with its long slow breathing, and Rafael found herself memorizing the weight of it—the shoulder, the warmth, the ordinary miracle of not being alone in a cold place—because she had learned, over nine years of copying other people's lives, that the moments you failed to memorize were the ones grief took first.

* * *

She woke near midnight to Sera shaking her shoulder, one hand pressed gently over Rafael's mouth before she'd fully surfaced.

"Voices," Sera breathed. "Below us. Coming up the pass."

Rafael listened. Beneath the wind: boot-crunch, many boots, and the low murmur of men moving with purpose rather than wandering. And threaded through it, unmistakable even at distance, a sound that turned her blood to well-water—

Bells. Small, clustered, official bells. The kind that hung from the harnesses of crown convoys so that everyone in earshot would know the law was coming and clear the road.

Prisoner bells.

Sera was already moving, checking her bowstring by feel, and when Rafael caught her eye in the last ember-light of the fire, she saw her own dread reflected and doubled.

"That's not a toll-gang," Sera whispered. "That's a convoy. Heading *up*—toward the court's back door." Her jaw tightened. "Rafael. If they're taking prisoners to the Undercourt by the mountain road..."

"Then Tomas might be on it," Rafael finished, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the winter outside.

She was pulling on her boots before the thought finished leaving her mouth.
