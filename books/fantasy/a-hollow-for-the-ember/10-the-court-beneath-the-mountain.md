## 10. The Court Beneath the Mountain

**Sera**

The convoy road was easy to follow because the convoy made no attempt to hide it.

That was the first thing that troubled Sera. Nine years of hunting had taught her that anything the court moved in daylight was either worthless or bait, and prisoners bound for the Undercourt were neither. She crouched at the ridge above the switchbacks and counted torches through the falling snow: eight guards, a wagon, and a line of walkers roped at the wrists, moving with the shuffling patience of people who had already been told there was nowhere to go.

"Six walkers," she murmured. "Maybe seven. Hard to tell."

Beside her, Rafael had gone very still in the way she did when she was frightened and refusing to spend the fear on anything useful. "Which one is he?"

"I don't know yet."

"You said you'd know."

"I said I'd *look*," Sera said, and immediately regretted it, because Rafael's face did the thing it did when she was trying not to hope — a small tightening around the mouth, like a door being latched from the inside. Sera had learned that face across a hundred miles of bad road and one very memorable argument about whether it was acceptable to eat the emergency rations on the second day. "Rafael. Look at the guards' spacing. They're not hurrying. If your brother were on that wagon, they'd have no reason to be gentle, and no reason to be slow either. This is a routine transfer. Routine means paperwork, and paperwork means records, and records we can read."

"You're comforting me with filing," Rafael said.

"I am comforting you with the only thing I have that works."

It got the ghost of a smile, which Sera counted as a victory and hoarded accordingly. Then Rafael's expression shifted — that other stillness, the one Sera had learned meant her hand was on something old. She'd pressed her bare palm flat against the rock of the ridge, and when she spoke her voice had gone strange and distant, like a woman reading a letter over someone's shoulder.

"Stone remembers weight," she said softly. "This road has carried prisoners before. Many. Going down. Never coming up."

"Cheerful."

"That's the job." She pulled her hand back and flexed the fingers, and Sera saw her wince — she always winced now, a little, after a reading. Since the toll-gang, since the wound in her side that Sera had stitched by firelight while Rafael made jokes that fooled neither of them, the readings seemed to cost her more. She hid it well. Sera had been a huntress long enough to know that the people who hid things best were the ones you watched closest.

"Back way," Sera said, returning to the map in her head. "The convoy's headed for the service gate at Hollowmouth. Which means the front gate is—"

"Unguarded by comparison," Rafael finished. "You've mentioned. Twice."

"Because it's a good plan."

"Because it's *your* plan. That's different."

It was, Sera reflected, a fair point, and she declined to concede it aloud. They moved down off the ridge as the snow thickened, two dark shapes against a white world, following a road that the mountain remembered all too well.

* * *

**Rafael**

The front gate of the Undercourt was a door in the side of the mountain, and it was beautiful, and Rafael hated it.

She had expected something grim — iron, chains, the architecture of punishment. Instead the cavern mouth opened like a cathedral's throat, its rim carved with figures she recognized from the Emberhold's oldest illuminated pages: the First Auditor, scales in hand; the Nine Signatories bowing; and above them all, worn nearly smooth by time, a single open hand. Pilgrim niches lined the approach, each one holding a candle stub and a small stone, and the stones were worn glossy from centuries of being touched by people asking for mercy from the thing they were about to enter.

"They come here *willingly*," Rafael whispered.

"Debtors do," Sera said. Her voice had gone flat and official — Rafael recognized the tone now, the huntress's armor of procedure — but her hand had found Rafael's sleeve and stayed there, which was not procedure at all. "That's the part nobody says out loud. The court doesn't drag most people down. They walk. Because owing feels like drowning, and drowning people will sign anything that promises air."

The gate itself stood open. Of course it did. A door you had to fight to enter was a door for enemies, and the Undercourt had no enemies, only account holders.

A figure sat in the gatehouse — a small, neat woman with spectacles and a ledger open before her, as if she had been waiting for them specifically and had merely gotten comfortable about it. She looked up without surprise.

"Two," she said, making a note. "One armed. One—" her eyes flicked to Rafael's bandaged side, then to Rafael's face, and something in her expression sharpened with professional interest, "—one carrying."

"Carrying what?" Sera asked.

"Debt, presumably. Everyone who comes down this road is carrying something." The woman turned the ledger around and slid it across the stone sill with one finger. "The gate requires a bond of entry. Your own name, your own hand, your own ember. You may not enter the court on another's account, and you may not enter on no account at all. The mountain does not host the weightless."

Rafael read the page. It was written in the old temple script — the same script she had copied for nine years, the same script she had burned herself out of a register with — and the terms were simple in the way that avalanches are simple. *I, the undersigned, enter of my own debt. What I owe below, I owe. What I am owed below, I may claim.*

"Rafael." Sera's voice, low. "We don't sign anything until we know what—"

"We've been over this."

"We've been over it *my way*, which was that we find another entrance."

"There is no other entrance. There's the gate, and there's Hollowmouth, and Hollowmouth has eight guards and a wagon." Rafael kept her eyes on the page. Her heart was doing something complicated and arrhythmic, but her hand, when she held it out over the ledger, was steady. Nine years of copying had given her that much: a scribe's hand does not shake in front of a superior. "Sera. My brother is somewhere under this mountain with his soul held as collateral against a contract our mother signed. The only way in is to owe. So I'm going to owe."

"You already owe," Sera said. "You owe me a very long argument about the emergency rations, and I intend to collect."

It was such an absurd thing to say at the mouth of hell that Rafael laughed — a short, cracked sound, half sob — and the gatekeeper wrote something down, and Sera's hand closed over hers on the sill, warm and certain, and Rafael signed.

The pen was bone. The ink was warm.

The moment her name left her hand, she felt it — a faint tug behind her sternum, like a hook set gently in cloth. Not pain. *Attention.* Somewhere below, something vast and patient had opened an eye and written her name in a book of its own.

"Account holder Rafael Voss," the gatekeeper said, and closed the ledger. "The stair is behind you. Mind the fourth landing; the amber pools there and it flatters the depth." She looked up at Sera over her spectacles. "You. Huntress. Your oath is void below. The court recognizes no crown down here — only debt. Do you enter as her surety?"

Sera was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "I enter as her friend," and signed with the bone pen in handwriting that was, Rafael noted, shockingly elegant for a woman who ate like a siege army.

The gatekeeper made her note. "Surety by friendship," she murmured. "Rare. Expensive. Good luck."

* * *

**Sera**

The stair went down for what felt like a penance, and then it opened, and Sera forgot how to be afraid because she was too busy forgetting how to breathe.

The Undercourt was not a dungeon. It was a *city* — a cavern so vast that the far walls were suggestions rather than facts, lit from below by veins of amber light that ran through the living rock like sap through wood. Terraces of pale stone descended in rings, and on the terraces stood buildings, and in the buildings worked thousands of figures, clerks and couriers and petitioners, and the whole vast machine ran with the soft, ceaseless sound of paper being turned.

Every light in the place was the same amber. And Sera, who had spent her life around embers and knew their colors the way a shepherd knows sheep, understood with a lurch what she was looking at.

They weren't lamps.

"They're *stored*," Rafael whispered. Her face was amber-lit and grey. "The light. It's the collateral. All those souls, they just — they keep them *lit*."

"Like a treasury," Sera said. Her voice came out wrong. "Like coin in a vault."

"Worse. Coin doesn't remember being a person."

They stood on the first terrace while the city flowed around them, and no one challenged them, and that was somehow the most horrifying thing yet. A pair of clerks passed carrying a ledger between them like pallbearers. A petitioner knelt at a counter, weeping quietly, while a functionary behind the grille explained something in the kind tone people use for weather. A child chased a rolling hoop down a side terrace, and Sera watched it go and thought: *they let children down here. There must be children's debts. There must be inherited accounts.*

She had served the law for eleven years. She had believed — had needed to believe — that the court beneath the mountain was the crown's necessary dark twin, unpleasant but orderly, the place where the worst accounts were settled so the surface could stay clean. Standing here, she felt the belief come apart with the quiet, terrible ease of wet paper.

"Hey." Rafael's shoulder bumped hers. "You've got your filing face on."

"I'm cataloguing exits."

"You're horrified."

"I can be both. I'm very talented." But she let Rafael take her hand, down here where oaths were void and no one was watching, and the amber light lay over their joined fingers like something almost warm. "Fourth landing," she said. "Mind the depth."

"The auditor's offices are in the central spire," Rafael said, nodding toward a tower of black stone at the cavern's heart, its crown lost in the amber haze above. "Every contract routes through there eventually. If Tomas's bond is held anywhere, it's held there." She hesitated. "Sera. When we find him — when this is done — I want you to know that whatever you decide to do about your oath, about going back—"

"Don't." Sera said it gently. "Don't negotiate with a future that hasn't happened yet. It's bad tactics. You taught me that on the pass, remember? *Deal with the mountain in front of you.*"

"I taught you that about an actual mountain."

"Everything's a mountain if you're stubborn enough."

Rafael laughed again, and this time it held together all the way through, and Sera thought: *whatever this place takes from us, it isn't getting that.*

They started down the terrace road toward the spire, two small figures in a city of amber light, and the mountain noted their passage in a book that no one had ever successfully lied to.

* * *

**Rafael**

The spire's ground floor was a waiting room, because of course it was. Even hell, Rafael thought, is mostly bureaucracy with good lighting.

A clerk with kind eyes and ink-stained cuffs took their names, checked them against a ledger with a small frown of concentration, and said, "Auditor Vess will see the new account holder now. The surety may wait."

"I don't wait," Sera said.

"Everyone waits," the clerk said apologetically. "It's the first rule of the court. Even the dead wait, technically. It's just that their cases move faster."

"I'll bet they do," Sera muttered, and sat down on a bench that was either stone or something pretending very hard to be stone, and Rafael squeezed her hand once — *stay, stay, stay* — and followed the clerk up a spiral stair into the amber dark.

The auditor's office was small. That surprised her. She had expected a hall; she got a study, cluttered and warm, with a stove, a dying plant someone was clearly fond of, and a tall thin figure behind a desk piled with contracts, who looked up at her with eyes the exact amber of the city's light.

"Rafael Voss," the auditor said. "Daughter of Maren Voss. Sister of the bonded. Reader of wax." A thin smile. "Yes, we know what you are down here. It's the surface that made you hide it. Sit."

Rafael sat. The chair was comfortable, which she distrusted on principle.

"I've come about my brother's bond."

"I know." The auditor folded her hands. "Before we discuss terms, account holder, there's a matter of the original contract. A question of *whose* soul, precisely, was sold." She drew a single yellowed page from the pile — Rafael's heart slammed against her ribs at the sight of her mother's signature, the smudged name, the wax seal she could read from across the desk if she only reached — and slid it forward.

"You should know," the auditor said, in the tone of someone about to ruin a life with impeccable courtesy, "that the court made an error. Nine years ago. And the mountain does so hate an unbalanced ledger."

Rafael reached for the page. The amber light of the city flickered, once, like a held breath.

"What error?" she asked.

The auditor smiled, and it did not reach her eyes.

"Sit back," she said. "This will take some explaining."
