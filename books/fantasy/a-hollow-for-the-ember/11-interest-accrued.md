## 11. Interest Accrued

The auditor's office smelled of hot wax and cold stone, which struck Rafael as the most honest thing she had encountered since descending into the Undercourt. Everything else down here was designed—amber light piped through veins of quartz in the cavern ceiling, corridors that curved just enough that you never saw where they ended, clerks with faces so smooth they might have been poured rather than born. But the office smelled like a scriptorium. Like home. It made her almost trust the person behind the desk, which she suspected was also a design.

"Sit," said the auditor.

The auditor was small. That was the first surprise. Rafael had imagined something tall and terrible after the gate-clerks' hushed deference, but the creature behind the desk—she decided, provisionally, on *she*—was barely taller than the ledgers stacked around her, gray as old moth wings, with spectacles perched on a nose that seemed assembled from three separate disagreements. She was pouring tea. There were two cups. One of them was for them. The other, Rafael noted, had clearly been poured first and drunk deepest.

"I am Auditor Vesh," the creature said, "of the Third Reconciliation. You are the Voss interest-holders, both of you, though only one of you signed herself in, which is either very brave or very sloppy, and I have learned not to guess which." She pushed a cup toward Sera, who looked at it the way she'd look at a poisoned dart. "Drink or don't. The tea is not contractual. Nothing in this room is contractual until I say it is, and I haven't said anything is yet, so do stop gripping that sword, huntress, you'll scuff my floor and the floor will bill you."

Sera did not stop gripping her sword, but she sat, which by Sera's standards was practically a nap by the fire.

Rafael sat too. Her hands were steady in her lap, which surprised her, because everything under her ribs was moving like a river in thaw. Nine years of hiding. Three weeks of mountains. One signature at the gate, her own name written in the Undercourt's amber ink, and now she was here, in the belly of the debt that had eaten her family before she was born.

"You have my brother," she said.

"We have your brother's *bond*," Auditor Vesh corrected, with the mild precision of someone who corrected people for a living and enjoyed it approximately as much as breathing. "Your brother himself is in Vault Row Nine, drinking terrible soup and complaining about it, which is, frankly, all any of us are doing. Now." She opened a ledger.

Rafael felt it before she saw it—the flare behind her eyes, the ember-gift waking in her fingertips where they rested on the arm of the chair. Old wood. Old wood remembered things. She had spent nine years learning not to flinch when the world spoke to her hands, and this chair had been sat in by ten thousand frightened people, and their fear rose up her arms like water finding a level.

She breathed. She let it pass through her. She kept her face still.

Vesh's spectacles caught the amber light. "Before we proceed," the auditor said, "I am required to inform you that the Court detects gifted persons within its walls. It is one of the few things we do well." A pause, dry as the paper. "You needn't bother hiding it, scribe. It's very loud."

Sera's chair scraped. Rafael put one hand flat on the table between them—not touching Sera, just near—and felt her friend go still.

"How loud?" Rafael asked.

"Loud enough that the gate-clerks drew lots over who would have to file the paperwork." Vesh took off her spectacles, polished them on a gray sleeve, and put them back on. "Which brings us, with admirable economy, to the matter of your mother."

*

Her mother's name was Wren Voss, and she had been dead nine years, and Rafael had spent those nine years building a shrine to her out of half-memories and the smell of cedar smoke. Wren Voss had sung off-key. Wren Voss had cheated at cards and been terrible at it. Wren Voss had gone into the winter coughing and come out of it dead, leaving two children, one carter's license, and a debt no one had ever named aloud but everyone in the family had learned to walk around like a hole in the floor.

"She sold a soul," Rafael said. Her voice came out level, which she was proud of. "To pay what she owed. Everyone knows that. We've known it since—" Since the letter. Since the sealed letter that arrived after the funeral, unsigned, saying only: *the account is not closed.* "She sold my brother's soul. That's why you took him. That's why the hunts found his name."

"Mm," said Vesh.

She turned the ledger around.

Rafael read contracts the way other people read weather—instinctively, totally, the habit of nine years of copying tithes and titles until the language of obligation ran under her skin like a second pulse. She read this one in four seconds, and then she read it again, slower, because the first reading could not possibly be right.

**Bonder: Wren Voss, of Thornfell, carter's widow.**
**Bonded: Wren Voss, same.**
**Collateral: one soul, the bonder's own, held against arrears of [figure illegible—no, not illegible, simply enormous].**
**In event of default: the bond descends to issue, jointly.**

"It's her name twice," Rafael said. "Both lines. She bonded *herself.*"

"Twice," agreed Vesh. "People find that confusing, so let me be plain, which I am told is a failing of mine." She folded her small gray hands. "Your mother owed the Court a great deal of money. Not soul-money at first—coin, ordinary coin, the boring kind, though the sum was extraordinary. Carters on the smuggler roads run debts, and your mother ran larger ones than most, and longer. When she understood she could not pay, she came down here—we have record of the visit, she was offered tea, she declined it—and she asked to pledge something better than money."

"The ember," Rafael whispered. The gift. Their mother had had it too; Rafael had always known, in the wordless way you know a thing you cannot say—that was why Wren had taught Rafael to hide her hands, to wear gloves, to laugh when she touched things and pretended she'd remembered a joke. *It runs in families,* her mother had said once, in the dark. *So the hiding does too.*

"No," said Vesh. "Not the ember. We don't buy embers, whatever the crown's witch-hunters think—they're not currency, they're *faculty*, and faculty doesn't transfer. No." The auditor's voice went quieter, and somehow that quiet was worse than anything loud could have been. "She pledged her soul entire. Held against her debt. To be collected at her death."

"And it was," Rafael said. "She died. It should have been collected. The account should be—"

"Closed. Yes. If she had died owing only herself." Vesh turned the ledger back around and tapped one gray finger beneath the final line, the clause Rafael had read three times and still could not make mean anything else. "*Descends to issue, jointly.* Your mother did not merely default, scribe. She died in arrears—interest having outpaced her collateral, which is common and always a surprise to the deceased. And when a bondholder dies in arrears, the shortfall passes down." She looked up over her spectacles. "You have a sibling. The bond passed to both of you together. Your brother was seized because the crown's hunters, in their infinite wisdom, misread the register—they assumed the bond named him alone, because he was easier to carry down a mountain than a temple scribe. Sloppy. But convenient. For us."

The office was very quiet. Somewhere far above, water dripped through the quartz veins of the ceiling, patient as arithmetic.

"So Tomas is sitting in your vaults," Sera said, and her voice was the flat one, the road-voice, the one Rafael had learned meant she was holding onto something with both hands, "for a debt that isn't even fully his."

"He is sitting in our vaults," Vesh said placidly, "as *collateral*, which is different from being guilty, though I grant the distinction has never once mattered to anyone standing in a vault." She spread her hands. "The bond names two holders jointly. The Court may collect against either. It collected against your brother because your brother was available. This is not justice. I want to be clear about that, huntress, since you people use the word professionally. This is *bookkeeping*. Justice is a rumor started somewhere else."

Rafael's hands had gone cold. "Then unname him. Take him off the bond. If it's joint, split it—one holder, one debt. Let him go and keep the bond on me."

"Ah," said Auditor Vesh.

There was a particular quality to that *ah*. Rafael had heard it before, from temple advocates, from tax assessors, from every species of person whose job was to hold bad news behind their teeth and let it out slowly, professionally, at the moment of maximum compliance.

"That's not how joint bonds work," Rafael said.

"That is not how joint bonds work," Vesh agreed. "A joint bond is one rope with two knots. You cannot untie one knot and leave the rope whole. What you *can* do—" she drew a fresh sheet of paper toward herself, and her pen scratched, and the sound of it went through Rafael like a hook through cloth, "—is what we call an assumption of liability. One holder steps out. Another party assumes the bond entire. The rope stays whole. The knot moves."

"To me," Rafael said. "From Tomas to me."

"If you wish. Today, if you wish. I could have the papers drawn before your tea goes cold, and I notice neither of you has touched your tea." Vesh's eyes, small and bright behind the spectacles, moved between them without cruelty and without mercy, the way a scale balances. "But understand what you're asking, scribe, because I am required to say it plainly and I enjoy saying things plainly. You would not be taking your brother's place at a desk. A soul-bond is not a fine and not a sentence. It is *holding*. Your soul sits in our vaults as surety until the original arrears—which, I will remind you, are your mother's arrears, compounded across eleven years—are discharged in full."

"In full," Rafael repeated. "How much?"

Vesh wrote a number on the paper and turned it around.

Rafael looked at it. She was good with numbers; she copied ledgers, she thought in columns. The number did not fit in any column she owned. It did not fit in any column the temple owned. She suspected, distantly, hysterically, that it might not fit in the valley's combined holdings unless you counted the mountain itself as an asset.

"Eleven years," she said. "Of interest."

"Of interest," Vesh confirmed, almost gently. "Your mother's debt was survivable when she signed. Debts usually are, at signing. That is how we get people to sign." She laced her fingers. "Now. Options. I am required to present options; presenting only one option is called extortion and there are rules." She held up one gray finger. "One: the bond stands against both holders. The Court holds your brother indefinitely and pursues you at its leisure, which, given your recent notoriety, will not be leisurely." A second finger. "Two: assumption of liability. Your brother walks free today. You take the bond entire, and the vaults keep your soul against a number you cannot pay, ever, at which point—" the third finger, and here Vesh paused, and for the first time the little auditor looked tired, anciently tired, tired in a way that had nothing to do with her body, "—at which point the Court collects the collateral itself. Which is the polite phrase for what happens when a soul is forfeit. You have seen the vaults. You know we do not lose things."

"And option three?" Sera asked.

Vesh looked at her. "There is no option three."

"There's always an option three," Sera said. "Roads, ledgers, sieges—there's always a third way nobody's written down yet. That's where all the interesting people live."

For a moment—half a heartbeat, no more—something moved behind Auditor Vesh's spectacles that might, in a less careful face, have been called a smile. Then it was gone, filed away, stamped and shelved.

"There is no option three *on the books*," Vesh said. "And I am, whatever you may have heard, entirely a creature of the books." She began squaring her papers, tap tap tap against the desk, a small gray metronome. "I will give you until tomorrow's bell to decide. The vaults permit family visitation between the fourth and fifth hours; you may tell your brother yourself, or spare him, that is your business. Most people spare him." She rose, and the rising made her seem suddenly much older, as if the desk had been holding up more than ledgers. "One more thing, scribe, and then I'll thank you to take your huntress and your silence and get out of my office."

"Yes?"

"Your mother declined the tea." Vesh gathered the cups, moving with the fussy economy of someone who had cleaned up after ten thousand griefs. "Everyone declines the tea, the first time. They think accepting hospitality means the Court has some claim on them. As if we needed help." She stopped, cups in hand, and did not quite look at Rafael. "Your brother accepts the tea. Every day. He thanks the clerk who brings it. He asks after the clerk's mother." A pause. "I have worked the Third Reconciliation for two hundred and six years, and I am telling you this for free, which you should find alarming: do not assume, when you see him tomorrow, that the man in the vault is waiting to be saved. He may be the only person down here who understands exactly what he's worth. People who understand that are dangerous to courts like ours. I collect them."

Then she showed them out, and the door closed behind them with a sound like a page turning.

*

The corridor outside was amber-lit and warm, and Rafael walked down it because walking was a thing her legs knew how to do while the rest of her reorganized itself around a new shape of ruin.

Not Tomas's soul. Never Tomas's soul. Her mother's own, signed twice in her own hand, and the debt had not been paid by dying—it had been *inherited*, split down the middle like an estate, delivered to her children in equal shares along with the carter's license and the off-key singing and the gift they'd both learned to hide. All those years. All those years Rafael had thought she was carrying a secret, and the whole time the heavier thing had been carried openly, jointly, entered in a ledger beneath a mountain, accruing.

*Rafa.* Tomas's voice, in memory, laughing on the cart bench. *You count everything twice. Who taught you that?*

*Mama,* she had said. *Mama taught me that.*

She stopped walking. Sera stopped a step later, turned, and stood there in the amber light with her hand nowhere near her sword, which was how Rafael knew she was frightened.

"It's my name," Rafael said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "It was always my name on that contract, next to his. Half and half. She didn't sell him. She sold herself and left us the bill, both of us, and they just—" she laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, nothing like a laugh, "—they just picked him up because he was *lighter.* Because he was on the roads where the hunters could reach. Nine years I hid my hands from the world and the world already had my name in a book under a mountain, and charged *interest* on it—"

"Rafael."

"—and if I take the bond he walks, and if I don't he rots, and either way the number is the same, the number is a *mountain*, Sera, nobody pays that, nobody pays that in a lifetime, they'd be collecting on my—"

"Rafael." Sera's hands were on her shoulders now, solid, warm, immovable as the passes. "Breathe. Then listen. In that order."

Rafael breathed. The corridor hummed faintly with distant clerical industry, the vast patient machinery of the Court grinding through its eternal arithmetic.

"Now listen," Sera said quietly. "That creature in there—Vesh—she told us more than she was required to. You caught it?"

Rafael blinked. "She said there was no option three."

"She said there was no option three *on the books*." Sera's eyes were bright and hard, hunter's eyes doing what hunter's eyes did best, which was find the gap in the hedge. "'I am entirely a creature of the books.' She said it like a woman laying down a map and pointing. Rafael—a court this old, this big, funding armies of hunters off forged soul-debts—" she lowered her voice further, until it was barely louder than the dripping water, "—a court like that doesn't keep one ledger. It keeps hundreds. Contracts, copies, amendments, archives. And somewhere in all that paper is the proof of what they've done, and maybe—" her grip tightened on Rafael's shoulders, "—maybe more than proof. Mistakes. Old entries. Precedents. A way through that isn't signing your life into a vault."

"The archives would be guarded."

"Everything's guarded. I've been the guard. Guards sleep." Sera almost smiled, and it transformed her face so completely that Rafael forgot, for a second, about the number, about the mountain of debt, about everything except the fact that she was falling in love with a woman who planned burglaries the way other people said prayers. "Fourth hour to fifth, visitation. That's your window with Tomas. Mine's the archive wing—I watched the clerks come and go at the gate; they badge through a corridor on the lower ring, west side, past the tea kitchen."

"You memorized the layout of a court you'd never seen before, from a doorway."

"I was a huntress for eleven years. Knowing where things are is the whole job." Sera stepped back, and the amber light caught the scar at her jaw, and her expression settled into something steadier and more frightened than confidence—into resolve, which is confidence's braver cousin. "Go see your brother. Tell him whatever you decide to tell him. And Rafael—"

"Yes?"

"When you touch things in that vault, and they talk to you—" Sera's voice dropped all the way down to the truth, "—listen hard. Down here, everything remembers. Maybe even the ledgers do. Maybe especially."

She turned and walked away down the amber corridor, toward the western ring, toward the paper heart of the Court, and Rafael stood a moment longer in the dripping light with her mother's debt humming in her blood like a struck bell.

Then she turned the other way, toward Vault Row Nine, toward terrible soup and her brother and the tea he accepted from his jailers, and went to find out what a man was worth when he already knew.
