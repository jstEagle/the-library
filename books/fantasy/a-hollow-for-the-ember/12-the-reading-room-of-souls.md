## 12. The Reading Room of Souls

The Reading Room of Souls smelled like a church that had been quietly robbing people for three hundred years.

Sera Ilvane had been inside the Undercourt for six days now, and she had learned to stop flinching at the smell. Amber and old vellum, mostly, with an undertone of hot stone—the whole cavern city was lit by veins of amber running through the rock, glowing a soft honeyed gold that made everything look warmer than it was. That was the trick of the place, she'd decided. Everything down here looked kind. The clerks smiled. The tea was good. The chairs were comfortable. And every soul in the vaults below had been signed away on paper that looked exactly as gentle.

She sat at a reading desk near the back of the contract archive, third gallery down, where the light from the amber veins came through thin enough that her eyes didn't ache by evening. Around her, clerks in dove-grey robes moved between the shelves with the unhurried grace of people who had never once been in trouble. Nobody questioned her presence. Her truce-papers said *crown business*, and crown business opened doors the way a crowbar did—loudly, but effectively.

What the papers did not say—and what she had not told Rafael, because telling Rafael would have meant admitting it out loud—was that she had come into this archive intending to find nothing. She had told herself a very tidy story on the walk down: that she would look at Tomas Voss's contract, confirm it was in order, and put the whole ugly suspicion to rest. That was what a huntress did. She checked. She verified. She trusted the record, because the record was the law made legible, and if you couldn't trust the record then there was no floor under any of it.

Six days ago she had still believed there was a floor.

* * *

It had started small, the way rot always did.

On the second day, pulling contracts for cross-reference, she had noticed that the Undercourt's intake ledgers used two different hands for the same clerk's name. Not two different spellings—two different *hands*. A careful eye could see it: the flourished *Merrin Doss* on the older volumes, and a slightly thinner, more hurried *Merrin Doss* beginning eleven years ago, continuing to the present day. Clerks retired. Hands changed. There were forty innocent explanations, and Sera had written most of them down in her notebook like a woman building a wall out of reasonable thoughts.

Then she had pulled the bond-registry for the northern circuit—the same circuit her own company hunted—and found that Merrin Doss's hurried hand had witnessed one hundred and sixty-two soul-contracts in nine years. Every single one belonging to a person later seized under the gifted-criminal statutes. One hundred and sixty-two. Out of the entire northern circuit, with its thousands of souls and its dozens of clerks, one man's pen had touched every contract the hunts depended on.

She had sat with that number for a long time, in the amber light, listening to the distant drip of water somewhere deep in the mountain.

Clerks specialized. It happened. A clerk who handled gifted-bonds developed expertise, and expertise drew work. She had written that down too. Her notebook was becoming a museum of innocent explanations, each one pinned and labeled, and beneath them all, growing louder every day, the thing she refused to write: *somebody built this.*

* * *

"You're back," said the archivist, when she presented her truce-papers on the sixth morning. His name was Pellam Ostry, and he had the round, contented face of a man who had spent his life among things more valuable than himself and had made his peace with it. "Third gallery again? You know, most crown visitors want the vaults. The holding floors. They want to *see*." He said *see* the way other people said *gawk*.

"Contracts," Sera said. "I'm verifying bonds against seizure records."

"Bless you." He shuddered delicately. "Fourteen years I've kept these shelves, and do you know how many people have ever asked to verify rather than collect?" He held up one finger. "One. And he turned out to be auditing us, so we don't count him fondly."

"Then I'll be the first you count fondly."

"I'll have a plaque made." He shuffled off toward the deep stacks, robes whispering, and Sera watched him go with her heart doing something complicated in her chest. Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape: Pellam Ostry was kind. He brought her tea without being asked. He had shown her, unprompted, which stools didn't wobble. And either he was complicit in whatever lived in these shelves, or he was a fool, or—

Or the forgery was so far above his head that fourteen years of honest shelving had never brushed its hem. That possibility frightened her most of all, because it meant the rot wasn't a bad clerk. It was a *structure*. Something with walls and funding and a name on a door somewhere in the capital.

She took her stool. She pulled the first box of the morning. And she began, page by page, to steal.

* * *

Not steal in the way the word usually meant. She hadn't smuggled out a single leaf—though she'd planned three routes for doing so, because a huntress who couldn't plan exits was just a tourist with a sword. What she stole was slower and heavier: copies. Her own hand reproducing theirs, line for line, seal for seal, date for date. Every copied page felt like lifting a stone off something alive underneath.

The method, once she saw it, was almost beautiful in its economy. That was the worst part. She kept wanting to admire it the way you admire a well-set snare even while standing in it.

Here was how it worked. The crown funded the witch-hunts—that was public knowledge; companies ate, horses ate, informants ate. But the crown's coffers had run thin for over a decade, and open taxation for a religious purge played badly in the lowland towns. So instead: soul-contracts. When a person was seized as gifted, their bond—their soul, held as collateral—was assessed at value by the Undercourt. The court extended credit against that collateral. The credit went to the crown. The hunts paid for themselves out of the souls they captured, and the ledger balanced, and everyone up-chain got paid, and nobody had to say out loud that the crown had begun eating its own people.

Except the souls weren't real debts. That was the crack she'd found, and once she'd found it she could not stop prying.

She had pulled twenty contracts at random from the northern circuit and traced each one backward to its origin. A debt required an original instrument—a signature, a consideration, something given and something owed. Eleven of the twenty had none. Oh, there were pages. Dates, witnesses, the hurried hand of Merrin Doss, wax seals in the Undercourt's own amber. But the underlying instruments referenced earlier documents that did not exist in any archive she could reach, or cited debts settled decades before the seizure date, or—in four cases—bore signatures of people who were provably dead before the ink was dry. One contract, for a carter named Aldous Wren, listed as its originating creditor a woman whose death-record Sera had personally filed eight years ago, because she'd been the one who found the body.

Dead creditors collecting live souls. The dead were very reliable debtors that way. They never disputed the account.

Sera copied that one out twice, her pen pressing hard enough to score the paper beneath.

* * *

The gentle humor of the place, if there was any, was that the forgery was *bad*. Not everywhere—not in the early years—but lately, in the last three or four volumes, it had grown lazy. Seals slightly wrong in their lettering. Marginal notations in a modern hand dressed up in antique phrasing. Once, gloriously, a contract dated the twelfth of Deepmonth in a year when, as any child raised in the north knew, Deepmonth had only thirty days because the census reform had cut one. Somebody in the capital had forged a document and forgotten to check the calendar.

Sera had laughed out loud at that one—a short, ugly sound in the quiet of the archive—and a passing clerk had glanced over, and she had converted the laugh into a cough with the smoothness of long practice, and the clerk had nodded sympathetically, as if to say *yes, the dust is terrible down here.*

But the laziness told her something important. Forgeries get lazy when nobody checks. Nobody checked because checking was her job—the huntresses', the captains', the whole apparatus of verification she had served for eleven years—and the apparatus had been reading these documents the way she herself had read them for a decade: looking for permission, not truth. You read a contract to confirm you were allowed to take the person. You did not read it to ask whether it should exist. The paperwork was sufficient. Her captain had said those exact words about Tomas Voss, standing in the snow, and she had swallowed them, and here they were again, rising back up out of six days of copying like drowned things finally surfacing.

*The paperwork is sufficient.* Four hundred pages of evidence that it never had been.

By midday her eyes burned and her hand ached and she had filled nineteen sheets, front and back, in a cipher of her own devising—hunters' field-code, the one they used for patrol reports, dull and unremarkable to any eye trained only for crowns and courts. She hid the sheets in the lining of her coat, sewn there on the first night with thread picked out of her own cuff, and the weight of them against her ribs was the strangest feeling she had ever carried: lighter than a sword, heavier than sin.

* * *

Pellam brought her tea at the afternoon bell, as he always did, and stood watching her shelve the day's boxes with an expression she couldn't parse.

"You copy everything," he observed. "Most readers excerpt. You copy."

"Habit." She kept her voice bored. "Field training. If it matters, write it twice."

"Mm." He set down the tea. Then, without any change of tone at all, he said: "You know the shelves are warded. Against removal, I mean. Ink remembers being moved. Whole leaves taken from this room arrive wherever they're going already screaming."

Sera went very still, teacup halfway to her mouth.

"But copies," Pellam continued, gazing mildly at the amber-lit shelves, "copies are just your own handwriting. Your own paper. Your own thought, really, once it's passed through you. The wards can't tell a stolen thing from a remembered one." He straightened a shelf-edge that did not need straightening. "Fourteen years. You notice things. Which boxes get pulled twice. Which names make visitors' hands shake." He turned, and his round face was suddenly much older than it had been at breakfast. "Aldous Wren's granddaughter comes every spring. Leaves flowers at the gate, though he's not in the public vaults. She thinks he's in the deep holdings. She signs the visitor book. I've seen her sign it nine times."

He left the tea and walked away, and Sera sat in the amber light with her heart hammering, trying to decide whether she had just been threatened or blessed.

She decided, after a while, that it was both, and that in the Undercourt those had probably always been the same document.

* * *

She worked until the evening bell, and then some—clerks drifted home, the archive emptied, and the last hour belonged to her alone with the shelves and the slow pulse of the amber veins. She saved the worst for last. She had known where it was since the second day, and had circled it like a tongue finding a broken tooth: the master volume for the current fiscal year, the one that reconciled bond-assessments against crown disbursements. The volume that tied the whole machine together in one column of numbers.

It took her an hour to find the pattern, and when she found it she had to put the pen down and press both palms flat against the desk and breathe, because the numbers were so clean. Disbursements to the northern hunt companies, quarter by quarter, matching—almost exactly, within a rounding error that was itself suspicious in its tidiness—the assessed values of bonded souls seized in the corresponding period. The hunts cost precisely what the souls yielded. Year after year. Circuit after circuit. As if someone, somewhere, set the quota by the budget instead of the budget by the quota.

Which meant the seizures weren't failing to find the gifted. The seizures were *fulfilling orders*. Somewhere upstream, a figure had decided how much revenue the year required, and the hunts had gone out and collected it, and the collection had faces—Tomas Voss's face among them, a carter with no ember at all, seized on paperwork that was sufficient.

Sera copied the reconciliation table in full. Her hand did not shake until the final line, and then it shook, and she let it, because some things deserved a shaking hand.

When she finished, she sat for a moment in the humming gold light and thought about her oath. She had sworn it at seventeen, kneeling on cold flagstones, and the words had felt like armor going on: *to uphold the register, to honor the record, to serve the law of the crown in all things written and sealed.* Eleven years she had kept it. And now here she sat, in the belly of the court itself, committing theft by lamplight—except that the oath had never actually said *the record as it stands*. It said *honor the record*. And a forged thing was not a record. It was a wound wearing a record's clothes. Honoring the register might mean exactly this: sitting in the dark with aching fingers, stealing the truth back one page at a time.

Duty inverted. She turned the phrase over like a coin. It was still duty. It had just been flipped to its other face, and the other face had been looking at her the whole time, waiting for her to turn it.

She sewed the last sheets into her coat lining, blew out her lamp, and went to find Rafael.

* * *

She found her in the guest cells—or the guest lodgings, as the Undercourt insisted on calling them, though the doors locked from the outside and the windows were painted on. Rafael sat cross-legged on the bed with her coat off, working at something in her lap, and she looked up when Sera came in with the particular alertness of a person who had spent nine years hiding and had never once stopped.

"You've been crying," Rafael said. Not accusing. Just seeing, the way she always saw, in that unnerving ember-gifted way that had nothing to do with her gift and everything to do with attention.

"The dust," Sera said. "It's terrible down here."

"Mm." Rafael set aside whatever she'd been working on. "And the dust made you copy four hundred pages of forged soul-contracts into your coat?"

Sera stared at her. "How—"

"You sew badly and you hum when you concentrate. You've been humming in the archive corridor for six days. Also you came in tonight walking like your ribs weigh forty pounds." Rafael's mouth twitched. "Also I read the archivist's memory off the tea tray yesterday, but mostly it was the humming."

"You—" Sera pinched the bridge of her nose. "The tea tray."

"He handles it. Things he touches keep echoes. I'm sorry. I was worried about you, and the tray was right there." She said it without apology, which was somehow worse, and then her expression shifted, the humor draining into something steadier and more afraid. "Tell me."

So Sera sat down on the painted-window side of the cell and told her. All of it. The two hands of Merrin Doss. The dead creditors. The calendar with its missing day. The reconciliation table with its beautiful, obscene arithmetic. She talked for a long time, and Rafael listened without interrupting, which was a skill Sera had never mastered and privately ranked among the world's great virtues.

When she finished, Rafael was quiet for a moment. Then she said, softly: "My mother's contract. The one with her name smudged. It'll be in there somewhere, won't it. In your copies."

"It might be. I couldn't search for it directly without explaining why I cared." Sera hesitated. "But the pattern fits. A debt that transferred. A name smudged where a name should be legible. They smudge the ones that are real, I think—the forged ones they leave crisp, because nobody's ever going to look closely at a lie. It's the true debts they blur."

"That's horrible," Rafael said, "and it makes perfect sense," and she laughed, a small cracked sound, and rubbed her face with both hands. "We came down here to free my brother and we're going to burn down the whole court instead."

"We came down here to free your brother," Sera corrected. "The burning is incidental."

"Sera Ilvane, huntress of the crown, describing treason as incidental."

"Describing *housekeeping* as incidental." She stood, and paced once, because her body needed to move when her mind was full. "There's a problem, though. The evidence is half of it. Copies prove the forgery exists, but not who ordered it, and the court will bury us in procedure before we can climb the chain. We need leverage the court itself respects." She stopped pacing. "And separately—tomorrow I want to see the holding vaults. Your brother's assessment hearing is in three days. After that he gets transferred deeper, into the collateral holdings, and no truce-paper in the world reaches that far down."

Rafael looked up at her, and in the amber light leaking under the door her eyes caught it and held it, faintly, the way certain stones did. "Then tomorrow," she said, "we go down together. You bring your stolen pages. I'll bring mine."

"Yours?"

Rafael smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "I've been reading the trays, Sera. The locks. The ledgers they carry past our door." She patted the bed beside her, where the something-she'd-been-working-on lay hidden under her coat. "The Undercourt keeps excellent records of everything except what its own clerks touch. And clerks touch *everything*."

Outside the painted window, deep in the mountain, something vast shifted in its sleep, and the amber veins flickered once, like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.
