## 13. Tomas, Unlit

The holding vaults lay below the amber galleries, down a stair that had been worn into a smooth trough by centuries of clerks' feet—clerks who came down carrying souls and went back up carrying receipts. Rafael had counted the steps on her third descent and stopped counting after two hundred, because the number had begun to feel like a debt of its own.

She wore the gray smock of an Undercourt records assistant now. It had taken Sera four days and one genuinely alarming amount of forgery to produce the badge that hung at her throat—a little wax seal stamped with the deep court's sigil, warm to the touch in a way that made Rafael's skin crawl. She did not touch it with bare fingers if she could help it. Everything down here was warm. Everything down here remembered.

"Assistant Voss," said the door-warden at the bottom of the stair, without looking up from his tally-slate. He was a broad, gray-faced man whose eyes had the particular flatness of someone who had worked underground so long that daylight had become a rumor.

"Assistant Voss," Rafael agreed. "Tray retrieval for the autumn reconciliation."

"The autumn reconciliation was signed off in spring."

"And reopened this morning." She let a note of weary officiousness creep into her voice—the tone she'd spent nine years perfecting in the Emberhold scriptorium, the tone that said *I am as bored by this as you are, but the ledgers demand it.* "You know how auditors are. If you'd like to take it up with them—"

The warden's slate-hand paused mid-tally. He looked at her then, properly, and Rafael saw him do the arithmetic every bureaucrat does: the cost of arguing versus the cost of compliance. Compliance won, as it always did. He waved her through the iron gate with a grunt, and the gate swung open on hinges that sang a low amber note, like a struck glass.

*Everything remembers,* she thought again, and stepped through.

* * *

The vaults themselves were not what she had expected. She had imagined cells, chains, screaming—something out of the fire-sermons the Emberhold preached about the court beneath Thornfell. Instead there were shelves. Endless shelves, floor to ceiling, receding into amber-lit dark, and on each shelf rows upon rows of small clay vessels, each one sealed with wax, each one labeled in the Undercourt's beautiful, terrible hand.

Souls, shelved like jars of preserves.

Rafael stood in the aisle for a long moment, her breath loud in her own ears, and felt the vessels feel *her*. Not seeing—they had no eyes—but aware, the way a sleeping room is aware when someone enters it. A faint pressure against her gift, hundreds of tiny murmurs, like snow settling on a roof.

She found the index plaque at the end of the first aisle and ran her thumb along its edge, careful, careful, only the metal, never the wax.

VOSS, T. — CARTER — BOND DEFAULTED (JOINT) — SHELF 114, ROW 3, TRAY 9.

Shelf 114 was deep in the third gallery, past vessels whose labels she made herself not read, because some of the names were ones she knew from Thornfell's churchyard, and she could not afford to grieve yet. Grief was a luxury for people who weren't mid-theft.

Row 3, tray 9 sat at shoulder height, and it was smaller than she'd imagined. A clay vessel no bigger than a loaf of bread, its wax seal unbroken, its label neat and final. Through the clay's thin places she could see a glow—not bright, not blazing, but steady. An ember banked in a hearth, waiting out the night.

*Tomas.*

Her hands shook. Nine years of copying other people's debts in a beautiful hand, nine years of hiding what her fingers could do, and here at last was the thing all that hiding had been for: a jar on a shelf, glowing faintly, with her brother's name on it.

She reached for it—

"You're late," said a voice behind her. "I've been waiting since Tuesday."

Rafael spun, tray clutched to her chest, heart slamming. And there, sitting on the floor at the end of the row with his back against the shelf and his legs stretched out like a man waiting for a coach that runs slow, was Tomas Voss.

Not a vessel. Not a dimmed thing. Her brother, whole and solid and grinning up at her, thinner than she remembered and dressed in the plain gray of a bonded debtor, but *Tomas*, with hay still somehow in his hair despite everything, because apparently even the Undercourt couldn't keep hay out of Tomas Voss's hair.

"Tomas—" Her voice cracked clean in half. She crossed the aisle in three strides and dropped to her knees and grabbed his shoulders, his face, his ears, checking him the way you check a child pulled from a creek, and he let her, laughing wetly, gripping her arms back just as hard.

"Ow. Careful. They don't feed you much down here, I bruise easy."

"You're—you're *here*. You're whole. The tray said—"

"The tray says my ember's held in bond. It doesn't say they took it out and looked at it." He tapped his own chest, over his heart. "Still lit in here. Dimmer, maybe. Like a bad winter. But mine." His grin faded a degree. "How did you get down here, Rafe? Tell me you didn't sign anything."

"I'm a records assistant," she said, with dignity, still holding his face. "I have a badge."

"A forged badge."

"A *provisionally authentic* badge."

He laughed, and then the laugh turned into something else, and he pulled her in, and they stayed like that a while, two children of Marisol Voss kneeling on the cold floor of the court beneath the mountain, holding on. Somewhere far above, the amber veins flickered. Somewhere farther, Sera Ilvane was keeping watch at their borrowed rooms, pretending she wasn't worried, which was how Sera worried best.

When Rafael finally pulled back, she kept her hands on his shoulders and studied him in the amber light. Thinner. Paler. There were new lines around his eyes that hadn't been there at midsummer.

"How long have you known?" she asked quietly.

He didn't pretend not to understand. That was one of the thousand things she loved about him; Tomas had never once in his life wasted time pretending.

"Since you were fourteen," he said. "The winter the Kellam coin came through the house. You picked it up off the table to count it and went white as whey and put it down like it bit you. Then you told me it was nothing, and you washed your hands twice, and you didn't eat your supper." He shrugged. "I'm a carter, Rafe, not a scholar, but I can read a road. You'd touched something and it had talked to you. I'd heard the old stories. Ember-gift. Our mother's line, the way the story goes."

"And you never said."

"What was I going to say? 'Hello, sister, I've noticed you have the thing that gets people burned'?" He shook his head. "No. What I did was start taking the long routes. The quiet routes. Kept your name off any manifest that went near a hunter's desk. Paid off every debt early, every single one, because I figured the less paper with our name on it, the safer you'd be." He smiled ruefully. "Turns out the paper that mattered was Mother's, and none of us knew it existed. So much for carting carefully."

Rafael's throat was tight. "Nine years. You carried that alone."

"We carried it together. You just didn't know I was carrying my half." He nudged her knee with his. "That's what family is, isn't it? Two people hauling the same load on different roads."

She laughed, or something close to it, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and then—because she was her mother's daughter, and because the tray was heavy against her ribs, and because Tomas had always been able to read *her* roads too—she watched his gaze drop to the shape under her coat, and watched him go very still.

"That's my tray," he said. Not a question.

"It's a tray with your name on it."

"Rafael."

"It's a *provisional* tray. For reconciliation purposes."

"Rafael." He said her name the way he said it when she was twelve and trying to talk him into letting her hold the reins on the ice-grade. He got to his feet, unhurried, and took the tray gently out of her arms before she could stop him, and set it on the shelf, and put both his big carter's hands on either side of her face. "Look at me. Whatever you're planning—and I know you're planning, you have your planning face, you've had your planning face since the day the hunters came—whatever it is, it ends with you signing something. Doesn't it."

She said nothing.

"Doesn't it."

"There's a clause," she said, very fast, the words tumbling out like water from a kicked-over bucket. "Joint bond, defaulted. One soul frees another. The auditor explained it—it's arithmetic, Tomas, it's just arithmetic, the court doesn't care *whose* ember covers the debt, only that it's covered, and mine is—mine is worth more than yours, actually, that's the absurd part, they value gifted embers higher, so it would more than cover—"

"*Stop.*"

She stopped. Not because he shouted—he hadn't—but because his voice had gone quiet in the specific way that meant the opposite of calm, the way it had gone quiet the day the tax-wagon backed over his dog when they were children.

"You are not trading yourself for me," Tomas said. "I will not allow it."

"You won't *allow*—Tomas, you're in a *jar*. Metaphorically. Your soul is literally on a shelf. You are in no position to allow or disallow anything, and I am standing here with a forged badge and a plan, and the plan is *arithmetic*—"

"The plan is you going hollow." His voice cracked on it. "I've seen what they do to the traded ones. There's a man two galleries over, Harl, used to be a ferryman on the Coldwater. Traded his ember to cover his brother's bond eight years ago. You know what's left? A kind, empty, pleasant man who smiles at everyone and can't remember his mother's face. He thanked me yesterday for a kindness I never did him, because he thanks everyone, because there's nothing *in* him to do otherwise." Tomas's hands tightened on her face. "That's the trade, Rafe. Not arithmetic. That's you, smiling at strangers, gone. And I'd rather sit in this jar until the mountain falls than walk out of here knowing what I walked out *on*."

"Tomas—"

"No. Listen to me. My turn to carry the load." He breathed, steadying himself, and when he spoke again his voice was softer, and worse. "You think I don't know what you've given already? Nine years folding yourself small in that scriptorium. Hiding the best thing about you in a city full of people who'd burn you for it. I watched you do it every festival-day, every market-day, flinching away from every handshake, wearing gloves in summer. You spent your whole life making yourself invisible so the world wouldn't notice what you are." His eyes were shining. "And now you want to make yourself *nothing* so I can be visible. No. No, Rafe. I refuse. Find another way. Steal me out. Forge the ledger—I know you've been learning how, don't look surprised, Sera talks in her sleep, or rather she mutters about wax seals in her sleep, which is somehow worse. Burn the vault down. Anything. Anything but that."

Rafael stood in the amber light with her brother's hands on her face and felt the truth of it settle over her like snowfall: that he had known, all along, and kept her secret, and paid his debts early, and taken the long roads, all so that she could stay whole. That the love she had thought she was sneaking in here to spend had already been spent on her, year after year, quietly, without receipt.

"I can't lose you," she whispered.

"You won't. But I'll be damned—" his voice broke, and he laughed at himself breaking, "—I'll be absolutely damned if I let you lose you instead. We'll find another way. Together. That's the family trade, isn't it? Same load, different roads?"

For a moment—one long, treacherous moment—she almost believed they could. Almost believed there was a road through this that didn't run over one of them.

Then the amber veins overhead flickered, and somewhere in the deep galleries a bell began to toll—three notes, pause, three notes—and the door-warden's voice echoed down the stairwell, calling a name that was not hers:

"Bond-holder Voss! Bond-holder Voss, present yourself! The auditor requires you at the reckoning-desk!"

Tomas went pale. "The reckoning-desk. That's where they finalize transfers." He gripped her wrists. "Rafe. What did you sign?"

"I didn't sign anything," she said—which was true, today—and then, because lying to Tomas had never once worked in thirty years: "*Yet.*"

The bell tolled again. Three notes. Pause. Three notes. And from the direction of the reckoning-desk, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a second set of footsteps descending the stairs—quick, light, angry, and wearing boots that Rafael had last heard walking beside her through every pass between Thornfell and the deep court's gate.

Sera was coming down. And Sera only came down like that when she had discovered something.

* * *

They reached the mouth of the third gallery in time to see her take the last steps two at a time, huntress-gray under the clerk's smock she'd stolen for the occasion, her braid coming loose, her face the color of old ash.

"Sera?" Rafael said. "What is it? What's wrong?"

Sera stopped in front of them, breathing hard, and pressed a folded page into Rafael's hands—the real paper, the court's own stock, watermarked with the deep seal. Her voice, when it came, was flat in the way Rafael had learned meant the opposite of calm, the same way Tomas's went quiet.

"It's the auditor's schedule," she said. "The transfer appointments. I pulled it off her desk while she was at the reckoning-desk, because I wanted to see how much time we had before your appointment." She swallowed. "Rafael. There are two names on tomorrow's slate. Yours and Tomas's. Joint transfer, both embers, full satisfaction of the Marisol Voss account."

"But that's—that's not possible, the auditor said one soul frees one—"

"She said one soul frees one," Sera agreed. "But that's not what the original contract says. I checked it against the archive copy." Her eyes came up, and they were burning. "The contract says *both*. It has said *both* from the beginning. Your mother didn't sell one soul to cover her debt, Rafael. She sold two. And the auditor has been waiting for you to volunteer the second one all along."

Behind them, on shelf 114, row 3, tray 9, Tomas's ember flickered once—like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.
