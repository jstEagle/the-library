## 14. What Duty Owes

The truce banner arrived on the third morning of the storm, which Sera considered a very typical piece of timing for the crown: never when the roads were passable, always when the tea was hot.

She saw the rider first—a single figure in hunt-grey picking down the switchbacks from the north col, a white pennant snapping from his lance like a handkerchief waved by a nervous man. Behind him, at a respectful distance that was anything but respectful, came six more.

"Company," she said, without looking up from the arrow shaft she was fletching.

Rafael looked up from her ledger-work at the far table—because of course Rafael Voss did ledger-work even in a mountain waystation during a blizzard, because there were apparently laws deeper than the crown's, and one of them was that idle hands offended her personally. "How many?"

"Seven. Grey cloaks. White flag." Sera tested the fletching against her cheek. "Truce colors."

"Do you trust them?"

"No."

"Good," said Tomas from the loft, where he'd been mending harness all morning and pretending not to eavesdrop, an activity at which he was both diligent and terrible. "Because I've met truces before. A truce is just an ambush that's had time to dress."

"Tomas," Rafael said mildly.

"I'm allowed opinions. I'm collateral."

"You're *resting*," Rafael corrected, "and you're going to stay up there where the draft can't find you." But Sera caught the look that passed between brother and sister—the quick, wordless arithmetic of it—and understood that they had already done this sum together, some night when they thought she slept. If the hunters came, Rafael would go out first. Tomas would argue. Neither would win. It was the kind of family choreography you could only learn by growing up inside it, and it made something ache in Sera's chest that she chose, for the moment, to ignore.

She went to the door and stood watching the riders come.

Seven grey cloaks. She knew five of them by name. The sixth was new—a young man with a soft chin who held his lance like it might go off. And the seventh rode at the front with his collar turned up against the snow and his jaw set in the particular way that meant Captain Aldric Vane had been rehearsing a speech the whole way down the mountain.

*Of course,* Sera thought. *Of course it's him.*

Aldric had taught her the reading of rights. Aldric had signed her first commission. Aldric had once carried her, feverish, eleven miles through the Ashvale marshes after a bog-spider got her boot, and had complained about the weight the entire way in a voice gentle as a lullaby. He was the best man she had ever served under, which was precisely why what she was about to do was going to hurt like a pulled tooth.

He reined up ten paces from the door and dismounted, stamping snow off his boots like a man arriving at a neighbor's supper. That was Aldric. He would make courtesy do the work of a siege engine.

"Sera Ilvane." His breath smoked. "You look well."

"I look frozen, sir. There's a difference."

"There usually is." He glanced past her at the doorway, at Rafael's shadow behind the curtain of hanging herbs, at the smoke rising straight and honest from the chimney. "May we come in? Truce terms. Unarmed." He lifted his hands, palms out, to show them empty—which meant nothing, since every hunter Sera had ever known kept at least three weapons she couldn't see, herself included.

"The truce holds while you're under this roof," Sera said. "My roof now. I'll want your blades at the door."

Aldric's eyebrows rose a fraction. Not offense—interest. *Look how she's grown,* the eyebrows said. *Look what the mountain has done to her.*

They surrendered steel at the threshold, seven knives and four swords and a truly alarming number of concealed hooks and wires, until the pile by the door looked like a blacksmith's divorce. The soft-chinned young one hesitated over a boot-knife and Sera simply looked at him until he added it to the heap with the expression of a boy surrendering a sweet to his grandmother.

Then they came in, dripping, and filled the little waystation the way a rockslide fills a streambed.

* * *

It should be recorded, for the sake of honesty, that Aldric Vane accepted tea.

That was the worst part. He sat at Rafael's table with his gauntlets off and his scarred hands wrapped around a clay cup, and he complimented the tea, and he asked after the passes, and he told a story about Corporal Bren getting thrown by his own horse twice in one afternoon, and everyone laughed—even Rafael, carefully, even Tomas, from the stairs where he'd come down despite direct orders because no power in heaven or the Undercourt could keep that man in a loft while strangers drank his sister's tea.

Sera watched her old captain perform warmth the way she'd once watched him perform justice: flawlessly, and with nothing underneath.

When the cups were empty, he set his down, turned it handle toward himself—a habit, a ritual, the small ceremony that preceded every formal address—and began.

"Sera Ilvane. By right of my commission and the law of the crown, you stand accused of desertion in the field, aiding a fugitive, theft of court property—" here his eyes flicked, briefly, to Rafael, cataloguing her the way he'd catalogue any asset, "—and obstruction of a lawful seizure. Eleven counts. Some of them carry the rope."

"I know the counts," Sera said. "I wrote half of them myself, before I understood them."

"And yet." Aldric leaned back. Outside, the wind threw a fistful of snow against the shutters, and in the silence after, he smiled. It was a good smile. It had opened doors for him for twenty years. "And yet here you sit, warm, fed, unbound, while your comrades freeze in garrisons and your name rots in the register. The court is generous, Sera. The court is offering you a road home."

"We're at the part," Tomas murmured to Rafael, "where the trap introduces itself politely."

"Hush," said Rafael, who was leaning forward slightly, listening with her whole body, and Sera realized with a small cold shock that Rafael wasn't listening to the words at all. She was watching Aldric's hands. Watching the table. Watching everything but his face, the way a person watches when they can read what things remember.

Sera filed that away and turned back to her captain. "What road?"

"Full reinstatement. Rank restored, plus a step—you'd make senior huntress by spring, and I'll say plainly that you've earned it twice over. Amnesty for the Voss woman." He nodded courteously at Rafael, who did not nod back. "Her record burns clean. No seizure, no bond, no questions. She walks out of this valley a free scribe with a spotless ledger, if such a thing exists." Another smile, smaller, almost fond. "And the boy goes home to his cart and his debts and his ordinary, ungifted life. Everyone under this roof sleeps soundly by midwinter."

"And the price," Sera said.

Aldric spread his hands. "The price is so small it embarrasses me to name it."

"Name it anyway."

"You surrender the documents you took from the Undercourt archive. All of them. And you give me your oath—your hunter's oath, the one you swore on the white banner—that no copy exists, that no word of what you found leaves this valley, and that the matter of the soul-contracts is closed." He said it smoothly, easily, a man reciting terms he'd been given rather than terms he'd chosen. "One bundle of paper, Sera. Against three lives and a career."

The fire popped. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere in the loft a mule shifted its weight and sighed with the deep moral disappointment only mules can achieve.

Sera looked at her captain for a long moment.

"Aldric," she said, "do you know what those papers say?"

"I know what the court says they say. Forgeries. Fabrications. Sedition dressed up as bookkeeping."

"They're not forged." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, because that was the cruelty of it—he deserved gentleness, this man who had carried her through a marsh. "I copied them myself, page by page, in the Reading Room, with the court's own ink. The crown has been manufacturing soul-debts for eleven years. Whole villages, Aldric. Bonded on paper that was written *after* the people died, backdated, signed by clerks who don't exist. The witch-hunts aren't funded by the treasury. They're funded by the souls we seize. We're not hunting criminals. We're harvesting collateral." She let that sit. "You've been reading rights to people whose rights were sold before they were born. So have I. That's what the papers say."

Something moved behind Aldric's eyes. She saw it—she wanted desperately to believe she saw it—some flicker of the man who had carried her through the marsh. Then it was gone, smoothed over by twenty years of discipline, and he shook his head slowly.

"I read the same reports you did, once. When I was young. I had questions too." He said it kindly, terribly kindly, the way you speak to someone grieving. "And then I learned what questions cost. Sera. Listen to me. You are standing in a snowdrift at the edge of the map, in love with a scribe who reads memories out of wax, holding papers that could bring down a ministry. You cannot win this. Nobody wins this. The most you can do is choose which pieces survive the board." He tapped the table, softly, once. "Save yourself. Save them. Bury the rest. That isn't cowardice. That's arithmetic."

"It's bookkeeping," Sera said. "There's a difference."

"There usually is," Aldric returned, and for one bare second they almost laughed together, the old reflex of two soldiers who had shared too many cold camps, and then the moment passed and was dead, and they both knew it.

"I need your answer before the storm lifts," he said. "Truce ends at dawn. After that, I have six hunters, a writ, and orders I can't refuse twice."

"I know," Sera said. "Give me until morning."

He inclined his head. His hunters took the loft and the lean-to; Tomas was banished upstairs again with the mule, protesting; and the waystation settled into the strained quiet of a house full of enemies sharing a fire.

* * *

She found Rafael outside, at the woodpile, in the blue hour before full dark.

This was, Sera reflected, becoming a habit of theirs—finding each other at woodpiles, at shrines, at the edges of fires, in all the marginal places where important things got said because neither of them could manage it in the middle of a room. Rafael had an armload of split pine and snow in her dark hair and was glaring at the axe as though it owed her money.

"He's wrong," Rafael said, without preamble. "Your captain."

"He's right," Sera said. "That's the problem. Every word of it. We can't win. The board is set against us. Taking the deal saves three lives and costs a bundle of paper." She leaned against the woodshed and watched her breath climb. "Eleven years of stolen souls, weighed against us. And the arithmetic comes out exactly the way he says."

Rafael set down the wood. She brushed the snow from her hair, which did nothing, because more fell immediately, and she made a small irritated sound that Sera had privately begun to treasure among the sounds of the world.

"When I worked in the scriptorium," Rafael said, "there was a rule about ledgers. My mistress taught it to me my first week. She said: *a ledger is never wrong, girl. Only incomplete.* Someone pays a debt in a currency the ledger doesn't recognize, and the books call it a loss forever. But the debt was paid. The value moved. It's just somewhere the ledger can't see." She picked up the wood again, hugged it to her chest, and looked at Sera with those steady ember-brown eyes. "Your captain is doing arithmetic. He's counting lives against paper. But he's using the wrong ledger. He doesn't count the villages. He doesn't count the dead who can't object. He doesn't count what happens to *you*, if you take his bargain and spend the next forty years knowing what you buried."

"And what does happen to me?"

"I don't know," Rafael admitted. "But I've read enough memories to know what buried things do. They don't stay buried. They wait. And they get heavier." A pause. Snow fell. "Also, purely as a practical matter—if you surrender me, I will be extremely difficult about it. I'll hide seals in your boots. I know where you sleep."

"That's not the threat you think it is. You already know where I sleep. It's three feet from where you sleep."

"Yes," said Rafael, and went faintly pink, and gripped the firewood tighter, and Sera thought: *oh.* Thought it the way you think a thing you have been thinking for weeks and have only now permitted yourself to notice thinking. *Oh. Well. There it is.*

Neither of them said anything else about it. There would be time—or there wouldn't, which was the other possibility, the one that made Sera's chest tighten—but either way, not tonight, not with six hunters sleeping thirty feet away and dawn coming like a verdict.

"Burn it," Rafael said quietly. "Whatever he gives you to sign. Burn it in front of him. Make it clean. If you're going to break with the law, break so thoroughly there's no road back, because a road back is the cruelest thing you can leave yourself. Trust me. I hid for nine years, and every year the hiding cost more than the crime."

Sera looked at her for a long moment.

"When did you get wise?" she asked.

"I've always been wise. I was just quiet about it. It's different from hiding." Rafael smiled, small and crooked. "Mostly."

* * *

Dawn came grey and windless, the storm spent, the world scoured white and silent.

Aldric stood in the yard with the truce banner furled and his hunters mounted behind him, their breath rising in seven grey plumes. On the chopping block between them lay the offer: a single sheet of court vellum, heavy and cream-colored, sealed with the white banner of the hunt, its terms written in the beautiful, expensive hand of a ministry clerk who had never seen the people those terms were priced in.

Sera stood opposite him with the paper in her hands and read it through once more, aloud, in a clear voice, every clause—reinstatement, amnesty, surrender, silence. She read it the way she'd once read rights to the seized: completely, formally, giving it every dignity the law required.

Then she walked to the fire pit, where last night's coals still breathed under their ash, and knelt, and laid the vellum on the coals.

It caught slowly at first, the way expensive things burn, reluctantly, as if insulted. Then the seal went up in a bloom of white flame and the whole sheet took at once, curling, blackening, the beautiful clerk's hand vanishing line by line into orange lace. Sparks climbed into the cold air. Seven hunters watched. The soft-chinned young one's mouth was open.

"Senior Huntress Ilvane," Aldric said quietly. It wasn't anger. That was the worst of it—it was grief, worn openly by a man who had trained himself never to wear anything openly. "Do you understand what you've just done?"

"Yes," Sera said. She stayed kneeling at the fire, watching the last corner of the offer gutter and die. "For the first time in eleven years, I understand exactly what I've done. I've paid a debt, Captain. In a currency your ledger doesn't recognize." She rose and brushed ash from her knees. "The truce holds until you're over the col. After that—come with writs, come with ropes, come with whatever the ministry sends. But know that the papers exist, and copies exist, and they're already moving, and no bargain struck in this yard will ever gather them back."

Aldric looked at her a long time. Then, unexpectedly, he bowed—not the shallow bow of an officer, but a deeper one, older, the bow of one soldier to another across an uncrossable line.

"I carried you through a marsh once," he said. "Best weight I ever complained about."

"I know," Sera said. Her throat was tight. "Go home, Aldric. While there's still a home to go to."

He turned, mounted, and led his six away up the white road, and the truce banner stayed furled, and the snow took the sound of the hooves the way snow takes everything: gently, and permanently.

Tomas appeared at her elbow with three cups of something hot and disgracefully strong. "Well," he said. "That was the most polite treason I've ever witnessed."

"Thank you. I practiced."

Behind them, Rafael stood in the doorway with her arms crossed against the cold, watching the riders shrink to specks. When Sera joined her, Rafael didn't look over—just leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder rested against Sera's shoulder, warm through two layers of wool.

"You burned it," Rafael said.

"You told me to."

"I tell you lots of things. You rarely listen that fast."

Inside, on the table where Aldric had sat, Rafael's copied evidence lay stacked and bound in oilcloth—every page of the forgery, every false signature, eleven years of manufactured souls. And beneath it, Sera noticed now for the first time, a second document she hadn't seen before: a single sheet in Rafael's own careful scribe's hand, covered edge to edge in figures, with a line drawn through the middle of it and one word written at the bottom in letters pressed hard enough to tear the page.

Rafael followed her gaze. And very quickly, very casually, slid the paper beneath the stack and turned away to fetch more firewood, whistling.

Sera stared after her.

"Rafael," she said slowly. "What was that?"

"Nothing," said Rafael Voss—who had lied to the Emberhold for nine years, lied to witch-hunters, lied to auditors, and was, Sera realized with a plummeting heart, catastrophically bad at lying to her. "Ledger work. Boring. You'd hate it."

It was the worst lie Sera had ever heard, and she had heard a ministry's worth.
