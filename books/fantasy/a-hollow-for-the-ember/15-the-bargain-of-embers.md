## 15. The Bargain of Embers

The auditor's office smelled of cold candlewax and old paper, which Rafael thought was either a very good sign or a very bad one. In her experience, the places that smelled most like a scriptorium were the places where the worst sentences got written.

She had told Sera she was going to sleep.

That was the first lie she had ever told Sera Ilvane, and it had come out of her mouth so smoothly that Rafael had frightened herself. Nine years of hiding an ember-gift taught you to lie with your face before your tongue caught up. She had kissed Sera's temple—Sera had been mending her boot by lamplight, frowning at the stitching like it owed her money—and said, "I'll take first watch after. Wake me if the walls start singing again."

"The walls don't sing," Sera had said.

"Then we've nothing to worry about."

And now here Rafael stood, in a corridor of amber glass and black stone, three levels below the guest galleries of the Undercourt, telling herself that this was not betrayal. It was arithmetic. Tomas's freedom on one side of the scale; the thing she had spent her whole life smothering on the other.

The thing she had spent her whole life smothering had never once kept her warm at night. Sera had.

*Arithmetic,* she told herself, and knocked.

* * *

The auditor's name, as far as anyone in the Undercourt knew it, was Master Quillon. He was a narrow man made narrower by his chair, which seemed designed to remind visitors that comfort was a form of debt. His desk held a single lamp, a scale of brass and bone, and a ledger bound in leather so dark it looked like a hole cut in the shape of a book.

"Voss," he said, without looking up. "Rafael. Sister to the collateral. You signed yourself in two days ago under entry—" he turned a page with one dry fingertip "—eleven thousand four hundred and six. You listed your trade as *scribe*. How charming. We get very few honest trades down here."

"I want to renegotiate."

That got his eyes up. They were pale, the color of watered milk, and they looked at her the way she imagined a man looks at a coin he suspects is clipped. "Renegotiation requires standing. You have none. Your mother's contract named no heir of record. The bond defaulted jointly to you and your brother, which is a legal curiosity I find distasteful but must honor."

"Then let me make it simple." Rafael set both hands flat on his desk. She felt the wood hum faintly against her palms—the desk remembered every desperate hand that had gripped it, and there were many—but she kept her face still. Nine years of practice. "One soul for one soul. My brother walks out. I stay."

Quillon folded his fingers together. "You are offering to assume the full bond."

"I'm offering to *pay* it."

"They are not the same thing," Quillon said, and for the first time something moved behind the milk-pale eyes—something almost like regret, or perhaps just indigestion. "Assumption transfers the debt. Payment extinguishes it. Payment requires—" he tapped the ledger "—consideration of value."

"My brother's soul is worth one soul. Mine is worth one soul. That's the whole catechism."

"Your brother's soul," said Quillon, "is unlit. Dimmed by nine years of proximity to our vaults, but never kindled. It is collateral. It has never been *worked*." He leaned back until his chair creaked like a ship in ice. "Yours, Voss. Yours is another matter entirely."

Rafael's mouth went dry. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Quillon agreed pleasantly. "You don't. But ledgers do."

He turned the great dark book around and slid it across the desk. The page was open to her own entry—her name, her false trade, the date of her signing—and beneath it, in ink that shifted like oil on water, ran a line of notation she had never seen before:

*Ember: active. Classification: reader. Magnitude: significant. Note: subject has concealed gift from all registering authorities for estimated nine years. Concealment compounds interest.*

"You read memories through touch," Quillon said. "You always have. And you hid it—from the Emberhold, from the crown's registrars, from the witch-hunters who would have burned you for it. Do you understand what that means, in terms of this court?"

"It means I'm a criminal."

"It means," Quillon said, "that you are the single most valuable asset to walk through our gate in forty years."

* * *

The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above, water dripped through the mountain in slow amber time, and Rafael became aware that her hands were shaking, and made them stop, because a scribe learns early that trembling ruins the line.

"Explain," she said.

Quillon rose and went to a cabinet against the wall—a tall thing of black wood, locked with a seal of green wax that pulsed faintly, like something breathing. He did not open it. He simply rested his hand on it while he talked, the way a man rests a hand on a dog's head.

"A soul-ember is not a coin, Voss. It is a *capacity*. Most people's embers are banked the moment they die—small, dim, good only for lighting the deep lamps and warming the vaults. But some few are born with embers that do things. Readers. Finders. Binders. The crown hunts them because unregistered capacity is power outside its books." He turned. "This court does not hunt them. This court *banks* them."

"And my mother?"

"Your mother sold us a bond on her own ember. Standard distress contract—debts above, creditors circling, a woman with two children and no leverage except the one thing everyone carries and no one reads the terms of." Quillon's voice was flat as a ruled line. "When she died, the bond defaulted to her issue. Jointly. Which means the court holds a claim on two embers: one unlit, and one—" he tapped the ledger again "—*significant*."

"So take mine. Both of them. Take mine and release him."

"That is what I am proposing," Quillon said. "I want to be certain you understand what you are proposing."

"I understand."

"Do you?" For a moment the milk-pale eyes were almost kind, which was worse than cruelty. Kindness in an auditor's office was like warmth in a snowfield—it meant the thing beneath was hungry. "Your gift dies with the transfer. Not sleeps. *Dies.* You will touch a wax seal and feel wax. You will hold your lover's hand and know nothing of her but what she tells you. Every memory you have ever read will remain—but the reading ends. Forever. And the hollow where the ember sat does not refill, Voss. I have seen it. It is a room in a house where the fire has gone out, and the family keeps walking past the door."

Rafael thought of the smuggler's coin, weeks ago now, and the memory that had poured off it like heat—the edict, the white banner, the fear. She thought of her mother's signature smudged on old wax. She thought of Sera's mentor dying alone in a cell, and how Rafael had pressed her palm to a shrine stone and given Sera his last hour, and how Sera had wept—not prettily, not gently, but like a wall coming down, like a woman who had carried a stone for ten years and finally set it on the ground.

She could not give Sera anything else like that, ever again. She would not be able to read the shrine stone at Coldwater. She would not be able to read Sera's letters, if Sera ever wrote her one. She would hold Sera's hand and know nothing of her but what she chose to say.

*Which is what most people have,* said a small voice in her head, the voice of her mother, practical even in memory. *Most people love blind, Rafa, and manage.*

But most people hadn't known what it was to see someone truly—to hold a stranger's grief in their palm like a bird with a broken wing and feel it go still.

"Yes," Rafael said. "I understand."

* * *

They drafted the instrument of transfer on vellum the color of old teeth. Quillon wrote; Rafael checked. It was, absurdly, the most comfortable she had felt since entering the Undercourt—two professionals bent over a document, arguing commas.

"This clause is wrong," she said, tapping the fourth line. "'The party of the second part conveys all right, title, and interest in the ember aforesaid'—you can't convey an interest in something that *is* the interest. Say 'conveys the ember entire, with all appurtenances.'"

Quillon looked at her over the rim of the lamp. "You are correcting the grammar of your own damnation."

"Somebody has to. If I'm going to be devoured, I'd prefer it be done in clean Emberhold script." She pulled the pen from his fingers. "Also, your ascenders are cowardly. Here—"

She rewrote the clause herself, in the small fierce hand of nine years' copying, each letter seated properly on its baseline like a well-behaved child. Quillon watched with an expression she decided to file under *professional appreciation*, because the alternative was too strange to think about.

"One more term," she said.

"The terms are fixed."

"Then consider it a rider. When my brother leaves, he leaves with his debts marked paid in full—*paid*, not transferred, not assumed, extinguished. No claim follows him out of this mountain. Not to his children, not to his children's children. The bond dies with me and takes its whole rotten family tree with it."

Quillon considered. "That is within my authority."

"Is it also within your honesty? Because I've read enough contracts in my life to know the difference between what a court can do and what it intends to do."

For a long moment the auditor said nothing. Then he took the pen back and added the clause himself, in his thin clerk's hand, and initialed it, and Rafael touched the wet ink with one fingertip—just briefly, just long enough—and read his intention the way she had read ten thousand seals.

It was true. Whatever else Master Quillon was, whatever the Undercourt was built on, the man meant to honor this one clause. Perhaps auditors, like scribes, had one small sacred thing they would not falsify. Perhaps it was the only reason either profession survived.

"There," he said. "Signed pending your mark."

Rafael looked at the space at the bottom of the page. Her name would go there. *Rafael Voss.* After tonight, the name would exist in the master records of the Undercourt, entered, registered, visible—more visible than she had ever been in her life. The irony arrived on schedule and sat down beside her like an old friend: the gift that she had hidden until hiding was as natural as breathing would be the very thing that bought her out of the world's ledger. To be seen at last, she had to pay with the seeing.

Her mother had sold a soul to keep two children fed. Rafael was selling hers to keep one alive. Between the two contracts lay thirty years of a family learning, over and over, that love in this world was denominated in flesh.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty office, because Quillon had stepped away to fetch the sealing wax and she needed one moment that belonged only to her. "Tomas. You stubborn ox. You're going to be so angry."

She thought of him refusing to run. *My debts are paid. I owe no one flight.* He had been wrong about the debts, poor fool—he had owed their mother's debt without knowing it, worn it like a coat he couldn't take off. But he had been right about the other thing, the thing underneath: he had never once tried to escape being her brother. He had known about her gift for years—years—and had said nothing, guarded it, lied to hunters with a carter's flat honest face. He had kept her secret the way you keep a coal alive in winter: cupped, breathed on, never spoken of.

So this was her answer to that. Not a speech. A signature.

Quillon returned with a stick of green wax and a seal of black iron. "The mark, Voss."

Rafael picked up the pen.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about signing away your soul: it feels exactly like signing a tithe ledger. The pen scratches. The ink dries brown-black. Your hand knows the motions because your hand has made them ten thousand times, and the hand does not care what the words say. The hand just makes the letters beautiful, because that is all the hand has ever been asked to do.

*Rafael Voss,* in her best Emberhold script. She dotted nothing, crossed nothing crooked. Perfect lines. Nine years of invisible work, and her last act as a scribe was flawless.

Quillon dripped the wax, pressed the iron, lifted it. The seal came away green and gleaming, and Rafael felt—nothing dramatic. No tearing, no cold, no bell tolling in the deep. Just a faint sensation behind her sternum, like a door closing in a distant room of a large house. Like a lamp going out in a wing she rarely visited.

She flexed her fingers. She reached out and touched the desk, casually, the way she had touched a thousand surfaces in her life without thinking.

Wood. Grain. Coolness. Nothing else.

The desk told her nothing. The desk was only a desk.

"Oh," Rafael said quietly. It was such a small word for such an enormous absence. It was the word you said when you stepped into a room you had lived in all your life and found the furniture gone.

"The transfer completes at dawn," Quillon said, rolling the contract with brisk, impersonal efficiency. "Your brother will be released at the upper gate by the seventh bell. He will find the passes snowed shut, I'm afraid. He may wait out the winter in the waystation at Coldwater, if the shrine-keepers permit. Court business concludes above ground at the thaw."

"Court business," Rafael repeated. "And mine?"

"Yours is concluded." Quillon paused, and then—she would remember this later, when everything burned, and wonder at it—he added, not unkindly: "You bargained well, Voss. Better than your mother did. She signed in tears. You signed in Emberhold script." He inclined his head, one professional to another. "The vaults will be warmer for you. That is more than most of us get said about us."

* * *

The corridor back to the guest galleries was long and lit in amber, and Rafael walked it slowly, because she wanted to memorize it. The last corridor she would ever walk as a reader. She trailed her fingertips along the wall as she went—black stone, older than the crown, older than the Emberhold maybe—and for the first time in nine years she let herself read everything, greedily, wastefully, like a starving woman at a feast she'd been invited to leave.

The stone remembered the cutting of the tunnels: chisels ringing, songs in a language she didn't know, men laughing at jokes whose punchlines had rotted away centuries ago. A seam of quartz remembered being admired by a girl with amber eyes, long dead. A patch of mortar remembered a mason's curse and a dropped trowel. Nothing important. Everything precious. The whole mountain murmuring its small ordinary memories, and Rafael walking through them with her palm open, drinking, drinking, saying goodbye to each one.

At the foot of the stair she stopped, because the last stone in the wall was different—warmer, somehow, though warmth was impossible this deep—and when she touched it, it gave her not a memory but a feeling, which had never happened before, and would never happen again.

It gave her the feeling of being *kept*. Of a thing set aside carefully, in the dark, by hands that meant to come back for it.

She stood there a long moment with her hand on the stone, and then she laughed—one short, cracked note that echoed strangely in the amber light—because of course. Of course the mountain would choose tonight to be sentimental.

She climbed the stairs. She washed her face in the basin so the redness would have an excuse. She went back to the room where Sera Ilvane sat mending her boot by lamplight, or sleeping, or waiting—Sera, who noticed everything, who had spent her whole career noticing what people hid, and who was going to notice this, because Sera noticed *everything*, and Rafael had perhaps one night left before—

No. Not one night. Dawn. The transfer completed at dawn. She had until dawn to be the last reader in the valley, and she intended to spend every hour of it looking at Sera Ilvane with the only eyes she would ever have.

Sera looked up as she came in. Her gaze went over Rafael's face the way it went over a road before marching it—checking for ambushes, checking for tracks—and Rafael watched her arrive, gradually, at the conclusion that something was wrong, and watched her decide, visibly, not to ask yet. Trusting Rafael to say it. Trusting Rafael the way Rafael had never trusted anyone, because trust was a thing you had to give blind, without reading the other person's heart first, and Rafael had never in her life loved blind until now.

"Bad watch?" Sera asked.

"The worst," Rafael said, and sat down beside her, and took her hand, and held on.

Below them, in the vaults, a lamp that no one had lit began, very quietly, to burn.

Above them, in the archives, a huntress's copied evidence lay hidden in a boot—and in the morning, Sera Ilvane would go looking for the auditor's countersignature, and would find instead a fresh green seal dated hours old, and a name on a transfer instrument that would stop her heart in the reading room of souls.

But that was morning. The night was still theirs, and Rafael meant to spend it awake.
