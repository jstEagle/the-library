## 16. Ledger, Broken

Sera Ilvane had spent eleven years learning to read a lie in ink.

It was the first thing they taught at the Huntress Academy, before blade-work, before the catechisms of law: *the ledger is the law made visible, and the law can be made to lie.* She had memorized the telltales—the way forged pressure left a faint halo around letters, the way true wax cracked along its grain and false wax along its mold-seam, the way a guilty hand pressed too hard on the descenders. She had failed her first examination because she'd been too eager, flagging three honest contracts as forgeries out of sheer suspicion. Her instructor had laughed and told her she'd make a fine huntress and a terrible accountant.

She had thought of that instructor often since Coldwater shrine. She thought of him now as she stood before the Reading Room's inner door with Rafael's transfer contract folded against her ribs like a knife she couldn't stop touching.

*Signed. Sealed. Done.*

Rafael had signed herself into the bond while Sera slept off the road-weariness in the pilgrims' gallery. The auditor—Veyl Corrinne, dry as old vellum and twice as brittle—had witnessed it with the serene satisfaction of a woman watching an account finally balance. *One ember for one brother. A fair exchange,* she'd said, as though souls were turnips.

Sera had gone looking for Rafael first. That was the mistake—or the beginning of the only right thing she'd ever done; she hadn't decided which. She'd found the holding vaults emptying, Tomas Voss blinking in amber light like a man waking from a long afternoon nap, and Rafael standing apart with her hands pressed flat against her own sternum, as though holding something in. Or something *gone.*

"You didn't," Sera had said, and Rafael had looked at her with those steady grey eyes and said, "You were asleep," as if that settled anything, as if sleep were a legal excuse, as if Sera hadn't spent eleven years learning that every contract signed in someone's absence was a wound wearing paperwork.

Now Tomas was above, in the care of a smuggler named Doss who owed Rafael more shelter-debts than he could count and who had taken one look at Sera's huntress coat and started whistling very loudly. And Sera stood before the master archive of the Undercourt with a stolen key-ring warm in her palm and eleven years of training screaming at her that what she was about to do was the single most criminal act of her life.

She let herself in.

* * *

The Reading Room of Souls did not look like a place where people were kept. It looked like a library that had been loved once and abandoned mid-affection. Shelves of slate rose in concentric rings toward a ceiling lost in amber gloom, each shelf crowded with ledgers bound in grey leather gone soft at the corners. Ladders on iron rails. Little brass plaques with debt-categories engraved in a script so old even the court's clerks probably read it wrong. Somewhere below the floor, water moved—a deep, patient dripping, the mountain keeping its own time.

Each book held a soul. Sera knew this the way you know a bridge will hold: by faith in the builders. She had read enough of these archives over the past weeks—under pretense of audit-review, under the captain's grudging writ—to know how they worked. Name of the bonded. Origin of the debt. Terms. Interest accrued in years, not coin. And at the front of each volume, pasted inside the cover like a flower pressed by a careful hand: a slip of ember-parchment, faintly warm, faintly alive. The vessel. Touch it and you touched the person, or so the clerks claimed. Sera had never dared.

She found the Voss ledger in the third ring, shelf forty-one, category *Inherited Bonds—Maternal Line.* Of course there was a whole shelf for that. Of course the court had a filing system for the sins of mothers.

The book was thinner than she expected. That was the cruelty of it—all that suffering, all those nine years of Rafael hiding in a scriptorium, all of Tomas's stubborn refusal to run, compressed into a slim grey volume with a brass number and a neat hand.

She carried it to the reading table beneath the great lamp and set it down, and her hands were shaking, and she noticed with distant professional interest that she was pressing too hard on the cover's edges. *A guilty hand,* her instructor would have said. Well. Guilt could stand in line behind everything else.

She opened it.

There it was. Fresh ink, barely two days old, sitting atop the older entries like a stone laid on a grave:

*Bonded: Rafael Voss, scribe. Assumption of bond per voluntary transfer. Prior holder released: Tomas Voss, carter. Witnessed: V. Corrinne, Auditor of the Third Ring.*

And beneath it, in the margin, in Corrinne's small dry script, the note that had finished breaking whatever was left of Sera's oath:

*Asset noted: gift of reading. Unusually valuable. Recommend retention of bond indefinitely.*

Not a fair exchange. Not a settling of accounts. An acquisition. The court had taken one Voss and, finding him worthless—*carries no ember at all*, she remembered writing herself, in her own neat report, back when procedure was still a religion to her—had traded up. Rafael's whole hidden life, the nine years of copied ledgers and concealed light, delivered neatly to their shelves because Rafael loved her brother more than she feared the dark.

Sera sat down slowly on the reading stool.

She had imagined, in the weeks since Coldwater, many versions of this moment. In some she argued before a tribunal. In some she published the forgery evidence and watched the whole rotten system collapse in daylight. In none of them was she alone in an underground library, holding a pen, contemplating the crime that would end her life as surely as any noose—if they caught her.

But they wouldn't catch her. That was the terrible thing. That was the thing she understood with a clarity that felt almost holy: she knew exactly how this court lied, because she had spent three weeks learning it from its own documents. The crown manufactured debts with a template, a pressure-technique, a specific batch of sealing-wax bought through a chandler in Thornfell. The Undercourt kept its records honestly—which is to say, it kept its lies immaculate—and an immaculate record has one weakness.

It believes itself.

* * *

She gave herself until the lamp burned down one full ring—that was the rule of the Reading Room, oil measured in rings, hours counted like coins—and she used the time the way she'd once used it planning seizures. Methodically. Without mercy.

First: the entry above Rafael's. She needed a prior holder whose bond had lapsed cleanly, without audit, without question. She walked the rings with the lamp on its rolling stand, running her finger down shelf-indexes, and found him in the second ring, category *Auditors' Auxiliary, Deceased.* Aldous Renner. Predecessor to Corrinne's office. Dead nineteen years, his bond formally dissolved upon death—except that dissolution, Sera saw with a rising, giddy horror, was recorded on a separate certificate, filed separately, and the *master ledger entry* still bore the original bonded name in the original ink.

The court's own thoroughness had built her a door.

If the master entry for the auxiliary fund showed a living bonded party—Renner, deceased but unbeknownst to the ledger—then the transfer chain was broken at its root. Rafael's assumption would reference a bond that, on paper, had never properly passed to Corrinne at all. The whole instrument would be voidable. Contestable. Auditable. And the Undercourt lived in terror of exactly one word, and that word was *audit.*

Second: technique. Here Sera knelt on the cold floor of a soul-vault and committed the purest blasphemy of her career. She took out the forged crown-contracts she'd copied evidence from—the ones proving the witch-hunts were funded by manufactured debts—and she studied them not as crimes, but as *lessons.*

Pressure on the descenders. The halo of re-inked strokes. The trick of matching aged ink by diluting fresh with a drop of the vinegar-water the clerks used to clean their pens—she'd watched a clerk do it absently, days ago, and her mind had filed it the way it filed everything, waiting. Waiting for this.

Third: nerve. This was the part no academy taught.

She returned to the table. She uncapped her pen. And she sat for a long moment with her hand hovering over Rafael's name, thinking—not of law, not of oaths, but of a storm-bound shrine and a woman who had given her a dead mentor's last memory like a gift wrapped in grief; of bandits' knives and shared bread on high passes; of Rafael saying *you were asleep* in a voice that tried to be gentle and failed because she was already saying goodbye.

"You absolute mule," Sera said softly, to a woman who wasn't there. "You beautiful, infuriating, self-sacrificing mule."

Then she wrote.

She wrote Aldous Renner's name into the line where Rafael's had been—but not over it. Overwriting was the amateur's error; it shouted. Instead she performed the delicate surgery she'd only ever seen described in confiscated manuals: she scraped Rafael's name with a prepared blade until the ink thinned to shadow, then laid the new name over the ghost, letting the old strokes show through just enough that a casual examiner would read *correction, properly witnessed,* and move on. She forged Corrinne's marginal hand beside it—she had become fluent in that dry script through three weeks of reading it—and added a note of her own invention:

*Bond mis-assigned at intake. Corrected to lawful prior holder per auxiliary register. — V.C.*

It was forgery wearing verification's clothes. It was a lie with excellent references. It would not survive a determined inquiry from anyone who checked the death-certificate file.

But no one would check. No one *ever* checked, because the Undercourt's certainty in its own immaculacy was the load-bearing wall of the entire edifice, and Sera Ilvane was about to kick it.

Her last act was the smallest and cost her the most. Inside the front cover, she slid out the ember-slip bearing Rafael's warmth—careful, careful, it fluttered against her fingers like a sleeping bird—and she set it loose in the gap between shelf forty-one and the wall, where dust and nineteen years of neglect would bury it. A bonded soul without its vessel-slip was, by the court's own archaic rules, an unenforceable bond. She'd found that clause in a footnote. Footnotes were where courts hid the truths that inconvenienced them.

She closed the ledger. The brass number caught the lamplight. Somewhere below the floor, the mountain dripped its patient count.

Sera stood in the amber dark of the Reading Room of Souls, an officer of the crown with forgery on her hands and treason in her heart, and discovered that she was smiling. Not happily. But wholly.

"Come and audit me," she whispered to the shelves, to the court, to eleven years of rote-read rights and white banners. "I dare you."

The lamp rang down another hour. Above, far above, winter was closing the passes—and somewhere between here and the surface, a woman who had just traded her soul away was going to learn that Sera Ilvane had never once in her life left a debt unpaid, least of all the ones she chose.

Sera snuffed the lamp, returned the key-ring to the pocket of the sleeping clerk she'd lifted it from, and walked out into the amber corridors of the Undercourt—past doors, past vaults, past the great gate—already composing, in the dry precise script of Veyl Corrinne, the forged memorandum that would summon the court's auditors to shelf forty-one.

Let them come. Let the whole rotten ledger come down around their ears.

By morning, the Undercourt would be ringing with the one word its masters feared—

and Sera intended to be standing next to Rafael when it did.
