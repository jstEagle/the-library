## 17. Audit of the Deep Court

The bell began at the hour of no particular hour, because down here there was no hour that belonged to anyone.

Rafael heard it first as a tremor in the amber light—every lantern in the cavern city flickering once, like a held breath—and then as sound, a deep bronze tolling that came up through the floor and into her teeth. She had been awake anyway. She had been awake since Sera slipped out of their alcove in the guest-galleries with her boots in one hand and Rafael's heart in the other, off to do whatever it was a huntress did when she had finally decided to hunt something worth hunting.

Now the bell rang again, and again, and all around her the Undercourt woke up wrong.

Doors opened along the gallery. Clerks in grey emerged, blinking, their ledgers clutched to their chests like infants. Somewhere below, a voice began shouting about seals and warrants, and another voice answered it, and then a third, and the beautiful orderly hum of the place—the hum Rafael had hated for its cruelty and grudgingly admired for its competence—began to stutter.

She found Sera on the gallery stairs, coming back up two steps at a time, her face lit with a wildness Rafael had only seen on her once before: the night she'd burned her captain's offer.

"It worked," Sera said. "It's already working."

"What did you do?"

"I changed a name." Sera laughed, breathless, and caught Rafael's sleeve as though she needed something solid. "One name, Rafe. One line in a ledger four hundred years old, and the whole court can't balance itself. The predecessor's estate is being audited because the bond shouldn't exist, and if the bond doesn't exist, then every debt collected against it is a theft, and if those are thefts—" She stopped, savoring it. "There are eleven thousand souls held under bonds traced to that line. Eleven thousand clerks just discovered they may have been robbing people for centuries."

"And they're blaming each other."

"They're blaming *everyone*." Sera pulled her toward the railing. Below, the great floor of the court had become a sea of grey robes, clerks streaming between desks, carrying armfuls of records that were suddenly evidence instead of scripture. At the center stood the Auditor's dais, empty, and beside it the great seal of the Undercourt—a disc of amber the size of a millstone, set into the stone floor, glowing with the slow pulse of everything the court held.

"An audit," Sera said. "A formal one. The bell means no debt can be collected, no soul moved, no contract sealed until the audit closes. Which means Tomas doesn't move either. Which means we have time we didn't have an hour ago."

Rafael looked at the seal. Even from here she could feel it, the way she felt old wax—the press of years, the weight of what had been pressed into it. Ten thousand signatures. Ten thousand bargains. Her own name among them, signed three days ago in ink that had cost her more than she could say.

"Sera," she said slowly, "how does an audit work? Truly?"

"Hunters' audits, you mean? A senior officer reads the record aloud and everyone stands very still hoping their names aren't in it."

"And here?"

Sera hesitated. "I don't know. I've never—no one has ever triggered one. It's in the founding law. The court audits itself or it dies; that's the legend, anyway. The clerks treat it like weather. Something that happens to other courts."

Below them, the first auditor descended to the dais—a tall figure in white, faceless behind a veil of gauze, carrying a single unbound book. The crowd stilled around it the way water stills around a dropped stone.

"The audit begins," Sera murmured. "They'll read the founding contracts first. Always the foundations. That's how my captain taught me—you check the footings before you trust the house."

Rafael's hand had drifted to the railing, and the railing was old, older than anything else she'd touched down here, and the gift flared before she could stop it—

—not the railing, through it, into the stone, into the amber, into the *seal*, and the memory rose in her like floodwater:

---

A cavern, but smaller then. Darker. No amber light except one lantern carried by a woman walking barefoot across the stone, and behind her men in the grey of the first clerks, and behind them a figure bound in chains that were not iron but something paler, translucent, humming.

The woman was not their mother. The woman was older than their mother by a thousand years, and she was afraid, and she was doing it anyway.

"*Name,*" said a clerk.

"*No,*" said the woman.

"*The bond requires a name.*"

"*Then take the hollow where my name was,*" the woman said, "and build your court on it," and the clerks looked at each other, and the oldest one nodded, and they pressed her—not her body, her *self*, the ember of her—down into the stone, into the amber, and the light that filled the cavern was her light, was *her*, and every contract ever sealed beneath it was sealed in the light of a stolen soul that had never consented, never signed, whose name they had taken and used as the first entry in the first ledger—

*Founding asset: one soul, unnamed, seized.*

*All subsequent entries derivative thereof.*

---

Rafael came back to herself gripping the railing hard enough to hurt, gasping, and Sera was holding her shoulders, saying her name, saying it twice, three times.

"The seal," Rafael managed. "Sera, the seal. The great seal—it isn't wax, it isn't metal, it's a *person*. The court was built on a stolen soul. The first entry in the first ledger wasn't a bargain, it was a seizure. Everything after it—every contract, every bond, every debt—" She was shaking. "Every entry is derivative. Every one. Including mine. Including Tomas's. The whole court is built on a theft so old nobody remembers it was a theft."

Sera had gone very still, in the way she went still before drawing a blade. "You're certain. You read it truly?"

"I read it truly."

"Then the audit will find it." Sera's voice had dropped to something fierce and quiet. "Rafe, don't you see? They check the footings first. If the foundation is a stolen soul, then the audit doesn't just unravel our debts—it unravels *all of them*. The court can't survive its own founding."

Down on the floor, the veiled auditor opened the unbound book and spoke, and the voice carried through the cavern without effort, the way bells carry.

*"The audit opens with the founding asset. Produce the original entry."*

Silence. Then a clerk near the dais, an ancient grey figure, said something too low to hear, and the auditor repeated: *"Produce the original entry."*

And the ancient clerk, in a voice like dry paper, said: *"There is no original entry. There is only the seal."*

The auditor turned toward the great amber disc. And Rafael saw—everyone saw—the light within it falter. Just once. Like a heartbeat skipping.

Like something down there, in the deep of the stone, hearing itself spoken of for the first time in a thousand years, and *stirring*.

* * *

Sera had been trained her whole life to read a room for danger, and the room below the dais was teaching her faster than any academy.

The clerks had stopped moving. Not calmed—stopped, the way soldiers stop when the order they receive contradicts the order they were given. The ancient clerk who had admitted there was no original entry stood alone now, a ring of his colleagues drifting away from him as though error were contagious. The veiled auditor waited at the seal's edge with the patience of someone who had done this in other courts, in other ages, and knew exactly how foundations failed.

And the seal itself—Rafael had told her, and now Sera could see it with ordinary eyes—the seal was pulsing wrong. Faster than before. Brighter. The amber light threw shadows that moved when nothing moved.

"You need to get out of here," Sera said.

"No."

"Sera—"

"That was me agreeing with you," Rafael said. "I'm not leaving. Whatever happens when that thing finishes reading its own history, I want my name in the room when it happens. Mine and yours and Tomas's—we started this. We don't get to be elsewhere for the ending."

Sera looked at her—at this stubborn, quiet scribe who had erased herself from a temple register with fire rather than let a ledger define her—and felt the thing she had been refusing to say for weeks rise in her throat like a swallow of hot wine.

"After," she said. "When this is over. There's a waystation at Coldwater shrine. It needs keeping. Someone stubborn enough to keep it."

Rafael smiled, tired and bright at once. "After," she agreed. "Say the rest later. We have a court to topple first."

They went down together into the crowd of frozen clerks, Sera's hunter's stride cutting a path, Rafael following in the wake of her shoulder. At the edge of the dais the veiled auditor turned its gauze-covered face toward them, and though it had no visible eyes, Sera felt it look directly at her, then at Rafael, and linger.

"You are the signatories of the disputed bond," the auditor said. It was not a question. "The living debtor and the huntress who falsified the record."

Sera's hand went to her hip out of pure habit, found no sword there—they'd been confiscated at the gate, both of them, politely, thoroughly—and settled for lifting her chin. "I am."

"Your forgery was competent," the auditor said. "Technically elegant. It will be entered into the record as evidence, not crime. Do you know why?"

"Because the record it exposed was worse."

"Because," the auditor said, "an audit cannot proceed on a clean ledger. Corruption must be *visible* to be weighed. Your alteration made the corruption legible. In the founding law, that is called *opening the books*, and it is the one act the law cannot punish, because the law exists to be opened." The gauze tilted. "You have done the court a service it will hate you for."

"And the founding asset?" Rafael asked. Her voice was steady. Sera marveled at it. "The soul in the seal. What happens to it when the audit reaches it?"

The auditor was silent for a moment. When it spoke again, the voice had changed—quieter, and for the first time, uncertain.

"That depends," it said, "on whether it wakes before we finish reading."

As if in answer, the seal flared.

Light poured up out of the amber like water finding a breach, and every lantern in the cavern guttered, and every clerk fell to their knees—not in reverence, Sera thought, but in the posture of people bracing for a roof to come down. Through the flare Rafael heard it, faint and vast, a voice worn smooth by a thousand years of silence, rising through the stone, through her bones, through the gift that had always been able to hear what others couldn't:

*A name. Asking for its name.*

* * *

The holding vaults were three levels down, and Tomas felt the seal's waking through the floor before he understood it.

He had spent nine years learning the language of stone from underneath—carters know roads by their bones—and the mountain was speaking now, a long groaning note like a wagon axle bearing weight it was never built for. The vault wardens had abandoned their posts. The doors stood open. Nobody had told him he was free because nobody yet knew what freedom meant, tonight, in this place.

So he did what Vosses did. He got up, tested his legs, and walked toward the trouble, because in his experience trouble left its back door open while it shouted at the front.

He found Rafael and Sera on the great stair, hauling a third figure between them—the veiled auditor, of all impossible things, its gauze veil burned away, revealing beneath it a face that was old and young at once, a face like unfinished business.

"The seal is waking," Rafael was saying, "and the audit can't close until it's read, and it can't be read while it's *screaming*, and the screaming is cracking the galleries—"

"So we give it what it's asking for," Tomas said.

Both women stared at him.

"It wants its name." He shrugged, the shrug of a man stating the obvious to scholars. "You two deal in names and papers. I deal in roads. And on the roads, when something's trapped and panicking, you don't read regulations at it. You open the gate and you call it gentle, and you stand clear of the hooves."

The auditor looked at him for a long moment. "That," it said, "is not in the founding law."

"No," Tomas agreed. "But it's in the catechism."

And above them, through the cracking stone, the amber light gathered itself like a lung filling, and the voice in the seal said—clearly now, unmistakably, in a language older than ledgers and newer than grief:

*I remember the lantern. I remember choosing to walk. I remember that I never chose to stay.*

*Read me. All of me. Or bury your court in the rubble of your silence.*

Rafael took Sera's hand. Tomas took the other side of the auditor. And together they climbed toward the great seal, toward the light, toward the founding truth that would either free them all or bring the mountain down on every debt ever written beneath it—

—and Rafael felt, through the soles of her feet, the first true crack open in the floor of the world, running straight as a scribe's rule from the seal to the gates, from the gates to the passes above, toward winter, toward home, toward Coldwater, where a waystation waited for keepers.

The audit, it seemed, had only just begun.
