## 18. The Long Winter Falls

The first thing I felt when the great seal broke was the cold.

Not the cold of stone or water—I had lived nine years with those—but a cold that came from inside me, from the place where my ember had burned since before I had words for it. It was as if someone had opened a door in my chest and winter had walked through it without knocking.

"Rafael." Sera's hand closed around mine, and even now, even with everything unraveling around us, some traitorous part of me noticed how warm she was. How warm she had always been. "Rafael, look at me. Can you stand?"

I could. That was the strange thing. The auditor's bargain had been signed, the transfer made, Tomas's name struck from the bond—and the price was already being collected, coin by coin, out of the only account I had ever truly owned. But my legs still worked. My hands still worked. Only the light behind my eyes, the one that let me read the memories of wax and wood and skin, was dimming like a lamp running out of oil.

Around us, the Undercourt was coming apart.

The audit had begun as a whisper—a clerk somewhere questioning a name in a ledger, a supervisor demanding to see the original contract, the original contract turning out to be written in ink that hadn't existed when it was supposedly signed. And then the whole vast machinery of the deep court had turned on itself, the way a dog turns on its own tail, snapping and snarling and not understanding that the thing it bites is attached to the thing doing the biting.

Amber light flickered along the cavern walls. Somewhere above us—or below; direction meant little down here—a gallery groaned like an old ship.

"We need to go," Sera said. "Now. While they're all looking at each other."

"And Tomas?"

"Tomas is already moving. He's meeting us at the pilgrim stair." She said it with the flat certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading orders, except these orders were hers now, self-issued, and she wore them better than any uniform. "He wouldn't leave without you. I told him you'd be slow because you're stubborn."

"I'm not stubborn."

"You signed away your soul's ember *in secret*, Rafael. In a court of auditors. While I was in another room. That is not stubbornness, that is a *vocation*."

Despite everything—the cold in my chest, the falling galleries, the fact that we were fleeing a subterranean city built on a stolen soul—I laughed. It came out cracked and small, but it came out. Sera's mouth twitched at the sound, and for one heartbeat, in all that amber chaos, we just looked at each other.

Then the ceiling of the Reading Room of Souls came down.

* * *

We ran.

There is no other word for it, though Sera would later insist on "tactical withdrawal," which is what hunters say when they are running and their pride has survived longer than their dignity. We ran through corridors of amber glass where soul-lanterns swung on their chains, throwing shadows that moved wrong, that moved like the people whose souls fed them. We ran past clerks who no longer saw us, who were tearing open ledgers and holding contracts up to the light like men checking coins for clipping. We ran past the auditor's empty desk, and I did not look at it, because I could not have borne to see whether my signature still sat there, glowing faintly, waiting to be honored.

My ember guttered as we went.

I want to describe it properly, because no one will believe me otherwise. It wasn't pain. Pain would have been simpler. It was subtraction. Each hundred steps, something small went missing—not a memory, not exactly, but the *texture* under memories. The smell of my mother's bread. The exact weight of Tomas asleep against my shoulder when we were children. I could remember that he had been heavy; I could no longer remember how heavy. The gift that had let me read the world was being paid out in installments, and the installments were *me*.

"Talk to me," Sera said. She had figured it out before I admitted it—of course she had; she was a huntress, trained to notice what people hid, and I had never once managed to hide anything from her that mattered. "Out loud. Anything. The road. The shrine at Coldwater. What you're going to eat when we get out of here."

"Bread," I said. "Warm bread. With too much butter."

"There. Keep going."

"Honey. The dark kind, from the hives at Coldwater, the ones old Marta keeps even though everyone tells her the mountain air is too cold for bees. The bees don't care. They're mountain bees. They have opinions."

"What kind of opinions?"

"That everyone else is soft."

Sera laughed—a real laugh, breathless and bright, absurd in that collapsing dark—and I clung to the sound of it the way a drowning woman clings to a rope. Because here was the thing I was learning about losing my ember: the world was going quiet. All my life, every object I touched had murmured to me, had told me whose hand had held it last and what they'd been afraid of. Now, when my fingers brushed the cavern wall, the stone said nothing. When I gripped Sera's hand, her palm was just a palm—warm, calloused, wonderful, but silent. I was becoming, for the first time in my life, an ordinary woman in an ordinary world.

And I found, to my astonishment, that I grieved it less than I'd feared. Because Sera had never needed my gift to be read. She had simply stood still long enough, in storms and shrines and smuggler passes, until I could see her with nothing but my eyes.

The gallery ahead of us was gone.

It hadn't fallen so much as *folded*—a whole span of amber-lit corridor crumpled like paper in a fist, dust boiling through the air, lanterns dead or dying. Beyond it, maybe thirty feet of broken stone, then the passage continued toward the pilgrim stair. Thirty feet. It might as well have been thirty miles.

"No," I said stupidly, as if denial were a tool.

"The stair's the other way," Sera said, already scanning the rubble. "Through the vault levels. Longer, but intact—probably. Nothing down here is *intact*, but—" She stopped. Her head tilted, listening, the way she did, and I realized I could no longer hear whatever she heard. My ears were fine. But the deep places of the mountain had always spoken to me through touch, through the faint memory in the stone itself, and that voice was going out like a candle in a draft. "Rafael. Your hands. Give me your hands."

I gave them. She pressed them flat against the nearest wall, and I felt—nothing. Warm stone. That was all. A wall, being a wall, telling me nothing about the thousand years of water and weight it had held back.

"I can't hear it," I whispered. "Sera, I can't hear the mountain anymore."

"I know." Her voice was gentle, and it broke something in me more thoroughly than any collapse could have. "I know, love. So I'll listen for both of us. Put your hands on my shoulders instead."

* * *

She carried me.

Not the whole way—my pride wouldn't allow it, such as my pride still was—but through the worst of it, when the dust thickened and the passages narrowed and my legs began to betray me in small, humiliating increments. She took my arm across her shoulders and bore half my weight as if it were nothing, this woman built like a drawn bow, who had once arrested people for the crime of being what I was. At some point I stopped walking properly and started stumbling, and at some point after that she simply stopped pretending and lifted me onto her back, arms hooked under my knees, and ran.

"You'll ruin your back," I mumbled into her hair.

"My back has survived worse. It survived the Tollgate of Teeth. It survived my captain's sermons. It will survive you."

"I'm not heavy."

"You weigh exactly as much as a woman who signs away her own ember and then apologizes for it," she said, "which is to say: far too little, and I will tell you so every day for the rest of our lives if you give me the chance."

I didn't answer. There was a lump in my throat the size of the mountain above us. *If you give me the chance.* As if the rest of our lives were a thing being offered, casually, in the middle of a collapsing underworld, as if it were the most natural sentence in the world. Maybe it was. Maybe that was the whole point of everything—the shrines, the tolls, the storm at Coldwater, the wound at the tollgate, the forged page in the master ledger—that all of it had been leading to two women in a dark place, one carrying the other, saying plainly what they had spent months not saying.

"Sera," I said.

"Don't talk. Save your breath."

"Sera, I love you."

Her stride hitched—half a step, no more, a hunter's discipline catching her before she fell. Then she kept moving, and her grip on my legs tightened, and her voice came back rough and certain:

"I know. I've known since the shrine. Since you gave me Ilvane's last memory and held my hands while I wept like a recruit. I've known since you lied to a bandit chief with your chin up and your knees shaking." A pause, breath ragged, boots striking stone. "I love you too. Obviously. I deserted the crown for you. Nobody deserts the crown for someone they merely *like*."

That was when the second gallery went.

* * *

The sound is hard to describe. Imagine the mountain clearing its throat. Imagine ten thousand tons of amber stone deciding, after millennia, to sit down. The passage behind us filled with dust and thunder, and the passage ahead—our passage—cracked along its crown like an egg.

Sera didn't stop. She couldn't; stopping was death. She ran into the falling dark with me on her back, and I did the only thing left to me: I wrapped my arms around her and put my forehead against her spine and trusted her completely. Not her skill, though she had that. Not her strength, though she had that too. *Her.* The specific, irreplaceable her, who had burned a captain's offer in front of his face, who had stolen justice page by page from a court of thieves, who had learned the smuggler's catechism in a single week and observed it better than I ever had.

A beam of stone came down ahead of us. She slid under it on her hip, taking the scrape across her shoulder that she would carry for the rest of her life and never once mention unless I asked. Another came down behind. She didn't look back. Hunters, I thought dizzily, are taught never to look back; scribes are taught never to look up; between us we made one nearly functional person.

Then—light. Real light, gray and thin and cold, pouring down a crevice ahead. The pilgrim stair. The way up.

"Tomas!" Sera shouted, and the mountain swallowed most of it, but a shape detached itself from the gloom by the stair—a big shape, a carter's shape, my brother's shape—with a lantern in one hand and terror and relief fighting openly on his face.

"You're alive," he said, in the tone of a man who has just won an argument with the universe. "Both of you. I told the stair-warden you'd come. He said the galleries were folding. I said you'd come anyway, because my sister is the most obstinate creature God ever set on a mountainside, and look—look at that. Right again."

"Tomas." I slid off Sera's back and my knees buckled and he caught me, and for a moment I was twelve years old again and he was hauling me out of the river at Ford's Bend, scolding me the entire time, because that was how Tomas loved: loudly, and while complaining.

"Up," he said. "The stair goes up four hundred feet to the old pilgrim door, and above that is snow, and above the snow is the pass, and the pass closes tonight—the warden says the sky's gone the color of dishwater. We climb now or we winter underground with the souls."

"Charming company," Sera said.

"You've met my sister's cooking," Tomas said. "Souls might be an improvement."

I laughed, and the laugh hurt, because my chest was so cold, because the ember was guttering low, low, lower than I had let myself admit. I looked at my own hands in the lantern light and they were just hands. Ordinary. Quiet. The hollow where the gift had lived felt enormous, roomy, echoing—a hollow shaped exactly like an ember, waiting.

"Can you climb?" Sera asked quietly, beside me. Her hand found mine in the dark. Silent, that hand. Just a hand. And yet it said more than any seal or stone or scrap of wax ever had.

"Yes," I said. And then, honestly: "No. But I will."

"Then we go together," she said. "Slow steps. I've got you."

* * *

We climbed.

Four hundred feet of stairs cut by pilgrims who had come, centuries ago, to trade their sins for absolution in the court below. Their footsteps had worn hollows in each tread, and I fit my boots into them gratefully, letting the dead show me the way—one step, then the next, then the next. Tomas went first with the lantern. Sera stayed behind me, one hand at my back, catching me twice when my legs forgot their business, saying nothing either time except *I have you*, which by then was less a phrase than a law.

Halfway up, the mountain shuddered again, deeper this time, and dust sighed down through the stairwell like gray snow falling upward in reverse. Behind us, I knew, the amber city was burying its ledgers, its lanterns, its long corruption. Whether anyone climbed out after us, I didn't know. Whether the court would survive its own audit, rebuilt by survivors or abandoned to the dark, I didn't know. I only knew that the founding truth I had read in the great seal—the stolen soul beneath everything—was loose in the world now, and loose truths are like loose stones: they come down eventually.

My ember gave its last flicker somewhere around step three hundred.

I felt it go the way you feel a fire die in a room you've slept in all your life: not suddenly, but all at once. One moment there was warmth, the old familiar banked heat behind my breastbone. The next, only the hollow. I stopped on the stairs. I couldn't help it. I put my hand over my heart like a child with a stomachache.

Sera's arms came around me from behind, her chin hooking over my shoulder, her breath warm against my ear in all that cold.

"Gone?" she asked.

"Gone. Or nearly. Or—" I shook my head. "I don't know how to measure a thing like this."

"Then we measure it together," she said. "Every day. However much comes back, however much doesn't. You gave it to save your brother. That's not a loss, Rafael. That's the finest entry anyone ever made in any ledger, and I have read *thousands* of ledgers, and I am an expert."

"You're biased."

"Completely," she agreed. "Bias is the one thing they never managed to train out of me. Come on. Three hundred steps down, one hundred to go, and then snow, and then home."

Home. She said it about a waystation at a roadside shrine that belonged to no one and everyone, and she said it without irony, and I believed her, because I had learned on the high passes that the mountains shelter their own, and somewhere on those passes, without ceremony or paperwork, I had become the mountains' own. And so had she.

We climbed the last hundred steps.

At the top waited the old pilgrim door, oak and iron, older than the court below, older than the crown's edicts, older than every debt anyone had ever signed. Tomas set his shoulder against it. Sera set hers beside his. Together they pushed, and the door groaned open, and cold clean air poured in—the first honest air I had breathed in days—and beyond it:

Snow.

Deep, blue-shadowed, wind-scoured snow, falling fat and slow out of a sky the color of dishwater, already ankle-deep on the threshold and rising. The pass was closing. The warden had been right. Winter was sealing the mountain like a hand closing over a coal to keep it safe.

Tomas looked at it and swore with feeling. Sera looked at it and did arithmetic—distance, daylight, drift depth—and I watched her face do the sums and arrive at an answer she didn't like.

"It's three miles to the treeline," she said. "Three miles through this, with the pass closing behind us and the light going. Rafael can't make it on her own feet. Not yet. Maybe not—" She stopped herself. Recalculated. Looked at me, and then at Tomas, and something passed between them, some carter-and-huntress treaty concluded in a glance. "I'll carry her. You break trail. We move now, we don't stop, and we argue about it later."

"I'm not luggage," I protested.

"No," Sera said, crouching in front of me, offering her back, her eyes level and warm and absolutely immovable. "You're the woman I love, and the weather is terrible, and I am carrying you. Get on."

So I did.

Tomas broke trail ahead, broad shoulders plowing the drifts, lantern swinging, cursing the snow with the fluency of a man who had driven carts through twenty winters. Sera carried me into the white dark, steady as a mule and considerably more beloved, and the storm came down over the pass behind us like a door swinging shut, erasing the stair, the door, the mountain's deep amber heart, all of it, everything, buried in silence.

I lay against her back and felt her heartbeat through her coat—strong, stubborn, unremarkable, mine—and in the hollow where my ember had been, something small and new stirred. Not the gift. Not yet. Maybe not ever the same gift. But something. An ember knows how to wait in ash. It's practically its profession.

"Three miles," I murmured into her ear.

"Three miles," she agreed.

"And then?"

She didn't break stride. Snow gathered on her shoulders; her breath tore away in rags of steam; and her answer came back simple as a footfall.

"And then home. And then breakfast. And then we find out what the world does next—because Tomas says there were riders on the south road before the storm, crown colors, and if the Undercourt's scandal has surfaced, they'll come asking questions." Her hand tightened on my knee. "Let them ask. We've got answers. We've got evidence. And we've got a valley full of people who owe us shelter debts."

Above us the snow thickened. Ahead, somewhere in the white, the treeline waited, and beyond the treeline the shrine at Coldwater, and beyond the shrine—everything we had chosen, everything we had paid for, everything still to come.

The winter fell. We walked into it anyway.
