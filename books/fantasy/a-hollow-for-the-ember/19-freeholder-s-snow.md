## 19. Freeholder's Snow

The mountain spat us out like a stone it had been chewing all winter.

One moment there was the dark of the galleries, the groan of collapsing rock behind us, Rafael a dead weight against Sera's shoulder and Tomas limping ahead with the lantern guttering in his fist—and then the tunnel bent, the air changed taste, and there was light. Not amber, not torchlight. *Daylight.* Thin, grey, snow-blind daylight pouring down through a crack in the world, and cold air that tasted like pine and clean water and nothing at all like ink or old debts.

Sera stood in the mouth of the cave with Rafael's arm over her shoulders and did not move for a long moment.

She had spent nine years learning to read terrain the way soldiers read faces—every ridgeline a threat, every treeline an ambush waiting politely to happen. She knew this slope. Knew it the way you know your own hand. The shoulder below Coldwater shrine, the scree fan, the black teeth of the pines where the valley began. Which meant they had come out on the north face, which meant the galleries under the Undercourt ran further than any map she'd ever seen, which meant—

Which meant none of that mattered right now, because Rafael was breathing in shallow, careful pulls against her neck, and Tomas was saying her name for the third time.

"Sera. *Sera.* Is she—"

"She's breathing." Her voice came out scraped raw. "She's breathing. Help me get her into the trees."

They got her into the trees. That was the whole of the next hour: one foot, then another, through snow past Sera's knees, Tomas breaking trail because his legs were longer and his soul, whatever state it was in, still apparently came with a carter's shoulders. Rafael murmured once—something about ledgers, something about a name being wrong—and Sera pressed her lips to her hair and said, "I know. I know. It's done. Sleep."

And miracle of miracles, she slept.

* * *

The shrine at Coldwater looked smaller than Sera remembered, which was ridiculous, because shrines don't shrink. But she'd first seen it as a huntress with a warrant and a full kit and the law of the crown sitting on her shoulder like a second cloak, and now she arrived with nothing but a stolen ledger page sewn into her coat lining, a deserter's brand waiting for her back at Emberhold, and a woman whose heartbeat she'd counted every hour of the last two days just to keep herself sane.

Shrines look different when you arrive as what the mountains call *sheltered.*

Tomas got the door open—it had swollen with ice, and he swore at it in three languages, two of which Sera suspected he'd learned from smugglers and one of which she was fairly sure he'd invented—and the warmth inside hit them like a living thing. Old hearthstone holds heat the way the Undercourt held souls: stubbornly, and past all reasonable expectation.

They laid Rafael on the bench by the fire, and Sera pulled off her gloves and put her bare hand against Rafael's cheek, and for one vertiginous second she forgot that *she* was not the gifted one, that touching things told her nothing, that all she had were eyes and hands and the ordinary stupid human ways of knowing another person.

Rafael's eyes opened halfway. Grey as thaw-water.

"Hi," Sera said, brilliantly.

"You're covered in soot," Rafael whispered.

"So are you."

"I'm dying. You have no excuse." Her eyes drifted shut again, but her mouth curled, and Sera sat there on the floor with her back against the bench like a dog that has been told to stay, and let herself shake.

Tomas built up the fire without being asked. He moved around the shrine the way he must have moved around it a hundred times on his runs—knowing which board creaked, where the dry wood was stacked, which hook held the kettle. Of course he knew it. This was a smuggler's shrine. Half the mountain's quiet roads probably paid their shelter debts here.

"There's tea," he said. "There's always tea. There's bread in the cold-box that might predate my arrest, and there's—" He stopped, holding up a bundle of waxed cloth he'd pulled from beneath the floor stones. His face did something complicated. "There's my mother's blanket."

He said *my mother* and Sera watched him flinch, because their mother had sold them both, in her way, and the word was a wound that hadn't decided yet whether it was grief or rage.

He spread the blanket over Rafael anyway. Some debts you pay forward without checking the ledger.

* * *

Rafael slept through the night and most of the next day, waking only to drink broth and make one observation apiece, delivered in the tone of a woman filing reports:

Observation one: "Your wound is seeping." (It was not seeping. It was healing. Sera had changed the dressing twice and would not be argued with.)

Observation two: "Tomas, you're standing differently." And when he frowned: "Lighter. You're standing lighter. They gave it back, didn't they. The ember-bond. I can't feel it anymore—not mine either—but I can tell by how you're standing."

"It's gone," Tomas said. He said it carefully, the way you test ice. Then again, louder, laughing, terrifyingly close to tears: "It's *gone.* Nine years I carried that thing and I never even knew its weight until—" He sat down hard on the floor. "What did it cost? What did it cost *you*?"

"Less than it should have," Rafael said, and looked at Sera, and Sera looked at the fire, because some accounts weren't ready to be read aloud yet.

Here is what Sera had done, in the dark, while the court tore itself apart above them: she had swapped one bonded name for another in the master record—a name eight decades dead, an auditor's predecessor whose own debts had dissolved with him. A forgery inside a forgery. The audit had swallowed it whole, because the audit was looking for exactly that kind of error everywhere else, and no one audits the auditors' ghosts.

And here was the cost, which she had not said aloud: when Rafael signed her ember away, the transfer had begun to take before the record was altered. Sera had seen it happen—the light going out of her, gradually, like a lamp running low—and by the time Sera's forged line took effect and voided the bond, part of the price had already been *paid*. Paid into the court's coffers. Gone.

Rafael's gift wasn't dead. Sera could see that already, in the small ways—Rafael pausing with her hand flat on the table sometimes, listening to wood grain; the way she'd touched Tomas's sleeve and gone quiet and then smiled at something she didn't explain. But it was dimmed. Thinned. A fire banked rather than extinguished, and no one could say yet how much would grow back.

Rafael hadn't complained. Rafael had woken up, confirmed her brother was free, and started inventorying the shrine's stores. Sera wanted to be furious about it and couldn't manage anything warmer than awe.

* * *

On the third day, the neighbors came.

Sera heard them first—of course she did, old habits—two sets of boots on the packed snow, unhurried, coming up the shrine path openly. Not hunters' approach. Hunters came quiet and split wide. These two walked straight up like people calling on a neighbor, because they were.

The elder was Maren Ostrow, who kept the waystation at the fork of the Coldwater road, a broad grey-haired woman with forearms like ship cable. The younger Sera recognized distantly from warrant postings: Denny Fairholt, smuggler, age twenty-something, crimes various, all of them the mild kind.

They stopped in the doorway and looked at Sera—at her hunter's coat, half-ruined, its insignia scorched off in the collapse—with expressions Sera had spent her career never once seeing aimed at her.

Maren solved it by stepping inside, setting a sack of flour and a crock of rendered fat on the floor, and saying, "You'll be needing more than shrine rations if you're wintering. Tomas, boy, you look terrible."

"I've been in a hole," Tomas said.

"Aye, we heard." Maren crouched by Rafael, studied her with the frank assessment of a woman triaging livestock, and nodded. "Alive, then. Good. Half the valley had money on alive."

"On—" Sera blinked. "People were betting?"

"Small bets. Friendly." Maren unpacked her own kettle with the ease of ownership, which apparently extended to other people's shrines. "Word came up the roads four days ago. Something broke under Thornfell. The deep court's records opened like a dropped purse, and everything spilled out—soul-debts, forged contracts, names going *void* all down the registers. Whole valley felt it, they say. Chimney smoke turned strange for an hour." She glanced at Sera. "Your name's on the deserters' list. Posted at Emberhold gate and the tollhouse both."

"I know," Sera said.

"Do you?" Maren poured tea for everyone including herself, uninvited, unhurried. "Because here's the thing about lists, girl. They're written down low, where the crown can reach them. And the crown doesn't reach up here in winter. Nobody reaches up here in winter. Winter reaches *us*." She handed Sera a cup. "Officially you're a deserter with a price on your head. Unofficially, you carried Rafael Voss out of a hole in the world when nobody else could, and you're sleeping under a shrine roof, which makes you sheltered. Those are two different countries, and you're standing in the second one. Get used to the borders."

Denny, shy as a colt, had meanwhile produced from his coat a paper-wrapped parcel of honey cakes and set them down next to Rafael with the reverence of a man leaving an offering. "My sister says thank you," he mumbled. "For the reading. Before. When her man went missing on the ridge. You found where he—" He stopped. "Anyway. Thank you."

Rafael, propped on her elbow, took a honey cake and said gravely, "Tell her the debt's discharged."

"Oh, she'll argue," Denny said, brightening. "She keeps books."

"*Everyone* in this valley keeps books," Sera muttered, and Maren laughed like a landslide.

* * *

That evening, while Tomas slept and Rafael dozed against her side, Sera sat up with the fire and finally let herself open the thing she'd been carrying unopened since the caves: the future.

She laid it out like a tactical problem, because that was the only language she had for it.

Item: she was a deserter. The penalty was the brand, or worse if they caught her armed. Item: the evidence she carried—the copied forgery pages, sewn into her lining, sweat-stained but legible—was worth more than her head to anyone who wanted the scandal buried, and worth more still to anyone who wanted it *proven*. Item: riders had been seen on the south road before the storm, crown colors, asking questions. Item: storms end.

She was still turning it over when Rafael spoke without opening her eyes.

"You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you fight the whole kingdom by yourself before breakfast." Rafael shifted, tucked her head under Sera's chin, and settled in like she'd been issued a deed to the spot. "Whatever the plan is, it has a flaw."

"It has several flaws. It's a good plan."

"Mm." A pause. The fire ticked. "When the investigators come—and they will come, they always come—what are you going to do?"

"Meet them. With the evidence. With my confession, in writing, sealed with my own name." Sera stared into the coals. "I spent nine years believing the law was a wall that kept the wolves out. Then I found out I was one of the wolves and the wall was a cage door we locked from our side. I'm not burying it. If they take me, they take me with the truth in my hands, and maybe—maybe some of the others come over. There were good ones, Rafe. Confused ones. Scared ones. Good ones underneath."

"And if they hang you for it?"

"Then I'll have been hanged for the only thing I've ever done that I'm proud of."

Rafael was quiet a moment. Then, muffled against Sera's collarbone: "Terrible plan. Deeply flawed. We'll need better shelter debts, more allies, and a legal argument so airtight it embarrasses them in front of witnesses." She yawned. "Fortunately, I know everyone who owes anyone in this valley, and you're dating the woman who forged the Undercourt's own records. Between us we are extremely dangerous to bureaucracies."

"Dating," Sera repeated, ears going warm. "Is that—we haven't actually—"

"We survived a cave-in, a soul-audit, and each other's worst secrets. That's dating. Everything after this is just logistics." She paused. "Also I love you. I meant to say it somewhere more dramatic but the dramatic venues keep collapsing."

Sera laughed—really laughed, the first time since Thornfell, a sound that startled her own chest—and pulled her closer, and outside the snow went on falling, deep and soft and indifferent to crowns and courts alike, sealing the passes, sealing the valley, sealing them in with the people who owed them shelter and the people they owed in turn, which in the mountains amounted to the same thing.

Home, she thought. So that's what it feels like. Less like a place than like a debt willingly kept.

She was nearly asleep herself when Tomas stirred by the far wall, sat up, and cocked his head at the window like a hound catching wind.

"Sera," he said quietly. "You'll want to see this."

She rose, careful not to wake Rafael, and parted the frost-furred shutter a finger's width.

Down the white throat of the Coldwater road, small with distance but unmistakable, came lantern-light. Four lights, moving slow, spaced like disciplined men. And above them, when the clouds thinned enough for starlight, a scrap of color that had no business in the high valley in deep winter:

Crown blue.

"They're early," Sera said.
