## 20. The Gift Made Visible

The roadhouse at Miller's Bend had once been a granary, and it still smelled faintly of rye and old summers no matter how many fires Sera built in the hearth. Rafael had been there three weeks when she first noticed that the neighbors came to look at her the way they used to look at the well—warily, hopefully, and with a coin already palmed.

It began, as most things in the valley did, with Widow Ansker.

The widow arrived on a gray morning with a basket of eggs she clearly could not spare and a face arranged into the particular stubbornness of a woman who has decided to be embarrassed later. She set the basket on the table between them as though it were a legal document.

"I'm told," she said, "that you can read things."

Rafael put down her cup. Across the room, Sera paused in the act of splitting kindling—not obviously listening, but the axe had gone very still, which was Sera's version of pricking up her ears.

"I can read memories," Rafael said. "In things I touch. Sometimes in people, if they'll let me."

"Then read this." The widow dug in her apron pocket and produced a thimble, dented, its enamel worn to a ghost of blue. "My husband's. He's been dead eleven years, and before he died he hid our savings somewhere only he knew, and he was the sort of man who thought dying without telling anyone was a form of wit." Her chin lifted. "I want my money back from him."

There was a silence. Somewhere by the hearth, Sera made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been laughter being strangled at birth.

"He hid it *from you*," Rafael said carefully, "and you want me to find it."

"I want what's mine. He'd have wanted me to have it too, eventually, when he'd finished enjoying the joke. That was the trouble with Halfrid Ansker. Everything was a joke, and the punchline always came too late for anyone to enjoy it but him." She pushed the thimble across the table. "Well? Do you take eggs or not?"

Rafael looked at the thimble. Nine years of hiding, of gloves and careful hands and flinching from every wax seal in the Emberhold—and here was a woman asking her to do the thing itself, out loud, in a public room, for eggs.

She picked it up.

The memory rose warm and small: Halfrid Ansker, younger than his daughter, kneeling in the root cellar with a lantern, laughing under his breath as he pried up the third floor stone—the one with the notch—and tucked an oilcloth bundle into the dark. And beneath that memory, older ones layered like sediment: a wedding, a quarrel about a horse, a long illness borne with the same private amusement, and at the very end, a hand reaching for the thimble on the sill because it was the shape of his wife's thumb and he wanted something of hers to hold.

Rafael set it down. Her eyes were wet, which annoyed her.

"The third floorstone in your root cellar," she said. "The one with the chipped corner. And widow—he wasn't laughing at you. At the end, he held this because it fit your thumb."

Widow Ansker stood very still for a moment. Then she said, gruffly, "Sentimental old fool," in the voice women use when they mean *I loved him*, took two eggs back out of the basket—"for the roadhouse, you've earned those"—and left at a pace slightly faster than dignity allowed.

Through the window they watched her go. Halfway across the yard she stopped, pressed the thimble to her mouth, and stood in the snow a long moment before walking on.

"Well," said Sera, resuming her assault on the kindling with unnecessary force. "That's one way to collect a debt."

* * *

By the week's end, word had traveled the way word did in the valley—which was to say faster than horses, slower than scandal, and with considerable embellishment along the way. It was said at Coldwater shrine that the Voss woman could tell you where your dead father's ring went. It was said at Thornfell market that she could speak with the dead outright, which was untrue, and that she charged a fortune, which was also untrue, though Rafael was beginning to think she ought to.

They came in ones and twos at first, then in a slow steady trickle, hats in hand.

A drover whose brother had vanished on the high passes nine winters ago, wanting to know if the knife found in a scree field had been his. (It had; Rafael felt the last climb in it, the cold, the decision to cut east toward shelter rather than west toward home, and gave the family a place to raise a cairn.) A farmer disputing a boundary with his neighbor, both men red-faced, each producing deeds; Rafael touched the old surveyor's stake and reported, to the delight of half the valley and the fury of the other half, that the original line ran through the disputed orchard and that the surveyor had been paid in cider to forget it. A young woman who simply wanted to know whether her mother, dead these two years, had ever forgiven her for leaving. (Rafael held her hands and let her feel the answer herself, because some things should not be secondhand.)

"You're building something," Sera said one evening, watching from the doorway as Rafael saw out a shepherd who had come to identify a lamb-mark burned into a stolen hide.

"I'm copying ledgers," Rafael said. "Different script. Same work. Somebody writes everything down, and somebody else spends their life making sense of it."

"It's not the same."

"No," Rafael admitted. "It's better. Nobody burns this register."

Sera came and sat beside her. Firelight did kind things to her face, though Rafael suspected Sera would deny having a face capable of kindness by firelight, on principle.

"When I hunted," Sera said slowly, "we had a word for people like you. *Unregistered practitioners.* It went in the report between *smuggler* and *unlicensed hedge-witch*. Three words, and then a man with irons." She turned her hands over, studying the palms as if the oath were still written there. "And now I watch you settle a land dispute that two magistrates couldn't, and the whole village walks away fed and watered and nobody bleeds, and I keep thinking: this is what the law was supposed to be. This is what we were pretending to do."

"And now?"

"Now I split wood for a witch." Sera's mouth curved. "Best posting I ever had."

* * *

Not all of it was gentle. Rafael learned that quickly.

An old man came wanting to know if his son had died owing money—a question that sounded simple until Rafael touched the son's carved pipe and found not debt but flight, and shame, and a name in Amberdale that the father had never heard, and a grandchild. She sat with him a long time deciding how much truth a man could carry home in one visit, and finally gave him all of it, because she had spent nine years learning what partial ledgers cost.

Two brothers came together over their dead mother's loom and nearly came to blows over what Rafael found in it—each certain the other had cheated him of his inheritance. What the loom remembered was neither theft nor generosity but a mother dividing cloth unevenly on purpose, year after year, giving the finer weave to whichever son had wept last, keeping a private arithmetic of comfort that no ledger could hold. Rafael told them this plainly. They stared at her. Then the younger one started laughing, helplessly, horribly, and after a while the elder joined him, and they left carrying the loom between them like a stretcher, still arguing, but arguing the way family argues—with the knives put away.

"They'll be back next month," Sera predicted.

"Over what?"

"Something else their mother did on purpose. That woman sounds like she's still running the valley from under her headstone."

"She is," Rafael said. "Most of them are. That's rather the point of everything I do."

* * *

Tomas came down from the high pastures twice in those weeks, driving late-season goods, and stayed three days the second time because the roadhouse cook had a daughter and the daughter had opinions about cart-axles that Tomas found irresistible. He watched Rafael work with an expression Rafael couldn't read at first—until she realized it was pride, plain and unguarded, and that it embarrassed him so thoroughly he kept disguising it as criticism of the roadhouse's benches.

"You know," he said, leaning against the doorframe while she saw out a nervous boy who'd brought his dead father's compass, "for nine years I thought you were just tired all the time. Tired and quiet and always wearing those gloves, even in summer, even washing dishes. Ma said you had weak skin." He snorted. "Weak skin. Nine years I believed my sister had weak skin."

"You never asked."

"Didn't want to know," Tomas said simply. "If I didn't know, I couldn't be made to tell. Best secret-keeping is ignorance. You taught me that yourself, actually—you were so good at it I figured it must be a family technique." He shrugged. "Anyway. Weak skin. Honestly, Rafe, of all the covers you could have chosen."

"It worked."

"It worked because everyone in this valley believes whatever's least interesting." He grinned. Then the grin faded into something more careful. "You're different, though. Out here. Did you notice?"

"Notice what?"

"You don't flinch anymore. When you touch things. You used to—there was this little thing your hand did, like touching a hot pan. All the time. Even I could see it and I see nothing." He tapped his own temple. "Now you just... reach. Like it's yours."

Rafael looked at her own hands, scarred at the fingertips from nine years of ink and secrecy, resting open on the table.

"It cost me almost everything to learn that," she said. "Don't let me forget it."

* * *

Winter deepened. The roadhouse filled each evening with teamsters and pilgrims and the occasional hunter off-duty, and Rafael developed routines: readings by the east window in the mornings, when the light was good and the queue was short; disputes after supper, when the whole room could serve as witness, which she privately considered the truest court in the valley. Sera appointed herself bailiff, clerk, and occasionally executioner of sentences—her preferred sentence being *chop wood for the complainant until the grievance is forgotten*, which worked more often than any fine.

It was Sera, too, who kept the list.

"You need a register," she said one night, sliding a fresh-bound book across the table. Plain paper, stitched well, the cover unmarked. "Every reading. Date, object, parties, finding. Signed."

"Sera. I spent nine years in registers."

"This isn't theirs." Sera's voice was quiet but it had iron in it, the way it did when she'd been thinking about something for days. "This one's yours. Look—if the crown comes asking, and they will, eventually, you want a record showing exactly what you've done. Every case. Every finding. Nothing hidden." She tapped the blank page. "Hiding is what got people hanged. Not the gift. The hiding. So we stop. We write it all down, in plain script anyone can read, and we keep it where anyone can see it, and if they come, we hand it over ourselves."

Rafael stared at her. "You want me to register myself."

"I want you to make yourself impossible to erase." Sera's jaw set. "I've seen what happens to people who live quietly hoping no one notices. I was the one who noticed them. Trust me—it's better to be loud."

So Rafael wrote. Every evening, by lamplight, while Sera sharpened the pen and fetched the ink and pretended this was merely efficient administration rather than an act of defiance dressed as bookkeeping. *Third day of Deepwinter. Object: brass thimble, property of H. Ansker, dec. Finding: savings in root cellar, third floorstone. Parties satisfied. Witness: S. Ilvane.* Page after page, a ledger of small mercies, each entry a small flag planted in open ground.

Sometimes, writing it, Rafael caught herself smiling. Nine years of copying other people's tithes in a script designed to be unreadable. Now she wrote in the plainest hand she knew, and every word was hers.

* * *

There were hard evenings too. A woman came seeking her husband, lost to the press-gangs on the coast road, and Rafael had to touch his carved letter-opener and tell her the sea had him, gently, and sit with her while she wept, because knowing was what she'd asked for and Rafael had promised herself she would never again decide what people were allowed to know. A boy of maybe twelve brought his father's hammer and asked, in the flat voice children use when they've already guessed, whether his father had been proud of him. The hammer said yes. The hammer said it in the memory of a hundred small moments—the boy's crooked shelf mended and praised, the boy asleep against his shoulder—that the father had apparently replayed nightly like a miser counting coins. Rafael told the boy all of it. He walked out taller. She cried a little after he'd gone, and let Sera pretend not to notice, which was its own kindness.

"You're collecting them," Sera observed, handing her tea. "All these griefs. You're carrying everyone's."

"Someone has to count them properly. That's all a ledger is, in the end. Proof that things mattered." Rafael wrapped her hands around the cup. "The Undercourt counted souls like coin. The crown counted us like debts. I'm trying to count the way it should have been done—as though every entry were a person."

"Mm." Sera settled beside her, shoulder to shoulder, watching the fire. "And who counts you?"

Rafael turned her head. Sera was looking at the fire, ears faintly pink, in the manner of someone who has said a thing deliberately and will die before admitting it.

"You do," Rafael said. "Apparently. In a plain hand, signed and dated."

"Don't make it strange."

"Too late. It's strange. We're two fugitives running a court out of a granary, and I love you, and you bind your own evidence books. It's entirely strange."

"Good," said Sera, and leaned her head against Rafael's, and let the strangeness stand.

* * *

It was near the turn of the year, on a bright hard morning with the snow squeaking underfoot, that the stranger rode in.

He was not from the valley—everything about him said so, from the city-cut of his coat to the way he looked at the roadhouse as though pricing it. He stamped snow off his boots in the doorway, surveyed the common room with the mild professional interest of a man cataloguing it, and asked for breakfast and directions to Thornfell in an accent that put vowels where vowels had no business being.

Rafael was mid-reading—an old saddle, a dispute over a sale, nothing delicate—and she felt his attention settle on her hands, bare on the leather, working openly in front of a dozen witnesses who showed no more alarm than if she were mending a sock.

The stranger watched for a long moment. Then he asked the cook, pleasantly, who the woman was.

"That's Rafe Voss," the cook said, with the flat pride of the valley. "She reads things. Settles our disputes. You got a dispute?"

"No dispute," said the stranger. "Only curiosity." And he ate his breakfast slowly, and asked a few more questions—how long she'd been here, whether she took payment, whether the huntress by the fire was the same Ilvane formerly of the Third Ward, and at that one his voice went very light and casual indeed—and then he paid, overpaid, tipped the cook, and rode south toward Thornfell at a pace just short of urgent.

Sera stood in the doorway watching him go, arms crossed, breath steaming.

"Crown coat," she said. "City buttons. And he knew my name before anyone told him." She turned, and her face had gone still and clear, the way it did before a fight or a decision. "Rafe. Get the register."

Rafael's stomach dropped—but her hands, she noticed, did not shake. Nine years of hiding had taught her what fear felt like from the inside, and this was not it. This was something else. Something like the moment before a seal breaks.

"The audit's coming," Sera said, taking the book from her and weighing it in her hands like a weapon. "Good. I've been waiting to be audited my whole life."
