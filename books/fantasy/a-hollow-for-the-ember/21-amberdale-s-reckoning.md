## 21. Amberdale's Reckoning

### Sera

They came up the valley road on the first hard morning of the thaw, three riders in crown grey with the king's writ sewn on their shoulders like a wound that hadn't healed properly. Sera saw them from the waystation porch while she was still holding a mug of tea she would never finish, and her hands did the old thing—the thing nine years of hunting had trained into them—counting blades, reading gaits, marking exits.

Then she made herself put the mug down and go out to meet them, because that was the whole point.

"You're Ilvane." The lead rider was a narrow woman with a face like a folded letter, all creases and no warmth showing. Investigator Corren Vail, if the introduction she'd rehearsed in her head was right. "Captain of the Third Hunt, listed as deserted since midwinter."

"Listed, yes." Sera stood in the mud of her own yard, deliberately unarmed, deliberately unhurried. "Deserted is a strong word for what I did. I'd call it *amended*."

Vail's eyes flicked past her to the waystation, to the shrine stone with its ribbons, to the smoke rising from the chimney where Tomas Voss was, at that very moment, burning porridge. "You're aware the penalty for desertion in the face of a lawful hunt is—"

"Stripping of rank, forfeiture of writ, and a cell beneath the Emberhold until the Assizes." Sera recited it the way she'd once recited rights to prisoners, by rote, in a voice that made the words sound like stones dropped down a well. "I wrote that sentence myself, eleven times, for eleven people who mostly didn't deserve it. Shall we go inside? I've made a ledger of my own. You'll want to sit down for it. Everyone does, eventually. It's a very long ledger."

Something flickered across Vail's folded face—recognition, perhaps, of a fellow creature of procedure meeting a procedure she hadn't planned for.

Inside, the waystation's main room was warm and smelled of woodsmoke and, faintly, of Tomas's ruined porridge, which he had heroically failed to remove from the air. Sera had spent three nights arranging the long table: the copied contract pages in order of date, the auditor's seals, the tally-books showing soul-debts entered against people who had never signed anything, the correspondence between the Undercourt's clerks and the crown's own magistrates. She had written her confession last, in her best register hand, the hand they'd praised at her commissioning, and she had signed it with her full name and her old rank, because if she was going to be hanged for honesty she intended the honesty to be legible.

Vail and her investigators sat. The younger one, a boy who couldn't have been hunting more than two years, kept looking at the shrine ribbons on the wall with the expression of a man realizing the map he'd been given had been drawn by someone with an interest in him getting lost.

"Where did you get these?" Vail asked, turning a copied contract page with two fingers, as though it might bite.

"The Undercourt's own archive. Reading Room of Souls, third gallery, past the seal-vault. The clerks there have a system for manufacturing debts—template signatures, aging wax with a warm iron and a little ash, entering the bond against a name before the person is even told they owe. The crown has been buying those debts at discount to fund the hunts. Every seizure you and I ever made on a soul-bond warrant was, statistically speaking, a robbery with paperwork."

"That's a serious accusation."

"It's a serious archive." Sera slid her confession across the table. "I've listed the gallery, the shelf, the clerk's mark, and the iron they use. I've named the magistrates. And I've signed it, which is more than any of them ever did."

The younger investigator leaned over his superior's shoulder. "Captain Ilvane," he said, and then caught himself, "—former Captain—why? You had rank. You had the road. You could have just—run. Everyone runs."

Sera thought about how to answer that, and decided the boy deserved the true version, because he was the kind of officer who asked *why* and that kind was rare and worth feeding.

"Because I reported my own mentor once, for the crime of mercy, and she died in custody, and I told myself the law was worth it. And then I watched this law seize a carter with no ember at all because his paperwork was sufficient, and I understood that the law wasn't worth anything at all unless it was worth *this*—" she tapped the confession, "—which is to say, worth being broken over, publicly, by someone it used to work for."

The room was quiet. From the kitchen came the small domestic catastrophe of Tomas dropping a pan, followed by his voice, pitched to carry: "Everything's fine! Nothing is on fire! The porridge was already dead when I found it!"

And then the door opened, and Rafael came in with an armful of split kindling and the particular stillness she wore when she was reading a room without touching anything, and Sera watched Investigator Vail's whole understanding of the situation rearrange itself, because everyone in the valley knew Rafael Voss now—knew what she was, knew what she did at the waystation, knew that half the settled disputes between Coldwater and the high passes went through her hands and came out fairer than any assize could have made them.

"Rafael Voss," Vail said slowly.

"Investigator." Rafael set down the kindling. Up close, Sera could see the fine new lines of tiredness around her eyes; the ember was regrowing, but slowly, the way a field regrows after fire, and readings cost her more than they used to. "You'll want to touch the evidence, of course. Verify it. I'd recommend gloves—the wax on the older pages remembers things, and you look like a woman who's had enough surprises for one ride."

Vail looked at her for a long moment. "You're offering to help me build a case against the crown's own magistrates."

"I'm offering to help you build a case against *forgeries*," Rafael said. "What the crown does about it is between the crown and its conscience. I've found conscience keeps better when it's given a ledger to work from."

* * *

### Rafael

The investigators stayed three days, which was two days longer than anyone expected and exactly as long as Rafael's patience lasted, though she was careful to spend most of that patience where Sera could see her spending it, because there was a particular pleasure in letting your beloved watch you be magnanimous.

It was not, she admitted to herself, entirely magnanimous. She read the investigators the way she read everything now—openly, with their permission, which was the whole revolution of her life in a single word. Vail had agreed on the second evening, stiffly, when Rafael explained that wax and ink remembered and that Rafael could tell her which of the copied pages had been handled by which clerk, and whether any of the crown's own warrants in the case file bore the same warm-iron aging as the forgeries.

"You understand," Vail had said, holding out her bare hand with the reluctance of a woman feeding a wolf, "that if this testimony is ever needed in a formal assize, they'll want you under oath, and they'll want to argue about whether a memory is evidence."

"Everything is evidence," Rafael had said. "The question is only who's brave enough to read it."

Now, on the third morning, she stood in the yard watching the investigators saddle up, and the younger one—the boy, whose name was Perrin and who had, over three days, asked Rafael eleven careful questions about the valley's unwritten laws and written down every answer—was saying his goodbyes with the furtive energy of a man about to do something.

"Captain Ilvane," he said to Sera, and didn't correct himself this time. "If the Assizes go the way the evidence says they'll go. If the hunts are dissolved, or re-formed, or—whatever happens." He looked at his boots. "Some of us would rather serve a law that's practiced than one that's decreed. If you were ever taking people. For the waystation. Or the passes. Or whatever this is."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Rafael watched her, and knew she was thinking of her mentor, of shrines, of the catechism of the mountain roads learned at Rafael's elbow with frost in both their beards.

"The waystation doesn't take oaths," Sera said finally. "It takes debts. You'd owe shelter to anyone who asks, silence to anyone who pays the toll, and honesty to the ledger. That's the whole catechism. It's harder than the oath was."

"Harder how?"

"You can't hide behind it," Rafael said. "That's the difficulty. And the point."

Perrin nodded as though she'd said something profound, which, Rafael reflected, she more or less had, and swung up into his saddle. Vail was already mounted, the case-boxes strapped behind her, her folded face unreadable except to someone who had spent three days learning its creases—and Rafael had spent three days learning its creases, with permission, and so she saw what was under there: not doubt, anymore. Relief. The terrible lightness of a woman who had spent her career carrying a law she'd suspected was hollow and had finally been handed proof, which meant she could finally put it down.

"Captain Ilvane," Vail said. "Former. The Assizes will sit at Emberhold in six weeks. Your confession will be read. You should know that some will call for your head regardless of the forgeries. Procedure loves its own punishments."

"Procedure and I have an understanding," Sera said. "It punishes. I amend."

Vail almost smiled. It was a narrow thing, gone in a breath, but Rafael caught it, and caught also—because the investigator's glove had brushed the gatepost, and the post was old oak, and oak remembers—the genuine article underneath: a woman who had once, years ago, refused an order she couldn't justify, and had been quietly reassigned for it, and had never told anyone.

"Safe roads, Investigator," Rafael said. "The passes are open now, but the high shrines still expect a toll of silence. Don't talk to anyone you don't trust. It confuses the stone."

Vail looked at her for a long moment, and this time she did smile, briefly, like a crack of sun through thaw. "I'm told the valley shelters its own," she said. "I'm beginning to think the valley might be right about more things than the crown is."

They rode out down the valley road, three grey riders, and the mud took their hoofprints, and the whole settlement came out to watch them go—Tomas with his porridge-stained apron, the widow Ansk with her two boys, the ferryman, the smith, a dozen neighbors who had learned in one winter that the crown's writ was paper and the valley's word was stone.

"They'll be back," Sera said quietly, when the riders were out of sight. "The Assizes. And after, if it goes well—commissioners, reformers, men with new forms for old crimes. The valley will be a *precedent* for a while. Everyone will want to look at us."

"Let them look," Rafael said. "That's rather the theme."

Sera laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and put her arm around Rafael's shoulders, and they stood in the yard in the thaw-smell and the mud while the neighbors drifted back to their work, and Rafael let herself lean, which was still new, still a small daily miracle, being allowed to put her weight on something and trust it to hold.

### Sera

The letter came a week later, carried up from the ferry by a boy on a shaggy pony, sealed with the Emberhold's sun-and-flame.

Sera read it standing in the yard, and read it again, and then stood holding it while the wind tried to take it and Rafael watched her face do something complicated.

"Well?" Rafael said.

"Two things." Sera's voice came out steadier than she felt. "One: the Assizes have been moved up. Four weeks, not six. Vail's evidence moved fast—faster than comfortable, which means someone powerful is either helping us or racing us. Two—" she looked at the second page, and had to start the sentence twice, "—two: they've summoned me to testify. Not as a deserter in the dock. As a *witness for the crown*."

"That's good," Rafael said carefully. "That's—Sera, that's what we wanted. Daylight."

"It's what we wanted, yes." Sera folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, the way she did everything. "But read the last line."

Rafael took it. Sera watched her read, watched her lips move slightly over the words, watched her still.

*The Assize further summons one Rafael Voss of Coldwater to give testimony as to the nature and admissibility of memory-evidence, the court being advised that no precedent exists.*

"They want me," Rafael said, "to stand up in the Emberhold—the place where I hid for nine years, copying other people's debts in other people's hands—and tell a hall full of magistrates that the thing I spent my whole life concealing is true, and real, and belongs in their law."

"Yes."

"And they want it in four weeks."

"Yes."

Rafael was quiet. Then, being Rafael, she did the thing Sera had learned to love and dread in equal measure, which was to look at the terrifying thing squarely and start making a list.

"Well," she said. "I'll need a new shawl. The grey one has a burn hole. And Tomas will want to drive us, and he'll pretend it's a burden, and we'll pretend to believe him. And you—" she looked up, and her eyes were bright and frightened and absolutely certain, all at once, "—you'll be in the hall, in whatever they let a deserter-witness wear, and when my hands shake, you'll be where I can see you."

"I'll be in the front row," Sera said. "I'll bring the ledger. The long one. In case anyone gets ideas about procedure."

Rafael laughed then, wet-eyed, and the wind came up the valley carrying the smell of the thaw, and somewhere below them the river was breaking its ice in long groaning reports like far-off doors opening, one after another, all the way down out of the mountains.

"Four weeks," Rafael said. "The Emberhold scriptorium, the assize hall, the seal-vault where I first read my mother's name in the wax. I spent nine years being invisible in those rooms." She folded the letter and handed it back, and her hand was steady. "Time they learned what was standing in the corner the whole time."
