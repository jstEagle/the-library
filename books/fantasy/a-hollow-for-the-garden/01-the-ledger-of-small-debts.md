## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The ink was the first thing you learned to fear.

Not the gods—though the temple spoke of them often enough, in the careful, hollow way that people speak of things they no longer believe will notice them. Not the debt, either, though debt was the true liturgy of the valley, recited daily, enforced hourly, forgiven never. The ink. Because ink was where a prayer became permanent, and permanence was where the temple kept its teeth.

Kestrel Quintana had been copying prayers for eleven years, and she had learned to make her hand look worse than it was.

This was not easy. She had been taught her letters by their mother, who had been taught by *her* mother, and so on back through generations of Quintanas who had all worked the temple's scriptorium and all understood the same essential truth: a scribe who writes too beautifully is given beautiful work, and beautiful work is noticed, and being noticed in the Valley of Merrow was how debts found you. So Kestrel wrote with a small deliberate tremor in the downstrokes, an occasional blot where no blot needed to be, letters that leaned just slightly as if tired. Her petitions looked like the work of a competent, unremarkable girl. The high scribe, Master Corvane, had assessed her once, years ago, tapping her ledger with one long finger, and pronounced her *adequate*—and adequate was the safest word in the valley. Adequate was invisible. Invisible was free.

Or nearly free. Nothing in Merrow was free. That was the first lesson of the ledger.

She sat now at her usual desk in the scriptorium's long hall, third window from the north end, where the morning light came through glass thick with bubbles and fell on the page like watered milk. Around her, forty other scribes bent over their boards, and the only sounds were nibs scratching, the cough of old Brother Aldous two rows back, and the bell from the temple proper marking the third hour. The hall smelled of tallow smoke, dust, and the particular sourness of cheap gall-ink. Kestrel had stopped noticing it years ago. You stopped noticing the smell of a place the way you stopped noticing a wall—you only felt it when you pressed against it.

"Petitioner for the noon ledger," called the door-warden, and a woman shuffled in from the waiting colonnade, a farmer's wife with flour still ghosting her knuckles and a baby asleep against her shoulder in a sling of undyed wool.

Kestrel set down her current page—a funeral rite for a cooper's son, six lines, billed to the family at four coppers per line plus the sealing fee—and reached for the clean petition form.

"Sit, mistress," she said. "Tell me what you'd ask of the temple."

The woman sat carefully, as if the bench might also cost her something. "It's for my husband's hip. He can't work the east field anymore, and we've the second planting coming." She said it quickly, the way people did, as if speed could shrink the request. "I know it's— I know asking costs. I wouldn't if we could manage without."

"No one manages without," Kestrel said, and dipped her pen. It wasn't comfort. It was arithmetic, and the woman seemed to take it as such, some of the shame going out of her shoulders. "Name?"

"Tamsin Hale. Husband's Marta's boy, Odell Hale."

Kestrel wrote it in the petition column: *Tamsin Hale, of the lower terraces, asks intercession for the body of Odell Hale, that his labor may be restored.* She read it back aloud, flat and complete, because the petitioner had to hear exactly what would be sealed into the record—the temple was scrupulous about that much. What you asked for was what you were charged for. The temple never cheated you on the wording. Only on everything else.

"The intercession fee is three coppers, or two days' labor at the granary," Kestrel said. "Plus the recording fee, one copper. Plus the seal."

"I have the coppers." Tamsin Hale counted them out one at a time from a knot of cloth, and Kestrel watched her do it and thought, not for the first time, that she had watched this exact scene several thousand times and it had never once become ordinary. A woman trading the small soft weight of her family's survival for words on a page that would go into a cabinet, and then into an archive, and then nowhere. Because here was the thing about the Valley of Merrow, the thing every child learned before they learned their letters: magic could not enter the valley. Whatever moved through the world beyond the border—and everyone agreed *something* moved out there, in the passes and the deep woods and under the mountain—it stopped at the boundary stones like weather against glass. Prayers copied in the scriptorium were not heard by anything. They were filed. The temple said the filing itself was holy, that the written word accumulated grace the way a cistern accumulated rain, and that when the faithful died their ledgers were weighed.

Kestrel had believed this for perhaps the first four years of her employment. Then she had spent enough evenings reshelving archives to notice that the ledgers were weighed by no one, that the cabinets held fifty years of petitions and not one recorded answer, and that Master Corvane's office had very good locks and very good wine.

She wrote the receipt. "Press your thumb here. The debt is entered against your household name; it clears at harvest assessment or in labor."

The woman pressed her thumb into the ink pad and then onto the paper, leaving a whorled gray mark, small as a seed. Kestrel had pressed ten thousand such seeds into ten thousand pages. The whole valley was a field of them, a crop of small debts, planted in ink, harvested in labor, and the temple stood in the middle of it like a mill.

"Will he—" Tamsin Hale hesitated at the door. "Will it help? The writing of it?"

And there it was, the question Kestrel was paid one copper a day plus meals to never answer honestly.

"The writing is the prayer," Kestrel said. "That's what we're told."

The woman nodded as if this were a real answer and went out into the light, and Kestrel looked down at the fresh page, at the thumbprint drying gray beside the words, and felt the old familiar pressure behind her sternum—not quite anger, because anger wanted action and action was dangerous, but its patient cousin, resentment, which could sit for decades without moving a muscle.

*The writing is the prayer. That's what we're told.* She was twenty-three years old and she had built her whole self inside that sentence, the way a snail builds inside its shell, and some mornings she could hear how thin the shell had gotten.

* * *

The noon bell released them for bread and small beer, and Kestrel took her portion to the stairwell behind the archive cabinets, where the draft came down cold from the mountain side of the building and nobody lingered. She ate quickly, mechanically, watching the dust turn in the light from the high slit window.

Wren was late again.

Her brother worked the grounds—hedges, graves, the long gravel walk where pilgrims who could afford shoes came to feel pious between fees. He had always been quiet, Wren, even as a child; their mother used to say he kept his words the way misers kept coin, counting each one before spending it. But since their parents died—since the fever winter, three years past—he had gone quiet in a way that was different. Not reserved. Absent. He would stand at the garden gate holding a rake and forget he was holding it. He answered questions a half-beat too late, as if the question had to travel somewhere far away to find him. And twice now—twice that Kestrel knew of, and she suspected more often—he had been found at night standing in the temple yard facing the mountain, motionless, eyes open, until someone shook him awake.

He never remembered it. Or he said he didn't. With Wren, since the fever winter, the difference between *didn't* and *wouldn't* had grown a border of its own.

He came up the stairwell at last, sideways through the door, carrying a pruning knife he had no reason to bring indoors, and sat across from her on the step without speaking. This was their custom. They ate together in silence because silence was the only language they both still trusted.

"You're doing the Vell margins today?" he said finally.

"Afternoon. Why?"

"Nothing." He turned the pruning knife over in his fingers, once, twice. "Just—if you're near the north cabinets. Doesn't matter."

Kestrel studied him. His hands were dirty in the way of someone who had been digging, though the beds he tended this month were all surface plantings. There was dirt under nails that should have been clean, and a scratch along one forearm half-hidden by his sleeve, and when he caught her looking he pulled the sleeve down with a small precise motion, like closing a shutter.

"Wren."

"Mm."

"What's under the north cabinets?"

"Nothing." Too fast. Everything with Wren lately was either nothing or nothing, delivered at two different speeds. He finished his bread in three bites, stood, and paused with one hand on the rail, not looking at her. "Kes. If I ever say something strange. In the night, or—or whenever. Don't write it down."

She blinked. "What?"

"Don't write it down." He said it the way other people said *don't follow me*, and then he was gone down the stairs, and Kestrel sat with the crust of her bread going stale in her hand and the cold draft moving over her neck, and thought: *my brother has just asked me, in the house of the written word, never to record him.*

Every instinct she owned—which was to say, every survival habit eleven years in the scriptorium had bred into her—told her to obey. Don't write it down. Don't look. Be adequate. Be invisible. The shell was thin but it held, and it had held through the fever winter, through the reading of the will that revealed their parents had left them nothing but their own outstanding accounts, through the slow discovery that grief in the Valley of Merrow came itemized.

But she was her mother's daughter, and her mother had taught her letters with a stolen primer and a warning: *they let us copy, Kes. They don't let us read. Learn the difference and keep it secret, and it will be the most valuable thing you own.*

Kestrel could read. Truly read—not just the petition forms and receipts the scribes were permitted, but the old script in the archive margins, the contract hands, the marginalia in dead abbots' books. She had hidden it for nine years, sanding down her handwriting, dulling her vocabulary, performing adequacy hour after hour while her actual mind walked freely through every locked shelf she was sent to dust.

So when the afternoon came, and the Vell margin inventory took her—naturally, unavoidably, in the ordinary course of duties—to the north cabinets, she did not look at them.

She was better than that.

She shelved fourteen folios, checked eight seals, noted two cracked wax stamps in her report, and was halfway back down the aisle with her cart when she saw it: the gap. One folio missing from the row, its neighbors leaning into the space like men into a doorway. And wedged at the back of the shelf, where the wood had warped away from the wall, a corner of paper showing. Not filed paper. Hidden paper—thrust there in haste, or dropped there in fear.

Kestrel stood in the aisle for a long moment, listening. Brother Aldous coughed two rows away. The bell did not ring. Dust turned in the light.

She drew the paper out with two fingers.

It was not a petition. It was not a receipt. It was written in the old contract hand, the dense legal script the temple used for land transfers and burial plots, and it bore a name in the signature line that stopped her breath:

*Wren Quintana.*

Below the name, lines of terms she could mostly parse—*consideration rendered... obligation transferred... party of the second part, being the Hollow Court beneath Mount Ceren*—and below that, a date. Three years past. The fever winter. The month their parents died.

Kestrel put the paper back exactly as she had found it, wedge and warp and dust, and picked up her cart, and walked down the aisle at her normal adequate pace with her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

*Party of the second part, being the Hollow Court beneath Mount Ceren.*

There was no Hollow Court. Everyone knew there was no Hollow Court. The mountain was a mountain; the stories about what lived under it were nursemaids' tales, told to keep children from the passes, the same as the stories about the border stones. Magic could not enter the valley. Nothing under the mountain could reach into it. That was the whole architecture of the world, load-bearing, unquestioned, the wall she had built her shell against.

And her brother's name was on a contract with whatever lived on the other side of it.

She finished her shift. She smiled at the right moments. She transcribed a widow's petition for a good marriage portion with a hand that did not tremble, and she pressed the widow's thumb into the ink, and she said *the writing is the prayer,* and her voice did not shake, and Master Corvane passed down the aisle at dusk and paused at her desk and looked at her for one moment longer than he ever had in eleven years.

"Adequate work today, Quintana," he said.

"Thank you, master."

His footsteps faded. The lamps were trimmed. The scribes went home into the blue evening, and Kestrel walked the long way, past the garden gate where Wren stood with his rake in the last light, facing not the mountain tonight but nothing at all, his lips moving around words that made no sound.

She did not write it down.

But that night, lying awake in the room they shared, listening to her brother breathe in the bed across from hers—too evenly, too slowly, like a man breathing on purpose—Kestrel Quintana broke her own rule in the only place the temple could never search. She wrote it in her head, in the old script, in the dark, line by line, sealing it where no cabinet could hold it:

*Petition, entered this night, by the hand of Kestrel Quintana, against whom all debts are kept: my brother signed something, three winters past, in the month our parents died, and the second party to the contract is beneath the mountain, and tomorrow I am going to find out what he sold.*

Somewhere below the floors, below the cellars, below the roots of the temple itself, she almost fancied she could hear an answer beginning.
