## 2. Prayers That Answer Back

The valley had a rule older than its name: nothing crossed the border. Not weather from beyond the ridgeline, not letters, not travelers with intentions—and above all, not magic. The temple taught it as comfort. *The gods cannot reach you here,* the high scribe said each dawn, in a voice like dry paper folding. *You are safe.*

Kestrel Quintana had copied that sentence four hundred and eleven times.

She knew because she counted. Counting was what she did instead of believing. Four hundred and eleven recensions of *the gods cannot reach you here*, rendered in temple script so fine it looked like frost on glass, and every one of them a small lie she was paid—no, *indebted*—to reproduce. The ledger said her family owed the temple nine years of service for the winter her father broke his leg and the healers came anyway, unasked, because the temple did not permit its flock to suffer without incurring cost. Nine years. She was six years in. Wren was seven, though he'd stopped counting out loud somewhere around the month their parents died, and after that he stopped saying much of anything at all.

So she sat in the scriptorium on the third morning of the week, in the gray light that came through windows of horn and oiled linen, and she wrote prayers for people who could not write them themselves.

That was the work. That was the whole architecture of the place, if you looked at it plainly, which no one did: the valley's faithful came to the temple with their griefs and hopes knotted up in their throats, and scribes like Kestrel unpicked the knots and set them down in formal petition-script, sealed them with wax and debt-marks, and carried them to the altar room where they were stacked in cedar cabinets and never, as far as anyone could tell, read by anything but moths. The temple did not claim the gods heard the prayers. The temple claimed something subtler—that the act of asking, properly formatted, properly filed, settled something in the asker. A prayer was a receipt for hope. You paid your tithe, you spoke your need, the scribe wrote it down, and the transaction closed. Nothing answered. Nothing needed to.

It was, Kestrel thought, the most honest dishonesty she knew. And she kept its books.

"Quintana." Mother Vess stood over her desk, a widow with hands like knotted rope and a smell of woodsmoke and sheep tallow that preceded her by a full breath. "Another one."

Kestrel took the slip of paper. Petitioners who could afford a copper got their words taken down in the front hall by the junior scribes; those who couldn't waited for Mother Vess, who collected the desperate ones in the courtyard and ferried their needs upstairs in fragments, half-remembered, like a woman carrying water in her cupped palms.

This one was written on the back of a market tally, in charcoal, in a hand that pressed hard enough to tear.

*For my daughter. Fevered six days. The herb-woman has done all she can. I am asking whoever keeps accounts in heaven to let her live and take me instead, and if there is no one keeping accounts then I am asking anyway, into the dark, because there is nowhere else left to ask.*

Kestrel read it twice. Then she looked up at Mother Vess, who was watching her with an expression Kestrel had learned to translate over six years: *do not editorialize.*

"She knows the temple can't cure the fever," Kestrel said quietly. "The infirmary charges by the night."

"The temple can't," Mother Vess agreed. "But a prayer isn't medicine, girl. It's a door you knock on so you know you knocked." She turned away, then paused. "Formal register. Full invocation. The father's poor, so mark it against the alms-debt, not his own account. And Quintana—" her eyes narrowed, "—write what he asked. All of it. Even the part about taking him instead. The temple does not soften petitions. We are not editors of grief."

*I am,* Kestrel did not say. *Someone has to be.*

She dipped her pen. Around her the scriptorium breathed its usual sounds—the scratch of eleven other scribes, the creak of the drying-racks where finished petitions hung like laundry, the distant bell of the morning office. Scribe Ollen two desks over was working through a land dispute with the grim pleasure of a man writing someone else's ruin. Beyond the horn windows, the mountain stood over the valley's northern edge, white-capped even in late summer, wearing its clouds like a hood pulled low.

Beneath the mountain, everyone said, there was nothing. Old mines, mostly played out. Old shrines, mostly collapsed. The founders' era, the temple called it—a superstitious age before the border was raised and the valley was made clean. Before, the teachers said, people had prayed to things with names, and the things had answered, and the answers had been terrible, and so the founders had built the wall of silence around the valley and locked the door behind them.

The gods cannot reach you here. Four hundred and twelve, now, if she counted this morning's office.

Kestrel bent over the widow's tally-paper and began to transcribe.

She worked the way she always worked, which was to say she worked twice: once in the formal script the temple required, and once in her head, in the plain speech the prayer deserved. This was her private liturgy, her small rebellion, invisible and costless. *Let her live. Take me instead.* Somewhere under the fine frost-work of temple calligraphy, the words stayed human. No one would ever see them. But they were there, the way a river is still moving under ice.

She reached the closing formula—*entered this day, by the hand of Kestrel Quintana, against whom all debts are kept*—and was reaching for the wax when the air in the scriptorium changed.

Later she would try to describe it and fail. It was not a sound. It was the absence of a sound that should have been there—the sudden sense that the room had been humming, very faintly, all along, and had stopped. Eleven pens went silent mid-stroke. The drying-racks stopped creaking. Even the bell outside seemed to hang between strikes, unwilling to fall.

And then, from the direction of the altar room—from the cedar cabinets where the prayers went to be unread—came a voice.

It was not loud. That was the worst of it. It spoke at the volume of a person standing close behind your shoulder, and it spoke in the plain speech, the human speech, the speech under the ice:

*So noted. The exchange is refused. The daughter lives; the father's offer is declined. Grief is not currency here.*

Every hair on Kestrel's arms rose. Beside her, old Scribe Maren dropped her pen, and the clatter of it hitting the floor was the loudest thing any of them had ever heard.

The voice did not speak again. The hum returned—or rather, Kestrel realized, the ordinary silence of the world returned, the silence that had always been there, that she had been told was safety. The bell fell. Somewhere below, a door slammed, and running footsteps sounded on the stairs.

No one moved. Eleven scribes sat frozen at their desks like figures in a wax museum, and Kestrel sat among them, her heart slamming against her ribs, her hand still holding the pen over the finished petition—and the petition itself, she saw with a strange detached clarity, was *glowing*. Faintly. The ink of the closing line had gone from black to a deep ember-red, cooling even as she watched, and where the wax seal should have gone there was instead a small scorched mark, round and precise, like a coin pressed into the paper by a thumb made of heat.

*The daughter lives; the father's offer is declined.*

Something had read it. Something had *answered*.

In the magicless valley. Behind the wall of silence. In the room where prayers went to become receipts.

The doors banged open and High Scribe Corvane entered, and the room remembered how to breathe.

He was not a large man, Corvane, but he occupied space the way cold occupies a cellar—invisibly and totally. His robes were undyed wool, temple-gray, and his face was the face of a man who had spent forty years being disappointed in advance. He crossed the scriptorium without hurry. He looked at no one. He walked directly to Kestrel's desk and held out his hand.

"The petition," he said.

Kestrel's fingers were not obeying her. She looked down at the paper—at the ember-cooled seal, at her own name in the closing line, *against whom all debts are kept*—and some instinct older than obedience made her slide her palm over it.

"High Scribe," she managed. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her. "I've nearly finished the sealing. It's marked against the alms-debt, per protocol—"

"*The petition.*"

She gave it to him. There was no version of the next ten seconds in which she did not give it to him; she understood that the way she understood gravity. Corvane took the paper, held it to the light, and studied the scorched seal for a long moment. Whatever passed across his face was not surprise. That was the detail Kestrel's mind seized on and refused to release, even as fear flooded everything else: he was not surprised. He was *inconvenienced*.

"Continue your work," he said to the room, without turning. "What occurred here is a matter of record-keeping error. A fault in the flue system produced an acoustic effect. You will speak of it to no one, including one another. Am I understood?"

Eleven heads bowed. Eleven pens resumed scratching, raggedly, like a heartbeat finding its rhythm again. Corvane folded the petition once, twice, precisely, and tucked it into his sleeve.

"Quintana. My study. After the midday office."

He left. The scriptorium scratched on. Kestrel stared at her blank sheet of fresh paper and discovered that her hands, now that they were free to shake, would not stop.

* * *

She did not go straight to the study. She was diligent, not stupid, and diligence had taught her that information was the only wealth a debtor could accumulate safely. So she spent the hour between the summons and the midday office doing what she did best: reading the temple's own records against itself.

The archive of received petitions was open to any scribe—it had to be, for cross-referencing debts—and it was organized by year, by month, by district. Kestrel pulled the registers for the past decade and ran her finger down the columns of closing marks. Wax, wax, wax, wax. Thousands of entries. Every petition sealed in plain tallow, every one filed, every one silent.

Then she checked the marginalia—the little annotations scribes left for anomalies. *Seal cracked in transit. Ink spoiled. Paper miscounted.* And there, three years back, in a hand she recognized as Corvane's own: *Flue fault, acoustic effect, suppressed.* Two months later, same note. Six months after that: *Flue fault. Relocated cabinet. Suppressed.*

Seven times in ten years. Seven impossibilities, each one explained away with the same borrowed excuse, each one *suppressed*—and each one, she noticed as she looked closer at the dates, occurring within days of a petition marked against the alms-debt. Prayers from the poorest petitioners. The ones with nothing left to offer but themselves.

*Grief is not currency here,* the voice had said. But someone, apparently, had been collecting it anyway.

She put the registers back exactly as she'd found them, aligning the spines to the finger-width, and went to keep her appointment.

Corvane's study smelled of lamp oil and cold iron. He sat behind a desk bare except for a single ledger—the master debt-book, the one every scribe feared, in which the temple recorded what the valley owed it down to the last egg and hour. He did not offer her a chair.

"You counted," he said.

"High Scribe?"

"This morning. During the office. Your lips move when you count—I've watched you do it for six years. Recitations, verses, debt-formulas. You count everything, Quintana. It's a scribal virtue and a personal failing." He opened the master ledger to a page near the middle and turned it toward her. Her own name looked back at her, in the column of her family's debts, in the temple's careful bookkeeping. "So count this. One incident. Seven precedents. Zero explanations given to the valley at large. What does that arithmetic suggest to you?"

Kestrel kept her eyes on the ledger. "That the flues fail often."

"Do not be clever with me. Cleverness is how your father got the healers sent to him, and look what it cost." Corvane laced his fingers. "I am going to tell you something I tell no scribe, because you are the only one in this room's history quick enough to have already found the registers, and I would rather you hear it from me than construct it yourself badly. What happened today was a resonance. An echo. The valley's border holds against magic absolutely—but a border, by its nature, defines two sides, and sound carries across walls, Quintana, even when the wall is made of silence. On rare occasions, a prayer written here resonates with... a listener on the far side. An acoustic effect. Nothing more. The words are not heard. They are merely *reflected*, the way a shout comes back off a cliff-face, and fools mistake the echo for a reply."

"That echo," Kestrel said, "knew the daughter's fever would break."

"The herb-woman's treatments were already working. Coincidence dressed as prophecy is the oldest fraud in the world, and the founders built this valley specifically to be free of it." Corvane's voice did not rise, which somehow made it worse. "Here is what will happen now. The petition is confiscated. It will be burned, unfiled, and no record of it will exist. You will not speak of this morning to any soul—not to your fellow scribes, not to your brother, not in prayer, not in your sleep. If you are asked, you heard a draft in the flues. And in exchange—" he turned the ledger back around, and drew one long line through a figure, "—I strike one year from your family's debt. Nine minus six becomes five. A generous price for silence, wouldn't you say?"

There it was. The temple's whole grammar in a single gesture: everything answered, everything purchased, everything owed. He was buying her silence the way the temple bought grief—with discounts against a debt that never shrank fast enough to free anyone.

And the terrible thing, the thing Kestral would turn over for nights afterward, was that she almost took it. One year. Three hundred and sixty-five mornings closer to a life that belonged to her.

But underneath the fear, in the place where she kept her counting, another number had lodged itself and would not come loose. *Seven times in ten years.* Seven echoes. Seven listeners-on-the-far-side, or whatever a cliff-face had to be before it could refuse an offer of a father's life with something that sounded horribly like mercy.

Cliffs did not decline things. Echoes did not know whether a fever would break.

"Five years," Kestrel said slowly, "minus one for silence. And the petition burned."

"Correct."

"And if I'd heard nothing—if the flues had simply failed and I was too dull to notice—you'd have struck nothing."

Corvane smiled. It was the first time she had ever seen him do it, and it was worse than his anger. "You see? You're learning the temple's arithmetic after all."

She bowed. She said, "Thank you, High Scribe," in the flat obedient voice she'd been perfecting since she was eleven years old, and she left his study with a year of her life refunded and a splinter of knowledge buried so deep in her that she suspected it would never come out.

Because here was what she knew, and what she could prove, and what she would spend every sleepless night until she understood it: the border did not hold. It had never held. Something on the other side of the wall of silence had heard a charcoal-scribbled prayer on the back of a market tally, had weighed a father's life against a daughter's, had *answered*—and the temple had known about it for at least ten years, had logged it, had suppressed it, had built an entire theology of safety on top of the lie.

The gods cannot reach you here.

Four hundred and twelve recensions. And every single one of them false.

* * *

She went looking for Wren at dusk, because if the world had cracked open, she wanted to stand next to the only person whose cracks she knew by heart.

She found him not in the groundskeeper's quarters but out past the herb gardens, at the low stone wall that marked the temple's northern boundary, where the ground began its long climb toward the mountain. He was just standing there, his rake abandoned in the dirt, his face turned up toward the white peak, and the evening light made him look younger than twenty and older than the dead.

"Wren."

He didn't startle. That was wrong too—he always startled; he'd been jumpy since the funeral. He simply said, without turning, in a voice scraped thin:

"You heard it too. Didn't you."

Kestrel went cold. "You were in the scriptorium?"

"No." Now he turned, and his eyes were wet and wide and full of a terror that had clearly been living in him far longer than one morning. "But I *hear* it, Kes. All the time. Every night, louder. Like something under the mountain calling roll." His hand found hers and gripped it, hard, the way he hadn't gripped her hand since they were children. "And tonight—for the first time—it used my name."

Far to the north, beneath its hood of cloud, the mountain seemed to lean a little closer.

Behind them, unnoticed, the first star of evening rose over the valley—and Kestrel, watching her brother shake, understood that the question was no longer whether the wall of silence had cracks.

It was why her brother, of all people, could hear through them.
