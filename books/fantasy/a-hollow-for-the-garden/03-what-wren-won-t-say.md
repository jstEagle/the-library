## 3. What Wren Won't Say

The knock came at the hour when the scriptorium lamps burned lowest and Kestrel's wrist ached from copying the same benediction forty times in a hand too fine to be questioned.

"Scribe Quintana." One of the night wardens, a boy barely older than Wren, stood in the doorway with rain still beaded on his shoulders. His eyes skittered away from hers the way they always did—she was a woman with ink-stained fingers and no family name worth bowing to, and wardens learned early which temple servants mattered. "Your brother's been found on the mountain road."

Kestrel set down her pen with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them. "Wren doesn't leave the grounds after dark."

"No, scribe." The warden swallowed. "He was asleep when we found him. Walking asleep. Three miles up the pilgrim path, barefoot, in this." He held out his cupped palms as if offering alms, and in them lay Wren's boots, dry and clean, left neatly by the temple gate.

* * *

They kept him in the infirmary cell behind the herb garden, behind a door with a lock that clicked shut like a small polite throat being cleared. Kestrel sat on the stool beside his cot and watched her brother sleep, and for the first time in the two years since their parents' funeral she allowed herself to look at him properly instead of glancing, the way one glances at a wound to confirm it hasn't worsened.

He had always been the loud one. That was the joke of it—the scribe who lived in silence all day and the groundskeeper who sang off-key while he trimmed hedges, who narrated his work to the roses, who could make even old Mother Ansel laugh on the worst market days. Their parents had died in the landslide on the eastern switchback, and grief had gone into Wren like water into chalk, and what came out of him afterward was quieter each month, until now there were whole days when he spoke only to say *yes* and *no* and *the roses need cutting*, and some nights not even that.

The temple called it mourning. The physician called it melancholia of the humors. Kestrel had copied out both explanations in her neat, obedient hand, and had never once believed either of them, because she had seen how the high scribe looked at Wren—not with pity but with *inventory*, the same flat assessing gaze he gave a crate of imported vellum or a chest of candle wax.

Now her brother lay with his hands open at his sides, palms up, as though waiting to receive something. Three miles of mountain road, barefoot, in his sleep. There was frost on the path last night. His feet were unmarked. Not reddened, not blistered, not even cold when she touched them. They were warm, in fact, warmer than his hands, warm as if he'd been walking on sun-heated stone—and the pilgrim path was black rock that hadn't seen sun in a week of rain.

"Wren," she said softly.

His lips moved. No sound came out at first, and then a thread of it, so low she had to lean close enough to feel his breath against her ear.

She did not know the language. She knew every script the temple taught—liturgical, mercantile, the old valley runes used for boundary stones—and this was none of them. It had too many sounds that lived between sounds, consonants that seemed to arrive before the vowels that should have preceded them. It was beautiful the way deep water is beautiful, and it made the hair on her arms rise because some animal part of her understood, without any word of it, that whatever her brother was saying, he was saying it to something *below*.

"Wren." She took his hand. "It's me. It's Kes."

The flow of syllables stuttered. Stopped. His eyes opened—not all at once but in stages, like a man surfacing from very deep water, and when they found her face they were clear and present and terrified.

"Kes." His voice cracked on it. "How long—how long have I been here?"

"Since they carried you down. You walked three miles in your sleep."

"I don't sleepwalk." He said it fast, automatic, the way he said *I'm fine* whenever she asked about the silence. Then his gaze went past her shoulder to the locked door, and something in his face folded closed like a book slammed shut. "I'm tired, Kes. Can you—can you go? I want to sleep."

"You were speaking just now. A language I've never heard."

"I wasn't."

"Wren—"

"*I wasn't.*" Louder than he'd spoken in months, and then immediately smaller: "Please. Please go. I'm sorry. I'm so tired."

She went, because staying felt like pressing on a bruise he couldn't explain, and because she had learned long ago that the fastest way to lose Wren entirely was to push where he couldn't follow. But at the door she turned, and he was already turned away from her, curled toward the wall, and she saw that his open palm—the one she'd held—had a faint mark pressed into the skin. A sigil, small as a coin, drawn in something darker than ink.

She had spent six years copying prayers. She knew every mark the temple used.

That was not one of them.

* * *

The high scribe's cabinet stood in his private study, and its lock was famous—or infamous, among the lower scribes, who speculated idly and never seriously, the way men speculate about the contents of a magistrate's cellar. Aldous Vane kept the keys on a ring at his belt and the ring never left him, except—

Except on the first evening of each week, when he descended to the reliquary vaults to verify the seals, alone, a walk of forty minutes down and forty minutes back, and everyone in the scriptorium knew better than to breathe loudly until he returned.

Kestrel had known this schedule for four years and had never once thought of it as anything but an old man's ritual. Tonight she thought of it as forty minutes.

She told herself, climbing the back stair with the lamp turned low, that she was only going to look. That Vane had confiscated the answered prayer two weeks ago—the petitioner Marisol's prayer, the one that had been *answered aloud* in a valley where nothing answered—and that it would be filed in the cabinet, and that reading it again might tell her what it had actually said, because she'd only glimpsed the first lines before Vane's shadow fell across her desk. And that if there was anything else in that cabinet bearing her brother's name, well. She was only going to look.

The lock was good work. Her hairpins were better. Six years of transcribing other people's secrets had taught her that every lock in the temple existed to reassure the faithful, not to stop anyone determined, and the cabinet's mechanism surrendered to her in ninety seconds with a click so soft she almost missed it.

Inside: ledgers. Contracts in their hundreds, rolled and ribboned, organized by parish and year. Debt schedules. Tithe assessments. The ordinary machinery of the temple's arithmetic, exactly as dull as she'd always believed.

And then, third drawer, bound separately in grey leather, a folder labeled in Vane's precise hand: *QUINTANA.*

Two documents.

The first bore her father's signature—the careful, laborious letters of a man who wrote rarely and feared writing—and her mother's mark beside it, the little bird she always drew instead of signing. It was dated eleven years ago. The language was contract-language, the kind Kestrel had copied a thousand times without ever parsing, and she parsed it now, slowly, twice, feeling the floor tilt beneath each clause.

*...in consideration of the discharge of all outstanding obligations of the estate of Quintana, amounting to nine hundred marks, the undersigned do hereby convey unto the Temple of the Quiet Word, in trust perpetual, one soul entire, namely that of the second-born child, Wren Quintana, aged seven years, such soul to be held, maintained, and conveyed onward as the Temple shall determine...*

Conveyed onward. She read those words five times. They did not improve.

The second document was newer. It bore only her own name, printed in Vane's hand, and below the name, a single line awaiting signature:

*In consideration of [blank], the undersigned conveys unto the Temple of the Quiet Word [blank].*

Every term blank. Every term *waiting*. Dated—she checked twice, because her eyes refused it the first time—dated the day after their parents' funeral. Two years ago. Two years she had worked in this building, slept under its roof, eaten its bread, copied its prayers, believing herself free of debt because she paid her sister's portion of it in labor, and the whole time a contract with her name on it had lain in this drawer, its terms unwritten, its purpose patient.

Her parents hadn't sold Wren's soul to settle their debts.

They had sold Wren's soul, and the temple had drafted a second contract for *her*, and had simply been waiting to decide what to write in the blanks.

The lamp guttered. Down the corridor, far below, a door opened and closed—the reliquary stairs, maybe, or maybe just the wind moving through the colonnade. Kestrel stood in the dark of the high scribe's study holding her family's ruin in both hands, and she found that she was not crying. She was doing arithmetic.

Nine hundred marks. A seven-year-old's soul. And the temple had taken Wren not as payment but as *inventory*—held, maintained, conveyed onward—and whatever the Hollow Court beneath the mountain was, whatever the petitioners' whispered stories said it was, the temple had been feeding it. Selling to it. And the silence that had been eating her brother for two years, the sleepwalking, the language from below, the sigil pressed into his palm like a brand of ownership—

He wasn't grieving. He was *claimed*. He had been claimed since he was seven years old, and everything since—the quieting, the walking, the words he spoke in his sleep—was the claim tightening.

She thought of Vane's inventory-gaze. Of the blanks in her contract, waiting for terms. Of six years of diligent, invisible, grateful service, and understood suddenly that her invisibility had never been her own accomplishment. Someone had designed it. Someone had kept her small and indebted and close, the way a gardener keeps a tree pruned—not to spare it, but to shape what it would bear.

Footsteps on the stair. Unhurried. Familiar.

Kestrel put the contracts back exactly as they had lain, closed the drawer, relocked the cabinet with shaking fingers that somehow did not shake enough to fail, and was halfway across the study toward the window when the door opened and lamplight spilled in and Aldous Vane stood in it, keys in hand, looking at her with no surprise whatsoever.

"Scribe Quintana," he said mildly. "You're up late."

Behind him, in the corridor, two temple wardens waited with their staves grounded, and their faces wore the particular blankness of men who had been told, in advance, exactly whom they would find here.

Vane stepped aside, courteous as a host. "Come downstairs," he said. "There are some clauses I think it's time you read."
