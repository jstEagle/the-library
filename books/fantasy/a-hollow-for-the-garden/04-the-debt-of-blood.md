## 4. The Debt of Blood

Kestrel did not sleep.

She sat in her narrow room above the scriptorium with the stolen image of the contract burning behind her eyes—*Wren Quintana, one soul, sold in perpetuity*—and listened to the temple bells count the hours she was not spending on penance or prayer or any of the small obedient rituals that had once made her feel safe. The candle guttered at midnight and she did not relight it. The dark suited what she was becoming.

By the third bell after midnight, she had stopped shaking.

By the fourth, she had a plan, which was to say she had nothing resembling a plan, only a name and a door: High Scribe Ondrel Vasse, whose office sat at the end of the Scriptorium's long gallery, whose lock she had picked twice before for reasons that had seemed important then and were laughable now. She knew his habits the way she knew the prayer-cycles—by years of watching from the corner of a room where no one watched back. He took his morning tea alone before the other scribes arrived. He read correspondence with his left hand pressed flat against the desk, an old habit, as if holding the world down. And he kept the debt-ledgers in the cabinet beneath the window, because he liked to work with the light on his hands.

She would knock. That was the part she kept returning to, absurdly. After everything—the sleepwalking, the language Wren spoke like a man greeting an old friend, the contract with her brother's name signed in their mother's careful hand—she would *knock*, because some part of her was still the girl who copied prayers for copper coins and believed the rules were walls rather than cages.

The fifth bell rang. She went down.

* * *

The scriptorium at dawn was a country she had ruled quietly for eleven years. Rows of desks, each with its inkwell and sand-shaker and the little brass lamp the temple issued like a promise of illumination. The smell of oak gall ink and beeswax and cold stone. Through the high windows, the mountain stood black against a sky going grey at the edges, and Kestrel looked at it the way you look at a creditor who has finally come to your door.

Ondrel Vasse was already there. Of course he was. He sat behind his desk with a cup of tea untouched at his elbow, and when she came through the door without knocking—she surprised herself; the knocking girl died somewhere between the stairs and the gallery—he did not look startled. He looked like a man who had set a second cup out hours ago and was only mildly disappointed by how long the tea had gone cold.

"Kestrel Quintana," he said. "I wondered which night you'd choose."

Her mouth was dry. "You knew I found it."

"I knew you *would* find it." He gestured to the chair across from him, and when she didn't move, he continued as if she had. "You copy prayers for the illiterate, child. Do you know what that means? It means every secret this valley whispers to its gods passes under your pen first. You are the most informed person in this building and the least aware of it. I have been waiting for you to become aware of it for six years."

"You put the contract where I'd find it." Her voice came out flat, strange. "The cabinet. The lock."

"I put it where anyone diligent enough might find it," Vasse said, "because diligence is the one virtue this valley still produces reliably. But yes. I hoped it would be you. You have your mother's habit of reading things she shouldn't." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle with the desk's edge, and Kestrel understood suddenly that every precise gesture she had ever catalogued about this man had been a performance of order laid over something that was not orderly at all. "Sit down, Kestrel. What I have to tell you is better heard sitting."

"I'll stand."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Respect, maybe, or its counterfeit. "As you like. Then let me begin with a question. What do you believe happens to a soul when it is sold?"

The words landed in the quiet room like stones into deep water. Kestrel made herself breathe. "Souls aren't sold. That's a story smugglers tell. The valley is clean. Magic can't cross the border. The temple keeps us—"

"—safe. Yes. I wrote that sermon myself, three times over thirty years. It improves with practice." He folded his hands. "The valley is clean because we keep it clean, Kestrel, and keeping anything clean requires a place to put the dirt. There is a market beneath the mountain. There has always been a market beneath the mountain. The founders built the border not to keep magic out but to keep the valley *quiet*—docile, fertile, faithful. A garden, they called it. And every garden needs tending, and tending requires payment, and payment—" he spread his hands, palms up, a scribe presenting a finished page, "—is rendered in the only currency the Court beneath accepts."

"No." It came out of her before she could shape it into argument. "No. My parents were poor, not monstrous. They wouldn't—"

"They were poor," Vasse agreed gently, and the gentleness was the worst thing yet. "Your father borrowed against the harvest two seasons running, and then the blight came, and then the fees, and the temple does not forgive debts, Kestrel, the temple *restructures* them. Your parents came to me in the winter of the year you turned nine. Your father wept. Your mother did not. Your mother asked questions—good ones, sharp ones—and when she understood there was no other door, she asked the only question left. *Which of us.*"

The floor seemed to tilt. Kestrel gripped the back of the chair.

"The Court wanted a soul young enough to be worth the trade," Vasse went on, in the tone he used for dictating ledger entries. "Aged souls are thin. Spent. Yours was already spoken for—though you don't know the half of that yet, and I confess I hope you never do. So it fell to Wren. Seven years old, healthy, unmarked. Your mother signed. Your father couldn't hold the pen." He paused. "They died within the year. The road down from the pass, in the thaw. The Court considers that a courtesy. Settled accounts are closed accounts."

Kestrel heard herself make a sound she did not recognize—low, animal, and she strangled it, because if she let it out whole she would come apart in front of the one man in the valley she needed intact. *Wren.* Seven years old. She had been nine, copying her first full psalter, proud of her even lines, while downstairs her mother signed away her brother in a hand so careful it looked like love.

"That's why he sleeps walks," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "That's why he speaks that language. They're calling him home."

"They've been calling him since the ink dried. The border muffles it. Mostly." Vasse rose and went to the cabinet beneath the window, and Kestrel's whole body tightened—but he did not open it. He stood with his hand flat on its top, looking out at the mountain. "Do you know why I let you find it? Why I have spent six years making sure you were overlooked, passed over, paid in coppers, invisible?"

"Because invisible scribes don't ask questions."

"Because invisible scribes *survive*," he said, turning. And now there was nothing gentle in him at all, and nothing cruel either, only a tiredness so vast it looked like stone. "Because the Court holds a claim on this family that is not yet closed, and claims, Kestrel, accrue interest. Your parents settled Wren's price. They did not settle yours. Your contract sits in that cabinet with a blank clause awaiting signature, and the Court is patient, and the temple is *not*, because the temple owes the Court more than souls now, and the only thing the Court values above a soul is—"

He stopped. Kestrel realized she had stepped forward without deciding to.

"Above a soul is what?" she said. "Say it."

Vasse looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him, for the first time since she had known him, look away first.

"There are old bloodlines in this valley," he said. "Founders' lines. Most of them thinned out generations ago into nothing worth mentioning. Yours did not thin. Yours was *kept*. Do you truly think a girl of no family, no patron, no talent worth the name, rises to chief copyist of the scriptorium at nineteen on merit? I arranged it. The temple arranged it. We have fed you, housed you, overlooked you, and kept you exactly where the light of attention could not reach you, because the day the Court comes to collect the balance of the Quintana debt, it will not want Wren." His hand lifted from the cabinet. "It will want what sleeps in your blood. And the temple intends to be standing between you and them when it wakes up. Or holding the pen when you sign it over. Depending on how the conversation goes."

The silence afterward had weight, texture, temperature. Kestrel stood in the middle of it and felt the architecture of her entire life rearrange itself around her with a sound like ice shifting on a lake. Every kindness re-read itself. The extra ration at midwinter. The scholarship of her apprenticeship. The way the older scribes never quite met her eyes. Eleven years of being nobody, and it had never been neglect. It had been *husbandry*.

"You're saying I'm livestock."

"I'm saying you're an inheritance," Vasse said, "and inheritances are kept until claimed."

"And Wren?" Her voice cracked down the middle. "Is my brother also an inheritance? Is that what he is to you? To them?"

"Wren is collateral that has begun, unfortunately, to answer the calls of its owner. Which accelerates matters." Vasse returned to his desk and drew a sheet of paper toward him, unhurried, and Kestrel saw with a distant, ringing clarity that it was a contract already drafted, already dated, the blank clause sitting in the middle of the page like an open grave. "So here is how the conversation goes, Kestrel. Sign, and the temple settles your family's account entirely. Wren's claim dissolves. He wakes tomorrow remembering nothing but groundskeeping and his sister. You continue as chief copyist with a salary worthy of the title. No one is harmed. The valley stays asleep and safe and clean."

"And if I refuse?"

He did not look up. "Then the Court collects directly, and the temple does not stand between you and them, and I will have lost the only negotiation I ever conducted with anything approaching hope." He dipped his pen. Held it out to her across the desk, nib gleaming wet. "Sign, child. It is the kindest page you will ever be offered."

Kestrel looked at the pen. At the blank clause. At the man who had been her whole sky for eleven years and was suddenly revealed as a wall with a door in it, and the door opened onto the mountain.

She thought of Wren at seven, missing teeth, teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass. She thought of him last night, walking in his sleep with his face turned toward the peak, speaking a language that predated every ledger in this building. She thought: *they sold him to pay for bread, and you kept me like a coin in a purse, and you are both, all of you, scribes of the same book.*

"No," she said.

Vasse closed his eyes briefly, as if in prayer, or arithmetic.

Then Kestrel moved—not toward the pen, but past the desk, past him, to the cabinet beneath the window. The lock gave to her hairpin in four seconds, because she had picked it twice before and locks, like men, grow complacent with routine. Inside lay the ledgers, and atop them, loose, waiting, her own contract: *Kestrel Quintana,* and below her name, the clause with its empty line, and beside it, in faded brown ink she recognized with a sickness that went all the way down to childhood, her mother's signature witnessing a document Kestrel had never seen.

She took it. She took Wren's too, from the drawer beneath.

"Kestrel." Vasse's voice had changed—still calm, but calm the way the air is calm before the mountain takes a storm. "You cannot run. The border does not open. Whatever you think you are, the valley is a closed book, and I am its—"

"You're its scribe," she said, backing toward the gallery door with the contracts crushed against her chest. "I know exactly what you are. I've been one my whole life."

She ran. Behind her she heard the crash of his chair, the alarm-bell rope taken down, his voice calling her name in the tone he used for correcting novices—*Kestrel Quintana, you will stop*—and she ran faster, down the stair, out into the blue hour before dawn, past the sleeping dormitories, past the bell-tower where old Maren was just waking to the rope, out through the gate where the temple's authority ended and the frost-hard fields began.

Behind her, the temple began to ring its bells out of order—alarm, alarm, alarm.

Ahead of her, at the valley's edge, the air shimmered faintly where the world ended, and Kestrel Quintana, who had been told all her life that crossing it meant death, ran straight at the border of unmaking with her brother's soul in one hand and her own name in the other.
