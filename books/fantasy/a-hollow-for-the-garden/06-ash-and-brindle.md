## 6. Ash and Brindle

The mountain road was a liar's road, and Ash had learned to read its lies the way other people read scripture.

It lied about distance most of all. From the switchback above the treeline, the pass to the under-city looked like an afternoon's walk—a gentle gray thread stitched between two shoulders of rock. But the mountain ate hours. It ate light. It ate the careless and the pious and anyone fool enough to travel it with a full purse and an empty head. Ash had watched it do all three, and had long ago stopped mourning any of them.

Tonight it was eating the last of the daylight, and Ash wanted to be through the brindle gate before dark, because the gate was one of the few honest things on this road: it closed, and what came out after it closed was not for sale at any price Ash could pay.

They crouched at the edge of the switchback, one knee in the scree, and did what they always did before moving: counted exits. The goat track down to the frozen stream. The hollow beneath the leaning stone that smelled of fox. The old pilgrim stair, half-collapsed, that no one used because no one remembered where it went—except Ash, who made their living remembering what everyone else had agreed to forget.

Three exits. Good enough.

That was when they saw her.

A body, at first. Just a shape crumpled against the roadside shrine like something the mountain had coughed up—a bundle of gray wool and dark hair, one arm flung out across the shrine's cracked step as if she'd been reaching for it when she fell. The shrine was one of the dead ones, pre-Court, its god's face worn to a smooth blank by centuries of weather and neglect. Pilgrims still touched it sometimes, out of habit. The god never answered. That was the arrangement now: the old gods slept, the new ones traded, and the road between them belonged to people like Ash.

Ash should have walked past. That was the rule, and rules were the only inheritance they'd ever gotten from anybody, so they kept them the way other people kept heirlooms. Don't stop for bodies. Bodies meant soldiers, or debt-collectors, or worse—the kind of trouble that followed you home and moved into your walls. Ash had a network to run, eleven runners on four roads, a reputation built on the twin pillars of discretion and never being interesting. A body on the road was interesting.

But the light was doing something wrong around the girl's arm.

Ash told themselves it was the sunset. The sun was going down behind the western ridge, dragging its red across the snowfields, and red light lies about everything—it makes rust look like gold and blood look like wine. So they told themselves that, and then they took two steps down the scree anyway, boots grinding, cursing themselves in the flat private voice they used for self-contempt.

Up close, the girl was young. Younger than Ash had expected—young enough that her face still had softness in it, the kind that hadn't yet been negotiated away. Her lips were cracked. There was frost in her eyelashes, which meant she'd been lying here longer than the cold should have allowed, and there were burns on her outstretched hand—not fire-burns, not rope-burns, but something finer, a tracery of reddened lines following the veins from wrist to fingertip, like someone had drawn a map of her own blood on top of her skin.

Ash knew those marks.

Ash had seen them once before, years ago, on the inside of their sister's wrist, in the last hour of the last night they ever spent together—and the memory hit the way the mountain hit travelers, without warning and without mercy, so that for a moment Ash wasn't crouching over a stranger at all but kneeling in a rented room in the under-city, holding Sable's hand while she laughed at them for crying, *don't be stupid, Ash, it doesn't even hurt.*

*It doesn't even hurt.* The last lie she ever told them.

Ash sat back on their heels and breathed until the road came back. The wind. The dying light. The smell of pine and cold stone and, faintly, underneath everything, something sweet—like honey left too long in the sun, like incense burned down to nothing.

Residue. Divine residue, soaked into this girl's skin like dye into cloth, and still bleeding off her in waves that even a hedge-witch's ruined talent could taste on the air. Ash hadn't felt anything like it since Sable. Hadn't felt anything like it, honestly, since the temple in Corren's Reach had stripped Ash's name off their rolls and burned their license and told the world they were no one—which was, in fairness, the one prophecy Ash had ever fulfilled.

The girl stirred. Her eyes opened halfway—gray eyes, unfocused, fixed somewhere past Ash's shoulder at the blank face of the dead god—and she said, in a voice scraped raw:

"I need to go down."

"Everyone does," Ash said. "That's the direction."

"The Court." She swallowed. "My brother. They have my brother."

And there it was. Ash had been running the mountain roads for nine years, and every soul who ever crawled up out of the valley said some version of the same sentence, usually right before they died or got robbed or both. *They have my brother. My daughter. My wife.* Grief was the Court's favorite currency; it spent better than gold, because you couldn't counterfeit it and you couldn't get it back.

"They have everyone's somebody," Ash said, standing, brushing scree off their knees. "That's rather the point of them."

The girl's hand shot out and caught Ash's boot. Fast, for someone half-frozen. Her grip was weak but her eyes had come into focus now, and they were furious—furious the way only the powerless can afford to be, spending rage they couldn't back with anything.

"You know the way down."

"I know lots of ways down."

"Then take me."

"No."

Her grip tightened. "I'll pay."

"With what?" Ash gestured at her—wool cloak, scribe's ink stains on her fingers, not a coin's weight anywhere on her. "You're carrying exactly one thing worth money, and you don't even know what it is."

Something flickered across her face. Not confusion. Calculation. She looked down at her own burned hand, at the map of light in her veins, and Ash watched her arrive at the same conclusion Ash had reached thirty seconds earlier, and watched her decide—visibly, stubbornly—not to show that she'd understood.

"What is it?" she said, badly.

"Oh, that's terrible," Ash said. "You're a terrible liar. You copy prayers for a living, don't you? Valley accent, ink stains, that particular way of sitting like you're waiting for permission. A scribe. They let you out of the pen?"

"I left."

"People leave the valley about once a decade, and the border eats most of them." Ash crouched again, despite themselves, despite the rule, drawn by the sweet rot-smell of divinity rising off her skin. "You crossed it. Alive. Walking. And it didn't unmake you—it *parted*, didn't it. Like water around a boat." They tilted their head. "What are you?"

"My name is Kestrel."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I'm answering."

For a moment they just looked at each other, and Ash felt the strange vertigo of recognizing something in a stranger—the same stubbornness, the same refusal to give a straight answer to a dangerous question, the same talent for surviving by being uninteresting. Except this girl had failed at that completely. She was the most interesting thing on this mountain, possibly in the whole valley's history, and she didn't even know it yet.

Which meant everyone else would know it soon. The Court's tasters worked the roads. The gatekeeper's moths could smell residue at a hundred paces. If Ash walked away, some broker would find her by morning, and by tomorrow night she'd be on a slab in the under-city being measured, weighed, and sold by the dram—and whatever she carried in her blood would end up thinning some hollow lord's wine.

Ash thought about walking away anyway. They were very good at it. They'd practiced on Sable's grave, on every friend the trade had eaten, on the person they'd been before Corren's Reach. Walking away was a skill like any other, and skills kept you alive.

But Sable's wrist, mapped in light. *It doesn't even hurt.*

"Here's what happens now," Ash said. "You're going to drink this." They pulled the flask from their belt—willowbark and nettle and a pinch of something older, hedge-medicine, the last legal remnant of their training. "Then you're going to walk, because I'm not carrying you. And when we reach the under-city, you're going to do exactly what I say, when I say it, and speak to no one unless I introduce you. In exchange, I keep you alive long enough to make an idiot attempt at your brother."

"And what does that cost me?"

"Everything, eventually. Everything always costs everything. But the price today is simple." Ash held up one finger. "Whatever you are—whatever's in your blood—you belong to yourself until you sell it. Nobody else gets to decide. Not the Court, not the valley, not me. That's the whole contract. That's the entire deal. Can you agree to that?"

Kestrel Quintana—if that was even her real name, though the ink stains said yes—looked at Ash for a long moment. Then she drank the flask dry, grimaced like a cat given medicine, and said:

"Yes. And thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thanking implies kindness." Ash hauled her upright, steadied her when her knees buckled, and pretended not to notice how she leaned on their shoulder for the first ten steps. "This is arithmetic. You're worth more alive. To me. For reasons that are mine."

"What reasons?"

"The kind you buy with silence."

* * *

They made the brindle gate as the last light failed.

The gate was a natural arch of striped stone—white bands and rust-red bands braided together like bread dough, which was where the name came from—and it marked the true border of the Court's territory. Above it, the mountain was merely dangerous. Below it, the mountain was *inhabited*. Ash paused under the arch, put two fingers to the old ward-stone set in its base, and murmured the counter-sign that cost them a drop of blood and a day of bad luck, standard rates.

The stone warmed. Somewhere far below, something noted their passage and filed it away.

"Down there," Ash said, "you're not a person. You're inventory. Everything is. The bread is inventory, the lamplight is inventory, the *names* are inventory—they trade in names, did you know that? Sell a man his own name back and he'll thank you for it." They glanced sideways at Kestrel, who was walking better now, though her burned hand stayed curled against her chest. "So we don't use yours. Down there you're Brindle. After the gate. It's a nothing-name, a road-name, one of hundreds. You answer to it, you sleep in it, and you keep your real one locked up tight where nobody can weigh it."

"Brindle," she repeated, testing it.

"Like the stripes. White and red." Ash started down the first stair, into the warm breath of the mountain, where the air already smelled of tallow and hot metal and the sweet undertone of souls in storage. "Fitting. Half of you is ordinary as bread, and the other half is going to burn somebody's house down."

Behind them, above the arch, the stars came out over the valley—the valley where prayers went up and nothing ever answered, where a whole population lived and died inside a wall they called protection. Ash had grown up outside that wall and still found it obscene: a nation of the deaf, praying into a pillow.

Somewhere below, in the dark, the Court was counting its stock. And somewhere among the shelves and sigils and sealed jars, if the girl's story was true, there was a soul-sigil with a groundskeeper's name on it, waiting for auction.

Ash had seen auctions. Ash knew exactly what happened to small, quiet things with valuable names.

"Keep up, Brindle," they said, and led her down into the mountain, telling themselves—as they would keep telling themselves for weeks—that this was arithmetic, that this was a transaction, that the tightness in their chest was just the altitude.

The mountain, which lied about everything, said nothing at all.
