## 7. The Court Beneath

The mountain did not have a door so much as a wound.

Ash had used it forty times in eleven years—slipped through the crack where the roots of the world had been pried apart and never quite healed—and every time, the same cold breath came out to meet them, smelling of wet stone and old incense and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad in a locked room.

"Stay close," Ash said, not turning around. "And whatever you see down there, don't touch it. Don't point at it. And if anyone asks your name, you don't have one."

Behind them, Kestrel Quintana made a sound that was almost a laugh. "You brought me down here to be nobody."

"I brought you down here because you were dying on my road." Ash adjusted the strap of their pack and started down into the dark. "Being nobody is the service I provide free of charge."

The tunnel swallowed them by degrees. First the light of the mountain morning went, then the grey suggestion of it, until the only illumination was the foxfire lanterns Ash had wired to the pack frame—pale green blooms that made their shadows move wrong on the walls. The stone here was veined with something that wasn't quite mineral and wasn't quite root, and when Ash's boot scuffed it, the vein pulsed once, faintly, like a sleeper disturbed.

Kestrel had stopped walking.

Ash turned. In the foxfire light her face was the color of old candle wax, but her eyes were open wide, fixed on the vein in the wall, and her hand was hovering an inch from it despite every instruction Ash had just given.

"It's warm," she said. "Ash, it's *warm*. Like skin."

"Everything down here is like skin eventually." Ash caught her wrist—not rough, but firm, the way you'd steady a drunk or a child near a well. "That's the first lesson of the Court, scribe. Down here, nothing is dead unless someone paid to make it that way. Come on."

They descended for what felt like hours. The tunnel widened, narrowed, forked; Ash took turns without hesitation, left at the split marked with a smuggler's chalk sign only they could read, right at the place where the ceiling dripped water that glowed faintly gold. Kestrel asked questions at first—*what is that, who built this, how far*—and then stopped asking, which Ash noted with professional approval. People who kept talking were people who hadn't understood yet. Silence meant the scale of the thing was landing.

It landed somewhere past the third hour, when the tunnel opened without warning onto a ledge above the under-city, and Kestrel made a small sound in her throat like a bird hitting a window.

Below them, filling a cavern so vast its ceiling was lost in darkness, spread the Court.

Terraces of stone cut into the mountain's hollow heart, descending in rings, each ring crowded with structures that had not been built so much as *repurposed*. Ash watched Kestrel's face as she worked it out—the columns too thick for any human architect, the stairways carved for legs longer than hers, the great arched doorways sized for things that had walked out of the world a thousand years before the valley existed.

"Those are temples," she said. Her voice came out flat with shock. "Those are *gods'* temples. I've seen the drawings in the temple archives. The old gods' sanctuaries. They're supposed to be ruins."

"They are ruins." Ash leaned against a broken pillar, letting her look. "Ruins with tenants. Your temple up top calls this place the Hollow Court like it's one thing, one enemy you could draw on a map. It's not. It's a city. It has districts. It has markets. It has—" they gestured at the nearest terrace, where lantern-light spilled from a colonnade and figures moved in the glow, dozens of them, hundreds, buying and selling in the slow deliberate manner of people conducting serious business, "—a fish market, more or less. Except the fish is souls."

Kestrel didn't answer. She was gripping the pillar hard enough that her knuckles had gone white, and Ash saw her lips moving, and realized with a jolt that she was reading. Reading the signage on the nearest colonnade, the old-god script that half the under-city still used because it was older than literacy and stuck to stone like lichen.

"You can read that," Ash said. Not a question. They'd suspected since the road, since she'd looked at the residue on her own arms like she was proofreading it.

"My temple says no one can read it anymore." Kestrel's voice was very quiet. "It's a holy mystery. Only the high scribes learn it, after ten years of service, and even then only fragments." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I transcribed prayers in it when I was fourteen. I thought I was being trusted with something sacred."

"Welcome to the Court, scribe. Everything sacred up there is inventory down here." Ash pushed off the pillar. "Come on. We want the Sigil Row before the evening bell, while the brokers are still hungry and stupid."

* * *

The descent into the city itself was a lesson in looking away.

Ash had learned it years ago, the hard way: you couldn't stare at everything, or you'd stop moving, and people who stopped moving in the Court got appraised. So they moved through the terraced streets with the loose-shouldered gait of a local, foxfire lanterns doused now because the streets lit themselves—great basins of luminous moss at every corner, and above them, strung between the colossal pillars like laundry lines, chains of small glass spheres that glowed with a light that was warm and golden and *wrong*, and Ash saw Kestrel clock it within thirty seconds because of course she did, she was a scribe, she noticed things.

"What's in the globes?" she asked, low.

"Light." Ash kept walking. "Just light. Bought from people who had some to spare."

"That's not—"

"Don't." Ash said it quietly, but they said it sharp enough that Kestrel fell silent, and they hated themselves a little for the sharpness, and hated the city more. "First rule of the under-city: don't ask what anything is. Ask what it costs. Everything else is a story someone tells you to change the price."

So Kestrel learned the market the way Ash had learned it: sideways, in glimpses, in the corners of the eye.

She saw the broker stalls first—long counters of black wood where soul-sigils lay displayed on velvet like jewelry, each one a small sealed mark of light, some bright as a struck match, some dim as a dying coal. She saw the customers: hooded figures, merchants in silk, a woman in the grey robes of a midwife examining a sigil held up to the moss-light with the same squinting attention she'd give a bolt of cloth. She saw the auction boards, slates of pale stone where names were chalked in columns—names, dates, reserve prices—and she stopped walking when she found herself reading them, because the names were valley names. Names she knew. A miller from two villages over. A girl she'd gone to copying-school with before the debt took her family's coin.

"Ash." Kestrel's hand closed on their sleeve. "These are people from *up top*. These are—"

"The temple sells maybe forty souls a year down here." Ash said it the way you'd report the weather. "Has for centuries. Where do you think the debt system goes? You think all those ledgers, all those tithes, all those families ground down to nothing—they think it buys candles?"

Kestrel had gone very still. "The temple teaches that the debts keep the valley safe. That sacrifice feeds the border. That we're protected."

"You are protected." Ash met her eyes. "Like livestock is protected. There's a difference, scribe, and it's mostly a matter of who eats."

They kept walking. Ash gave her the tour they'd never wanted to give anyone: the Street of Sealed Voices, where you could buy the last words of the dead; the Weeping Gallery, a repurposed god-shrine whose ceiling fresco showed a goddess with her hands open in blessing, now hung with cages of small singing things that might once have been memories; the exchange house where fortunes were measured in *years* rather than coin, because a healthy young soul traded at a decade of light apiece, and everyone knew the rates the way valley folk knew the price of wheat.

And through all of it, Ash watched Kestrel Quintana come apart and reassemble herself, over and over, in public, without screaming, and revised their estimate of her upward for the third time since the road.

She wasn't crying. That was the thing that got Ash, later, thinking about it. She wasn't crying, and she wasn't numb either—her face was doing everything, grief and horror and fury cycling behind her eyes like weather—but she held it, all of it, because some part of her had apparently decided that falling apart in front of these people would be a kind of surrender, and Kestrel Quintana did not surrender to markets.

"Where," she said finally, as they crossed a bridge over a canal of slow silver water, "do they keep the new ones? The recent acquisitions. Wren was sold—" her voice snagged on the name and steadied, "—Wren was sold weeks ago. He wouldn't be on display yet. He'd be in holding. Inventory."

Ash stopped walking.

Because that was the other thing about Kestrel Quintana: she thought like a merchant when it mattered. She'd spent one day in hell and already learned to ask about logistics.

"Sigil Row," Ash said. "Fresh stock goes through the Assaying House first—they weigh the sigil, grade the brightness, decide which tier of buyer gets offered it. Then holding vaults under the Row until auction night. Auctions run every ninth day." They checked the skyless dark overhead, where a great bronze bell hung from a chain that vanished upward into blackness, and beneath it a slate board that Ash could read from here. "Next bell is tomorrow at dusk. Which means tonight, the Row will be full of brokers doing final appraisals."

"So he could be on the board already."

"He could be anywhere on the Row." Ash hesitated. They considered lying, or softening it, and discarded both, because she'd find out in an hour anyway and he'd rather she hear it from them. "Kestrel. When we find him—if we find him—he won't be a person yet. He'll be a sigil. A sealed light in a box. What's inside doesn't wake up until it's bought and unbound. So whatever you're picturing—him waiting, him knowing you came—put it away. It'll just slow you down."

Kestrel looked at them for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and said, "Show me the Assaying House," in a voice that made Ash glad, briefly and irrationally, that they were on the same side of whatever was coming.

* * *

Sigil Row at dusk was a cathedral of commerce.

The old god's sanctuary had been stripped of everything holy except the bones of its architecture—the towering pillars, the star-map floor done inlaid metals dulled by centuries of feet—and filled instead with long tables, brass scales, velvet trays, and the low murmur of appraisal. Brokers moved between the tables carrying sigils in lead-lined boxes. Buyers drifted along the edges with the unhurried confidence of people who had never in their lives been the merchandise. Overhead, where a god's face had once been carved into the vaulted ceiling, someone had hung a chandelier of those glowing glass spheres, dozens of them, and the light they cast was rich and golden and made everyone beneath it look prosperous.

Ash had a routine for the Row: walk the perimeter, read the auction board, note which brokers owed them favors, leave. Ten minutes, in and out. They'd done it forty times.

Tonight they got four steps inside before Kestrel stopped breathing.

Ash followed her gaze across the hall, to the auction board—the great slate by the eastern pillar where tomorrow's lots were chalked in a broker's careful hand—and to the column of names, and to the fourth line down.

**LOT 31 — SIGIL OF WREN QUINTANA, MALE, 19 YEARS. GRADE: UNUSUAL. RESERVE: BY SEALED BID ONLY.**

Kestrel read it the way she must have read everything, Ash thought—completely, instantly, the whole line at once—and then she read it again, slower, and Ash watched her hand rise toward the slate and stop an inch away, trembling, not quite touching the chalk letters of her brother's name.

"*Unusual,*" she whispered. "What does unusual mean? Why is his grade—Ash, why is the reserve sealed bid? Ordinary souls have posted reserves. I can see them. Why is his different?"

And Ash, who had been asking themselves the same question since the road—who had seen divine residue crawling up a dying scribe's arms and told themselves it was a fluke, a trick of the light, anything—looked at the board, and at the girl beside it, and felt the last comfortable lie they had left go out like a snuffed lamp.

Because Ash had spent eleven years in the Court, and they knew what *unusual* meant on an auction slate. Unusual meant bright. Unusual meant the kind of sigil that drew buyers from the deep terraces, the ones who never bid on ordinary stock, the ones who paid in favors and memory and names. Unusual meant that somewhere in the assaying vaults below their feet, someone had weighed Wren Quintana's soul and found something in it worth more than years.

And unusual meant that the soul in question had come from the same bloodline, the same house, the same parents—as the girl standing in front of Ash with divine light still ghosting under her skin, staring at her brother's price tag with her whole world burning down behind her eyes.

"Kestrel," Ash said carefully, "we need to talk about your family tree."

But Kestrel wasn't listening anymore. She was looking past the board, across the hall, to where a broker in dove-grey robes had emerged from the vault stairs carrying a lead-lined case, and where—at a table near the far pillar—a buyer had risen from their seat. Tall. Hooded. Moving with a patience that didn't belong to anything human.

The broker set the case on the velvet. Opened it.

And across the whole length of Sigil Row, above the murmur of trade, above the chandelier of stolen light, a sound rose from the little box—one thin, clear note, like a struck glass, like a boy's voice heard through a wall—and every broker in the hall went silent, and every hooded head turned, and Kestrel Quintana made a sound Ash would hear for the rest of their life.

"That's him," she said. "That's *Wren*."
