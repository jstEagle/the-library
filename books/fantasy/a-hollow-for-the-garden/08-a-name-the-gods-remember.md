## 8. A Name the Gods Remember

The shrine should not have been there.

Kestrel had walked this stretch of mountain road twice already, once on her own feet and once half-carried by Ash, and both times it had been nothing but a shelf of granite jutting from the slope, furred with lichen, unremarkable as any other stone between the valley's edge and the under-city's mouth. Now, in the hour when the light went the color of old honey, the rock stood open like a door.

Not broken. Open. The crack that split its face was clean-edged, deliberate, wide enough for a person to enter sideways if she held her breath and did not think about what she was doing. Inside, a light burned that had no source she could name—amber, but deeper than firelight, the color of resin held up to the sun.

She should have called for Ash. Ash was twenty paces down the switchback, arguing with a mule about a fallen pack, and Kestrel opened her mouth to shout—and did not. Something in the shrine's light felt like a hand cupped around a candleflame in wind. Private. Waiting.

*You were always going to come alone,* said a voice inside her own skull, gentle as dust settling. *That is how you were made.*

Kestrel's hand found the scribe's knife at her belt—the one tool of her trade sharp enough to matter—and she stepped through the crack before she could decide whether that was courage or compulsion.

* * *

The interior was larger than the stone. That was the first impossibility; the second was the floor, which was mosaic, thousands of tiles of green and gold arranged in a pattern she recognized with a lurch: the valley. The whole valley, rendered in miniature, every field and lane and rooftop of home laid out beneath her boots—except that here the valley had no border. The mosaic ran to the walls and kept going, up over the curved ceiling, mountains and beyond-mountains and a darkness at the crown of the dome where stars should have been but weren't. Where something else was. A absence shaped like attention.

In the center of the floor knelt a figure.

It had been a statue once, she thought. Or a god wearing the shape of one. It was perhaps ten feet tall if it stood, carved of some pale wood gone silver with age, and it was cracked from crown to knee—a single fissure, the same clean break as the stone outside, as though something had been removed from it long ago and the removal had never healed. Its face was worn smooth. No eyes, no mouth, only the suggestion of features, the way a face looks through fogged glass.

Moss grew in the hollows of its shoulders. Someone had left offerings at its knees: a clay cup of rainwater, gone green; a comb missing three teeth; a child's shoe, small enough to fit Kestrel's palm, crusted with mountain mud decades old.

"Hello," Kestrel said, because her mother had raised her to be polite even to things that frightened her, and because her voice was the only weapon she trusted in a place like this.

The statue did not move. But the light deepened, and the voice came again—not in her skull this time but in the air, resonant, the way a bell sounds after the striking has stopped.

*Vessel-of-the-Valley. Daughter-of-the-Tended-Line. Sela-un-Karesh.*

Kestrel flinched at the last word. It landed in her chest like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known she carried. "That's not my name."

*No,* the remnant agreed, and there was something in its tone that might have been sorrow, in a being so old that sorrow had worn smooth as its face. *Your name is the small one. The cage-name. We gave your line true names once, when we still had breath enough to speak them.*

"My line." Her voice came out flat. "My line is debtors. My line is a ledger in the temple scriptorium, page four hundred and eleven, Quintana, outstanding balance carried forward nineteen years. My line copies prayers for people who can't write them."

*Yes.* The light pulsed, slow, patient. *And who do you think taught the temple to keep ledgers?*

* * *

She sat down. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended their argument with her and folded, and she found herself cross-legged on the mosaic valley, on the tiny painted fields where she had grown up, looking up at a cracked god while it told her the story of her own blood.

"We bred you," she repeated. "The founders. The ones the temple says crossed the mountains fleeing the Sundering. The ones who built the border to keep magic out. They bred people. Like—" her mind reached for the comparison and hated it— "like breeding oxen. Like breeding wheat."

*Like tending an orchard,* the remnant said. *Do not mistake me. I do not excuse it. I am what is left of one of them—one of the nine who made the valley, who drank too deep of the world's divinity in the war-years and found they could not stop drinking. We built the border not to keep magic out, little scribe. We built it to keep our hunger fed.*

"The temple sells souls," Kestrel said. "Wren's soul. I know. I've seen the contract."

*Souls are the vintage,* the remnant said. *But souls ripen slowly, and the Court beneath grows impatient, and impatient things take what is near instead of what is ready. Your family's line was different. You were not planted to be harvested. You were planted to be waited for. Divinity does not grow in a soul that knows it is divine—it curdles, it rushes, it flowers wrong. So the founders seeded certain bloodlines with dormant inheritance and then buried the knowledge so deep that even the seed forgot itself. Generations of ordinary lives. Debts and prayers and copied scripture. A cage, yes—but a cage is also how you keep a thing safe until it is time.*

"Time for what?"

*Time for the valley to need saving.* The light guttered, and for a moment the statue seemed smaller, older, more broken. *We did not plan everything. Even gods are poor prophets. But we planned enough. Nine lines, little scribe. Nine seeds scattered through the valley's families, tended by the temple without the temple fully understanding what it tended. Eight have been harvested already—taken quietly, over the centuries, by the Court, by accident, by grief. You are the last. The last vessel of the Tended Line. And you walked out of the valley through a border built to kill anything with magic in it, and it parted for you like water around a stone.*

Kestrel looked down at her hands. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained, calloused at the middle finger from the reed pen, a scar on the left thumb from Wren's first attempt to teach her whittling when they were children. Hands that had written ten thousand prayers for other people's salvation and never once believed any of it applied to her.

"I'm not divine," she said. "I'm a scribe. I'm good at margins. I alphabetize. Last month I spent three days deciding whether to file a petition under 'miracle' or 'misunderstanding,' and I chose 'misunderstanding' because 'miracle' would have required a second signature."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of something very old considering her words with enormous care.

*Yes,* the remnant said at last, and now the sorrow in it was unmistakable. *That is exactly what we hoped you would be. Diligent. Humble. Careful with power because you never believed you had any. Do you see? The cage was not cruelty, Kestrel Quintana. The cage was craft. Every year you spent believing yourself ordinary was a year your inheritance slept safely. If the temple had told you what you were at twelve, you would have burned out like a lamp with too much oil poured in. If the Court had known, you would have been harvested before your first moonblood. So we let you be small. We let you be invisible. We let you resent your ledgers and love your brother and hide the depth of your literacy behind careful, mediocre penmanship—*

"Stop." Her voice cracked on the word. "Stop. How do you—"

*I watched,* the remnant said gently. *We all watched. Every vessel, every generation. It was the one mercy we permitted ourselves—to witness the lives we had engineered. I watched your grandmother refuse a bribe. I watched your mother teach you letters by tracing them in flour. I watched your father sign the contract that sold your brother, and I watched him weep afterward in the woodshed where he thought no god was looking.*

Kestrel's throat closed. She thought of her father's hands, which had smelled of sawdust and ink, and of the night he died, and of Wren going quiet afterward the way a house goes quiet when a load-bearing wall comes down.

"He didn't have a choice," she whispered.

*He had choices,* the remnant said. *They were all terrible. That is what debt is, little scribe: a narrowing of choices until the smallest one looks like freedom. The temple learned that from us. We invented the ledger. We invented the debt. We built a valley where salvation could be owed, because owed things can be collected, and collected things feed hungry gods. Everything you hate about your life, we designed. And then we had the arrogance to watch it, and call our watching mercy.*

The light dimmed further. In the gloom, the cracked statue looked less like a god and more like what it was: a hollow thing, a shell, something vast that had eaten itself down to a whisper.

"Why tell me this?" Kestrel asked. "Why now?"

*Because you asked the right question at the border,* the remnant said. *When it parted for you, you did not say 'why me.' You said 'what am I.' Most vessels never ask. They live and die in the cage and the seed sleeps forever, and that, too, we counted a success. But you asked. And asking wakes things.*

"I don't want it to wake."

*Too late.* Not cruel. Simply true, the way arithmetic is true. *It woke the moment you stepped through. The question is not whether you carry divinity, Kestrel Quintana. The question is what you will do with the carrying. The Court wants to harvest you. The temple wants to spend you. The founders' design wants to use you. Everyone who has ever touched your life has wanted something from the seed. Has anyone ever asked what *you* want?*

Kestrel sat in the mosaic valley, on the painted fields of home, and found that she was crying—quietly, without drama, the way she did everything, tears tracking down into the corners of her mouth. She wiped them with the heel of her hand and was furious at herself for it.

"I want my brother back," she said. "That's all. I want Wren whole. I don't want to be a vessel. I don't want to be the last of anything. I want to go back to the scriptorium and copy prayers and be annoyed about the ink rations and eat supper with my brother in the groundskeeper's cottage. I want my small life. It was mine."

*It was ours,* the remnant said softly. *That is the grief, isn't it? Not that the cage was cruel. That it was comfortable. That you loved the bars because they were the only shape you knew. Little scribe, I will not lie to you: you may never get the small life back. What wakes cannot fully sleep again. But hear this, and weigh it well.*

The light gathered itself. For a moment the statue seemed to stand, though its knees never moved, seemed to lean toward her across the centuries, and the amber glow filled the shrine like honey filling a jar.

*Divinity given freely is a different thing than divinity taken. The founders took. The Court takes. The temple takes. Everything hollow in this world became hollow by taking. But you—you were made to hold, not to seize. If you learn the difference, you may become something none of us planned. Something new. The last seed of a rotten orchard, growing into a tree no one planted.*

"And if I don't learn?"

*Then you will be harvested,* the remnant said, *and the valley will have no one left, and the hunger beneath the mountain will walk out through the border you broke, and it will be very hungry indeed.*

* * *

She stayed until the light failed. The remnant gave her what it could: names—the old names of the nine founders, most of which meant nothing to her; a warning—that the Court's gatekeeper could smell awakened vessels the way sharks smell blood; and one gift, pressed into her palm as she rose to leave. A tile. One piece of the mosaic, the size of a coin, showing a single painted field. When she closed her fingers around it, it was warm, and it stayed warm, and she understood without being told that it would always be warm, and that somewhere in the world there was a hole in a mosaic floor shaped exactly like it.

"What is it?" she asked.

*A promise,* the remnant said, its voice fading now, sinking back into the stone the way water sinks into drought-cracked earth. *A piece of the design that chose to be yours instead. Carry it. When the moment comes—and it will come, little scribe, the moment always comes—you will know what it is for.*

At the crack in the stone she paused, one hand on the lichen-furred lip of the granite, and looked back. The statue knelt in its failing light, worn-faced, fissured, patient. Smaller than it had seemed. Older. Sadder.

"Were you happy?" she asked. "Watching us. All those years. Were you happy?"

The silence stretched so long she thought the god had gone.

*No,* it said at last. *But we were present. In the end, little scribe, presence is all any of us managed. Be better present than we were.*

She stepped out into the honey-colored evening. Below her on the switchback, Ash had given up on the mule and was scanning the slope, and when their eyes met Ash's expression flickered—from annoyance to alarm to something quickly buried under professional cynicism.

"You've been in there an hour," Ash said, jerking their chin at the shrine, which was already closing, the crack sealing itself like a wound remembering it was stone. "That thing doesn't open for anyone. Forty years I've run this road, and it has never once—"

"It knew my name," Kestrel said. "My real one. Apparently I have one."

Ash stared at her. Then at the sealed stone. Then at her, again, longer, and Kestrel watched the smuggler recalculate her entire worth with the speed of someone doing sums they'd done before.

"Well," Ash said finally, swinging back up onto the road. "That explains the residue. Come on, then, Sela-whatever-it-was. The Court opens at dusk, and if we're not through the gate before the lamps are lit, the gatekeeper charges double—and trust me, girl, you do not want to owe that creature double."

Kestrel closed her hand around the warm tile and followed.

Behind them, the mountain waited. Beneath them, the Court was waking. And somewhere in the dark of her own blood, something that had slept for nine generations turned over, opened one eye, and began, patiently, to learn her name.
