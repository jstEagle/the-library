## 9. The Price of Passage

The gate to the Hollow Court was not a door. It was a debt.

Ash had known that the first time they'd seen it, eleven years ago, when they'd come down the mountain road with a smuggler's smile and a dead man's ledger and learned that everything beneath the mountain ran on the same currency: what you owed, and who you were willing to owe it to. The gate was a slab of black basalt set into the roots of the mountain itself, tall as a cathedral door and carved with faces—hundreds of them, thousands, each one a soul that had passed through and never come back out. Their mouths were open. Ash had never once looked at them long enough to wonder if they were screaming or singing.

Tonight they looked.

*Don't,* Ash told themselves, and looked anyway.

Kestrel stood at their shoulder, wrapped in Ash's spare coat, her hands still faintly luminous where the divine residue hadn't finished fading. She'd slept four hours on the road, woken screaming once—a name, not Wren's—and then gone quiet in a way Ash recognized from mirrors. The quiet of someone doing arithmetic with their own life.

"The gatekeeper," Ash said, "is called Vess. She'll ask what you're carrying. You say nothing. I do the talking."

"What if she asks you something you can't answer?"

"Then we're both dead, and you won't have to worry about your brother." Ash tugged the coat straight. "One more thing. Whatever happens down there—whatever you see me give up—you don't get to thank me for it."

Kestrel's eyes narrowed. "Why not?"

"Because thanks makes it real." Ash started toward the gate. "And I've spent eleven years pretending it isn't."

* * *

Vess received them in an antechamber hewn from living rock, lit by lamps that burned without oil—the flame fed on something else, something that made Ash's teeth ache to look at. The gatekeeper herself had been beautiful once, in the way of statues: marble cheekbones, a mouth carved for pronouncement. But the Court took its tolls in flesh as readily as coin, and Vess had paid in years. Her skin hung loose at the jaw. Her eyes were the color of old coins left in water.

"Ash Brindle." Her voice filled the chamber without effort. "The hedge-witch who sells salvation by the ounce. They told me you'd sworn off the mountain after the Marrowfield affair."

"I swore off getting caught. Different thing entirely." Ash spread their hands. "I need passage to the auction floor. Deep Court. And before you tell me no one gets there without standing, let me save us both the theater."

Vess smiled without warmth. "Passage to the deep Court is bought with standing, little witch. Standing, or blood, or souls. Which have you brought me?"

"None of those." Ash reached into their coat and drew out a slip of vellum, sealed with wax the color of dried roses. They held it between two fingers, the way one holds a live coal. "I'm spending a favor."

The temperature of the room changed. Kestrel felt it too—Ash saw her glance toward the doorway, toward the dark, some animal instinct telling her to run. Even the lamp flames leaned, as if listening.

Vess went very still. "That seal," she said slowly, "was issued by the Undermistress herself. There are perhaps five such favors outstanding in all the mountain. Every broker, every bone-counter, every hollow-eyed thing in this Court has wondered for a decade what you were holding, Ash Brindle, and why you never spent it. Men have killed for less than the *rumor* of that paper."

"I know. It's been very useful being rumored about." Ash set it on the stone table between them. "It buys two passages to the auction floor. Unmarked. Unwatched."

"It could buy you a seat beside the Undermistress. A district. Immunity for your whole network." Vess didn't touch the vellum. "You understand what you're trading away. That favor is worth more than your life. More than ten lives."

"It's worth exactly one thing," Ash said. "And I'm not spending it on my life."

Silence. Somewhere deeper in the mountain, water dripped—or something that mimicked water.

"You'll want to know why," Vess said at last, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than cruelty. "The Court likes to know why. Debts make more sense with reasons attached."

"No reason. Business."

"Ash." Vess leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the ruin of her face. "I have sat at this gate for sixty years. I have watched men sell their mothers and women sell their children and children sell themselves because the alternative was hunger. Nobody spends an Undermistress favor on *business*. What name are you carrying down there? Whose sigil are you going to stand in front of and pretend you don't recognize?"

Ash's jaw tightened. They could feel Kestrel watching them, and hated it—hated being seen from that angle, hated that the girl who'd collapsed on their road with godlight under her skin was about to learn the one thing Ash Brindle had built a career on never telling anyone.

"Brindle," Ash said finally. "Sera Brindle. Sold in the year of the Gray Frost. She'd have been nineteen."

The lamps flickered. Vess closed her eyes briefly, the way priests do.

"She came through my gate," the gatekeeper said quietly. "I remember her. Red hair. She argued the price for three hours—not for herself. For a boy back home. Her brother, I assumed." Her eyes opened, fixed on Ash. "She asked if the Court ever released souls that appreciated in value. I told her no. She said, *'Then he'll just have to be worth more than I was.'*"

Ash's throat worked. Eleven years, and the words still landed like a blade between the ribs. "That sounds like her."

"Is she why you became what you are? The smuggling, the ledgers, the network that touches every market on the mountain?" Vess shook her head slowly. "You've been searching the inventories for a decade. Don't insult me by denying it—I process the requests for archive access, and yours comes through every season like tax day."

"I stopped looking four years ago," Ash said flatly. "Souls don't appreciate. You told her that yourself."

"And yet here you are, spending the one favor that could have bought her back a hundred times over—to buy passage for someone else's brother." Vess turned her gaze to Kestrel for the first time, and Ash watched the gatekeeper read the girl the way brokers read contracts: the residue, the exhaustion, the ferocity banked behind fear. "Ah," Vess said softly. "I see. It's not the brother you're buying. It's the sister standing next to you."

"Don't," Ash said.

But Vess was already smiling, terrible and knowing. "You couldn't save Sera, so you're saving this one. That's not business, little witch. That's penance wearing a merchant's coat." She picked up the vellum at last, turning it over in her fingers. "The Court will honor it. Two passages, unmarked. But hear me, Ash Brindle, and hear me well: a favor spent is a shield broken. From tonight, you are only what you appear to be—a smuggler with no protections and many enemies. When the Court comes to collect whatever else it decides you owe, there will be nothing between you and the collection."

"I know what I'm buying," Ash said. "I've known for eleven years. I was just hoping I'd die before I had to pay for it."

Vess pressed her thumb to the rose-colored wax. It flared, guttered, and went out, and the vellum crumbled to ash that smelled—for one strange moment—of a kitchen, and bread, and woodsmoke. Sera's smell. Ash kept their face still through sheer professional discipline and thought: *there goes the last thing she ever gave me.*

"Passage granted," Vess said. "The auction begins at moonrise. Quintana's sigil is lot forty-one." She paused, and added, almost kindly: "The Court values promises above gold, scribe. Remember that, when the bidding starts. Gold will not save him."

* * *

They descended.

The stair into the deep Court wound down through the mountain's bones, past galleries where the old gods' abandoned temples had been gutted and repurposed—prayer halls become counting houses, altars become scales, saints' niches stuffed with ledger-books bound in leather that Kestrel looked at too long and then deliberately stopped looking at. Ash walked half a step ahead and did not explain anything, because explaining meant talking, and talking meant thinking about the ash-smell of bread and woodsmoke still clinging to the inside of their nose.

Kestrel broke first. Of course she did. Scribes always needed to narrate; it was how they digested the world.

"You had a sister."

"Had a contract with a sister's name on it. Different thing."

"You searched the inventories every season for a decade."

"I searched *markets*, girl. There's a difference. Markets are honest about being dishonest." Ash took the next stair sharply. "Drop it."

Kestrel dropped back a step—but she didn't drop it. Ash could feel her thinking, could practically hear the quill scratching behind her eyes. Then, quieter:

"Wren used to argue with the temple clerks. About interest rates. He was fourteen and he'd stand in the debt office and explain to grown men that compounding weekly was theft dressed as arithmetic." Her voice caught, steadied. "They fined him for insolence twice. He paid the fines by copying extra prayers. He never told me. I found the entries in his ledger after—" She stopped. Started again. "After our parents died, he stopped arguing. He just went quiet. I thought it was grief. I think now maybe he knew. Maybe he'd done the arithmetic on himself and understood what the numbers meant."

Ash said nothing. The stair turned. Below them, the first glow of the auction hall seeped up through the rock like heat from a forge.

"When Vess said your sister asked to be worth more than her price," Kestrel went on, relentless as transcription, "you looked like a man reading his own death warrant. I know that look. I wore it yesterday." She caught Ash's sleeve—not grabbing, just anchoring, the way one anchors a page against wind. "You spent the thing that could have saved her. To help me save him. Why?"

"Because someone should get out," Ash said, before the cynicism could catch up and stopper it. "Because I spent eleven years telling myself the mountain doesn't change, debts don't forgive, and the best you can do is profit from the rot. And then a temple scribe fell off the border of the world into my road with dead gods' light under her skin, asking me to help her steal a soul back from the Court, and for the first time in eleven years the rot looked like something you could actually fight." They pulled their sleeve free, gentler than they meant to. "Don't make a speech about it. Speeches are for funerals and auctions, and we're late for one of them."

They came down the last stair together, into the light.

* * *

The auction floor had been a god's nave, once. Ash could still read the architecture beneath the corruption: the vaulted ceiling where frescoes of harvest and mercy had been painted over with sigils of ownership; the altar dais now a bidder's box draped in black silk; the font, dry these thousand years, repurposed as a brazier where soul-sigils were warmed before sale to make them *sing*—a thin, sweet keening that carried through the whole hall and made the buyers lean forward like dogs at a whistle.

Lot forty-one stood on a pedestal near the center: a small glass reliquary, and inside it a curl of light the color of green wheat under a summer sky. Wren Quintana. Even contained, even sold, the boy's soul looked stubborn. Ash thought, absurdly: *he argues even in captivity.*

Around the pedestal, the crowd gathered—bone-counters in gray, Court factors in white masks, a fat broker whose rings clicked when he rubbed his hands. Above them, on a balcony of black iron, sat the auctioneer: a figure whose face was smooth as an egg, whose voice, when it came, seemed to arrive from inside the listener's own skull.

"Lot forty-one," the egg-faced voice said, and the singing light shivered. "A young male soul, valley-born, unspoiled, recently harvested. Note the provenance, honored bidders: temple-certified, debt-cleared, and—rarest of rarities—*unclaimed by any patron.* The Court opens bidding at three hundred weight of promise."

Gold meant nothing here. Ash had known that, had warned Kestrel on the stair, but watching her face as she understood it—that slow drain of color, the realization that every coin she'd imagined stealing, begging, borrowing was worthless paper in this place—was its own small wound.

"Three hundred," rasped the fat broker.

"Three-fifty," said a white mask.

Kestrel's hand found Ash's arm. "What do I bid?" she whispered. "I have nothing. I have *nothing*, Ash—"

"You have the one thing Vess told you they value." Ash turned and looked at her fully—at the residue still ghosting her knuckles, at the terror and the iron underneath it. "Promises. Sealed in blood. Listen to me, scribe, because this is the part that can't be taken back: whatever you pledge on that floor, the Court will hold you to. Not the letter. The *spirit*. They'll decide what your promise means, and they'll collect it whenever they like, however they like, for as long as they like. You could walk out of here today and thirty years from now something with a collector's voice will knock on your door and say *the Court calls in its claim.* Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Kestrel said.

"They could take your inheritance. The divinity in your blood. They could take your *future*—every year of it, owed forward."

"I said yes." Her chin came up, and for a moment Ash saw not the frightened copyist from the scriptorium but the woman who had stepped through the border of the world expecting to die. "He's my brother. Bid."

The auctioneer's voice rolled over the hall. "Four hundred. Four hundred fifty. Do I hear five hundred weight of promise?"

Kestrel stepped out of the shadows of the pillar.

Every masked head turned. Ash stayed where they were, heart hammering a smuggler's rhythm—*invisible, invisible, stay invisible*—and watched the girl walk alone into the light of a god's gutted church, toward the small green flame of everything she had left in the world.

"Honored Court," Kestrel Quintana said, and her voice did not shake, though her hands did, "I bid a promise."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The egg-faced auctioneer tilted, considering, and Ash felt the whole mountain lean in to listen.

"Name it," said the voice from inside everyone's skull.

"My inheritance," Kestrel said. "The divine blood in my veins—the portion that has not yet woken. I pledge it, sealed in blood, to the Hollow Court... contingent on one condition."

Murmurs. The fat broker's rings clicked. Somewhere above, the auctioneer waited with the patience of stone.

"Condition?" the voice prompted.

"He comes with me. Whole. Unspoiled. Tonight." Kestrel drew the little scribe's knife from her belt—the same knife she'd used to copy prayers for the illiterate, Ash realized, the same knife, repurposed—and opened her palm without flinching. "My blood for my brother. Witnessed and sealed."

The auctioneer was silent for a long moment. When the voice came again, it had changed—warmer, hungrier, the voice of something that has just found a flavor it intends to savor for decades.

"The Court accepts," it said, "with *delight*."

The knife flashed. Blood struck the stone floor, and the floor drank it, and high on its pedestal the little green light flared like a sunrise—Wren Quintana, singing.

And Ash Brindle, standing unseen in the shadow of a dead god's pillar, felt the fresh absence where the favor used to be, and the older absence where Sera used to be, and understood with perfect clarity that they had just acquired a family of two—and that the Court, which collected everything, would come eventually to collect them all.

Above the dais, the auctioneer raised a hand, and the great doors of the deep Court began, slowly, to open.

"One formality remains," the egg-faced voice said sweetly. "The seller wishes to meet the buyer."
