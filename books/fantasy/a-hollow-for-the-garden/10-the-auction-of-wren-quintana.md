## 10. The Auction of Wren Quintana

The Hall of Scales had been a temple once. Kestrel could see it in the bones of the place—in the vaulted ceiling where frescoes of forgotten gods had been scraped down to gray plaster, in the alcoves where altars had stood and now stood auction blocks draped in black silk. The Hollow Court did not destroy its holy places. It repurposed them, the way a scavenger repurposes a corpse: everything useful kept, everything sacred stripped.

She stood at the back of the crowd with Ash's knife against her ribs and Ash's warning ringing in her ears. *Don't bid with coin. Don't bid with favors. And whatever you do, don't let them see what you are until the moment you have to.*

The crowd was a lesson in what the Court valued. Near the front, merchants in lacquered masks that covered only their mouths—so the buyers could watch each other's eyes while hiding their words. Behind them, brokers with ledgers chained to their belts, souls' worth tallied in columns Kestrel itched to read and knew better than to try. Scattered through the middle, ordinary people with hollowed-out expressions, come to buy back what they'd sold or to sell what they couldn't bear to keep. And everywhere, threading between them like water finding cracks, the Court's agents in bone-gray livery, their faces smooth and ageless in a way that made Kestrel's skin crawl. The founders' servants. The founders' hands.

Above the auction block hung Wren's soul-sigil.

It floated in a glass reliquary no bigger than a lantern, turning slowly, catching light it shouldn't have been able to catch. Kestrel had expected—she didn't know what. A shadow. A wisp. Something ghostly and small. Instead she saw *Wren*: not his face, but the shape of him rendered in lines of pale gold, like a drawing done by someone who loved him. She could see the way he hunched his shoulders when he thought no one watched. The restless left hand he'd had since childhood, always folding paper birds out of scrap. Even here, even sold, even held in glass for strangers to appraise—the sigil fidgeted.

*He's still in there.* The thought hit her like a blow to the sternum. *He's still him.*

"Lot forty-one," called the auctioneer, a woman whose voice carried without effort, whose mouth-mask was silver instead of lacquer. "One soul-sigil, provenance: valley-born, temple-registered, debt-defaulted. Age of separation: eleven years. Condition—" she paused, and something in the pause made the crowd lean forward, "—unusually intact. The Court notes with interest that this sigil has resisted dissolution. Bidding opens at three hundred crowns."

Kestrel's stomach dropped. Three hundred crowns was more money than the scriptorium paid her in a decade. She had four coins and a stolen prayer-book and a knife borrowed from a woman who trusted her exactly as far as she could throw her.

Beside her, Ash murmured, "Unusually intact is bad. Intact means useful. Useful means the Court's own agents will bid."

And they did. A bone-gray figure near the front raised one finger. A merchant countered. The broker's chains rattled as numbers climbed—three fifty, four hundred, four seventy-five—and above them all Wren's sigil turned and turned, folding its restless hand around nothing.

Kestrel had known, walking in, that coin would never be enough. Ash had told her plainly on the road down from the pass: *The Court doesn't want your money. Money is what people use when they have nothing real to trade.* But knowing it and standing beneath the glass reliquary watching strangers put prices on her brother were different animals entirely, and the animal in Kestrel's chest was clawing to get out.

"Breathe," Ash said quietly. "You look like a woman about to do something stupid."

"I am a woman about to do something stupid."

"Good. Just do it *late*. Let them commit first. Desperation reads as weakness; patience reads as value." Ash's eyes stayed forward, masked-mouth hidden under a scarf, hands loose at their sides. "I've sold enough souls' worth of contraband to know how these rooms work. They smell hunger. Starve yours."

The bidding passed five hundred. The Court's agent raised a finger again, unhurried, and Kestrel understood with cold clarity that if the Court bought Wren's sigil, she would never get it back. Merchants resold. The Court *kept*—kept the way the founders kept, feeding on what endured.

Six hundred. Six-fifty. A merchant dropped out with a curse. The broker beside him wrote something and shook his head.

"Now," Ash breathed.

Kestrel stepped out of the crowd.

The room noticed her the way a body notices a splinter—a ripple of attention, small and sharp. She walked to the front, past the merchants and the brokers and the bone-gray agent who turned his smooth face toward her with an expression like curiosity stretched over emptiness. She stopped below the block, below the reliquary, close enough that if she jumped she might touch the glass.

"I don't bid in crowns," she said.

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Somewhere behind her, Ash exhaled through their teeth.

The auctioneer tilted her head. "Then you waste the hall's time, girl. Coin or silence."

"I bid a promise." Kestrel pulled the knife from her belt—Ash's knife, its handle warm from her grip—and opened her left palm before she could think better of it. "A promise sealed in blood, witnessed by the Court. That's currency here. Isn't it? That's why the gatekeeper took Ash Brindle's favor. Blood-promises outrank gold."

Murmurs moved through the hall like wind through wheat. The auctioneer's silver mask caught the light as she turned toward the wall—to the alcove Kestrel hadn't dared study too closely, where a scale the size of a coffin hung from iron chains. On one pan sat a stack of coins representing six hundred crowns. The other pan hung empty, waiting.

"The Court accepts blood-promises," the auctioneer said slowly, "from those with something worth promising. What do you offer, valley-girl?"

Kestrel looked up at the sigil. At the restless golden hand, folding and unfolding. *Hold on, Wren. I'm coming. I'm coming the only way I can.*

"I offer my inheritance," she said. "Whatever sleeps in my blood. I promise it to the Court—freely given, blood-sealed, collectible at a time of the Court's choosing."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on her eardrums. In the alcove, the empty pan of the great scale trembled—actually trembled, though nothing touched it—and the whole hall seemed to lean toward her the way flowers lean toward a window.

The bone-gray agent rose from his seat.

He crossed the floor without hurry, and up close he was worse: features arranged like a memory of a man, skin the color of candle tallow, eyes that reflected light without holding it. He stopped an arm's length away and breathed in through his nose, slow, deliberate. Tasting the air.

"There it is," he said softly. His voice was many voices, slightly out of sync with itself. "Dormant. Unopened. The founders will want to meet the vessel."

"I'm not a vessel. I'm a bidder."

"You're both." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anything. "That's what makes you valuable. Name your terms, little scribe."

"The sigil. Whole and unclaimed. No lien, no residue, no whisper left in it. My brother walks out of this hall free of the Court forever—and whatever I owe, I owe alone. Not him. Never him again."

The agent considered. Around them the hall had gone utterly quiet; even the brokers had stopped writing. Above, the sigil had stopped turning. It hung in the glass facing her, and Kestrel would have sworn—would have staked her remaining four coins—that the restless hand had gone still, pressed flat against the inside of the reliquary, the way Wren used to press his palm to hers across the dinner table when the debts got mentioned and neither of them wanted to hear.

"Agreed," said the agent. "The scale will judge the weight."

He drew a thin blade of gray metal from his sleeve and gestured to her open palm. Kestrel hesitated for half a heartbeat—half a heartbeat in which every sensible voice she owned screamed about contracts and traps and the high scribe's blank clause waiting for her signature—and then she thought of Wren sleepwalking toward the mountain, speaking a language no one knew, and she held out her hand.

The cut was precise and painless, which was somehow worse than pain. Her blood welled dark and slow, and the agent caught three drops on the flat of the blade and carried them to the scale.

He set the blade on the empty pan.

The chains groaned. The pan sank—not fast, but with terrible inevitability, the way a verdict sinks into a courtroom—and the pan bearing six hundred crowns rose, rose, rose, until it clanged against the beam above. The hall erupted. Brokers shouting revised valuations, merchants demanding to know what lineage produced a blood-promise worth more than six hundred crowns, the auctioneer's gavel cracking down for order and being ignored.

Kestrel barely heard any of it. She was watching the agent lift the reliquary from its hook and carry it down to her, and she was watching the sigil inside it press both hands now against the glass, and she was thinking, with a strange floating calm: *I just promised myself to the thing under the mountain. I just became a debt.*

"For lot forty-one," the auctioneer declared over the din, "sold—to the blood-promise of the Quintana girl. Witnessed and weighed. The Court thanks you for your patronage."

The agent held out the reliquary. "Your brother, whole and unclaimed, as bargained." His smooth face bent close, and his many-voiced whisper slid under the noise of the hall like a needle under skin. "Understand what you've purchased, vessel. You've bought him with yourself. The Court does not forget. The Court does not forgive. And the Court *always collects*."

"When?" Kestrel asked. Her hand closed around the glass. It was warm. "When do you collect?"

The agent straightened. For a moment his reflectionless eyes flicked upward—toward the mountain's roots, toward the deep places beneath the Hall of Scales—and something moved behind his face that might, in a living man, have been fear.

"Soon," he said. "Sooner than you'd like. The founders have waited a long time for blood like yours to walk willingly through our door." He stepped back, and the crowd parted for him, and he was gone into the gray of the hall.

*Kestrel.* Ash was beside her, gripping her elbow hard enough to bruise, steering her toward the doors. *Walk. Don't run. Running tells them the merchandise wants to escape.* But Kestrel couldn't make her feet obey properly; she kept looking down at the reliquary in her arms, at the sigil pressing against the glass, and halfway to the doors she stopped, right there in the middle of the hall, and thumbed the reliquary's seal.

The glass opened with a sound like a held breath released.

Gold light spilled over her hands—warm, warmer than it had any right to be—and the sigil rose out of the reliquary and hovered before her chest, trembling, and Kestrel did the only thing she could think to do. She pressed her bleeding palm against it.

The hall vanished.

For one instant she was nowhere, everywhere, drowning in a warmth that had a name—*Wren, Wren, Wren*—and somewhere inside the warmth a voice that was eleven years rusted and half-dissolved and unmistakably her brother's whispered:

*...Kess?*

Then the light collapsed inward, sank into her cupped hands, and lay there glowing faintly beneath her skin like a coal wrapped in cloth. Heavy. Alive. *Hers to carry.*

Around them the auction was already moving on—lot forty-two, a broker's soul, lightly used—and no one in the Hall of Scales cared that a girl stood in the aisle holding her brother's soul against her heart. That was the horror of the place, Kestrel thought. Not the cruelty. The *bookkeeping*. Everything weighed, everything entered, everything filed.

Ash pulled her through the doors into the mountain street, where the under-city's false dawn was kindling along the cavern roof, and only there, in the shadow of a defaced shrine, did they stop.

"You won," Ash said. Their voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone keeping themselves very still. "Do you understand what you won?"

"My brother."

"You also signed yourself over to the founders of this place. Whatever's sleeping in your blood, they now hold the deed." Ash's jaw worked. "I've seen blood-promises collected, Kestrel. The Court doesn't take payment. It takes the *payer*."

Kestrel looked down at her hands. Beneath the skin, the glow of Wren's soul pulsed gently, in rhythm—with her heartbeat, she realized. With her heartbeat. Already they were learning to beat together again.

"Then I'll pay them," she said, "the same way I paid everyone else in my life. Late, unwillingly, and after making sure they regret ever lending to me."

Despite everything, Ash laughed—a short, startled bark—and for a moment the smuggler's cynicism cracked and something almost fond showed through. Then the fondness died, because the bells began.

Deep in the mountain, bronze bells that Kestrel had never heard ring began to toll—not the measured peal of an hour, but an urgent, arrhythmic clamor, spreading outward through the under-city like a stone dropped in water. Doors slammed. Market stalls crashed shut. In the gray distance, bone-gray figures emerged from every archway at once, moving with terrible purpose, all of them flowing in the same direction.

Toward the Hall of Scales.

"What did you do?" Ash breathed.

"Nothing." Kestrel clutched the warmth in her hands. "I paid. I *paid*, that's all—"

But even as she said it, she remembered the agent's whisper, the flick of his dead eyes toward the mountain's roots. *The founders will want to meet the vessel.* And she understood, with a dread that settled into her bones like cold water, that the bells weren't calling collectors after her.

They were calling her *in*.

An invitation. From the things beneath the mountain. Delivered in bronze and panic to every ear in the under-city:

*Come. The founders are awake. The founders wish to speak with the Quintana girl.*
