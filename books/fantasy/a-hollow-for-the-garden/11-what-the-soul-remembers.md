## 11. What the Soul Remembers

The sigil broke like an egg.

That was the first thing Wren understood, in the moment after Kestrel pressed the cracked seal of amber to his chest and the Court's auction-house dust still hung in his throat: that a soul does not return the way a lost thing returns. It hatches. It comes out wet and screaming and remembering everything at once.

He was on the floor of a rented room above a noodle stall in the under-city, and his sister was holding him, and he could not remember how to be held.

"Say something," she whispered. "Wren. Say anything."

He opened his mouth. What came out was not a word. It was a sound from beneath the mountain, a syllable of the Court's trade-tongue, the language of ledgers and liens, and it tasted of cold iron and old honey. He clapped a hand over his own lips as if he could stuff it back in.

Kestrel went very still. In the lamplight her face was all sharp angles and dried tear-tracks, and there was a new mark on her wrist—a blood-seal, dark as a bruise, where she had signed away a promise to creatures who ate souls for supper. He knew what that mark meant. He had spent four years learning what every mark in the Court meant, because down there, marks were the only things anyone said honestly.

"You won," he managed. His voice came out rusted. "You actually—you shouldn't have."

"I should have done it four years ago." She laughed, or tried to; it came out broken in the middle. "You're free now. That's what matters. You're out."

*Free.* The word sat in Wren's chest like a stone dropped into a well, and he waited for the splash, and no splash came.

Because here was the thing Kestrel did not understand yet, cradling him on this filthy floor with the triumph still shining on her face: the Court had never kept his soul in a jar. A soul in a jar is inert. A soul in a jar is *wasted inventory*. No—the Hollow Court had put his soul to work. They had let it listen. Four years of listening, pressed up against the walls of their vaults, soaking in the whispers of the founders, the true ledgers, the debts written in names older than the valley itself.

And now all of it had come home with him.

* * *

He slept, or something near sleep, and dreamed in the Court's architecture: corridors of black glass, doors marked with sigils instead of numbers, and behind every door a voice reciting terms. *One soul, groundskeeper, aged nineteen, sold against future earnings by guarantors Tam and Ilse Quintana, payment rendered in full upon—*

Upon.

The dream always stopped there. Upon what? He had heard the clause completed ten thousand times in the vault-dark, and waking, he could never hold it. It slipped through his mind like water through a fist. But he remembered the tone of the voice that read it—not the dry recitation of a clerk but something almost gentle, almost pitying—and he remembered that after the reading, there had been laughter. Not cruel laughter. Worse. *Satisfied* laughter. The sound of accounts balancing.

He woke gasping in the grey hour before dawn, and Kestrel was awake too, sitting cross-legged beside the pallet they'd dragged him onto, watching him with those scribe's eyes that missed nothing and understood less than she thought.

"You were talking again," she said quietly. "In your sleep. That language."

"It's not a language, exactly." He rubbed his face. "It's more like—it's grammar for ownership. Every word is a contract. When I speak it, I'm not saying things. I'm *binding* things."

Kestrel absorbed this the way she absorbed everything, silently, filing it somewhere behind her eyes. "Then don't speak it."

"You think I haven't tried?" The bitterness surprised him, coming out of his own mouth. "Kes, you can't unlearn a language by wanting to. And you can't stop dreaming in it either. Do you know what I did down there, while you were copying prayers upstairs in your nice quiet scriptorium? I listened. That's all a soul can do when it's been sold. It listens. And the Court knows that. They *count on* it. Why do you think they keep souls conscious?"

"They keep them—" Her voice cracked. "They use them?"

"For storage. For collateral. For company." He laughed, and hated the sound. "You'd be amazed how much a founder will chatter to a soul pinned in a vault. We're furniture that hears. And I heard four years of it, Kes. I heard what the temple pays them. I heard which families sell first, and which ones get *encouraged*. I heard—"

He stopped.

The room was very small and very warm and suddenly he could not breathe in it. He got up, crossed to the window, shoved it open. Below, the under-city was waking: hawkers crying prices in three tongues, a godless shrine being swept by some hired penitent, the mountain's throat exhaling its perpetual sulphur breath. Somewhere down the street, a child was singing a skipping song about the Hollow King, and the words were wrong, garbled through generations, but the tune was old. Older than the valley. Maybe older than the song's own meaning.

"Wren." Kestrel's voice behind him, careful. "What did you hear?"

And there it was. The question he had been dreading since the amber seal cracked open against his chest. Because he loved his sister—he loved her with the whole ragged remainder of himself—but love and truth were different currencies down here, and he had learned the exchange rate the hard way.

"The night our parents died," he said, "they weren't coming home from the lowland market."

Silence. He heard her breathing change.

"The wagon was found on the mountain road," she said slowly. "Halfway up. Everyone said they got lost in the fog, that the mule spooked—"

"Everyone said." He turned around. His sister's face in the grey light looked so much like their mother's that it hurt to look at directly, so he looked at the wall instead. "Kes, I was there. Not my body. My soul, in the vault, listening. And two nights before the wagon was found, our parents' names came up in a settlement hearing. Their debt had been called due. All of it, at once—which never happens, unless someone wants it to happen. And the high scribe's own clerk was there, presenting the terms on the temple's behalf. And the terms included a rider. A little rider, tucked at the end, in the binding-grammar. I've translated it a hundred times since. It says: *in lieu of coin, one soul per guarantor, delivery voluntary or otherwise.*"

"Voluntary or otherwise," Kestrel repeated. Flatly. Like a scribe copying a line she didn't want to believe.

"Our parents refused to sign me over." Each word cost him. He made himself spend them anyway. "So the debt was accelerated. So the road home happened to be fogged. So the mule happened to spook. Kes—the Court doesn't take. That's the beautiful lie of it. The Court only ever *waits*, and lets people walk themselves into the ledger. Someone wrote our parents into that ledger, and it wasn't the founders. Founders don't climb. Founders send clerks."

Kestrel sat down on the pallet very slowly, the way people sit when their legs have stopped consulting them. "The high scribe," she said. "Master Ondrell."

"I don't know the name. I know a voice. Dry, patient, smells of vellum and lamp-oil. Reads contracts aloud like lullabies." Wren watched her go white. "You know him."

"He taught me my letters." Her hands had gone to her wrists, rubbing them as if the ink stains could be scrubbed off. "He taught me everything. He told me our parents' deaths were an accident of weather. He *wrote the condolence letter*, Wren. I copied it out fair because his hand was tired. I copied my own family's ruin in my best script and he praised me for the neatness of it."

The horror in her voice was so pure, so unarmored, that Wren almost took it back. Almost softened it. Told her that dreams were unreliable, that vault-whispers blurred, that grief makes patterns out of noise. All of it would even have been partly true.

But down in the Court he had learned one thing worth keeping: a contract does not care how it feels to read. Truth is truth whether or not it is bearable, and mercy that lies is just a slower cruelty.

"I'm not telling you to make you hate him," he said. "I'm telling you because you signed something last night. That mark on your wrist—I know what those are. Blood-promise, sealed to the Court's registry. And if Ondrell is who I think he is, then he already knows you have it, and he already knows what you bid, and Kes—he will come for you with terms. He'll offer you forgiveness. He'll offer to erase everything. That's what clerks do. They don't fight. They *refinance*."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll find out what 'voluntary or otherwise' means from the inside." He said it gently. Gently didn't help. Nothing would.

* * *

Ash returned near midday with bread, cheese, a bottle of something that smelled like it could strip varnish, and the particular swagger of a smuggler pretending yesterday hadn't happened. Wren had met them properly only once—briefly, in the chaos after the auction, a hard grip on his shoulder and a look that assessed him like cargo—and he still didn't know what to make of them. They moved like someone perpetually leaving a room. Even standing still, Ash seemed to be calculating exits.

"So," Ash said, dropping the food on the table. "The famous brother. How's freedom treating you?"

"It's heavy," Wren said.

Ash snorted. "Everything worth carrying is." Then they caught sight of his face—or maybe the way Kestrel wasn't looking at either of them—and the swagger dimmed a degree. "Ah. Hell. What did I miss?"

"Tell them," Kestrel said quietly. She hadn't eaten. She was sitting exactly where she'd been sitting for hours, and Wren realized with a lurch that she had not moved because moving would mean deciding something, and she was a woman built to decide things. "Tell them what you told me."

So he told it again—the settlement hearing, the rider, the fogged road, the satisfied laughter. He told it plainer this time, stripped of feeling, the way the Court taught you to say terrible things: as line items. Ash listened without interrupting, which Wren suspected was rare for them, and when he finished, Ash was quiet for a long moment, turning the bottle in their scarred fingers.

"Fogged roads," Ash said at last. "Spooked mules. Convenient accidents." Their voice had gone flat and distant. "My sister's soul was sold eleven years ago. Family debt. And you know what I was told? That she ran off to the coast with a tinker. Ran off. At fourteen." A short, ugly laugh. "I believed it for six years. Then I started running goods past the mountain, and I learned what the under-city does with runaways, and I did the arithmetic nobody wants to do. I've spent five years telling myself I'd buy her back someday, when I had enough. Enough of *what*, though? There's no enough. That's the whole design. The ledger has no bottom page."

Kestrel looked up at that. Something passed between her and Ash—some recognition, raw and wordless, of two people who had both been lied to by institutions wearing kind faces. Wren watched it happen and felt, absurdly, like a stranger at his own family's table. Which, he supposed, was exactly what he was. Four years gone. Four years listening. He had come back knowing everything about the world and nothing about his sister, who had apparently crossed the border of unmaking, bargained with gods, and bid her own inheritance at a soul-auction while he'd been sleeping off his rescue.

"When did you become like this?" he asked her, before he could stop himself.

She blinked. "Like what?"

"Like *that*." He gestured vaguely—at her, at the blood-mark, at the whole impossible shape of her. "You used to flinch when the high scribe raised his voice. You apologized to furniture. Now you're out-bidding the Hollow Court."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly filed away. "When did you become someone who talks in contract-language in his sleep?" she shot back. "We're allowed to have changed, Wren. We paid for it."

"We paid different prices, though, didn't we." It came out sharper than he intended, and he saw it land, saw her flinch the old familiar flinch, and hated himself for throwing it. "I'm sorry. I don't—Kes, I don't know how to do this. Be a person again. Down there, I was simple. I listened, I endured, I counted days. Now I'm out and everyone keeps looking at me like I'm saved, and I don't feel saved. I feel *delivered*. Like a parcel. And parcels don't get a vote about where they end up."

The room went silent. Ash, wisely, became fascinated by the cheese.

Kestrel crossed the floor and sat down next to him on the pallet, shoulder to shoulder, the way they used to sit as children on the temple steps, sharing one stolen apple between two mouths. She didn't touch him. She seemed to understand, somehow, that touch was too much right now—that his skin still remembered being inventory, and hands on him felt like handling.

"I can't give you back the four years," she said. "I'd burn the temple down for the attempt, but it can't be done. So I'll do this instead. I'll sit here. As long as it takes. You don't have to be grateful, and you don't have to be whole, and you don't have to be my brother today. Just be in the room. That's the whole ask."

Wren stared at the floorboards. His eyes burned. "That's a terrible contract," he said finally, voice cracking. "No term length. No consideration. Nobody writes clauses like that."

"Somebody has to be the first," Kestrel said.

And despite everything—despite the vault and the whispers and the dead walking in his memory—something in Wren's chest loosened, one thread of ten thousand. He leaned, very slightly, until his shoulder touched hers. It was not wholeness. It was not gratitude. It was barely anything at all.

It was a start.

* * *

The knock came at dusk.

Three knocks, evenly spaced, unhurried—the knock of someone whose time is valuable and who knows yours is worth less. Ash was on their feet before the third knock finished, knife out of their boot in a motion too smooth to be practice. Kestrel rose more slowly. Wren felt the binding-grammar rise in his throat unbidden, words of lien and claim stacking themselves behind his teeth like soldiers at a gate.

Through the door's thin wood, a voice. Dry. Patient. Smelling somehow of vellum and lamp-oil even through the plank.

"Sister Quintana." Master Ondrell, high scribe of the valley temple, speaking pleasantly into a stranger's rented room at the edge of the under-city. "And young Wren, restored to us—what joy. I've come with terms. May I come in? Or shall we conduct our business on the landing, where the neighbors might hear how generous the temple can be?"

Kestrel's hand found Wren's. Her grip was ice-cold.

Behind his teeth, the binding-grammar waited—and for the first time, Wren wondered whether the Court's language might cut both ways.
