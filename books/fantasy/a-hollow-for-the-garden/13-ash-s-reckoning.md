## 13. Ash's Reckoning

Ash had always known the debt would come due. That was the first thing the smuggling trade taught you: nothing is forgiven, only deferred. Every favor is a coin in someone else's pocket, and pockets get turned out eventually.

They'd sworn, years ago, never to spend the one they carried. A gatekeeper's favor, sealed in blood on the inside of Ash's left wrist—a thin brown line like an old scar, shaped vaguely like a key. *One passage through the Court's doors, no price asked.* Ash had won it back when they were nineteen, half-dead, having smuggled a dying priest's last confession past three checkpoints of the Hollow Court. The gatekeeper had pressed her thumb to Ash's wrist and said, *This buys you anything once. Spend it well or don't spend it at all.*

Ash had kept it for eleven years. Through the lean winters. Through the night their whole caravan nearly froze in the high passes. Through the long gray stretch of years when there was nothing left to want except the thing they could not have: a sister's soul, sold to the Court before Ash was old enough to know what a soul-sigil was.

They'd told themselves it was for Marisol. Someday. When they found her sigil in some auction lot, some collector's vault, some fat merchant's trophy case—then the favor would buy her back, and everything Ash had done since would finally mean something.

Instead they'd spent it on a scribe with ash-colored residue drying on her skin and a brother's name on an auction slate.

Ash sat on the cold steps of the waystation above the under-city, turning their wrist over in the gray pre-dawn light, watching the blood-seal fade from brown to pale gold as its power drained away. Spent. Gone. Eleven years of careful hoarding, traded for a stranger.

*Not a stranger,* said the part of Ash that was getting harder to silence. *The person who held your hand while you wept over a slate.*

"Couldn't sleep either?"

Kestrel stood in the doorway, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and old wool. Wren slept inside—truly slept, for the first time since his rescue, though his dreams made him murmur in that language no one in the valley knew. The soul-sigil hung at his throat now, dim but steady, like an ember banked for the night.

"Sleep's for people with clean ledgers," Ash said.

Kestrel came down the steps and sat beside them without asking. She'd learned that about Ash quickly—the not asking. It was the only currency Ash respected.

"The seal," she said quietly. "It's fading."

"Favors don't linger once spent." Ash flexed their fingers. "That's the point of them. You use it, it's used. No take-backs in the Court's arithmetic."

"You spent your one free passage. On us." Kestrel's voice did the thing it always did when she was working up to an argument—went flat and precise, a scribe reading terms aloud. "You should have saved it. For your sister."

"I've been saving it for eleven years, Quintana. You know what saving gets you?" Ash laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp. "A wrist with a scar on it and a sister who's been someone else's property since she was nine years old. Saving is just losing slowly."

Kestrel said nothing. Below them, the under-city smoldered with its eternal lamplight, the markets of repurposed temples glowing like coals in a grate. Somewhere down there, souls changed hands by the dozen. Somewhere down there, maybe, a sigil with Marisol's name still waited in a drawer.

Ash had looked. Three nights running, while Kestrel bargained and Wren healed, Ash had walked the market rows asking quiet questions of people who owed them. Nothing. No sigil, no record, no trace. Either Marisol had been consumed—fed into the mountain's hunger like grain into a mill—or she'd been sold so far up the chain that even the gatekeepers didn't know where she'd landed.

The not-knowing was worse than grief. Grief at least had a body to bury.

"Ash." Kestrel's hand hovered near theirs, not quite touching. "What happens now? You paid the gatekeeper. The debt's settled."

Ash looked at her—at this woman who'd crossed a border built to kill her, bid her own godhood at auction, and still somehow thought debts *settled*, like the world ran on honest arithmetic.

"No," Ash said. "The debt's *transferred*. That's how blood-seals work. I didn't erase what I owed the Court. I moved it. From me—" they tapped their own chest "—to whatever I spent the favor buying." They let that sit. Watched her understand.

"To Wren. To me."

"To the promise I made on your behalf. Which the Court will collect." Ash stood, brushing grit from their trousers, and forced their voice into the easy drawl that had gotten them through every bad negotiation of their life. "But hey, look on the bright side. Collection agents are slow. Bureaucracy's the same everywhere. We've got time to—"

The lamps below went out.

All of them. Every street, every stall, every shrine-window in the under-city, snuffed in a single breath, as if the mountain itself had inhaled.

Ash had seen a lot of things go wrong in eleven years of smuggling. They had a whole taxonomy of trouble, sorted by speed. This was the fastest kind: the kind where the world simply stopped pretending.

"They're here," Ash said.

* * *

They came up the mountain road at dawn, six figures in gray, walking in step like a single creature wearing six coats. No horses. The Court's collectors never rode; riding implied urgency, and urgency implied the outcome was in doubt.

Ash met them alone at the waystation gate, because that was how it was done, because protocol was the last armor anyone got to wear. Behind them, in the station's single room, Kestrel argued and Wren—pale, hollow-eyed, clutching his sigil—stood between them like a wall that didn't know it was made of paper.

"You'll stay inside," Ash had told them. "Both of you. This is my ledger. My ink."

Kestrel had grabbed their sleeve at the door. "Whatever they ask—"

"Quintana." Ash had smiled, and hated how easily the smile came, how many times it had lied for them. "I've been cheating creditors since before you could write your name. Let me work."

Now the six stopped ten paces out, and the one in front pulled back her hood. The gatekeeper. Of course. The Court sent the face you trusted to collect the debts you regretted—that was cruelty refined into procedure.

"Ash Brindle," she said. Her voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of someone reading a menu. "Eleven years ago I gave you a favor sealed in blood. Last week you spent it. Do you recall the terms?"

"One passage, no price asked," Ash said. "For myself and two companions. Terms met."

"Terms met," the gatekeeper agreed. "The passage was given. But a blood-seal doesn't forgive, Ash Brindle. It obligates. By spending the favor, you assumed the obligation it once covered. Your original debt to this Court—which you have carried, unpaid, since the night you stole a dead man's confession across our threshold." She smiled gently. "You thought the favor was a reward. It was a consolidation. We do love our consolidations."

The ground seemed to tilt. Ash kept their feet through sheer professional stubbornness. "And what does the Court say I owe?"

"Two things." She raised one finger. "First: the confession you smuggled. Its contents damaged certain arrangements. We require the damage repaired—you will return to our service as a courier, five years' term, and your little network with you. Routes, contacts, safehouses. All of it transfers to Court ownership."

Ash's stomach dropped through the mountain. Their network. Nine years of building it—every contact cultivated, every route mapped, every safehouse stocked with the particular care of someone who had nowhere else to belong. Forty-three people who trusted Ash Brindle to move what needed moving and ask nothing else. People who'd chosen Ash after everyone else had thrown them away.

"And the second thing?" Ash asked, though they already knew. The gatekeeper's eyes flicked, just slightly, toward the waystation door.

"The scribe. Kestrel Quintana. The Court holds claim to a promised inheritance, sealed in her blood at auction. We have decided to collect early." The gatekeeper's smile widened by a degree. "Deliver her to the Hollow Throne, and your debt dissolves entirely. Your network stays yours. And perhaps—" the smile turned almost kind, which was the cruelest thing yet "—perhaps we find a sigil for you. A sister's. There are drawers, Ash Brindle. Deep ones. Things even we forget we keep."

There it was. The whole trap laid out in the open, dressed in kindness, because the Court had learned long ago that fear makes people run but hope makes them walk.

Marisol. A drawer. A maybe.

Ash thought about their sister's face, which they could barely remember anymore—nine years old, gap-toothed, laughing at something Ash couldn't recall. Thought about forty-three names in a ledger sewn into their coat lining. Thought about a woman inside the waystation who'd held their hand over an auction slate and said, *your grief counts too,* as if that were a thing anyone had ever told them before.

"You know what the funny thing is?" Ash said. "I actually considered it. For about four seconds. That's longer than I'm proud of."

"We know," the gatekeeper said. "We counted."

"Yeah. You would." Ash reached into their coat—not for a weapon, though the collectors tensed—and drew out the ledger. Small, oilskin-wrapped, worn soft at the corners from nine years against their ribs. Every route. Every contact. Every safehouse. The whole architecture of the only life Ash had ever built.

"You want my network," Ash said. "Here's my counteroffer."

They struck flint to steel, and touched the flame to the oilskin.

The collectors moved fast—but not fast enough. Fire caught oiled leather the way gossip catches a small town, and Ash held the burning book in bare hands, feeling the heat bite, watching forty-three names curl and blacken one by one. The pain was enormous and clarifying, like a bell rung inside the skull.

"No!" The gatekeeper lunged forward, and for the first time the pleasant mask cracked, showing something underneath that was older and hungrier and very much not human. "Those routes took decades to—"

"Burn," Ash agreed cheerfully, through their teeth, through the smoke. "Funny thing about fire. Doesn't negotiate."

The ledger came apart in their hands. Ash dropped the ruin of it and ground the last embers under their boot, and stood there with palms blistered and smoking, breathing hard, lighter than they had felt in nine years and poorer than they had ever been.

"There," they said. "No network to transfer. No courier to conscript. Just me, broke and burned, exactly like I started." They grinned at the gatekeeper, and meant it, and the grin cost them everything they had left. "Your move."

The gatekeeper stared at the ashes. When she looked up, all pretense of pleasantness was gone, and what remained made Ash's hindbrain scream *run* in a voice inherited from ancestors who'd had good reasons.

"Then we take the alternative," she said. "The scribe. And the brother. And you, Ash Brindle, will watch, and live, and remember what your sentimentality purchased." She lifted one gray hand. "Collectors. The waysta—"

Fire answered her.

Not Ash's fire. It roared up from the ground around the waystation in a ring of blue-white heat, and Kestrel stood at the center of it, arms out, hair whipping in a wind that hadn't existed a moment before, divine residue blazing along her skin like veins of ore in dark stone. Her eyes had gone the color of the border at dawn.

"You were saying," she said, "something about collecting?"

Behind her, Wren stepped into the light—and he was speaking again, that unknown language pouring off him fluently now, and the mountain itself seemed to lean in and listen.

The gatekeeper's hand lowered slowly. Six gray figures regarded the burning ring, and Ash watched recalculation move through them like weather.

"Well," Ash said, staggering back toward the fire, toward their people, cradling their ruined hands. "That's going to complicate the paperwork."

The gatekeeper's gaze found Ash's over the flames, and her voice, when it came, was almost soft.

"You understand what you've done. The Court does not send collectors twice, Ash Brindle. Next time, it sends the mountain." She turned. "You have until the new moon."

Six figures in gray walked back down into the dark, and the lamps of the under-city relit behind them, street by street, like eyes opening.

Kestrel let the fire die and caught Ash before their knees finished giving out. "Your hands—"

"Are the least of my problems." Ash laughed—genuinely, helplessly—as she steered them inside. "Nine years, Quintana. Nine years of work. Gone in ninety seconds." They sank onto the bunk, blistered palms up, and found they were smiling and could not stop. "Best trade I ever made."

Wren crouched in front of them, studying the burns with his strange, distant eyes. Then he spoke—one word in that unknown tongue, gentle as falling snow—and warmth spread through Ash's hands like water, deep and strange, easing the pain to a dull throb. Whatever else the Court had put in him, some of it was kind.

"Told you I was worth more alive," Ash managed.

Outside, far below, the mountain rumbled once—a long, low sound, like something vast turning over in its sleep, or waking up.

The new moon was nine days away.
