## 14. The Hollow Throne

The gatekeeper's favor bought them a corridor that should not have existed.

It opened behind a wall of the under-city's market district—a seam in the stone that Ash pressed their palm against and whispered something to, something old and ugly, and the wall sighed apart like a wound reopening. Beyond it lay stairs going down into dark that was not merely the absence of light but the absence of *permission*, as though the mountain itself had decided this place should be forgotten and had done its forgetting thoroughly.

Kestrel went first. She did not know why she insisted on it—only that her blood had begun to hum the moment they passed beneath the Court's outer halls, a low resonant note like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, and the humming grew louder with every step downward. It felt less like fear than like recognition. Her body knew where it was going even if her mind refused to follow.

"You're glowing," Wren said quietly behind her.

"I'm not."

"A little." He held up his hand, and in its shadow she saw it: a faint gold tracery along her own knuckles, like veins of ore in grey stone, pulsing in time with the hum. "The deeper we go, the brighter. Whatever's down there—" he hesitated, and Kestrel heard him choose his words carefully, the way he had been choosing all his words since she'd pulled his soul-sigil from the auction block, since he'd woken in her arms remembering things no rescued man should remember, "—it knows you're coming."

"Good," said Ash, from the rear, where they always walked, where they could watch both the path behind and the two of them ahead. "Let it set out plates."

But Ash's voice had lost its smuggler's levitation somewhere around the third turning of the stair. Kestrel had learned to read them over these weeks—the way their silence sat differently depending on what it was carrying—and this silence was heavy with a knowledge they were still deciding whether to share.

"Ash," she said, not stopping, not turning. The stair was narrow and the dark pressed close and she found it easier to say things to the back of someone's head. "You've been here before. To the heart."

A long pause. Footsteps. Then: "Once. Years ago. Looking for my sister's sigil, before I knew better than to look." Another pause, longer. "I got as far as the antechamber. There was a door, and voices behind it, and the voices—" Ash laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "You'll hear them yourself in a minute. No point spoiling it."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not a comforting person. I'm a person who gets you through doors." Their hand landed briefly on Kestrel's shoulder, squeezed once, let go. "Whatever you see down there, whatever you *feel*—and you will feel it, Kestrel, it will reach into you like a hand into a drawer—remember it wants you to feel it. That's how it feeds."

*How it feeds.* Kestrel turned the phrase over as the stair descended, and descended, and the air grew warm and thick as breath.

* * *

They came out into a cavern so vast that Kestrel's eyes refused, at first, to give it scale.

Stalactites hung from a ceiling lost in darkness above, some of them thicker than temple towers, and from their tips dripped water that fell for seconds before striking pools below—each drop landing with a sound like a small bell, like a coin dropped into an endless purse. And between the stalactites, filling the cavern's middle distances, stood pillars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Pale columns rising from floor to unseen roof, each one carved—or grown—in spirals and whorls that caught the faint gold light of Kestrel's own hands and threw it back warped.

She was three steps into the cavern before she understood what the pillars were made of.

Souls. Not souls as the temple described them—not the neat abstraction of the ledger, *one soul, valued at*, not the glowing sigils in their velvet cases at auction. These were souls the way rivers are water: poured together, layered over centuries, compressed by weight and time into translucent stone. Each pillar was a life. Some were thin as candles. Some were vast as cathedrals. And as Kestrel's light touched them, they *responded*—a shiver running up the nearest column, faces surfacing in its depths like fish rising to feed, mouths opening on silent words.

She read their lips without meaning to. They were praying. After all this time, after everything—they were still praying.

"Oh," Wren breathed beside her. His hand found hers. His grip was cold. "Oh, Kess. This is where they put us. This is where we *go*."

"Not all of us," Ash said grimly. "Some get spent. Coin gets spent. But most—" they gestured at the forest of pillars, and their voice cracked on the gesture, just slightly, just enough. "Most get *saved*. That's the joke of it. The Court doesn't destroy souls. It hoards them. It's the richest miser in the world, and the currency is everyone you've ever mourned."

Kestrel walked to the nearest pillar and laid her palm against it.

The soul inside flared toward her touch like a moth toward flame—and then, recognizing something in her, recoiled. She felt its terror as clearly as she felt the stone's chill: a terror shaped like worship, like a prayer offered too many times to something that never answered, until the pray-er forgot there had ever been anything else to do.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. She didn't know who she was apologizing to. All of them. Any of them. "I'm going to stop this."

The pillar shivered again—but differently now. Leaning toward her, almost. Like a flower toward sun.

Behind her, Ash made a low sound. "Don't do that. Don't promise things to souls, Kestrel. Promises are currency here. You've already spent more than you know."

*The promise of her inheritance,* sealed in blood at the auction. Yes. She carried that debt like a second heartbeat. She straightened, wiped her palm on her coat, and looked toward the far end of the cavern—where, past the last ranks of pillars, the rock opened into a hall of worked stone, ancient and vast, and within it burned a light that was not gold and not fire and not any color she had a name for.

The throne room. The heart of the Hollow Court.

And standing before its entrance, as though they had been expected all along—which, Kestrel realized, they had—were figures in robes of undyed wool, their faces hidden, their hands folded. Temple robes. Valley robes. The high scribe's own order.

"They followed us," Wren started.

"No," Ash said. "They were summoned. There's a difference." Their jaw tightened. "Down here, everything is either owned or owed. We're owed."

* * *

The antechamber was smaller than the cavern, human-scaled, and it was here that Kestrel finally understood why Ash had gone quiet on the stairs.

The walls were covered in names.

Not carved—*grown*, in the same pale soul-stone as the pillars, letters rising out of the rock like scar tissue. Tens of thousands of names, in scripts spanning centuries: the angular temple hand she copied every day; older scripts, flowing and strange; scripts she had no name for at all. And threaded among them, unmistakable even across the gulf of years—

Quintana. Quintana. Quintana.

Her family name, repeated down the wall like a refrain. Her grandmother's hand she recognized from the old household ledger. Names she didn't know, generations of them, reaching back further than the valley itself claimed to have existed.

And near the bottom, in her mother's careful script: *Wren Quintana*. And beside it, in her father's: *Kestrel Quintana* — unsigned. A blank space where a signature should be. Waiting.

"The founders' rolls," Ash said softly. "Every soul the Court ever took. Every contract ever signed. My sister's name is here too. Third gallery over—I checked, that day, before I ran." They didn't look at the wall. They looked at the door beyond it, the great door of black wood banded in metal that was not iron, and their voice went flat and hard. "The people who built your valley wrote their families into this wall, Kestrel. Think about what that means. Think about *when* it means."

Kestrel thought about it, and the thinking was like stepping off a ledge.

The valley was founded, the temple taught, by refugees fleeing a world full of magic—a world of chaos and predation, saved by the founders' wisdom in building the border, the blessed emptiness where prayers rose unanswered and no power could touch the innocent. The founders were saints. The border was mercy.

But the wall in front of her said otherwise. The wall said the founders had known the Court intimately. Had sold to it. Had *signed* to it, generation after generation, their own children's names entered in the rolls like entries in a ledger.

The border wasn't a refuge from the Court. The border was a *farm fence*.

"We need to go in," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Nothing felt steady. Everything felt like the moment on the stair when the ground tilts and you learn which way you've actually been leaning all along.

"Kestrel." Wren's grip tightened. "The whispers. In the Court, when I was—when I was only a sigil, they talked. About the ones on the thrones. They said—" He swallowed. "They said the gods used to be people. That's all. They said it like it was the saddest thing in the world."

Ash pushed open the door.

* * *

The throne room was a garden.

That was her first thought, absurdly—that someone had planted a garden underground and forgotten to tell the sun. Pale growths climbed the walls, soft-fronded, luminous, beautiful in the way that deep-sea things are beautiful: gorgeous and wrong, evolved for a darkness that never ended. Paths of white gravel wound between beds of the pale growth, and the air smelled of honey and rot in equal measure, sweet and spoiled at once.

At the room's center, on a dais of fused soul-stone, stood the thrones.

There were seven of them. Not chairs—Kestrel saw that immediately—they were shapes that had once perhaps been chairs, or perhaps had been people kneeling, grown over and merged and hollowed, each one occupied by a figure that might once have been called a god.

They were vast and they were starved. That was the second thing she understood. The figures on the thrones wore bodies the way a famine wears a country: skin stretched over shapes that suggested grandeur long collapsed, faces that had been beautiful in the way mountains are beautiful and were now beautiful the way ruins are. Light moved beneath their skins—not the warm gold of Kestrel's own inheritance but a cold, ravenous silver, pooling in their hollows, dimming and brightening with a rhythm like slow breathing. Like appetite.

Seven founders. Seven saints of the valley. Seven hungry things on seven thrones, sustained by the pillars in the cavern beyond, fed for four hundred years on the prayers and souls of the people they had fenced into the dark.

One of them turned its head toward Kestrel.

Its face had been a woman's, once. Kestrel knew it the way she knew her own reflection—not because she'd ever seen this face, but because she saw its bones in her own every morning. The same cheekbones. The same deep-set eyes. The founder looked at her with an expression that was not menace and not welcome but *recognition*, terrible and tender, the way a starving woman looks at bread.

"*There* you are," it said. Its voice filled the garden like pollen, soft and everywhere. "Little vessel. Little cup. We have waited so patiently to be filled."

Kestrel's knees nearly buckled. Not from fear—from *kinship*. That was the horror of it. Some part of her, the part that hummed, the part the gods had cultivated through four hundred years of careful breeding, leaned toward the throne with something like homesickness.

She gripped Wren's hand and made herself speak. "You're the founders."

"We are what the founders became." The woman-thing smiled, and the smile was her grandmother's smile, her mother's, the one Kestrel made in mirrors without knowing. "We built the border, child. We built it to keep the world's magic *out*, yes—but mostly to keep our garden's magic *in*. Do you know how divinity is made? Not born. *Grown*. Seeded in bloodlines, tended across generations, ripened in silence where no other power can compete for the harvest." Its silver eyes moved over her, weighing, appraising, loving. "You are the finest harvest we have ever grown. And you walked here *yourself*. We hardly had to call."

Beside her, Wren made a wounded sound. Ash had gone very still, one hand drifting toward the knife at their belt in a gesture that was pure habit and perfectly useless.

"You're lying," Kestrel said.

"We are many things, but we have never needed to lie." The founder rose from its throne—it unfolded upward and upward, taller than the hall should have allowed—and the other six stirred, their attention converging on Kestrel like heat. "Corruption is not a fall, little scribe. No one falls. We chose, each morning, for four hundred years, to take a little more rather than a little less. That is all hunger is. A choice, repeated, until it stops feeling like a choice." It extended a hand—long-fingered, elegant, patient. "Your temple sells souls to feed us. Your parents signed your brother away to settle debts to us. Your whole valley kneels each dawn and sends its longing down the mountain into our mouths. Everyone serves. The only question we have ever asked anyone—" the hand hovered, open, generous, monstrous, "—is whether they will serve *knowingly*."

The garden waited. The six thrones waited. Somewhere beyond the walls, ten thousand souls prayed into the silence.

And Kestrel Quintana, copyist of prayers, keeper of small debts, felt the fragment of divinity in her blood rise toward that open hand like water finding its level—and understood, with perfect clarity, exactly how easy it would be to say yes.

Then she saw the high scribe step out from behind the seventh throne, robes immaculate, ledger in hand, smiling like a man whose trap had closed precisely on schedule.

"Scribe Quintana," he said. "We really must discuss the terms of your contract."
