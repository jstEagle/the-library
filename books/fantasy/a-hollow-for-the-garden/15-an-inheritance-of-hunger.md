## 15. An Inheritance of Hunger

The throne room had no throne.

Kestrel understood that now, standing on the black glass floor with Ash's knife warm in her sleeve and Wren's soul-sigil beating against her sternum like a second heart. The chamber beneath the mountain was vast and ribbed like the inside of a throat, and at its center, where a throne should have been, there was only a shallow basin of stone—dry, cracked, ancient—and four figures seated around it in a ring.

They had been beautiful once. She could see it the way you could see the shape of a burned house in its foundations: the long bones, the grave dignity, the faces that still held the architecture of leadership. But beauty had been eaten out of them. Their skin was the gray of old candle smoke. Their eyes were wells with no bottom rope. Where their hearts should have beaten, something else moved—a slow, tidal shifting, like water under ice.

The founders. The valley's first protectors. The gods beneath the mountain.

*The hungerers*, said the voice in her blood, the fragment of divinity she carried, small and cold and awake.

"You are Quintana's daughter," said the one who had been a woman. Her voice was beautiful the way a landslide is beautiful—all gravity, no mercy. "We have waited a long time for you."

"I've heard that before," Kestrel said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Behind her, at the mouth of the passage, she could hear Ash breathing—quick, controlled, the breath of someone calculating exits. There were none. The walls had closed behind them like a throat finishing a swallow. "From a cracked shrine. From your gatekeeper. Everyone keeps telling me I was expected."

"Because you were." The woman-founder rose. The movement was wrong—not stiff, but *economical*, as though motion cost her something precious and she refused to waste it. "Sit, child. No—" a gesture, almost gentle, as Kestrel's hand went to her sleeve, "—keep your knife. We are not afraid of iron. We were afraid of nothing for a very long time, and we have learned to regret it."

Kestrel did not sit. She stood where she was, on ground that hummed faintly against her boots, and made herself look at each of them in turn. Three men. One woman. Founders' names from the temple's oldest liturgies surfaced unbidden: Aldous. Merrin. The twins, Corvan and Corvane. She had copied those names ten thousand times in the scriptorium, in gold ink, above prayers asking them for rain and safe births and forgiveness of debts.

They did not look like anything that forgave debts.

"You know what we are," the woman said. It was not a question.

"The temple says you're gods."

"The temple says many things." Something moved across the woman's face—an expression too ruined to be called a smile, but shaped like one. "The temple says the valley is protected. The temple says magic is poison. The temple says souls ascend when they die, whole and singing. And every year the temple sends us a tithe of souls down the deep road, and every year the valley sleeps on, dreaming safely, because the temple has taught it that ignorance is a kind of grace." She spread her gray hands. "We are not gods, Kestrel Quintana. We are what people become when they try to be."

* * *

They told her the story without being asked, and that frightened her more than anything—their eagerness, their terrible patience, the way all four voices braided together until it was hard to tell whose memory was speaking.

They had been the valley's founders. That much was true. They had come over the mountains eight hundred years ago fleeing a war whose name had been deliberately forgotten, and they had found a green country and built a town and raised walls against the dark, and it had not been enough. The dark came anyway—in the form of a blight, or a horde, or a god; the story changed depending on which founder spoke. So they had done what desperate leaders do. They had gone looking for power.

"We found divinity," said the twin on the left, his voice a dry rustle. "Not a god. Divinity itself—the raw substance, pooled in the roots of this mountain like water in a cistern. Unclaimed. Unshaped. We drank."

"It saved the valley," said the other twin.

"It saved the valley," the woman agreed, and her voice cracked like river ice. "And then it began to eat us. That is the part no hymn remembers. Divinity is not a gift, child. It is an appetite. It poured into us and hollowed us out and left us hungry, and the hunger does not end. We fed it our own humanity first—our names, our faces, our capacity to sleep. Then we fed it the mountain's creatures. Then the years took everything else, and we learned that souls quiet the hunger longest of all."

"So the temple," Kestrel said. Her hands had gone cold. "The debt system. The tithes. You built all of it."

"We built none of it," said the man who must have been Aldous, the eldest, his voice like stone settling. "That is the elegance of it. We were starving, and the first temple priests found us—we do not remember whether they sought us out or stumbled down the deep road by accident—and they saw what we were, and they understood immediately what we could be *used* for. A hidden god is a lever. They built the ledgers. They invented the debts. They taught the valley that safety has a price, and they set the price themselves, and they have grown rich and fat on a fear they manufactured while we grew thin on the souls they sold us." His well-eyes fixed on her. "You hate the temple. Good. Your hatred is accurate. But understand: we are not the temple's masters. We are its larder. Its furnace. Its dirty secret buried under a mountain of hymns."

"And my brother," Kestrel said. "Wren. His soul. My parents' debt."

"A payment," the woman said simply. "One soul among thousands. The temple collects; we consume. He would have been a whisper in the dark by winter's end, if you had not taken him back." She tilted her head, and the motion was birdlike and awful. "Which brings us to why you are here, and why we have let you walk this far, and why the gatekeeper's price was so strangely easy to pay."

Kestrel's hand found the sigil at her breastbone through her shirt. It pulsed once, warm, like a sleeping animal acknowledging her.

"You want me," she said.

"We want what is *in* you." All four founders leaned forward at once, a single synchronized motion, and for one vertiginous moment Kestrel saw them as they truly were—not four figures but one hunger wearing four masks, a single mouth with four ways of opening. "Your bloodline, child. Do you imagine it was accident? Your ancestors were ours—temple-bred, generation upon generation, vessels cultivated to hold divinity without breaking. Every few centuries the line thins and fails, and the temple quietly marries it back into strength. You are the finest vessel in nine generations. The residue on your skin when you crossed the border—you wore it like a crown and didn't know it. The fragment you carry woke the moment you stepped past the wardstones. We felt it. After eight hundred years of eating scraps, we *felt* it, the way a dying man feels rain."

"We offer you a trade," said Aldous, and his voice went soft, and softness from that mouth was worse than any roar. "You carry a seed of what we swallowed whole. Give it to us—willingly, freely, for forced gifts sour in the stomach—and we will give you everything."

The chamber changed. Or perhaps it didn't change—perhaps only her perception did, the fragment in her blood unfolding like a lens, showing her what the founders wanted her to see.

She saw the valley. Not as she had left it—terraced fields, whitewashed temple, the border stones humming their flat dead note—but whole and golden and *safe*. She saw Wren whole, laughing, his silence healed, working the temple gardens he loved with dirt under his nails and no contracts bearing his name anywhere in the world. She saw Ash forgiven, reinstated, their smuggler's sins expunged, a shop in the lower town with a painted sign. She saw the ledgers of the temple burned and the debt-slaves freed and the high scribe kneeling in the market square confessing everything. She saw the border come down—not violently, not in storm and ruin, but gently, like a wall becoming a fence becoming a hedge becoming a memory—and magic flowing into the valley like spring water into a dry field, and prayers answered everywhere, in kitchens and cradles and hospital wards, answered *kindly*, because she would make them answer kindly, because she would be there, watching, forever—

Forever.

"There it is," the woman whispered, and her ruined face was radiant with longing. "You feel how easy it would be."

And Kestrel did.

That was the horror of it—not the temptation's size but its *ease*. She had expected the offer to come dressed in fire and thunder, demanding some monstrous price, some ritual atrocity she could point to and refuse. Instead it asked for almost nothing. A fragment. A sip from a cup she'd been born carrying. And in exchange: Wren. Ash. The valley. Everything she loved, secured in a single gesture, guaranteed for eternity, signed in the smallest possible coin.

Her hand was already rising. She watched it rise as though it belonged to someone else, palm turning upward, the fragment in her blood leaning toward the four gray figures like a plant toward light. She could feel them waiting, patient as mountains, and underneath the patience something ravenous, and underneath the ravenousness something that might, in a language older than hunger, have been *relief*—

*No.*

The word was not hers. It came from the fragment itself, cold and clear, and Kestrel seized it like a drowning woman seizing a rope.

Because she had seen it. In the golden vision, in the half-second before the wanting swallowed her, she had seen the basin at the center of the room—dry, cracked, empty—and she had understood what it was. A feeding trough. Eight hundred years old. And she had looked at the founders' faces, at the beauty eaten out of them, and understood that they had not chosen this hunger once, at the beginning, in desperation, facing the dark.

They chose it *every day*. Every soul they ate was a morning's decision. Every year the temple sent its tithe and every year they opened their mouths, and somewhere in eight centuries there had been a thousand mornings when one of them might have said *enough, let us starve, let us end as ourselves instead of continuing as this*—and no one ever had. The hunger was not a fall. It was a practice. It was a discipline, kept daily, perfected.

And she had felt, in the golden vision, exactly how good it would feel to keep it.

"No," Kestrel said aloud.

The chamber went very still.

"My brother's freedom isn't mine to sell," she said. Her voice shook and she let it. "Neither is Ash's pardon. Neither is the valley's safety. You're offering me the world, and the price is that I become the thing the world needs protecting *from*—and I've read enough ledgers to know a bad contract when it's read to me slowly." She closed her open hand. The fragment in her blood recoiled from the founders like a cat from water, and she felt, absurdly, proud of it. "You were people once. People who were afraid, who reached for power to save what they loved, and it ate you, and instead of stopping you made feeding it your life's work. I'm not going to join you. And I'm not going to feed you."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind her, Ash exhaled.

Then the woman-founder laughed.

It was a terrible sound—rust and gravel and something genuinely amused underneath, and Kestrel realized with a lurch that the amusement was real, that these things still contained laughter, that the hunger had never managed to eat *that*.

"Oh, vessel," the woman said. "Oh, perfect little vessel. Did you think we wanted your *consent*?" She rose fully now, unfolding to a height no human spine should allow, and the other three rose with her, and the wells of their eyes filled with a light that was not light. "Consent sweetens the meal. Consent is vintage wine. But we have drunk unwilling souls for eight hundred years, child, and we are still here. If you will not pour yourself out for us—"

Aldous's hand closed around the empty basin, and the black glass floor began to sing.

"—then we will take the cup from your hands."

The fragment in Kestrel's blood flared white-hot, and far above her, faint through eight hundred feet of mountain, she heard—or imagined she heard—the border stones begin, very softly, to crack.
