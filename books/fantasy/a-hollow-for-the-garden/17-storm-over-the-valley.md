## 17. Storm Over the Valley

Ash had smuggled contraband past wardens, bribed gatekeepers with smiles sharper than knives, and once talked their way out of a Hollow Court cell by convincing the jailer he'd already been released. They were, by their own honest accounting, very good at not being afraid.

So it was infuriating to be afraid now.

They stood at the valley's edge with Wren Quintana on one side and a border that was coming apart like wet paper on the other, and Ash was afraid in a way that had nothing to do with dying. The unmaking of the border had started as a sound—a low, tectonic groan beneath the mountain's roots—and then Kestrel had walked into the wall of nothing that had kept magic out of the valley for three hundred years, and the wall had *listened to her*, and now it was unraveling thread by golden thread into the dark.

"Tell me again why we're standing here," Ash said, "instead of running in the opposite direction like sensible criminals."

Wren didn't answer right away. He hadn't answered much since his soul came back to him; he spoke the way a man speaks who has spent years listening to whispers and is still deciding which voice is his own. But when he did speak, his eyes stayed fixed on the fraying border.

"Because she asked us to hold it."

"She asked us to hold a *border*," Ash said. "A thing made of absence. With what, exactly? Our winning personalities?"

"With what crosses," Wren said simply.

And that, Ash thought, was the whole problem with the Quintana siblings. They said things like that—plain, quiet, unbearable—and then they turned out to be right.

* * *

The first thing through the breaking border was not a monster. Ash had braced for monsters. Monsters they knew how to handle: monsters could be bribed, blinded, or outrun.

The first thing through was a prayer.

It came off the mountain like weather—a pressure change, a taste of copper and rain—and somewhere down in the valley, in some farmhouse Ash would never see, someone's desperate little wish got *answered*. Ash felt it happen the way you feel a door open in a far room: a draft of something vast moving past. A sick child's fever broke. A well that had run dry filled with water that tasted faintly of lightning. Small things. Enormous things. Three hundred years of unanswered prayers, stacked up behind the dam like floodwater, and Kestrel had just pulled the first stone loose.

"Oh," Ash said quietly. "Oh, this is going to be a *mess*."

Wren almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a smile Ash had seen from him since the Court. "Good mess or bad mess?"

"There's no such thing as good mess, kid. There's only mess you get paid for and mess you don't." Ash shifted the strap of their pack—the last of their kit, the tools of a trade that had maybe two days left to live—and squinted down the valley road toward the temple district, where lights were flaring awake one after another, like a body realizing it had been touched. "This one we don't get paid for."

The storm proper arrived an hour before dawn.

It came down out of the mountains as visible wind—not the gray wind of the valley but wind threaded with light, carrying seeds of old magic that had waited generations in the high crags for exactly this. It hit the terraced fields and the barley *sang*. It hit the shrine at the crossroads, the little painted one where farmers left eggs for a god whose name the temple had struck from the records, and the shrine cracked open like an egg itself, and something inside it stretched, delighted, and began granting wishes indiscriminately to anyone within earshot of the road.

Ash watched a farmer drop to his knees in front of it and weep because his lame mule was suddenly, visibly, walking without pain. Then the god remade the mule's other three legs too, just to be thorough, and the farmer started laughing instead, hysterically, and couldn't stop.

"That's going to be most of them," Wren said. "Laughing or weeping. Sometimes both."

"You say that like you've seen it."

"I heard it," Wren said, "for years. Under the mountain. All those sold souls, all those prayers the temple collected and never sent anywhere—they didn't vanish. They *waited*. The Court fed on them, but prayers are stubborn things. They want to be answered more than anything else in the world." He looked down the valley, and his face did something complicated. "They've been waiting for my sister."

* * *

The temple's authority did not shatter all at once. It shattered the way ice shatters on a pond in spring—one crack, then another, then everywhere at once, so fast that afterward no one could say which crack had been first.

Ash saw the beginning of it from the ridge above the temple district, where they'd gone against every instinct of self-preservation because Wren had gone, and because—though they'd have denied it under torture—someone had to keep an eye on the kid.

The high scribe had come back. Of course she had. Ash had watched her leave the valley weeks ago with her retinue of ledger-clerks and debt-collectors, hunting the fugitive Quintana girl, and now here she was again, standing on the temple steps in the middle of a magical storm, holding a ledger over her head like a shield.

She was shouting. Even from the ridge, Ash could hear the shape of it: *order, obedience, the temple protects you, kneel and be recorded.* The old liturgy. The words that had governed every life in the valley for centuries—every debt, every tithe, every prayer copied out in scriptorium ink and filed away to be answered by no one.

But the storm was answering prayers in the streets, and the people of the valley were not stupid. They had only ever been *kept* ignorant, which is a different thing, and the difference showed now in how fast they understood.

A woman near the temple steps held up her hands and shouted a question at the sky—Ash couldn't hear the words, but the shape of it was unmistakable, the universal shape of *why*. And the sky answered. Rain fell upward around her for the length of a heartbeat, silver beads rising like reversed tears, and the woman looked at her own wet palms and then at the high scribe, and Ash watched three hundred years die in her expression.

The crowd turned. Not violent—not yet—but turned, the way a body turns on a fever. The high scribe kept shouting about ledgers, and someone in the crowd laughed, actually laughed, and the laugh spread like the fire it would become. Clerks came spilling out of the scriptorium with armloads of debt-records, trying to restore order the only way they knew how, by *recording*, and the wind took the pages. Ash watched a man catch one—a record of his own family's debt, four generations of small cruelties itemized in temple ink—and read it by the light of a shrine burning gold, and then walk, very calmly, to the temple's great bronze doors and nail the page to them.

Others followed. Within the hour the doors were white with paper, a blizzard of exposed debts, and the temple stood revealed in the only language its people had ever truly been taught to read.

"They're going to burn it," Wren said. His voice was strange. "The temple. Maybe the whole district."

"Probably," Ash said. "Can't say I'll mourn the real estate."

"The records," Wren said. "The debt-records. If they burn those—" He stopped. Ash watched him wrestle with it, the boy who had been a groundskeeper, who had swept the temple paths and known every name on every grave marker, who understood better than either of them what those papers meant to people. Some of them were the only proof certain families had ever existed at all.

"Huh," Ash said, and meant it. Leave it to the quiet ones to find the tragedy inside the revolution. "Well. Maybe somebody'll save a few boxes. People usually do. It's the one thing I learned running contraband—everyone's a sentimentalist about *something*."

But Wren wasn't listening anymore. He had gone rigid, staring past the temple, past the burning-gold shrines, down to where the valley floor met the mountain's roots—and Ash followed his gaze and felt their stomach drop.

The ground was opening.

Not violently. That was the horror of it. The earth along the old processional road simply folded aside like a curtain being drawn, and from underneath came a light that was not light—the hungry amber glow of the deep places, the Court's glow, the glow Ash had spent a lifetime learning to avoid—and out of it, climbing patiently up roads that had never existed until this moment, came the founders' servants. The hollow things. The collectors.

Coming for the valley. Coming for the harvest that had just been made ripe: a whole valley full of people, newly flooded with magic, none of them knowing the first rule of it, which was that power always wants to be *used*.

"No," Wren breathed. "No, she's not done yet. She's still down there. She's still *talking* to them."

"Then we hold the door," Ash said, "like she asked."

And discovered, saying it, that they weren't afraid anymore—or rather, that the fear had finally found something worth being afraid *for*, which is the only cure for fear that has ever worked, and Ash Dunmore, disgraced hedge-witch, failed smuggler, professional coward, stepped forward onto the broken border with a smuggler's kit full of stolen wards and a heart full of things they'd never once said aloud, and began to work.

* * *

Holding a border, it turned out, was mostly smuggling in reverse.

Every trick Ash had ever used to move forbidden things quietly across forbidden lines, they now used loudly, in reverse, to keep worse things from crossing. They strung wards between the standing stones the founders' road had exposed—wards bought with blood-money, built by hedge-witches like Ash's mother, designed for exactly this war and never once used for it. Wren stood at the center of the line with his soul-sigil bared on his palm, and the sigil sang, and the dead of the Court—souls still trapped in the deep places, souls not yet freed—heard it and hesitated, and hesitation was all Ash needed.

They fought for hours. Or minutes. Time had gone strange, stretched thin by the storm. Ash bled from a cut over one eye and didn't remember getting it. Twice the line buckled and twice Wren pulled it straight again by doing the one thing no one in the Court expected anyone to do: he *forgave* the hollow things as they came at him, spoke to them in the whisper-language he'd learned underground, called each one by whatever name it had carried before it hollowed, and each time, some small part of the horde faltered, remembered, and turned aside.

"You're killing them with kindness," Ash panted, re-weaving a ward with shaking fingers. "Literally. That's the most unsettling thing I've ever seen."

"It's not kindness," Wren said. "It's accuracy. They're mostly empty. But there's a name-shaped hole in every one of them, and holes remember their shape."

Dawn came gray and gold together, storm-light and morning-light arguing across the valley, and with it came the moment Ash would carry for the rest of their life.

The mountain spoke.

Not the founders—they were deeper, older, busier. This was the mountain itself, the bedrock and the roots and the sleeping divinity Kestrel had walked into, and it exhaled. The exhale rolled down the valley as a wave of pressure that flattened the storm-wind and stilled the shrines and made every person in the valley, kneeling or standing or nailing debts to doors, look up at the peak.

And on the peak, silhouetted against the sunrise, stood a figure with light running off her like water off slate, and even at this distance, even through the exhaustion and the blood in Ash's eye, there was no mistaking the posture: a scribe's posture, shoulders slightly stooped, head bent over something held in her hands.

Writing.

Kestrel Quintana stood on top of a waking god-mountain, in the middle of the liberation of everything she'd ever known, *writing*.

"What is she—" Ash started.

Wren made a sound Ash had never heard from him, raw and young, and gripped Ash's arm hard enough to bruise. "Look at the valley. Look at the *ground*."

The wave of the mountain's exhale had reached the temple district. And everywhere it passed, the debt-records nailed to the bronze doors, the ledgers clutched by fleeing clerks, the contracts locked in cabinets, the whole vast architecture of the temple's accounting—it was washing away. Ink lifting off vellum like smoke off a wick. Numbers dissolving. Names remaining—only names, only people, stripped of everything owed.

Debts written in water. She was writing the valley's freedom in a language only she knew, in scriptorium hand, letter by letter, and the mountain was carrying it down like spring meltwater, and the founders below were screaming—Ash could feel it in their teeth, a subsonic fury, the hunger feeling its food supply learn to walk away—

And then the screaming stopped.

All at once. Like a door closing. The pressure in Ash's teeth vanished. The hollow things on the broken road stopped mid-stride, swayed, and came apart—not destroyed, but *released*, unraveling into motes of amber light that drifted up through the storm like reversed snowfall, and among them, if you knew how to look, were faces. Brief, grateful, gone.

The valley went quiet in the way of places that have just survived something enormous and haven't yet thought of what to do next. Somewhere a child was crying. Somewhere else, someone had begun, tentatively, to sing—an old song, a pre-temple song, one the ledgers had tried to bury, and voices were joining it street by street.

Wren sat down abruptly in the mud of the broken road and put his face in his hands.

Ash stood a moment longer, looking up at the peak. The figure there had lowered her hands. She was coming down. Ash could see her picking her way along the ridge, small and human and moving like someone whose legs had recently held up the sky, and something in Ash's chest did a thing they refused to examine.

"Well," Ash said, to cover it, turning to offer Wren a hand up. "Nobody's paying us for this one either. You realize we're zero for two."

Wren took the hand. When he looked up, his eyes were wet and he was smiling properly for the first time since Ash had known him. "She did it."

"She did it," Ash agreed. "Which means the hard part's over."

It was, of course, the exact wrong thing to say, and Ash knew it the moment the words left their mouth, because the universe had a sense of humor and Ash had spent a lifetime on its bad side.

Down in the valley, at the edge of the singing crowds, at the foot of the temple whose doors were white with dissolved debts, a knot of figures was climbing the road toward the broken border. Ledgers under their arms. Faces grim and familiar. And at their center, unhurried, upright, her robes immaculate amid the ruin, walked the high scribe—alive, impossibly, her retinue around her like a blade still sheathed—and her eyes found Ash and Wren on the ridge from half a mile away, the way a needle finds north.

She raised one hand. Not a threat. An invitation.

Behind her, the crowd's song faltered, wavered—and began, uncertainly, to follow her instead.
