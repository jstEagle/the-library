## 18. A Hollow for the Garden

The heart of the mountain was not a hall. It was a wound.

Kestrel stood at the edge of it with Ash's knife warm in her fist and Wren's name still burning on her tongue, and she looked down into the place the founders had made of themselves, and she understood at last why the Court had no throne room. Thrones were for kings. This was a feeding trough.

The Hollow Throne—if it could be called a throne—was a basin of black glass at the center of a cavern that had once, she realized, been a garden. She could still see it beneath the corruption: the terraced walls where vines had grown, the channels where water had run, the great skylight far above where sunlight must have poured down like honey. The founders had built their city in the mountain's chest and planted a garden in its heart, because they had still been human enough to want something green to look at.

Now the terraces were slick with a resinous dark growth that pulsed faintly, like the inside of a throat. The water channels ran with something thicker than water. And in the basin at the center, four shapes sat entwined—had sat entwined for three hundred years, if the shrine-remnant's memory was true—fused at the shoulders and hips where they had leaned into one another and never pulled apart, grown together like trees planted too close, their faces still human enough to be worse than monstrous.

They had been beautiful once. That was the horror of it. Kestrel could see the beauty the way you could see a face under frost.

"You came," said the one who had been called Merren, when the mountain still had a name that wasn't Hollow. Her voice came from all four of them at once, or perhaps from the cavern itself, resonant in the black glass. "We felt you crossing the border. We felt you the way a starving man feels bread carried past his door."

"I'm not bread," Kestrel said.

"You are exactly bread." Merren's head turned. The others' heads turned with it, a half-beat late, the way reflections lag. "Bred for it. The Quintana line. Do you know how many generations we tended your bloodline? How carefully we chose the marriages, the placements, the quiet little scribes and groundskeepers and midwives? You were a garden too, Kestrel Quintana. We watered you for two hundred years."

"I know." Her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "The remnant in the shrine told me. Vessel for dormant divinity. You planted it, you pruned it, you waited for harvest." She stepped closer to the basin's edge. Behind her, she heard Ash shift—Ash, who had sworn they would hold the door, and was holding it, knife drawn, back to the tunnel. "Here's what your garden grew, though. Something you didn't plan for."

"Nothing grows here that we did not plan."

"Then plan for this." Kestrel opened her hand.

The fragment of divinity rose from her palm like heat-shimmer given substance. It had no color she could name; it was the light of the valley on a morning before any debt was owed, it was the feeling of Wren's breathing when he was an infant and she was six and allowed to hold him, it was every small unforced thing she had ever loved, condensed into something the old words called *grace* and she had decided to call, privately, *the part of me that is not for sale.*

The founders went still. All four of them. For the first time since she had entered the cavern, the pulsed dark growth along the walls went still too, and the silence was so total she could hear her own heart, and beneath it, faintly, the mountain's—slow, deep, and hungry.

"You would feed us," Merren said, and there was something in the voice now that had not been there before. Kestrel took a moment to identify it, and when she did, her stomach turned: it was *hope*. Three hundred years of hunger, and they could still hope. That was the last human thing left in them. "You would give yourself. Freely."

"I would give a fragment," Kestrel said. "Not myself. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone."

"We know," the founders said together, and the words came out like a confession dragged over broken glass. "We know the difference. That was our sin. We were given power—found it, uncovered it, it rose out of the mountain's roots like water—and we took it. All of it. We took and took and told ourselves it was ours by right, ours by discovery, ours by hunger, and the taking hollowed us, and the hollow filled with appetite, and the appetite needed feeding, and we built the valley to feed it." Merren's face moved—grief, or the memory of grief, worn smooth by centuries. "We built the temple. We built the ledgers. We built the border that keeps the valley docile and magicless, a pen for the flock. We did all of it, and every day we chose it again. Do you understand, little scribe? It was not a fall. There was no single moment. We fell the way water falls—constantly, and always choosing down."

"I know," Kestrel said. "I read your contracts. I copied your ledgers for six years. I know exactly how it works—one small taking at a time, each one reasonable, each one necessary, until the taking is the whole of you." She thought of the high scribe's face in the lamplight, of the blank clause awaiting her signature, of her parents' names in a column of debts. "I've been on the other end of your arithmetic my whole life. So here is mine."

She held the fragment higher. It brightened. The dark growth on the nearest terrace flinched away from the light like a hand from a flame.

"You cannot take this," she said. "You've already tried—in the auction hall, in the tunnel, with your gatekeeper and your agents and your whispers in my sleeping brother's mouth. You cannot take it, because it isn't mine the way souls are mine, the way Wren's soul was mine to steal back. Divinity that is seized becomes hunger. That's your whole story. That's the trap you built for me and the trap you built for yourselves. If you take this by force, it completes you. It makes you whole the way a fire makes a forest whole. Everything burns brighter for a moment, and then there is nothing left but the burning."

"We know," the founders whispered.

"So I'm not offering it to be taken." Kestrel's voice cracked, and she let it. There was no performance left in her, no ledger, no careful script. This was the one prayer of her life she would speak plainly. "I'm offering it to be *given*. Freely. Unforced. You can't compel a gift. You can't ledger a gift. A gift only exists in the space between one open hand and another—and if your hands can't open, then it dies here in the air between us, and you stay hungry, and I walk out of this mountain, and the border comes down tomorrow anyway, and the valley wakes up, and your feeding pen is finished. Those are your choices. Not surrender. Not victory. Just—this."

The cavern breathed. The four shapes in the basin stirred, and for a moment Kestrel saw them as they had been—four founders, young and terrible and luminous with stolen light, standing in this garden with dirt on their hands, believing that power was a thing you could hold without being held.

"Why," said the one who had been called Ivo, and his voice was alone for the first time, small and cracked, a man's voice and not a mountain's. "Why give anything to the thing that sold your brother. That ate your parents. That built its whole architecture of suffering around your bloodline like a trellis."

Kestrel looked at him for a long time.

"Because someone has to be first," she said. "That's all. Someone has to put down the ledger. Someone has to stop the arithmetic. Your whole system runs on the belief that no one ever will—that everyone has a price, that every hand closes eventually. And you're right about almost everyone. You were right about my parents, God forgive them. You were right about the temple. You might even have been right about me, if you'd come to me a year ago, before Wren went silent, before I learned what my copying was *for*." She stepped to the very edge of the basin. The black glass was warm under her feet, faintly alive, and she made herself stand on it. "But you weren't right about Ash, who burned their whole network rather than sell me. And you weren't right about Wren, who walked toward your mountain every night in his sleep and never once opened his mouth to invite you in. And you're not right about me. So. Someone has to be first. It might as well be the girl you grew in your garden."

She turned her hand over.

"I give this freely," she said, and the old words rose in her—not the temple's script, not the Court's contract-language, but something older that lived in her blood where the founders had planted it, words for open hands and unbarred doors. "No debt. No claim. No return. Take it if your hands can still open. If they can't, then I'm sorry, and I mean that, and I will still be sorry after you're gone."

She let it go.

The fragment did not fall. It *opened*—that was the only word her mind could hold onto afterward, the way a fist opens, the way a door opens, the way a hand opens over a table to show it holds nothing and therefore everything. Light poured out of it, not downward into the basin but *outward*, into the air, into the walls, into the four entwined shapes, and Kestrel felt it leave her the way you feel a word leave your mouth: completely, and without loss. What she had given was gone. What she was remained. That was the miracle she would spend the rest of her life trying to explain to people who had only ever known ledgers: that a thing given freely does not diminish the giver. It was the one equation the temple had never written down.

For a heartbeat, nothing.

Then the founders *wept*.

It was not a gentle thing. Three hundred years of hunger had grown over whatever they had been like ivy over a door, and the gift did not cut the ivy—it went *through* it, found the door beneath, and opened it from the other side. The four shapes shuddered. The fusion at their shoulders strained, and where it strained, light came through like dawn through a cracked shutter. Merren's mouth opened and what came out was not a mountain's voice but a woman's, thin and astonished, saying a name—*Aldous*—the name of one of the others, the name of a person, and the one called Ivo answered with a name of his own, and for a moment they were not a feeding thing in a garden's corpse but four people remembering, all at once, across three centuries of appetite, that they had loved each other.

That was the unmaking. Not the light. The remembering.

"Take our hands," Merren said—or the woman who had been Merren, her voice unraveling into something lighter with every word. "We cannot reach each other anymore. We grew too far in. Take our—"

Kestrel was already moving. She dropped to her knees at the basin's edge and reached into the black glass, into the resinous dark, and found a hand—a human hand, old and lightless and shaking—and held it. Behind her, boots on stone: Ash, who was supposed to be holding the door, crossing the cavern at a run because of course they were, because Ash Brindle had never in their life held a door when a hand needed holding. Ash took the second hand. And then a third grip, smaller, colder, familiar—Wren, who had followed her down without being asked, who stood at the basin's edge with his ruined half-soul and his sleepwalker's eyes and took the third founder's hand without a flicker of fear, because Wren had been hearing their whispers for years and had never once been afraid of them, only sad.

"Freely given," Kestrel said, and the old words said it with her, and the mountain said it with her, and the valley far above, waking to its first morning of magic in three hundred years, said it in ten thousand small answered prayers at once.

The founders came apart the way frost comes off a window in sunlight—slowly, beautifully, and all at once. The fusion dissolved. The dark growth on the terraces thinned to smoke. The four shapes grew translucent, then bright, then root-like, threads of pale light sinking down through the black glass into the stone beneath, into the mountain's deep veins, and where they sank, the cavern began—Kestrel saw it happen, would swear to it for the rest of her life—the cavern began to *green*. Moss first, in the cracks. Then the pale ghost-shapes of roots, unfurling through stone as if three hundred years of buried spring had been waiting only for permission. Water rose in the old channels, clear and cold and running the right direction. Through the distant skylight, impossibly, impossibly, a thread of morning light came down.

The last of them to go was Merren. Her face hung in the air above the basin, no longer fused to anything, no longer hungry, just a woman's face with dirt still ghosted on her hands, and she looked at Kestrel with an expression that had no name in any ledger.

"You'll have to guard it," she said. "The border. Not a wall now—a door. Doors need keepers, or they become walls again. Someone will always be building walls."

"I know," Kestrel said.

"You'll want to be ordinary. I know that look. I had it once." The ghost-face smiled, and it was a human smile, tired and real. "Be ordinary in the ways that matter. Keep the door. And when the hunger comes for you—and it will, little scribe, it comes for everyone who carries what you carry—remember what you did here. Remember that you put your hand down *open*."

Then she was light, and the light was roots, and the roots were the mountain, and the Hollow Throne was a basin of clean water with morning in it, and the garden at the heart of the world began, very quietly, to grow back.

Kestrel knelt at the water's edge. Her hands were filthy and shaking and there was resin under her fingernails and she was crying without any interest in stopping, and Ash sat down heavily beside her, and Wren sat down on her other side, and for a long time none of them said anything, because the mountain was saying enough for all of them—groaning and settling and, somewhere far above, letting the first true daylight in three centuries down through its broken crown.

"Well," Ash said finally. Their voice was wrecked. They cleared their throat and tried again. "Well. That's the Court finished. The gatekeeper's going to wake up very confused. Half the under-city owes debts to a hole in the ground now." A wet, disbelieving laugh. "I burned my entire network for this. I want it noted that I burned my entire network, and what I got was a cave with moss in it."

"The best garden in the world," Kestrel said.

"The only garden in the world with a smuggler in it."

Wren said nothing. But he reached over and took Kestrel's hand—the hand that had given, the hand that was still open—and held it, and did not let go, and that was more words than he had spoken in a year.

Kestrel looked up through the skylight, at the small square of morning sky, and felt the fragment of divinity that remained in her settle like a coal banked for the night. Not a crown. Not a throne. A coal. Enough to warm. Enough to light. Never enough to hollow, if she was careful, and she would be careful, and when she failed she would remember an open hand in a dark garden.

Then she felt it—faint, far away, at the edge of the valley where the border had been: a tremor, like a held breath finally releasing, like a door swinging wide. Magic, flooding home into a land that had forgotten its own name. And with it, carried on the wind down the mountain road, the sound of the temple's bells, ringing all at once, though no hand had touched them.

"They're waking up," Wren said. His voice was rough from disuse, but it was his. "Everyone. They're all waking up."

Kestrel stood, and wiped her face, and looked at the long road home.

"Then we'd better be there when they do."
