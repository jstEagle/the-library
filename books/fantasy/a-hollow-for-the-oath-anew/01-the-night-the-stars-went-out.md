## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with auditing a guild that trafficked in impossible things, Felix had learned over seven years of ledgers, was that the impossible things were always entered in someone else's handwriting.

Tonight's handwriting belonged to Marisol Quen, junior broker, third desk from the window in the Vess counting-house. Her column totals were immaculate. Her item descriptions were a catastrophe. *One (1) dream, gently used* — whose dream? Whose use? Felix tapped the quill against the entry and sighed at the candle.

"You're making the face," said Tam, the night clerk, from his corner by the stove. Tam was seventy if he was a day and had survived three guildmasters, two fires, and one incident involving a love potion and the entire Vess dockworkers' choir. He made tea like it was a religious rite and commentary like it was breathing.

"I'm not making a face."

"You're making the face you make when someone's written 'one (1) dream' without specifying whose."

Felix turned the ledger toward him. "It doesn't even say what kind of dream."

"Does it matter? It's been paid for. Client's happy. That's the whole business." Tam poured two cups without asking, because asking would have implied Felix might refuse, and they both knew better. "You know what your problem is, Okonkwo? You think the ledgers are supposed to make sense. They're not. They're supposed to balance. Sense is extra."

Seven years ago, Felix would have argued. Seven years ago, Felix had been a storm-caller with a name that opened doors in three realms and a temper that closed them faster. Now Felix was the Hollow Guild's auditor-in-exile — technically *resident accountant*, but everyone knew what exile looked like: careful hands, careful words, a small room above the counting-house, and a life measured out in columns that balanced because Felix made them balance, every single night, because it was the one thing in Vess that obeyed rules.

The city outside the window was doing its evening business. Lamplighters on Cordwainer Row. The fish-market closing up with its usual argument about weights. Above it all, the border wards hummed their low, constant note — Felix could feel it through the floorboards some nights, like a cat purring under the city — and beyond the wards, the stars did what stars do, which is nothing, reliably, forever.

Felix liked stars precisely because of this. You could not call a storm out of them. You could not ruin anything with them. They were the only part of the sky that had never once asked anything of Felix Okonkwo.

"Third cup," Tam announced, to no one, as the door opened.

Ilsa Vane came in the way she always did, as though the room had been expecting her and should be grateful. Master broker of the Hollow Guild, keeper of the founder's seal, owner of exactly one expression, which was a mild and patient interest that Felix had long suspected was painted onto a much busier face. She was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in grey so fine it made other people's black look cheap.

"Auditor." She set a slim folio on the desk. "The quarterly reconciliation. Nothing urgent."

"Nothing urgent," Felix repeated, "from the woman who walked here in the rain instead of sending it with a runner."

"It's good weather for thinking." Ilsa took the offered tea, drank, and set it down untouched thereafter, which was her ritual and everyone pretended not to notice. "How are the Quen entries?"

"Someone bought a dream."

"Mm. Grief-dream, most likely. Widow on Tanner's Lane. We broker those quietly." A pause, delicate as a scale settling. "You'll mark it *miscellaneous comfort, services rendered*. As usual."

"As usual," Felix said, and wrote it down, and hated the small warm lie of it — that the guild's crueller trades could be made tidy with the right phrase. Seven years of phrases. Seven years of balancing.

Ilsa lingered at the window a moment longer than she needed to. Felix noticed, because noticing was the job. She was looking south, toward the wards, toward the dark beyond them where the stars were.

"Goodnight, Auditor," she said, and left, and her footsteps on the stairs were too quick for a woman who'd come about nothing urgent.

Tam looked at Felix. Felix looked at Tam.

"Rain, my foot," said Tam. "That sky hasn't dropped a drop since noon."

* * *

It happened at the eleventh hour, and it happened all at once, the way catastrophes do when they've been polite enough to wait for business hours to end.

Felix felt it first as silence. The wards' low hum — the purring-cat note that had been the soundtrack of seven years of sleep — stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a heart stops, and the silence after it was so complete that Felix heard the candle flame flicker.

Then the light through the window changed.

Not dimmed. *Changed.* The faint silver wash that starlight laid over rooftops simply withdrew, like a sheet pulled off a table, and Vess went dark in a way cities do not go dark, a way that has nothing to do with lamps. Felix stood so fast the chair went over behind them.

"Tam—"

"I see it." Tam was already at the window, and his face, when he turned back, was the face of a man watching something he'd hoped never to see twice. "Stars. All of them. Every last—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "In seventy years I have seen clouds hide stars, smoke hide stars, one memorable fog hide stars. I have never once seen the sky *not have* them."

They went out into the street together, because standing inside a thing makes it smaller, and this refused to be made small.

Cordwainer Row was filling with people in nightclothes. The lamplighter stood in the middle of the road with his pole still on his shoulder, looking straight up, having entirely forgotten he owned a job. Somewhere a child was crying, and somewhere else a woman was saying *it's the wards, it's just the wards, look at the ward-towers* — except the ward-towers' blue crown-light, visible from anywhere in Vess on any night of Felix's exile, was out too. Both lights gone. Sky and shield, extinguished in the same breath, like two candles snuffed by the same hand.

"The bells will start soon," Tam murmured. And they did — the alarm bells, then the temple bells, then every bell in Vess arguing with every other bell, the city's oldest and worst orchestra tuning up for panic.

Felix stood in the street with tea going cold in their hand — Tam had insisted; ritual was ritual — and felt the dread arrive not as a shout but as a very quiet voice saying: *this is yours again. This is the shape of your old life. Something enormous and wrong in the sky, and everyone in a ten-mile radius about to need someone to blame.*

Storm-callers blamed storms. That was the arithmetic of three realms. And when there were no storms to blame, well. There were still storm-callers.

"Go inside, both of you," said a neighbor, Mrs. Adeyemi from the chandlery, pressing a lantern into Felix's free hand with the fierce kindness of people who had decided Felix was theirs years ago and never consulted Felix on the matter. "Whatever's happening, it's not an accountant's problem."

"No," Felix agreed faintly. "It never is."

But the dread had teeth now, and it was whispering something worse than *blame*. It was whispering: *three days.*

Because here was the thing Felix had not told Tam, had not told anyone, had been telling themselves was coincidence for seventy-two hours: three days ago, in the quarterly reconciliation Ilsa had delivered in person, in the rain, in a hurry — there had been an entry. Page forty-one, bottom margin, in ink so pale it might have been an accident. A contingency line, the kind auditors write for events that haven't happened yet:

*Provision: celestial default. Effective upon failure of the high covenant. See founding ledger, restricted.*

Felix had read it four times and filed it under *clerical error*, because the alternative was unthinkable, and because clerical errors were the only storms Felix was allowed to chase anymore.

Celestial default. Failure of the high covenant.

The stars hadn't fallen. They'd been *called due*.

* * *

By the second hour past midnight, Vess had organized its terror the way Vess organized everything: badly, loudly, and with committees. Felix watched from the counting-house window as ward-soldiers doubled the gates, as priests of the Astral Order formed a chain to the observatory, as someone — bless them, whoever they were — started handing out bread in the market square, because panicked cities at least panic better fed.

Tam had gone home to his daughter's. Felix had stayed, because the ledgers were here, and because if the world was ending, Felix intended to meet it with the books balanced.

Page forty-one lay open under the lamp. Felix read the provision line again, and then, for the first time in seven years, reached beneath the desk for the key they had sworn never to use — the little iron key on a cord around their neck, worn smooth by seven years of being touched and never turned.

The restricted archive was down eleven steps and behind a door that the guild officially described as *structural*. Inside, the founding ledger sat where it had sat since before Felix was born: chained to its lectern, warded, cold as river stone.

Felix unlocked it. The wards recognized the key — recognized, Felix realized with a fresh crawl of dread, *the key, not the keyholder*, which meant the founder had planned for hands they'd never met — and the great book opened itself to a ribbon marker that someone had placed there recently. Recently. Within days.

The page bore the guild's founding covenant, and beside it, in the founder's own cramped hand, a schedule. Thirteen entries. Thirteen turns of some moon Felix had never heard of, each with a date, and the first date was tonight.

And in the margin, in different ink — newer ink, ink that matched page forty-one of the quarterly reconciliation, ink that Felix had spent three days pretending not to recognize — a single line:

*The auditor must not connect this to the exiled storm-caller before the first turn. If they do, everything fails.*

Felix stood in the cold archive with the lamp shaking slightly in their hand, and understood, with terrible clarity, two things at once.

The first was that the quiet life was over. Had been over, probably, for seven years, and tonight was merely the invoice arriving.

The second was that someone inside the Hollow Guild had known the exact night the sky would go dark — had written it down, had hidden it, had counted on Felix *not* to read it — and had used Felix's own exile, Felix's own erased history, as the lock on the secret.

Above, through the archive's slit window, the starless sky pressed down on Vess like a held breath.

Thirteen turns, said the schedule.

Felix blew out the lamp, locked the book, and went upstairs to pack.
