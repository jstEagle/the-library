## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The Hollow Guild kept its ledgers in a cellar that smelled of tallow, dust, and old money—the three smells Felix had come to think of as the smell of safety. Seven years of audits had worn a groove in the floor between the candle desk and the debt shelves, and this morning Felix's feet found it automatically, as if the body could be loyal to a routine even while the sky above it was busy ending.

Three days. That was what the ledger said. Entry 4,411, dated the third of Emberfall, in Ilsa's own cramped hand: *Ward maintenance deferred. Payment outstanding. Do not press.* And beneath it, in the margin, so small Felix might have missed it if they hadn't spent seven years learning to read margins: *When the lights go.*

Felix had checked the entry four times since dawn. It hadn't changed. The stars had.

Now the guild hall above them rang like a struck bell—brokers shouting, clients weeping at the doors, someone in the counting room praying loudly and incorrectly to three different gods—and down here in the cellar, Felix stood over the master broker's private ledger and felt the quiet life they'd built come apart along its seams.

"You're up early," said Ilsa Vane from the top of the stairs.

She came down the way she always did, unhurried, one hand on the rail, her gray braid pinned up like something she'd decided about herself decades ago and never revisited. She wore her grief the way she wore everything: neatly, and slightly too well-fitted to be accidental.

"Entry forty-four eleven," Felix said.

Ilsa paused on the last step. "You'll want to be careful quoting numbers at me today, Okonkwo. Everyone in this city is going to want someone to blame by nightfall, and you are standing in a cellar full of paper that proves we knew things."

"*We* knew things?" Felix turned the ledger toward her. "Or *you* knew things? Because this is your hand, Ilsa. *Do not press.* You wrote that three days before every star in the sky went out like a pinched wick."

Ilsa looked at the entry for a long moment. Then she did something Felix had never seen her do in seven years of audits: she sat down without being asked, on the client's side of the candle desk, and folded her hands like a woman arriving at a meeting she'd been dreading.

"You were always too good at this," she said. "I told Corvane not to hire an exile with a head for figures."

"Corvane hired me because I was cheap and no one would look for me here."

"He hired you because you were cheap, no one would look for you here, and—" Ilsa stopped herself. It was a small stop, a door closing half a beat too late, but Felix had made a profession of half-beats.

"And?" Felix said.

"And nothing." She smoothed her skirt. "Ask your question properly, if you're going to ask it."

Felix pulled out the chair across from her and sat, which they almost never did; auditors stood, that was the etiquette, standing meant the interview would be brief. Sitting meant it would not. Ilsa noticed. Of course she noticed.

"The founder," Felix said. "Old Man Hollow himself. The covenant. What did he trade with, and who did he trade it to?"

"That's two questions."

"I'm feeling generous."

For a moment Ilsa almost smiled, and Felix felt the old warmth rise unbidden—seven years of shared tea at this desk, of Ilsa correcting their penmanship and slipping them extra coal rations in winter, of a friendship built inside the walls of a lie neither of them had named. Then the warmth hit the entry in the ledger and curdled.

"There was a bargain," Ilsa said finally. "Before the wards, before Vess was a border city instead of a border battlefield. The founder traded with something beyond them. Something that lived in the dark between the stars—or *is* the dark between the stars, depending which account you read. In exchange for the city's survival, it took—" Her hands moved, a broker's gesture, weighing something invisible. "It took collateral."

"What kind of collateral?"

"A promise. A covenant. Terms renewed on a schedule." She said it smoothly, and that smoothness was how Felix knew it was rehearsed, because Ilsa Vane lied the way bad coiners stamped coins: cleanly enough at a glance, wrong under weight.

"On what schedule?"

"Century to century."

"And the payment?"

"Renewal fees." She met Felix's eyes. "That's all I know."

It was such a flat, complete, finished lie that Felix nearly applauded. Seven years of auditing impossible debts had taught them that people told the truth in fragments and lies in whole cloth, and Ilsa had just handed over a bolt of it, hemmed and pressed.

"All right," Felix said mildly, and stood, and went back to the shelves.

"Felix—"

"I'm just putting things away." They ran a finger along the spines. "You know what's strange? The founding covenant is supposed to be archived in the iron case. Charter documents, original terms, the whole sacred paperwork of this guild. I inventoried that case in my first year. Fourteen items. I counted them again last spring." They turned around. "Thirteen."

Something flickered across Ilsa's face—not guilt exactly. Guilt would have been easier. This was more like a woman watching a scale tip and calculating whether she could still add weight to her side.

"Inventory errors happen," she said.

"To me? Ilsa, I once caught a bridge-witch shorting us half a brick. Nothing gets lost in this cellar. Things get *removed*." Felix crossed back to the desk. "So either you have it, or you know who does. And given that you've already lied to me twice this morning, I'm going to go with the version where you have it, and I'm going to keep talking until you hand it over, because I have nowhere else to be until the sky sorts itself out."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke. My humor's better than my threats, but only slightly."

Ilsa looked at them for a long time. Above them, the panic in the guild hall rose and fell like weather. Somewhere a shutter banged. And then, with the air of a woman paying out rope, Ilsa reached into her bodice and set a folded page on the candle desk.

Not the missing charter page. Older. The vellum was brown as tea, cracked along the folds, and the writing on it was in a script Felix didn't recognize—except that some part of them did recognize it, the way you recognize a song from childhood before you remember the words.

"This isn't the covenant," Felix said.

"No. It's a fragment of it. The rest burned, or was buried, or was eaten—I don't know. This piece has passed master-broker to master-broker since the founder died, with instructions never to show it to anyone." Ilsa's voice had gone quiet. "I'm showing it to you because you were going to find it anyway, and because I'd rather you heard it crooked from me than straight from anyone else."

Felix unfolded it.

There were perhaps thirty lines of that half-remembered script, and then, at the bottom, pressed into the wax of a seal gone amber with age: a sigil.

A storm-circle. Three nested rings broken by a single lightning stroke.

Felix had grown up under that seal. Had watched their mother stamp it on letters, their father carve it into the lintel of their house in the storm-country. Had been exiled partly *because* of it, because a family marked by storms was a family the crown counted like ammunition.

"That's not possible," Felix said. Their voice sounded far away to themselves. "My family had nothing to do with the founding. We're— we're nobody. We're farmers who annoy the weather."

"Read the third line again."

"I can't read it. That's the point."

"Then let me tell you what it says," Ilsa said, "and you can decide whether to believe me. It says: *the oath-keepers shall be drawn from the discarded, and the discarded shall know their own by the mark of the broken ring.*" She tapped the seal, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Your family didn't stumble into storm-calling, Felix. Your family *made* the covenant. Or helped make it. The founders were exiles—all of them. People the law threw away, who bargained with the dark for a city that wouldn't have them. And when the bargain needed keeping, century after century, it was their blood that got called on to pay."

The cellar was very still. Felix became aware that they were holding the page hard enough to crease it, and made themselves loosen their fingers one at a time.

"My exile," they said slowly. "Seven years ago. The storm I 'called.' The trial that took eleven days and should have taken one. The sentence that sent me exactly far enough away to be useless and exactly close enough to be watched."

"Now you're asking the right questions."

"Who arranged it?"

"Felix."

"*Who arranged it?*"

Ilsa held their gaze, and for the first time all morning she looked tired—not the performance of tiredness she wore like her braid, but the real thing underneath, seven years deep at least. "I can't tell you that yet," she said. "Not because I don't want to. Because there's a piece you're missing, and if you have the name before you have the piece, you'll do something brave and stupid, and I have invested seven years in you not doing anything brave and stupid."

"So I'm an investment."

"Everyone in this guild is an investment. That's what a guild *is*." She stood, smoothing her skirt, reassembling herself piece by piece. "The hunters will come within two days—the crown needs someone to blame for the dark, and a known storm-caller hiding in a border city is a gift-wrapped answer. When they come, you'll need to decide whose side of this desk you're actually on. Take the page. Read it. Ask the child in Tallow Row about the old tongue—she remembers more than anyone suspects."

"You're giving me homework."

"I'm giving you a fighting chance. Try to appreciate the difference." At the stairs she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back. "For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much this morning—I am sorry. About all of it. Especially the parts you don't know about yet."

And she was gone, up into the noise and the starless morning, leaving Felix alone with a scrap of dead language, a broken-ring seal, and the growing, sickening certainty that the last seven years had not been a punishment at all.

They had been a *placement*.

Felix sat back down, spread the fragment flat, and began, line by line, to copy it into their audit book—in case the original was taken from them next—when boots sounded on the cellar stairs. Heavy ones. Military ones.

Captain Rhee of the border ward stepped into the candlelight, rain-wet, sword still belted on, and said the words that made the morning's betrayal feel like a kindness:

"Okonkwo. We need to talk before the hunters get here, and I only know they're coming because I was ordered to lead them."
