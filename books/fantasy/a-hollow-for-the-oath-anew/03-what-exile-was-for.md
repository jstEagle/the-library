## 3. What Exile Was For

Felix had not slept. This was not unusual—seven years of guild ledgers had trained sleep out of them the way a millstone trains flour out of wheat—but tonight the wakefulness had a different texture. It sat in the chest like a stone dropped into a well, and no matter how Felix leaned over the desk, the splash never came.

The torn page lay under their palm. The family seal pressed into the wax at its corner was unmistakable: the Okonkwo storm-knot, three interlocking spirals, the mark of people who had once been trusted to hold the sky's temper and were now trusted to hold nothing.

Someone in this guild had known who Felix was before Felix ever signed an employment contract. Someone had engineered the whole shape of their life—the exile, the border, the quiet clerkship in Vess—and filed it away like any other entry.

*Debt incurred: one sky. Debtor: the founder. Collateral: see attached.*

There was no attachment. There never was, with impossible things.

A knock came at the guild door. Not the knock of a client—clients knocked twice, apologetically, as though asking a favor of the building itself. This was a soldier's knock: three strikes, evenly spaced, the rhythm of someone who had knocked on a great many doors and expected most of them to open or die.

Felix blew out the lamp out of pure habit and then, annoyed at themselves, relit it. Hiding was for people whose lives still fit inside them.

The door opened on Captain Rhee.

Seven years had silvered her temples and added a scar through one eyebrow, but she stood exactly as Felix remembered: boots planted, hands visible, wearing authority the way other people wore raincoats—functionally, without vanity. She looked at Felix for a long moment, and something in her jaw worked, as though she were chewing on seven years and finding them undercooked.

"You've got taller," she said finally.

"You've got older."

"Everyone does. It's the fashion." She stepped inside uninvited, closed the door, and did the thing that made her Rhee: she checked the corners of the room first, then the window, then the door again, as if the room might have grown a second door out of spite. "I'm not here to arrest you."

"That's what someone here to arrest me would say."

"It is," Rhee agreed. "But they'd say it while reaching for the irons, and my hands are empty. Look." She turned out her pockets with the weary theater of a magician whose trick had failed. A bent spoon. A stub of chalk. Three copper coins and a button she clearly didn't recognize. "That's the entire contents of my life at present. If I'd come to arrest you I'd at least have brought the irons. They're good irons. Guild-forged."

"The Hollow Guild doesn't forge irons."

"They do now. Business has diversified since the stars went out." Her humor had a crack down the middle of it. She set the spoon on the desk, considered it, and put it back in her pocket, which told Felix more about her state of mind than anything else she'd said.

"Why are you here, Captain?"

Rhee pulled out the chair across from Felix, held it, looked at it as though it might be a trap, and sat anyway. "Because seven years ago I escorted a storm-caller out of the realm on orders I didn't believe, and I've spent every year since deciding whether that made me a coward or a professional. And because three nights ago the sky went dark, and yesterday the crown decided it was your fault."

"My fault. I haven't called so much as a gust in seven years. I flinch when doors slam."

"I know," Rhee said simply. "That's why I'm here instead of the hunters. But the crown needs a culprit, and storm-callers are the traditional vintage. There's a warrant. Your description is 'tall, sallow, suspiciously literate.'"

"That describes half of Vess."

"Half of Vess is about to have a very bad month, then." She leaned forward. "Listen to me, Okonkwo. They're not sending soldiers. Soldiers ask questions; I'm a soldier, and look how much trouble I am. They're sending hunters. Ward-breakers. People whose job is to end storms by ending the storm-caller, and who have never once in the history of the profession checked whether the storm agreed with their theory."

The stone in Felix's chest turned over. *Dread* was too small a word for it. Dread was for things that might happen. This was a thing that had already been set walking toward them, boot by boot, from the capital.

"How long?"

"A week. Maybe less. The roads are bad and the hunters complain beautifully, which slows them down." Rhee almost smiled. "You could run. That's the sensible thing. You ran once already; you know the route."

Felix looked down at the torn page, at the storm-knot seal, and felt the strange new weight of a thought that would have been unthinkable a week ago: *I don't want to run.* Not because Vess deserved them—they'd paid their taxes and kept their head down, and the city had repaid them with anonymity—but because the ledger on this desk knew something, and running meant never learning it, and Felix had spent seven years being a person things happened *to*. The exile. The escortee. The entry in someone else's book.

"No," Felix said. "I'm done being carried out of places."

Rhee studied them. Whatever she saw seemed to settle some account of her own. "Good," she said gruffly. "Because I didn't walk three days through the rain to watch you sprint off at the first sign of weather."

"You walked three days through the rain?"

"It's been raining since the stars went out. Something to do with the wards, the priests say. Everything's something to do with the wards now. It's very tedious." She paused, and the crack in her humor widened into something more honest. "Okonkwo. Why did they exile you? Really? Because I read the order, back then. *Unlicensed conjuration, endangering the realm.* But I stood next to you when it happened, and what I remember is a nineteen-year-old screaming at the sky to stop, and the sky stopping. That's not endangerment. That's a child begging a fire to go out."

Felix's throat tightened. "You're asking the wrong person. I've asked myself that question for seven years and gotten nowhere. The storm came when I didn't call it, and left when I begged it to, and everyone decided that proved I controlled it." They slid the torn page across the desk. "And then last night I found this. My family's seal, in the founding covenant of this guild. Someone knew. Someone arranged all of it."

Rhee picked up the page with the care of a woman handling a live coal. She turned it toward the lamplight. Her face did something complicated.

"I've seen this seal before," she said quietly. "On the original exile order. In the margin. As a *recommendation*."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were listening.

"Whose recommendation?" Felix asked, though the well in their chest already knew the depth of the answer.

Rhee set the page down. "I don't know. But whoever it was wanted you in Vess specifically, in this guild specifically, and wanted you alive. Which means either you're the luckiest exile in history—" she tapped the storm-knot "—or somebody spent a great deal of money making sure you ended up exactly where you are. And Okonkwo, nobody spends that kind of money on a person unless that person is either a debt or an investment."

Outside, the rain thickened against the shutters, patient as a creditor.

* * *

Captain Rhee had escorted many people out of the realm, and she had a private taxonomy for all of them.

There were the weepers, who cried at the border stone. There were the cursers, who cursed until the road curved and took the curse's momentum with it. There were the planners, who spent the whole journey explaining how they'd be back within the year, and whom she privately rated highest for survival but lowest for wisdom. And there had been exactly one storm-caller, seven years ago, who had done none of these things—who had ridden in silence for two days and then, at the border, turned around and said, "Thank you for not making it worse," which had haunted Rhee ever since, because it was the only time anyone had thanked her for doing her duty, and the only time her duty had felt like something needing forgiveness.

She had thought about that exile perhaps once a month for seven years, which by her accounting was roughly eighty-four times too often for a professional.

Now she sat in a lamplit office above a guild of impossible things, watching that same exile—older, thinner, sharper at the edges—stare at a scrap of wax as though it were a mirror, and Rhee understood she had just crossed a line there was no uncrossing. You could escort a prisoner out of the realm and call it duty. You could not sit across a desk, hand over state secrets, and warn a fugitive of the hunters coming for them, and call it anything but treason.

It should have frightened her more than it did. What it actually felt like was relief—the specific relief of a splinter coming out after years.

"The hunters will reach Vess in six days," she said, because plans were easier than feelings. "They'll start with the guild registers. Every name, every transaction. If your name appears anywhere in those books—"

"It won't. I keep the books. I appear in none of them." Felix said it flatly, and Rhee caught the odd grief underneath it: a person so thoroughly erased even their own erasure was tidy.

"Then we have six days to find out why someone wanted you here badly enough to forge an exile."

"We," Felix repeated.

Rhee shrugged, uncomfortable. "I'm retired as of this morning. I left a letter of resignation on my commander's desk. It said, quote, *personal reasons*, unquote, which is technically true; the personal reason being that I refuse to hunt a person I helped save." She dug the bent spoon out of her pocket and set it on the desk between them, like a token, like a pledge. "This was my mother's. I've carried it through two campaigns and one divorce. If I'm going to be a traitor, I want collateral."

Felix looked at the spoon, then at her, and something loosened in their face—the first expression Rhee had seen on them that wasn't dread or arithmetic. It might have been the beginning of trust. It might have been exhaustion. With exiles, she suspected, the two lived in the same house and shared a bed.

"You're a strange woman, Captain Rhee."

"I'm a strange *former* captain. Get it right; it's the only promotion I'll ever get."

Felix almost smiled. Then the smile died, and they turned the torn covenant page over, and Rhee watched them read whatever was written on the back—the faint, pressed-through ghost of ink from the sheet that had once lain beneath it—and go very still.

"What?" she asked.

Felix pushed the page toward her. On the reverse, mirrored and faint, the impression of a single line:

*The thirteenth moon turns whether we pay or not.*

Below it, a date. Rhee counted backward out of old habit, the way soldiers count everything—rations, paces, days.

"Thirteen nights from now," she said slowly. "That's not a warning, Okonkwo. That's a deadline. Somebody wrote down when the world ends, and then tore the page out and hoped nobody would do the sums."

Felix was already pulling the master ledger toward them, hands moving fast, the clerk and the storm-caller arriving at the same purpose for the first time in seven years.

"Then we'd better find out who's been keeping the account," Felix said, "before the collector comes to Vess."
