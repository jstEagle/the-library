## 4. The Thirteenth Moon

Captain Rhee had arrested smugglers, deserters, one very apologetic tax collector, and a man who had tried to steal the harbor bell by floating it out on a raft at night. She considered herself difficult to unsettle.

She was unsettled.

The astronomer-priest had sent for her by name — *Captain Rhee, come alone, come before the moon rises* — which was either an honor or a trap, and in Rhee's experience the two were separated by little more than paperwork. So she climbed the spiral stair of the Starfall Temple with her sword loose in its scabbard and her jaw set, past murals of constellations that had stopped making sense three days ago, when the sky above Vess had gone black as spilled ink.

Brother Anselm was waiting for her at the top, in a round chamber open to the dark. He was small, ancient, and wrapped in so many robes he resembled a stack of laundry that had learned to walk. He did not turn when she entered. He was looking up.

"You came," he said. "Good. I'd hate to deliver bad news to an empty room. It feels unappreciated."

"Then give me good news first," Rhee said. "Balance it out."

Anselm chuckled, a dry rustle like pages turning. "Ah, a woman after my own ledger. Very well. Good news: the stars are not dead." He raised one knotted finger toward the black. "Look closely. Where the Hunter's Belt should be. See how the darkness there is... thicker? Velvet rather than wool?"

Rhee looked. She saw nothing, but she had learned long ago that admitting this to priests led to sermons.

"They're veiled, Captain. Not extinguished. There is a difference between a lamp put out and a lamp covered, and the difference is hope." He finally turned to face her, and his eyes were milky-white, blind as boiled eggs. Blind eyes, looking directly at her anyway. It was deeply unpleasant. "The bad news is everything else."

"I assumed."

"Do you know why the temple keeps astronomers who cannot see?"

"Budget cuts."

"Humor. Wonderful." Anselm shuffled to the chamber's center, where a bronze armillary sphere stood tall as a man, its rings frozen mid-turn. "We keep them because the sky can be read in other ways. By tide-tables. By the pull on compasses. By the way pigeons sulk." He laid his hand on the sphere's coldest ring. "And by what is written here. This instrument is older than Vess itself, Captain. Older than the crown. It was built to track a bargain, not the seasons."

"A bargain."

"The founding covenant." Anselm's voice lost its twinkle entirely. "You escorted a storm-caller out of this realm seven years ago. Felix Okonkwo."

Rhee's hand went to her sword hilt on instinct, then she made herself let go. "That's not public knowledge."

"Neither is this." He turned the sphere's outermost ring with both hands, grunting with effort. Somewhere inside the brass, gears engaged with a sound like teeth closing. A hairline seam split along the meridian circle, and from within slid a single sheet of vellum, brittle and browned, dense with two languages — one Rhee recognized as old legal script, one she did not recognize at all, though something about its curves made her think of roots growing through stone.

"Read the parts I've marked," Anselm said. "I have marked them because I cannot read them anymore. My eyes went out the same night the stars did. Symmetry, the gods enjoy. I memorized them decades ago, but I would like confirmation that my memory hasn't begun inventing things. Old men are unreliable narrators."

Rhee took the vellam carefully. The legal script was legible enough:

*...and should the oath stand unbroken for a hundred years, the veil shall lift, and the sky shall return as it was given.*

*But if thirteen turns of the thirteenth moon pass without renewal —*

She stopped. Read it again. The next line was in the root-language, and beneath it, in a later hand, someone had written a translation in ink that still smelled faintly of iron:

*— then the Hollow opens fully, and what waits beyond the wards shall step through, and the city of Vess shall be counted among its holdings, every soul within it, forever.*

"How many turns?" Rhee asked. Her voice came out flat, the voice she used for casualty counts.

"One has already passed," said Anselm. "The night the wards fell. We are on the second."

"And the renewal? What does renewal require?"

"That," said Anselm gently, "is why I sent for you specifically. Because the covenant names its renewers. Oath-keepers, they were called. Nine of them, bound to the founding, each carrying a piece of the vow. And the covenant, being an old and spiteful document, does not say *find new keepers*. It says the original nine, or their heirs in spirit — those exiled, discarded, forgotten by law, as the founders themselves were." He tapped the vellum. "It wants people like you threw out of the realm, Captain. People like your storm-caller."

Rhee set the page down very slowly, because her hands wanted to shake and she refused to permit it. "Seven years ago I rode Felix Okonkwo to the border under escort. They didn't want to go. They wept, actually, though they'd deny it. They kept saying they hadn't meant to call the storm. That they didn't know how it happened."

"And do you believe them?"

"I believe storms don't ask permission," Rhee said. "I also believe a person who calls one by accident is more dangerous than one who does it on purpose, because at least the second kind knows where they're standing." She looked up at the blank sky, at the velvet-thick patch where hunters once wheeled. "Thirteen turns. That's — what, weeks? A season?"

"Thirteen lunar months," said Anselm. "A year and a day, roughly. But the covenant is precise: *thirteen turns of the thirteenth moon*. The thirteenth moon, Captain. Not twelve. There is a moon the calendar forgot, the one the founders hid inside the count, the one that rises only over the Hollow. It comes once, at the end. Everything must be reforged beneath it, at the covenant's origin, or not at all."

"Where's the origin?"

"Beneath the city. Beneath the guild." His blind eyes seemed almost pitying. "You know the place. Everyone in Vess knows the Hollow Guild, the brokers of impossible things. Few remember that impossible things must be bought from somewhere."

* * *

Rhee walked back down through the temple and into streets that had changed texture overnight. Three days of darkness, and Vess had not panicked — Vess had *adapted*, which was somehow worse. Lamplighters now worked double shifts and had tripled their prices. Children wore charms against the dark that their grandmothers had woven from curtain tassels. A fishmonger was selling "starless mackerel" at a premium, claiming they tasted of mystery. Rhee bought one. It tasted of mackerel.

But beneath the adaptation ran fear like groundwater. She saw it in how quickly people stepped aside for her uniform — not respect, exactly. Hope. A soldier might mean someone knew something. A soldier might mean someone was doing something.

She found herself doing arithmetic she hated. Thirteen turns. The city held perhaps forty thousand souls. Forty thousand souls, weighed against nine scattered oath-keepers who probably didn't know they were oath-keepers, one broken covenant, and whatever waited behind the wards wearing the sky like a stolen coat.

Every small thing suddenly felt load-bearing. The lamplighter who nodded to her each morning. The bridge-witch everyone pretended not to see living under the eastern span. The baker who gave her the burnt rolls for free because soldiers liked burnt rolls, apparently. All of it — all of it — standing on a bargain struck before any of them were born, by people whose names had been polished out of the record.

Her escort of Felix Okonkwo rose in her memory with terrible clarity. The border road, the rain, the moment at the boundary stone where protocol said she should ride away and she had instead sat her horse a while longer, watching a storm-caller with mud on their boots look back at a city that had discarded them. Felix had asked her, quietly: *Do you think they'll ever call me back?*

And Rhee, following orders, following the law, had said: *Laws don't call anyone back. They only push.*

She had thought it honest. Now it sounded like an epitaph.

* * *

She found Anselm again at dusk — he insisted dusk mattered now, "the hours have opinions lately" — sitting in the temple's ground-floor kitchen drinking tea he could not see and clearly did not trust.

"If the keepers refuse?" she asked. "If they're found and they say no?"

"Then Vess is consumed," Anselm said, into his cup. "The covenant does not negotiate. Believe me. I have tried arguing with it through the sphere. It has the conversational warmth of a tax audit."

"And if they can't be gathered in time?"

"Consumption," he repeated, with the mildness of a man discussing weather. "Though I will note, Captain, that the covenant says *reforged at its origin*. It does not say *by whom*, precisely. Only that the keepers must be willing, and the price—" He paused.

"The price being what?"

Anselm smiled, and it was the saddest smile Rhee had ever seen on a priest, which was a competitive field. "That is the part I could never translate. The root-language grows vague exactly there, as though the word for *price* and the word for *self* were the same word, or nearly." He set down his cup. "Which brings me to my request, and I warn you it is an impertinent one."

"Impertinence is free in this city. Ask."

"Find your storm-caller. Cross the border, if the border lets you. The covenant's keepers will need someone to gather them, and gatherers are rare — most folk with your talents prefer breaking things apart." He tilted his head. "Also, I confess, I simply like you. You arrest people with courtesy. In dark times, courtesy is a lantern."

Rhee stood, buckled her sword properly, and looked at the blind old priest who had just asked her to commit treason in a warm tone.

"The crown blames storm-callers for the darkened sky," she said. "There are hunters assembling. If I find Felix Okonkwo first, I'll be protecting them from my own kingdom."

"Yes," said Anselm placidly. "I did read the situation correctly, then. How reassuring. I'd hate to have sent you into a simple errand."

Rhee snorted despite herself. Then she looked toward the window, toward the velvet-dark east where the border lay, and felt the vertigo settle over her properly at last — not of heights, but of scale. Forty thousand lives balanced on nine strangers and a word she couldn't translate.

Somewhere beyond the wards, an exile was keeping ledgers, unaware the whole sky had been entered into their column of debts.

"Where do I even start looking?" she asked.

Anselm turned his blind face toward the east, and for a moment — just a moment — Rhee would have sworn he was looking straight through the walls, across the miles, at a guild hall full of counting-books.

"Why, Captain," he said, "I'd begin with whoever knew the stars were going out before they did."

* * *

That night, Rhee wrote two letters.

The first was her resignation, addressed to the Crown Guard, sealed with her captain's signet. She did not send it. She left it in her desk drawer, half-finished, the way one leaves a door open one isn't ready to walk through.

The second was addressed to nobody, because she had no address for it. It said only:

*Felix — the sky is a debt, and I am coming to collect you before someone else does. Ride east. Trust the ones the law threw away. — R., who owes you an apology seven years late.*

She gave it to a pigeon. The pigeon sulked, which Anselm had told her was how pigeons read the sky, and flew east into the dark.

Rhee watched it go until the black swallowed it, then tightened her cloak and began, quietly and without ceremony, to pack.
