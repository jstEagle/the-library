## 5. Debts Owed the Hollow

The debt-ledgers of the Hollow Guild were kept, by ancient custom, in a room with no door.

Not *no entrance* — there was a hatch in the floor, and Felix had spent seven years climbing down into it every morning with a lantern and a pot of tea. But no door, because doors could be barred and doors could be broken, and the founder had reasoned that anything worth keeping safe was worth keeping unreachable. So Felix sat each day in a round stone room below the guildhall, surrounded by shelves of ledgers that recorded every impossible bargain the Hollow had ever brokered: a widow's grief traded for her husband's shadow, a fisherman's luck sold at interest, a king's insomnia purchased outright and never repaid.

Felix had always believed the ledgers were history. It took the stars going out to teach them they were ammunition.

* * *

The first name was Maren Ostreyk, and she owed the Hollow a bridge.

The entry ran three pages. Forty years ago, the bridge-witch of Vess — then young, then celebrated, before the collapse of the Saltspan — had come to the guild with nothing but talent and desperation, asking for the impossible: a span across the drowned quarter that would hold against any flood. The Hollow had brokered it. Something old and patient under the river had agreed to hold her stones together, in exchange for a debt to be named later. The debt was named eleven years ago, when the Saltspan fell and nine people drowned. Maren Ostreyk's payment, deferred twice already, was simple: when called, she would stand where the Hollow required and do the work the Hollow required, no questions, no refusal.

Felix found her living above a chandlery in the Tallow District, selling candle-charms to sailors who wanted their wives kept faithful and their hulls kept dry. She opened the door on the second knock, saw Felix's face, and began closing it again.

"The Saltspan wasn't my fault," she said through the gap.

"No," Felix agreed. "It was the river's. And I'm not here about the Saltspan." They held up the ledger page, folded to show the seal. "I'm here about what you owe."

Maren looked at the page the way people look at old wounds — with hatred, and familiarity. "You're the exiled one. Okonkwo. The storm-child."

"I'm the keeper of the ledgers now. Which makes me, as of three days ago, the only person in Vess who knows the sky is collateral."

That got the door open wider.

She let Felix in, made tea out of habit rather than hospitality, and listened while Felix laid it out: the covenant, the darkness, the thirteen turns. She laughed once, without humor, at the part where something beyond the wards had eaten the stars.

"And you want me to do what? Stand under the sky and shake my fist at it?"

"I want you to come to the Hollow's heart and hold stones together again. That's all you've ever done, Maren. Held things together while everyone else decided whether it was worth saving."

The silence went on long enough that the tea went cold. Then Maren said, "If I refuse?"

"Then the debt comes due in full, and the thing under the river collects however it likes. I'd rather it never came to that. Honestly, I'd rather burn the ledger than use it." Felix turned the page toward her, to the clause at the bottom, written in the founder's hand. "But Ilsa left me no clean instruments. Only these."

"You hate this," Maren said, studying Felix's face with the frank appraisal of someone who read people the way she read water. "Using it. I can see it sitting on you like wet wool."

"Yes."

"Good." She stood, and began pulling a battered tool-roll from under her bed. "A person who enjoys blackmail is dangerous. A person who hates it and does it anyway — that's just someone trying to save a city." She slung the roll over her shoulder. "Well, keeper? Where are we standing?"

* * *

Corvin Hale did not speak. This was, according to the guild records, a choice he had made thirty years ago, on the day he cast the great bell of Vess and heard — so the entry delicately put it — *what answered from inside the bronze.*

His debt was older and stranger than Maren's. He had asked the Hollow to teach him the ringing-words, the tones that could wake iron and calm storms, and the Hollow had asked in return that he lend his hands whenever called upon. Three times in forty years the call had gone out. Three times Corvin Hale had set down whatever he held and walked, silent, to wherever the ledger said to walk.

Felix found him in the bell-foundry district, in a workshop that still smelled of charcoal though the forge had been cold for years. He was mending a small hand-bell with a patience that seemed geological. When Felix entered, he looked up, and then at the ledger page Felix laid on the bench, and something in his shoulders settled — not resignation exactly. Recognition. As if he'd been waiting thirty years for this particular visitor.

"You know why I'm here," Felix said.

He nodded.

"It's going to be dangerous."

Another nod. Then he reached beneath the bench and brought up a slate, wiped it clean with his sleeve, and wrote in a careful, schoolroom hand:

**THE BELL IN THE HOLLOW'S HEART HAS NOT RUNG IN 300 YEARS.**

Felix stared at it. "There's no bell down there."

**THERE IS. IT IS THE ROOM ITSELF. THE FOUNDER HAD ME LOOK AT THE PLANS ONCE, WHEN I WAS YOUNG. SHE THOUGHT I WOULDN'T UNDERSTAND. THE WHOLE CHAMBER IS CAST BRONZE UNDER THE STONE. ONE DAY SOMEONE WILL NEED TO RING IT.**

Felix thought of the round room with no door, the one they'd sat in for seven years, drinking tea among the ledgers. Sitting, all that time, inside an unstruck bell.

"Why didn't anyone ring it?" they asked.

Corvin wiped the slate slowly, and wrote:

**BECAUSE NO ONE WANTED TO HEAR WHAT ANSWERS.**

Then he took down his tools, blew the dust off them, one piece at a time, and followed Felix out into the darkened streets like a man keeping an appointment three decades overdue.

* * *

The third name was the hardest.

Pip was eleven years old, or thereabouts — no one in Vess's low quarters knew exactly, least of all Pip — and owed the Hollow nothing. That was precisely the problem. Felix had searched the ledgers twice, three times, looking for some clause, some bargain, some lever. There was none. What Pip had was worse than a debt: a gift. The child remembered words. Old ones. Covenant-tongue, the founding language of the oath, which everyone else in the realm had forgotten or burned out of the record books centuries ago. Pip had learned them from a grandmother who had learned them from nobody anyone could name, and recited them skipping rope in the alleys behind the fish market:

*One-for-all-and-none-alone, the hollow holds what's given home—*

Children's nonsense, everyone said. Except Felix, reading the founding fragments Ilsa had missed, had recognized the meter of the original covenant buried in the rhyme like a coin in bread dough.

So there was no blackmail available. There was only begging, and Felix hated begging even more than they hated blackmail, and stood outside the leaning tenement where Pip lodged with four other orphans and a one-legged landlady, rehearsing opening lines and discarding all of them.

In the end Pip solved the problem by finding Felix first, materializing out of an alley mouth with the suddenness of weather.

"You're the ledger-keeper," the child said. "I seen you buy herring. You always pay exact."

"I do," Felix said. "Pip, I need to ask you something strange."

"Everyone needs something strange lately. Sky's broke." Pip said it the way other children said *it's raining.* "Gran said if the stars ever went out, someone from the Hollow would come asking. Said I was to listen to 'em."

Felix crouched. "Your grandmother knew about the covenant?"

"She knew lots. Knew she was dying too. Said the words had to live in somebody's mouth, and mine was the right shape." Pip shrugged, elaborately casual, the way children are when guarding something precious. "What's in it for me?"

Honesty, Felix decided, was the only currency left. "Nothing I can promise. Danger, probably. Cold. Bad food. And afterward, if we live, a city that owes you everything and won't know your name."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a merchant weighing coin.

"That's rubbish terms," the child announced. "I'll come anyway." And then, quieter, with the skip-rope rhyme suddenly audible underneath the words: "'Cause Gran said the hollow holds what's given home. You give it back, yeah? Whatever got taken?"

"I intend to try."

"Right then." Pip stuck out a grubby hand, and Felix shook it, and felt something in the old covenant shift its weight, like a sleeper turning over.

* * *

They gathered that night in the guildhall — the witch, the bell-founder, the child, and the keeper — around a table where Felix spread the founding fragments beside the debt-ledgers, and tried to make arithmetic out of hope.

Four of them. Against a deadline measured in moon-turns, with hunters in the city and a traitor somewhere close, and a chamber below them shaped like a bell no one dared ring.

"It's not enough," Maren said flatly. "Witch, smith, child, clerk. We look like a bad joke."

"We look," Felix said, "like exactly who built the covenant in the first place. Exiles and castoffs and people the law discarded. I'll explain on the road." They touched the fragment where the old tongue's meter ran beneath the translation. "There are more names. In the ledgers. People who bargained with the Hollow and were never collected from — debts the founder never called in. Debts that have been waiting decades for someone to ask."

Pip, upside-down in a chair, raised one hand like a student. "What if they say no?"

"Then I'll beg," Felix said. "And if begging fails—" they looked at the ledger, and hated it, and picked up the pen anyway, "—then I'll collect."

It was Corvin, wiping his slate, who ended the night. He wrote slowly, deliberately, and turned it toward the table:

**THE FOUNDER LEFT ONE MORE DEBT UNCOLLECTED. BIGGEST ONE. FIRST PAGE OF THE FIRST LEDGER. HAVE YOU READ IT?**

Felix frowned. They had catalogued every volume, indexed every entry. "The first page of the first ledger is blank."

Corvin shook his head, and wrote:

**IT ISN'T BLANK TO EVERYONE.**

Outside, the thirteenth moon was rising — swollen, wrong-colored, counting down — and Felix understood that whatever was written on that first page had been waiting for them specifically, since before they were born.
