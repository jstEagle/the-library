## 6. Ilsa's Arithmetic

Ilsa Vane had learned arithmetic at her father's knee, and her father had learned it from a thing with no knees at all.

That was the joke she never told. It was a good joke. It would have killed anyone who heard it.

She sat in the counting room above the Hollow Guild's vault, where the ledgers of impossible things were kept under triple lock and a silence that had teeth, and she did what she always did on the nights when the sky embarrassed her: she checked the sums.

The first ledger lay open before her. *One sky, darkened, thirteen turns.* The second: *One city, Vess entire, preserved through famine, plague, the war of the river kings, and one very bad winter that the histories now blamed on wolves.* The third ledger was smaller, bound in grey leather gone soft as a mole's ear, and it held only a single line, written in her father's hand, in ink that still smelled faintly of lightning:

*Paid in full by Aldous Vane, who asked nothing for himself.*

Her father had been a liar of tremendous discipline. That line had cost him more effort than the bargain itself.

* * *

People imagined — when they imagined the master broker of the Hollow Guild at all — that Ilsa kept the books because she loved them. This was true, but not in the way they thought. She loved them the way a surgeon loves anatomy: intimately, gratefully, and with a steady awareness that everything she touched was already dying.

Every debt in those ledgers was real. That was the guild's genius and its horror. When a widow needed rain for a field that had forgotten how, the guild found rain. When a merchant needed his rival to forget a grudge, the guild found forgetting. And when the price came due — because it always came due; Ilsa had never once seen an entry close at zero without help — the guild collected with the gentle efficiency of tide.

Her father had built all of it on a single founding transaction, and Ilsa had spent forty years making sure no one read the fine print, including, for most of those years, herself.

She had been nine years old the night he finally told her. He had taken her up to the observatory roof, which was reckless even by his standards, and pointed at the stars — the real ones, burning, arrogant, beautiful — and said, "Count them."

"I can't," she said. "There are too many."

"Good," said Aldous Vane. "Now count the people in Vess."

She counted. She was very good at counting. "Eleven thousand and some."

"And which would you rather lose?"

She remembered being furious at him. She remembered thinking it was a cruel question, a broker's question, the kind of question that turned children into accountants. She had not yet understood that it was also the only honest question there was.

"The stars don't know we're here," she had said at last, in the sulky voice of a nine-year-old philosopher.

"No," her father agreed. "They don't. And that, my dove, is the whole of the covenant."

* * *

Here is what the founding ledger actually said, the page Felix Okonkwo had torn free with their clever, guilty hands:

*In exchange for the preservation of Vess — its walls, its wells, its people, and its posterity — the undersigned covenants that once each hundred years, the Hollow shall yield up one willing soul to the party beyond the wards, that the account may be kept open and the sky kept lit.*

*Willing,* the contract insisted, three times, in three different hands, as if repetition could make a thing merciful.

It could not. Ilsa knew this better than anyone alive, because she had been the one to find the volunteers.

Her father had handled the first century himself. She never learned who it was — he burned that page while she watched, and the look on his face taught her more about the cost of arithmetic than any sum ever had. But by the second century, Aldous was dead, and the thirteenth moon was rising, and the guild's master broker was a young woman with her father's ledgers and none of his talent for pretending the math didn't stink.

So she had gone looking. Not for a victim. She wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only in her own skull, where no auditor could reach it: *Ilsa Vane did not go looking for a victim.* She went looking for someone whose staying would cost less than their going. A debtor so deep in the guild's red that another century of life would only deepen the hole. A stranger with no one to mourn them properly. Someone whose name, if it appeared in a missing-persons ledger, would gather dust instead of tears.

She found three candidates over two years. She interviewed all of them. She offered each of them the same bargain her father's ghost seemed to offer from every corner of the counting room: your life, freely given, against eleven thousand strangers you will never meet.

Two refused. She let them go, and hated herself a little for the relief, and a great deal for the disappointment.

The third said yes.

His name had been Tammas Reeve. He had been a ferryman with a bad lung and a good singing voice, and he had owed the guild eleven years of labor for a favor Ilsa still declined to specify in her own private records, out of shame that was either decent or cowardly depending on the hour. He had sat across this very desk, coughing, and said, "Eleven thousand? Truly?" as though she might be inflating the number to sweeten the deal.

"Truly," she had said.

"Well then." He'd looked out the window at the lights of Vess, at the smoke rising off ten thousand suppers. "Someone ought to pay for all that. Might as well be a man who sings."

He sang on the way down. She heard it through the floor of the Hollow for six hours, growing fainter, until it stopped, and the stars stayed lit, and the city slept on, ungrateful, unaware, alive.

Ilsa had gone home and vomited and then, the next morning, entered the payment in the ledger in a steady hand, because a bookkeeper's grief is a private religion and she kept hers immaculate.

* * *

Which brought her, ninety-nine years later, to the present difficulty.

The covenant had come due again. Ilsa had known for a decade — she had known since the astronomers began muttering about the moon's count, since the wards started tasting faintly of hunger — and she had done what she always did. She had begun the search. Candidates. Arithmetic. The long cold walk through other people's lives, weighing.

And then Felix Okonkwo had walked into her audit with storm-light still caught under their fingernails like glitter, and ruined everything by being exactly what Ilsa needed and refusing, with the whole stubborn weight of their exile-honed conscience, to know it.

Because here was the sum Ilsa had run, alone, at this desk, a hundred times, and it balanced every single time:

Felix Okonkwo. Storm-caller. Exiled for a tempest they swore they hadn't meant — and Ilsa believed them, which made it worse, because it meant the realm had already discarded them once, had already written them into the column of acceptable losses. No family in Vess. No ties. Seven years of careful, quiet living, yes, but seven years is a candle-end against eleven thousand lives and a century of light. And — the figure that made the whole equation sing — Felix was *already marked*. The crown blamed storm-callers for the darkened sky. Hunters were coming regardless. If the covenant took Felix, the world would simply nod and say the fugitive got what fugitives get.

No grief. No questions. No arithmetic visible from outside at all.

It was perfect. It was so perfect that Ilsa had lain awake for three nights after realizing it, staring at the ceiling, listening to her own heart conduct its small, stupid rebellion.

And so she had engineered it. The torn page left where Felix would find it. The lie about her father told badly enough to be seen through. The exile itself — gods forgive her, the exile — arranged seven years ago, when Felix was nineteen and brilliant and dangerous, because Ilsa had read the moon-count even then and begun keeping a short list, and a storm-caller raised outside the realm's law was the rarest entry on it: a sacrifice the world had pre-approved.

She had told herself it was mercy. She had told herself a great many things. She was, after all, her father's daughter, and the family trade was telling yourself things in a steady hand.

* * *

On the twelfth turn of darkness, Ilsa climbed to the observatory roof, because sentiment is a debt too, and she paid hers on schedule.

Below her, Vess glowed with fear-lanterns. Above her, nothing. The sky was a black lid, smooth as the inside of a coffin, and she felt the old familiar vertigo of standing inside her own arithmetic: *eleven thousand below, one soul owed above, and every road between them running through me.*

"You could tell them," she said aloud, to the dark, to her father, to the thing beyond the wards that was surely listening with whatever passed for ears. "You could explain the math. They're clever. They'd understand."

The dark said nothing. The dark was very good at saying nothing. It had, after all, been practicing for twelve turns.

"They won't understand," she admitted. "They'll do something noble and catastrophic. That's what people like them do. People who were exiled for storms they didn't mean to call — they spend their whole lives trying to mean something, and then they mean it at the worst possible moment."

She took out the grey ledger, the one with her father's last lie, and beneath it she wrote a new line in her own hand:

*Candidate identified. Willingness: pending. Probability of willingness: low. Probability of catastrophe: high. Recommendation: proceed anyway, because the alternative is everyone.*

Then she paused, pen hovering, and added, in smaller script, almost tenderly:

*I am sorry. I have checked the sums four times. There is no other door.*

She blotted the page. Somewhere below, in the city she had bought and bargained for and bled for in the only currency she had, a bell rang the hour, and Ilsa Vane went downstairs to prepare the Hollow's heart for a guest who would arrive, knowing her, hating her, and — this was the part that frightened her — possibly understanding her perfectly.

She just hoped Felix Okonkwo would be reasonable about it.

She had checked the odds on that, too.

They were terrible.
