## 7. Storm Without a Caller

The drowned bridge had been drowning for a hundred years, and it showed no sign of finishing the job.

Felix stood at its broken spine and looked down at the water that had swallowed the road to Vess's eastern shore—black, patient water, thick with the ghosts of pilings. Half the bridge still stood, arcing out over the flood like a rib torn from some enormous patient animal. The other half was rumor.

"We could go around," said Tam, the bell-founder, who could not speak but wrote on a slate he carried slung across his back like a shield. He wrote this now and held it up: *AROUND = 3 DAYS.*

"Three days we don't have," Felix said. "The moon turns in four."

The child, Nima, crouched at the bridge's edge with her chin nearly touching the water, murmuring old words at it the way other children murmured at stray cats. The water did not respond, but Felix noticed the ripples near her mouth moved against the wind. The bridge-witch, Sefa, stood apart from all of them with her arms crossed, radiating the particular displeasure of a woman who had been dragged out of a very comfortable disgrace.

"And if the water's deep enough to drown a road," Sefa said, "it's deep enough to drown whatever lives under roads."

"Nothing lives under roads," Rhee said.

"You'd know, Captain. You've spent your whole career marching on them."

Rhee didn't rise to it. She had been rising to Sefa's baiting for two days now and seemed to have decided, with soldierly discipline, to stop spending ammunition on it. She stood at the landward end of the bridge with her hand resting on her sword hilt—not gripping, just resting, the way a farmer rests a hand on a fence post—and she watched the reed-beds behind them.

That was what made Felix's stomach tighten. Not the bridge. Not the black water. Rhee watching the reeds.

* * *

They crossed at dusk, because Nima said the water liked to be asked at dusk, when it was sleepy. It was the most unsettling sentence Felix had heard in seven years of guild ledgers, which were nothing *but* unsettling sentences, and so they crossed.

The bridge held. Of course it held; Sefa muttered at every third stone and pressed her palm flat against the gaps, and where her palm touched, the mortar remembered being whole. That was the thing about oath-keepers, Felix was learning. They were not powerful people. They were people who had been broken in specific ways, and the breaks let something through.

Tam went first with Nima on his shoulders, then Sefa, then Felix, then Rhee walking backward, watching the reeds.

They were halfway across—the gap yawning beneath them, the far shore a smudge of willow—when Rhee said, quietly, "Down."

Not *duck.* Down. And then the first crossbow bolt came out of the reed-beds and buried itself in the stone an inch from Felix's knee with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Hunters!" Rhee had her sword out now, actually out, and Felix understood in one cold flash that she had known since noon. Had seen something in the reeds, or smelled their cookfire, or simply felt the day sitting wrong on her shoulders, and had said nothing because there had been no other crossing and no point frightening them before it was time to be frightened.

More bolts. They came in threes, disciplined threes, from three places in the reeds, which meant at least nine hunters, which meant a crown company, which meant someone in Vess had sold them. Felix thought, absurdly, of the ledgers. *Debts owed the Hollow,* page after page after page. Everyone owed someone. Someone had just paid theirs.

"To me! To me—" Rhee was pulling them back toward the landward span, toward cover, toward the reeds' blind spot, and it was good tactics and it would not work. Felix could see it would not work, because the hunters were not trying to take the bridge. They were shooting at the stones Sefa had mended. A bolt struck one of her sealed gaps and the mortar crumbled like old bread, and a whole yard of bridge sighed and slid into the water between Felix and the shore.

Cut off. Nine hunters, one sword, one witch whose craft was patience and whose patience was now standing on a shrinking island of bridge with three civilians and a child.

A second volley. Tam turned his broad back to it and folded around Nima like a bell around its clapper, and a bolt skipped off his leather apron and another tore through his sleeve, and he did not make a sound, because he couldn't, and somehow that was the worst sound of all.

Felix looked at the water.

That was the mistake. Or the beginning of it. Because the water was black and patient and deep, and somewhere below its surface, in the place where drowned roads go, something in Felix's chest sat up and took notice. It had been seven years since Felix had let that thing in their chest even stretch. Seven years of ledgers and ink-stained fingers and counting other people's impossible debts so as never once to count their own. Seven years of being a person who filed storms instead of making them.

The sky over the reed-beds had gone the color of a bruise without Felix noticing. That was how it always started. Not with lightning—with weather getting ideas.

"Felix." Rhee's voice, low and urgent, her back against Felix's back now, sword up, holding a line alone. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm not thinking anything."

"You're breathing like you used to breathe. The night of the storm." She said it without turning around. "I escorted you out of the realm, Okonkwo. I remember your breathing."

Another volley. Sefa screamed—not fear, fury—as a stone she had just mended burst apart under her hands. Nima was saying the old words now, fast, scared, and the water below was answering, rising up the pilings in ropes and tendrils, and none of it was going to be enough. Felix did the arithmetic they were best at, the only kind they'd ever been allowed: nine hunters, one sword, four turns of the moon left, and a covenant that needed every one of these broken people alive.

The math came out one way. It had always come out one way. That was what exile had been *for*—so that Felix would never again stand at the moment where the math came out this way and have to choose.

"Felix," Rhee said again, and this time it wasn't a warning. It was almost gentle. "It's all right."

And that—that gentleness, that permission from the one person who had seen what Felix was and walked them to the border anyway—that was what broke the dam.

Felix stopped holding it.

There was no gesture. No word of power. That was the lie the ballads told, that storm-calling was a craft with rules, a thing you learned. It wasn't a craft. It was a door in the chest, and Felix had nailed it shut with seven years of small honest work, and now they took the nails out one by one, deliberately, awake, eyes open, and the sky came through.

It came up from the water first. The drowned river breathed out a fog that was not fog; the air went thick and green-dark and tasted of copper and the inside of clouds. Then the light changed—not dimmed, *changed*, the way it does in the last second before hail—and every hunter in the reed-beds must have felt their neck prickle, because the volleys stopped, and someone shouted an order that dissolved halfway into a different word entirely.

Then Felix called the lightning down into the reeds.

Not at the men. Felix would remember, later, with a grief that never quite healed, that even now—even choosing—they had tried to aim it at the empty ground between them, the way you fire a warning shot. But lightning is not a warning shot. Lightning is lightning. It hit the flooded ground and the ground *remembered being sky*, and the blast went through the reed-beds like a bell through a cathedral, flinging men and water and mud and burning reeds in a wide dark fan. Screaming. Horses somewhere, screaming too. The concussion rolled across the bridge and Tam wrapped himself around Nima again and Sefa grabbed Felix's coat and hauled, hauling them away from the edge as though Felix might fall upward into the storm.

And the rain came. Warm rain, violent rain, rain like the sky wringing out a rag, and under its cover the hunters fled—Felix saw them go, limping shapes through the smoke, alive, most of them, alive because Felix had aimed at the ground—and the thunder walked off eastward grumbling about the rudeness of being summoned, and the sky began, slowly, to put itself back together.

* * *

Afterward, the bridge island was very quiet.

Sefa was binding a gash on Tam's arm with strips of his own ruined sleeve, working fast and saying nothing, which from Sefa was a eulogy. Nima sat on a fallen stone with her knees drawn up, watching Felix with an expression no child should know how to make—the expression of someone revising a story they'd believed.

Rhee stood at the broken edge, looking at the scoured, smoking reed-beds, at the crater of black glass where the lightning had landed, at the dead fish floating belly-up in a perfect ring.

"So," she said.

"Don't," Felix said.

"I escorted you out of the realm for calling a storm by accident." Rhee turned. Her face was wet—rain, or not rain. "One storm. Accidental. Three people hurt. They burned your family's name off the rolls for it. And now I've watched you do it on purpose, aiming it, holding it, and I'm supposed to—what? Say well done?"

"Every accusation they ever made," Felix said. Their voice came out level, which cost more than screaming. "Confirmed. By my own hand, in front of witnesses. You should arrest me, Captain. It's what the law says."

"The law also says the sky can stay dark thirteen turns and then eat a city," Rhee said. "I've stopped taking the law's side in arguments." She looked at the black glass a long moment. "You saved us. You know that. All of us. Even them, probably—you fired at the dirt like a man apologizing while he does it."

"I *was* apologizing while I did it."

"I know." Something moved through her face, too quick to name. "That's the worst part, Okonkwo. I believe you."

Felix looked down at their own hands. They looked the same as always—ink-stained, ledger-keeper's hands, hands that had spent seven years proving they belonged to a harmless person. But the air around them still tasted of copper, and the water below was still rising gently, hopefully, along the pilings, like a dog that had finally been let off its rope and wanted to know if it could stay off.

Seven years fleeing the weapon. One afternoon choosing it. And the terrible, quiet truth underneath both: it had felt like coming home. That was the part Felix could never tell anyone. Not the power—the *relief*. The door open. The chest unnailed.

Nima tugged Felix's sleeve. When Felix looked down, she pointed—not at the storm's wreckage, not at the hunters' trail. At the sky, eastward, where the clouds were tearing open.

Through the tear, where stars should have been, there was nothing. But the nothing had *moved*. Shifted, in the way a held breath shifts. And far off, past the willows, past the drowned road, something answered the thunder—one long note, low, patient, like a bell rung underwater.

Tam heard it. His head came up sharply. He snatched his slate and wrote, his hand shaking:

*THAT IS NOT THUNDER. THAT IS THE HOLLOW. IT KNOWS YOU CALLED.*

Felix read it twice. The rain had stopped. The water had gone still. Somewhere beneath the drowned bridge, beneath the drowned road, beneath seven years of careful ordinary life, the old covenant had felt its first true storm in a century—and it had recognized the hand that made it.

"Then it knows where we're going," Felix said, and picked up their pack. "Good. We're done sneaking."

Rhee looked at them—at the stranger who had just become exactly what the crown said they were—and shouldered her sword.

"Then we'd better get there first," she said, "and pray the Hollow likes you better than the law does."

Behind them, far off under the water, the bell rang again. Closer this time.
