## 8. The Origin of the Oath

### Felix

The stair down into the Hollow had no business existing.

Felix had kept the guild's ledgers for seven years and had never once seen a door beneath the counting room—only a wall of grey stone that smelled faintly of rain. Now the wall stood open like a mouth, and the stairs went down farther than any cellar in Vess had a right to go.

"Seven years," Felix said, holding the lantern high. "Seven years I dusted that wall."

"You dusted a wall?" asked Wren, the child who remembered the old tongue. She was eleven, fearless, and had appointed herself the company's official commentator somewhere around the drowned bridge. "That's a weird job."

"It was one of my jobs. I had many. All of them were weird."

The bell-founder, Corvo, walked behind them with his hands loose at his sides, the way he did when he wanted to be ready to catch someone falling. He couldn't speak—the old tongue had taken his voice as payment for casting bells that could be heard across realms—but he tapped his knuckles twice against the stone when he agreed with something, once when he didn't. He tapped twice now, which Felix chose to interpret as *yes, your job was weird* rather than *I agree we should descend into this ominous hole.*

Behind them all came Captain Rhee, sword drawn, and behind Rhee came the bridge-witch Maren, muttering ward-litany under her breath like a woman rehearsing an argument she intended to win later.

The stairs ended in a hall that stole the breath from everyone at once.

It was round, and vast, and carved from the living dark of the world itself. And it was full of names.

They covered every surface—walls, floor, the great ribbed vault overhead—tens of thousands of names cut into the stone in a script older than the crown, older than the wards, older than the city they'd bought. Some were deep and fresh-cut. Most were shallow, worn smooth, half-erased, as if the stone itself had been slowly forgetting them.

Felix raised the lantern and read the nearest one aloud without meaning to.

"*Sera of the Ashline, who gave her winters.*"

Below it: "*Dov, who gave his father's face.*"

"*Mira, who gave the road home.*"

Nobody spoke. Even Wren had gone quiet, which Felix suspected was a first.

"They're not graves," Maren said softly. Her fingers hovered over the carvings without touching. "They're receipts."

### Rhee

Rhee had escorted condemned people before. She knew what fear looked like on a person walking toward something they couldn't come back from, and so she recognized it now—not on Felix's face, but on her own hands, which would not stop checking her sword hilt though there was nothing in the hall to fight.

The astronomer-priest, Brother Anselm, had come down with them uninvited and unapologetic, carrying his brass instruments like a man bringing tools to a surgery. He was already circling the hall, reading the names, making small sounds of scholarly delight that Rhee found deeply inappropriate.

"These are exiles," Anselm said. "Look at the formulae. Every name is paired with a forfeiture. A memory surrendered. This—" he swept an arm at the vault, "—is the founding covenant. Not signed. *Paid.*"

"The crown teaches that the Hollow Guild was chartered by King Aldous the Third," Rhee said.

"The crown teaches many flattering things about itself." Anselm crouched by the oldest carving, the deepest one, set alone at the hall's center like a keystone. He brushed away seven years—or seven centuries—of dust. "Here. The first entry."

Felix came to stand beside him. Rhee watched them read it, watched their lips move, watched the color leave their face in the specific way color leaves a face when a person finally understands the shape of the cage they've been living in.

"*Vess Okonkwo,*" Felix read aloud. Their voice cracked on their own family name. "*Who gave everything, and asked only that the sky remember.*"

"That's—" Felix stopped. Started again. "That's my grandmother's name."

"No," said Anselm gently. "That is *your* name. Or nearly. Names change shape down generations, but debts don't." He straightened up, joints cracking like green wood. "The covenant wasn't forged by kings or brokers, Captain. It was forged by thirteen exiles. People your law discarded. Storm-callers, oath-breakers, witches, thieves—people the realm threw past its borders to die. They found the thing beyond the wards, and instead of begging it for mercy, they offered it a trade."

"What trade?" Rhee asked, though some part of her already knew, because she'd spent three days watching Felix not sleep.

"Themselves," Anselm said. "Their memories. Their names. Everything that made them *them*, fed into the stone, in exchange for a sky over Vess that would never fall. Protection bought not with gold, which the thing beyond doesn't want, but with identity—which it wants desperately, because it has none of its own."

Wren tugged Rhee's sleeve. "So the stars going out means—"

"Means the payments ran out," Rhee said. "Or stopped being made."

She looked at Felix. Felix was still staring at their grandmother's—no, at *their own* name, cut into the heart of the world, worn almost smooth.

"Ilsa knew," Felix said quietly. "Her father knew. They've been managing the payments for a century. Choosing who forgets." They turned, and their eyes were terrible and clear. "And they chose me for exile so I'd never find this room. Because if I ever saw my own name here, I might ask why it wasn't finished."

Corvo tapped the stone twice.

Everyone looked at him.

He pointed at the keystone carving—at the words *who gave everything*—and then, deliberately, at Felix, and then he shook his head slowly, and tapped the stone *once.*

*No,* he meant. *Not everything. Not yet.*

### Maren

Maren had been thrown off a bridge by her own coven, so she considered herself something of an expert on institutions that made promises with other people's lives. Standing in the hall of names, she felt the old familiar anger rise—and then, unexpectedly, something else underneath it.

Pity.

Because these weren't villains' names. She traced them one by one. *Who gave her sister's laugh. Who gave the taste of bread. Who gave twenty years of Sundays.* Small things. Human things. The currency of ordinary love, spent to buy a roof for strangers.

"They weren't heroes," she said aloud, mostly to herself. "Heroes get statues. These people got *erased.* That's not glory, that's just—cost."

"Then why do it?" Wren asked. The child had settled cross-legged in the center of the hall like she owned it, which, Maren reflected, in about forty years she probably would.

Maren looked at Felix, who was running their thumb along the worn groove of their own name. "Why does anyone pay for someone else? Ask them."

Felix didn't answer right away. When they did, their voice was steady in the way Maren had learned meant the opposite.

"Because someone paid for me. My whole life, Vess took me in when the realm wouldn't. The guild fed me, taught me ledgers, gave me a wall to dust." A wet, surprised little laugh. "I thought I was hiding from my exile. I was living inside someone else's payment and calling it luck."

Anselm had moved to the far wall, where the carvings changed character—newer, shallower, hurried. His lantern light caught something that made him go very still.

"Master Okonkwo," he said. "You should see this."

The newer section wasn't names. It was arithmetic. Columns of figures in Ilsa's precise hand, decades of them, each line pairing a date with a forfeiture and a *substitute.* Maren read three lines and felt her stomach drop through the floor.

"She didn't let the payments lapse," Felix breathed. "She *rerouted* them. Look—every century the covenant demanded a willing sacrifice, a full surrender. But she found loopholes. Partial payments. Substitutes. Strangers from beyond the border, people nobody would miss, giving fragments instead of one person giving everything." Felix's hand flattened against the wall. "She's been cheating the thing beyond the wards for sixty years. Buying Vess time with other people's memories."

"And the math finally failed," Rhee said grimly. "Three days before the stars went out. The ledger knew."

Corvo crossed the hall, knelt at the keystone, and did something none of them expected. He pressed both palms flat against his grandmother's—no, against *the first exile's*—carving, closed his eyes, and hummed.

It was the only sound he could make, and Maren realized with a jolt that it wasn't random. It was a bell-note. The fundamental tone of the old covenant bells, the ones that used to ring across Vess on the night of the turning. He was answering the stone. Stone and bell, question and response, call and—

The hall lit up.

Not firelight. Not lamplight. The carvings themselves began to glow, faint and silver, thousands of names waking at once, and in the center of the floor a pattern emerged that had been invisible until now: thirteen lines radiating outward from the keystone, one for each original exile, each line ending in an empty socket of stone shaped exactly like—

"A bell," Maren whispered. "There were thirteen bells. One for each founder. The oath isn't reforged with words. It's *rung.*"

Anselm was laughing, actually laughing, the mad old goat. "Twelve sockets survive! Find me twelve bells cast in the old way and the covenant can be renewed!"

"Twelve," Felix repeated. Their eyes had gone distant, doing the terrible arithmetic Maren was beginning to recognize. Twelve bells, twelve keepers—and then the thirteenth socket, the keystone, the one that demanded a willing sacrifice of everything.

"Where are the bells?" Rhee asked.

Corvo opened his eyes. He looked, Maren thought, like a man who had just remembered something he'd sold a long time ago, and he raised one finger and pointed straight up—through the vault, through the stone, through the whole stacked weight of the city above.

At the guild hall. At Ilsa's counting room.

At the twelve bells hanging silent in the broker's tower, which everyone in Vess had always assumed were decorative.

"Oh," said Wren, with the flat satisfaction of a child confirming the world was exactly as suspicious as she'd always believed. "She's had them the *whole time.*"

Felix turned toward the stairs, and the light of ten thousand forgotten names silvered their face, and Maren saw the moment the dread in them became something harder and cleaner.

"Then we've been doing this backwards," Felix said. "We don't need to find the oath-keepers. We need to take the bells back from the woman who stole them—and ask her, one last time, whose name goes in the thirteenth socket."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the first thunder of the twelfth night rolled across the darkened sky.

One turn left.
