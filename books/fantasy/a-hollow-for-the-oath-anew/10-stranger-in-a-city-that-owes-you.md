## 10. Stranger in a City That Owes You

The stars came back on a Tuesday, which Captain Amara Rhee had always privately considered the most unremarkable day of the week. It suited them. They returned without ceremony, without trumpets, in ones and twos at first—like shy guests arriving at a party they'd been told was cancelled—and then all at once, so many and so bright that the whole of Vess stood in the streets at midnight and wept up at them.

Rhee had not wept. Rhee had stood in the courtyard of the Third Barracks with her arms crossed and her jaw set, because a captain does not cry in front of her soldiers, and because she was fairly certain that if she started she would not stop for a week.

"Captain," said Corporal Denn beside her, sniffling openly. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The face where you're crying but pretending it's wind."

"There is no wind, Denn."

"Exactly," said Denn, and handed her his handkerchief, which was the single most decent act of his military career.

That had been five weeks ago. Now the stars were old news, or as close to old news as anything got in Vess, where the bakeries had produced STARLIGHT LOAVES within two days and the tanners were already selling star-patterned leather that Rhee suspected would look terrible on anyone. The city rebuilt itself the way it did everything: loudly, badly, and with an enormous amount of arguing about whose fault the original problem had been.

The crown, to its credit—or rather, to its terror—had quietly dropped the matter of storm-callers. There was a proclamation. Rhee had read it four times looking for the word *exile* and found only a great deal of language about *unfortunate misunderstandings* and *the realm's enduring gratitude*. Nobody apologized. Crowns never did. But the hunters were recalled, the warrants burned, and Rhee kept her copy of the warrant list as a souvenir, because she intended to be very old someday and very petty about it.

Which left one piece of unfinished business.

* * *

The guild hall had been rebuilt twice over, once by fire and once by argument. It stood on the same corner in the Hollow district as before, but wider now, with a new roof of blue-green slate and a sign that read THE HOLLOW GUILD — BROKERS OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS, EST. LONG AGO, STILL TRYING.

Rhee stood across the street for a quarter hour working up her nerve, which was ridiculous. She had faced down border raiders, a flooded bridge, and one memorable incident involving a wyvern and a tax dispute. But this was different. This required her to walk into a building and possibly lie to someone who deserved better than lies, and Rhee had never been good at lying. Her mother had called her face "honest to a fault," which was a polite way of saying Rhee couldn't bluff at cards to save her life.

Inside, the hall smelled of ink and wood shavings and someone's lunch. A clerk with ink-stained fingers directed her upstairs—"Ledgers are through there, if you're settling a debt; complaints are Tuesdays"—and Rhee climbed the stairs past walls hung with the new covenant, copied out in a fair hand, thirteen names signed at the bottom where once there had been none.

She knew every name. She had helped gather most of them. Bridge-witch Osla Vane, restored to her bridge and insufferable about it. Bell-founder Tammas, who had spoken exactly four words since the night the stars went out and made each one count. The child, Wren, who remembered the old tongue and used it mostly to insult people in ways adults couldn't prove.

And at the bottom, in handwriting Rhee recognized the way you recognize a scar—

*Felix.*

No family name. No seal. Just the given name, offered like a coin placed carefully on a table.

Rhee pushed open the ledger room door.

Felix Okonkwo sat at a desk by the window with their sleeves rolled up and a pen behind one ear, squinting at a column of figures with the expression of a person personally betrayed by arithmetic. Sunlight came through the mended window and caught the grey threading their temples. They looked—Rhee searched for the word and hated herself for taking this long to find it—they looked *lighter*. Like someone had taken a stone out of them that they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it was there.

"Can I help you?" Felix asked, without looking up. "If it's about the Vane account, I've told the bridge-witch three times that emotional damages are not a recognized line item."

"It's about a debt," Rhee said.

"Everyone's is." Felix finally looked up, and smiled the polite, absent smile of a stranger. "Sit, if you like. Fair warning, I'm behind on everything and I intend to stay that way until at least noon."

Rhee sat. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. She had rehearsed this conversation eleven times on the walk over. In six of the rehearsals she told them everything. In four she turned around at the door. In one she cried, which she had ruled out as logistically unsound.

"You don't know me," she said.

"Should I?"

"No," Rhee said, and heard her own voice do something complicated around the word. "No. You shouldn't. But I know you. Or—I knew someone who looked like you. Seven years ago I escorted a storm-caller out of the realm at dawn. They didn't say much on the road. They kept looking back at the city like it owed them an apology."

Felix's pen had gone still.

"They were exiled for a storm they swore they never meant to call," Rhee went on, quieter. "I believed them then. I believe it more now. And when the sky went dark, I thought—that person deserved to see the stars come back. Everyone does. Even if they don't remember why it matters."

The silence stretched. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere below a bell rang the half-hour—the new bell, cast by Tammas, tuned by a child who hummed in a dead language.

"I'm sorry," Felix said at last, gently. "I don't remember any of that. I don't remember much of anything before the guild, honestly. The brokers say I walked in off the street some weeks ago and asked for work, and Master Ilsa took one look at me and hired me on the spot, which everyone agrees was unlike her." A small frown crossed their face, there and gone, like a fish surfacing. "Sometimes I dream about rain. Standing in it. Not running from it. Just standing. Isn't that strange?"

"No," Rhee said. "I don't think that's strange at all."

Felix studied her for a long moment. Rhee felt her honest-to-a-fault face doing what it always did, and braced for the question—*you're lying, aren't you? Who am I? Tell me who I was*—

But Felix only nodded slowly and picked the pen back up.

"Well," they said. "Whoever they were, I hope they're somewhere good. Someone ought to have told them thank you, I think. For whatever it was." They glanced up, wry. "You said you knew them. Did you ever thank them?"

Rhee thought about the drowned bridge. About the wall of water that should have killed her and hadn't. About a figure on the broken span with lightning in both hands and grief on their face, choosing, deliberately, to spend themselves rather than let her fall.

"Every day," she said. "Since the stars came back."

Felix smiled—a real one this time, crooked and warm, and for half a heartbeat Rhee saw the person she'd marched out of the realm at dawn, the one who'd cracked a terrible joke about the weather even then.

"That's good," Felix said. "Debts should be acknowledged. That's rather the whole point of the place." They slid a fresh ledger across the desk. "Now. You said you had business. Name and account, please, and I promise I'll pretend your handwriting isn't terrible."

Rhee laughed despite herself, and gave a name—not her rank, just her name, plain—and watched Felix write it down in a book full of impossible things.

* * *

She stayed an hour longer than she meant to. Long enough to learn that Felix took their tea with too much honey ("a stranger's habits," Rhee thought, or perhaps not so strange after all), that they were teaching Wren double-entry bookkeeping and the child was teaching them insults in the old tongue, that they laughed easily now and flinched at nothing.

Long enough to understand the shape of the choice she'd already made.

Because here was the arithmetic Rhee had done on the walk over, the sum she'd checked eleven times: Felix Okonkwo had paid for the sky with everything they were. Their name, their home, their history—all of it poured into the dark like oil into a lamp. And what remained, this light-eyed stranger with honey in their tea and rain in their dreams, was not what was *left over*. It was what they had *chosen*. A person built entirely out of giving.

To hand them back their grief now—to say *you lost a home, you lost a name, everyone you loved forgot you*—would not be a gift. It would be a bill, presented to someone who had already paid in full.

So Rhee drank her tea. She complained about the crown's proclamation. She let Felix talk her into volunteering for the guild's escort rotation, which was transparently a scheme to get free ward-soldier protection and which Rhee agreed to on the spot, because some debts you settle by staying near the debtor.

At the door, pausing with her hand on the frame, she said: "One more thing. Hypothetically. If someone had lost something—something big—and didn't know it. Would you tell them?"

Felix considered this with the seriousness they brought to ledgers.

"I think," they said, "that what a person doesn't carry, they're free to carry something else instead. So no. Unless they ask." A pause. The frown again, brief, like a cloud crossing a mended sky. "And if they ask... I'd want whoever knew the answer to be kind about it. Kinder than the truth requires."

"I can do that," Rhee said.

"Good." Felix smiled and returned to their columns. "Come back Tuesday. Complaints day. You strike me as a woman with complaints."

"More than you could ledger," Rhee said, and went out into the morning.

* * *

Vess in autumn was a city learning its own name again. Scaffolding everywhere, arguments everywhere, the smell of bread and river water and new-cut slate. On the corner by the guild hall, someone had chalked a message on the wall in a child's uneven hand, in the old tongue, and beneath it a translation added by a grown-up who clearly hadn't been consulted:

*WE REMEMBER WHAT WE GAVE.*

Rhee stopped and read it twice. Then she took out a stub of chalk—she'd started carrying one weeks ago, telling herself it was for marking patrol routes—and added a line of her own, small, underneath:

*AND WE KNOW WHO PAID.*

She didn't sign it. She didn't need to. Somewhere upstairs, a stranger with rain in their dreams was balancing books that could never balance, and the whole city owed them everything, and not one soul in Vess—not the crown, not the guild, not even the person themselves—would ever collect.

Rhee pulled her captain's coat tight against the wind, and walked into the crowd, keeping, as she would keep for years, the only oath left worth swearing: to come back on Tuesdays, to drink too-sweet tea, and to guard, with a soldier's stubbornness, the peace of someone who had bought it for them all.

Behind her, high overhead, the stars waited patiently for evening—thirteen moons mended, and counting.
