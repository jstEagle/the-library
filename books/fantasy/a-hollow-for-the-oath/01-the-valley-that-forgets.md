## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The mist came down off the ridge at dusk, the way it always did, and Paloma Hartwell counted his charges the way a shepherd counts sheep: merchant, two drovers, forty-one head of wool-heavy ewes, one boy too clever for his own good. All present. All alive. That was the whole of the contract, and he intended to keep it to the letter, because in the valley of Harrowmere an oath was the only thing that ever kept its shape.

"Master Hartwell." Aldous Vey, wool merchant, owner of the finest voice for complaining in three parishes, drew his cart alongside. "The fog's thick as tallow. Are we certain this is the road?"

"No," Paloma said.

Vey waited for more. Vey always waited for more. Paloma gave him none, and after a moment the merchant huffed and turned back to his reins, muttering about prices paid for guides who spoke like gravestones.

Paloma let it wash over him. He had learned long ago that words were weather — they passed through you and left nothing behind. He checked the mist-line ahead: a pale wall where the road bent around the shoulder of Cindermount, soft-edged and patient. Travelers called it beautiful. The ones who'd crossed from outside called it a door closing behind you.

He raised two fingers and the column slowed. The drovers knew the drill. Lambs tied close, lanterns hooded, no singing past the stones. There were six boundary-stones between here and the valley floor, each carved with the old marks that no god answered anymore, and you did not speak loudly near them for the same reason you did not shout in a sickroom.

They walked into the mist together.

It took them, as it took everything — sound first, then color, then the sense of distance itself. Paloma moved by count and habit: four hundred paces along the wheel-ruts, left at the split oak, downhill where the ground told him so. Beside him, Vey's breathing went quick and shallow. First crossing. They were always frightened their first time, and Paloma could remember — distantly, like a story someone else had told him — that fear was supposed to be contagious. It wasn't. Not anymore. Nothing was.

*Nothing is,* he corrected himself. *Not anymore.*

At the third stone the mist thinned and the valley opened beneath them: green folds of pasture going blue with evening, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless air, the town of Harrowmere laid out along its river like coins spilled from a purse. Somewhere a bell rang the hour. The sheep smelled grass and surged forward, and the drovers laughed, and Vey made a noise of relief so profound it was nearly religious.

"You see?" the merchant said, clapping Paloma on the shoulder. "I told my wife there was nothing to it. A little fog, a little superstition."

"There's never been anything to fear inside the line," Paloma said. "That's rather the point of it."

"And outside?"

"Outside," Paloma said, "is not my business."

Vey laughed at that, delighted, as though Paloma had made a joke. Perhaps he had. He was told he was funny sometimes, dry as August, though the laughter of others reached him the way light reaches the bottom of a well — visible, distant, belonging to someone else.

They came down into Harrowmere as the lamps were lit, and the contract ended at the door of the Woolwrights' Hall with a handshake and a purse of silver that weighed exactly what it should. Paloma counted it anyway. Not from distrust — from habit. Habit was the mortar that held the hollow places together.

"You'll stay for supper," Vey said. It was not quite a question. "My wife makes a lamb pie that would make a bishop weep. And the guild will want to toast you — safe delivery through the line, third time this season. You're something of a legend, Master Hartwell."

"I'm a road with legs," Paloma said. "Legends cost more."

But he stayed, because refusing required a reason and reasons required feelings, and it was simpler to sit at a long table while men he did not dislike toasted his name and meant it. He ate. He drank moderately. He said the right things when addressed and nothing when not, and if anyone noticed that his eyes never quite warmed, they put it down to the trade. Guides were like that. Soldiers were like that. Men who worked the mist-line were like that.

Only old Maren, the oath-keeper, looked at him across the hall with her clouded eyes and held the look a moment too long, the way you look at a house where the lights have all gone out but the chimney still smokes.

He nodded to her. She did not nod back.

* * *

His rooms above the chandler's shop were clean and cold, because cold was honest. Paloma lit one lamp, set the purse in the lockbox, and performed the evening rite that every sworn man in the valley performed whether they believed or not — and in Harrowmere, belief had been optional for a generation.

He knelt. He drew the oath-blade — a plain thing, iron and ashwood, no jewels, no edge worth honing — and laid it flat across his palms.

"The vow holds," he said. "The hand holds. The god holds."

The blade did not answer. It never answered. It had not answered since before Paloma was born, since the last turn of the moon when the god of Harrowmere — the Quiet One, the Under-Hill Lord, whatever name the old books gave Him — had gone silent mid-prayer, mid-oath, mid-breath. The priests had finished the liturgy to an empty room. The oaths sworn afterward had held anyway, which was the strange mercy of the valley: the vows did not seem to know their maker was gone. They held the way a bridge holds after the engineer dies. Out of momentum. Out of stubbornness.

Or out of debt, Maren said once, watching Paloma sheathe the blade. *Everything that holds, holds because something owes.*

He rose, knees cracking, thirty-eight years old in a body that felt like borrowed equipment. In the window, the valley lay quiet under a fat white moon. Twelve turns since the silence, the scholars said. Twelve turns of a moon that came around thirteen times some years, though no one liked to say so aloud, because the thirteenth turn belonged to the things under Cindermount, and speaking of them was thought to be an invitation.

Paloma did not believe in invitations. He believed in contracts, rates, roads, and the exact weight of silver. He was good at his work. People trusted him. Children waved at him in the market square and he waved back because he had learned the motion, and if any part of him remembered what waving was supposed to feel like, that part lived behind glass, underwater, very far away.

He had been twenty when he'd come up the mist-road from outside, half-dead, carrying nothing anyone had ever identified. He had no memory of why he'd come, only the iron certainty that he could not go back. The valley had taken him in the way valleys do — with soup, suspicion, and eventually a job. The oath-keeper of that time had sworn him in, and the blade had accepted him, and Paloma Hartwell had become a fixture, a legend, a road with legs.

What he had traded to be that, he did not know. What it had cost, he could not feel. That was rather the point of it.

He banked the lamp and slept without dreaming, as he always did, as he suspected dead men did.

* * *

Three days later, the storm came down off Cindermount like something chased.

It arrived past midnight, rain driving sideways, thunder rolling along the valley walls and echoing back on itself until the whole bowl of the world seemed to be drumming. Paloma woke — not from the noise; noise had never woken him — but from the silence underneath it. A hole in the storm, shaped exactly like his door.

He lay in the dark and listened. Rain on slate. Wind in the eaves. And beneath it, faint and rhythmic, a knocking. Not urgent. Patient. Three knocks, a pause, three knocks — the cadence of someone who knows the door will open eventually, because it has always opened eventually, everywhere, forever.

Paloma took the oath-blade from its peg. It sat warm in his grip, warmer than it should, the way it sometimes did near the boundary-stones, near the old marks, near anything that remembered the god who no longer answered. He went down the stairs with the lamp unlit, because he could find his way blind, and opened the door onto a wall of rain.

A boy stood in it.

Eight years old, perhaps nine. Barefoot. Soaked to the skin and shivering not at all, which was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was his eyes, which were the grey of the mist-line and just as depthless. The third wrong thing was that he looked up at a tall armed stranger in a doorway at midnight, in a storm, and showed no fear whatsoever — the calm of a child who has been somewhere much worse than this and carries the receipt.

The boy opened his mouth and spoke.

The words were nothing Paloma knew. They were older than the road, older than the stones, older than the names of the hills — syllables that seemed to arrive slightly before the boy's lips moved, as though language itself were remembering them on his behalf. Any scholar in the kingdom would have heard gibberish, or worse, a tongue from before tongues.

Paloma understood every word.

*The account is open,* the boy said, in that language like riverbeds and roots. *The account is open, and I am the payment, and the moon turns thirteen times this year.*

Paloma stood in his doorway with rain blowing past him into the hall, and felt — for the first time in eighteen years, faint and far away, like a knock from inside a coffin — something move in his chest.

He stepped aside and let the boy in.
