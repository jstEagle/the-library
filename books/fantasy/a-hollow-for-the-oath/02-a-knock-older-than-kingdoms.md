## 2. A Knock Older Than Kingdoms

The storm came up the valley like something being chased.

Paloma heard it before he saw it — a low grinding in the west, where the peaks shouldered the sky, and then the first fat drops hitting the dry-packed earth of his yard with a sound like knuckles on a door. He was mending the hinge on his woodshed when the wind arrived, tearing the last of the autumn leaves off the rowan tree and sending them skittering across the stones like startled birds. By the time he had the axe and the firewood under cover, the rain had gone from scattered to solid, a grey curtain drawn across the world, and the light was failing fast.

He stood under the eave of his house and watched it come. This was the hour he hated most, though hate was perhaps too strong a word for what he felt about anything. The hollow hours, he called them privately — the stretch between work's end and sleep's beginning, when there was nothing left to do but be a man alone in a house that did not care whether he returned to it or not. In the valley, everyone had their evenings: the miller had his cards, the smith had her songs, old Maren had her gods and her ledgers of oaths. Paloma had a roof, a blade, and the particular silence of a man who has traded away the part of himself that would have minded.

He went inside. He lit the lamp. He set water on to boil because that was what one did at this hour, and sat down at his table with a whetstone and the oath-blade, though the blade never dulled and never needed him. It was habit more than devotion. The god it answered to had not spoken in a generation; the steel still held its edge the way a dead man holds his posture, out of habit rather than intent. Paloma ran the stone along it anyway, slow strokes, watching the lamplight slide along the metal, and let the sound fill the room the way other men let conversation fill theirs.

* * *

The knock came at the ninth bell, when the storm was at its worst and no sensible creature was abroad.

Three knocks. Small ones. A child's fist, if a child's fist could hit a door hard enough to be heard over weather like this.

Paloma set down the whetstone. He did not reach for the blade, which was itself strange — twenty years of mercenary work does not wash out of a man, and strangers at doors in storms were how bad stories traditionally began. But something in the knock's rhythm, patient and unafraid, put him in mind of nothing so much as a debt collector. And debts, in the valley, were the one thing that always found their man.

He opened the door.

The boy stood on his step with the rain streaming off him as though it were an inconvenience rather than a tempest. Eight years old, perhaps. Thin as a winter branch, dark-haired, dark-eyed, dressed in clothes that had been fine once — Paloma could see the ghost of embroidery at the collar, worked in a pattern of interlocking circles that made his eyes ache slightly if he looked too long — now soaked through and crusted with mud from a road that, as far as Paloma knew, ended ten miles short of his door. The boy was not shivering. That was the first wrong thing. Any child caught in this cold would be blue-lipped and chattering by now. This boy stood straight as a fence post, water running off his chin, and looked up at Paloma with the calm appraisal of a merchant pricing a horse.

The second wrong thing came when the boy spoke.

"*Veth ka orun. Veth ka ilen.*"

The words went into Paloma's ear and meant themselves before he could stop them. *I am come. I am owed.* Not translated — understood, the way one understands the meaning of a scream or the shape of one's own name. His mouth knew the language the way his hands knew the sword: without asking permission, without any memory of learning.

Paloma had served in three companies and crossed the mist-line twice in his youth, back when he still took contracts beyond the valley. He had heard the trade-tongues of the coast, the camp-cant of the border wars, the liturgical drone of the river temples. No scholar living, Maren liked to say, could read the script on the standing stones above the tarn, and those stones were younger than whatever this was. This language was older than kingdoms. It was older than the idea of kingdoms. And it lived in his mouth like it had been waiting there.

"No," Paloma said aloud, to the boy, to the night, to whatever was listening. "No, you've got the wrong door."

"*Veth ka orun,*" the boy repeated, with the mild insistence of someone stating a fact of geography. *I am come.*

"Go home." Paloma's voice came out flat and steady, which surprised him. Underneath the steadiness his heart was doing something it had not done in years — hammering, actually hammering, against ribs that usually kept it at a polite distance. "Whatever you want, whoever sent you—"

The boy tilted his head. Rain dripped from his nose. Then, with the terrible patience of the very young, he reached up and pressed two small fingers against Paloma's chest, over the sternum, right where a heart would be if a man still kept one there.

"*Ilennath,*" the boy said softly. *Empty place.*

Paloma shut the door.

He stood with his back against it, breathing, listening to the storm try to tear the roof off. Through the wood he could hear the boy — not crying, not pleading, not even moving. Just standing there, in the rain, in the dark, with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world and knew exactly where it was supposed to be.

*Empty place.* The words echoed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for them to hit bottom. They didn't.

He told himself he would wait until the boy gave up. Children grew cold; children grew frightened; children, eventually, went home. He counted a hundred heartbeats. Two hundred. The knocking did not come again, but neither did the small footsteps retreat from his step. When he finally cracked the door open — telling himself it was only to check, only to confirm the boy had gone — the child was still there, soaked and silent and looking up at him with those dark, unhurried eyes, and the sight of him did something to Paloma's chest that he had no vocabulary for, some deep tectonic shift beneath the hollow places, like ice shifting on a lake before it breaks.

"One night," Paloma said, and heard his own defeat in it. "You're frozen through. One night, food, fire. Then tomorrow I take you to Maren, and she sorts out whatever this is, because I want no part of it."

The boy considered this offer with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he stepped past Paloma into the house, dripping, and paused just inside the threshold to look around the single room — the table, the lamp, the whetstone, the blade — with an expression that was not wonder and not judgment but recognition, as if confirming that everything was where he had been told it would be.

"*Orun,*" he said, satisfied. *Come. Arrived. Home, almost.*

"It is not," Paloma said, closing the door against the storm, "*your home.*"

But the boy was already peeling off his ruined shoes, neat as you please, as though he had done this in a hundred houses. As though he had been expected.

* * *

The boy ate like a bird and dried like a cat, close to the fire but never quite touching it, arranged in a small tight knot of limbs on Paloma's one good chair. Paloma fed him barley soup and half his loaf, and the boy ate with a courtesy so precise it unnerved him more than greed would have — thanking each dish silently with a little bow of the head, as though the food might take offense otherwise. When Paloma asked his name, the boy said a word that was less a name than a note, a single high syllable that seemed to hang in the air after it was spoken, and then, seeing Paloma's face, tried again.

"Wren," he said. The common tongue sat strangely in his mouth, each word placed down carefully like a stone crossing a stream. "Bird-name. Small bird." He touched his own thin wrist. "Fits."

"Who gave you that name?"

Wren considered the question with the seriousness it apparently deserved. "The road," he decided.

"And who gave you the road?"

Something moved behind the boy's eyes then — the first flicker of anything like fear, quickly folded away, folded so smoothly and so young that it chilled Paloma worse than any tears would have. Children who learned to hide fear that early had been taught by someone, or something, and the teaching was never kind.

"Beneath," Wren said finally. He pointed down, at the floor, at the earth below the floor, at whatever lay beneath the earth below the floor. Then he pointed at his own chest, at the place a soul is commonly believed to live, and made a small opening gesture with his fingers, like a door swinging wide. "Sold," he said, in careful common tongue. "Not mine now. Borrowed." He smiled then, quick and bright and utterly wrong, a smile with no fear left in it at all. "Until moon comes back thirteenth time. Then—" he spread his empty hands, and the gesture needed no translation in any language.

Paloma sat very still. The fire popped. Outside, the storm worried at the eaves like a dog at a bone.

"You'll sleep here tonight," he said at last, because there was nothing else that could be said to a child who talked like that, "and in the morning we're going to see Maren, and she's going to tell me this is nonsense, and then you're going home. Wherever that is."

Wren nodded agreeably, as one nods at an elder's harmless fictions. Then he slid off the chair, padded to the hearth, and lay down on the warm stones with his back to the fire, curled small, and was asleep within minutes — truly asleep, deeply, the sleep of the utterly exhausted, and Paloma realized with a start that the composure had been effort, all of it, every minute of the calm, and the boy had simply run out of strength to hold it.

Paloma sat up most of the night with the oath-blade across his knees, watching the child breathe.

It was near the fourth bell, with the storm dying to a sullen drizzle, that Wren began to talk in his sleep. Not common tongue. The old language poured out of him soft and continuous, a murmur of names and numbers, and Paloma, who could not stop his own understanding any more than he could stop his own heartbeat, caught fragments: *the ledger, the tally, the turn of the moon, the debt, the debt, the debt comes due.* And once, terribly, clearly, in a voice not entirely the boy's own:

"*Ilennath veth ka.*" *The empty place is come.*

Paloma looked down at his own chest. At the hollow behind his sternum where feeling used to live, the quiet country inside him where nothing hurt and nothing sang, the price he'd paid so long ago he'd stopped counting it as a price at all.

The boy slept on, small and borrowed and sold, and Paloma sat in the grey hour before dawn with the dead god's blade across his knees, understanding — slowly, the way ice understands spring — that the knock at his door had not been an accident, and that whatever was owed in this valley, it had known precisely which empty place to find.
