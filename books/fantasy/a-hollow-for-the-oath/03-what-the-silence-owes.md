## 3. What the Silence Owes

Maren Osgood had kept the last oath-house in the valley for forty-one years, and in all that time she had never once been afraid of a child.

She was afraid now.

The boy sat on her floor among the scattered chalk-lines and ledger-weights, cross-legged, patient as a stone, while she knelt over the transcription she had made of his speech. She had written it phonetically at first — the shapes of the sounds as her old ears caught them — and then, somewhere past midnight, the shapes had begun to resolve. Not into any tongue she knew. Into something older than knowing. The letters assembled themselves under her hand the way frost assembles on glass: not chosen, but *recognized*.

Oath-script. The first script. The one the god had spoken before the god went quiet.

"Say it again," she said to the boy.

He looked up at her with eyes the color of river ice and repeated it without hesitation, without inflection, the way a bell repeats its note when struck. She did not understand how he knew to wait for her chalk. He simply waited, and when she nodded, he spoke again, and each phrase settled onto the slate of her memory like a debt being entered into a book.

Because that was what it was. That was the horror of it. It was not a prayer or a plea or a wandering child's nonsense. It was structured. It had clauses. It had a *date*.

Maren set down her chalk and pressed her gnarled hands flat against her knees until they stopped shaking.

*The thirteenth turn closes the sale.*

Outside, the storm that had brought the boy to Paloma Hartwell's door had spent itself against the valley walls, and morning came up thin and gray through the oath-house window. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled. A dog barked at nothing. The valley went about its small, forgetful business, and Maren sat in the house built for listening to a silent god, holding a debt notice written in the voice of an eight-year-old boy.

She needed Paloma Hartwell. And she hated needing anyone, but most of all him — because he would come when called, punctual as grief, and he would feel nothing about any of it, and some mornings that hollowness of his frightened her more than the Undercourt ever had.

She rose, wrapped her shawl, and went to fetch him.

* * *

Paloma came when called, because that was what he was for.

He stood in Maren's doorway with rain still dripping off his oilskin, the boy's hand swallowed inside his own, and waited for the old woman to speak. Behind him the lane glistened. Ahead of him, Wren tugged free and walked into the oath-house like someone returning to a place rather than entering one, which Paloma found himself noting and filing away in the part of him where notes went to be forgotten.

"You transcribed it?" he asked.

"Sit down, Hartwell."

He sat. He was good at sitting, at waiting, at standing in doorways and riding escort and doing all the thousand small services a hollow man performed so well that people mistook him for whole. The wool merchant from the mist-line contract had wept with gratitude at journey's end, clasped his arm, called him the steadiest man in the valley. Steady. Yes. A stone is steady. A stone feels nothing either.

Maren unrolled her transcription across the table between them, weighting the corners with candlesticks, and Paloma looked down at the script and felt — nothing. That was the thing about the old letters. Everyone in the valley could see them; only the sworn could read them, and reading required the god's attention, and the god had not attended to anything in a generation.

Except.

Paloma leaned closer. His breath fogged the parchment. The letters were moving — not crawling, not alive, but *settling*, the way sediment settles in still water, arranging themselves into sense. And beneath the settling, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, something in his chest turned over.

Not feeling. He didn't have feelings. But the hollow where feelings used to live had a shape, and for one moment the shape remembered being full.

"Maren," he said quietly. "I can read this."

"I know." Her voice was dry as old chalk. "So can I. First time in thirty years. Which tells you what's writing through it." She tapped the page. "Read it aloud. All of it. I want to hear if you flinch."

He read:

*"Let it be entered that the soul of the child called Wren, of no house, of no line, was sold in good faith by those who held it, to the Court Beneath, for consideration rendered and received. Let it be entered that the sale completes upon the thirteenth turning of the moon, at which time the property shall present itself or be collected. Let it be entered that no oath sworn above ground shall avail against collection, save only oaths sworn to the One Who Listens, whose silence is noted, whose silence is filed, whose silence shall be answered when the accounts are called."*

The room was very quiet after that. Rain ticked in the eaves. Wren sat on the floor drawing in spilled chalk dust, making spirals, humming a tune with no melody in it.

Paloma set the parchment down with great care, the way a man sets down something that might go off.

"The thirteenth turn," he said. "When does it fall?"

"Nineteen days," Maren said. "I've been counting since the boy arrived. We're on the twelfth turn's wane now." She lowered herself into her chair across from him, joints cracking like green wood. "You understand what this is, Hartwell? It's not a curse. Curses are passions. This is *bookkeeping*. Somebody sold that child's soul, properly, legally, with witnesses, and the court below has kept the account current ever since. Nineteen days, and the sale closes, and the boy walks down the mountain whether his feet agree or not."

"And the clause about the god."

"Ah." Maren smiled without any warmth in it. "You caught that too. *Whose silence is noted.* They're keeping books on Him, Paloma. On our god. Do you know why He went quiet?"

"No one knows why He went quiet."

"No one *asks*," she snapped. "Forty years I've swept this floor and lit these candles and recited the offices to a god who doesn't answer, and everyone in this valley treats the silence like weather. Like something that happened *to* Him. But debts don't happen to gods, Hartwell. Debts happen *because of* gods. You don't fall silent because you're tired. You fall silent because you opened your mouth once and promised something, and now the bill has come due and you cannot pay it, and every year you stay quiet the interest grows." She jabbed a finger at the final line of the transcription. "*Whose silence shall be answered when the accounts are called.* That's not poetry. That's a lien. The Undercourt holds paper on our god, and this child — this child's soul is collateral that's about to change hands."

Paloma looked at the boy. Wren had drawn a spiral, and inside the spiral, smaller spirals, and inside those, smaller still, until the chalk dust became a pattern that hurt to follow, a pattern that went down and down like a stair into the dark. The boy hummed. His eyes were distant and calm and utterly unafraid, and Paloma thought: *he knows. Some part of him has always known what he is. Sold goods. Waiting inventory.*

Something moved in the hollow of his chest again. He identified it, distantly, the way he'd identify weather coming over the ridge. Not feeling. Just pressure. Just the ghost of a shape pressing against the inside of a locked door.

"What happens to the god," he asked, "if the sale closes?"

Maren was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft, which from Maren was worse than shouting.

"I think the silence ends," she said. "One way or another. Either He pays, or He's seized, or the debt is discharged against whatever He pledged. And I think — Hartwell, I have thought this for years and never said it aloud — I think there were others. Before the boy. Other souls sold, other notices sent up the mountain, other times the thirteenth turn came round. And each time, our god paid. Quietly. Out of His own — " she gestured vaguely, at the shrine, at the cold hearth, at the empty air where presence used to pool — "out of Himself. Until there wasn't enough left of Him to make a sound."

Paloma sat with that. Outside, the valley went on forgetting: carts, dogs, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless gray. Nineteen days. The safest place in the world, and the danger was already inside it, sitting on the floor drawing spirals in chalk dust.

"So the village votes," he said. "They'll vote to give him up."

"They'll vote tonight. Aldous Thorne is already going door to door with his arithmetic. Nineteen days versus two hundred years of peace. It's not a hard sum, for people who've never had to feel the answer." Maren fixed him with her pale, relentless eyes. "And what will you do, Hartwell? You'll stand in the square and say nothing, same as always. You'll do your duty to whoever wins the vote, because duty is all you've got left, isn't it? They took everything else and left you the obedience."

He should have felt stung. He registered that he should have felt stung, the way he registered that fire was hot, as information rather than experience.

"He stays with me until the vote," Paloma said, standing. "After that, he goes wherever the valley decides. That's the law of oaths."

"That's the law of cowards wearing oaths' clothes," Maren said, but he was already at the door, and the boy had already taken his hand again, small fingers lacing through his with the unselfconscious trust of someone who did not know he was inventory, and Paloma walked out into the gray morning with the debt notice burning behind him like a brand.

Halfway down the lane, Wren tugged his sleeve. Paloma looked down. The boy pointed back at the mountain — the high peak above the mist-line, where the clouds never lifted, where no map marked anything but stone — and then he touched his own chest, right over the heart, and looked up at Paloma with those river-ice eyes.

And he spoke. Not the old language. Common tongue, rough and rusted, three words he had clearly been saving:

"*It knows you.*"

Paloma's stride did not break. His face did not change. He was, after all, the steadiest man in the valley.

But in the hollow where his heart had been, something knocked — once, softly, like a fist on a far-off door — and did not stop knocking for the rest of the day.
