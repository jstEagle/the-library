## 4. Thirteen Turns

The village met in the tithe-barn because it was the only roof big enough to hold every grudge at once.

Paloma stood at the back with his arms crossed and Wren's weight against his leg — the boy had taken to standing pressed there like a burr, silent even now, watching the lanterns with eyes that reflected them too cleanly. Maren occupied the front bench beside the oath-stone, her staff across her knees like a drawn blade she was choosing not to use. The rest of the valley filled the middle: wool merchants and millers, the ferryman's widow, the cooper whose son Paloma had once pulled from a flooded sluice. Good people. Practical people. People who had spent their whole lives inside the mist-line learning the one law that mattered.

*Oaths hold. Wonders don't.*

And a debt notice written in a dead language on a child's skin was neither.

"The moon turns thirteen times this season," said Aldous Crane, the miller, from the platform. He was a broad man with flour worked so deep into his knuckles it looked like age. "Everyone here can count. Everyone here knows what that means."

"It means nothing," someone said near the front. "It's a story."

"It's a story with a signature." Crane held up a copy of the rubbing Maren had taken from Wren's shoulder — charcoal on cloth, the old script looping like water finding its way downhill. "Isbet of the Undercourt read this herself before she left. Or tried to leave. She said—" he consulted a paper, unnecessarily, for effect, "—she said the sale closes on the thirteenth turn. She said the court beneath the mountain sends for what is owed. And she said, and I quote, *they always come.*"

The barn went quiet in the way barns do when everyone has already decided something and is waiting for permission to say it aloud.

Paloma felt Wren's small hand find two of his fingers and hold on.

He waited for the familiar nothing. That was how it always went: the hollow man stood in the crowd and watched the world argue about itself, and inside his chest there was only the wide flat silence where feeling should have been, smooth as a river stone, worn by years. He had stood in this same barn when the fever took the Halloway children and voted for quarantine without flinching. He had stood here when the ferryman drowned and spoken the eulogy in a level voice while the widow wept into her sleeve. Nothing touched him. That was the bargain he'd made, though he could not remember making it — the god's protection ran both ways, Maren said; the oath kept him safe from the world and the world from him, and what it cost was everything a man was supposed to carry under his ribs.

But tonight, listening to Crane lay out the arithmetic of surrender, Paloma felt something move in that silence.

It was not much. It was like feeling a draft through a wall you'd believed solid — a thin cold thread of *something* where there had never been anything. It hurt, faintly, the way a numb limb hurts when the blood comes back. He almost stepped away from it. Habit told him to step away from it.

Wren's grip tightened on his fingers, and Paloma stayed exactly where he was.

"Nobody's saying we harm the boy," Crane was saying, gentling his voice the way men gentle voices when they are about to propose cruelty. "The Undercourt doesn't want him dead. They want what they bought. We walk him up to the mist-line on the twelfth night, we set him outside it, we light no lamps, we open no doors. Whatever comes for him comes and goes, and the valley keeps its peace another hundred years. It's how our grandfathers survived. There are stones older than this barn that mark where they did the same."

"There are," agreed old Sefa from the weaving benches, and her voice was terrible in its calm. "I've seen them. I pulled moss off one when I was a girl. There was a name cut into it. Half a name. The weather took the rest."

"That was a different time—"

"It is never a different time," Sefa said. "It is only ever us, deciding who stands outside the line."

The murmur rose and broke against her and rose again. Paloma watched faces: the ferryman's widow looking at her hands, the cooper looking anywhere else, young Tam Halloway — grown now, bearded, the boy from the sluice — staring hard at Paloma as if a mercenary's opinion were worth more than anyone else's in the room. Maybe it was. That was the trouble with being the valley's sword. When the valley wanted to do an ugly thing, it looked to the man paid to do ugly things and hoped he'd nod first.

Maren rose.

She did not raise her voice. She never needed to. "I am the last oath-keeper in this valley," she said, "and I will tell you what the silence owes, since apparently no one asked me before calling this meeting." She let the pause sit. "Our god went quiet a generation ago. You all know the story — the prayers stopped being answered, the wonders stopped working, and the wise decided the god had died or fled or never been. I have spent thirty years reading what records survive, and I will tell you what I believe. Our god did not go silent. Our god went *broke*."

The word landed like a dropped anvil.

"Debts came due," Maren continued. "Bargains made above were called in below. Souls sold, hearts sold, years sold — the whole rotten ledger of what desperate people trade when they think no one is keeping accounts. One by one the god's protections were foreclosed. That is why the mist-line holds. Not love. Collateral. And that child—" she pointed her staff at Wren, and the boy did not flinch, "—is the newest entry in a book that has been eating this world since before any kingdom you can name. Sending him out is not paying a debt. It is feeding the thing that holds the ledger. And things that are fed come back hungry."

"Then what would you have us do?" Crane demanded. "Fight the Undercourt? With what? Oatcakes and righteous speeches?"

"With whatever it costs," Maren said. "That is what an oath is."

"And what does your oath cost, keeper?" This from the widow, bitter and low. "You keep your post. My husband kept his ferry. Who pays for the boy? Because it won't be you. It'll be whoever the court decides to take instead. A debt doesn't vanish. It moves."

Paloma heard himself speak before he had decided to.

"She's right."

Every head turned. His own voice sounded strange to him — rough, like a door long unopened.

"A debt doesn't vanish," he said, stepping forward out of the shadow of the doorway. "It moves. So let's stop pretending we can aim it. You put that boy outside the line, and the court takes him — or it doesn't, and it takes someone else, and you spend the next hundred years cutting half-names into stones. I've done contract work for twenty years. I know how creditors talk. They don't want the boy. They want the *sale closed*. And there's more than one way to close a sale."

"Name it," said Crane.

"You buy it back. Or you break the contract. Either way, someone walks down that mountain and does it in person." He paused. "Someone with a blade that still counts for something inside these mists, and nothing at all outside them. Someone who's already lost the only thing worth taking."

The silence stretched. In it, Paloma became aware of the thing moving in his chest again — stronger now, unmistakable, a hot wire pulled taut behind his sternum. Fear. He catalogued it the way he catalogued terrain: unfamiliar, elevated, dangerous. He had not been afraid in nineteen years. He had forgotten how much of fear was grief wearing armor.

"You'd die for a foundling who can't even say your name," the widow said. It wasn't quite mockery. It was worse — it was bewilderment.

"He's learning," Paloma said. "Yesterday he said *bread*. Badly. But he said it." Something that was almost, horribly, a smile moved across his face. "And my name's not worth much anyway. Ask anyone."

A few people laughed despite themselves, the way crowds do when a joke lands too near the truth. The tension cracked just enough for Crane to call the vote, and Paloma listened to the valley decide against him — hands rising one by one, practical hands, kind hands, the cooper's and the miller's and finally, agonizingly, young Tam Halloway's, who would not meet his eye. Only Sefa stayed seated, and Maren, and the widow after a long moment, her hand rising halfway and falling like a bird with a broken wing.

"Carried," Crane said heavily. "Twelfth night. The mist-line. I'm sorry, Hartwell. Truly. But the valley comes first. It always has."

"The valley comes first," Paloma agreed. "That's why I'm taking it with me."

* * *

They gave him until the twelfth night and no more, and posted no guard on his door, because guarding it would mean admitting what they were letting happen. Paloma spent the days as he'd spent every day of his hollow life — sharpening, packing, checking straps — except that now each motion rang against that new hot wire in his chest instead of falling into silence, and he kept having to stop and breathe through it like a man walking off a wound.

Maren came the second evening with a satchel of salt, a page torn from a ledger, and no pleasantries.

"Isbet left this," she said. "Her notes on the sale. Read the margin."

He read. The script was cramped, clerical, furious. *Two entries, same date, different ledgers. Whoever this soldier is, the court has sold to him before. Check the massacre records. Check the year.*

"I don't remember any massacre," Paloma said.

"No," Maren said quietly. "You wouldn't. Would you?"

He looked at her, and the wire in his chest pulled tight enough to sing.

Outside, the storm that had brought Wren to his door had passed, but the sky still churned, and through a tear in the clouds the moon showed early — thin, new, counting toward its impossible thirteenth turn. Wren stood in the doorway behind them, watching it, and spoke his first full sentence of common tongue, clear and small and terrible:

"We go down. Before it goes dark."

Paloma folded the ledger page into his breast pocket, over the place where his heart should have been, and reached for his sword.

"Yes," he said. "We go down."
