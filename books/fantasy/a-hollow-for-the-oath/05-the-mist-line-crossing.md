## 5. The Mist-Line Crossing

They left before dawn, while the village still slept off its shame.

Paloma carried Wren on his back through the wet streets, the boy's arms looped loose around his neck, his breath warm against Paloma's collar. The bundle of supplies at Paloma's hip held three days of bread, a skin of water, a flint, and nothing else worth naming. Maren had pressed a second skin into his hands at the gate — brackish, she'd said, but it would last — and had looked at him the way one looks at a man walking into deep water.

"The blade will die," she'd told him. "The moment you cross. Whatever it was to you in here, it will be iron and nothing more out there. Do you understand what you're giving up?"

He'd understood. He'd nodded. And now, with the mist-line a pale scar across the valley's throat a mile ahead, he found he could not make himself look at the sword at his belt.

The valley was waking around them. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin gray threads. Somewhere a dog barked, and a woman's voice called a child home, and the ordinary sounds of the ordinary morning went on as though no one had voted in the tavern two nights past to hand an eight-year-old boy to the dark. Paloma did not hate them for it. That was the worst part. He had stood in that room and watched their faces — the miller's fear, the smith's pragmatism, old Sela weeping without changing her vote — and he had felt nothing at all, and he had understood them perfectly, because he was made of the same arithmetic. A valley of hollow arithmetic, and one boy.

One boy who had, three days ago, said his first word.

* * *

They had been sitting by the fire in Paloma's cottage, the storm long gone but the damp still in the walls. Wren sat cross-legged on the floor, sorting Paloma's whetstones by some system of his own, and Paloma had been mending a strap, and neither of them had spoken in an hour. It had been, Paloma realized with a strange distant surprise, the most comfortable silence of his life — though *comfortable* was a word he handled like a coin from a dead kingdom, knowing its shape, not its worth.

Wren held up the coarsest stone, the one Paloma used for the oath-blade, and frowned at it. Then he said, in a voice cracked from disuse, like a door opening in an empty house:

"Sharp."

Paloma's hands went still. "Yes," he said carefully. "Sharp."

"Sharp," Wren repeated, tasting it. Then, pointing at the fire: "Hot." At the bread: "Bread." At Paloma, after a long, considering pause, during which the boy's dark eyes moved over Paloma's face as though reading something written very small: "Hollow."

The word had gone through Paloma like cold water through cloth. Not *Paloma*. Not *man* or *friend* or *sir*. The boy had looked at him and named the thing itself.

"That's not a word for people," Paloma had said, too quickly.

Wren had tilted his head, unconvinced, and gone back to his stones. But that night, falling asleep in the cot Paloma had given up for him, the boy had murmured one more word, half-swallowed by sleep: "*Home.*"

Paloma had sat by the fire until the coals went gray, telling himself the ache in his chest was nothing. A draft. A memory with no name attached. The valley's magic, perhaps, fraying at its edges where a boy who spoke the old language slept under his roof.

He had not believed himself. He was learning, slowly and against his will, that he could not.

* * *

Now the mist-line rose before them out of the morning, and it was nothing like the maps described.

The maps showed a border. What stood before them was a wall of slow silver, forty feet high, hanging in the air without wind to move it, and where it touched the ground the grass grew silver too, and then gray, and then it simply stopped being grass — became something like frost, something like ash, something like the memory of grass. Paloma had crossed it a hundred times in his years of contract work. He had never once looked at it the way he looked at it now: as a door he was walking through in the wrong direction.

Wren slid down from his back and stood at his side, small hand finding Paloma's without ceremony. The boy stared up at the wall of mist and did not flinch. He never flinched. Paloma had begun to suspect the boy had already been to the place they were going, in some way that had nothing to do with roads.

"Scared?" Paloma asked. It was a test. He wasn't sure of what.

Wren considered the question with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he shook his head, and pointed at Paloma's chest, and said, with terrible clarity: "Scared."

Paloma almost laughed. "That's not — I'm not—"

But his heart, that tenant he had never met, was hammering against the walls of him like something trying to get out. Or in.

He drew the oath-blade.

It had never left him in thirty years. It had come to him at fourteen, in the chapel of the silent god, when the old keeper had laid it across his palms and spoken the words that made him what he was: a hollow man, sworn and sealed, protected in the valley where the god's silence did not reach. The blade had a weight beyond iron. It had a *presence* — a low hum at the edge of hearing, a warmth in the grip, a sense of being watched over that Paloma had stopped noticing the way a man stops noticing his own heartbeat. It had killed for him. It had stood between him and the valley's rare dangers. It was the one constant, the one proof that the god, silent or not, had once held him in something like regard.

He held it up. The mist did not part for it. The blade's hum, if he imagined he could hear it, was already fading — or he was only already grieving it.

"Last chance," he said, to Wren, to the valley, to the god who did not answer. "Turn around, and we both keep what we have."

Wren squeezed his hand. Once. Firm. A small fist making a decision older than kingdoms.

Paloma sheathed the blade, and walked into the mist.

* * *

It was cold, and then it was not cold, and then it was the strangest thing of all: it was *loud*.

Not sound. The mist made no sound. But sensation — the world's whole inventory of sensation — came at him like water through a broken dam. Color first. He had not known color could be loud. The gray of the valley was a kind of mercy he had never recognized, and now the world beyond the mist-line hit him with green so violent it hurt, green of wet moss and new leaves, and the brown of turned earth, and the blue of a sky that went on and on without the valley's soft ceiling of haze. His eyes watered. He staggered. Wren's hand tightened in his, an anchor.

Then the smells. Wet earth and pine sap and, absurdly, the ghost of woodsmoke from a hearth miles away, and each one arrived with a passenger. Woodsmoke brought a kitchen. A kitchen brought a woman's voice, humming. The humming brought—

He went to his knees in the wet grass.

"Paloma." Wren's voice, small and alarmed, the word still new in his mouth. "Paloma."

"I'm fine." He was not fine. Memory was flooding him, and it was not the clean memory of the valley, where the past lay behind glass. This was raw. This was *unpaid*. He could smell the massacre — he could smell it, blood and burned thatch and the sweet rot beneath, a village whose name he had not thought of in thirty years because in the valley he *couldn't*, because the hollowing had taken the name with everything else — and he could hear the screaming, and he could feel, with terrible precision, what it was to be fourteen years old and crouched in a root cellar with his hands over his ears, making the bargain that would let him live.

*I will not feel this. Take it. Take all of it. Let me live and I will not feel anything ever again.*

He had thought, all these years, that the bargain had been made in the chapel. That the keeper's blade and the god's seal had hollowed him. But he knew, kneeling in the grass on the far side of the mist, that the chapel had only formalized it. The sale had been his own. His own voice, fourteen years old, offering his heart to whatever was listening in the dark.

And something had been listening. Something had taken the deal. And something, he understood now with a cold that had nothing to do with the morning, kept accounts.

"Paloma." Wren crouched beside him, and did a thing no one in the valley had ever done: he put his arms around Paloma's shoulders, and held on. The boy was small and light and smelled of woodsmoke and wool, and he held on the way you hold on to something you intend to keep.

Paloma shook. He was not proud of it. Thirty years of composure came apart in the wet grass of an ordinary hillside, and he wept — not gracefully, not quietly, but the way a man weeps who has thirty years of tears banked and accruing interest. He wept for the village with no name. He wept for the keeper who had died believing Paloma was at peace. He wept for the hollow man he had been, who had walked through decades of gray and called it living.

And underneath it all, quieter, he wept for the blade.

Because when the shaking finally slowed, and he wiped his face with a sleeve that did nothing, and he drew the oath-blade to look at it — to see what he had lost — the sword lay in his hands like iron and nothing more. Dead weight. No hum. No warmth. No watchfulness. The god's protection, gone, exactly as Maren had promised. He was outside the valley now. He was prey, and marked, and mortal, and feeling everything, and the one constant of his life was a piece of worked metal.

Wren looked at the blade, then at Paloma's face, and said his longest sentence yet, each word placed like a stone across a stream:

"God. Not. Gone." He touched the flat of the dead blade. "Sleeping. Here—" he touched his own chest. "And here." He touched Paloma's.

"The god is a story, Wren. Stories don't cross the mist-line."

But even as he said it, Paloma felt it — faint as a pulse under a bandage, faint as a memory of warmth in the grip of a cold sword — something in the iron that was not iron. Something that had not died. Something that was, perhaps, waiting to see what he would do next, now that he was the one thing he had never been in thirty years:

Unprotected. Unhollowed. Awake.

Wren stood, and tugged his hand, and pointed east, where the land fell away toward hills he had never seen and a mountain beyond them that the maps named only with a blank space, as though the cartographers had agreed not to look at it.

"Go," the boy said.

Paloma sheathed the dead blade. He picked up his pack. He took the boy's hand, this small creature who had walked out of a storm and into the gray rooms of his life and turned every light on, and they walked east together, out of the valley's long shadow, into a world that could feel him coming.

Behind them, at the mist-line, the silver wall hung silent. And if a blade's hum could survive its own death, it might have said — very softly, in a language older than any kingdom — *thirteen turns, hollow man. She is counting.*
