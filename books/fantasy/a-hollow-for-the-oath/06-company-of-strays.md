## 6. Company of Strays

Wren counted things.

That was the first thing Paloma learned about the boy beyond the valley's edge, once the terror of the crossing had drained out of both of them and left only the long gray road east. Wren counted the crows on the fence posts (nine), the stones in the creek bed where they drank (too many; he gave up laughing), the days since the storm that had carried him to Paloma's door. He scratched each one into the dirt of his memory with a small deliberate finger, as though numbers were a rope he could climb back up into himself by.

Paloma watched him do it and felt something in his chest turn over — not feeling, exactly, not yet, but the place where feeling would live if it ever came home. Outside the mist-line, the hollow in him echoed. Every kindness was a bell rung in an empty church: he heard it late, and faintly, but he heard it.

The boy looked at him across their small fire on the second night and said, in careful, broken Common, "You count too."

"Do I?"

"When you think no one sees." Wren touched his own wrist, where a soldier might wear a tally. "Steps. Coins. Ways out."

Paloma fed the fire another branch rather than answer. The boy was right, and being seen that clearly should have been intolerable. Instead it was like sunlight through a shutter crack — thin, and warm anyway.

* * *

They found Isbet on the fourth day, or perhaps she found them.

She was sitting in the middle of the road ahead of them, which was the first strange thing, because the road east of the valley saw maybe a cart a week and no sensible person sat down in the middle of one. She had spread her belongings around her in neat squares — clothes folded to knife-edge precision, a writing case, a clay lamp, three books bound in skin the color of drowned things. She sat among them like a merchant whose market had died.

She stood when she saw Wren.

Paloma's hand went to his blade before he decided anything at all. The woman froze, then very slowly raised both hands, palms out, fingers spread. Ink stained every nail.

"I'm not going to touch him," she said. Her voice was dry as ledger paper. "I want you to understand that first. Whatever else is true about me, I need you to believe that."

"Who are you?" Paloma asked.

"Isbet Corvane. Scribe, Third Quill, Court of Sold Things." She swallowed. "Formerly. As of eleven days ago, when I ran."

Wren stepped forward before Paloma could catch his sleeve. The boy studied her the way he studied everything — counting — and then he spoke in that old tongue that lived under language, the one Paloma understood without wanting to. He asked her something.

Isbet's composure cracked straight down the middle. She answered in the same tongue, haltingly, like a woman walking on ice she'd only read about, and whatever she said made Wren's face go carefully blank in a way Paloma had learned to dread.

"He says," Isbet translated for Paloma's benefit, though Paloma hadn't needed it, "that I have ink on my hands that never dries. And I told him that's true, and that it's why I'm here." She looked at Paloma directly for the first time. "Your boy's sale was written by my office. My hand copied the fair copy. I didn't know what I was copying until it was too late to uncopy it. So now I'm going to help you steal him back, or die trying, because it's the only entry I'll ever get to correct."

Paloma should have refused her. Everything he'd been trained to be — the competent, respected, empty man of the valley — said refuse her, strangers are liabilities, oaths are for fools. But the hollow in his chest turned over again, and he heard himself say, "Can you cook?"

She blinked. "Badly."

"Good. Neither can I. We'll starve together." He turned back up the road. "Keep up."

Behind him he heard Wren laugh — the first time Paloma had heard it — a sound like water finding a channel, and Isbet's surprised silence, and then the scuff of her boots following.

* * *

Torv came to them on the ninth night, out of a fallow field, standing motionless among the stubble like a boundary stone that had gotten ideas above its station.

It was tall as two men and built of river-gray stone fitted without mortar, and it had been standing there, Isbet judged from the moss on its shoulders, for thirty years. Its maker had carved no face. There was only a smooth oval where a face would go, and set in the center of the oval, a small round window of amber glass, and behind the amber glass, a light. Dim. Patient. Still burning after thirty years in an empty field.

"It's a golem," Isbet whispered. "Bound servitor. Someone paid the court for it, once. Then abandoned it here when the work ran out." She said it the way people said *orphan*.

Paloma approached it the way he'd approach a wounded bear. Up close he could see the seams where its maker's chisel had slipped, the repairs done in mismatched stone, the way its left arm ended not in a hand but in a clubbed mass of rock that had clearly been that way from birth. Abandoned, yes. But also, he thought — looking at the amber light — *left running*. Left waiting.

"They don't wait," Isbet said. "Not this long. Not unless—"

Wren walked past both of them, straight up to the thing, and put his small hand flat against its shin.

The amber light brightened.

The golem did not move otherwise. It did not kneel, did not speak — Paloma learned later it had no voice given to it either, another economy of its maker's — but the light behind its breast-glass swelled like a coal breathed upon, and the whole vast frame trembled once, all its stones shifting a hair closer to one another, the way a man shivers when someone says his name after years of silence.

"What did you do?" Paloma asked Wren.

The boy looked up at him and answered in the old tongue, and Paloma translated it aloud before he understood he meant to: "He said hello. Nobody ever says hello to them. They're just used."

Isbet made a small sound. When Paloma glanced back at her, she had turned away and was doing something complicated with her eyes and the horizon.

"We can't take a golem," he said, because someone had to be practical, and apparently that curse had fallen to him.

But Torv followed them anyway, at a distance, all that night and the next day, keeping always just at the edge of the firelight like a dog unsure of its welcome, until on the second evening Wren simply patted the ground beside him and the ground, being stone-adjacent, obliged. Torv sat. The earth settled a full inch. Nobody moved camp.

By morning nobody remembered what the camp had been like without it.

* * *

The nights grew cold as they climbed into the hill country, and cold made fires, and fires made talk, and talk was the thing Paloma had avoided for twenty years the way other men avoid debt collectors.

He couldn't avoid it now. Wren demanded stories, and Isbet supplied terrible ones — Undercourt gossip, mostly, scandalized whispers about magistrates and their vanity collections — and when she ran out she demanded stories back, and Paloma discovered that his supply was nearly empty. Twenty years of contracts. Twenty years of escorting merchants through fog and killing nothing worse than bandits and boredom. He had done nothing and felt less, and telling it aloud made it sound like someone else's life, some other hollow man's, and that was almost a mercy.

"You were a soldier before," Isbet said one night. It wasn't quite a question. "Before the valley."

"There was a before," Paloma said. "I keep the details in the same drawer as everything else."

"The massacre," Isbet said quietly. "Everyone from that region has a version."

"And everyone's version is wrong, including mine, because I wasn't in my right mind when mine was made." He poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. Sparks climbed. "Ask me something else."

Wren, curled against Torv's stony flank — the boy slept better against the golem than anywhere, said the warmth was different, said it felt like being remembered — lifted his head and asked something in the old tongue.

Paloma translated automatically, and then wished he hadn't. "*What did you sell?*"

The fire popped. Isbet looked at him with a scribe's terrible attentiveness, the look of someone who records things whether or not they should be recorded.

"Nothing," Paloma said. "I've sold nothing. That's rather the point of me. I'm the hollow man. Empty as a drum. What you hear when I speak is just the shape of the emptiness."

Wren held his gaze, unblinking, eight years old and older than kingdoms, and did not believe him. Children never do. They haven't yet learned that adults' lies are load-bearing.

"Sleep," Paloma said gruffly. "Long road tomorrow."

* * *

On the twelfth night, the danger finally caught up with them, because dangers do, and because Isbet had been honest about one thing: the Undercourt did not like losing property, and it especially did not like losing scribes who knew where the ledgers slept.

They came at moonrise — four shapes in gray, faces like unfinished sculpture, moving with the horrible economy of things that have sold their hesitation. Paloma killed two of them with the oath-blade before he remembered it was supposed to be dead outside the valley, and marveled later, half-asleep with blood in his hair, that it had burned alive in his hands the moment Wren was threatened. Oaths hold, the valley taught. Apparently some of them held further than the valley knew.

Torv killed the third. There was no technique to it, only mass and grief and thirty years of waiting redirected into ten seconds of motion, and afterward the golem stood over the ruin of the thing with its amber light guttering like a candle in wind, trembling, and Wren pressed his palm against its ankle until the light steadied.

The fourth escaped, running east, toward the mountain.

Isbet watched it go and her face did the arithmetic none of them wanted done. "It will report," she said. "We have until the thirteenth turning — six days now, five — and then every door beneath that mountain closes, and every bargain in every ledger locks forever."

"Then we walk faster," Paloma said.

But that night, after, when Wren was asleep against Torv and Isbet had taken first watch, Paloma sat awake by the coals and examined the strange new architecture inside himself. Four days ago he had been one man with a sword and a contract. Now he was — he counted, because counting was how Wren made sense of the world and it seemed suddenly a fine method — one mercenary, one stolen child, one disgraced scribe, one abandoned god of clay and patience. One fire. One direction.

Belonging, he thought, watching Isbet wrap Wren in her spare cloak while the boy slept, believing herself unobserved. Nobody had drawn up terms. Nobody had signed. There was no contract at all, and still it held, and still it held, and that was either the most dangerous magic he'd ever encountered or the only kind worth having.

In the morning they broke camp early and walked east, toward the mountain, toward the court where everything was for sale.

And in the bottom of Isbet's recovered satchel, wrapped in oilcloth she had not yet shown them, lay a ledger open to a page with two entries in the same faded hand, dated the same day, decades apart — one for a soul the size of a child's, and one for something of Paloma Hartwell's that he had spent twenty years pretending he'd never owned at all.
