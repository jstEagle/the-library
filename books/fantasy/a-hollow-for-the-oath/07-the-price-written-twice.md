## 7. The Price Written Twice

The ledgers smelled of cold iron and old wax, the way all Undercourt records did, and Isbet hated that she still found the smell comforting.

She had told herself she was only organizing them. That was the scribe's sickness — you could take the girl out of the archive beneath the mountain, but the archive stayed in her hands. Three nights camped in the ruin of a shepherd's cot on the valley's far slope, while Torv stood watch outside like a patient boulder and Wren slept curled against Paloma's bedroll, and Isbet sat by the fire with her stolen ledgers spread around her like the petals of a black flower, sorting entries by year, by court, by price.

She had burned her name to buy their passage into the Undercourt's outer halls. She had not burned her training. Her hands still knew how to read a debt column faster than most people read a prayer.

*Entry 4,411,* she wrote in her own shorthand, copying for safety. *Sale of soul, minor, male, age indeterminate. Seller: unknown, signature pressed in ash. Buyer: the Undercourt, perpetual holding. Consideration: one winter's mercy, three loaves, passage across the mist-line.* The date was thirty years old. The ink had gone brown as dried blood.

Wren's sale. Or the sale of whatever Wren had been before he was Wren — before someone pressed his small hand into ash and gave him to the court beneath the mountain in exchange for bread and a road out of a dying village. Thirty years ago. Which meant the boy sleeping ten feet away was older than he looked, or time moved strangely where souls were kept, or — and this was the possibility Isbet liked least — the Undercourt had been aging him slowly, deliberately, letting him ripen toward the thirteenth moon like fruit left on a branch so it would fall at exactly the right moment.

She turned the page.

And stopped.

Her first thought was that she had mis-sorted. Scribes mis-sort; it is the occupational sin. She checked the year against the ledger's spine-marking, then against the court's fiscal seal stamped in the margin — a closed eye over an open hand, the sigil she had once been proud to press into wet clay. The date matched. Same season. Same month.

Same day.

Two entries, one date, decades old. The first she already knew: Wren's soul, sold for bread. The second read:

*Sale of heart, adult, male. Seller: present, of sound mind, signature witnessed. Buyer: the Undercourt, perpetual holding. Consideration: survival entire — the seller shall walk away from the burning while all within it do not.*

Isbet read it four times. The fire popped. Outside, Torv shifted his weight with a sound like a millstone turning, and somewhere down the slope a night bird called the same three notes over and over, as if it too were trying to make sense of something.

*Survival entire.* That phrasing had a name in the court's liturgy. It was called the Massacre Clause, and it was rare, and it was obscene, because it did not purchase goods or years or talent. It purchased exemption. One person, plucked whole from a slaughter, in exchange for the one thing that made the surviving worth anything at all.

She looked up from the page at the man asleep across the fire.

Paloma Hartwell slept the way hollow men slept — flatly, completely, without dreams or the small motions of dreaming, like a sword in a scabbard. She had watched him for weeks now and told herself it was tactical observation. He was their blade, their map, their knowledge of the valley. But she knew what she was really watching for. She was watching for the flicker. For grief, for fear, for tenderness — anything that would prove there was a man inside the mercenary and not just a very well-maintained absence.

There had been flickers lately. Since Wren. Small ones, like light under a door.

And now here was the door itself, written in her own court's hand: *one heart, sold by its owner, witnessed, sound of mind.*

He had done it himself. No one had taken it. Somewhere, decades ago, in a burning place whose name the entry did not record — the clause never recorded names, that was part of its cruelty, the buyer got anonymity along with everything else — Paloma Hartwell had stood in front of an Undercourt factor while everything he loved went up behind him, and he had signed.

She thought about how he spoke of the god. *My oath holds. My blade counts. The god is silent but the oath stands.* He wore his silence like armor and called it faith. She had assumed the god had failed him. That some divine abandonment had scooped him out and left the shell.

No. The shell was self-made. Purchased. Filed and stamped and shelved in the deep stacks, third gallery, west wall, between a merchant's courage and a widow's memory of her mother's voice.

Isbet closed the ledger and found her hands were shaking.

It was not the discovery that shook her. She had spent forty years in the Undercourt; she had catalogued worse than a man selling his own heart. What shook her was the arithmetic underneath it, the terrible symmetry she could not unsee: two entries, one date. A child's soul sold by someone who wanted him to live. A man's heart sold by himself because he wanted to live. The court had taken both, filed both, held both for thirty years — and now both seller and sold were sitting around the same fire walking toward the same mountain, and only one of them knew what he had paid.

*He doesn't remember.* She was almost certain of it. The clause took memories with it — that was how the court guaranteed satisfaction. You could not regret a heart you could not recall. Paloma spoke of his past the way a man reads a stranger's letter: accurately, distantly, without heat. The massacre he "barely remembered." She had thought that was trauma. It was inventory. His past was in the same vault as Wren's soul, and when they reached the Court of Sold Things, he was going to see it on a shelf.

Or — and here her stomach turned, scribe's mind always finding the crueler column — he might find it and choose it again. Because that was the other thing about the Massacre Clause. It could be renewed. The court loved a repeat customer. And a man who had traded feeling for survival once, offered the same bargain at the moment of greatest danger, with a child's soul on the scales and his own terror screaming at him —

She had seen men sign twice. They always signed faster the second time.

* * *

She waited until morning, until Wren was off gathering firewood with the serious concentration of a boy who had decided providing fuel was his sacred duty, until Torv had wandered to the stream to stand in it motionless, which was apparently how golems fished. Then she set the open ledger on the flat stone between them and said, "I need to show you something, and I need you to stay sitting down."

Paloma looked at the ledger, then at her face, and something in him went very still — the stillness of a man who has walked into enough ambushes to recognize the shape of one. "Whose death is it?"

"Yours," Isbet said. "Thirty years ago. Mostly."

She turned the book toward him and put her finger under the entry and made herself watch his face instead of the page, because the page she already knew.

He read it the way he did everything — thoroughly, without hurry, twice. She saw his eyes reach the consideration line. *Survival entire.* She saw him stop there, and she saw the exact moment the words stopped being words and became a mirror.

"Where," he said. His voice came out level. Too level. Level the way ice is level right before it cracks under weight.

"The entry doesn't say. The clause never does." She kept her voice gentle, which cost her; gentleness had been burned out of her own ledger long ago. "But I can tell you what the clause means, because I processed eleven of them in my years in the archives. It means there was a fire, or a battle, or a flood, and everyone in it died except you. It means you walked out while it happened. And it means you paid for the walking with—" she tapped the page, "—this."

"My heart."

"What the court files as *heart* isn't only love. It's grief. Fear. Joy. Regret. The whole wet animal weight of being a person. You didn't sell your tenderness, Paloma. You sold your capacity to be wounded, and tenderness just — went with it. Like selling a house and finding the garden comes too."

He was quiet for a long time. Down the slope, Wren's voice rose in a small triumphant bark — he had found a deadfall, he was dragging branches twice his size, he was gloriously alive — and Paloma's eyes went to the sound without his permission. Isbet saw it happen. Saw the pull of it. Whatever lived in the hollow of him had heard that boy and turned toward it like a flower toward a window.

"That's why I understand him," Paloma said finally. "His language. The old tongue. Hollow men are half-court already. We're written in their books. I've been reading his language since the night he knocked on my door because—" his jaw worked, "—because I'm in the same catalogue."

"You're adjacent entries," Isbet said. "Same date. Same shelf, probably. Third gallery, west wall." She hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. It slipped out, the archivist's reflex, and she watched it land on him like a blow.

He looked down at his own hands. Turned them over, as if checking whether they were still his.

"I don't remember signing," he said. "I remember smoke. I remember a door. I remember choosing the door." A pause, and then, quieter, with something raw moving underneath the levelness: "I've told myself for thirty years that the god took it from me. That I was robbed. That my hollowness was a wound someone else gave me." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "I wasn't robbed. I was the seller. I stood in front of one of your factors and I *haggled*, didn't I. I'd have haggled. That's who I was."

"I don't know," Isbet said honestly. "But I know this, and you need to hear it before we're inside the mountain, because inside the mountain no one will say it plainly: the court will offer it back to you. That's how they work. They will put your heart in front of you — beating, waiting, thirty years of everything you missed stored inside it — and they will let you hold it, and then they will tell you the price is Wren. Take back yourself, give up the boy. And Paloma—"

"I'd sign," he said.

The words fell between them like a dropped blade.

"Not forever. Not if I had a breath left to fight with. But in that room, with it in my hands—" He shook his head slowly. "You don't understand what it's like wanting nothing. Wanting is a country I was exiled from before I was old enough to know its name. If they put wanting in my hands, I don't know what I'd do. You should plan for the worst version of me."

Isbet nodded slowly. She had expected this — had feared it, had hoped against hope she was wrong — and hearing him say it plainly was somehow worse and better at once. Worse, because it confirmed the trap. Better, because a man who knows his own weakness is harder to kill than one who doesn't.

"Then we plan for it," she said. "Together. Before we descend. I'll write the terms of our counter-bargain tonight and you'll witness them, and if the court puts your heart in your hands, you'll look at this page first." She pulled a fresh sheet toward her, dipped her pen. "Because there's one thing your entry doesn't say, Paloma, and I only understood it last night. Look at the buyer's mark on yours. Really look."

He bent over the ledger. The closed eye over the open hand — but beneath it, small, almost invisible, a secondary notation in the factor's private shorthand. Isbet had spent forty years learning that shorthand. It was the reason she had survived her disgrace rather than being fed to the court's silence like the others.

Paloma frowned. "What is it?"

"It says *reserved*," Isbet said. "Your heart isn't warehoused stock. Someone in the court marked it for themselves, thirty years ago, the day you signed. And Paloma — the factor who made that notation, the one who reserved a hollow man's heart for his own collection—"

She looked up at him, and let him see in her face how bad it was.

"—is the magistrate himself. He'll be waiting for us. And he already knows exactly what you'll trade."
