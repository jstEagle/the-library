## 8. Descent Beneath the Mountain

The moon's twelfth turning found us at the mountain's root, where the stone had been carved so long ago that the carvings had forgotten they were carvings and become simply the shape of the world.

We came down through a seam in the rock that Isbet called the Tithe-Gate — a crack barely wide enough for Wren, let alone a man carrying a dead sword and a scribe carrying a ledger heavier than her guilt. Above us, the last of the valley's mist clung to the peaks like a mourner who would not leave the grave. Below, the dark went down without end.

"Once we pass the threshold," Isbet said, "nothing is free. Not air. Not answers. Not mercy." She said it the way a priest says a liturgy — not to inform us but to arm us. "Every soul down there has sold something. Remember that when they smile at you. Ask yourself what it cost them, and what they'll want back."

"And what did you sell?" Torv asked.

The golem's voice was gravel poured slow. He had asked it every night since we'd left the crossroads shrine, and every night Isbet had answered with silence or a joke, and tonight she did neither.

"My name," she said. "A long time ago. I was the Undercourt's chief registrar. I wrote the bargains. I kept the ledgers. And when I found an entry I couldn't stomach, I tried to unwrite it, and the Court took my name as penalty. Do you know what it is to walk through a room full of people who knew you, and have none of them able to say what you were called?" She adjusted the strap of the ledger on her shoulder. "You learn to answer to *hey*. To *you there*. You learn that a person is mostly a word, and if you take the word away, what's left has to be enough."

Wren tugged my sleeve. He had learned eleven words of common speech by then, and he spent them like coins he feared would be recalled. "*Go now?*"

"Now," I said.

I went first, because I always went first, because that was the shape of the oath even with the god silent and the blade cold. The oath-blade had been dead since the mist-line — no hum, no warmth, just steel — but I carried it anyway. A man should carry the weight of what he's lost. It keeps him honest about what he still has.

The Tithe-Gate opened onto a stair cut in spirals, descending into a light that had no source. It was the color of candleflame seen through honey, and it did not flicker, and it cast our shadows in four directions each.

At the bottom of the stair was the city.

I had imagined the Undercourt as a dungeon. What waited beneath the mountain was a market. Streets of black stone polished to mirror-brightness, lined with stalls and galleries and counting-houses, all of it lit by that sourceless gold. And everywhere, people. Thousands. Merchants and clerks and children running errands, wearing fine clothes and finer faces, and every one of them missing something. I learned to see it within a hundred steps: the woman selling lamps whose eyes held no color; the man weighing grief in little brass scales, his own hands trembling though his face was serene; a child — a child, gods help me — trading laughter at a booth stacked with bottled songs.

"They're not prisoners," Isbet murmured beside me. "That's the thing outsiders never understand. No one here was dragged. Every one of them signed."

Wren pressed close against my leg. His hand found mine, small and cold, and I felt the touch land somewhere in my chest that had been boarded over for thirty years. Outside the valley, everything landed there. Grief. Fear. Tenderness. It was like walking with a wound that had finally decided to bleed.

"Keep your hood up," I told him. "Keep your mouth shut. If anyone speaks to you, look at me and I'll answer."

"*Yes,*" he said. His eleventh word.

We moved through the market toward the Court proper, and the bargains began before we'd gone two streets.

A vendor of memories offered me my mother's face for the price of my left hand's skill with a blade. A moneylender with no shadow offered passage past the outer ward for "one true memory, freely given." An old woman selling luck looked at Wren too long, and I put my body between them and walked faster.

"That's how it works," Isbet said quietly. "They don't rob you. They wait until you *want* something badly enough to pay. The whole city is a snare shaped like a bazaar. The desperate come down here to buy their way out of desperation, and every purchase digs the hole deeper."

"And you sold your name because—"

"Because I wanted to unwrite a boy's bargain," she said, "and unwriting costs more than writing. I paid, and the bargain stood anyway, and I've spent forty years learning that the house always wins." Her jaw tightened. "Until now. Now we steal instead of buy. That's the only trade they've never planned for."

Torv brought up the rear, and the crowd gave him room. A golem is a rare thing anywhere, and rarer here, where flesh is the only currency anyone trusts. I caught people staring at him — at the maker's mark worn half away on his shoulder, at the moss in the seams of his arms — and I understood that they saw him the way I'd once seen myself: something built for a purpose, then set aside.

The outer ward of the Court was a gate of green bronze taller than the mountain seemed to allow, worked with thirteen moons in a ring, twelve of them tarnished and one — the thirteenth — bright as a new coin. Two wardens flanked it, and they were not people. They were bargains given legs: shapes of fused contracts, parchment and wax and bone, and where a face should be, a single line of script that read *PAID IN FULL*.

Isbet stopped ten paces out. "They'll ask for toll. We can't pay what they'll ask. So we don't negotiate. Torv — the gatehouse. There's a winch behind the left pillar. If the gate falls closed, nothing inside gets out and nothing outside gets in, and the wardens must stand at their post, because standing at their post is the only order written into what they are."

"And while they stand," Torv said, "you walk through."

"While we run through," Isbet corrected. "And Torv — after the gate closes, you won't be able to follow. The winch jams. That's why it's never used. I'm sorry. I should have said this sooner, and I didn't, because I was a coward, and because I hoped—"

"I knew," Torv said.

The golem said it simply, the way he said everything, and I felt the floor of the world tilt a little.

"You knew?"

"I am made to hold things," Torv said. "Doors. Lines. Other people's weights. My maker built me to hold and then left, and holding is the only prayer I know." He looked down at Wren, who had gone very still, understanding more than his eleven words could carry. "Little one. When you are grown and someone asks who saved you, say: a gate, and a golem, and a name burned to ash. Say all three. That is the whole of me."

Wren crossed the space between them and wrapped both arms around the golem's mossy forearm, and Torv knelt — slowly, stones grinding — and rested his great cracked brow against the boy's hair.

I had watched men die. I had made men die. But I had not, in thirty hollow years, felt anything like what moved through me watching a machine love a child better than most fathers manage flesh. Outside the valley, I felt everything. That was the curse of leaving. That was also, I was beginning to suspect, the cure.

"The twelfth moon sets in four hours," Isbet said, voice rough. "When it sets, the thirteenth begins, and the sale closes, and no power under any sky reopens it. Move."

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong: quickly, and from a direction no one was watching.

The gate itself opened easily — Isbet spoke a registrar's phrase and the bronze groaned apart, and the wardens turned their script-faces toward us and demanded toll, and we ran, and for fifty strides it was working.

Then the alarm sounded. Not a bell. A *word*, spoken by the walls themselves, in the old language — Wren's language — and every citizen in the market turned toward us at once, and I understood that in a city where everyone has sold something, the one crime that cannot be bought off is theft.

"Wren!" I swung him onto my back. "Hold on and don't look at their faces!"

Behind us, Torv reached the gatehouse. I heard the winch take his weight, heard chain and counterweight, heard the wardens' script-faces flare as they moved to stop him — and stopped themselves, because their post was the gate, and the gate was closing, and the only order written into what they were would not let them leave it.

"TORV!" Isbet screamed it, and her scream had a name-shaped hole in the middle of it, because she had no name to pour into the calling.

The golem looked back at us once across the narrowing gap. Moss and stone and the maker's mark worn to almost nothing. He raised one enormous hand — not waving, I realized. Holding. Holding the door, holding the line, holding us, the way he'd held everything since the day he was made and abandoned.

"Hold fast," Torv said. "All of you. That is the whole of me."

The green bronze came down like a verdict. The sound went through the mountain and through me, and Wren's fingers dug into my shoulders, and Isbet made a noise I will hear until I die.

Then hands were on us from the dark ahead — court guards, or things dressed as them — and there was no time to grieve, which may be the cruelest feature of the Undercourt: it takes your sacrifices and doesn't even give you a moment to feel them.

They marched us down a corridor of white stone into the heart of the Court, Isbet's ledger confiscated, my dead blade taken, Wren torn from my back and carried ahead of me, screaming in the old language words I understood perfectly and wished to God I didn't.

*The thirteenth moon rises. The buyer comes to collect.*

They threw us into a holding cell of glass, and through the glass I watched a clerk ink a fresh entry into a ledger that mirrored Isbet's own, and the entry read:

*Lot thirteen. One soul, child, male, lightly used. Reserve price: waived. Buyer: the Court itself.*

And beneath it, in older ink, faded but not faded enough:

*Companion lot. One heart, adult, male, seller's own. Unclaimed thirty-one years. Still beating.*

Isbet read it over my shoulder. She went white.

"Paloma," she whispered. "They never sold your heart. They've been *keeping* it. It's here. It's been here the whole time — and tomorrow, when the thirteenth moon rises, they mean to sell you both together."
