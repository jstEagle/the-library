## 9. The Court of Sold Things

The magistrate's hall was built from doors.

That was the first thing Paloma understood, standing on the threshold with Wren's small hand locked in his own: every surface around him had once belonged somewhere else. Doors of black oak and pale birch, doors studded with iron, doors carved with saints whose faces had been chiseled smooth — all of them pried from their frames across a hundred kingdoms and set into walls that rose beyond the reach of torchlight. Some still bore their hinges. One, near the ceiling, still had a knocker, and as he watched it swung once, softly, though no hand touched it.

*Everything here was taken,* he thought. *And everything here is for sale.*

Isbet had told them as much on the descent, her voice fraying with each stair. *Don't touch anything. Don't admire anything. In the Undercourt, wanting is a bid.* He kept his eyes down now, fixed on the floor — flagstones cut from tombstones, he realized, their old names worn to ghosts beneath his boots — and walked.

Wren's grip tightened. The boy had not spoken since Torv fell. He did not need to. His silence said what his voice could not yet shape in common tongue: *you came this far; do not stop.*

They crossed the hall between shelves of wares, and Paloma made the mistake, one single mistake, of looking.

There were hearts in glass jars along the third shelf, suspended in fluid that glowed like drowned starlight. There were voices coiled in boxes, humming when the draft passed over them. There was a woman's laugh pinned under a bell jar like a moth. A soldier's courage hung from a hook, folded neat as a cloak. And everywhere, everywhere, the price tags — little slips of parchment in Isbet's careful scribe-hand or older hands than hers, sums written in years, in names, in first memories, in the color blue.

He looked away too late. Something on the fourth shelf had already felt him notice.

"Paloma Hartwell."

The voice came from everywhere at once, the way water comes through a cracked hull. It was neither cruel nor kind. It was *clerical*. That was somehow worse.

At the far end of the hall, upon a dais of stacked ledgers, sat the magistrate. It had been human once — perhaps. Now its skin had the gray sheen of vellum, and where its eyes should have been there were two wax seals, unbroken, stamped with sigils Paloma almost recognized. When it moved, papers rustled somewhere inside it, deep, like wind through a dead man's letters.

"You are not on my ledger," the magistrate said. "That is an irregularity."

"I'm here for the boy." Paloma stepped forward, putting himself between Wren and the dais. Behind him he heard Isbet's breath catch — she knew, better than any of them, what stepping forward meant in this place. "His soul. You hold it. I've come to buy it back."

"Buy." The magistrate turned the word over like a coin of doubtful weight. "With what, hollow man? You carry no coin. You carry no name worth recording. You carry—" It paused, and the wax seals seemed to fix on him, and something in the hall went very quiet, the way a courtroom goes quiet before a verdict. "Ah. But you do carry something. Come. Let us consult."

It lifted one hand, and a ledger flew to it from a shelf three rows away, pages riffling like a startled bird. The magistrate read. Somewhere in Paloma's chest — in the hollow where his heart should have been — a cold wind blew through empty rooms.

"Entry four thousand ninety-one," the magistrate recited. "Date: the nineteenth of Reaping, forty-one years past. Seller: Paloma Hartwell, aged nine years. Goods received: one heart, whole, beating. Price paid: survival. Witnessed by: the fire at Aldermoor." It closed the book gently, almost tenderly. "You have come a long way to visit your property, sir. Shall I fetch it out?"

"No," Paloma said.

"Yes," said the magistrate, and snapped its fingers.

A case rose from beneath the dais — plain wood, no lock, no glass. Inside, on a bed of black velvet, lay a heart.

It was beating.

Forty-one years in a box beneath a mountain, and it beat steady and patient as a man walking home, as if it had never doubted he would come. It was smaller than he remembered. It was redder than he remembered. And when Paloma looked at it, the hollow in his chest — the one he had carried so long he had mistaken it for peace, for competence, for the simple flatness of a well-run life — that hollow *ached*, sudden and total, like a limb waking after hanging dead.

He heard himself make a sound. He could not name it.

"There it is," the magistrate said softly. "Still waiting. They always wait, you know. Hearts are the least resentful of the sold things. Souls grow bitter. Names grow strange. But a heart only ever waits." It leaned forward, vellum skin creasing. "Take it, Paloma Hartwell. Take it back, feel everything you surrendered, weep at last for Aldermoor, love whom you choose. It is yours. It has always been yours."

"And the boy?"

"The boy is a separate entry." The magistrate spread its hands, and the great hall of stolen doors seemed to lean in around them. "One soul, child-aged, sold by parties unknown, payment due at the thirteenth turning. The sale closes tonight. Unless—" the wax seals gleamed, "—unless paid off. The outstanding balance is one soul, freely given, of equal weight. Yours will do nicely. Take your heart, hollow man. Feel again. And give me the boy's soul in exchange, and walk out of here *whole*."

Whole. The word rang in him like a struck bell.

He saw it, in one blinding flash: walking out of the mountain with his chest full and warm, Wren's hand in his, the valley ahead, Isbet laughing at some joke he would finally understand because jokes would land in him again instead of sliding off. He saw grief too — Aldermoor, the fire, his mother's voice, all of it waiting to flood in — but grief was the price of the rest, and oh, it would be worth it, it would be *worth it* —

Beside him, Wren tugged his sleeve. The boy looked up. He didn't speak. He simply opened his free hand, palm up, the way he'd done the first storm-night at Paloma's door: *here. Whatever you need. Here.*

And Paloma understood, with terrible clarity, what the magistrate was really offering. Not his heart. His heart was bait. What it offered was the oldest bargain in the world, the one that had filled these shelves: *your comfort for someone else's cost.* Take back your feeling. Let the boy pay. Every citizen of this court had stood exactly here, in front of exactly this choice, and every one of them had reached out.

He thought of Maren saying, *the god went silent because the debts came due.* He thought of Torv holding the gate, stone arms wide, buying seconds with its body. He thought of Isbet burning her name page by page to open their road, growing fainter with each word, until no record anywhere remembered who she'd been.

Survival always costs someone. He had learned that at nine years old, in the smoke at Aldermoor, when he'd made this same trade the first time without knowing what he signed.

He would not sign it twice.

"No," Paloma said.

The magistrate tilted its head. "You refuse your own heart?"

"I refuse the terms." His voice came out low and even, the voice he used mid-contract, the wool-merchant voice, the voice that did not shake. "You want a soul of equal weight. Fine. But you named the wrong goods." He drew a breath, and the cold air of the court went into him like a blade. "I don't sell you Wren's soul. And I don't take mine back and leave him. I offer you something else. Everything else."

"Paloma," Isbet whispered behind him, ragged. "What are you—"

"Every year I have left," he said to the magistrate. "Every year between now and my natural death. All of it. Not my heart — my *time*. The years I'd have lived. Give them to the debt. Weigh them against a child's whole life and tell me they don't balance."

The hall went utterly still. Even the knocker on the high door stopped swinging.

The magistrate's wax seals regarded him for a long moment. "An unusual bid," it said at last. "Time given freely is heavier than time taken. It would cover the boy's balance. It would leave you—" it consulted nothing, needing no ledger for this, "—short. Very short. Years, hollow man. Not decades. You understand what you bid?"

"I understand I'm bidding."

"And your oath?" The magistrate's voice curled with something that might, in a kinder creature, have been curiosity. "You bear the mark of a sworn blade. An oath to a god who no longer answers. Such oaths are collateral. If you spend your years elsewhere, the oath must be discharged. Broken. Do you understand *that*?"

Paloma thought of the silent god. Of forty years of kneeling to a silence that had never once answered, of the blade at his hip that held its edge only inside the valley, of the order he'd served like a lamp tended in an empty house. It had never been faith. It had been habit wearing faith's clothes. And habit, he understood now, standing in a hall of everything people had traded away, was just another thing sold cheaply, without ever knowing its price.

He drew the oath-blade.

It hummed faintly against his palm, valley-blessed, god-marked, the last proof of a covenant with a god who had left the line open and never spoken. He raised it, and spoke the words of severance, the ones Maren had taught him and prayed he'd never use:

"What was sworn to silence is ended by speech. I break it. I break it clean. Let the god hear me, wherever gods go — or let the silence keep the pieces."

He drove the point into the tombstone floor.

The blade shattered like ice. The sound it made was not metal — it was a bell, rung backward, a note unwinding itself — and the shock of it rolled through the hall of doors, and every door in every wall swung open at once, just an inch, just a breath, as if the whole court had gasped. Somewhere far above, impossibly, he thought he heard thunder. Or applause. Or nothing at all.

When the echo died, the magistrate stood. It had not stood in a long time; its joints crackled like a closing book.

"Bid entered," it said. "Bid weighed." The wax seals turned toward Wren, toward the ledger that had flown open between them, its pages streaming upward like smoke. "One life, freely offered, against one soul, unlawfully held. The court finds..." It paused, and for one moment the clerical emptiness of its voice thinned, and something almost like wonder came through. "...the debt paid in full."

The ledger slammed shut. From the dais, from the shelves, from the walls of stolen doors, a light rose — warm, gold, ordinary as lamplight — and poured into Wren.

The boy arched. His mouth opened. And Wren *screamed* — not in pain, but in language, in that old tongue older than kingdoms, words tumbling out of him like a river breaking a dam, and Paloma understood every one of them, understood that they were *names*, the boy speaking his own true name for the first time since it had been taken, claiming it aloud in a court where names were currency and this one was no longer for sale.

Then it was done. The light faded. Wren swayed, and Paloma caught him — caught him and felt it, felt the boy's heartbeat against his own hollow chest, fast and alive and *his*, and the ache of that, the unbearable sweetness of it, hit the emptiness in him like dawn hitting a dark room.

"Your account is settled," the magistrate said, settling back onto its throne of ledgers. Its voice had gone flat again, clerical, final. "The boy may go. The scribe may go — what remains of her. The years are entered and non-refundable." The wax seals fixed on Paloma one last time. "You will want to know how many you bought him with. We do not usually say. But you broke an oath in my court, freely, in good faith, and courtesy is owed." A pause, long as a held breath.

"Three years, Paloma Hartwell. Perhaps four, if you live gently."

The hall began to dim around them, doors closing one by one, the court folding itself away like a merchant striking camp. Paloma gathered Wren against his side and turned to Isbet — nameless, flickering, grinning at him through tears she perhaps no longer had a name to cry with.

"Come on," he said, and his voice finally shook, and he let it. "We're going home. All of us. Before I change my mind about living gently."

Behind them, on the dais, the case of black velvet remained open, the heart still beating, still waiting, patient as ever.

The magistrate looked down at it, and did not close the lid.

* * *

They climbed for hours, up stairs that had not existed on the way down, past gates that stood unlocked, past the place where Torv had fallen — and there, wedged in the rubble, they found a single stone hand, severed clean, its fingers curled open as if offering something. Isbet wrapped it in her cloak without a word. Wren walked between them the whole way, and somewhere on the ninth turning of the stair, in the dark, in common tongue, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge, the boy said his first word:

"Home?"

Paloma's chest ached, hollow and full at once.

"Yeah," he said. "Home."

Above them, at the top of the stair, a seam of gray light widened — dawn, real dawn, the valley's mist-line glowing silver where it waited to erase their wounds and remember their names.

Beyond it, Maren was watching the sky.
