## 10. A New Oath, Spoken Aloud

**Paloma**

The mist-line did not recognize him.

That was the first thing he understood as they climbed the last mile toward the valley, Wren's small hand in his, Isbet limping beside them under a borrowed cloak. For thirty years the mist had parted for him like a servant acknowledging its master — *here is a hollow man, here is one of ours* — and now it simply stood aside, indifferent, the way weather is indifferent to anyone.

He was no longer special here. He was only a man coming home.

The feeling that rose in him at that thought nearly buckled his knees. Grief and relief braided together into something too large for one chest, and he had to stop on the path, hand pressed flat against his sternum, because the heart they'd given back to him — no, the heart he'd *left* behind, all those years ago, still beating patiently on a magistrate's shelf — was hammering like something newly born.

"Paloma?" Wren tugged his fingers. The boy's voice was still thin, still learning common tongue one word at a time, but he had learned this one fast: Paloma's name, said like a question, said like an anchor.

"I'm well," Paloma said, and discovered it was true, and discovered that truth could hurt. "Just remembering how to walk."

Isbet snorted softly. Her hands were wrapped in linen where her name had burned itself out of her — a scribe without a name, which in the valley would make her either nothing or free. He suspected she intended to find out which.

They came down through the wool-pastures as the sun went copper, and the village saw them from a long way off. He watched the watchfires kindle, watched figures gather at the gate, and felt — *felt*, the way a man feels rain after years underground — the whole complicated weight of what waited there. Maren's face. The faces of people who had voted, three turns of the moon ago, to give a child away.

Wren squeezed his hand. Paloma breathed, and walked on.

* * *

**Maren**

She met them at the gate, and the first thing Maren noticed was that Paloma Hartwell was crying.

Not weeping — she doubted he knew how yet; the muscles of it were thirty years rusted — but tears standing in his eyes, spilling when he blinked, and him looking almost offended by them, like a man betrayed by his own plumbing. Behind him, a boy with storm-grey eyes. Behind them both, a woman whose name Maren could feel missing the way you feel a pulled tooth.

"You went out hollow," Maren said. Her voice cracked on it. She had buried too many oath-keepers' hopes to spend another easily, and still her hands were shaking. "You come back—"

"Whole." Paloma's voice was rough. "Mostly. Ask me again in a year."

"And Torv?"

The silence answered before any of them could. Isbet looked down. Wren's grip on Paloma's hand tightened until the knuckles went white.

"The gate," Paloma said finally. "It held because he held it. I'll tell it properly, when I can. Not tonight."

Maren nodded, because some griefs are doors you don't force. She stepped aside and let them through, and the village flowed around the returned ones like water around stones — curious, wary, hungry for the story. She heard the whispers already: *the Hartwell man, back from beneath the mountain; the mute boy speaking; the scribe with no name.*

Let them whisper. Let them learn. The valley had spent a generation believing silence was safety, and here came three living proofs that silence was only a debt accruing interest.

"Your god," she said quietly, walking beside Paloma up the lane. "The blade you carried. It's dead, isn't it. I can see it in how you hold your hip — reaching for a weight that isn't there."

"It died at the mist-line," Paloma said. "Or maybe it died long before, and I just couldn't feel the difference." A ghost of a smile. "Turns out I couldn't feel much of anything."

"And what do you swear by now?"

He stopped in the middle of the lane. Lanterns were being lit in windows; somewhere a mother called a child in to supper, and the boy beside him flinched at the ordinary sweetness of it, then didn't.

"That," Paloma said, "is what tomorrow is for."

* * *

**Wren**

The house was small and smelled of woodsmoke and old wool, and Wren decided, standing in the doorway with his boots muddy from the road, that it was the best place in any world.

He had words now. Not many. They sat in his mouth like smooth river stones, and he turned them over carefully, hoarding the good ones. *Home.* That was a good one. He hadn't known it applied anywhere until this week.

Under the mountain, he had been a ledger line. He remembered the court in flashes — cold gold, the magistrate's smile like a crack in porcelain, his own name spoken in a language that made his bones itch. He remembered the shelf where his soul had sat, small and bright as a coal, waiting to be spent. And he remembered Paloma standing in front of that shelf, holding his own heart in two hands like something breakable and precious, and choosing.

*Take it,* the magistrate had offered. *Walk away whole. The boy stays ours.*

And Paloma had set his heart down among the wares, and picked up the coal that was Wren, and said words in the old language — Wren's language, the one older than kingdoms — words that meant *this one is mine to keep, and I am his, and no court between heaven and hell will unmake that.*

Then he had broken his god-oath over his knee like dry bread, and offered the years instead. All the stolen ones. The decades of numbness he'd bought with his heart — the magistrate took them gleefully, and Wren had watched Paloma age ten years in ten breaths, hair going grey at the temples, lines carving themselves around his eyes, and Paloma had *smiled* while it happened, because he could feel it, every year leaving him like coins from a purse, and feeling anything at all was worth more to him than youth.

Wren didn't have the words for all that yet. But he had *home*, and he had *stay*, and tonight, sitting on the hearthstone while Isbet mended her sleeves and Paloma pretended not to cry again, he used both.

"Stay," Wren said. "Home. Stay home."

Paloma looked at him across the fire, and his face did about six things at once, and finally settled on something Wren decided was the truest expression he'd ever seen on anyone.

"Yes," Paloma said. "That's the plan, little wren. That's exactly the plan."

* * *

**Isbet**

She wrote the account by lantern light, because a scribe writes or she isn't a scribe, even a nameless one. Especially a nameless one — the record was all she had left of herself, and she meant to make it honest.

*Torv fell at the gate of the Undercourt on the twelfth turning,* she wrote. *He held it for nine hundred heartbeats against everything the court sent. His maker abandoned him; we did not. If any reader finds this: he chose. Remember that golems can choose.*

Her pen hovered. Then:

*I burned my name for our passage. I do not regret it. A name is a door the past walks through uninvited. I have built a new one out of better materials.*

Below the window, in the yard, she could see Paloma teaching Wren to whittle — badly, both of them, wood curls everywhere, laughter carrying up through the cold air. Her chest did the thing it had started doing somewhere on the road south, the thing she'd once catalogued in a margin as *unexplained warmth, cause unknown.* She knew the cause now. She'd simply never had a word for belonging that wasn't written in a contract.

Tomorrow there would be an oath. Maren had told her — the last oath-keeper, preparing to train a successor, or perhaps several. Paloma meant to speak it aloud, in front of everyone, and Isbet had been asked to witness, and she had said yes before he finished asking.

She dipped her pen one final time.

*The debt is paid. The price was everything, and it was cheap. We are going home, and home is not a place. Write that down. Someone should.*

* * *

**Paloma**

They gathered at dawn in the old oath-circle above the village, the one where the silent god's stone still stood, moss-eaten, its carved face worn blank by weather — fitting, Paloma thought, that the god had ended as anonymous as its servants.

Maren stood at the circle's edge. Isbet stood at his left, her bandaged hands folded. Wren stood at his right, gripping two fingers of his hand like a man holding onto a rail in high wind. Below, half the village had climbed up to watch, because a hollow man breaking one oath and making another was the most wonder the valley had seen in a generation — and wonders didn't hold here, but oaths did, and this was going to be an oath.

Paloma laid his dead blade on the god-stone. It weighed nothing now. It hadn't since the mist-line.

"I swore to a god who stopped listening," he said, and his voice carried clear in the cold morning. "I kept that oath through thirty empty years, and it kept nothing for me. I don't regret the keeping. I regret what it cost, and who I let it make me." He looked down at Wren, who was watching him with those storm-grey eyes, absolutely certain. "So this time I'm swearing to something that answers back."

He knelt — to the boy's level, not the stone's.

"Wren. Isbet. And whoever else ends up at our table, because there will be others; there always are." A ripple of something like laughter through the crowd. "I swear my hands to your keeping and your keeping to my hands. I swear to be felt, and to feel — the grief and the joy both, all of it, no more trading pieces of myself away. I swear that no court, no god, no bargain struck in darkness gets to decide what we are to each other. We decide. Every morning, we decide again."

His voice caught. He let it.

"This I swear aloud, where the wind can carry it and the valley can hold me to it. Witnessed—" he glanced at Isbet, who inclined her head, and at Maren, who was smiling through tears she wasn't bothering to hide, "—and witnessed, and witnessed. So it holds."

"So it holds," Maren said, and the crowd took it up, ragged and then strong: *so it holds, so it holds,* rolling down the hillside into the waking village.

Wren threw his arms around Paloma's neck, and Paloma held him, and felt all of it — the small fierce weight of him, the morning cold, Isbet's hand landing warm on his shoulder, the ache in knees that had aged ten years in a single bargain, the terrifying, unbearable, ordinary fullness of being a man with a heart.

Later — much later, when the crowd had gone and the bread was shared and Wren was asleep against Isbet's side — Paloma stood alone a moment at the circle's edge, looking east, where the mountains ran blue into the distance and the world beyond the valley went on with its courts and its bargains and its debts.

Somewhere out there, other ledgers held other names. Somewhere out there, other children were coal on a shelf, waiting for someone hollow enough to survive the road and stubborn enough to want more than survival.

He smiled, grey-templed and mortal-hearted, and turned back toward home.

"Well," he said to no one, to everyone, to the life ahead. "We'd best get started."
