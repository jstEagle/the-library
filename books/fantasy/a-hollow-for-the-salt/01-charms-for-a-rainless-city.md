## 1. Charms for a Rainless City

The rain never came to Greyharbor, and Sebastian had learned to be grateful for that in the way a man is grateful for a scar that has stopped aching — not because it healed, but because he'd grown used to the shape of it.

Twenty years in the city and it hadn't rained once. Not a proper rain. The clouds came up from the sea some mornings, grey and heavy with promise, and the whole city would tilt its face upward like beggars at a rich man's door, and then the clouds would break apart against the headlands and drift off to weep on someone else's fields. The farmers inland called Greyharbor cursed. The dockworkers called it honest. Sebastian called it nothing at all, because thinking about weather was the fastest way to think about everything else he wasn't allowed to think about.

He set out his stall in Saltlane Market an hour after dawn, as he did every morning, laying out his wares on a strip of faded blue cloth: luck-knots braided from tarred cord, each one knotted seven times with a word whispered into the middle knot; weather-bones, which were chicken bones boiled clean and painted with spiral patterns that meant absolutely nothing; salt-charms in little leather pouches; and his one indulgence, a single jar of honey from the hill farms, sold by the spoonful to sweeten the tea of anyone who could afford sweetness.

None of it worked. That was the joke of it, the private joke he'd been carrying so long it had worn smooth as a river stone. None of it worked, because magic didn't work in Greyharbor — or rather, *he* didn't work in Greyharbor, because using the gift here would be like striking a match inside a powder store. The city was full of crown wardens and informers and bored soldiers looking for anything unusual, and a storm-caller's work was nothing if not unusual. So he sold knots and bones and pouches of salt, and he whispered words into knots that were only words, and the dockworkers bought them because hope was cheaper than fish and lasted longer on the tongue.

"Morning, Okonkwo." Brannick, the harbor master's clerk, a man built like a bollard with a face like wet rope. He stopped at the stall every day and bought nothing every day, but he liked to talk, and Sebastian had learned that a man who talks at you daily will also talk *about* you to others, and what he says about you keeps you safe. "Cold one."

"It's Greyharbor," Sebastian said. "They're all cold ones."

"Heard they're posting something on the Customs House today. Big proclamation, criers and all. Crown business." Brannick picked up a luck-knot, turned it over, put it down. "You registered your trade license this year?"

"Paid the fee in spring."

"Good man." He moved along, and Sebastian watched him go and felt the small cold stone in his gut that had lived there for twenty years and had lately begun, very slightly, to warm.

Crown business. There was always crown business. But criers, and proclamations posted where the whole market could see them — that was new. That was the kind of new that made old men remember old times, and old times in Greyharbor included burnings.

He shook it off and turned to his first customer of the day, a stevedore named Olla with forearms like hams and a permanent squint from twenty years of salt glare.

"The big knot," she said. "For my boy's first crossing. He ships out on the *Merrow's Tithe* tomorrow."

Sebastian took down the largest luck-knot, the one with the double-braided core. He held it out and let her take it, and when her fingers closed around the cord he said the words he always said, the ones that cost him nothing because they were only words: "Fair winds find you. Fair winds keep you."

Olla paid him three coppers and a smile, and walked away holding the knot like it was her son's hand.

*Fair winds.* If only she knew. If only any of them knew that the man selling them fair winds had once pulled a gale out of a clear sky over the Bay of Sorrows and drowned a fleet with it, and had spent every day since wishing he'd drowned too. That the storm was still in him. That it never left. It sat coiled under his skin like a sleeping thing, like a second heart made of thunder, and every morning he woke and pressed it down, and every night he lay awake listening to it turn over in its sleep.

Some days it hummed. On days like today — dry, bright, the air smelling of tar and frying fish — it hummed like a struck string, and he had to keep his hands busy so they wouldn't do what hands wanted to do when the storm sang, which was open, and reach, and call.

So he kept them busy. He straightened bones. He re-knotted cords. He counted his coppers twice.

* * *

Midmorning brought the crowd, or what passed for one in Saltlane: dockworkers between shifts, fishwives arguing over the morning catch, two wardens in grey-and-white livery walking their slow circuit past the stalls without stopping, because Sebastian's stall was a fixture, as boring and reliable as a fence post, and nobody watched fence posts.

A girl stopped at the edge of his cloth.

She was small — eight, nine years old, hard to say with street children, who aged sideways instead of up. Dark skin gone grey with harbor dust, hair cropped short and uneven, probably by herself with a stolen knife. She wore a coat three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled six times, and she stood looking at his wares with the particular stillness of a child doing arithmetic: what can I carry, how fast can I run, where are the exits.

Sebastian knew that arithmetic. He'd done it himself, once, in another country, in another life.

She reached for a weather-bone, snatched it, and ran.

She was fast. She was genuinely fast, cutting through the fishwives' skirts and under a cart axle, and she'd have made it if she hadn't looked back. They always looked back. It was the looking back that told you everything — a professional thief never looks back, because a professional thief doesn't care whether the victim saw. This child looked back the way you look back at a house you're leaving.

Sebastian didn't shout. He didn't call for the wardens, who were conveniently forty feet away and would have been delighted to beat a street thief within an inch of her life as a public service. He simply watched her go, and then he noticed what she'd taken.

Not a bone. Not a knot.

The little salt-charm, the plain one, the cheapest thing on his cloth — except that it wasn't cheap, not really, because it was the one charm on the table he'd made honestly. Real salt from a real storm, gathered off his own windowsill the one morning in twenty years when the sea had thrown spray high enough to reach the third floor. He'd charged nothing for it and never sold it, because selling it would have been like selling a tooth.

And the child had known. Children always knew. They couldn't have said how they knew, but they could tell a real thing from a false one the way dogs could tell a storm coming, and this half-starved scrap of a girl had walked past thirty worthless charms and taken the only true one.

Sebastian sat down slowly on his stool.

The market went on around him. Olla's son shipped out tomorrow. Brannick would come back at closing time to talk about the proclamation. The wardens made their circuit. The sun climbed over a sky that would not, could not, give them rain.

He thought: *I should feel robbed.*

He thought: *I haven't given a true thing to anyone in twenty years.*

* * *

Closing time came down slow, the way it always did, the market emptying by degrees until there was only Sebastian folding his blue cloth and Brannick ambling up for his evening talk.

"That proclamation," Brannick said, without preamble. He was holding his hat, which he never did unless something was wrong. Hat in hands meant bad news delivered gently. "Posted at noon. You see it?"

"I don't read much," Sebastian said, which was a lie so old it had family.

"Gifted Purge, they're calling it. Registration of all persons carrying the gift, voluntary-like, for their protection." Brannick turned his hat in his hands. "My cousin remembers the last purge. Fifty years back. Says the registration lists got used for something else after."

"For what?"

"For finding people." Brannick put his hat on. "Anyway. Cold one today."

He shuffled off, and Sebastian stood in the emptying market with his folded cloth under his arm and the storm humming under his skin, louder now, as if it had heard the word *purge* and found it funny.

That night he climbed to his room above the chandler's shop and barred the door and lit no lamp, and stood at the window looking out at the black harbor water, and let himself do the thing he almost never did: he opened the door on the storm, just a crack, just enough to feel it.

It surged against the crack like a dog against a gate. Twenty years of pressing it down and it was still there, still vast, still *his*, still singing the old songs in the old tongue. He breathed and let a thread of it run through him, out through his fingertips, and far across the harbor the water shivered. Just shivered. A ring of ripples where no wind touched, seen by no one, costing him nothing but the ache of wanting more.

He closed the door on it. He always closed the door.

* * *

Three mornings later, the girl came back.

She didn't come to the stall. She came to the mouth of Saltlane and stood there in the early light, half-hidden behind a stack of crab pots, watching him with the fixed attention of a cat watching a bird it has decided not to pounce on yet. She had the salt-charm on a cord around her neck. He could see it when the light caught it, a small white gleam against her throat.

She'd kept it. She was wearing it.

Sebastian sold a knot to a rope-maker, argued cheerfully with a fishwife about the price of dried cod, and did not look at the crab pots. When he finally did glance over, casual as breathing, the girl was closer — at the corner of the chandler's shop now, close enough that he could see she was thinner than she'd seemed, that there was a healing cut above her eyebrow, that her eyes were the color of deep water and just as unreadable.

"You want something?" he asked the air near her, not quite looking at her. "Or are you shopping?"

Silence. Then, from behind the crab pots, in a voice like gravel poured through a small sieve: "It works."

Sebastian's hands went still on the cord he was braiding.

"What works?"

"The charm." She stepped fully into the lane, arms crossed, chin up, daring him to make it strange. "The salt one. I slept with it and I didn't dream the drowning dream. First time in a month." Her eyes narrowed. "It works, and none of your other stuff does, and I want to know why."

There it was. The arithmetic again, but different now — not *what can I steal* but *what can I get away with asking*. And underneath it, barely visible, the thing children hide worst of all: *please don't send me away.*

Sebastian looked at the child for a long moment. At the coat with its six-fold sleeves. At the charm glinting at her throat. At the cut above her eye that somebody should have tended and nobody had.

"You're right," he said quietly. "Most of it doesn't work. It's cord and bone and talk." He held her gaze. "The salt one worked because salt remembers storms. That's all the magic there is in it. That's all the magic there is anywhere, really. Remembering."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "I'm Wren," she said, as if concluding a negotiation.

"Wren."

"You're the charm-seller."

"I'm the charm-seller."

"Okonkwo." She pronounced it carefully, having clearly rehearsed. "Brannick says you're from across the sea. Brannick says you never talk about before. Brannick talks too much."

"Brannick does talk too much." Sebastian found, to his mild alarm, that his face was doing something it hadn't done in months. It took him a moment to identify the expression as a smile. "Shouldn't you be stealing breakfast somewhere?"

"Already did." She patted her coat, where a roll-shaped bulge suggested the success of the operation. Then she hesitated, and the hesitation cracked her composure down the middle, and for one unguarded moment she was just a small, tired, hungry child standing in a cold lane. "The wardens took Tammas yesterday. Off the *Saltwife*. He's a net-mender. He never hurt anyone." Her voice dropped. "They said he was gifted. They took him to the Customs House and he didn't come back and everyone's saying the proclamation means they'll take more, and my friend Sef says people who are different should hide, and I thought—" She stopped. Restarted. "I wanted to tell someone who isn't a kid. That's all."

She turned and walked off down Saltlane before he could answer, small and quick and gone, the salt-charm flashing white at her throat like a tiny distant lightning strike.

Sebastian stood in his lane with his half-braided cord hanging forgotten between his hands.

*Tammas the net-mender. Taken. The proclamation. The purge.*

The storm under his skin had gone very quiet, the way it went quiet before lightning.

He looked down at the charm table, at the rows of beautiful useless things, at twenty years of careful anonymity laid out on a strip of faded blue cloth. He thought about registration lists used for finding people. He thought about a child wearing true salt against her throat in a city that was about to start hunting anyone the salt might recognize.

He thought: *walk away. Fold the cloth. You've done it before. You know the roads.*

He thought: *she looked back.*

That evening, after dark, Sebastian did something he had not done in twenty years of careful, patient, invisible life.

He began to prepare.
