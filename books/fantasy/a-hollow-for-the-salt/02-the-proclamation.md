## 2. The Proclamation

The bells woke the city before the criers did.

Sebastian heard them from the loft above the charm-shop — six bells instead of the usual five, and a seventh struck flat and urgent, the way you'd ring for fire or plague. They lay in the dark listening to it roll across Greyharbor's rooftops, down through the tannery rows and out along the seawall, and they thought, with the particular calm of a person who has spent twenty years waiting for one specific sound: *there it is.*

Then they got up and put the kettle on.

That was the trick of dread. You didn't fight it at first contact. You made tea. You fed the coals in the little stove. You watched the grey light come up over the harbor and counted the things that still needed doing before the world changed shape, because a list was something your hands could hold when nothing else could.

The kettle sang. Sebastian poured, and did not drink, and listened to the street below begin to fill.

* * *

By midmorning the criers were out.

Sebastian stood in the doorway of Okonkwo's Charms & Small Fortunes — the ampersand had cost them three extra coppers from the sign-painter and had been worth every one, because it made the shop look like a thing that had always existed, a thing with no history worth investigating — and watched a man in crown livery climb the fountain steps in Saltmarket Square with a scroll in his fist.

"By order of His Majesty Aldous the Third," the crier read, voice pitched to carry off the water, "and by counsel of Lord Commander Aldery, Warden of the Realm's Purity: be it known that a Great Purging is declared against the Gifted corruption."

The square went quiet in the way markets do — not silent, never silent, but the noise changing texture, haggling dropping to murmurs, a hundred conversations suddenly about the same thing.

"All persons possessing the Gifted taint shall present themselves for registration within seven days, at any ward-house, under penalty of law. Registration ensures protection. Concealment is treason. Harborers of the concealed are traitors also, and shall share their fate." The scroll crackled as he turned it. "Let no man say he was not warned. The realm will be made clean."

A woman near the fish stalls crossed herself with an old gesture, pre-Church, thumb to sternum, the kind you only saw in people old enough to remember why it was forbidden. A dockhand spat. Two boys chased each other through the crowd shouting *gifted, gifted* in the bright merciless way of children who have not yet learned which words draw blood.

And everywhere, underneath it all, the sound Sebastian knew best: people glancing sideways at their neighbors. Measuring them. Asking themselves the question the proclamation wanted asked — *who do I know? Who have I always half-suspected?*

Twenty years, Sebastian thought. Twenty years of luck-knots and weather-bones, of being a harmless eccentric with a talent for knots and a shop that smelled of salt and hemp, and it would take seven days for some neighbor to remember the time the rain had followed them home, or the night the lightning had come down the chimney smoke and nobody could explain it.

They went back inside and closed the door, and stood with their hand flat against the wood, feeling their own pulse.

Under their skin, coiled where it had coiled for two decades, the storm stirred. It always stirred when they were afraid. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it: the gift answered fear like a dog answers a whistle. Sebastian breathed slow, four counts in, four counts out, until the pressure behind their breastbone settled back into its long sleep.

*Not yet,* they told it. *Not yet.*

* * *

The first customer of the day was Old Sefa, who came for her monthly luck-knot and stayed to talk, which meant she came to talk and bought the knot as cover.

"They say it's because of the fisherman," she said, settling onto the stool by the counter that had been hers for eleven years. "Out on the Point. The one whose nets always came up full."

"Tomas Ilves."

"That's him. They say his boat was struck by clear-sky lightning twice in one season, and the second time it didn't burn so much as — " she waggled her fingers, " — *bend*. Like the lightning was sorry about it."

Sebastian tied off a loop of red cord, steady-handed. "Lightning does what lightning does."

"Mm." Sefa looked at them over her spectacles with the flat frankness of the very old. "And I suppose you'll tell me weather-bones are just chicken bones, too."

"I've never claimed they were anything but chicken bones."

"No. You never claim anything. That's what I like about you." She took her knot, pressed a coin into the counter's worn groove, and paused at the door. "Whatever this purge is, charm-seller — it isn't about lightning bending. Aldery doesn't raise seven bells for weather."

She left. Sebastian sat with the red cord in their hands and let the truth of it settle cold into their stomach, because Sefa was right, and Sebastian had known she was right since the bells.

Seven bells. A Warden of the Realm's Purity. *Registration ensures protection.* No crown in living memory had cared enough about stray gifts to build machinery for finding them. This wasn't superstition or politics. Someone wanted the Gifted found, counted, and gathered — and gathered things get shipped somewhere.

They had spent twenty years not asking where.

* * *

Night came early in autumn, and with it, work.

Sebastian waited until the lamplighter passed, then barred the door, shuttered the windows, and went down into the cellar with a candle and a shovel.

Beneath the floor stones, beneath the false bottom of the coal bin, wrapped in oilcloth and wax: a sea chest. They hadn't opened it since the day they'd buried it, nine years ago, when a new ward-reeve had started asking friendly questions about the previous tenant. Inside lay everything they had been before Greyharbor.

They knelt over it for a long moment, hands on the lid, and let themselves feel the weight of what tonight was. Not a decision — the decision had been made years ago, quietly, the way you keep a boat patched against a storm you hope never comes. Tonight was only the storm arriving.

They opened the chest.

Salt-crusted leather. A coat that had once been grey and was now the color of old iron. Beneath it, wrapped separately because it deserved its own wrapping: a coil of braided cable, black and silver, humming faintly against Sebastian's palms even now, even after all these years, like a struck bell too far away to hear properly. Conductor's line. There were perhaps four such coils left in the world, and Sebastian had stolen theirs from a burning tower while the council that had made it screamed at each other about whether they were a weapon or a child.

Both, the council had finally decided. Both, and therefore too dangerous, and therefore exile — gentle exile, paid exile, a shop in a magic-free city and a stipend that arrived through intermediaries and stopped without explanation whenever Sebastian asked questions.

*Keep them far from the coast,* the council's last letter had said, in the hand of someone Sebastian had loved. *Keep them small. If they ever learn what sleeps below Karstholm—*

The letter ended there. It had always ended there. Sebastian had spent twenty years telling themselves the rest didn't matter.

Tonight, reading it again by candlelight in a cellar that smelled of coal and old salt, they admitted what they had always known: it mattered. Everything about the purge's timing mattered. Somewhere under the sea, something was waking up, and crowns did not build purges for things that slept.

Sebastian folded the letter away and began, methodically, to burn their life.

Not literally — not yet. But piece by piece, through the small hours, they dismantled twenty years of careful anonymity. The ledger of sales went into the stove, page by page: every name, every address, every pattern a clever inquisitor might read backward into a map of who trusted the charm-seller and why. The letters from Sefa. The drawings children had traded for luck-knots. The receipt from the sign-painter, kept all these years out of sentiment.

The false wall behind the shelving came down next. Behind it: coin, sewn into belts. Papers in three names, none of them true, all of them good. A knife that was more than a knife, though Sebastian had never needed to learn exactly how. And at the very bottom, wrapped in a child's blanket because there had been nothing else to wrap it in —

They stopped. Their hands hovered over the bundle and shook, just slightly, and the candle flame leaned toward them as if drawn, and outside, far off over the harbor, thunder muttered in a sky that had been clear all day.

*Steady.* They breathed. Four counts. The flame straightened.

Inside the blanket lay a single object: a small brass compass whose needle pointed not north but down, and slightly east, toward a place in the sea that charts marked only as deep water. It had been their father's. It had been pointing at Karstholm since before anyone alive had spoken that name aloud in Sebastian's hearing.

They had never once followed it. Twenty years of discipline, distilled to a needle they refused to read.

Sebastian wrapped it away again, and put it in the bag anyway, because exile had taught them at least one honest lesson: you don't get to choose what you carry. You only choose whether you run or stand.

* * *

Dawn found them at the counter, packing the last of it — the go-bag by the door, the shop stripped to something anonymous, the stove cold and full of ash that had been a history. They had planned the route weeks ago, in the abstract way of a person hoping to never use it: south along the coast road, then inland, then gone. Alone. One set of footprints leaves fewer stories.

Which was when they remembered the boy.

Wren. The thief with the river-grey eyes who had lifted a storm-charm off the display table eight days ago, and whom Sebastian had watched dart into the alley and deliberately not chased. The child had come back twice since — once to leave a bent copper on the counter like payment, once just to look in the window, quick as a bird at a feeder. Street kid. No family anyone knew. Sleeping, if the harbor gossip was right, in the loft of the rope-walk or the hollow under Custom House stairs.

A child alone in a city where *concealment is treason* and *harborers share their fate*, in a week when neighbors would be measuring neighbors, when boys were already chanting *gifted* in the streets for sport.

Sebastian stood in their emptying shop with the go-bag in one hand and the door key in the other, and understood that the plan — the clean, solitary, twenty-years-in-the-making plan — had just developed a hole in it shaped like a skinny twelve-year-old.

"You're not responsible for her," they said aloud, to the empty room. Their voice sounded strange after a night of silence. "You gave her a charm. That was the whole of it."

The room, being empty, declined to argue.

But the bells rang six, and the criers would be out again, and somewhere out there a child who stole charms because charms were the closest thing to magic the city allowed was about to learn what a Great Purging was.

Sebastian locked the door, pocketed the key, and turned toward Saltmarket — and stopped.

Across the street, leaning against the fishmonger's post with the unhurried patience of someone being paid by the hour, stood a woman in plain travel clothes watching the shop. She wasn't very good at it. Her eyes tracked Sebastian's reflection in the window glass, and her right hand rested inside her coat in the manner of a person keeping a grip on paper rather than steel — a warrant, maybe, or a list.

An inquisitor's watcher, or something wearing one's patience. Already. On the first morning.

Sebastian smiled at the reflection, lifted a hand in a small friendly wave, and walked — unhurried, harmless, a charm-seller out for morning bread — in the opposite direction from the shop, toward the rope-walk, toward the hollow under Custom House stairs, toward the one loose end in their life that had eyes and a name.

Behind them, faint under the market noise, thunder rolled again over a clear sea.

The past, refusing to stay buried. Fine. Sebastian had spent twenty years learning to dig.
