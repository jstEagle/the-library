## 3. What the Tide Brought In

Wren had three rules for surviving Greyharbor, and they'd learned all of them the hard way, which was the only way rules ever stuck.

One: never sleep in the same place twice in a row. Two: never let anyone see how much food you have. Three: when the soldiers come, be somewhere else.

Wren had been following rule three since before they could remember why it mattered. It lived in their body now, deeper than thought. So when the bells of the customs house started ringing wrong — not the lazy noon clang but a fast, ugly triple-strike that meant *trouble on the docks* — Wren's feet were moving before their brain caught up.

They were crouched in the lee of a fish-crate stack behind Brannigan's Smokehouse, working through the morning's take: half a heel of bread gone hard as slate, a pickle of dubious origin, and one perfect pear that Wren was saving for reasons they couldn't explain even to themselves. The pear had been sitting in their coat pocket for two days. Every time they thought about eating it, something in their chest said *not yet*, and Wren had learned to trust that voice. It was the same voice that told them which pockets to lift and which to leave, which doorways were watched and which were safe. The other kids called it Wren's luck. Wren privately suspected it was something else, but suspicion was a luxury, and luck was free.

The bell rang again. Triple-strike. Then a fourth ring, which had never happened before, and which made the hair on Wren's arms stand up like a cat's.

They climbed the crate stack the way they'd climbed it a hundred times, handholds worn smooth by their own palms, and looked out over the fish market.

Soldiers.

Not the usual two bored customs men with their hands resting on their belts, collecting bribes and ignoring everything smaller than a smuggling barge. These were crown soldiers, twenty of them at least, in grey-and-white livery Wren had only ever seen on proclamations and the occasional grandee passing through toward the palace district. They moved in a line across the market square, and the line wasn't searching. It was herding.

At the center of it, a man Wren knew.

Old Tam. Everyone knew Old Tam. He fished the outer shoals alone because no crew would have him — he talked to his nets, they said, and the nets answered, and once he'd pulled in a catch so impossible that the harbormaster had made him throw half of it back out of pure superstition. He was a fixture of the docks the way the lighthouse was a fixture: strange, salted, permanent.

He wasn't permanent now. Two soldiers had him by the arms and a third had a hand twisted in his collar, and Tam wasn't fighting, which was the worst part. He was looking down at the cobbles like he'd already read the end of this story and didn't want to watch the middle.

A woman in a grey coat stood apart from the soldiers. She had a ledger under one arm and a look on her face that Wren had seen before, on the faces of people counting money that wasn't theirs. She said something. One of the soldiers pulled something from inside Tam's shirt — a cord, a pendant, some small thing that caught the light — and held it up, and the woman nodded like a merchant confirming weight.

Then she took out a knife and cut the pendant cord herself, and dropped both pieces into an iron box another soldier carried, and Tam made a sound. Small. Like a man watching his own name get erased.

"Marked," someone whispered near Wren's crates. A fishwife, pressed back against the smokehouse wall with her basket clutched to her chest. "They're saying there's a list. Registration first, they said. Said it gentle-like, like it was a kindness."

"What happens after registration?" asked the boy who helped her carry baskets.

She didn't answer. That was answer enough.

Wren should have left then. Rule three. Be somewhere else. Their legs were already coiled for it, the old animal knowledge humming.

But Tam looked up. Just for a second, as they turned him toward the customs house — just long enough for his eyes to sweep the market the way a drowning man sweeps a shoreline. And Wren, idiot, curse-struck, moved. Shifted their weight. Made a small sound, or maybe just breathed too loud, or maybe did nothing at all except exist in the one direction Tam was looking, wearing the face of a child who cared.

Tam saw them. His eyes went wide, and then — this was the part Wren would replay for the rest of their life, turning it over like a stolen coin — he shook his head. Slowly. Once. *Don't.* His mouth shaped a word without any sound in it, and Wren read it off his lips the way they'd learned to read lips through smokehouse windows:

*Run.*

So Wren ran.

* * *

They ran the way water runs, finding every gap, every drain, every crooked seam between buildings that grown men had forgotten existed. Through the tannery yard where the smell covered everything including fear. Over the wall behind the cooperage by way of the broken stair nobody had fixed in nine years. Down through Salt Row, where the houses leaned together overhead like conspirators, and out into the warren of courts and passages behind the chandlery streets.

Only when they reached the low arch beneath the aqueduct — the one spot in Greyharbor where you could hear your own heartbeat instead of the city — did Wren stop and let the shaking start.

It started in their hands and worked upward. They sat down against the wet stone and put their head between their knees and breathed the way the breathing went when nobody was watching: ragged, ugly, honest.

Because here was the thing Wren hadn't told anyone, the thing they kept below the waterline of themselves like ballast:

The iron box. The pendants. The list.

Wren had a pendant too. Had carried it since before memory, found knotted into the hem of the blanket they'd been abandoned in — a little thing of sea-glass and wire, warm to the touch in a way that made no sense, warmer on stormy days. They wore it hidden, always hidden, taped flat against their ribs under their shirt with strips of linen renewed monthly, a ritual performed in private with the solemnity of other children's prayers.

And last night — last night, of all nights — the pendant had been warm. Warmer than Wren could ever remember. Warm enough that they'd woken at midnight with their chest glowing like a banked coal, and lain in the dark of the cooperage loft listening to a wind that smelled of lightning though the sky stayed clear, and felt something in the world turning over, slow and vast, like something enormous rolling in its sleep beneath the harbor floor.

The proclamation had come at dawn. Criers on every corner. *By order of the Crown: all persons bearing the gift shall present themselves for registration.*

And now soldiers, and an iron box, and Old Tam's face doing that terrible nothing.

Wren understood, in the wordless way they understood weather coming, that the list had a shape. That the shape was getting filled in, name by name, pendant by pendant. And that somewhere on that list, in ink not yet dry, there was room for a street kid with sea-glass against their ribs.

They needed help. Not the kind the other street kids could give — shared corners, split takings, a lookout's whistle. Real help. Adult-shaped help. The kind with walls and locks and the power to stand between a child and a line of soldiers.

There was exactly one person in Greyharbor who might give it.

* * *

The charm stall sat in its usual place at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, and Sebastian Okonkwo was mending a luck-knot with the patient fingers of someone who had done ten thousand small tasks to avoid ten thousand large thoughts.

Wren had been stealing from Sebastian for eight months.

That was how it had started, anyway. First the honey-bread, then the copper bit from the till, then a whole string of small crimes committed with the specific boldness of a kid testing whether the world had teeth. Most adults in Greyharbor had teeth. The fishwives boxed ears. The chandlers set dogs. The night-watch clipped you over the head and asked questions after.

Sebastian had watched Wren pocket the honey-bread with an expression of mild interest, met their eyes across the stall, and slid the rest of the loaf to the edge of the counter.

"For the road," they'd said. "You look fast. Fast people need feeding."

Nobody gave Wren things. Things given came with hooks in them. But the bread had no hook, and neither had the second loaf, nor the coin slipped into Wren's pocket "by accident" when they weren't quick enough to steal it, nor the afternoon Sebastian had spent teaching them knots — real knots, sailor's knots, *storm-knots*, Sebastian had called them with a strange smile — while Wren pretended not to notice that the lesson was also a meal, because there was always food where Sebastian was, appearing out of nowhere like weather.

Wren had a theory about Sebastian. Everybody in the lanes had theories. Mine-owner's bastard, disgraced priest, foreign spy, runaway noble. Wren's theory was simpler and stranger: that Sebastian was hiding. Not from debt or scandal, but from something bigger, the way a whale hides from the whole ocean. You could see it sometimes, in the pauses. Sebastian would go still — mid-sentence, mid-knot — with a look on their face like a person listening to a sound no one else could hear. On those days the air around the stall felt thick, charged, the way it did before summer storms, and Wren's pendant would warm against their ribs until they had to press a hand flat over it.

Wren had never said any of this aloud. But they'd decided, somewhere in month four of the honey-bread economy, that if the world ever came for Wren Kestrel-who-had-no-other-name, they were running to the charm stall.

The world had come. Wren ran.

They came around the corner at a dead sprint, ducked under the stall's awning, and grabbed Sebastian's sleeve with both hands — the first time they had ever touched the charm-seller on purpose, and it felt like grabbing a mooring post in a flood.

"They're taking people," Wren said. All the words they'd planned on the run — the bell, the soldiers, the grey coat woman, the iron box — collapsed into that. "Off the docks. Off the *docks*, Sebastian. Old Tam. They took Old Tam, and he looked at me and said run, and I ran, and there's a box, they're putting the pendants in a box—"

"Wren." Sebastian's hands came up and closed over Wren's wrists, and the strangest thing happened: the shaking stopped. Not gradually. All at once, like a door closing on a windy room. Sebastian's grip was warm and absolutely steady, and underneath the smell of rope and charcoal dust that always hung about the stall, Wren caught that storm-smell again, sharp and green and electric. "Breathe. Slower. Now tell me what the soldiers looked like."

"Crown livery. Grey and white. Twenty, maybe more, and a woman with a ledger, and they cut Tam's pendant off and put it in an iron box, and Tam didn't fight, Sebastian, Tam *never* fights anyone, he talks to his nets—"

Something crossed Sebastian's face. Wren had spent eight months learning that face the way they'd learned the lanes, and what crossed it now was grief — old grief, deep-water grief — and under the grief, something waking up. Something that had been asleep a long time and was very tired of it.

"The proclamation," Sebastian said quietly. "This morning."

"You heard it too?"

"I heard it." Sebastian looked past Wren, down Cooper's Lane, where the ordinary sounds of the city went on — cart wheels, gull cries, a knife-grinder's wheel — all of it suddenly sounding thin, like music played in an empty house. When Sebastian spoke again, each word came out placed carefully, like stones across a flooded stream. "Wren. Listen to me. Where do you sleep?"

"The cooperage. Off Saltings Lane. Why—"

"Don't go back there."

"It's my stuff, I've got—"

"*Don't go back there.*" Not loud. But the air pressure changed. Wren felt it in their ears, a faint pop, and every gull within earshot went up off the rooftops at once, screaming, as if the sky itself had flinched. Sebastian closed their eyes for a moment, mastering something, and when they opened them again they were calm in a way that frightened Wren more than the soldiers had. "If they have a list, they'll work outward from the marked ones. Tam talked to his nets. Who else is on the docks who talks to things? Who else has warmth in their pockets?" A pause, delicate as a blade. "Who else carries sea-glass against their ribs?"

The world tilted. Wren stepped back and their hip hit the stall counter and they gripped it, staring. "How did you—"

"I've watched you for eight months, little wren. I know exactly what you carry." Sebastian came around the counter, and up close, without the stall between them, Wren saw for the first time how tall they actually were, and how tired, and how much like a drawn bow. "You have a choice, and I won't make it for you. You can run — out the east gate, blend with the carter traffic, take the coast road south. You're quick enough. You might make it."

"And the other choice?"

Sebastian looked at them for a long moment. Whatever they saw in Wren's face seemed to settle something.

"The other choice," they said, "is that you stay near me, and we find out together what the crown actually wants with its gifted. Because I promise you, Wren, it isn't registration. Nobody builds iron boxes for paperwork."

Down the lane, a bell began ringing again. Triple-strike. Then four. Then five, six, seven — every bell in Greyharbor catching it and passing it along, the whole city turning into one great alarm, and beneath the ringing, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving in formation. Moving toward Cooper's Lane.

Sebastian's hand closed around Wren's shoulder, and it was steady as bedrock, and warm as the sea-glass, and Wren — who had three rules, whose third rule was *when the soldiers come, be somewhere else* — broke it forever.

"I'm staying," they said.

Sebastian smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile; there was lightning in it somewhere, banked and waiting. "Then stay close," they said, "and whatever happens next — don't be afraid of me."

Behind the stall, in the shadowed doorway of the shuttered chapel that everyone said had been empty for thirty years, a figure in a grey coat unfolded from the dark, and a woman's voice said:

"That depends entirely on what happens next, charm-seller. Inquisitor Maren Vale. You're both under observation — and I suggest you decide, quickly, whether that's a problem for you."
