## 4. Maren Vale, Inquisitor

The bell rang at dawn, and Maren Vale was already awake.

She had been awake for most of the night, in fact, lying on the narrow cot in her quarters above the Inquisition house, listening to Greyharbor breathe itself toward morning — the groan of ships against pilings, the first carts on cobblestones, a gull screaming somewhere like it had been personally wronged by the sunrise. She knew every sound this city made. She had spent eleven years learning them, and eleven years learning which of those sounds meant someone was about to have the worst day of their life.

The bell meant that day had come for a great many someones.

She dressed in the dark. Black wool, black boots, the grey sash of her office knotted at the waist with the particular precision of a woman who had once been reprimanded for a crooked knot and had never allowed herself a crooked knot again. The seal went into the inner pocket, over her heart, where it sat like a second heartbeat: cold iron, the crown's open eye stamped into its face. She touched it once, the way she always did, and told herself it meant the same thing it had always meant.

It means I am the law. It means the law is me.

It had been easier to believe when she was twenty-six.

* * *

By the time she reached Saltmarket Square, the criers were already hoarse. She heard the tail end of the proclamation bouncing off the shuttered fronts of the fishmongers' stalls:

"—by order of His Majesty's Grace, all persons harboring the Gifted taint shall present themselves for registration within seven days. Shelterers will be charged as accomplices. The Gifted will be remanded to the crown's custody for assessment and—"

Assessment. That word again. Maren had read the writs until the ink swam. Assessment, custody, processing. Nowhere in any of them did it say where the Gifted would be taken after processing, or what happened to them there. She had asked. She had been told, very politely, by a clerk with soft hands and no eyes worth trusting, that such information was above her station.

Maren Vale had broken smuggler rings, root and branch. She had pulled three corrupt harbor magistrates out of their own pockets. She had once spent four days without sleep tracking a child-killer through the drainage warrens under Tanner's Row, and when she found him she had not raised her voice even once. She was not a woman who frightened easily, and she was not a woman who let things go.

But she had let this go. Filed it under *above my station*, the way you file a splinter too deep to dig out, and told herself the body would learn to ignore it.

The square was filling anyway. Not with the Gifted — the Gifted, if they had any sense, were already running — but with the curious, the hungry, the ones who liked to watch. Greyharbor had gone nine years without rain and eleven years without magic, ever since the crown's edicts had scrubbed the last registered practitioners out of the city rolls, and the people here had developed an appetite for spectacle the way starved bodies develop an appetite for anything.

"First sweep begins now," Maren said, and forty soldiers moved because they were good soldiers and she was good at making them move.

* * *

The first sweep was Fisher's Lane, and Fisher's Lane was a mistake before it began.

Not hers. The orders came down from Lord Aldery's office, names attached, addresses attached, and one of the names was Odo Brannigan, net-mender, sixty-one years old, who had fished the Greywater estuary since before Maren could walk. The notation beside his name said: *suspected weather-sense; unregistered; neighbor report.*

A neighbor report. Someone on Fisher's Lane had looked at an old man who could feel a storm coming in his knees and decided that was worth a soldier's boot on his door.

Maren led the column herself because if it was going to be ugly, she wanted to be the ugliest thing on the street. That was the job. You stood between the machinery and the people it ground, not to stop the grinding — she had long since stopped pretending she could stop anything — but so that the grinding happened in front of someone who would remember doing it right.

Odo Brannigan did not run. That was the worst part. When the door came down — Maren had ordered it knocked first, politely, twice, and then not politely — he was sitting at his table with a bowl of barley porridge going cold in front of him, and he looked up at the six soldiers filling his kitchen and said, "Took you long enough."

"You knew we'd come," Maren said.

"I've known since the criers started." He folded his big cracked hands on the table, careful, deliberate, a man showing he was no threat. "My girl's in Vell. Married a cooper. If you write to her, tell her it was quick." He paused. "Tell her it didn't hurt."

"We're not—" Maren stopped. She had been about to say *we're not butchers*, and she had realized, standing in this old man's kitchen with the porridge steam still rising, that she did not know that to be true anymore. "You'll be assessed," she said instead. "Processed. That's all I can tell you."

"That's all anyone can tell anyone," Odo said. "That's rather the point, isn't it, Inquisitor?"

They took him out through his own front door, past his neighbors, who watched from windows with the particular blankness of people memorizing faces for later. One woman spat. Not at Odo. At Maren. Maren noted the face — thin, gray-streaked hair, a charm-knot hanging from her doorpost, the cheap kind sold at markets, luck-braids and weather-bones, nothing in them but string and animal teeth and hope — and filed it away, and hated herself a little for how smoothly the filing went.

* * *

By midday they had taken four from Fisher's Lane and the streets around it. A herb-woman whose sin was knowing which moss stopped bleeding. Two brothers who ran messages and had never once gotten lost, which the report called *uncanny navigation*. A girl of maybe fourteen who had been overheard talking to gulls, and who fought like a wildcat when they came for her, and who was crying by the time they got her into the wagon, and who looked at Maren through the slats and said, "You've got it too. I can hear it in you. Like a struck bell that won't stop ringing."

Maren walked away from that wagon at a pace just short of running, and her hands did not shake, because she had trained them not to shake, and that was somehow worse than shaking.

The girl was wrong, of course. Maren had been tested at fifteen, along with every other ward-child in the capital's orphan asylums. Cold iron, quicksilver drops, the whole humiliating battery. Nothing. She was the emptiest vessel the Inquisition had ever poured into its uniform, and that was precisely why they'd chosen her — a null could walk into a room full of hidden practitioners and feel nothing, want nothing, be fooled by nothing. *You are the blade because you are not the fire,* her instructor had said, back when that had sounded like a compliment.

So why did the girl's words sit in her chest like a swallowed coal?

Because you're tired, she told herself. Because it's been a long day and it isn't half done. Because duty is heavy and heavy things press.

She had gotten very good, over eleven years, at explaining things to herself in words that fit inside the law.

* * *

The lead came at dusk, and it came from the least likely place: the confiscated-charms inventory.

Every sweep turned up trinkets. Half the city wore them — luck-knots, salt-bags, weather-bones strung on leather — because a rainless city will buy hope from anyone who sells it, and ninety-nine charms in a hundred were exactly what they appeared to be: string, bone, greed. Standard procedure was to burn the lot. But procedure also required cataloguing, and cataloguing required someone to actually look at the things, and by the third day of the purge everyone else on her staff had learned to hand the odd ones to Vale, because Vale noticed things, and noticing things near Vale was safer than not.

This one had come off a dockworker named Pell, arrested that morning in a sweep Maren hadn't led. A storm-charm, the inventory said. Weather-bone core.

Except when Maren turned it under her lamp, she saw that it wasn't weather-bone at all. Weather-bone was gull or cormorant, light and porous, bleached by sun. This core was dense, dark, veined faintly blue-white like lightning frozen in stone, and when she held it up to the lamp the veins seemed to move — slowly, lazily, the way clouds move when you watch them long enough.

And it was warm. Slightly, evenly, impossibly warm, in a room where the lamp was the only fire.

"Pell says he bought it off a charm-seller," the sergeant reported. "Works the docks. No name anyone remembers — they just call him the charm-seller. Sells luck-knots mostly, cheap ones, honest junk. But Pell swears his knee's been dry through three wet fogs since he started wearing this."

"Wet fog doesn't soak a knee anyway."

"No, sir. But Pell swears it."

Maren set the charm down on the table and looked at it for a long moment. Everything about it was wrong. Real workings — true gift-work — hadn't been seen in Greyharbor in eleven years. The registry was clean. The city was clean. She had helped make it clean, street by street, name by name, and she knew — she *knew*, the way she knew her own signature — that a working of this quality did not survive eleven years of purges by being careless enough to sell its product to dockworkers.

Unless it wasn't careless. Unless it was hiding the way she hid: in plain sight, behind a routine so dull nobody thought to look twice.

Or unless it was bait.

"Pull everything on the charm-seller," she said. "Descriptions. Locations. Dates. I want a pattern by morning."

"There isn't much pattern, sir. That's the thing. He's everywhere and nowhere. Docks on tide-days, market on rest-days, sometimes the lane behind the chandlery. Never the same stall twice. Nobody knows where he sleeps. Nobody even agrees what he looks like — tall, short, young, old, depends who you ask."

Maren felt something turn over in her chest, slow and cold, like a ship changing tack. *Nobody agrees what he looks like.* That wasn't carelessness either. That was craft. That was a person who understood exactly what she was and had built a life designed to slide through hands like hers.

She should have felt the hunter's thrill. Eleven years ago she would have. Instead she felt something itch, deep under the sternum, in a spot she couldn't scratch — the same spot that had itched since the proclamation, since Odo Brannigan's cold porridge, since a girl in a wagon told her she rang like a struck bell.

"Docket it," she said. "Priority flag. And sergeant — when we take him, I do the interview. Personally."

"Yes, sir." The sergeant hesitated at the door. "Begging pardon, sir, but Aldery's office flagged the same name an hour ago. They want him brought straight to the harbor warehouses. No interview. Direct transfer."

"Transfer where?"

"They didn't say, sir. They said the seal on the order was higher than mine to read."

Higher than a sergeant's. Maren wondered, briefly, whether it was higher than hers, and then wondered why the wondering felt like standing at the edge of a drop.

"Then they can have him," she said, "after I've interviewed him. That's procedure. Procedure is the only thing keeping this purge from being a mob with better funding, and I will hang onto procedure with both hands if it kills me."

The sergeant left. Maren sat alone with the lamp and the impossible charm, and after a while she picked it up again and held it in her closed fist, and it stayed warm, patient, alive, like a small captured piece of a sky that had forgotten this city existed.

*We're not butchers,* she had almost said to Odo Brannigan.

She put the charm in an evidence box, locked it, and carried the key home in the pocket over her heart, next to the seal, where the two pieces of iron lay together and said two different things, and she pretended, one more night, that they said the same one.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, of water.

Not the sea — she'd grown up landlocked, in the orphan asylum at Vell, and had never learned to love the ocean the way harbor folk did. This was older water, dark water, water with weight and intention, pressing up from underneath something vast. In the dream she stood on a shore that was not any shore she knew, and beneath her feet, far down, something enormous turned over in its sleep, and the turning made a sound like a name being spoken in a language made entirely of pressure.

She woke before dawn with the taste of salt in her mouth, though she kept no salt in her quarters.

On her desk, where she had left it locked in its box, the evidence seal was unbroken. The charm inside was cold for the first time since she'd catalogued it. And scratched into the lid of the box, in a hand she did not recognize, in letters small enough to be missed by anyone who wasn't looking for them, were five words:

*Ask Karstholm. Ask what wakes.*

Maren Vale sat very still in the gray light and listened to Greyharbor wake around her, and for the first time in eleven years, the itch under her sternum had a shape, and the shape was a question, and the question was already burning through every answer she had.
