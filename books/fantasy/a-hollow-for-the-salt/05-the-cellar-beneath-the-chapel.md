## 5. The Cellar Beneath the Chapel

The chapel had been dead longer than Sebastian had been alive.

They reached it an hour past midnight, cutting through the tannery district where the stink covered their passage and the streets went unpaved and unpatrolled. Wren kept pace without complaint, which told Sebastian more than any words could have. A child who didn't complain about walking was a child who had learned that complaining changed nothing.

"Here." Sebastian stopped before a wall of blackthorn gone feral, swallowing what had once been a gate. "Mind the thorns."

"What is it?"

"A church that stopped believing in itself." Sebastian worked the latch hidden under a curtain of bramble — smugglers' architecture, older than the blackthorn, built by people who needed doors that weren't doors. The panel swung inward on oiled hinges. Twenty years ago, Sebastian had oiled them. It seemed someone else had kept the habit since; the metal moved like it had been tended within the season. "In, quick."

Inside was a nave stripped of everything holy. The pews had been burned for kindling decades back. The altar was a scar on the floor where someone had pried the stone up and found nothing underneath worth taking. Moonlight came through a broken window in slanted bars, laying stripes across the dust like a cell door.

Wren stood in the middle of it, arms wrapped around themself, the stolen luck-knot still knotted into their hair. They looked at the broken window, the empty altar, the dark.

"It's not much," Wren said.

"It's dry, and it's off every map I know of." Sebastian set down the satchel they'd carried from the shop — everything that mattered now fit in one bag, which was either freedom or poverty depending on how you looked at it. "There's a cellar. Smugglers used it for cargo. We'll use it for sleeping."

"You've been here before."

Not a question. Wren missed nothing. "Once," Sebastian said. "A long time ago."

* * *

The cellar stairs were stone, worn to a smooth dip in the center by two centuries of feet. At the bottom, the air turned cool and mineral, tasting of old salt — Greyharbor's whole history in a breath. Sebastian lit no lamp. Instead they felt along the wall until their fingers found the seam they remembered: a shelf that wasn't a shelf, a hollow behind it where the masons had left a gap for reasons lost to time.

Their hand closed around a stub of candle, then a flint. Then, out of habit so deep it had become a reflex, they almost struck it —

— and stopped.

Because lighting a fire was nothing. Lighting a fire was what any fugitive did. But the thing they wanted to do, the thing their hands ached to do after twenty years of pressing the want flat, was something else entirely.

"Wren," they said. Their voice came out strange. "Come here a moment."

The boy picked his way down the stairs. In the dark he was mostly eyes and elbows, thin as a gull's keel. He stopped at the bottom step and waited, because he was the kind of child who waited, who watched first and spoke second, and Sebastian thought: *someone taught him that the hard way.*

"I'm going to show you something," Sebastian said. "And when I show you, you can't ever tell anyone. Not anyone. Not even if they hurt you. Especially if they hurt you. Do you understand?"

Wren's chin lifted. "You're Gifted."

The word landed in the dark between them like a dropped coin.

"The criers today," Wren said. "The Purge. That's why you're hiding me. That's why you burned your shop." His voice cracked on the last part. "I saw the smoke. I'm sorry. I'm sorry about your shop."

"Don't be sorry about the shop." Sebastian sat down slowly on an overturned crate, joints aching with the particular exhaustion of a body that had spent all day pretending to be ordinary. "Sit. Please."

Wren sat. Close. Closer than necessary, knees almost touching Sebastian's knee, and Sebastian realized the boy had chosen to sit near them, in a strange cellar, on the worst night of his life, because nearness to Sebastian was the safest thing he knew. Something in Sebastian's chest shifted like ballast in a hull.

"Yes," Sebastian said. "I'm Gifted. And here is the thing about the gift: it doesn't like being starved. Twenty years I've fed it crumbs. Tonight it gets a meal. Watch."

Sebastian cupped both hands together, the way you'd hold water from a stream.

They hadn't done this in twenty years. They hadn't dared. A light could be seen through shutters. A light could betray. For two decades Sebastian Okonkwo had been a person made of locked doors, and this was the innermost one, and opening it felt like pulling a splinter out of the heart — pain first, and then the shocking sweetness of the space where the pain had lived.

They let go.

Light bloomed in their cupped hands.

Not flame. Nothing so crude. It was a small warm radiance, gold-tinged at the edges, steady as a held breath, casting the cellar in amber. It smelled faintly of rain coming over dry ground — petrichor rising out of stone. The shadows leaned away from it. Dust motes drifted through the glow like slow golden insects, and where the light touched the damp walls, the salt crust glimmered as though the rock itself remembered being treasure.

Wren made a sound. Small. Unfinished. The sound a person makes at the edge of a cliff when the view takes their breath before the fear does.

"That's not a trick," he whispered.

"No."

"That's not a lantern behind your back or oil on your hands or — " He leaned forward, close enough that the light caught the sharp planes of his face, the grime, the chapped lips, the eyes gone huge and wet. "It's warm. I can feel it. It's *warm.*"

"Storm-light," Sebastian said. "The oldest kind. Before there were prayers, there was weather, and before weather had names, someone learned to call it. My grandmother called it that. Her grandmother before her." The words came easier than expected, like water finding an old channel. "I haven't let myself do this since I was younger than you are now. Do you understand what that means? Twenty years. Twenty years of carrying this" — the light pulsed gently, as if agreeing — "and never once letting it out where anyone could see."

"Why?" Wren asked. And then, before Sebastian could answer, answered himself: "Because of the Purge. Because people like the criers said. Because they'd take you."

"They'd take me long before the Purge," Sebastian said quietly. "But yes. People fear what falls from the sky. Lightning doesn't ask permission. Rain doesn't knock. So storm-callers learned to be small. Learned to hide. And some of us hid so well we forgot we were anything at all."

The light trembled. Just slightly. Sebastian steadied it, and their hands, and their breath.

"But look at it," they said, and their voice did something embarrassing, went rough in the middle. "Look at it, Wren. All those years, and it still comes when I call. It didn't forget me. I tried to make it small and it just... waited. Patient as weather."

Wren reached out one finger, hesitated, looked up — asking permission, always watching first — and Sebastian nodded. The boy touched the edge of the glow.

He laughed.

It burst out of him like a bird startled off water, high and cracked and disbelieving, and he snatched his hand back and then put it right back in because he couldn't help himself. "It tickles. It's like — like standing in the sun but the sun likes you. Like the sun *chose* you."

"Careful," Sebastian said, smiling despite everything, despite the burned shop and the criers and the soldiers dragging a fisherman through the streets this morning, despite all of it. "That's how it starts. First it tickles. Then you start talking to clouds."

"Do you? Talk to clouds?"

"When I'm very lonely."

Wren laughed again, and this time it didn't crack, and Sebastian thought: *there it is. There's the sound he'd have made every day of his childhood if the world had been worth a damn.*

* * *

They ate hard bread and dried fish from the satchel, sharing the light between bites. Wren ate the way street children eat — fast, hunched, guarding the plate with his forearm — until he caught himself doing it and made himself slow down, deliberately, like a man practicing trust.

"Tell me about the gift," he said, mouth half full. "How do you know if you have it? Could I — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "When the lightning came over the harbor last month. The big forked one. Everyone ran inside and I stayed out. I wanted it to come closer. Is that — does that mean anything?"

Sebastian looked at him for a long moment.

Every instinct said: *tell him nothing. Let him stay small. Small is safe. You of all people know how well small works, and how much it costs.*

But the boy had stolen a charm because he'd wanted luck, and luck was just weather you couldn't see yet.

"It might mean something," Sebastian said. "Hold out your hand. Palm up. Don't try to do anything. Just notice."

Wren held out his hand. The storm-light pooled in his palm like water in a cupped leaf.

"Now breathe slow. In through your nose. Out through your mouth. And while you breathe, pay attention to your skin. What does the air feel like on it?"

"Cold," Wren said. "Cellar-cold."

"Beneath the cold. Underneath it. There's a pressure sometimes, like the moment before a summer storm breaks. Like the sky leaning down. Can you find it?"

Wren's brow furrowed. His breathing slowed. The cellar went very quiet — quiet enough that Sebastian could hear the tide somewhere beyond the walls, the city's old heartbeat.

Then the light flickered.

Not guttered — *answered*. It leaned toward Wren's open hand the way a plant leans toward a window, and for half a heartbeat the glow doubled, gold flooding gold, and the salt on the walls threw back sparks like a field of tiny stars.

Wren snatched his hand back as if burned. "What was that? What did I *do?*"

"Nothing," Sebastian said, and their heart was hammering against their ribs like a fist on a door. "You did nothing at all. That's what frightens me. You didn't reach for it, Wren. It reached for you."

The boy stared at his own palm. Turned it over. Turned it back. "Is that bad?"

Sebastian thought about the proclamation nailed to the customs house. Registration, then the hunt. Thought about the fisherman dragged barefoot over cobblestones. Thought about twenty years of locked doors, and how the locks had never once made them safe — only small.

"It's dangerous," Sebastian said honestly, because this child had been lied to enough by everyone else in his short life. "From tonight on, it's the most dangerous thing about you. But it's also yours. Nobody gave it to you and nobody can take it out of you. They can only kill you for it, and I intend to make that very difficult."

Wren absorbed this with the terrible practicality of children. Then: "Will you teach me?"

"To hide it? Yes."

"No." Wren looked up, and the storm-light caught in his eyes and stayed there, reflected, as if some of it had decided to live in him now. "To use it. Like you. To call things."

"*No,*" Sebastian said, too fast, too loud, and the light flinched with their voice. They made themselves gentler. "Wren. Every person I've known who called storms openly is dead or exiled. Teaching you isn't a kindness. It's painting a mark on you."

"You painted a mark on yourself tonight," Wren said. "You showed me. Why'd you do that, if hiding is the only safe thing?"

Sebastian opened their mouth. Closed it.

Why indeed. Because the boy had looked at the dark like he was used to it. Because he'd said *sorry about your shop* and meant it. Because twenty years of smallness had bought Sebastian nothing but a hollow where a life should be, and tonight, holding a handful of light in front of a starving child, they had felt more like themselves than on any day of two careful decades.

"Because," Sebastian said finally, "some things shouldn't have to stay buried just to survive. Ask me again someday. When we're somewhere safer than a smugglers' cellar. If I say yes, I'll teach you everything my grandmother taught me. But not tonight. Tonight you sleep."

Wren nodded slowly. He didn't look defeated. He looked like a boy filing something away for later, which was worse, or better, depending.

* * *

Sebastian banked the light down to an ember-glow and set it floating near the ceiling, where it hung like a captive star. Wren curled up on the bedroll Sebastian spread for him — the good one, the one Sebastian would have taken for themself a week ago without thinking — and was asleep within minutes, the way the young and the exhausted sleep, completely, like falling.

Sebastian sat awake against the cellar wall and watched the light breathe.

Twenty years. They turned the number over like a stone, looking at all its faces. Twenty years since the council's judgment, since the ship west, since the day they'd sworn off calling and meant it, mostly. Twenty years of selling luck to dockworkers and telling themselves the hollow in their chest was contentment.

One night with a stolen child and a cupped handful of light, and the hollow had a shape now. It wasn't emptiness anymore. It was a room. And rooms could be filled.

*Foolish,* they told themselves. *You've known him one day. One day and a stolen charm. This is how it starts — this is exactly how it started last time, with people you'd have died for, and look how that ended.*

But the light hung above the sleeping boy, gold and patient, and Wren had pulled the luck-knot from his hair in his sleep and wrapped the cord twice around his wrist, and Sebastian looked at that small stubborn knot and thought: *the world took everything from me once because I let it happen alone. Maybe that was the mistake. Not the storms. The aloneness.*

Above, faint through the chapel's broken window, the night wind changed. Sebastian felt it before they heard it — a pressure shift, a wrongness in the air, the particular taste of a large number of boots moving in one direction.

They rose silently and climbed the stairs to the nave, keeping to the shadows, and pressed their eye to the gap in the blackthorn.

Torches. Four, maybe five, moving up the tannery road in formation. Not a patrol — patrols wandered. These moved with purpose, and their purpose was a straight line toward the abandoned chapel that appeared on no map Sebastian knew of.

Which meant someone else knew of it.

Sebastian stood in the dark doorway with the torchlight growing at the end of the lane, and felt the old storm wake fully beneath their skin for the first time in twenty years — not a crumb, not a cupped handful, but the whole coiled weight of it, lifting its head.

Behind them, below, the ember-light drifted down the stairs, drawn to the sleeping boy like a moth that had finally found its flower.

Sebastian closed their hand around the door-latch and began, quietly, to plan.
