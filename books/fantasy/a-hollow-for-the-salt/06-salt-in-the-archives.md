## 6. Salt in the Archives

The Archive of the Inquisition kept its secrets the way Greyharbor kept its rain: grudgingly, and only in small amounts.

Maren Vale had spent eleven years learning its moods. The clerks who guarded the lower stacks responded to flattery; the ledgers themselves responded to patience. She had neither to spare this morning. Her boots still smelled of the fisherman's blood from yesterday's sweep — she'd scrubbed them twice, and the smell had gone into her anyway, into the creases of her hands, behind her teeth.

Tomas Ellery. That was his name. The gift-marked fisherman they'd dragged off the quay while his daughter screamed into a soldier's shoulder. Maren had signed the intake order herself. *One male, forty-odd years, minor weather-sense. Transferred per directive.* Eleven years of signing such lines, and it had never once itched like this.

She told herself it was the daughter's face. Nothing more.

The Archive was built like a ship turned inside out — iron ribs, tarred beams, windows too small for the light they admitted. Down on the third sub-level, where the purge records lived, the lamps burned low and blue because full flame was a fire hazard among paper this old. Maren's shadow stretched long and wrong across the shelves.

"Vale." The head archivist, a dried-out man named Corvus with ink permanently stained to his second knuckle, didn't look up from his desk. "You're not cleared for the Karstholm annex."

"I'm lead inquisitor on the Greyharbor sweep," she said. "I need the transfer manifests. Every gifted taken under the Purge Writ goes somewhere, and I can't file my reports without knowing where."

"They go where the Writ sends them."

"The Writ says 'secure containment pending disposition.' That's not a place, Corvus. That's a shrug."

He looked up then, and there was something in his face she'd never seen on it before — a careful blankness, the look of a man standing very still so as not to wake something. "Inquisitor. There are orders above your clearance and mine both. You were given your sweep parameters. File within them."

"I've filed within them for eleven years."

"And been commended for it." He returned to his ledger. "Don't spoil the record now."

She should have left it there. That was the thing she would turn over later, in the dark, again and again — how easy it would have been to walk away, to write *transferred per directive* one more time and let the itch scab over. Eleven years of obedience had a weight to it, a groove worn smooth, and her feet knew the way out of the Archive without her asking them.

Instead she said, "Then I'll have it from the Lord Commander directly," and watched Corvus's pen stop moving.

* * *

Lord Aldery received her that evening in the purge headquarters above the old customs house, a room that had been a counting floor once and still smelled faintly of salt and arithmetic. He was younger than his reputation — everyone said so — with the kind of handsome that looked manufactured, cheekbones like a coin die, hair oiled to architectural precision. He poured wine without asking if she wanted any.

"Inquisitor Vale." He handed her the cup. "Your sweep numbers are excellent. Better than the coastal chapters are managing. I've mentioned you to the Crown."

"Thank you, my lord." She didn't drink. "I have a filing question."

"A question." He smiled as though she'd brought him a gift. "Ask."

"The transfer destination for taken gifted. The manifests route through a seal I don't recognize. Karstholm."

Something happened to the room. Not dramatically — the fire didn't gutter, no door slammed. But Aldery stopped moving the way prey stops moving, and Maren had hunted enough men to know the difference between a predator's stillness and a cornered animal's. For one unguarded half-second he was the latter.

"Karstholm is a Crown facility," he said. "Naval. Decommissioned fortress, used for storage."

"Storage of what?"

"People, technically." He said it lightly, tasting the word. "The gifted require particular handling. Ordinary prisons are... insufficient. Their gifts leak. They unsettle other prisoners. Karstholm is remote, warded, staffed by specialists." He refilled his own wine. "You've done your work well, Vale. The ones you send us arrive healthy. Some of the other chapters aren't so gentle. It matters — damaged stock is less useful."

The word went through her like a fishhook. *Stock.*

"Useful for what?"

"Rehabilitation," Aldery said, and smiled his manufactured smile, and she understood that she was being dismissed, thanked, and warned all in the same gesture. "The Crown has great plans for the gifted, Inquisitor. Greater than hiding them in holes. Go home. Sleep. You look tired."

She walked out with the wine untouched and her heart going hard against her ribs, and she could not have said whether it was fear or fury. Both, probably. They'd been rooming together for days now, ever since the charm-seller's empty stall, ever since the witness statement that hadn't fit.

*The charm-seller.* That was the other thing scratching at her. Sebastian Okonkwo, luck-knots and weather-bones, twenty years of quiet commerce on the Salt Row — and every scrap of intelligence on them came back clean. Too clean. No debts, no vices, no informants who'd ever sold them out, no neighbors who remembered anything useful beyond *polite* and *kept to themselves*. Twenty years of living leaves sediment. Okonkwo had left none. Either they were exactly what they appeared — a dull, decent tradesman — or they were the most disciplined fugitive Maren had ever hunted.

And the child. Wren. A street thief, eight years old maybe, vanished off the Row the same night the sweep began. Coincidence sat poorly with Maren. It always had. Coincidence was what lazy inquisitors wrote when they didn't want to dig.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight she went back to the Archive, after midnight, when Corvus had gone home to whatever dried-up life he kept, and she picked the third sub-level lock with two hairpins and eleven years of knowing which locks the Inquisition bought cheap.

* * *

The Karstholm annex was one shelf, iron-gated, at the back of the sub-level where the lamp never reached. The gate lock was better than the door lock — Crown work, expensive — which told her more than the documents did. Nobody spends money guarding boring paper.

Inside: manifest books, forty years of them, bound in grey leather gone soft with damp. She took the oldest first and worked forward by lamplight shielded with her cloak, and the pattern assembled itself slowly, the way a coastline assembles out of fog.

Forty years of transfers. Gifted taken in purges across six coastal provinces — weather-senses, tide-whisperers, bone-readers, hearth-healers, all the small magics the Crown had spent a century criminalizing. Every manifest listed them the same way: name, gift-class, condition on arrival. And then, for the vast majority, a final entry in a hand that never changed across four decades:

*Disposition: retained.*

Not executed. Not released. Not imprisoned-with-review, which was what the law promised. *Retained.* Decade after decade. Hundreds of names. Men and women taken at thirty and simply — kept. No discharge entries. No death certificates. The dead got paperwork; Maren had processed enough of it to know. These people produced no paperwork at all. As far as the Crown's own records were concerned, they had ceased to exist as individuals and become inventory.

She found Tomas Ellery's name three books back from the newest. *Minor weather-sense. Condition on arrival: good. Disposition: retained.* Filed nine days ago. Alive, then. Probably. Whatever *retained* meant, he had been it nine days ago, and his daughter was somewhere in Greyharbor sleeping in a doorway believing her father was in a cell with a trial coming.

There was no trial coming. Maren understood that now with the flat certainty of a woman reading her own signature on a lie. There had never been trials. The Purge Writ's language of registration and lawful process was theater, and she had been the theater's best actress for eleven years, commendations pinned to her chest like costume jewelry.

Her hand was shaking. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain starting.

She worked backward through the older books looking for the beginning of it, and found instead something stranger. The earliest manifests — sixty, seventy years back — were different. Same format, same grey leather, but the dispositions read *transported: Karstholm (construction labor)* and then, later, *transported: Karstholm (maintenance)*. Maintenance. Seventy years of maintenance requiring fresh gifted labor every season, forever, at a decommissioned fortress that stored nothing.

And in the oldest book of all, on the first page, in faded brown ink, a founding order bearing a royal seal she recognized from history lessons — the old king, the grandfather, the one who'd drowned. The handwriting was different from every clerk's hand that followed. This was written by someone powerful enough to write their own orders.

She read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, barely voiced, because some things needed to be said in air to be believed:

*"The works beneath Karstholm proceed. The King in the salt turns in his sleep. Send me those whose blood remembers the storm, for the waking requires much, and the binding will require more."*

The King in the salt.

Maren sat down on the cold floor with her back against the shelf and read it a fourth time, and the Archive's blue lamplight seemed suddenly very thin, a skin of light stretched over an ocean of dark, and she was sitting on top of seventy years of something enormous and patient.

She was not a fanciful woman. That was her reputation — Vale the precise, Vale the incorruptible, Vale who believed in evidence and procedure and nothing else. So she did what her nature demanded: she copied the founding order out word for word, note-perfect, and she copied the disposition patterns, and she noted the seal numbers on the transfer manifests and cross-referenced the escort units, building the case the way she'd build any case, brick by evidentiary brick. If this was madness, it would at least be documented madness.

It was nearly dawn when she finished, and it was dawn when she carried her report upstairs to be countersigned through channels — because that was procedure, and procedure was the last plank of the deck she had left to stand on.

* * *

The countersignature came back in four hours. That alone should have warned her; channels took days.

The report returned to her desk with the countersignature of Undersecretary Halloway affixed, and Maren read her own report top to bottom and found it gutted. Cleaned. The founding order — gone. The disposition analysis — replaced with a single bland paragraph confirming that Karstholm was a secure storage facility operating within Writ guidelines. Her recommendation for immediate investigation — struck. In its place, in her own words, her own phrasings, her own dry cadence, a paragraph recommending *no further inquiry, the matter being administrative rather than criminal in nature.*

They hadn't just buried it. They'd made her bury it. Someone knew her writing well enough to wear it like a stolen coat, and somewhere in the machinery above her, a clerk or worse had taken her evidence and dressed it in her voice and sent it back down wearing her name.

Above her signature. Above the seal she'd sworn on, the one she'd pressed into wax eleven thousand times, meaning it every time.

Maren sat very still in her office while the harbor bells rang the morning watch, and she thought about Tomas Ellery's daughter screaming into a soldier's shoulder, and about *stock*, and about a king drowned seventy years ago writing *the binding will require more*, and about the word *more* sitting in her stomach like swallowed anchor-chain.

Eleven years. She had fed people into this. Efficiently. Commendably. She had been the smooth hinge on a door that opened onto — onto what? A fortress under the sea. A king turning in his sleep. Blood remembering storms. She didn't believe it, not yet, not in her bones — but she believed in the cover-up, and the cover-up was proof of shape if not substance. Honest administrations did not rewrite reports in their officers' hands. Honest administrations did not spend seventy years maintaining nothing.

Corvus had known. His careful blankness, his *don't spoil the record*. How many others? How deep did the knowing go, and how high?

She thought about burning the returned report. She thought about carrying it to the Lord Commander himself, to the Crown, to a newspaper — she imagined each road and watched each one close. Aldery owned the command. The Crown had signed the founding order. And the newspapers in Greyharbor printed what the customs house allowed.

Which left the road inquisitors were not supposed to take: the road where you kept digging alone, off the record, below the record, in the dark under the dark.

She unlocked her bottom drawer and took out the folder she'd been avoiding for three days. *Okonkwo, S. Charm-seller, Salt Row. Status: fled, likely with juvenile associate (Wren, surname unknown).* Witness statements. The too-clean history. And one item she'd pulled from the Row herself and couldn't make fit: a weather-bone from Okonkwo's stall, confiscated as evidence, that her own gift-blind hands could swear was nothing but carved gull-bone — except that it had rained, briefly, locally, impossibly, over her courtyard the night she brought it home. Four minutes of rain in a city that hadn't seen weather in a generation. She'd told herself it was sea-fog.

She'd told herself a lot of things.

Maren put the folder in her coat, and the forged report in her safe, and the founding-order copy against her ribs where her oath used to sit, and she stood at her window watching the grey water of the harbor move without wind, and she made the decision the way she did everything: precisely, and past the point of being able to take it back.

If the law was a harvest, then she was finished being the sickle.

She needed the charm-seller. And the charm-seller, if the smugglers' whispers on the Row were worth their price, was hiding in the one place in Greyharbor even the Inquisition had forgotten — down past the salt-chapel ruins, in the old cellar country under the cliffs.

She checked her blade, checked her seal — the seal that might be the last honest thing she owned — and went out into the morning to hunt the only person in Greyharbor who might know what the King in the salt was waiting for.
