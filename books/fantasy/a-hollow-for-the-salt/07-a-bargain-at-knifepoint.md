## 7. A Bargain at Knifepoint

*Sebastian*

The chapel smelled of salt rot and old candle smoke, and Sebastian had almost — *almost* — let themselves believe it was safe.

Three days they'd been there. Three days of Wren sleeping curled against the broken font like a cat in a sunbeam, of teaching the child to whistle charms into river stones, of pretending that the world outside the smugglers' chapel hadn't turned its face toward them with a knife in its hand. The tide came in through the flooded crypt twice a day and went out again, faithful as anything in Greyharbor, and Sebastian had started to think in tides instead of hours. It was a dangerous way to think. Tides made you feel like time belonged to you.

They were mending a luck-knot when the air changed.

Not a sound. Not a smell. Something older than either — the particular pressure of a person who has been trained to move quietly deciding to stop moving quietly. Sebastian's hands went still over the knot. Twenty years of exile had built an instrument out of their skin, and the instrument was singing one note: *someone is here who knows how to kill you.*

"Wren," they said softly. "Behind the altar. Now."

The child didn't argue. That was the thing about street kids; they knew the weather of a room before any adult did. A whisper of bare feet on stone, and then nothing.

Sebastian set down the knot. Stood. Turned.

Maren Vale stood in the doorway where the door should have been — the door had been gone since before the chapel was abandoned — with rain running off her grey coat and a blade in her hand that caught what little light the lanterns gave. She looked exactly as she had in the market square the day of the Proclamation, except tired. Except human, which was worse.

"Inquisitor," Sebastian said. "You're a long way from your criers."

"Sebastian Okonkwo." She said the name like she was laying it on a table between them. "Storm-caller. Exiled by decree of the last storm council, ratified by the crown, twenty-two years ago. Registered charm-seller under three false names since." Her eyes moved across the chapel — the font, the stones, the dark mouth of the crypt stairs. "Harboring an unregistered gifted child."

"You've been busy."

"I don't want the child."

The words dropped into the room and sat there. Sebastian kept their breathing even, but under their skin the storm stirred, twenty years of held lightning pressing against the walls of its cage. *Not yet,* they told it. *Not unless.*

"No?" Sebastian said. "Then what does the crown's finest huntress want at midnight in a drowned chapel? Confession?"

"I want to know," Maren Vale said, "what Karstholm is."

And that — that was not a question Sebastian had prepared for. They had prepared for arrest, for fight, for flight, for the ugly arithmetic of how far they could carry Wren on a night's sleep and half a loaf. They had not prepared for an inquisitor to stand in the rain and ask them a question like a person asking, not a blade asking.

"You know what Karstholm is," Sebastian said carefully. "Everyone knows. The drowned fortress. The old prison. The purge ships go there."

"The purge ships go there," Maren repeated. "And nothing comes back. No transfer records. No prisoner manifests. I signed orders for forty-one people, Okonkwo. Forty-one. I have been to every holding cell this kingdom keeps, and none of them are holding my forty-one." She took one step forward, and her boot splashed in the shallows pooling across the chapel floor. "I rewrote a report last week. Or rather, it was rewritten above my signature. I wrote that the harvest shipments were irregular. Someone decided I had written that everything was regular. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Sebastian understood. Understanding arrived cold and slow, like water finding a crack.

"I'm telling you," Maren said, "that someone above me is erasing what I see. And I am standing in a smugglers' chapel at midnight asking a fugitive storm-caller to tell me why, because everyone I am supposed to ask would sooner erase me too."

Under the altar, very faintly, Wren breathed. Sebastian heard it because they were listening for it with their whole body.

"You could be lying," Sebastian said. "This could be the trap. Corner the charm-seller, wear the honest face, get them to talk about Karstholm so you can report who else knows."

"Yes," Maren said. "It could be. You'll have to decide." She turned the blade in her hand — not a threat, Sebastian realized, just a soldier's habit, hands needing work — and then, deliberately, she reversed it and drove it point-down into the soft wood of a pew. It stood there, quivering, hilt up. Offered. "There. Now neither of us is armed, if we don't count the obvious."

"The obvious being?"

"You." Her voice didn't waver. "I read your file, Okonkwo. All of it, including the parts that aren't supposed to exist anymore. You once called a storm off the Vell coast that sank six slaving ships and didn't drown a single sailor. Six ships, and every man aboard swam home. Whatever they exiled you for, it wasn't cruelty." Her jaw tightened. "So. Are we talking, or are we fighting? Because I'm too tired to do both."

Outside, the tide turned. Sebastian felt it in their teeth, the great slow breath of the sea drawing back from the crypt stairs, and thought: *twenty years.* Twenty years of being small and careful and no one. And here was the law itself, soaked through, asking to be taught.

"Sit down, Inquisitor," Sebastian said. "It's a long story, and it starts before your kingdom had a name for salt."

* * *

*Maren*

She did not sit. Sitting put your weight somewhere and Maren Vale had spent eleven years learning never to put her weight anywhere she couldn't lift it from in half a second. But she stayed, and she listened, and she kept her eyes on Okonkwo's hands because hands told you things faces wouldn't.

Okonkwo talked the way she imagined they sold charms — measured, warm on the surface, something coiled underneath. They told her about the Salt King.

She'd heard the name. Everyone raised within earshot of the coast had heard it, the way you heard about ghosts in the walls of old houses: a story to frighten children, a king beneath the sea, older than the crown, bound there by the first storm-callers when the world was young. Nursemaid nonsense. She'd thought.

"They didn't kill him," Okonkwo said. "They couldn't. He isn't alive the way we're alive. So they bound him — starved him, chained his hunger under the fortress, and set a watch on him. Generations of watchers. My line was one of them." Their hands moved as they spoke, small unconscious gestures, shaping the story in the air. "Karstholm wasn't built as a prison for people, Inquisitor. It was built as a lid."

"And now?"

"And now your crown is shipping gifted people there by the boatload, and no one comes back, and the sea around the fortress glows at night." Okonkwo's voice went flat, and flatness, Maren had learned, was worse than anger. Anger wanted something. Flatness had already given up wanting. "What do you think a lid is for, if not to keep something from getting out? And what do you think it takes to open one?"

Maren thought of the harvest ledgers. Of the word the clerks used — *harvest* — when every other document in the kingdom said *transfer*. Of the royal physician's seal on requisitions she'd assumed were for medicines. Blood. They'd been requisitioning blood, and she had signed the escort orders without reading past the column totals.

"I need to sit down after all," she heard herself say, and was distantly surprised to find her legs agreeing.

She sat on the pew with the knife still standing in it, close enough to take if she changed her mind, and put her head briefly in her hands. Eleven years. Eleven years of sweeps and registrations and the long grinding machinery of the purge, and she had told herself it was mercy — registration instead of pyres, custody instead of killing, the modern way, the civilized way. She had walked gift-marked fishermen out of their own kitchens with their children screaming, and told herself the cells were better than the fire.

The cells were a throat.

"There's more," Okonkwo said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which she hated. "Isn't there. You didn't come all this way for a ghost story."

Maren lifted her head. This was the part she had rehearsed on the walk through the rain, and the rehearsal fell apart now, the way rehearsals do, and what came out was just the raw thing underneath.

"My sister," she said. "Tamsin. Gifted. Small craft — she could sweeten milk, keep bread from molding, make a fever break. Nothing. Village-craft. When the first registrations came, I told her to sign. I said, *it's a list, Tam, it's just a list, it's safer to be counted.*" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. "She was counted. She was on the third shipment to Karstholm. Fourteen months ago."

The chapel dripped. Somewhere below, the sea moved against stone.

"I pulled her file last week," Maren went on, because now that the breach was open the rest came through it whether she willed it or not. "Her transfer record ends at the dock. There's no arrival entry. There's no cell assignment. There's a number — a requisition number — and when I traced it, it routed to the same office as the blood requisitions." She looked up at Okonkwo, and let the inquisitor die off her face, and what was left was just a woman in a wet coat. "I think my sister is in that fortress. Or I think she was, until whatever they're doing with her was finished. And I can't know which. Do you understand? I can't even know which grief to carry."

For a long moment Okonkwo said nothing. Then they crossed the chapel floor, and Maren's hand twitched toward the knife hilt out of pure habit, and they saw it and stopped, and waited, and only when she shook her head — *no, stay* — did they come closer and crouch down in front of her like a person approaching a spooked animal.

"Tamsin Vale," they said. "Third shipment. I can't tell you she's alive. But I can tell you that the taken are worth more to whoever runs Karstholm alive than dead. Harvest means they want the source, not the meat." Their eyes were steady, brown, unbearably calm. "If your sister is there, she can be gotten out. That's not comfort, Inquisitor. That's tactics. But tonight it's all I have."

"It's enough," Maren said, and found, to her fury, that it was.

* * *

*Sebastian*

The truce, when it finally took shape, took shape the way most true things do — sideways, unglamorous, in the space between one sentence and the next.

"So what happens now?" Maren asked. She had recovered herself, mostly, spine straightening by degrees like a ship righting. "I walk out of here and report the chapel, and we do this properly, with irons?"

"You could," Sebastian allowed. "Though I'd wake the storm first, and you'd drown in a chapel, and Wren would run, and everyone would be poorer for it."

"You'd kill an inquisitor over a stolen charm-child."

"I'd protect a child over a kingdom's paperwork, yes." Sebastian let a little of the storm show through their eyes as they said it — just a glint, just enough. Let her see what she was negotiating with. "But that's not the bargain I'm offering."

"Then offer it."

Sebastian stood, and paced, because thinking had always lived in their feet. "Here's the shape of it. You go back. You keep signing what they put in front of you, and you keep copying what crosses your desk — names, numbers, ships, dates. Everything. You bring it to us. In exchange, when we move against Karstholm — and we will move — you're inside it, not outside. You get your sister. We get the taken. And your crown gets to keep existing, which it will not do if the thing under that fortress wakes up hungry."

"That's not a truce," Maren said slowly. "That's treason. From both directions."

"It's a bargain at knifepoint," Sebastian said, nodding at the blade still standing in the pew. "Those are the only kind that hold. Both parties remember the knife."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the empty doorframe. Below them, the tide had gone fully out, and the crypt stairs rose dripping into lamplight like the steps of some drowned church going up to heaven.

"If I do this," she said at last, "and they catch me, they won't give me a trial. They'll rewrite me. I've seen it done. One week I'm an inquisitor; the next, there's a warrant with my face on it and a clerk swearing I was always a traitor." She met Sebastian's eyes. "You should know what you're asking. I'm not brave, Okonkwo. I'm thorough. There's a difference, and the difference gets people killed."

"Good," Sebastian said. "Brave people charge. Thorough people win." They pulled the luck-knot from their pocket — the one they'd been mending when she came, red thread and river-stone, the first knot they'd ever tied for keeping rather than selling — and held it out. "For your pocket. It doesn't work. It's just thread. But my mother gave me one like it, and I'm told the sentiment survives translation."

Maren looked at the knot for a long moment. Then she took it, and her fingers brushed Sebastian's, and both of them pretended not to notice that the touch of another person's hand was a shock, after so long, like the first cold wave of a tide coming back in.

From behind the altar came a small, unmistakable sniffle, and then a sneeze, and then the sound of a child giving up on stealth entirely.

"I know you're there," Maren called, not unkindly. "You breathe like a bellows, little thief."

Wren emerged, chin up, fists ready, positioning themself squarely between the inquisitor and Sebastian with the fierce arithmetic of the small. Maren studied the child — the sharp face, the storm-grey eyes, the birthmark at the wrist that Sebastian had spent three days pretending not to recognize for what it was.

"Royal line," Maren said quietly. Only to Sebastian. Only once. "That mark. I've seen it in the genealogies."

"Not here," Sebastian said, and their voice came out harder than they meant it to. "Not yet. Some knowledge is a knife too, Inquisitor, and that one's pointed at *them*."

Maren held their gaze. Then she nodded, once, and picked her knife out of the pew, and slid it away, and became again the grey coat and the tired eyes and the walking wound of a woman who had chosen, tonight, badly or well.

"Three days," she said at the doorframe, rain gathering her back into the dark. "There's a supply ship out of Greyharbor on the third day. After that, the next manifest goes to Karstholm, and I'll copy it before it goes. Come to the fish-market at dawn, fourth day. Ask for eels. I'll know."

"And if it's a trap?" Sebastian called after her.

Maren paused. For the first time all night, she smiled — a thin, cracked, genuine thing.

"Then we'll both be surprised," she said, "and one of us will be dead," and she was gone into the rain, and the chapel was quiet, and Wren crept out and pressed against Sebastian's side like a burr.

"Was that good," Wren whispered, "or bad?"

Sebastian watched the empty doorway, and felt the strange new weight settle into their chest — heavier than solitude, warmer than safety. An inquisitor in their pocket. A name — *Tamsin* — added to the ledger of reasons. A ship, three days out.

"Yes," they said.

* * *

*Maren*

She walked the long way back through the flooded streets, and the rain washed her clean of nothing, and she found she didn't want it to.

In her pocket, her fingers kept finding the knot. Red thread, river-stone, worthless. She'd taken evidence from a hundred scenes tonight and the only thing she'd carried out was a charm that didn't work, and her oath lay somewhere back in that chapel floor, drowned with the rest of the smugglers' church.

*Treason, from both directions,* Okonkwo had said. Yes. But it had a cleaner shape than her duty had ever had. For eleven years she had served a law that was turning out to be a mouth, and now, at last, she knew what it fed on.

At the edge of the inquisitors' quarter she stopped under a lamp, took out her notebook, and on the first blank page wrote two words in her precise, damning hand:

*Karstholm. Lid.*

Then she closed it, and went in through the rain to sign whatever they put in front of her, and wait for the third day.
