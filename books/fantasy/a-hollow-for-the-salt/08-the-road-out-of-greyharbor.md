## 8. The Road Out of Greyharbor

They left before the gulls woke.

Sebastian had always imagined that if they ever fled Greyharbor, it would be in a storm — rain hammering the cobbles, thunder covering the sound of their footsteps, the whole city too busy battening down to watch one charm-seller slip away. Instead the night was dry and cold and painfully clear, the kind of sky that showed every star and hid nothing, and they went out through the fish-gate with a stolen lantern shuttered to a coin of light and Wren's small hand locked in theirs like a shackle.

The child hadn't let go since the cellar.

It had been two days of hiding beneath the ruined smugglers' chapel while Maren came and went with news, and in those two days Wren had learned to sleep pressed against Sebastian's side like a burr, to eat only what Sebastian ate first, to flinch at boots on stone above them. Sebastian knew the shape of that fear from the inside. Twenty years ago they had been exactly that small and exactly that certain that the world wanted them dead.

"Keep your hood up," Sebastian murmured as the gate passed overhead. "If anyone asks, you're my nephew. Salt-sick. We're taking you to your mother in Tarrowmouth."

"I don't have a mother in Tarrowmouth."

"You do now. Her name is Odile and she makes terrible soup and she worries about you constantly." Sebastian squeezed the hand. "Lie like you mean it or don't lie at all."

Wren nodded, solemn as a funeral. Then, after ten steps: "What about her?"

Maren walked three paces behind them, hooded, unarmored, wearing a plain gray traveling cloak over clothes that still smelled faintly of the chapel's dust. She looked like what she was pretending to be — nobody. She did not look like an inquisitor. Except, Sebastian thought, for the way she moved. Twenty years of watching crowds had taught Sebastian how people carried authority even when they stripped it off. Maren walked like someone used to having room made for her.

"She's our cousin," Sebastian said. "Odile's sister. Disgraced. The family doesn't talk about her."

"I can hear you," Maren said quietly.

"Good. Then you know your part."

Behind them, Greyharbor lay gray and sleeping under its permanent haze of coal smoke and sea fog, and somewhere inside it, Sebastian knew, the purge was already turning over in its sleep, getting ready to wake hungry. They'd heard the bells at midnight — not the usual hour-bells but the short, urgent triple-strike that meant a sweep was moving through a district. The chapel district. Their district.

Maren had come back soaked to the knees with the news and one look at her face had ended any argument about leaving that morning instead of now.

"They found the cellar," she'd said. "Not because of me. A neighbor sold them the rumor of smoke. We have until dawn, maybe less."

So: dawn, and the coast road north, and forty leagues of open country between them and the saltmarshes where Maren swore there were people who could hide them. Forty leagues with a hunted child, a hunted storm-caller, and a disgraced inquisitor whose face was known in every garrison from here to the capital.

Sebastian had survived twenty years by never trusting anyone further than they could throw them. Now they were betting all three lives on a woman who had spent last week hunting children just like Wren.

The thought sat in Sebastian's chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The coast road was a pale scar between hedgerows, running north with the sea hidden behind dunes to the left and farm country falling away to the right. By full light they were three miles out, walking fast, and Wren had finally stopped gripping Sebastian's hand hard enough to grind bone.

Maren set the pace. It annoyed Sebastian how good she was at it — how she read the road ahead, chose the stretches where they'd be visible versus those where the hedges gave cover, timed their crossings of the farm lanes. She did it without asking anyone's opinion, the way she must once have directed sweeps, and every time she raised two fingers to halt them at the sound of cart wheels, Sebastian's teeth ached with the effort of not resenting it.

They stopped at midday in a hollow where a stream cut under the road, drank, ate hard bread and harder cheese from Maren's pack.

Wren ate like a small animal, hunched over the food, eyes up. Watching the road both ways. Watching Maren.

"Why are you helping us?" the child asked suddenly. No warning. Children asked questions like knives thrown flat.

Maren didn't flinch. Sebastian watched her decide not to. "Because I was wrong," she said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only true one I have right now." Maren tore her bread into pieces she wasn't eating. "I spent six years believing the Gifted were dangerous. That registration protected everyone, including them. I told myself the ones who ran were hiding something." She looked at Wren, and Sebastian saw her make herself hold the child's stare. "Then I followed the orders far enough back and found out what the orders were actually for. And I discovered that being wrong isn't a wound you can bandage. It's a debt. This is me paying some of it."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Sebastian says debts get paid or they rot."

"Does he." Something flickered across Maren's face — not quite a smile. "He's right."

"He. Hm." Sebastian chewed. Twenty years of careful neutrality, undone by pronouns in front of an inquisitor. "I'm whatever the day requires. Today I'm a charm-seller with a sick nephew."

"And yesterday?"

"Yesterday I was nobody. It was a very comfortable job." Sebastian stood, brushed crumbs from their coat, and looked north along the empty road. "We should make the ferry crossing at Miller's Reach before dark. After that the road climbs and we'll want light."

Maren rose too, and for a moment they stood close enough that Sebastian caught the smell of her — woodsmoke and ink and something underneath like iron. "Okonkwo," she said, low, so Wren wouldn't hear. "At the checkpoint, if it goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong."

"If it does. Take the child and run. Don't stop for me. My seal buys us one miracle, maybe. Not two."

Sebastian looked at her. Really looked, for the first time since the chapel — at the hollows under her eyes, at the way her hand kept drifting to her belt where the seal hung wrapped in cloth like something shameful. She expected to be left. She had already priced herself into the arithmetic of the escape and found her own value wanting.

"Don't be stupid," Sebastian said. "Nobody gets left. That's the whole point of what we're doing. That's the entire lesson, Vale, and I'll thank you to learn it faster than I did, because it took me twenty years and cost more than I can afford again."

She stared at them. Whatever she'd prepared to say next died somewhere behind her teeth.

"Come on," Sebastian said. "Nephew. Cousin. Walk."

* * *

They smelled the checkpoint before they saw it — woodsmoke and horse and the sour-milk stink of men who'd been sitting in one place too long. Maren heard it first, actually; she stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into her back, and held up the two-fingered halt, and tilted her head like a bird.

"Miller's Reach," she breathed. "There shouldn't be a garrison post here. It's a ferry landing, not a border."

Sebastian eased forward along the hedge until they could see through a gap in the thorn. Below, where the road bent down toward the river and the flat-bottomed ferry, four soldiers had built a fire beside a painted barrier board. Two horses tethered. A sergeant with a ledger. And beyond the barrier, a queue: three carts, a dozen travelers on foot, all of them waiting while soldiers worked down the line checking papers, turning packs out onto the ground.

Checking faces.

"They're not looking for smugglers," Sebastian said. The words came out flat. "Look at the ledger. He's writing names. Every traveler, name and business and destination. That's a sweep protocol. That's *purge* protocol."

"They've extended past the city walls." Maren had come up beside them, close enough that her shoulder nearly touched Sebastian's. Her voice had gone very quiet and very controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against something enormous. "Aldery said the first phase would take a month. It's been five days."

"Maybe he's motivated."

"Or maybe he knows exactly who left Greyharbor and which road they'd take." Maren's jaw tightened. "Someone reported the chapel search result fast. Fast enough to seed the roads overnight."

Below, a soldier pulled an old woman's bundle apart and shook out her linens in the mud. She protested. He laughed. The sergeant wrote something down.

Wren made a small sound. Sebastian looked down and saw the child's face gone white, hands balled, and recognized the expression — not fear this time. Fury, in a body too small to hold it properly. The air around Wren felt different. Thicker. Sebastian's own skin prickled in answer, the old storm under their sternum lifting its head like a dog hearing its name, and they clamped down on it hard, and reached out and covered Wren's clenched fists with one hand.

"No," Sebastian said softly. "Whatever's waking up in you right now — no. Not here. Lightning is a signature, little wren. One bolt and every gifted-hunter within ten miles knows precisely where we are, and worse, they'll know *what* you are, and then no seal in the kingdom saves us."

Wren's breath came ragged. "He's tipping out her things. She's old."

"Yes. And if you burn him where he stands, they'll tip out hers, and mine, and yours, and everyone in that line, and then they'll come for the marsh folk, and everyone we're trying to reach. Anger is fuel. It is not a plan." Sebastian held the child's gaze until the fists loosened. "Breathe. Fold it small. I'll teach you properly when there's sky enough for mistakes."

"There's another way across," Maren said. She'd turned away from the checkpoint, scanning the river's course upstream, and Sebastian watched the inquisitor reassemble itself around her like armor being buckled on piece by piece. "The river bends east half a mile up. There's a ford near the ruined mill — knee-deep, maybe thigh. Cold, slow water. We cross there, cut through the mill fields, rejoin the road beyond the ridge. Adds two hours."

"Two hours we may not have if Aldery's riders are behind us."

"Better two hours than that barrier." Maren paused. Her hand had gone to her belt again, to the wrapped shape of the seal, and Sebastian saw her thumb move over it once, unconsciously, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless we don't avoid it." She turned, and her face in the hedge-shadow was unreadable. "Think. Three fugitives skirting through wet fields leave sign — footprints, broken hedge, a frightened farmer's report by evening. We'd be described by dark. But three travelers who walk straight up to that barrier, give their names calmly, and get waved through by an inquisitor's warrant—" she exhaled, "—we become nobody again. Officially nobody. There's no description to chase because the record says we were checked and cleared."

"You'd show your face to that sergeant." Sebastian kept their voice level. "Your face is on purge bulletins, Vale. You burned your own offer of pardon in front of witnesses. If he recognizes you—"

"He won't. Inquisitors are paperwork to men like that. He'll see the seal and his spine will do the thinking." But her hand hadn't left the seal, and Sebastian understood suddenly, with a clarity that hurt, what this was costing her. That seal was the last organ of her old life. Six years of oath and rank and certainty, wrapped in a rag, and she was proposing to spend it on a ferry checkpoint to save a stolen child and an exile she'd been sent to arrest.

"One last time," Sebastian said quietly.

"One last time." She unwound the cloth. The seal sat in her palm — heavy silver, the crown-and-lantern sigil of the Inquisition, worn smooth at the edges by six years of her grip. She looked at it the way you look at a grave. "After this it's a lump of metal. Anyone can forge a seal. Only one person alive can spend *this* one, and after today she stops existing."

"Maren." Sebastian reached out and closed her fingers back over it. "When it's done — when we're through — you say goodbye to it however you need to. However long that takes. Understood? Nobody gets left, and nothing gets mourned on a schedule."

For a moment she just breathed. Then she nodded, once, and put the seal away, and became someone else entirely.

Sebastian watched it happen and it chilled them more than any magic ever had. Shoulders squaring. Chin leveling. The tired fugitive folding away like a map, and underneath her, rising, Inquisitor Maren Vale — not the hollow woman from the first sweep, but something sharper, a blade remembering its purpose. She took Wren's shoulder. "You're my son. You're feverish, you're miserable, and you don't speak unless spoken to. Can you be miserable?"

Wren, with the ghost of true theatrical genius: "That's easy."

"*Gods help us,*" Sebastian muttered, "the child has range."

* * *

The barrier looked taller up close, which was absurd, because it was a painted plank on trestles. What was tall was the silence of the queue, the way travelers studied their own boots, the ledger lying open on the sergeant's knee like a mouth waiting to be fed.

"Hold there." The sergeant looked up. Young, red-faced, bored, dangerous the way bored men with weapons are dangerous. "Name, business, destination. Papers."

"Vale." She produced the seal without hurry, and the change in the man was immediate and total — the boredom draining out of his face, replaced by the particular panic of a soldier realizing the day has gotten above his station. He came off his stool. Behind him, the other soldiers straightened. "Inquisitor, second chair, office of the Purge Commission. These are my retainers and a ward of the commission. We're traveling north on commission business. You'll find no entry required."

"Of course — I — didn't realize—" He was fumbling for a fresh page. "Should I record—"

"You should record nothing." Maren's voice dropped half a register, and Sebastian, standing at her shoulder with Wren's hot little hand in theirs, marveled at it. Not cruelty. Just weight. The absolute confidence of someone who has never once been contradicted by a man with a ledger. "This matter predates your posting and will outlast it. You saw a commission party pass southbound. Nothing else. Am I clear, Sergeant?"

"Clear, Inquisitor."

"Good." She paused. Looked at the queue, at the old woman kneeling in the mud repacking her scattered linens, and something moved behind her eyes that she did not let reach her face. "And Sergeant. The commission takes a dim view of property damage against registered persons. See the lady's things are carried to the ferry for her."

"Aye — yes, Inquisitor."

The barrier swung aside. They walked through. Sebastian kept their pace lazy, their face bland, their heart slamming against their ribs like something trying to get out, and did not breathe fully again until the road bent past the ridge and the checkpoint dropped from sight behind the hill.

Wren lasted fifty paces before dissolving into giggles — high, wild, slightly hysterical giggles, the sound of terror converting itself into something survivable. "*You should record nothing,*" the child gasped, imitating Maren's cadence with horrifying accuracy, and even Sebastian laughed, a rusty, startled sound they barely recognized as their own.

Maren didn't laugh. She had stopped in the middle of the road, and she was holding the seal out at arm's length, looking at it.

Then she knelt, set it on a milestone, drew her knife, and hammered the pommel down twice. The silver cracked across the crown-and-lantern. She left it there in the grass for the crows and stood, and her face when she turned around was stripped bare, younger and older at once.

"Well," she said. "Now I'm nobody too."

Sebastian looked at her — at the cracked seal gleaming on the milestone behind her, at Wren still hiccupping laughter, at the long road climbing north into country none of them knew — and felt the strange, terrifying architecture of the last week settle into place around them. Not a rescue. Not an arrest. Not a flight, even.

Three nobodies, walking the same direction.

"The ford's ahead," Sebastian said gently. "Two hours lost, like you said. We'll walk fast and arrive angry."

But as they climbed, Wren tugging at each of their hands, swinging between them like a bridge, Sebastian glanced back once at the thin blue line of the coast, and the sea beyond it, and felt the storm under their skin turn restlessly toward the water.

Something out there had noticed them. Sebastian couldn't have said how they knew. They only knew the deep was paying attention now — and that the road north ran straight toward it.
