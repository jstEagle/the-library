## 9. Storm Lessons

The road out of Greyharbor was a scar of packed clay and old cart-ruts, running north along the coast like it was afraid of the water. Wren walked in the middle, which had become their place without anyone deciding it. Sebastian on the left, where the sea was. Maren on the right, where the cliffs were. Wren between them, small enough that both of them sometimes forgot to look down and check.

Three days now since the checkpoint. Three days since Maren had held up her seal one last time and lied with her whole face, and the soldiers had waved three fugitives through like they were nothing at all. Wren kept waiting for the fear to wear off. It didn't. It just got quieter, the way a bruise gets quieter — still there when you pressed on it.

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, half-swallowed by gorse. Maren built a fire the way she did everything: efficiently, without wasted motion, and then sat with her back to it so she could watch the road. Sebastian sat across from Wren and turned something over in their fingers.

It was a charm. One of Wren's stolen ones — the luck-knot from the first day, the one Wren had taken off the cart and never given back. Sebastian must have found it in Wren's pocket while Wren slept, because there was no other way they'd have gotten it, and they held it up now between two fingers like a question.

"You stole this," Sebastian said.

"I borrowed it."

"You stole it," Sebastian said again, but gently, "and I let you go. Do you know why?"

Wren shrugged. "Felt sorry for me."

"Because your hands were shaking." Sebastian set the knot down on the flat stone between them. "Not from cold. From *wanting*. You wanted it so badly your body couldn't hold still. That's not greed, little wren. That's the gift, knocking."

Wren's stomach did something complicated. "I don't have any gift. The criers said—"

"The criers said a lot of things." Sebastian's voice went low, the way it did when they talked about Greyharbor's rainless years, about the storm coiled under their skin like a sleeping animal. "The criers said people like me were plagues. Here's what I say. There's weather in everything alive. Most folk are calm days. Some of us—" they spread their hands, and for one heartbeat the firelight bent toward them, just slightly, like the flames were leaning in to listen, "—some of us are storms."

Maren didn't turn around. But Wren saw her shoulders go still.

"Feel it," Sebastian said. "Close your eyes. Don't reach for anything. Just listen."

Wren closed their eyes. The world went dark and close: the crackle of the fire, the far-off grind of surf, Maren's breathing, Sebastian's breathing, the wind worrying the gorse. Ordinary things. Boring things. Wren almost said so.

And then, underneath — faint as a coin dropped down a well — something else. A hum. Not a sound exactly. More like the feeling you get before thunder, that pressure behind your ears, except it wasn't coming from the sky. It was coming from everywhere. From the ground. From the sea. From the fire itself, a thin bright thread of it, and from Sebastian—

From Sebastian most of all. A vast slow rolling thing, like a cloud bank seen from very far away, coiled tight around a person-shaped quiet.

Wren's eyes flew open. "There's something *under* everything."

Sebastian smiled, and it was the saddest smile Wren had ever seen on them. "That's the world, little wren. And you can hear it. Which means the purge lists aren't wrong about you after all."

* * *

They traveled north for six more days, and each evening, Sebastian taught.

Not magic, not at first. Listening. They'd sit at the edge of camp with the sea below them and practice naming what Wren heard in the deep hum of things: the salt-heavy patience of the tide, the dry rattle of wind through dead grass, the iron taste of a coming rain that hadn't yet decided to fall. Wren got good at it fast — too fast, maybe, because Sebastian's face did a strange thing every time Wren learned in an afternoon what had taken Sebastian years.

"Is that bad?" Wren asked once, catching the look. "Me being quick?"

"It's bad the way a lamp is bad in a dark room full of soldiers," Maren said from her post by the fire. She said things like that now. She'd stopped pretending to be one of them only some of the time, and started being one of them all of the time, and nobody had marked the day it happened.

"She means you're noticeable," Sebastian translated. "To anyone who knows how to look. Which is why we don't *do* anything yet. Listening is safe. Doing is not."

But Wren was eleven, and eleven-year-olds are made mostly of wanting, and wanting doesn't listen to sense.

It happened on the ninth night, in the ruins of an old tollhouse with a broken roof and a chimney that still stood like a raised finger. Rain had been threatening all day — real rain, the first since Greyharbor, rolling in off the sea in grey banners. Wren could feel it now the way they'd never felt weather before: a huge slow pressure walking toward them across the water, patient as a debt collector.

They were practicing listening when the first distant flash lit the clouds.

Wren felt it go through the hum like a bell struck underwater. And something in Wren's chest *answered*. Leaned toward it. A hot, prickling, hungry feeling, like the moment before a sneeze, except bigger, except it filled their whole body, except it was reaching up their arms and into their hands before they knew what was happening—

"Wren." Sebastian's voice, sharp. "Let it go."

"I'm not holding it!"

"You're *pulling*. Stop pulling—"

Lightning came down.

Not from the clouds — or rather, yes from the clouds, but *crooked*, bending mid-fall like a dog hearing its name, and it struck the chimney of the tollhouse ten feet from where Wren sat, and the world went white and deafening and the smell of burnt stone filled everything, and Wren was on their back in the dirt without remembering falling, ears ringing, hands tingling, and above them the rain finally broke and came down in sheets.

Sebastian was there instantly, hands on Wren's face, turning it, checking eyes, checking breath. Their own face was white as bone.

"Are you hurt? Are you hurt anywhere?"

"No." Wren's voice came out tiny. Then, because it was true and enormous and couldn't be kept inside: "Did you see it? It *came to me*. I called it and it came—"

"I know." Sebastian sat back on their heels, and the rain ran down their face, and they looked older than Wren had ever seen them look. "I know it did."

Maren appeared out of the rain with her cloak over all three of them somehow, herding them back against the tollhouse wall where the stones still steamed. Her eyes went to the split, blackened chimney, then to Wren, and Wren braced for the inquisitor-look, the purge-list-look.

What they got instead was Maren crouching down to their level, rain dripping off her chin, saying quietly: "How did it feel?"

Wren thought about lying. Didn't. "Like being *seen*. By something huge. Like it had been looking for me my whole life and finally found me."

Maren nodded slowly, and looked at Sebastian, and something passed between the two adults that Wren couldn't read and hated not being able to read.

"That's how it starts," Sebastian said to Maren, not to Wren. "That's exactly how it started with me."

"And the lists?" Maren asked. "If a strike like that happens near a town—"

"Then every gifted-sensor in three counties feels it. Yes." Sebastian dragged a hand through their wet hair. "We can't stay on the open road. Not anymore."

Wren sat against the warm scorched stones and listened to the adults plan in low voices, and hugged their knees, and tried to make the joy and the terror fit inside them at the same time.

Because here was the truth, and Wren held it up to the light and turned it over like the stolen charm: lightning had answered their hand. Lightning had *known their name*. Nothing in Wren's whole ragged, hungry, invisible life had ever answered them before — not the orphan-stewards, not the dock bosses, not the city that stepped around them like a puddle. And now the biggest loudest oldest thing in the sky had come down out of a storm to touch them, and it was the single most wonderful thing that had ever happened.

And it was also, the criers would say, the reason to hunt them.

Both things were true. Wren was learning that the world was big enough for both.

* * *

They left the coast road the next morning, cutting inland through hills the color of old bronze, and the mood in the little company changed. Sebastian taught differently now — not just listening, but *holding*. How to feel the pressure building and let it pass through instead of grabbing. How to be a window and not a door.

"You grabbed it," Sebastian explained, walking backward ahead of Wren on the trail so they could talk face to face. "That's the instinct. It answers you and you grab, because grabbing is what hungry people do. But the storm isn't a thing you take, Wren. It's a thing you *agree with*. Big difference."

"How do I agree with a storm?"

"Same way you agree with anybody. You stop trying to win." Sebastian grinned suddenly, and for a second they were the charm-seller again, the one who'd let a thief go. "Storms respect manners. Terrible tempers, excellent manners."

Wren practiced. All that day and the next, walking, resting, washing their face in streams — practicing letting the hum move through them without catching on it. Mostly they failed. Once, startling a rabbit, they sent a static crackle up their own arm that made their hair stand on end, and Maren laughed — actually laughed, a short rusty sound like a gate opening after years shut — and Wren decided in that moment that the gift was worth any risk, if it bought sounds like that out of Maren Vale.

On the third inland day, the hills ended at a long green valley, and Sebastian stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into their back.

"What?" Wren said. "What is it?"

Sebastian didn't answer. They were staring down the valley, at a thread of smoke rising from somewhere among the trees — ordinary smoke, hearth-smoke — and at the land around it, which Wren now, with new ears, could *hear*: a low woven hum, many-stranded, like a choir humming in another room. Gifted. Dozens of them. Hidden.

"We're not the only ones running," Maren said quietly, hand drifting to her knife out of habit.

"No." Sebastian's voice had gone strange. "We're not. And I know this valley." They swallowed. "Twenty years ago, I swore I'd never come back here."

The smoke rose thin and straight into the empty sky, and somewhere beneath it, the hum of hidden lives went on singing, unaware that it had just been heard by a child who could call lightning — and by the exiled storm-caller who had just led them straight to its door.
