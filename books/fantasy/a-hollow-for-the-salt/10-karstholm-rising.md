## 10. Karstholm Rising

They reached the cliffs at dusk, and Maren wished they hadn't.

The path up from the coast road was a goat-track of loose shale and old sheep-cropped grass, the kind of climb that would have been nothing to her a year ago — before the purge, before the chapel, before a charm-seller with storm in their skin had looked at her across a cellar full of candlelight and asked, *what did you think you were hunting?* Now every step felt like an accounting. Her boots found the rock. Her hands found the cold. Behind her, Wren scrambled with the easy animal grace of a child who'd grown up on rooftops, and Sebastian climbed last, quiet as falling snow, which she was learning meant they were afraid.

She didn't ask how Sebastian knew to be afraid. She could see it herself now.

Karstholm rose out of the sea two miles north, black against a bruised-orange sky, and it was wrong in a way her eyes kept trying to correct and failing. She'd read about it in the archives — everyone had, if they'd read anything at all: the drowned fortress, the old king's folly, swallowed by the sea three hundred years ago and left to the gulls and the fish. What the histories didn't say was that it stood *up*. Towers above the waterline, whole walls of it, a crown of masonry jutting from the waves like a rotted tooth. The tide should have taken more. The tide takes everything, eventually.

Unless something under it wanted to keep its bones.

"There." Wren's voice, small against the wind. The child pointed down at the water below the cliffs.

Maren followed the finger and felt her stomach turn over slowly, like something dying.

Ships. Three of them, riding low in the water, anchored in the lee of the fortress where the swell couldn't reach. Longboats were running between ship and shore — no, not shore; there was no shore, only a half-drowned quay at the base of Karstholm's seawall, and men moving along it in files, and between each file, figures being marched. Even at this distance, even in the failing light, she could tell what those figures were. They walked the way the taken walked. Shoulders in. Heads down. Not resisting because resistance had been processed out of them somewhere between the wagon and the hold.

"Count the boats," Sebastian said. Their voice was flat, which Maren had learned was worse than any shaking.

"Four loads off the first ship," Wren said. "Five off the second. I can't—" The child stopped counting. Maren didn't blame them. She'd stopped too.

Two hundred people, maybe more. Two hundred gift-marked souls hauled out of Greyharbor and the coastal towns and the river villages, registered and catalogued and shipped, exactly as the orders said. Exactly as she had signed for, district by district, name by name, in her neat inquisitor's hand.

*Efficient,* Commander Aldery had called her once, approvingly. She had worn the word like a medal for six years.

Now she watched the longboats nose up to the drowned quay and disgorge their cargo into the dark mouth of a tunnel at the fortress's base, and the word tasted like ash and salt.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a standing stone — one of the old marker-stones from before the kingdom, before the church, before any law worth the name, furred with lichen and leaning like a tired man. Sebastian built no fire. Instead they knelt and pressed both palms flat to the earth, and Maren felt it happen again, that thing she still hadn't gotten used to: a warmth spreading through the ground like spilled tea through cloth, a soft gold glow rising between the stones, light without flame, heat without smoke. A hearth made of nothing but will.

Wren held their hands out to it the way children hold hands to any fire, instinct older than caution. But the child's eyes weren't on the warmth. They were on the sea.

"It's glowing," Wren said.

Maren looked. And this time she couldn't unsee it.

Around the base of Karstholm, where the black water met the black stone, the sea was lit from beneath. Not moonlight — there was no moon yet, and the glow was green anyway, a sick phosphorescent green, pulsing slow and rhythmic, like breath. Like something enormous breathing in its sleep. Each pulse spread outward from the fortress in rings, fainter and fainter, until it died against the open water.

"That's not algae," Maren said. "That's not anything natural."

"No," Sebastian said. They hadn't moved from their crouch by the earth-light. Their face, lit from below, looked carved out of something older than their years. "It's feeding."

"The harvest," Maren said. "The blood they're shipping in—"

"Goes into the water. Or near enough. There are channels under that fortress, old ones, cut when it was built. I can feel them from here." Sebastian's jaw tightened. "Twenty years I told myself the stories about Karstholm were stories. Drowned king's curse, sailors' talk, nothing. And all that time it was sitting here drinking."

"Drinking what?"

"Gifted blood, Maren." They said it plainly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "What do you think the registration rolls were for? What do you think the wagons were for? You've known since the archives."

"I've known since the archives that they were shipping people somewhere," Maren said. "I didn't know what for." She heard her own voice go hard and was grateful for it, because underneath the hardness was the other thing, the thing climbing her throat. "I signed the transport orders for the eastern districts. Did you know that? Forty-one names in the spring alone. I checked the manifests. I confirmed the counts. I wrote *received in good order* at the bottom of the reports."

Sebastian said nothing. Wren had gone very still, the way street children went still when the adults started saying true things.

"I thought they were going to prisons," Maren went on, because now that the door was open she couldn't stop it, twenty years of discipline pouring out through the crack. "That's what I told myself. Harsh, unjust, but survivable. People come back from prisons. And then the archives showed me Karstholm, and I rewrote my report — I *rewrote* it, they changed my findings above my own signature, and instead of screaming about it to every magistrate in the capital I packed a bag and ran, because I am a coward, and I have been a coward for six years, and every person on those boats is on those boats partly because I was good at my job."

The wind came off the water and pushed at them all, cold and smelling of salt and something else, something rotten-sweet underneath.

"You're not a coward," Wren said suddenly.

Maren looked at the child. Wren was hugging their knees, chin on top, eyes fixed on her with that terrible directness street kids had, the look of someone who had audited every adult who ever claimed authority over them and found most of them wanting.

"You came back for me," Wren said. "In the chapel. When you could've arrested us. And you used your seal at the checkpoint even though it burned you. Cowards don't do that. I know cowards. I grew up with cowards. They run and keep running and they don't look back and they definitely don't sit on a freezing cliff telling the truth."

"Wren—"

"I'm not done." The child's voice cracked but held. "You did bad things. Fine. Bad things happened. But you're *here*, and you're telling us, and that's more than anyone else ever did." Wren shrugged, small and fierce. "So stop drowning in it. We need you walking."

The silence after that was long. Out on the water, the green pulse beat on, patient as a heart.

Maren found she couldn't speak. She nodded instead, once, and Wren nodded back, and that was the whole of the forgiveness — offered, not granted, not refused, just set down between them like a stone neither of them would pick up tonight. Maybe never. Maybe that was all right. Some debts you don't pay; you just carry them differently.

* * *

Later, when Wren had finally gone down into exhausted sleep wrapped in both their cloaks, Maren sat at the edge of the earth-light across from Sebastian and let the quiet stretch until it broke on its own.

"How long has it been doing this?" she asked. "The feeding."

Sebastian turned a smooth grey pebble over in their fingers. In the gold light their face was unreadable, but their hands gave them away — they always had. The pebble turned and turned.

"My grandmother's stories said the Salt King slept light," they said. "That he dreamed hungry. That every few generations someone fed him, and every time he fed, he woke a little more." The pebble stilled. "The last storm-caller council bound him properly, three hundred years ago. It cost them everything — that part of the story nobody argues about. Nine callers walked into the water and none came out, and the binding held for two centuries. Then it started to fray. Frayed bindings don't fail all at once. They leak. And a leaking binding around a hungry thing is..." They gestured at the sea, at the green rings spreading and dying, spreading and dying. "It doesn't need much. Just a sip, now and then. A drowned sailor. A gifted child lost to fever. Small cruelties the world provides free."

"And now we're providing them wholesale."

"Now the crown is providing them wholesale." Sebastian's voice dropped. "Someone figured out what the old councils knew. Someone in a palace worked out that the King under Karstholm can be woken — and decided waking him was worth two hundred lives a season. Or they think they can control him. They always think that."

"Who?"

"I don't know. Aldery runs the purge, but Aldery's a hammer. Hammers don't choose what nails are for." Sebastian looked at her across the light. "You served the crown longer than I've been alive. Who benefits?"

Maren turned it over. Names moved through her mind like fish — chancellors, admirals, the Queen's confessor, half-remembered dinner conversations from a life that felt like it belonged to another woman. One name snagged and stayed.

"Aldery reports to the Office of Tides," she said slowly. "Everyone thinks it's a harbor authority. Charts, dredging, lighthouse levies. Nobody asks questions about it because it's boring. But it has its own budget line, its own guard, and its own archive annex that even inquisitors can't enter without written cause." She laughed, short and bitter. "I filed written cause twice. Denied both times. I thought it was bureaucratic spite."

"It wasn't spite."

"No. It was a lock on a door that leads to *that*." She nodded at the fortress. "And I spent six years walking past it."

Sebastian was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "There's something else. About why the crown needs so much blood, so fast. The old rites — the ones that woke the King the first time, before the binding — they needed royal blood mixed with the oldest gift. King's line and storm-caller line, together. That's what the stories say." Their eyes stayed on hers. "Aldery isn't harvesting randomly. He's looking for something specific. Someone specific."

The wind picked up. Below them, a fifth longboat loaded at the second ship, and the green light pulsed, and pulsed, and pulsed.

"Then we get there before he finds them," Maren said.

"We?" Sebastian raised an eyebrow. "You've confessed your sins to a child and a fugitive on a clifftop. There's no road back to your oath from here, inquisitor. You understand that."

"I understood it at the checkpoint," Maren said, "when I pressed my seal into a sergeant's hand and watched him wave us through knowing it was the last lawful thing I'll ever do." She pulled her cloak tighter. "My oath was to protect the realm from what threatens it. Something under that fortress is threatening it, and the men feeding it are wearing the crown's colors, and I am the only officer of the law who knows. So yes. *We.* Unless you'd rather I stay on this cliff and write a report nobody will read."

Something moved across Sebastian's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first she'd seen there. "You'd make a terrible outlaw. Too many principles."

"So I'm told." She lay back against the standing stone and looked up at the first stars pricking through. "Get some sleep. Both watches are yours tomorrow; I took the cliff watch tonight."

"You took the cliff watch because you can't sleep."

"I took the cliff watch," Maren said, "because someone has to count the boats."

Sebastian held her gaze a moment longer, then inclined their head — acknowledgment, or surrender, or maybe both — and settled beside the sleeping child. Within minutes their breathing slowed into sleep's rhythm, deep and even, though Maren noticed their hand stayed curled loosely around Wren's shoulder all night, an anchor thrown in sleep.

She watched the water.

The longboats kept coming. Ship three arrived on the midnight tide, riding low, and began its own grim ferrying. By the third hour Maren had counted eleven boatloads off it and stopped trusting her numbers, because the numbers were unbearable and she needed her mind sharp. Instead she watched the fortress itself, and the green pulse beneath it, and tried to learn its rhythm the way she'd once learned the rhythms of suspects in interrogation rooms — the tells, the hesitations, the pattern under the noise.

And that was when she saw it change.

The pulse had been steady all night, slow as a sleeper's heart. But as the eleventh longboat grounded against the drowned quay, the rhythm stuttered. Quickened. The green rings came faster, brighter, spreading wider than before — wide enough now that the glow reached the base of her cliffs, staining the foam at the rocks below a luminous, hungry green.

Then, through the wind, she heard it.

Not a sound exactly. More a pressure, a low vibration in the rock under her, in her teeth, in the marrow of her — a note so deep it was less heard than endured, rising from under the sea like a bell struck in a drowned cathedral. It lasted three heartbeats. Four. Five.

On the water, every longboat stopped rowing at once. The soldiers sat motionless, oars shipped, heads bowed toward the fortress like men in prayer — or men compelled. For ten full seconds nothing on that whole black sea moved except the light.

Then the boats resumed, oars dipping, and the moment passed as if it had never been, and far below in the hollow dark of the tunnels, two hundred captured strangers were being marched deeper into the earth.

Maren's hand found Sebastian's shoulder and shook, and the storm-caller came awake instantly, fully, the way soldiers wake, eyes already on the sea.

"You felt it," she said.

"Yes." Sebastian was on their feet, pulling Wren's cloak over the sleeping child with hands that were not quite steady. "It knows they're coming. It's — pleased." They spat the word. "We have days. Maybe less. When it's fed enough to fully wake, no rite on earth binds it cheaply."

"Then we don't have days to spend on this cliff." Maren stood, joints aching, and looked north at the drowned fortress wearing its crown of black towers, its moat of hungry light. "There's a smugglers' tunnel network under these hills — my predecessor's files mentioned it, old routes from the free-trade days. If any of them still run, they'll come out inside Karstholm's foundations. Under the waterline."

Sebastian stared at her. "Under the waterline. Through flooded rock. With a child."

"With a child who can already call lightning," Maren said, "and a storm-caller who can bend the sea, and one disgraced inquisitor who knows the fortress's garrison rotations better than the men serving them." She allowed herself the smallest, sharpest smile. "I've read the invasion plans for that place, Sebastian. All of them. Written by better soldiers than Aldery, for wars that never came. I know where the old gates are. I know where the cells are."

"And the water?"

"The water," Maren said, "is your department."

Below them, the green light pulsed once more — slower now, satisfied, digesting — and in the pause between beats, Maren thought she saw, deep down in the foundation rocks of Karstholm where no window had ever been, a single point of pale light flicker and die. Like a lamp carried through a tunnel. Like someone down there, among the taken, still fighting.

"Pack up," she said. "We're going in tonight."
