## 11. The Drowned Fortress

The sea at Karstholm did not move.

Sebastian had known tides all their life — the patient breathing of Greyharbor's harbor, the long swell that rocked the fishing boats, the way water leaned toward the moon like a dog toward its master. This was none of that. The water around the drowned fortress lay flat as poured glass, and beneath it, far down where the light failed, something glowed. A pale, wrong luminescence, like a lamp burning under skin.

"It's not reflecting anything," Maren said beside them on the cliff. She'd said it three times now, each time quieter, as if repetition might make it less true. "There's no light source down there. Nothing to reflect."

"I know."

"Then what is it?"

Sebastian didn't answer. They were watching the ships instead — three of them, low in the water, being warped against the fortress's seaward wall by rowboats full of soldiers. Even from this distance, even through the grey curtain of drizzle, they could see the captives being marched down gangplanks. They moved wrong. Not stumbling, not resisting — just *moving*, one foot after another, like puppets whose strings had been cut but whose bodies remembered the dance.

Wren made a small sound in their throat. Sebastian put a hand on the child's shoulder without looking away.

"How many," Wren asked, "do you think are down there?"

"More than we can carry out." It was the truth, and Sebastian gave it to the child because everyone else in their short life had fed them prettier lies. "But not more than we can wake up."

Maren turned. In the hooded dark of her oilskin, her face was a pale smudge. "You still haven't told me how you plan to get inside. The gates are above the waterline. There's a garrison. There are—"

"Look at the wall again. Below the gate. What do you see?"

She looked. Sebastian watched her find it: a seam in the ancient stonework, half-drowned, running down into the black water. "A tunnel mouth."

"Smugglers built half the coast's fortifications, back when smuggling was a religion. Karstholm was a toll station before it was a prison. There's a flood-tunnel that runs from the seaward wall down into the heart of the rock. My grandmother ran cargo through it when she was younger than Wren."

"And it's flooded."

"Obviously."

"Then how—"

Sebastian finally turned to look at her, and let a little of the storm show through. Just a little. Enough that the rain around them bent outward, a hand's width of dry air in the wet grey world. "I'm going to hold the sea open," they said. "And we're going to walk down into it."

* * *

Getting down to the water took the rest of the day. The cliffs above Karstholm were riddled with goat paths and old quarry cuts, and Maren proved better at reading them than Sebastian expected — she moved like someone who had hunted fugitives over worse ground, which, Sebastian supposed, she had. Wren scrambled ahead of them both, quick as a rat in a granary, and twice stopped to point out sentries before either adult saw them. The child had a talent for watching. It would keep them alive, if nothing else did.

They reached the shingle beach below the seaward wall as the last light went. Up close, the fortress was worse than it had been from the cliffs. The stone was furred with white growths, bone-colored, branching like coral — except coral didn't grow this far north, and coral wasn't *warm*. Sebastian touched one with two fingers and snatched the hand back. It pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of geology.

"Don't touch anything," they said. "Either of you. Nothing here is dead, and nothing here is asleep."

"Comforting," Maren muttered.

They waited for full dark. Then Sebastian waded out until the water reached their chest, and put both hands flat on the surface, and began.

Storm-calling was never quiet work, but this was the quietest magic Sebastian had ever done. No thunder, no wind. Just a slow, careful argument with the ocean itself — *not tonight, not here, not this water* — pushing a hollow down into the deep the way a smith pushes a hole through hot iron. The sea resisted. Of course it did. But it resisted the way a tired animal resists, heavy and slow, and somewhere far below, the glowing thing pulsed once, twice, and seemed to consider them.

Sebastian held the hollow anyway. Air filled it — real air, pulled thin from the surface and packed down hard — a corridor of breathing space sunk into the flooded tunnel, wide enough for one person, lit by nothing.

"Wren first," they said through gritted teeth. "Walk straight down. Don't touch the walls. Don't stop. I'll be right behind you."

"What about me?" Maren asked.

"You're third. If the tunnel collapses, swim up and don't look back."

"I won't leave you."

"You will if I tell you to."

"No," Maren said, "I won't," and stepped past Wren into the dark, and Sebastian had no time to argue because holding the sea open was eating their strength like a fever eats a body, and every second spent talking was a second stolen from the hollow.

They descended.

It should have been terror, walking down a drowned stairway with the ocean pressing in from every side, held back by nothing but an old woman's smuggler's route and one exiled storm-caller's stubbornness. And there was terror — Sebastian felt it singing in their teeth, in the backs of their knees. But underneath the terror was something else, something they hadn't felt in twenty years, something so familiar it hurt:

Power. Unhidden. Used for what it was for.

The storm under their skin uncoiled, inch by inch, and for the first time since Greyharbor, since the exile, since the council's cold faces and the long road south — it *fit*.

* * *

The tunnel bottomed out in a chamber that had been dry once. Sebastian could see the old waterline marked on the walls, six feet up, stained black. Now the room stood knee-deep in seawater that glowed faintly from below, and the air tasted of salt and iron and something older — something like a temple that had been left too long with a body in it.

Maren struck a shuttered lantern. Its light found the walls, and Sebastian heard Wren's breath catch.

Bones. Not scattered — arranged. Set into the mortar between the stones in patterns that spiraled inward, spirals within spirals, all of them turning the same direction. Down. Everything in this place pointed down.

"The original builders didn't do this," Maren said quietly. She was an inquisitor; she knew construction the way a butcher knows meat. "This is newer. Decades, maybe. Someone's been... decorating."

"Feeding," Sebastian corrected. "You decorate a room. You feed a thing that lives in it."

They waded on. The tunnel climbed gently, then opened through a broken grate into a wider gallery — and there Sebastian stopped, because the gallery was lined with doors.

Cell doors. Iron, rusted at the hinges, each one fitted with a slot at head height. And from behind them came sounds. Breathing. Shuffling. A low, continuous murmur that might have been prayer or might have been counting or might have been nothing human at all.

Wren pressed close to Sebastian's side. "Are those—"

"Yes."

Sebastian approached the nearest door and looked through the slot.

The cell beyond was small and dry and lit by that same pale glow, seeping up through cracks in the floor. A man sat against the far wall. He had been a big man once — Sebastian could see the frame of him, the broad shoulders — but he was diminished now, emptied, his skin hanging loose as though someone had poured him out and left the shape standing. His eyes were open. They tracked to the slot. To Sebastian's face.

He smiled. It was the worst thing Sebastian had ever seen, that smile — grateful, vacant, and utterly without hope.

"Fresh," the man whispered. "You're fresh. They'll come for you soon. Don't fight it. Fighting makes it hurt longer."

"We're not staying," Sebastian said. "We're getting you out."

The man's smile didn't change. "Out," he repeated, tasting the word like a foreign coin. "There isn't any. There's just down. Everyone goes down, eventually. The King is hungry."

His eyes drifted past Sebastian, up and to the left, focusing on something very far away, and he began to hum.

Sebastian stepped back from the slot. Their hands were shaking, and it wasn't fear.

"Maren. The locks."

She was already moving, pulling a strip of picks from inside her coat — of course she carried them, of course, an inquisitor's habits died harder than inquisitors — and knelt at the door. "These aren't standard cells," she said, working fast. "No garrison keys fit these. These locks are old. Pre-kingdom. Whoever built the fortress didn't build them for prisoners."

"For what, then?"

Maren glanced up, and in the lantern light her face was grim. "For keeping things *in* while keeping them *usable*. Like a larder."

The lock clicked open. Sebastian pushed the door, and the big man looked up at them with those empty eyes, and Wren slipped past Sebastian before anyone could stop them and crouched down in front of him.

"Hey," Wren said. "Hey. Look at me. What's your name?"

The man blinked. "Name," he said.

"Your name. Everyone has one. Mine's Wren."

A long silence. Then, slowly, like a man surfacing from deep water, the big man's eyes focused. "Corvin," he said. "Corvin Tideborn. I had a boat. The *Kestrel*. Did they—" His gaze sharpened suddenly, and he grabbed Wren's wrist, not hard, but desperate. "Did they take my crew? Tall fellow, gap teeth, called Brann?"

"I don't know," Wren said. "But we're going to find out. Can you walk?"

"I can walk." Corvin hauled himself upright using the wall, swaying, and Sebastian saw the truth of what had been done to him: not wounds, not chains. Drainage. Something in this place had been drinking him, slow and steady, the way a leech drinks, and what remained was a man-shaped remnant who had forgotten his own name until a street child gave it back to him.

"Stay behind us," Sebastian said. "If we're attacked, get down and stay down."

"Attacked by what?" Corvin asked.

Sebastian didn't answer. Because down the gallery, deeper into the fortress, the glow was brightening — pulsing now, rhythmic, faster than before. And beneath their feet, through the stone, through the water, through everything, Sebastian felt it: attention. Vast and slow and rising, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

It had noticed them. Or rather — it had noticed *Wren*. The child's blood sang in a key the thing below recognized, and the whole drowned weight of Karstholm was beginning to lean toward them like a plant toward light.

"Maren," Sebastian said quietly. "How many cells in this gallery?"

She counted doors in the lantern light. "Thirty-one."

"Open all of them."

* * *

It took time they didn't have. Some of the taken couldn't stand; those, Wren and Corvin supported between them, forming a straggling column of the emptied. Some wept. Some laughed, high and terrible. One woman refused to leave her cell and had to be carried, thrashing and calling out a name no one answered to.

And through it all, the glow brightened. The pulse quickened. Somewhere below, water began to move — really move, for the first time, a slow churn like something swallowing.

Sebastian counted heads as they formed up at the gallery's seaward end. Twenty-two alive out of thirty-one cells. Nine cells empty, their occupants already gone down, wherever down was. Twenty-two people who had been poured out and left standing.

Twenty-two reasons.

"This way," Sebastian said, and led them toward the flood-tunnel, and felt the storm rise in their chest like a tide answering the moon.

Rescue had become war. Fine. Sebastian Okonkwo had been bred for war — they had simply spent twenty years pretending otherwise.

Behind them, far below, something vast exhaled, and the water in the tunnel began to rise.
