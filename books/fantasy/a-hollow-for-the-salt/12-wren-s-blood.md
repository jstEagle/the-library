## 12. Wren's Blood

The records room of Karstholm had been a chapel once. Maren could tell by the vaulted ceiling, the stubs of stained glass still clinging to their leaden frames, the way the light came down in dusty columns that wanted to be holy and wasn't anymore. Someone had built shelves where the pews had been, and someone else had filled those shelves with the crown's worst arithmetic: ledgers of names, manifests of bodies, requisitions stamped with seals she'd spent her life believing meant something.

She sat at a table made from a door laid across two barrels, and she read by candlelight because the fortress's drowned heart drank the daylight before it reached this deep.

Sebastian stood watch at the door. They hadn't spoken in an hour. Wren was asleep — actually asleep, curled on a pallet of sailcloth in the corner, one arm flung over their eyes, breathing the small steady breaths of a child who had finally decided a place was safe enough to stop listening in. Maren kept glancing over to confirm it. She told herself it was tactical. A rested asset moved faster. That was the language she'd been taught to use, and tonight the language felt like a coat that no longer fit across the shoulders.

The ledger in front of her was bound in grey leather gone soft with damp. Its spine read, in faded gilt: *Tidal Succession — Restricted, by Order of the Lord Harvester.*

Lord Harvester. Not purge commander. The title Aldery used in Greyharbor — Lord Aldery, Commander of the Gifted Purge — was the public face. This was what he answered to, or perhaps what he *was*, when the doors were shut and the sea came close.

Maren turned the page with two fingers, as if the paper might bite.

It was a breeding record.

No — not quite. She forced herself to look at it plainly, the way she'd been trained to look at anything, because looking away was how lies survived. It was a *lineage* record. Names going back nine generations, each entry annotated in a clerk's careful hand: *gift confirmed, grade three. gift confirmed, grade five. no manifestation — retained for stock.* Retained for stock. People described in the same flat terms a horse-breeder would use, except a horse-breeder would have shown more tenderness toward the mare.

And threaded through the generations, every third or fourth name, a small mark in red ink: a circle with a line through it, like an eye crossed out. She'd seen that mark before. Three days ago, on a scrap of Wren's clothing, stitched into the hem by some mother or grandmother who had probably never known what the stitch meant — only that her own mother had told her never to leave it off.

*The old families,* Sebastian had said, when Maren showed them the scrap. They'd gone very quiet, in the way of people standing at the edge of a drop. *Storm-lines. The first gift. Before the crowns, before the councils. The blood that came up out of the marshes when the world was young and wet.*

Maren turned another page and found the summary she'd been dreading since she'd understood what kind of book she was holding.

It was written in a different hand than the rest — older ink, a heavier pressure, the letters of someone accustomed to being obeyed rather than copied. A preamble, the way a legal document has a preamble, laying out its own justification so that no reader could later claim ignorance:

*Let it be set down plainly, for those who come after: the King beneath the salt does not wake to power alone. Power is common. The marshes breed it like minnows. What wakes Him is mingling — the oldest blood poured fresh against the royal line, given freely or taken by force, it matters not to Him, though the manner decides whether He rises gentle or rising hungry. Nine times in the long centuries we have fed Him the near-kin of kings. Nine times He has stirred and slept again, unsatisfied, for the old blood thins. We are down to the last vessels of it. When the final vessel is found, the harvest completes, and the Kingdom enters its true inheritance.*

*Final vessel.* Maren read the phrase four times. Her eyes kept snagging on it the way a boot snags on a root.

Then she did what she had been avoiding doing since sundown. She looked at the last page of entries, the current generation, the living names — and there, second from the bottom, in ink fresher than everything around it, added within the last season by a hand she recognized because she had watched it sign death warrants in Greyharbor:

*Wren — taken from the Saltmarsh road, autumn. Grade unmeasured. Lineage confirmed by hem-mark and blood-scruple. STATUS: THE VESSEL. Do not harm. Do not drain. Deliver whole to the Deep Chamber upon conjunction of tides.*

Below it, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, almost tender: *Conjunction falls at the dark of the coming moon. Eleven days.*

Eleven days.

Maren sat back. The candle flame leaned in a draft that shouldn't have existed this far underground, and somewhere above them, through fathoms of stone and black water, the sea made a sound like something turning over in its sleep.

She thought about waking Sebastian. She thought about it for a long time, and here is what stopped her: not strategy. Shame.

Because Maren Vale had signed papers too. Not these papers — never these, she'd have sworn it on anything you asked her to swear on — but papers in the same grammar. *Subject transferred. Subject processed. No further inquiry required.* She had believed, right up until the night she found her own report rewritten above her signature, that the grammar meant justice. And now she was sitting in the enemy's archive learning that the enemy had a longer memory and a more patient grammar than any inquisitor's oath, and that the child sleeping ten feet away had been entered into a ledger before Maren ever learned their name.

If she woke Sebastian and told them, she would have to watch their face do whatever it did. And she had discovered, over the weeks of flight and shared bread and shared watches, that she could not bear to watch Sebastian's face break again. She'd seen it once, at the cliffs above Karstholm, when they'd understood what the glow in the water meant. Once was enough for a lifetime.

So she did the cowardly thing first. She kept reading.

There were appendices. Of course there were appendices; bureaucracies are the same everywhere, even the ones that serve drowned gods. One listed the previous eight vessels — names, ages, outcomes. Every outcome was the same word in the same clerk's tired hand: *Unsatisfactory. Line failed. Vessel consumed without effect.* Consumed without effect. Eight children, spread across three hundred years, each one carried to a Deep Chamber and given to the tide, each one insufficient, each one mourned by no one but the mother whose hem-stitch hadn't saved them.

The ninth entry was blank. Waiting. Dated eleven days hence.

Maren closed the ledger. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd have expected shaking. Instead there was a heat behind her sternum, low and slow, like a forge banked for the night, and she understood distantly that this was fury — real fury, not the performative zeal she'd been issued with her seal, but the private kind, the kind that doesn't announce itself, the kind that waits.

"Sebastian," she said quietly.

They turned from the door. In the candlelight their face was all planes and shadow, twenty years of hiding carved into it, and Maren saw that they already knew. Of course they knew. You don't stand watch outside a records room for an hour while someone reads a grey leather book and fail to do the arithmetic.

"How bad," Sebastian said. Not a question. A request for confirmation.

"She's the last one." Maren heard her own voice go flat, the inquisitor's cadence, the voice she used for verdicts. "Eight before. Three hundred years. Royal blood against the old lines, over and over, and every time it failed because the old blood had thinned too far. Wren's line didn't thin. The hem-marks — the mothers kept it alive without knowing why. Stitched into every garment, generation after generation, a chain of women passing down a secret nobody remembered the reason for." She pushed the ledger across the table. "Aldery didn't start the purge to cleanse the kingdom. The purge *is* the search. Every gifted person taken was either fuel or a filter — most of them drained to feed the fortress, and the rest screened for the lineage. Wren isn't collateral. Wren is the *point*. Everything else — the criers, the registrations, the pyres in the public squares — that's theater to keep anyone from asking why certain children get taken whole."

Sebastian picked up the ledger. They read fast, lips moving slightly, and Maren watched the color leave their face in stages, like a tide going out.

"The dark of the moon," they said.

"Eleven days."

"And the conjunction—"

"Is when the Deep Chamber can be opened. I checked the tidal tables in the harbor master's annex before we came down. It's the one window in the season. If we're not out of this fortress with Wren before then—" Maren stopped. Finished sentences have weight, and she couldn't lift that one yet.

Sebastian set the book down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you want to throw through a wall. Then they walked to the corner and stood over the sleeping child for a long moment, and Maren watched their shoulders, and their shoulders said everything their face refused to.

When they spoke, they didn't turn around. "I was exiled to keep me from this place. My council knew what Karstholm was. They let me believe I'd done something shameful, let me carry it twenty years, because a storm-caller near the King might bind him — or crown him — and they couldn't risk which way I'd choose." A breath. "And the whole time, they were right to be afraid. Because I'm standing here now, and I'm looking at that child, and I am thinking about what I would burn to keep them from that chamber. Anything. Anyone. So yes. They were right."

"You're not them," Maren said. "You're not the thing in the water."

"No." Sebastian turned, and their eyes had lightning in them, banked but present, the first time Maren had seen it there since the tunnels. "But I'm the only thing between Wren and the thing in the water, and I need you to understand what I'm about to say, Maren, because I'm only going to say it once. Nobody tells Wren. Not the vessel part. Not the chamber, not the conjunction, not the eight children who came before. Wren is eleven years old and has already lost everyone twice over, and I will not have their last weeks — however many we win — spent carrying a ledger entry with their name in it."

"That's a burden," Maren said slowly, "and burdens carried alone—"

"*Nobody tells Wren.*"

The words cracked like ice giving way. Down in the corner, the child stirred, murmured something about gulls, and went back under.

Maren held Sebastian's gaze and felt the old itch under her skin — the duty-itch, the wound she'd been touching since the day her report came back rewritten. Only now she understood the wound had never been about broken rules. It was about this: good people deciding, alone, in candlelit rooms, what other people weren't allowed to know. She'd served an institution built on exactly that decision, stacked ten thousand times, called wisdom.

And she was about to make it anyway. Because Sebastian was right, and because the alternative was watching a child rehearse their own funeral.

"All right," she said. "Nobody tells Wren. But hear my condition, storm-caller, and it's not negotiable. We don't decide alone either. There are elders in the Saltmarsh who've kept the old rites alive for generations — they'll know things about the binding that aren't in this book, things the harvesters never bothered to learn because they only ever wanted the waking. When we're clear of this fortress, the three of us sit down with them, and the plan gets made in the open. Whatever it costs, whoever pays it — that gets decided by more than two exhausted people and a candle."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind their eyes — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of trust, which in Maren's experience was the same emotion wearing different clothes.

"Agreed," they said.

Maren nodded and reached to snuff the candle, and that was when Wren spoke from the corner, small and sleep-blurred and absolutely awake:

"Tells Wren what?"

Neither of them moved. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if it too had stopped breathing.

Wren sat up, dragging the sailcloth blanket around their shoulders like a cloak, and looked from Maren to Sebastian with the particular terrible clarity of children who have learned to read adults the way sailors read weather. "You've been talking quiet for an hour," they said. "Quiet-talking means bad news. Grown-ups do it when they think you're asleep." Their chin came up, and Maren saw the hem-stitch at their collar, the little crossed-out eye, and thought: *a chain of women, and it ends here, in a drowned chapel, pointed at me.*

"There's a book," Wren said. "Isn't there. About me."

The silence stretched. Sebastian opened their mouth — Maren could see the lie assembling itself, gentle and well-made, the kind of lie that love builds when it's scared — and something in Maren rose up and got there first.

"Yes," she said. "There's a book about you."

"Maren." Sebastian's voice, sharp with warning.

"They asked." Maren didn't take her eyes off the child. "And I have spent my whole life watching institutions decide that children are better off not knowing, and I watched where that road goes. So no. Not this. Not with her." She crouched, slowly, bringing herself down to Wren's level, and chose — deliberately, knowing the cost — the truth shaped for a child rather than the truth shaped for a ledger. "There are people who want to use your blood for something old and bad. We found their plans. We're going to stop them. That's all you need to know tonight, and I'll tell you more tomorrow, I promise, because you deserve to hear it from us and not find out the way I found out — alone, in the dark, from a book."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "Am I the important one? In the book?"

Maren's throat closed. Beside her, Sebastian had gone very still.

"You're the one we're not letting them have," Maren said. "That's what matters. The rest is their arithmetic, and we're burning their ledgers, all of them, starting with this one."

Wren looked at the grey leather book on the table. Looked at Sebastian. Looked at the candle, and the dark doorway beyond it, where the sea breathed its slow sleeping breath through fathoms of stone.

"Good," the child said finally, with a fierceness that had no business fitting inside such a small body. "Because I'm not going in any water. And when we burn it—" a jerk of the chin at the ledger, "—I want to watch."

Above them, faint but unmistakable, the fortress groaned. Not settling-stone groaning. Something longer, slower, a sound with intention in it, and the candle flame bent hard sideways though there was no draft, and in the black water somewhere below the Deep Chamber, eleven days early or not, something ancient rolled toward the surface of its long sleep — drawn, perhaps, by the simple fact of proximity, the oldest blood in the world sitting ten feet above its resting place, warm and alive and refusing.

Sebastian blew out the candle.

In the dark, their hand found Maren's shoulder and squeezed once — *we move tomorrow, we move at dawn, we move now if we must* — and Maren squeezed back, and the three of them sat together in the blackness listening to the sea listen to them.
