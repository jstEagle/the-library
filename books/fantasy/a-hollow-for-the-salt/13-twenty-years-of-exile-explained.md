## 13. Twenty Years of Exile, Explained

The old man in cell forty-one had been dead for three days.

Sebastian knew it the way they knew weather — not with the eyes but with something older, something that lived in the marrow and read pressure like scripture. The cells of Karstholm's lower tier were drained near to nothing, their gifts siphoned off in slow silver threads through the walls, and a body without a gift was just meat waiting for permission to stop. Cell forty-one had gotten its permission.

Except the old man opened his eyes.

"Storm-born," he said, and his voice was dry as a rope left out in salt air. "I'd know that hum anywhere. You're humming right now, girl. Boy. Whatever you're calling yourself this decade."

Sebastian stood very still in the corridor, one hand on Wren's shoulder, keeping the child behind them. The light Maren had conjured from a stolen lantern guttered against wet stone. Around them, the freed prisoners of the upper tiers were moving toward the tunnels in a ragged, whispering river, guided by marsh-folk smugglers who knew these flooded ways better than the crown ever had. But here, in the deep tier where the drained ones lay, there was only dripping water and the old man's voice.

"I'm not—" Sebastian started.

"You're an Okonkwo." The old man laughed, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing shook his whole ruined frame. "You think I wouldn't know? I sat across a council table from your grandmother for eleven years. I voted on your exile, child. I signed my name to it."

The words went through Sebastian like cold water through a cracked hull.

Behind them, Wren made a small sound. "Seb?"

"It's all right," Sebastian said, which was a lie, and Wren knew it was a lie, and let them say it anyway. That was the kind of trust the child gave now — trust that didn't require truth, only presence. Sebastian's chest ached with it.

They stepped closer to the bars. Up close, the old man was worse: skin like parchment stretched over driftwood, eyes sunk back but burning, still burning, some last coal of the gift the harvesters hadn't quite finished taking. His wrists bore the crown's binding-cuffs, iron gone green with rust. He'd been here a long time. Decades, maybe. Long enough for rust to bloom.

"Who are you?" Sebastian asked.

"Tomas Okoye. Once third voice of the Storm Council." He smiled with too few teeth. "Once the man who argued hardest for sending you away."

* * *

Twenty years ago, Sebastian had been told a simple story, because simple stories are what exiles are given so they stop asking questions.

*You called a storm that killed nine people. The council stripped your name. Go east, go far, go where the sea forgets you.*

They had been twenty-two years old and drowning in grief, and grief is fertile soil for guilt. They had believed it. They had carried it through two decades of Greyharbor fog, selling luck-knots to dockworkers, flinching every time thunder rolled over a city that had outlawed it, pressing their gift down and down until it became a coiled thing under the skin, a secret kept even from themselves. Twenty years of believing they deserved the hollow they lived in.

"You lied," Sebastian said now. Their voice came out flat and strange. "There was no storm. There was no—"

"There was a storm," Okoye said. "You did call it. That part was true, or true enough. Nine people died when it came ashore at Vell's Landing, and you were the one who pulled it out of a clear sky, because you were young and furious and someone you loved had just been taken by the crown's first harvest ships." He paused. "That part they cut out of your sentence, didn't they. The reason. The ships."

Sebastian's mouth went dry. They remembered. Of course they remembered — they had spent twenty years trying not to remember. A woman named Isolde, taken from a fishing village in the night, her gift of tide-sense noted in some clerk's ledger, and then nothing, no trial, no word, just an empty house and a village that stopped saying her name. Sebastian had torn a hurricane out of heaven over it. And afterward, when the council came for them, no one had mentioned Isolde at all. No one had mentioned Karstholm. No one had mentioned why the crown was collecting gifted folk in the first place.

"They needed you angry," Okoye said. "Anger makes storms. But they also needed you *gone*, and guilt makes obedience. So they gave you both."

"Why?" The word scraped out of Sebastian raw. "Why send me away? If I was guilty, if I broke your laws—"

"Because of what you are." Okoye gripped the bars, and his knuckles whitened around rust. "Not what you did. What you *are*. Listen to me, child, because I don't have much breath left and less time. Your line goes back further than the council. Further than the kingdom. Back to the ones who sank the Salt King the first time."

The lantern flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

"The Salt King," Sebastian said slowly. They had heard the name only recently — from Maren, pale-faced over rewritten reports; from the marsh elders' coded letters; from the mouths of prisoners who whispered about the glowing water and the shapes that walked beneath Karstholm wearing the faces of the drowned. An ancient power. A hunger under the sea. Something the old kingdoms had buried rather than killed, because some things cannot be killed, only weighted down.

"The first binders were seven," Okoye said. "Seven lines, seven gifts, woven together into a single rite. Six of those lines have thinned over the centuries — married out, burned out, hunted out. But yours never thinned. Yours *concentrated*. Every generation of Okonkwos born truer to the old blood than the last. Do you know why the council never let your family hold seat? Why your grandmother sat at our table for eleven years and was never once named to it?"

"She never spoke of it."

"Because we made her promise not to." His laugh again, terrible and thin. "Your line can do two things, storm-born. It can bind the Salt King — complete the seventh weave, renew the weight, keep him sleeping another thousand years. Or it can wake him. Crown him. Give him a living anchor in the world instead of a grave. Your blood is a key that turns both directions. And twenty years ago, the crown learned it too."

* * *

Wren had crept forward despite everything, the way children do, and was crouched at the edge of the lantern light, watching the old man with the fierce unblinking attention of someone memorizing a story they intend to keep forever. Sebastian should have sent them back up the tunnel. Didn't. Some truths belonged to the found family whole, or not at all.

"The crown learned," Sebastian repeated. "Learned what?"

"That we existed. That a storm-caller of the old blood was living in reach of the coast." Okoye's eyes flicked to Wren, then back. "It wasn't the crown that told them, mind. It was us. One of us. A councilman who thought the King should be woken — thought a god under the sea would make the gifted safe forever, make us kings ourselves. He sold your family's location for a seat at the new order. We caught him before he could sell more, but the damage was done. The crown's harvesters started looking for you specifically. Not any storm-caller. *You.*"

"So the council—"

"Exiled you. Yes." He said it plainly, no apology in it, and Sebastian found they respected that more than groveling. "We could have hidden you. We tried hiding others; the crown found them anyway, eventually, one by one, because hiding is a thing that ends. Exile doesn't end. Exile is a door that stays shut behind you. We took your name, your standing, your right to return — we made you worthless to them, a criminal among criminals, a ghost. And we made you believe you deserved it, because a fugitive who thinks she's being punished runs in straight lines. A fugitive who knows she's being *protected* starts asking who from, and starts going back."

Sebastian leaned against the wall. The stone was cold and wet and real, and they needed it to be real, because everything else had just come loose from its moorings.

Nine people dead at Vell's Landing — that was theirs. That storm was theirs, called in rage and grief, and no rewriting of history would lift it from them. But the twenty years after — the fog city, the luck-knots, the loneliness dressed up as contentment, the gift pressed flat and starved — none of that had been punishment. It had been a wall. A wall built by people who loved their family enough to be hated by it.

"They let me hate them," Sebastian said quietly.

"Your grandmother wanted to tell you. On her deathbed she nearly sailed east herself." Okoye closed his eyes. "We stopped her. We told her the exile only worked if it was real — if you believed it to your bones. She agreed. Then she cried for a week where none of us could see, and she never forgave us, and neither did I forgive myself. I've had thirty years in this cell to work on that. The harvesters take the gift slow, you understand. Slow enough that you have time to think. Time is the one thing they can't drain."

"What happened to the council?" Sebastian asked. "To the others?"

"Gone. Scattered, dead, harvested." He opened his eyes. "I stayed close on purpose. Someone had to be inside Karstholm when the crown finally moved — and they always meant to move, child. The purge isn't panic. It's a schedule. They've been collecting the six thinned lines for years, and now they're collecting the seventh's key." His gaze went to Wren again, deliberate this time, and Sebastian's whole body went taut as a hawser in a gale.

"Don't," Sebastian said.

"The child has royal blood in them somewhere back — bastard line, hidden line, doesn't matter which. The records in the archive tower say it plain. Old blood plus old gift. A king's descendant and a binder's heir in the same small body." Okoye's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not telling you anything you don't already feel in your teeth, storm-born. You've known since the day you let the little thief walk away with your charm. You don't rescue strays. You recognized your own."

Wren looked up at Sebastian. In the lantern light their face was very serious, very young, and utterly unafraid in the way that terrified Sebastian most about them.

"Is that true?" Wren asked. "Am I a key?"

"You're a kid who steals charms and hates turnips," Sebastian said. "Everything else is just words old men use to make children into tools."

Okoye chuckled, and the chuckle cost him; he sagged against the bars. "Spoken like your grandmother. Listen — both of you, listen. The rite needs a life freely given. That hasn't changed since the first binding. But the first binding wasn't one life. It was seven, freely given together, each carrying the others. The crown wants you to think it's a single sacrifice because a single sacrifice is easier to arrange. Easier to force. Easier to *steal*." His hand shot out through the bars and caught Sebastian's wrist with shocking strength, the last ember of a drained man's will. "Whatever they've told you the rite requires — whatever the elders remember, whatever you've decided you owe — remember the seven. Remember that the weave was never meant to bear its weight alone."

Then his strength was gone, and his hand slid back through the bars, and he smiled at them with his too-few teeth.

"Now go. The alarm bells will start soon — the upper tiers are already loose, I can hear it in the water. And storm-born?" He was sinking down against the wall now, settling in like a man making himself comfortable for a long dark. "When you see the sea glow wrong — and you will — don't look away. Looking away is how it wins the first time."

* * *

They climbed.

The tunnels up from the deep tier were half-drowned, black except where Maren's stolen lantern bobbed ahead, and Sebastian used the gift openly for the first time in the fortress — bending the water aside, holding air pockets steady, hauling Wren through a submerged junction by the collar while the child kicked and swore with impressive fluency. The storm coiled under their skin felt different tonight. Bigger. As if twenty years of compression had been load-bearing, and now that the truth had come out from underneath it, the whole structure was shifting, resettling, becoming something with room in it.

*Exile reframed,* they thought, hauling themselves up a rung of ladder slick with weed. *Still exile. Still mine. But not what I thought.*

They emerged into the upper galleries to find controlled chaos tipping toward the other kind. Freed prisoners everywhere — dozens, more than the plan had accounted for, drawn up out of cells by rumor and hope, limping, weeping, some too weak to walk and being carried. The marsh smugglers were shouting routes. Somewhere above, iron boomed against iron: the fortress waking to what had happened in its belly.

Maren found them by the great stair, seal-broken sword drawn, face streaked with soot and seawater. Her eyes went from Sebastian to Wren to the direction of the deep tier, and she read something in Sebastian's face, because she always did, because that was the uneasy miracle of her.

"What did he tell you?" she asked.

"That my whole life was a wall someone built around me," Sebastian said. "And that the wall's coming down either way."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning Aldery knows we're here. Meaning he's known since we breached the outer locks — this place is a throat, Maren, and we're already swallowed." Sebastian lifted Wren onto their hip, the child too tired to protest, and looked up the stair toward the sound of gathering boots. "Meaning we get everyone out now, or the harvest gets its key after all."

Above them, torchlight bloomed at the top of the stair, and a voice like a headsman's axe rang down through the galleries:

*"Seal the fortress. No one leaves Karstholm."*
