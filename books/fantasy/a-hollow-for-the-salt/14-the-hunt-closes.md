## 14. The Hunt Closes

The alarm began as a bell and ended as a scream.

Sebastian heard it through the stone of the drained cells — a low, groaning toll that came up through the floor like the fortress itself clearing its throat. Then the screaming started, human voices layered over it, and beneath all of it a sound they had been dreading since the moment Maren pried open the first cell door: boots. Hundreds of them. Coming down.

"They know," Wren said. The child stood in the middle of the corridor with a freed fisherman's hand still wrapped around theirs, eyes wide and wet in the witchlight Sebastian had coaxed out of the flooded dark. "They know we're here."

"They knew before we did," Sebastian said. "They just didn't believe it."

Maren came around the corner at a run, her inquisitor's coat gone, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and dark with someone else's blood. Behind her moved a river of people — thirty, forty of the taken, gaunt and gray-skinned, some walking only because the person beside them held them up. Drained for weeks. Bled slow into jars and barrels and whatever vessels Aldery's alchemists used, and still alive, because the gift didn't die easy. It starved. It clung.

"East stair's cut off," Maren said, breath ragged. "Portcullis dropped. They're funneling us toward the deep galleries."

"Then we don't go where they want." Sebastian turned to face the crowd of hollow-eyed survivors and raised their voice the way they hadn't raised it in twenty years — not the voice of a charm-seller haggling over luck-knots, but the voice that had once called thunder across the Vellish coast and made grown soldiers kneel. "Listen to me! The water is ours. Every drop in this rock remembers the sea, and the sea remembers *us*. When I say run, you run. When I say hold, you hold. And when the water comes—" they met Wren's eyes, and something passed between them that needed no words, "—you let it carry you."

A woman near the front, one arm cradled against her ribs, said hoarsely: "You're the storm-caller. The one from the stories. We thought you were dead."

"Everyone thought everyone was dead," Sebastian said. "That was rather the point of this place."

The bell tolled again, closer now, and the boots came louder, and there was no more time for speeches.

* * *

Lord Aldery had not come alone, and he had not come quietly.

He stood at the junction of the deep galleries where three tunnels met, framed by two ranks of purge troopers in salt-white half-armor, and he looked — Sebastian thought, with a strange detached clarity — like a man who had rehearsed this moment his whole life and found reality slightly disappointing. He was younger than his reputation. Broad, ruddy, a commander's jaw under a commander's helm, and eyes that held no cruelty in them at all, which was somehow worse. Cruelty you could hate. What Aldery had was *certainty*, polished smooth as sea glass.

"Sebastian Okonkwo," he said, and the name rang off the wet stone like a coin dropped in a well. "Twenty years I've been told you were ash on the wind. My predecessor's reports said so. The council's records said so." He smiled without warmth. "Lies make such poor mortar, don't they? Everything built on them eventually shows the crack."

"You're standing in the crack," Sebastian said.

Around them, the freed captives pressed back against the tunnel walls. Wren had gone very still at Sebastian's side, small fingers hooked into the hem of their coat. Maren stood a half-step forward, deliberately visible, deliberately unarmed, and Sebastian watched Aldery's gaze find her and flicker — one heartbeat of genuine surprise, quickly buried.

"Inquisitor Vale." His voice softened into something almost gentle. "I offered you a pardon. I want you to understand that what happens next was your choice, made freely, twice."

"I do understand," Maren said. "That's why I'm standing here."

"Then stand aside."

"No."

Aldery sighed, and it was the sigh of a man closing a ledger. He lifted one gloved hand, and the two ranks of troopers leveled crossbows into the crowded gallery, and Sebastian felt the old thing under their skin — the storm they'd swallowed and sat on for twenty years, coiled tight as a fist around their heart — finally, *finally* ask permission instead of forgiveness.

"Last chance, Okonkwo. Walk out. Give me the child. The rest are already dead; they simply haven't stopped moving yet. You can't save them. You can barely save yourself."

"That's where you're wrong," Sebastian said. "I'm not here to save them."

Aldery frowned. "Then what—"

"I'm here to *free* them. There's a difference. You wouldn't understand it. You've never been owned by anything except your own certainty."

And then Sebastian reached down into the water beneath the fortress — the black flooded galleries below, the drowned halls, the whole cold weight of Karstholm's sunken half — and pulled.

* * *

The sea came up through the stone like a verdict.

Not a wave. Not a flood. It rose out of the floors themselves, sheeting up between flagstones, bursting from drainage grates and arrow-slits, a wall of black water that filled the gallery from wall to wall in the space of a breath — and then *stopped*, held there, trembling, a vertical ocean with a corridor of air carved through its heart. Sebastian stood at the center of it with both hands raised and blood running freely from their nose, and every freed captive in the gallery stood dry inside the eye of their making.

The crossbow bolts fired anyway. Forty of them. The water took each one and cradled it, slowing it to nothing, letting it drift down like leaves.

"Run," Sebastian said, through gritted teeth. "*Now.*"

They ran. All of them — the gaunt woman with the broken arm, the fisherman still holding Wren's hand until Maren swept the child up onto her hip, forty bodies splashing through ankle-deep shallows where the held water bled away behind them, chasing Sebastian's will down the western gallery toward the old smugglers' stairs. Sebastian walked last, backward, holding the wall of the sea against forty men who had never in their lives been told *no* by anything they couldn't arrest.

Aldery didn't retreat. That was the terrible thing about him. He waded forward into the shallows, sword drawn, shouting orders — "Fall back to the pump rooms! Seal the bulkheads! *Drown them out!*" — and even retreating he sounded like a man reading scripture.

"He'll flood the lower galleries," Maren gasped, when Sebastian caught up, running now, the storm inside them howling at the cost of what they'd spent. "There are winches — he'll drown his own troopers to get us."

"He'd drown the whole kingdom if it kept his ledger balanced." Sebastian spat red into the water. "Which way?"

"The cistern route. It's madness."

"Everything today is madness. Madness is faster."

* * *

The cistern route was, indeed, madness: a service tunnel that ran beneath the fortress's great reservoir, a vaulted throat of dripping stone where the water pressure above their heads was measured in tons. Sebastian could feel it pressing down, patient and enormous, the way you feel a sleeping animal above a thin roof. They put forty exhausted people in that throat and told them to move fast and touch nothing.

Wren refused to be carried anymore. The child ran at Sebastian's side, breathing hard, and somewhere in the last hundred yards of corridor Wren's hand found Sebastian's again — not for comfort this time. For grounding. Sebastian felt the faintest tremor pass between their palms, a static hum, a tiny answering spark.

"You feel it too," Sebastian said, not slowing.

"It's loud down here," Wren whispered. "Like the water's talking."

"It is. Don't listen too hard. Not yet. When this is over, I'll teach you properly, and you'll learn it slow, and no one will ever put you in a jar for it." Sebastian squeezed the small hand. "That's a promise. I've broken a lot of promises in my life, little wren. Not that one."

Behind them, deeper in the fortress, the bell changed its rhythm — quick triple strokes, urgent, the signal for breach or collapse. Then came a sound none of them mistook: masonry giving way, a long grinding roar, and a rush of displaced air that smelled of disturbed silt and old drowned things.

"They've opened the reservoir sluices," Maren said flatly. "He's flushing us out. Or burying us. With Aldery it's always both."

Ahead, the tunnel forked. Left climbed toward the cliff-face vents — a long crawl, too slow for forty people, a death trap if the water caught them in it. Right descended toward the old smugglers' dock, the one Maren had charted from confiscated records, the one that might still have boats.

"How long?" Sebastian asked.

"At full sluice? Four minutes to fill the low galleries."

"And the dock?"

"Flooded at high tide. Which is now."

Sebastian looked back down the tunnel at the ragged column of the freed — men and women who had been bled for months, who should have been dead, who were running anyway because hope is a muscle and theirs had atrophied but not died. They looked at Wren. At Maren, who had burned twenty years of oath in six days and hadn't once asked for absolution.

Then Sebastian did the arithmetic they had sworn off doing twenty years ago, and hated how easily it came.

"Left," they said. "Everyone climbs. Now. Go, go — Maren, take them, count heads, don't wait for me."

"What are you—"

"Somebody has to argue with the water." Sebastian was already turning back toward the roar building behind them. "It responds better to a firm tone."

"*Sebastian—*"

"Maren." They caught her shoulder, one beat, two. "You got them out of the cells. Get them out of the rock. That's your justice. Let me have mine."

She stared at them — and Sebastian saw her weigh it, saw her calculate lives the way she'd been trained to, and hated herself for it, and chose correctly. She grabbed Wren, who was fighting to stay, who was screaming something Sebastian deliberately did not let themselves hear clearly, and drove the whole column up the left fork into the climbing dark.

Then Sebastian turned alone to face the flood.

* * *

It came as a black horse comes: fast, huge, and utterly indifferent to argument.

The reservoir burst its sluices and the water hit the low gallery like a battering ram, and Sebastian stood in its path with their arms spread and their feet planted and said, aloud, in the old tongue their grandmother had beaten into them before the councils took everything else:

"*Mine.*"

The word cost them. Everything cost them now; the well inside had been drawn down to mud and dregs. But the water heard — the water always heard — and for one impossible instant the flood hesitated, a fifty-ton question mark suspended in the tunnel, foam writhing along its face like a living thing trying to remember whose side the sea was on.

"Go around," Sebastian told it, gasping. "Go *around me*. Take the right fork. Fill the dock. Drown their boats, drown their winches, drown their *ledger* — but these people climb free. Do you hear me? These people are the sea's own, and the sea does not eat its own!"

The water decided.

It split around Sebastian like a river around a stone, roaring into the right-hand passage in a brown avalanche, and Sebastian stood in the eye of it shaking, nose streaming blood, knees buckling, laughing — actually laughing — at the sheer stupid audacity of having commanded a flood and been obeyed. Somewhere far above, through forty feet of rock, they heard the vent grate bang open, heard voices, heard Maren's voice cutting sharp and clear as she hauled people out into the grey coastal light.

Justice, seized outside any courtroom. No trial. No sentence read. Just water and will and forty people breathing open air.

Then the ceiling came down.

* * *

Later — much later, after the coughing, after the digging, after Maren and two of the stronger freed men hauled Sebastian out of the rubble by the arms while Wren screamed their name in a voice that cracked on the second syllable — Sebastian lay on the shingle beach below Karstholm's cliffs and watched the fortress burn.

Not all of it. Just the seaward towers, where the pump rooms and the alchemists' stores had been, where the barrels of harvested gift-blood had sat waiting for shipment. The flood had taken the lower galleries and given them back to the sea; the fire had taken the stores and given them to the sky. Between the two of them, Lord Aldery's great work was drowning in its own machinery.

Aldery himself had survived — of course he had; men like him floated. From the beach they could see him on the upper ramparts, small with distance, directing salvage operations with the same terrible calm. He would rebuild. He would write his report. He would call this a setback, and the crown would believe him, because the crown had built him for exactly this.

But he had lost forty prisoners. He had lost his harvest. And he had lost, Sebastian thought, watching the man's tiny distant figure turn slowly — searching the shoreline, searching for *them* — the comfortable certainty that the hunted were helpless.

"He's looking for us," Wren said. The child sat pressed against Sebastian's side, soaked to the bone, refusing all offers of dry cloaks. "He won't stop now. Will he?"

"No."

"Good," Wren said, with a ferocity that startled them both. "I don't want him to stop. I want him to chase us until he's tired. And then I want—" the child faltered, searching for a word big enough, and settled on the truest one: "—I want him to *know*."

Sebastian pulled Wren close and looked out at the burning fortress and the wrong pale glow still seeping from the waters around its drowned foundations — the glow they'd seen from the cliffs, the glow that meant something ancient down there had begun, very slowly, to open one eye.

"He knows," Sebastian said quietly. "And soon enough, little wren, we'll all know exactly what he woke. Come on. The marsh folk are expecting us, and I intend to arrive alive, insufferable, and late."

Maren helped them up. Her hand stayed on their arm a moment longer than balance required.

"You held a flood," she said. "Alone. On an empty tank."

"I've been saving up for twenty years."

"Don't do it again."

"No promises," Sebastian said — and for the first time in two decades, the promise-breaking felt less like exile and more like being *alive*.

Behind them, deep beneath the burning fortress, the sea glowed a little brighter.
