## 15. Refuge at the Saltmarsh

The marsh didn't want them at first.

That was Wren's first thought, wading through water that came up to their ribs and smelled like old eggs and secrets. The reeds grabbed at their arms like they were checking pockets. Somewhere ahead, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and Maren swore under her breath for the fourth time since dawn.

"Is it much farther?" Wren asked.

Sebastian didn't answer right away. They stood hip-deep in the black water with their head tilted, listening to something Wren couldn't hear — or maybe something Wren could hear now too, if he stopped flinching from it. Ever since the road, ever since the lightning lesson, there had been a low hum at the bottom of everything. Like a bee trapped under a floorboard. Sebastian said that was normal. Sebastian said a lot of things were normal that Wren was pretty sure weren't.

"It's farther," Sebastian said finally. "It's always farther. That's the point."

"The point of what?"

"Of a place that doesn't want to be found." They glanced back, and even soaked to the bone and gray with exhaustion, they managed half a smile. "Keep walking, sprat."

Wren hated that name. Wren had decided about three days into running that they hated it, and had also decided, around the same time, that they'd gut anyone else who tried to use it. Both of those things could be true. Maren had explained that this was called being complicated, and that she was complicated too, and that most people worth knowing were.

The water deepened, then shallowed, then did something strange — it parted. Not dramatically, not like a story. It just seemed to decide, all along a winding line through the reeds, to be somewhere else. A path of wet mud opened where there had been no path, and the hum under the world shifted key, and Wren felt it against their sternum like a hand pressing flat.

Sebastian exhaled slowly. "They know we're here."

"Is that good?" Maren asked. Her hand hadn't left her knife since the checkpoint. Wren suspected it never would again.

"It's either very good," Sebastian said, "or it's over. Either way we stop swimming."

* * *

The village didn't look like anything until it looked like everything.

One moment there were reeds and rotting boardwalks and herons standing in the shallows like bored clerks; the next, Wren blinked and there were houses on stilts, dozens of them, leaning together like old friends at a funeral, their roofs thatched with living grass that moved when there was no wind. Lanterns hung from poles that grew out of the water itself — actual growing lanterns, glass-bellied gourds full of light that pulsed slow as sleeping hearts. People stepped out onto the walkways. Not soldiers. Not criers. Just people: an old woman with eel-gray braids, a man mending a net with fingers that glowed faintly blue at the knuckles, two kids Wren's age staring with the frank rudeness of kids everywhere.

Wren's chest did something complicated.

Because he knew these people. Not their names, not their faces. But he knew the way the glowing-fingered man kept his hands inside his sleeves out of habit, the way the old woman scanned the horizon before she looked at you, the way every single one of them stood like someone who had learned to make themselves small. He'd seen it in every alley in Greyharbor. He'd worn it himself, every day of his life, like a coat.

They're like me, he thought, and then corrected it, because Sebastian would correct him: They're like us.

"You're late," said the old woman with the eel braids.

"We're alive," Sebastian said.

"That's what I said." But her mouth twitched, and she reached out and gripped Sebastian's forearm hard, and Sebastian gripped back, and something passed between them that Wren couldn't read but recognized anyway — the way you recognize a language you don't speak but have heard your whole life.

"Ilsette," Sebastian said. "These are mine. Maren Vale. And Wren."

Mine. Wren felt the word land somewhere under his ribs and stay there, warm as a swallowed coal.

Ilsette looked at Maren the way you look at a snake you've found in your boot. "An inquisitor."

"Former," Maren said.

"There's no such thing as former."

"There is now. I burned my seal in front of Lord Aldery's own courier." Maren's voice was flat, but Wren had learned to hear the thing underneath Maren's flat voice, the way you learn to hear rain coming. "You can check with him, if you like. I'll wait."

A silence. Then Ilsette laughed — one dry bark, like a door slamming — and turned away. "Feed them. The tall one looks like she eats inquisitors for breakfast and is still hungry. The little one looks like nobody's fed him in years."

"Nobody has," Wren said.

"Then we'll fix that," Ilsette said, without turning around, and Wren had to blink fast at the reeds for a second, because apparently that was the kind of thing that could still get him, after everything.

* * *

The food was fish stew with marsh greens in it, and it was the best thing Wren had ever eaten, which he told everyone, twice, and the second time the whole table laughed and the glowing-fingered man — Doss, his name was Doss — ruffled Wren's hair like it was nothing, like touching a kid's hair was just a thing a person did.

Afterward, while the light-gourds brightened for evening and the water outside turned copper and then violet, Wren sat on the edge of a walkway with his feet dangling over the black water and watched the village happen.

And it happened. That was the thing. People came and went on the boardwalks carrying buckets and bundles and each other's children. Someone played a fiddle badly in a house across the channel and someone else yelled at them to stop and neither of them meant it. A woman walked past balancing a tray of candles that lit themselves as she went, flame by flame, casual as breathing. Two old men argued over a chessboard whose pieces moved when they weren't looking, cheating shamelessly at each other.

Nobody hid. Nobody flinched. Nobody made themselves small.

"You're going to fall in."

Maren sat down beside him, careful of her bad knee. She still held her knife, but looser now. Looser was progress. Wren had been keeping track.

"No I'm not."

"You fell in yesterday."

"That was a push. Sebastian pushed me."

"Sebastian says you tripped."

"Sebastian is a liar with nice hair."

Maren snorted, which from her was basically rolling on the floor laughing. They sat in the quiet for a while, watching the lights come up.

"They're not afraid," Wren said. It came out smaller than he meant it to. "Here. Did you notice? Nobody's afraid."

"I noticed."

"In Greyharbor everybody's afraid. Even the people who aren't gifted are afraid. Especially them, maybe." He picked at a splinter on the boardwalk. "I used to think that was just how the world was. Like weather. Like rain." He paused. "Like the thing Sebastian does, actually. Fear. It just falls on everybody and you get used to being wet."

Maren was quiet long enough that he looked over. Her face was doing the thing it did when she was looking at something far away that wasn't there anymore.

"I spent eleven years making sure of it," she said. "The fear. Sweeps, registrations, the lists." Her thumb moved along the knife's spine, back and forth. "I told myself it was order. That fear was just the price of peace, and better they paid it than bled for it." She looked at him then, and her eyes were steady, which somehow made it worse. "Then I saw the ships at Karstholm, and I understood that I wasn't keeping the peace. I was sorting the harvest."

Wren didn't know what to say to that. So he did the thing Sebastian did, which was scoot closer until their shoulders touched, and say nothing at all. Maren let out a breath that shook, just slightly, at the end, and put her arm around him, awkward as a plank of wood, and that was fine. That was fine.

Across the channel, the fiddle started up again, and this time nobody yelled.

* * *

The dreams came, because the dreams always came.

In them he was small again, in the years between his mother going into the tide and Sebastian catching him stealing a luck-knot, and he was hungry in the specific way that hollows you out and makes the world go soft at the edges. In the dream he was standing in Greyharbor's fish market and everyone had turned to look at him, and their faces were wrong — smooth, like eggs, like the shapes the Salt King wore in the drowned fortress — and the crier's voice rang off the walls saying REGISTER REGISTER REGISTER, and Wren opened his mouth to say he had nothing to register, no papers, no name even, really, just Wren, just a bird-name, a scrap-name —

— and woke up gasping in a soft bed in a dry room with a woven blanket and a shell lamp burning low, in a house on stilts above safe black water.

For a moment he didn't know where he was. Then he heard it: footsteps on the boardwalk outside, unhurried. Voices. A dog — an actual dog, first one he'd seen in weeks — barking once and being told to hush. The ordinary sounds of a place where morning was allowed to arrive without permission.

He lay there and listened until his heart slowed.

Later — much later, sun already high and gold on the water — he found Sebastian on the widest walkway, sitting cross-legged with their palms up and their eyes closed, and a dozen marsh kids arranged around them in a ragged half-circle, including the two rude ones from yesterday, who turned out to be named Pip and Sorrel and turned out to be twelve and ten and turned out to be exactly as fearless as they looked.

"—doesn't come from your hands," Sebastian was saying. "Hands are just where it leaves. It starts lower. Here." They touched their own sternum. "Everyone feels it different. I feel weather. Doss feels what things are made of. Ilsette hears lies — don't lie to her, she finds it insulting. What do you feel, sprat?"

"Nothing," said Pip.

"Liar," said Ilsette, passing by with a basket, without breaking stride. Pip went scarlet. The other kids howled.

Wren hung back at the edge of the boards, not quite part of the circle. He'd done the lesson already, on the road, the day the lightning answered his hand — the day the sky cracked open for him like it had been waiting all his life, and Sebastian's face had gone through wonder and terror so fast it was almost funny, almost, if you didn't know what the gift cost now, if you didn't know that the crown wanted what was in his blood enough to build a fortress of drowning for it.

But he stayed anyway. He liked watching Sebastian teach. Sebastian taught like they were handing out something precious and couldn't quite believe anyone wanted it.

"Again," Sebastian said. "All of you. Palms up. Don't reach for it. Just listen for it. It's been listening for you your whole life."

Wren closed his eyes. Put his palms up, even though he wasn't officially in the lesson. Felt for the hum.

And the hum rose to meet him.

Not lightning this time. Something gentler. The water beneath the walkway stirred, all along the channel, a long ripple moving outward from where he sat, and the light-gourds brightened in answer, one by one down the boardwalk, like lamps being lit by someone walking home. The kids gasped. Pip said a word that got him thumped by Sorrel. And Wren sat in the middle of it with his eyes shut and his heart hammering, feeling the whole village notice him at once — and finding, to his enormous surprise, that he didn't want to disappear.

That was new. That was so new it hurt.

When he opened his eyes, Sebastian was looking at him with an expression Wren had no name for yet. Something proud and frightened and fierce, all braided together.

"Well," Sebastian said. "Don't show off."

"You showed off first," Wren said. "Every day. For weeks."

"I'm an adult. My showing off is dignified."

"It's raining on your head right now. You're doing it right now. There's a personal cloud."

"A dignified cloud."

The kids shrieked laughter, and the little cloud over Sebastian's head drizzled on them with great dignity, and Wren laughed until his ribs hurt, and thought: I could stay here. The thought arrived whole and dangerous, a live coal in a dry field. I could stay here forever. Pip and Sorrel. Doss and his glowing hands. Ilsette who hears lies. A bed. A blanket. A dog.

Hope, he was learning, felt exactly like that. Exactly like standing on solid ground for the first time in your life and knowing, in some animal place below knowing, that the ground was borrowed.

* * *

He found Ilsette at dusk, because he'd worked up the nerve, and because there was a question sitting in him that wouldn't keep.

She was on her porch shelling beans into a bowl, and she looked up when he came down the walkway, and she said, "Ask it, then. You've been circling since supper."

"How long has this place been here?"

"Longer than the crown. Longer than the purge. Longer than the word 'purge.'" A bean rattled into the bowl. "My grandmother's grandmother was born on these stilts. Before that, our people lived in the hills, and before that, the coast, and every time the kingdom decided we were a problem, we came here, where the roads drown and the reeds hide what they love." She looked at him. "We survive by being small, child. By being quiet. By never, ever mattering."

"But Sebastian matters."

Something flickered across the old woman's face. "Yes."

"And I matter. You all saw today. The water. The lights."

"Yes."

"So—" His voice caught. He made himself finish. "So we can't stay."

Ilsette set down the bowl. She took his chin in her hand, gentle as anything, and made him look at her, and her eyes were the color of the marsh at evening, green shot through with dark.

"Child," she said, "nothing in this world is free. Not safety, not belonging, not stew." Her thumb brushed his cheekbone. "But listen to me. We have hidden from kings for six generations, and we are still here. Whatever comes down that road — and something will, because you three brought trouble in your pockets the way travelers bring lice — whatever comes, you will not face it alone. That is the whole of what we offer. It isn't forever. It's enough."

Enough. Wren turned the word over all the way back down the walkway, in the gathering dark, past the lit gourds and the bad fiddle and the smell of somebody's bread.

He was halfway to the house he shared with Sebastian and Maren when he heard the dogs start up.

Not one dog. All of them, all along the village, a rising wall of barking, and under it the fiddle stopping mid-note, and doors opening, and Ilsette's voice ringing out sharp across the water: "Lights down. Lights DOWN."

Wren ran. He hit the main walkway as the whole village went dark around him, gourd by gourd, and out at the edge of the marsh, far off across the drowned flats, torches were moving. A line of them. Orderly. Patient. The way torches move when whoever carries them knows exactly where they're going.

A hand closed on his shoulder — Sebastian, pulling him close, their voice low and calm in a way Wren had learned to distrust completely.

"Get Maren," they said. "Tell her there's a rider at the gate under a white cloth. Tell her—" Sebastian paused, and Wren felt them go very still. "Tell her the rider is wearing Aldery's colors. And tell her the rider is asking for her by name."
