## 16. What Maren Owes

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy on a mud-spattered horse who wouldn't meet her eyes.

Maren read the letter twice by the light of the marsh village's single watch-fire. *Come alone. Come unarmed. Come, and be forgiven.* The seal was Aldery's — the crowned gauntlet of the Purge Command, pressed deep enough into black wax to leave a bruise in it. Beneath the formal lines, in his own hand, a single sentence: *You were the best of us, Vale. That is why I am offering this once.*

She almost laughed. Almost.

Around her, the marsh village was waking. Somewhere behind the reed screens, Wren was arguing with an elder about breakfast. Sebastian's voice drifted from the drying racks, low and patient, teaching some child the names of knots that weren't really knots. The smell of woodsmoke and brackish water and frying fish. A whole life happening in the open, unhidden, the way these people had lived for generations under the nose of every law Maren had ever sworn to.

She folded the letter into quarters and put it inside her coat, against her heart, where she used to keep her oath.

"You're going." It wasn't a question. Sebastian stood at the edge of the firelight with two cups of something hot, and they held one out to her like it was the simplest thing in the world.

"I'm going."

"They'll have archers."

"Probably."

"They'll offer you everything you lost," Sebastian said quietly. "And it will look exactly like everything you wanted."

Maren took the cup. Her hands were steady. They had been steady through eleven years of purges, through doors kicked in before dawn, through the faces of people she'd dragged out of their homes in their nightclothes. Steady hands had never once been a sign that she was doing right.

"It won't fit anymore," she said.

Sebastian studied her for a long moment, storm-grey eyes catching the firelight. "No," they said finally. "I don't suppose it will." They touched her shoulder — brief, warm, gone — and walked back toward the sound of Wren's voice.

Maren drank the tea, saddled the marsh pony no one would miss, and rode north toward the road and whatever was left of her name.

* * *

Aldery had chosen the site like a man who understood theater: the ruined tollhouse at the crossroads above the saltmarsh, its stone walls still standing, its roof long since burned away so the grey morning sky became the ceiling of the room. He sat at a table that had been carried out from Greyharbor — she recognized the grain of it, the Inquisitors' Hall mark burned into one leg — as if he'd brought a piece of the law itself out here to sit in judgment on the wild country.

Two guards flanked him. Four more at the door. Archers on the broken wall, as promised. And beside the table, in a chair too fine for it, a scribe with fresh parchment and a pen already inked.

"Maren Vale." Aldery didn't rise. He never rose; rising was for equals. "You look terrible."

"The marsh doesn't agree with me."

"No," he said. "I imagine nothing agrees with you anymore. Sit."

She stayed standing. It was a small rebellion, and he noticed it, and let it pass, which told her how confident he was. Confident men could afford generosity.

"You've read my letter."

"I've read your letter."

"Then you know what I'm offering." He slid a document across the table. The scribe's pen hovered. "Full pardon. Not just for the desertion — for everything. Your rank restored. Your seal restored. A commission in the new order, when the purge concludes and there is a kingdom left worth administering. You'd have a hand in shaping what comes after, Vale. Men twice your age would kill for that chair."

She looked at the document without touching it. The wax seal gleamed. The language was beautiful — she'd helped draft documents like it, once, in the years when she still believed the words meant what they said.

"And in exchange?"

"The child." Aldery leaned back. "Wren. Delivered alive to Karstholm within the fortnight. You know where the boats go. You've seen the fortress. You'll deliver the child to the water gate, and you'll walk away clean."

"Alive," Maren repeated.

"For now." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty she could have fought. This was administration. "Vale, listen to me. I've watched you since you were nineteen years old and too clever for your uniform. You are not a traitor. Traitors believe in something else. You believed in the law, and the law failed you, and you've been floundering ever since like a swimmer whose boat sank under her. I'm offering you a boat."

"The law failed *them*," Maren said. "The people we took. The ones in the cells at Karstholm, drained white as bone. Did you see them? Did you go down into the tunnels and look at what your harvest actually is?"

Something moved behind Aldery's eyes — not guilt. Irritation. "I've seen the reports."

"The reports are wrong. You know they're wrong. You signed orders based on numbers that don't add up, and somewhere between your desk and those cells, human beings became cargo." She heard her own voice rising and made herself stop. Made herself breathe. "What wakes down there, Aldery? What are you actually feeding?"

For the first time, the commander of the Gifted Purge looked tired. "You think I don't ask myself that?" he said. "You think I sleep well? There are things older than the crown, Vale, things the first kings bargained with to hold this coast, and the payments came due whether or not anyone remembered signing. I am paying a debt this kingdom has owed for nine hundred years. I did not incur it. But someone must settle it, and I would rather it be settled with strangers' blood than with the realm's." He spread his hands. "That is statesmanship. It is ugly, and it is necessary, and history will call it wisdom if it works."

"And if it doesn't work? If the thing under Karstholm takes the blood and wakes anyway and wants more?"

"Then it will want more regardless of what you choose today." His voice hardened. "Your conscience changes nothing except whether you drown with the rest of us or stand on the shore afterward. Deliver the child. Be forgiven. Help me build whatever can be built on the far side of this. Or—" he gestured at the marsh beyond the ruined walls, the grey water, the hidden village, "—go back to hiding in the reeds with a storm-caller and a stolen key, and be hunted until the hunting ends the only way hunting ends."

The scribe's pen scratched. Writing it all down, every word, the offer and the threat, so that later there would be a record proving she'd been given every chance.

Maren looked at the pardon again. Really looked.

She thought about the first purge she'd ever led, in the fishing quarter of Greyharbor, a woman with river-gift hands pulled sobbing from her own kitchen while her neighbors watched from windows and did nothing, because the law had told them doing nothing was righteousness. Maren had been twenty-three. She'd written the report herself. She'd written *subject processed without incident*, and the words had tasted like ash even then, and she had swallowed the ash because the alternative was admitting that eleven years of her life had been spent feeding people into a machine.

She thought about the cellar beneath the abandoned chapel, and the small warm light Sebastian had made — the first magic she'd ever seen that wasn't being used to hurt anyone. Just light. Just wonder, after twenty years of starvation.

She thought about Wren, asleep against Sebastian's shoulder on the road out of Greyharbor, one small hand fisted in the storm-caller's coat like a person holding on to the only solid thing in a flood.

*Subject processed without incident.*

"Aldery," she said. "Do you know what I owe?"

He frowned. "This isn't about debts—"

"Everything I did for eleven years, I did because I believed the law was the shape of goodness. That if I followed procedure, if I filed the right forms, if I kept my hands steady, then whatever came out the other end was justice. That was my payment. That was what I collected instead of a soul." She picked up the pardon at last. The vellum was heavy, expensive, real. "And then I saw the cells under Karstholm, and I understood that the machine I served grinds finer than any of us ever admitted, and that my steadiness was part of the grinding. So yes. I owe a debt. But not to you."

"Vale—"

"Not to the crown. Not to the law. To every person I processed without incident." She turned toward the brazier at the edge of the ruin — the one the guards had lit against the morning chill, coals glowing orange in the grey light. "You asked me to deliver a child to the water gate. Here is my delivery."

She fed the pardon into the coals.

It caught slowly — good vellum resists — and then all at once, the crowned gauntlet curling black, the beautiful language blooming into flame, heat washing across her face. The scribe stopped writing. One of the archers shifted on the wall. Aldery sat very still, watching his offer burn in front of him, and Maren watched him watch it, and felt something in her chest come loose that had been locked since she was nineteen years old.

"There," she said softly. "Delivered."

"You understand what you've done." Aldery's voice was flat now. All the warmth of the offer gone, and underneath it, the thing that had always been there. "There is no second letter. There is no trial. From this hour you are a fugitive of the purge, and the purge does not negotiate, and it does not forget. Every inquisitor in the kingdom will carry your face."

"I know."

"You'll die in a ditch, Vale. Or worse — you'll die in one of my cells, and I will not be able to protect you from what happens down there, because you've just made yourself cargo." He rose at last, finally, the way men only stand when they've stopped pretending you're a person. "Was it worth it? Burning eleven years of service for one child you've known a month?"

Maren considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty, because they had served together a long time and lies between old colleagues are a kind of theft.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "you gave me my first seal, and you told me the seal was heavier than it looked, and that one day I'd understand what it weighed. I never understood until this morning." She looked at the last corner of the pardon, curling and bright. "It weighed exactly this. It weighed everything I'd do to keep it. And I am so much lighter now."

She walked out past the guards, past the archers who didn't draw because Aldery hadn't given the word — and she understood, walking, that he wasn't letting her go out of mercy. He was letting her run because a running fugitive leads you home. To the marsh. To the others.

She mounted the pony at the crossroads and did not look back at the tollhouse.

* * *

They followed her. Of course they followed her. She counted three riders in the scrubland within the first mile and stopped trying to lose them by the fourth, because losing them wasn't the point anymore. The point was that they existed, and that Aldery knew it, and that the clock on the marsh village had started running.

She pushed the pony hard anyway, cutting off the road onto the elder paths only the marsh folk knew, doubling through a peat bog that swallowed hoofprints, and came into the village as the light went long and gold over the reeds.

Sebastian was waiting at the gate. Alone. Which meant Wren was somewhere safe, which meant Sebastian had already guessed everything.

"They offered you the world," Sebastian said.

"They offered me a chair at the table where the world gets eaten." Maren swung down. Her legs ached. Her hands, for the first time in eleven years, were shaking — not from fear, from the sheer strangeness of having nothing left to hold. "I burned it."

"I know. We watched the smoke from the ridge." Something moved across their face — not quite a smile, but its foundation stones. "Eleven years, Maren. You burned eleven years in thirty seconds."

"Nineteen," Maren said. "Counting the academy. Counting everything I was before I was anything else." She looked past Sebastian, at the village — the drying racks, the children, the smoke, the ordinary miracle of people living unhidden. "I keep waiting to feel afraid. Properly afraid. I'm a fugitive with no seal, no rank, no name worth anything. I should feel like the ground's gone."

"And?"

"And it feels like breathing," she said, surprised to hear it true. "It feels like the first full breath in years."

Sebastian stepped aside and let her through the gate.

Inside, the elders were already gathered around the great fire, and their faces told her the news had outrun her. An old woman with river-light in her eyes looked up and said, "The commander's riders were seen at the north causeway. A dozen. Maybe more. They move at dawn."

"Dawn tomorrow?" Maren asked.

"Dawn today," the old woman said. "They're already coming."

And from the doorway of the longhouse, small and fierce and wide awake, Wren looked at Maren with eyes too old for the face and said the thing that settled it, the thing that made the burning worth every ember:

"Then we fight. Right? We're not running this time. We're going to *fight*."

Behind the child, far to the north, over the drowned fortress on the horizon, the sea had begun to glow.
