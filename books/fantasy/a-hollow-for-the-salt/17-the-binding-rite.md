## 17. The Binding Rite

The elders came for Sebastian at dusk, when the marsh light went the color of old pewter and the reeds began their evening whisper.

There were three of them, which felt right. Old magic liked threes. The eldest was a woman called Sefa, bent as a heron, with eyes the pale grey of a sky that had forgotten how to rain. She had led them through the saltmarsh on the second night of their flight, through channels no soldier could chart, and she had not once asked what they carried with them. She had only looked at Wren — at the boy's sleepless face, at the faint blue shimmer beneath his skin when he dreamed — and said, *Ah.*

Now she stood in the doorway of the reed-house where Sebastian slept, and behind her the other two waited with the patience of people who had been waiting a long time.

"You've seen it," Sefa said. Not a question.

"I've seen the water glow." Sebastian rose from the cot. Their joints ached the way they always did now, an ache like weather coming. "I've seen what's left of the ones they drained."

"Then you know why we're here." Sefa stepped inside. The house was one room, all woven reed and driftwood, smelling of peat smoke and dried fish and the particular clean rot of the marsh. On the wall hung charms — real ones, worked openly, the first Sebastian had seen displayed without shame in twenty years. A knot of braided cord. A bird skull painted with white dots. A storm-glass gone dark as ink.

Sebastian hadn't made anything like that since Greyharbor. Hadn't dared.

"The rite," they said. "You want to teach me the rite."

Sefa lowered herself onto the stool by the cold hearth with the care of someone whose bones had opinions. "We want to teach whoever is going to use it. That may be you. It may be no one. But you should know what your grandmother's people knew, before the councils scattered us." She looked up, and her pale eyes were terribly kind. "Sit, storm-caller. This will take until morning."

* * *

It took until morning.

The other two elders were brothers, Odo and Pellin, and between the three of them they held the rite the way a rope holds a boat: frayed in places, knotted in others, but holding. They spoke in turns, sometimes finishing each other's sentences across two hundred years of accumulated memory, and Sebastian sat with their hands flat on their knees and listened the way they had once listened to the council chambers of Karstholm — hungry, afraid, unable to stop.

"The King is not a god," Odo said. He was the younger brother, or the less bent one, anyway, and he paced as he talked, restless as water finding a crack. "That's the first lie. It's older than gods. It's hunger that learned to think. When the first storms walked the earth, it was already under the sea, dreaming, pulling. Everything gifted bends toward it a little. You've felt it. Don't pretend you haven't."

Sebastian had felt it. All their life. The pull at the base of the spine when lightning walked too near the coast. The way the sea seemed to listen when they raised wind. They'd thought it was conscience. It had never occurred to them it might be appetite.

"The binding is not a battle," Pellin said. He rarely moved; he sat like a stone and his voice came out low and even, a counterweight to his brother. "Everyone thinks it's a battle. The last time, three hundred years ago, the binder went down expecting to fight, and the King ate her expectation along with everything else. No. The binding is a *gift*. Freely given, fully meant, held open like a door."

"And the gift is—" Sebastian started.

"A life," Sefa said simply. "A whole one. Not taken, not spent, not lost. Given. The King can take anything it wants. What it cannot do — what it has never once been able to do — is refuse something handed to it freely. Taking is its nature. Receiving is its wound." She folded her hands. "You walk into the deep. You open yourself the way you'd open a fist around a caught bird. And you give it everything. Every year you would have lived. Every storm you would have called. Every morning. And it takes them, because taking is what it does, and in the taking it swallows the hook."

"For how long?"

"Until the binding fails," Pellin said. "Which is to say: forever, if it's done right. The last binder's name is still spoken in the water off Karstholm. Fishermen won't say it above certain depths."

The fire had burned down to embers. Outside, the marsh birds had gone quiet, that particular silence that means dawn is deciding whether to bother. Sebastian looked at their own hands — charm-seller's hands, knot-worker's hands, hands that had spent twenty years pretending to be nothing but clever — and understood, with a calm that frightened them more than any fear could have, that they had known this was coming since the cellar beneath the chapel. Since the small warm light. Since Wren.

Since before Wren, maybe. Since the day the council exiled them and they'd spent two decades telling themselves it was punishment, when all along it had been *placement*. A piece set far from the board so it couldn't be played. Except the board had come for them anyway, and now there was nowhere left to put them except the square that mattered.

"It has to be freely given," Sebastian said slowly. "Fully meant. What does that mean, exactly? Fully meant?"

Sefa's gaze didn't waver. "It means you can't go down there hoping someone pulls you out. It means you can't go down there hating the ones who let you. It means—" and here her voice gentled, which was worse than any hardness, "—it means you have to mean it, child. All of it. The giving and the leaving."

Sebastian thought about Maren's face when she'd burned Aldery's pardon offer — the strange peace of a woman who had finally found the shape of her own spine. They thought about Wren asleep in the next house over, curled around a stolen luck-knot he still wouldn't give back, lightning sleeping in his blood like a banked fire.

They thought: *I have spent twenty years being no one. I can spend one night being everything.*

"I need to learn it," they said. "All of it. Before the tide turns."

* * *

They learned it in three days.

Not the words — the words were simple, almost insultingly simple, an offering in the old tongue that Sebastian's grandmother might have sung over a birth. What took the time was the *shape* of it. The opening. Odo drilled them on it in the shallows behind the village, waist-deep in cold green water, teaching them to unclench.

"You're holding," he kept saying. "Every time, you're holding. Twenty years of hiding, and you hold your gift like a knife in a fist. You have to open. All the way. Like you're handing someone your own heart and trusting them not to drop it."

"I don't trust anyone not to drop it," Sebastian said, teeth chattering.

"Then you'll fail," Odo said cheerfully, "and the King will wear your face out of that water, and we'll all die. Again. Try once more."

On the third evening, in the last light, Sebastian opened.

It was nothing like calling a storm. Storms were *theirs*, obedient, shaped from the outside. This was turning themselves inside out — letting the power run backward, letting the sea look *through* them instead of at them. For one long moment they hung in the cold water feeling utterly transparent, feeling every hidden thing lit up like a lantern, and the marsh around them went still, and somewhere far north, under the drowned fortress, something vast turned over in its sleep and *noticed*.

Then they closed their fist again, gasping, and the moment passed, and Odo gripped their shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"That," he said quietly, "is the door. Now you know where it is."

* * *

The lying started that same night.

It was easier than Sebastian expected, which shamed them. They had imagined lying to Wren would be like lying to a mirror. But Wren was twelve and exhausted and so happy lately, in his fierce guarded way, that the lies slid right into the spaces where trust lived.

"There's a final working," Sebastian told him, sitting on the edge of his cot while he pretended not to be sleepy. "Elders' business. Warding, mostly. Boring. You'd hate it."

Wren gave them the look — the one that said *I survived Greyharbor's streets, you think I can't smell a lie?* — but he was tired, and hope makes children credulous, and after a moment he just said, "Will it hurt?"

"No," Sebastian said, and hated how easily it came. "It won't hurt at all."

To Maren, they lied differently, because Maren couldn't be lied to gently. With her they lied with logistics.

"The rite needs three days of preparation and then it's done," they said, standing in the doorway of her hut while she cleaned a blade that didn't need cleaning. "After that, we move. South, maybe. Or back to Greyharbor, once the purge collapses. There'll be work for a disgraced inquisitor who knows where all the bodies are buried."

Maren looked up. Her eyes were sharp and grey and missed nothing, and for a moment Sebastian thought — *she knows, she sees it, she's going to say it* — and their heart slammed against their ribs like a trapped bird.

But Maren only nodded slowly. "Three days," she said. "And then we decide together where we go."

"Together," Sebastian agreed, and smiled, and the smile held, and they walked out into the dark before it could break.

* * *

The last evening, Sefa found them packing.

Not much to pack. The luck-knots. The storm-glass. Wren's stolen charm, which Sebastian had bought back from him honestly, overpaid for, and which now sat wrapped in oilcloth like the relic it was. They were wrapping each item with the care of someone setting a table for people who would sit down to eat without them, and Sefa watched from the doorway for a long time before speaking.

"You haven't told them."

It wasn't a question either. These marsh elders didn't seem to believe in questions.

"They'll try to stop me."

"Yes."

"And they'd be right to. By their lights." Sebastian tied off the oilcloth. "But the rite doesn't take volunteers by committee, Sefa. One life, freely given. If Wren knew, he'd offer himself — he's twelve and he thinks he's disposable, because the world taught him that. And Maren—" their voice caught, just slightly, "—Maren has paid enough. She burned her whole life in front of Aldery. She gets to keep whatever's left."

"And you?" Sefa said. "What do you get to keep?"

Sebastian looked at the little bundle. At the charms. At the life measured out in knots and bones and small warm magics.

"Twenty years of being no one," they said. "And this. This one thing that's mine. That nobody else can do." They met the old woman's eyes. "You said it yourself. The gift has to be fully meant. I can't mean it if I'm spending my last days watching them plan a future I intend to steal from them. So I lie. Because loving them *is* the meaning. Do you understand? The lie isn't against the rite. The lie *is* the rite."

Sefa was quiet a long moment. Then she crossed the room, slow as tide, and did something none of the elders had done: she touched Sebastian's face, one dry palm against their cheek, the way you'd touch a child or a dying friend.

"My mother bound a river-spirit when I was young," she said. "She told us she was going to visit her sister inland. We believed her for eleven years. Then the river stopped flooding, and we understood." Her hand fell away. "I have hated her and loved her in the same breath for fifty years, storm-caller. I would not wish that arithmetic on anyone. But I will not take this from you, either. It's yours. Cruel and holy and yours." She straightened. "The tide turns tomorrow at moonrise. Come alone. Tell no one. And mean it."

"I will," Sebastian said. And meant that, too.

* * *

Moonrise came up red.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the village with their bundle on their shoulder, looking back at the reed-houses, at the thin threads of peat smoke rising straight into the windless dark. In Maren's hut, a lamp still burned — she kept late hours, sharpening, mending, keeping watch the way she kept everything, close and fierce. In Wren's window, a smaller light. The boy still slept with a candle, a street habit he'd never lose, and Sebastian had stopped teasing him about it months ago because some fears you carry like a charm and hand down like an inheritance.

*I taught him to call lightning,* Sebastian thought. *I taught a twelve-year-old to hold a death sentence in his hand, and I called it a lesson, and I called it love, and both of those were true.*

They turned toward the water.

The path to the deep channel was a mile of black mud and whispering reeds, and Sebastian walked it slowly, deliberately, letting the marsh memorize them. Here: the place Wren had fallen and laughed. Here: the pool where Maren had finally, awkwardly, splashed them back. Here: the flat stone where all three of them had eaten smoked eel while the elders pretended not to watch, and Wren had said, mouth full, *this is what family's supposed to feel like, isn't it,* and Sebastian had said yes, and meant it, and meant it still.

At the channel's edge, the water lay flat and patient as a held breath. Far to the north, past the horizon, Karstholm glowed its wrong glow, brighter tonight, brighter every night, the King stirring toward waking as Aldery's harvest piled up in its cells. Three days, the elders had said, before the harvest reached its fullness. Three days before the hunger woke all the way and came looking for the key in Wren's blood.

Unless someone got there first. Unless someone opened the door.

Sebastian knelt at the waterline and unwrapped the bundle one last time. The luck-knots. The storm-glass. Wren's charm. They laid them in a row on the flat stone, arranged the way you'd arrange things for someone coming home — and then, with fingers that only shook a little, they wrote the note they'd been composing in their head for three days, the note that would buy the others enough confusion to grieve in safety rather than follow in fury.

*Gone to finish the warding. Don't follow. Trust the elders. There's soup in the pot.*

It was a terrible note. It was the best they could do.

They weighted it with the storm-glass, stood, and looked north one last time. The glow on the horizon pulsed, slow, like something breathing in its sleep. Like something that had already begun to dream of them.

"Twenty years of being no one," Sebastian said aloud, to the marsh, to the water, to the two lamps burning behind them in the dark. "One night of being everything."

They stepped into the channel.

The mud took their feet. The water climbed their shins, their knees, their waist, cold as honesty. Behind them, very faintly, a lamp flared — Maren's window, or Wren's, or both — and Sebastian did not turn around, because turning around was the one thing the rite forbade, and because if they turned they would see what was actually happening in those huts: Maren reading the terrible note by lamplight, her face going white and then hard; Wren sitting bolt upright in bed, the candle guttering, saying to the empty room, in a voice cracked with twelve-year-old fury and grief:

*"Don't follow"?*

Because Wren had read the note first. Had slipped out an hour ago, had found the bundle on the stone, had read the terrible words in the terrible handwriting, and had run — not away, but *back*, barefoot through the reeds, toward Maren's hut, toward the one adult whose job it was to stop exactly this kind of thing.

And the last thing Sebastian heard, as the black water closed over their head and the deep channel pulled them north toward Karstholm and the King's long hunger, was not the sea at all.

It was voices. Two of them. Shouting their names into the dark.
