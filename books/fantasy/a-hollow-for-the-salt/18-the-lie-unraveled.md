## 18. The Lie Unraveled

Wren knew something was wrong the way you know a storm is coming before the sky admits it — not by seeing, but by feeling. The air in the marsh village had gone thick and sweet, like the hour before rain, and Sebastian had stopped looking at them.

That was the tell. Sebastian always looked at Wren. Checked their boots were dry, their knots were tied right, that they'd eaten. For three days now, Sebastian's eyes slid past Wren like Wren was a door they didn't want to open.

So Wren did what street kids do. They watched. And they counted.

Sebastian left the elder Maren's hut at dusk on the third day carrying a bundle wrapped in oilcloth — the same bundle they'd spent two nights sewing shut while pretending to mend a net. They walked not toward the shore path but toward the old salt-cutters' causeway, the one that only went one place.

Karstholm.

Wren followed, low and quiet, the way they'd followed cutpurses through Greyharbor alleys back when following was how you stayed fed. The marsh squelched under their feet. Ghost-lights drifted over the reeds, the little warm sparks the hidden gifted used to mark safe paths, and Wren slipped between them without touching any, because touching them made them flare, and flaring lights got people caught.

Sebastian stopped at the edge of the causeway where the mud gave way to old stone. They stood there a long moment, shoulders squared, and said, to the empty dark, in a voice gone rough: "You can come out, sprat. You've been breathing too loud since the second reed bed."

Wren's stomach dropped. Then it hardened into something else.

They came up out of the reeds with their fists already balled. "You're going alone."

Sebastian turned. In the ghost-light their face was older than Wren had ever seen it — older than the charm-seller face, older even than the storm-caller face from the tunnels under Karstholm. "Go back to the village."

"You're going *alone*." Wren's voice cracked on it, and they hated that, hated being small, so they pushed louder. "The rite needs a life freely given. That's what the elders said. That's what you've been sewing into that bundle all week instead of sleeping. You're going to give *yours*."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Wren's eyes were burning and they let them burn. "Don't you dare say my name soft like that. Don't you dare stand there and lie to me with your whole face after everything. You found me stealing a luck-knot and you *let me go*. Nobody lets anybody go. Nobody's ever—" Their breath hitched. "You don't get to leave me the way everyone else leaves. Not you."

Sebastian closed their eyes. When they opened them, they knelt — down to Wren's level, the way they did when Wren was sick with fever on the road, when the checkpoint soldiers nearly found them, every time the world got too big. "Sprat. Listen to me. What I am — what my blood is — it fits the King's chains. Mine alone. If anyone else touches the rite, it could crown the thing instead of binding it. Do you understand? It wouldn't just kill you. It would wake it *hungry*, with your grief as its first meal."

"That's a guess," Wren said. "The elders said shared grief might hold. Maren said—"

"Maren doesn't know." Sebastian's jaw tightened. "And she won't. I told her I was scouting the approach to the fortress. She believed me because I've never lied to her."

"Then you're lying to her too," Wren said, "which means you're lying to both of us, which means you think we're stupid, or you think we're weak, or you think we love you so little we'd let you drown yourself in the dark rather than argue." They stepped closer, mud sucking at their boots. "I'm none of those things."

For a moment Sebastian said nothing. The marsh breathed around them — frogs, wind, the far-off hiss of the sea against the causeway stones. Then Sebastian reached out and took Wren's chin gently in one calloused hand, the way they had the first day, in the chapel, when Wren had been nothing but a stolen charm and a bad cough.

"I have buried everyone," Sebastian said quietly. "My council. My teachers. Everyone who ever stood near me when the storms came. I will not bury you. I would rather the King eat me whole than watch it take one hair of your head. That is not a thing you can argue me out of, sprat. It's the only true thing I have left."

"Then it's a good thing," Wren said, "that I'm not asking you to argue."

They pulled the knife.

Not at Sebastian — never at Sebastian. Wren pressed the flat of it against their own palm, hard enough to sting, and watched Sebastian's face go white as fish-belly. "You said freely given. You said the cost has to be given, not taken. Well. I'm giving. Right here, right now, if you take one more step down that causeway without me, I'll open this hand and bleed into the marsh and let the sea decide whose gift it wants more. Maybe mine's not royal enough. Maybe it does nothing. But maybe it does *something*, and then you'll have died for nothing anyway, and I'll have died angry at you, and that's the worst ending there is."

"*Wren.*"

"You taught me to feel the storm," Wren said, and their voice shook but held. "First lightning answered my hand. You stood there crying and laughing at the same time and you said, *it's yours now, it was always yours.* So it's mine. And if it's mine, then what it gets spent on is *mine* to choose. You don't get to spend my inheritance for me. That's exactly what Aldery tried to do. That's exactly what the purge was." They lowered the knife slowly, palm stinging, heart slamming. "You want to bind the Salt King? Fine. Bind it. But you'll look me in the eye and tell me the truth first, and then you'll decide whether you trust me — really trust me — or whether I was just another stray you were planning to abandon once it got inconvenient."

The words landed. Wren saw them land, saw them go in deep, and felt instantly sick for saying them — but didn't take them back. Some things you couldn't take back, and shouldn't.

Sebastian sat down on the causeway stone like their legs had quit. They put their face in their hands. And Wren, who had never once seen Sebastian cry — not in the tunnels, not when the prisoners recognized them, not when the old storm-caller spoke their exile aloud — heard a sound come out of them that was half laugh and half sob.

"Twenty years," Sebastian said through their fingers. "Twenty years I built myself into a person who could walk into the deep alone. One child with a knife unmakes it in an evening."

"Not a child," Wren said, sitting down beside them. Close. Shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd slept on the cold road, sharing warmth. "Your family. Which means you don't get to make plans for one anymore."

* * *

They argued until the moon set. Truly argued — Sebastian laying out every danger, every way the rite could twist wrong, and Wren countering with everything the elders had said, everything Maren had dug out of the captured records, every scrap of stubbornness a street kid could carry. Somewhere past midnight it stopped being an argument and became a plan, built the way Wren built things: scavenged, patched, held together with knots nobody else would trust but that somehow never failed.

"We split the cost three ways," Wren insisted. "Me, you, Maren. Shared grief. The elders' oldest version of the rite — the one they said the councils abandoned because it was 'inefficient.' Three lives given freely, braided. The King binds to the bond itself. It can't consume what it can't pull apart."

"If it fails—"

"Then it fails with all of us holding the rope," Wren said. "That's still better than you dropping it alone."

Sebastian stared out at the black water for a long time. "Maren will be furious."

"She'll be furious either way," Wren said. "Better furious and alive with us than furious and left behind."

"And if she tries to stop us—"

"She won't," Wren said, with the absolute certainty of someone who had watched Maren Vale burn a pardon offer in front of Lord Aldery's own messenger. "She's done choosing the safe thing. She's ours now."

They were wrong about one part. Maren wasn't furious.

She was already there.

She stepped out from behind the causeway's broken marker post — she must have followed them from the village, or never left, or known somehow, the way she always seemed to know — with her arms crossed and her inquisitor's coat buttoned to the throat and an expression on her face that Wren had last seen directed at a squad of Aldery's soldiers blocking a bridge.

"How long?" Sebastian asked hoarsely.

"Since you told me you were 'scouting the approach.'" Maren's voice was flat as slate. "You've scouted nothing in your life, Okonkwo. You improvise catastrophically and apologize afterward. A scouting trip would have been completely out of character." She came forward and dropped a heavy satchel at their feet. Salt-ward charms from the elders. Rope. Flares. Dried fish. "I packed for three days. I assumed whatever you were actually doing, it would need supplies and it would go badly."

Wren grinned despite everything. "Told you."

"You told me nothing," Maren said, but her eyes flicked to Wren — to the bandage already wrapped around their palm, to the tear tracks drying on their cheeks — and something in her face shifted, softened, cracked. She crouched in front of Wren, the way she never crouched for anyone. "He tried to leave you behind."

"He tried to leave everybody behind," Wren said. "We voted him down."

"There wasn't a vote," Sebastian muttered.

"There's always a vote," Wren and Maren said together, and even Maren's mouth twitched.

Then Maren stood, and the softness folded away, and she was the inquisitor again — except pointed, now, like a blade finally aimed the right direction. "Understand me, Sebastian. I burned my oath. I burned my pardon. Everything I have left in this world is standing on this causeway arguing with me in the dark. If you walk into that water alone, you are not sparing us. You are robbing us. You are deciding our grief for us, and I have had enough men in crowns and robes deciding things for me to last several lifetimes." She held out her hand — the one bearing the scar from the tunnel collapse, the one that had hauled Wren out of the flooding cell at Karstholm. "So. Are we going to bind this thing together, or are you going to make me arrest you? Because I still remember how."

Sebastian looked at the hand. At Wren. At the long black line of the causeway running out toward the drowned fortress, where the sea glowed its wrong, hungry glow.

"The elders will say it's madness," Sebastian said at last. "Three-way rite. Untested since before the councils."

"The elders also hid in a marsh for six generations instead of fighting," Wren said. "No offense to the elders."

"Some taken," Maren said dryly.

Sebastian laughed — a real one, cracked and wet and surprised out of them — and took Maren's hand, and pulled Wren close under their other arm, and for one moment the three of them just stood there on the old salt-cutters' stone with the hungry sea ahead and the marsh behind, holding on.

"Freely given," Sebastian said quietly. "All three. No lies between us from this step forward. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Wren.

"Agreed," said Maren.

They shouldered the packs. They stepped onto the causeway together, three sets of boots on ancient stone, the ghost-lights of the marsh fading behind them and the wrong glow of Karstholm rising ahead out of the water like a wound in the world.

And beneath their feet, far down in the dark where the Salt King dreamed its long hunger, something vast turned over — and began, for the first time in a thousand years, to listen.
