## 19. Karstholm, Second Descent

The fortress woke before we did.

I felt it through the stone at three bells past midnight — a pulse in the walls, slow as a drowned heart, and every gift-marked prisoner in Karstholm felt it too. The cells below us began to sing. Not with voices. With something older than voices: a low harmonic hum that came up through the floodwater and settled into the bones like cold.

Wren sat up in the dark of our hiding niche and said, "Sebastian. It knows we're here."

"I know."

"It's *glad* we're here."

That was the part I couldn't answer. Because Wren was right, and the gladness was the worst thing I had ever felt through my skin — vast and patient and warm as bathwater, a hunger so old it had forgotten it was hunger and thought itself love.

Maren struck flint to the stub of a candle. "Tide turns in an hour. The tunnels drain on the ebb — that's our window down to the holding cells."

"And then?"

"Then we open every door in this place," she said, "and we burn whatever's left of the machinery that keeps those doors shut." She looked at me across the candle flame. "And you know what comes after that. Say it out loud. I want to hear you say it."

"The rite," I said. "At the King's heart. Before dawn."

She nodded once, and didn't say what all three of us were thinking — that the elders' rite wanted a life freely given, and that I had spent two days perfecting the lie that would get me to that heart alone. Two days of small misdirections. A false turn here. A story about a side passage there. Maren watched me now the way she'd once watched suspects, and I understood that my lie was already half unraveled, and that she was letting me keep it anyway, because there was no time for anything truer.

"Stay behind me," I told them both. "Whatever you see down there — however it looks — stay behind me."

* * *

The draining tunnels were ribbed throats of black stone, slick with weed, and we went down them single file with the water sucking away around our ankles. My storm coiled under my skin so tight it ached. Twenty years I had kept it leashed. Tonight I let it off the chain one link at a time, and each link sang.

The first guards found us at the second junction — four of them, lanterns high, and one look at Maren's face made them hesitate, because even now they knew her seal, her stride, her authority. She used the half-second their hesitation bought us. I used everything else.

Lightning is not a weapon, whatever the ballads say. It's a question you ask the air, and the air answers *yes*. I asked it four times. The junction went white, then dark, and when the afterimages faded there was nothing standing but smoke and the smell of ozone and scorched iron.

"Don't look at their faces," Wren whispered.

"They chose this," Maren said, flat. "Every man on Aldery's harvest crews chose it." But her voice had a crack in it, and I heard her filing that crack away to grieve later, if later existed.

We went deeper. And the deeper we went, the more the fortress stopped being a fortress.

It's hard to describe what an ancient power waking looks like from inside its own body. The corridors began to breathe. Not draft — breath. Long slow inhalations that pulled the candle flame toward the deep places, exhalations that pushed it back. Water moved against gravity in thin sheets along the walls, climbing toward the heart-chambers, and where it climbed, shapes formed in it. Faces first. Then hands. Then whole figures, translucent and salt-white, standing in the flooding passages like people waiting at a shore for someone who isn't coming home.

"The drowned," Maren breathed. "The harvest. All the ones they shipped here—"

"No." My mouth had gone dry. "These are older than the harvest. Look at their clothes."

They wore rags of eras nobody alive could name. Corselets eaten to lace. Crowns of woven kelp. They had been coming here, giving themselves here, for longer than Greyharbor had been a city. The Salt King hadn't been imprisoned by these waters. It had been *fed* by them, generation upon generation, freely given, until some council of storm-callers twenty years ago decided the feeding had to stop — and built their dam of one exiled child's absence, and called it peace.

One of the shapes turned toward Wren.

It had a young face. It raised one salt-white hand — not grasping, just *offering*, the way you offer your palm to a bird — and Wren, curse their brave stupid heart, reached back.

"Wren!" I caught their wrist. The shape's hand passed through mine like cold smoke, and where it touched, my skin prickled with frost and longing both.

"It wasn't going to hurt me," Wren said.

"No. It was going to *invite* you. That's worse." I pulled them close. "Listen to me. Everything down here will feel like kindness. That's how it hunts. It doesn't chase. It *welcomes*. You do not accept any invitation in this place. None. Do you hear me?"

Wren's jaw set — that stubborn set I'd first seen over a stolen luck-knot in a Greyharbor market, a lifetime ago. "Then how does the rite work? The binding needs a life freely given. Isn't that just — accepting the invitation?"

The question went through me like a spear. Because it was true. The line between the binding and the feeding was one hair wide, and the only thing on our side of it was choice — real choice, made clear-eyed, by someone who knew exactly what they were walking into.

"We make sure it's ours," Maren said quietly, from behind us. "Not its. That's the whole difference. Come on."

* * *

The holding cells were cut into the fortress's lower ring, behind a gate of green bronze that hummed when I touched it. The lock was salt-magic, crown-work, keyed to blood the crown considered property. Maren broke it with her inquisitor's seal — the last act that seal would ever perform, though none of us knew it yet — driving the sigil into the mechanism like a wedge. The bronze shrieked and split.

What was behind it made Wren cry out.

Rows of cells. Hundreds. And in them, the taken — the fisherman from the Greyharbor docks, the midwife whose name had been read off a crier's list, children barely older than Wren — all of them alive, all of them *drained*, gray-skinned and hollow-eyed, their gifts siphoned off through channels cut into the cell floors. The channels ran downhill, glowing faintly, carrying stolen brightness toward the center of the fortress like veins carrying blood to a heart.

"They're not killing them," Wren said, horror climbing their voice. "Why aren't they killing them?"

"Because dead blood is spent blood," I said. "Aldery wants them alive. Kept. Milked." I put my hands against the nearest cell door and asked the lightning a different question this time — not *yes*, but *open*.

The doors came apart in sequence, one after another down the row, hinges bursting, locks shattering. I walked the corridor pulling life back up through the drainage channels as I went — not much, I'm not that generous a vessel, but enough. Enough that the drained ones lifted their heads. Enough that a woman with a midwife's steady hands took charge of the weakest without being asked. Enough that when I said *"the tide pools above, follow the marsh folk's markers, go now"* they moved, hundreds of them, a river of the half-dead flowing uphill toward the surface and the boats the marsh village had sent.

Maren stood at the junction directing them with her inquisitor's voice, the voice that had once herded the gifted onto ships, using it now for the opposite purpose, and I saw her hands shaking and her spine straight and both were honest.

That's when the alarm sounded — not a bell, but the fortress itself. Every wall in Karstholm lit from within with a slow red glow, and the water in the channels reversed, and somewhere far below, something enormous rolled over in its sleep and began, deliberately, to wake.

* * *

Lord Aldery found us at the third ring, between us and the heart.

He had brought forty men and a caged cart, and in the caged cart were six gift-marked prisoners recaptured from our escaping river, and he stood in front of them with drawn sword and said, in the reasonable tone of a man discussing freight rates:

"The charm-seller. The runaway child. The inquisitor." He smiled at Maren. "Vale. Your pardon letter is still on my desk. It has your name spelled correctly and everything. One more chance."

"You never intended a pardon."

"I intend outcomes." He gestured, and his soldiers leveled crossbows — not at us. At the caged cart. "Here are my outcomes. Walk to the heart-chamber and give the King what it wants, or watch me open these cages and feed it halfway there myself. Either way, dawn sees it awake, and the crown controls the only door it wakes behind. That's all this has ever been. Management."

"You'd starve it awake," Maren said, "and then hold the leash of a god with hostages."

"I'd hold it with *incentives*, Vale. It's how kingdoms work." His eyes found Wren, and there it was — the thing underneath the reasonableness, wet and eager. "Though I'll confess the arithmetic improves considerably with the key delivered intact. Royal blood and the oldest gift. The archivists were very thorough. Shall we?"

Wren stepped forward before I could stop them.

"You don't get to use us," they said. Their voice cracked in the middle and they said it anyway, louder. "You don't get to use *anyone*. That's the whole — that's what all of this is *about*—"

A crossbow bolt left its rest.

I have moved fast exactly twice in my life. The first time, twenty years ago, I had torn a storm out of a clear sky and burned a harbor town's fleet to splinters, and it cost me everything. The second time was in that corridor. I caught the bolt eleven inches from Wren's chest and held it, quivering, in a fist full of answering lightning, and every soldier in that corridor saw the sky live inside a person and forgot, briefly, which of them was supposed to be afraid.

"No more management," I said.

What followed was not a duel. It was weather.

I gave the corridor a storm in miniature — thunderclap pressure that threw men off their feet, rain falling upward, lightning walking along the ranks on legs of white fire, careful, careful, *careful*, because Wren was beside me and the freed captives were behind me and the whole art of my gift, learned over twenty years of starving for it, was precision. Maren waded into the chaos with a captured blade and did the ugly close work that storms can't. Wren —

Wren stood in the middle of it with their arms spread and their eyes closed, and the lightning bent around them like they were its favorite child, and I understood in one lurching instant that the gift in them was not a lesser echo of mine. It was the oldest kind. The kind the King itself would recognize.

Aldery fought his way toward the cart, toward leverage, toward hostages, because men like him always reach for the same lever. He got the cage door open. He grabbed the nearest prisoner — a boy of maybe ten — by the collar and hauled him out as a shield.

"Stand down," Aldery shouted over the storm, blade at the boy's throat, "or I—"

The fortress interrupted him.

The floor beneath the third ring split along a seam no mason had ever cut, and the water that came through it was not water anymore. It was shaped. Dozens of shapes — the drowned, the harvested, centuries of the freely given rising through their own channel-veins, drawn by the scent of a gift being spent so lavishly overhead. They rose around Aldery like a congregation around a pulpit, and they were smiling, all of them, the terrible welcoming smile I had warned Wren about.

Aldery's blade touched salt-water.

That was all it took. The shapes didn't seize him. They *received* him. He screamed something — orders, I think, at the very end, still trying to manage it, still trying to give instructions to the ocean — and then the water folded over him with the gentleness of a tide closing over a footprint, and the boy he'd held stumbled free, and Lord Aldery, architect of the purge, harvester of the gifted, was taken by his own crop.

The soldiers fled. I let them. There was no victory in drowning men who would spend the rest of their lives hearing that smile in the rain.

* * *

The heart-chamber opened off the third ring like a wound.

We stood at its threshold while the fortress shuddered around us, and looked down into a flooded cathedral vast enough to swallow Greyharbor whole. Far below, at the center of the black water, something glowed. It brightened as we watched. It brightened the way an eye brightens when it opens.

"There," Maren said. "The elder's map puts the binding-stone at the chamber's center. The rite has to happen *there*, in the water, at the heart. No shortcuts."

Below us, the glow gathered itself, and through the water we heard it — felt it, in teeth and marrow — the Salt King's voice, if a hunger that predates language can be said to speak. It said, and it said directly, warmly, individually, to each of the three of us:

*Come. You're expected. You've always been expected.*

Wren's hand found mine. Maren's hand found Wren's shoulder.

"Sebastian," Maren said quietly, "whatever you've been planning alone — whatever lie you've been carrying for two days — lose it now. We go down together or not at all."

I opened my mouth to deny it, and the glow below flared, and the whole fortress groaned toward dawn, and there was no time left for lying to the people who loved me.

So instead I said the truth, or the beginning of it: "There's something you both need to know about how the rite ends."

And we started down into the water.
