## 21. After the Flood

The water took three days to leave.

Maren watched it go from the seawall at the Saltmarsh, hour by hour, the way you watch a fever break in someone you'd already begun to mourn. It didn't retreat so much as surrender — sliding back off the fields in long grey sheets, pooling in the ditches, draining toward Karstholm with a sound like a city exhaling. Beneath it, the land emerged mud-slick and shining, every furrow intact, every fence post still standing, as if the flood had been less an act of destruction than a procession that had passed through and remembered, at the last moment, its manners.

She hadn't slept. None of them had, really. Sleep was for people whose bodies believed they were safe, and hers still woke her every hour with her hand already closed around a hilt that wasn't there.

"You're going to fall in," Wren said, appearing at her elbow with the particular silence of children raised on streets.

"I'm standing fine."

"You're swaying." The kid held out half a loaf of bread, torn rather than cut, still warm from whatever communal oven the marsh folk had fired up the moment the water cleared their hearths. "Sebastian says you have to eat or you'll get mean."

"Sebastian said that?"

"Sebastian *thought* it very loudly." Wren grinned — a gap-toothed, salt-crusted grin, and Maren felt the ache of it somewhere under her ribs. Three days ago this child's blood had been the most dangerous substance in the kingdom. Now they were standing on a seawall arguing about bread.

She took the loaf. It was good bread. That was the thing about survival nobody warned you about — how ordinary everything became, almost immediately, almost insultingly. Her hands were still shaking. The gulls were screaming. The bread was warm.

* * *

They came out of the marsh over the following week like animals emerging after a hard winter.

Maren counted them from the ridge above the village, because counting was the one discipline she had left that still made sense. Forty-one freed captives from Karstholm's cells, walking on legs that remembered prisons better than paths. Sixty or more marsh folk who'd lived hidden so long their gift-names were family secrets. A handful of strays drawn in from the coast road by rumor — rumor traveling faster than any crier ever had, because rumor didn't need a proclamation.

They came limping and hollow-eyed, and the marsh folk met them with soup.

That was all. That was everything. There was no ceremony of welcome, no oath of allegiance. Old Sefa — the marsh elder who'd taught them the rite, whose face was a topography of wrinkles like dried riverbeds — simply stood at the village gate with a ladle and a kettle and said, "You're here. Eat first. Grief keeps."

Grief keeps. Maren had turned that phrase over for days. In the Order, grief was something you processed, reported, filed. In the marsh, it was something you put down carefully, like a tool you'd need again later, and got on with feeding people.

Forty-one freed. She made herself count the other number too, the number she kept locked behind her teeth: two hundred and six names on the harvest manifests she'd memorized before burning them. Two hundred and six taken before Aldery fell. Some had died in the tunnels. Some had drowned when the fortress shuddered and let the sea back into its lower halls. And some — this was the part she couldn't stop touching, the wound she'd carried since the archives — some had been drained so far past recovery that they'd walked into the binding rite's aftermath like sleepwalkers and simply lain down in the shallows, breathing slower and slower, until they weren't.

The Salt King hadn't taken them. The King was bound. But the harvest had, and harvests don't reverse.

On the fourth evening she found Sebastian sitting at the tideline where the drowned had been laid out, before the marsh folk had sung them into the water properly. Sebastian sat there now just looking at the sea, and Maren lowered herself onto the cold sand beside them without asking permission, because asking would have broken something.

"Two hundred and six," she said.

"I know."

"Sixty-one we saved."

"I know that too." Sebastian's voice was rough as beach gravel. They'd spent twenty years learning to sound like a harmless charm-peddler, and even now, with the storm long gone quiet under their skin, they hadn't entirely shed it. "And I know what you're doing with those numbers, Vale, because I've done it myself every night since the tunnels. You're dividing them like it's arithmetic that balances."

"It doesn't balance."

"No. It never does." Sebastian picked up a flat stone and skipped it once, twice, three times across the retreating tide. "But sixty-one is sixty-one. Sefa says the marsh counts the living first and the dead forever, and never lets the second number eat the first. I'm trying to learn how."

"And are you?"

"No." A ghost of a smile. "But Wren brings me bread when I fail, so my incentives are aligned."

* * *

The news from the capital arrived the way news did now — in fragments, carried by traders and refugees and one increasingly unreliable pigeon.

Aldery was dead. That much everyone agreed on, though the details shifted with each telling: crushed in the collapsing galleries, taken by the sea, consumed by the very hunger he'd fed. Maren privately suspected all three accounts were true at once, and that the truth was worse than any of them. She'd seen his face at the end. She would be seeing it for years.

The purge, meanwhile, had collapsed like a chair with a sawed leg. Without Aldery's seal and the royal writ he'd forged — forged, the fragments insisted; the young king himself had been a dupe, or nearly a dupe, or possibly complicit until the evidence reached him, depending on which faction was doing the telling — the whole apparatus had no legal spine. Registrations were suspended. Hunts were called off. Inquisitors were recalled, or fled, or simply stopped being inquisitors, hanging up their seals like coats out of season.

Except no one had told the outer provinces. Maren heard of two sweeps in the fishing villages east of Greyharbor that had gone ahead anyway, local magistrates drunk on borrowed authority. She heard of a gifted girl in Corrow's Bend who'd been locked in a granary pending "review" by an office that no longer existed. The machine was dead, but its limbs kept twitching, and each twitch landed on someone.

"We should go," Sebastian said, when she read the fragments aloud. "Corrow's Bend is four days' ride."

"With what army?"

"With three people and a reputation." Sebastian's mouth quirked. "Word's spreading, Vale. The storm-caller who flooded Karstholm. The inquisitor who burned the king's pardon. The child who bound the deep. We're a story now, and stories travel faster than magistrates."

"That's not a plan, that's a legend with delusions of competence."

"It's the only kind I've ever had." Sebastian looked at her steadily. "You don't have to come. You could stay. Sefa would—"

"Sefa would feed me soup until I agreed to plant turnips." Maren stood, brushing sand from her knees. "I spent nine years enforcing a law that was eating people. I'm not going to spend the next nine watching its corpse do it more slowly. Four days' ride. Fine."

From the seawall, Wren whooped loud enough to scatter gulls. They'd been listening the entire time. Of course they had.

* * *

She surrendered her seal on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were market day and market day meant witnesses.

Sefa helped her build the platform — one plank across two barrels, set up at the edge of the square where the fish stalls usually ran. Word went round the marsh the night before, passed door to door, and by midmorning there were perhaps two hundred people gathered, marsh folk and freed captives and coast-road strays, all wearing the careful blankness of people who had learned not to hope in public.

Maren climbed onto the plank. Her inquisitor's seal hung at her belt where it had hung for nine years: a disc of grey steel stamped with the eye-and-tide of the Order. It weighed nothing. It had always weighed nothing. That was the obscenity of it — that something responsible for so much had been light enough to forget you were carrying.

She'd written a speech. She'd burned it at dawn, because every draft sounded like either a confession or a performance, and she owed these people neither. What she owed them was simpler.

"The Order of the Tidewatch issued me this seal," she said, and her voice carried flat and clear across the square the way it had carried through a hundred interrogation rooms, though those rooms had never felt this loud. "With it, I registered the hunted. I signed transport orders. I told myself I was keeping people safe inside the law while the law was loading them onto ships to Karstholm. Two hundred and six people went into those cells. I am going to carry the difference between that number and sixty-one for the rest of my life, and I am not asking anyone here to forgive it."

Silence. Somewhere a gull screamed. Wren stood near the front, very still, watching her with eyes too old for the face.

"So here is what happens now. This seal has no authority left. The Order that issued it is scattered, the writ behind it was forgery, the man who directed me is dead. But paper dies slowly in the provinces, and some magistrate somewhere will still flinch at this stamp." She unhooked it from her belt. It lay in her palm like a coin, like a stone, like a tooth pulled from a jaw. "So I'm giving it away. Publicly. So there's no question, ever, of whose hand it sits in, or what it's used for, or whether Maren Vale is still anyone's hound."

She held it high, let them all see it, and then — this was the part she hadn't planned, the part that arrived from somewhere below thought — she walked to the seawall at the square's edge and dropped it into the sea.

It made a very small sound. That was all. Nine years, and a very small sound.

For a long moment nobody moved, and Maren stood on her plank feeling like a woman who had jumped and not yet hit water. Then old Sefa stepped forward, dipped her ladle into the soup kettle, and held it up.

"She eats first," Sefa announced to the square, in the tone of a judge passing sentence. "Then she carries wood like everyone else."

The laughter that broke over the square was ragged and wet and real, and someone started clapping, and Maren stood there sentenced to wood-carrying and soup and the ordinary labor of staying alive, condemned by acclamation to a punishment no court would ever enforce, and found — to her genuine astonishment — that her eyes were burning.

*That's it,* she thought. *That's the whole trial. Verdict: community service. Sentence: indefinite.*

It was the only sentence that had ever fit.

* * *

They left for Corrow's Bend three days later, and the leaving was its own strange lesson.

Because the marsh village — the place that had sheltered them, fed them, taught them the rite that saved the world's oldest hunger — did not ask them to stay. Sefa walked them to the gate with a sack of bread and dried fish and said, "Hidden things that only hide rot. Go be visible. Come back when you need feeding." Which was, Maren was coming to understand, the highest blessing the marsh conferred.

Wren cried a little and pretended not to. Sebastian hugged the old woman like she was made of something breakable, which, given that she'd outlived three purges, seemed statistically unlikely but emotionally correct.

And then the coast road opened northward, four days of grey water and green hills, and Maren rode at the rear of their absurd three-person column and felt the strangest sensation of her adult life.

Lightness.

Not happiness — she distrusted happiness the way she distrusted unlocked doors. Not peace, either; the numbers were still there, two hundred and six against sixty-one, and they would always be there. But underneath the exhaustion and the grief and the bone-deep tiredness of a body that had been at war for months, there was something new, something she had no professional vocabulary for.

She wasn't carrying the seal anymore.

Nine years it had hung at her hip, and she'd genuinely not known how heavy it was until it was gone — the way you don't notice a stone in your boot until you take the boot off. Every step now was a step without it. Every decision ahead belonged to her alone, unratified, unauthorized, hers.

"Vale." Sebastian had reined back to ride beside her. "You're smiling."

"I'm recalibrating."

"You're smiling *and* lying about it. Growth." Sebastian glanced ahead to where Wren rode, practicing small lightning between their fingers — little blue sparks that made their pony flick its ears in resignation. "Corrow's Bend. One locked granary, one confused magistrate, one legendary trio of outlaws. What's the approach?"

Maren considered. The old answer was procedure: assess, document, escalate through channels. The channels were ash. The procedure was ash. What remained was three people, a reputation, and the open road.

"Knock on the door," she said. "Politely. Then impolitely if required."

Sebastian laughed — a real laugh, startled out of them — and spurred forward to tell Wren the plan, and Maren followed at her own pace along the bright, receding edge of the sea, light in a way she had no right to be and no intention of apologizing for.

Behind them the marsh sang its low green song. Ahead, four days north, a granary lock waited to meet three people the law had finished with.

She found she was looking forward to it.
