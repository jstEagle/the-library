## 22. A Hollow Filled

The shop had been a smokehouse once, and it still smelled faintly of herring no matter how many times Maren scrubbed the walls. Sebastian had stopped minding. There was something honest about a smell that refused to be charming — in Greyharbor they would have paid good coin for a charm that made a room smell of nothing at all, and here the room simply smelled of what it was.

They opened the shutters on the morning side and let the marsh light in. It came green-gold through the reeds, filtered through fog that would burn off by midmorning, and it fell across the shelves Sebastian had built themselves: luck-knots braided from marsh-grass instead of harbor rope, weather-bones strung on cord, salt-rings for thresholds, small glass vials of storm-light that glowed like trapped summer evenings.

No more pretending. That was the strangest part, even after seven months. In Greyharbor every charm had been a lie — knotted string sold as knotted string, with a wink and a story, because the magic inside it had to stay coiled and hidden or die on the gallows. Here, when old Sefa bought a rain-charm for her garden, she held it up to the light and said, "This one's yours, isn't it? I can feel it humming." And Sebastian said yes. Yes, it was theirs. Yes, it hummed. The word *yes* still fit strangely in their mouth, like a boot worn on the wrong foot all these years and suddenly set right.

The bell over the door — Wren's idea, salvaged from a wrecked fishing skiff — rang out its cracked little note.

"You're open," Wren announced, as if this were news, as if Sebastian hadn't opened the shop at dawn every day since the frost broke. They were fourteen now, or fifteen; they'd lost count of their own birthdays somewhere in the years before the chapel cellar, and had decided last winter that their birthday was the day the flood receded, which made them eight months old and also ancient. They dropped a basket on the counter. "Maren says we need more nails. She says the roof is trying to kill her."

"The roof has been trying to kill her since spring," Sebastian said. "It's October."

"She takes it personally." Wren was already drifting along the shelves, touching everything, which was allowed now, which had always been allowed here, because nothing in the shop was locked and nothing in the shop was a lie. They picked up a storm-light vial and turned it, watching the glow chase their fingers. The scar on the back of their hand — where the first lightning had answered them, on the coast road, in another life — caught the light too.

"How is the roof actually?" Sebastian asked.

"Holding. Barely. She'll have the whole south slope off by tomorrow if nobody stops her." Wren set the vial down with the care of someone who knew exactly what lived inside glass like that. "I told her I'd help after lessons."

"Lessons," Sebastian echoed, and couldn't keep it flat. It never came out flat anymore. "How are the lessons?"

Wren grinned, and for a moment they were eight years old again, stealing a charm off a market blanket in Greyharbor, certain no one saw, certain no one cared. "I held the cloud shape for a full minute yesterday. Without you helping. Just me and the sky." Their voice dropped, went quick and hungry. "And then it *rained*, Bas. Not much. A little drizzle, just over the east field. But I did that."

"You did that," Sebastian agreed.

"Sefa's beans are going to be insufferable about it."

"Her beans deserve it. They've had a hard decade."

Wren laughed and took the nail money off the counter hook, and the bell rang its cracked note behind them, and Sebastian stood alone in the shop for a moment with their hand pressed flat against the counter, feeling the ghost of a storm not their own fading out of the air.

That was the other thing no one had warned them about, in twenty years of exile: that teaching would feel like this. That the gift, which had always been a hunger and then a wound and then a weapon, could become a language spoken between two people. When Wren reached for the sky now, Sebastian felt it — a tug at the base of their skull, a second heartbeat in the weather. The Salt King's binding ran through the three of them like a root system, and everything they'd poured into it that terrible night had never gone away. It had just changed shape. Grief had become ground to grow things in.

* * *

At noon, Sebastian closed the shop — a luxury that still made them laugh at themselves; in Greyharbor, closing meant losing coin, and coin meant eating — and walked out past the reed-cutters' platforms to the dry fields on the village's far edge.

*Dry* was generous. The fields east of the marsh had gone crack-skinned and pale over the long summer, the wells dropping, the elders rationing water with the grim competence of people who had survived worse. The marsh folk had weathered droughts before. But they had never, in living memory, been able to do anything about one except endure it.

Sebastian stood at the field's edge and took off their coat, folding it over a fence post the way they used to fold it over a market stall in Greyharbor, back when calling weather meant death and so they'd called nothing, ever, not once, not even alone, because the body keeps its secrets badly and storms leave traces.

Twenty years of holding it in. Twenty years of the hollow.

They breathed out, slow, the way they'd taught Wren — *don't grab the sky, invite it* — and reached.

The response came the way it always did now, but it still made their eyes sting every time: easily. Without cost. The clouds were there, waiting offshore beyond the fog line, fat and gray and patient, and when Sebastian lifted their hands the nearest ones simply *came*, rolling inland with a low grumble of contentment like a dog summoned from a sunny doorstep. The wind shifted. The smell of the marsh went green and mineral. Somewhere behind them, a child whooped — one of Sefa's grandkids, sprinting out of a doorway with arms spread wide, because rain was still new enough here to be a festival.

Sebastian let it fall. First a scatter of fat drops, darkening the cracked earth, then a proper soaking rain, steady and warm-tasting, drumming on the reeds and the platforms and the new shingles of a roof across the village that Maren was, at this very moment, probably falling through.

They stood in it without shielding themselves. Rain on an upturned face. It should not have felt like a miracle. It had rained on them ten thousand times. But every drop of it, for twenty years, had fallen on someone else's terms — weather that happened *to* Sebastian Okonkwo, weather they had no part in, weather that mocked the thing coiled under their skin by being everywhere and forbidden. This rain was theirs. Called, shaped, given freely to a field of beans and barley that would feed a village that knew exactly whose work it was and left a bowl of stew on their doorstep anyway, unasked.

*I am a storm-caller,* they said silently, to no one, to everyone, to the young person they had been on the dock at Greyharbor selling lies with a smile. *This is my name. This is my work.*

The clouds, having delivered, began to drift east toward the sea, and Sebastian let them go, and walked home through the wet with their boots squelching and their heart absurdly, embarrassingly full.

* * *

Maren had not fallen through the roof, but only because Wren had arrived in time to hold the ladder.

Sebastian found them both at dusk, mud-splattered and arguing companionably on the slope behind the house, surrounded by a graveyard of rotten shingles. Maren looked up as Sebastian approached, and there it was again — the look Sebastian still hadn't gotten used to, the one where she assessed them first with the old inquisitor's reflex, cataloguing threat, and then something warmer overrode it, and she just... relaxed. Seven months, and she still did the double take. Sebastian suspected she always would. Some training went down to the bone.

"It's fixed," Maren said, in the tone of a woman reporting a battle won at ruinous cost. "Mostly. The ridge beam wants replacing properly, come spring. And there's a leak over your bedroom I've patched with tar and prayer."

"Prayer works?"

"No. Tar works. Prayer was for me." She wiped her hands on a rag already more mud than rag. Her seal-hand — the right one, the one that had burned Lord Aldery's pardon offer to ash in front of his face — flexed absently around the hammer. It did that sometimes. Sebastian had learned not to comment. "Rain came early. Good rain?"

"Wren's beans will be insufferable."

"Maren!" Wren's head popped up over the roofline, upside down, hair full of straw. "Tell Bas about the lightning rod."

"There is no lightning rod. There is a piece of iron I nailed up as a joke."

"And the storm *liked* it," Wren insisted, dropping down to sit on the ridge, legs dangling over the edge, entirely comfortable at a height that would have given Sebastian a heart attack two years ago. "When the rain came through this afternoon, the air over the roof went all — tingly. Charged. Like it was deciding. I think if there'd been actual lightning it would've hit the rod instead of us. That's real, right? That's a thing?"

"That's a thing," Sebastian said. "We'll build a proper one. You can design it."

Wren's whole face lit, and they scrambled down the ladder with none of the caution Sebastian had spent months teaching and none of the regret either, and landed in front of them, breathless. "Tomorrow? Can we start tomorrow? I want to run the wire down into the ground myself, I read about grounding in the elder's books—"

"After your lessons."

"After lessons, yes, obviously, that's what I said—" They were already halfway to the house, talking about copper and depth and whether storms could be *friends* with a building rather than enemies of it, and disappeared inside, and a moment later there was the crash-and-clatter of someone starting dinner with more enthusiasm than coordination.

Sebastian and Maren stood in the wet grass in the blue hour, neither moving to follow yet.

"He's going to burn the kitchen down one of these nights," Maren said.

"One of these nights," Sebastian agreed. "We'll eat outside. It'll be character-building."

Maren snorted. Then quiet again. The frogs had started up in the reedbeds, the great evening chorus of the marsh, and the first lamps were kindling in windows down the lane, and smoke rose straight up from chimneys because the wind had gone soft.

"I got a letter today," Maren said.

The words landed differently than they would have a year ago. Sebastian kept their voice level. "From whom?"

"The capital. The new administration — Aldery's successors, such as they are." She turned the hammer over in her hands. "They're formalizing pardons. All the purge survivors. The taken who came out of Karstholm alive, and the families of the ones who didn't. Restitution, land grants, official apologies." A pause. "And me. Full restoration. My rank, my title, my seat. They want me to advise on the new Gifted Registry — the friendly kind, they say. Voluntary. Protective."

The frogs filled the silence. Down the lane, a door slammed cheerfully; Sefa's grandkids were still celebrating the rain.

"What did you write back?" Sebastian asked.

"I wrote that I was needed here." Maren looked up, finally, and her face was doing something complicated — grief and refusal and a strange peace all tangled together, the way it always did when the capital touched her. "There was a time I would have crawled back to them on broken knees for that letter. My oath was the whole shape of me. Take it away and there's nothing left." She turned the hammer over once more, then drove it into the tool belt at her hip with a decisive click. "Turns out there was quite a lot left. It was just buried under the oath, like a foundation under a bad house."

"They're lucky to lose you."

"They don't think so." Almost a smile. "They'll send a better offer in spring. They always do. And in spring I'll write the same letter back, and eventually they'll stop." She glanced toward the house, where lamplight and the smell of something burning-but-recoverable were escaping through the open window. "Besides. If I leave, who holds the ladder?"

* * *

Dinner was burnt fish and perfect bread and a argument about whether storms could be friends with buildings (Sebastian: technically yes; Wren: *told you*; Maren: neither of you is allowed near my roof). Afterward Wren washed up — badly, singing — and Maren mended a net by the fire because her hands needed occupation the way other people's minds needed rest, and Sebastian sat in the good chair by the window with a charm half-braided and did no work on it at all.

This was the hour that used to frighten them most, back in Greyharbor. The hour after work, before sleep, when the shop was dark and the streets were loud with other people's lives and the storm under their skin had nothing to do and started gnawing. They had filled it with accounts and inventory and careful, careful invisibility. They had told themselves it was enough. They had almost believed it, some nights.

Now the same hour was full of dishwater songs and net-mending and the tick of the stove cooling, and the storm under their skin lay quiet and warm as a sleeping animal, and the hollow that twenty years had carved was — not gone. Sebastian had learned not to expect gone. Hollows filled slowly, and some of the filling was scar tissue, and some of the people who should have helped fill it were dead or drowned or scattered across the bottom of Karstholm's bay, and grief didn't retire just because the war ended.

But the hollow had a floor now. Things grew in it. Wren's laughter grew in it. Maren's terrible tea grew in it. The word *home*, used without flinching, grew in it.

"Bas." Wren appeared at their elbow, drying their hands, wearing the particular expression that meant a question had been loading all day. "Can I ask something weird?"

"Those are the only questions worth asking."

"When you were in the deep. At the King's heart. Before we—" They stopped, the way they always stopped there, the way all three of them had quietly agreed to stop there, at the edge of the memory none of them could say aloud without their voices breaking. "Did you know we'd come? Did you know we wouldn't let you?"

Sebastian set the unfinished charm down. Looked at the fire, at Maren's bent head over the net, at this child they had let steal a charm once because loneliness had cracked them open wide enough to be kind.

"I hoped," they said honestly. "Right up until my feet left the ledge, I hoped. But hope isn't knowing. And I'd have done it anyway. That's the truth. I'd have paid it all alone, gladly, because I thought that was what the rite demanded — one life, freely given." They reached out and tapped Wren's scarred hand gently. "You taught me the rite was lying. Or maybe we were all reading it too small. One life, freely given — but nothing ever said the life had to be *spent*. Only given. And you gave yours to the binding too, that night. Both of you. Three gifts instead of one, and the King choked on it. Choked on being loved by people who wouldn't trade each other."

Wren considered this with the fierce concentration they brought to everything. Then: "So the lesson is the lesson was wrong."

"The lesson is that old powers read rules the way inquisitors read orders." Maren spoke without looking up from the net, dry as driftwood. "Literally, and to suit themselves."

Wren laughed, kissed Sebastian on the top of the head — a liberty permitted precisely once per week, per treaty — and went upstairs to bed, and the floorboards announced every step, and then the house settled into its nighttime sounds.

* * *

Much later, Sebastian stood out on the porch under a sky rinsed clean by afternoon rain. The stars over the marsh were brutal and bright. Out past the reedbeds, far off, the sea lay black and calm — calm all the way down now, calm to the drowned bones of Karstholm, where something vast slept bound in a knot of shared grief that no single death could ever have tied. On clear nights like this, Sebastian could feel it down there, faintly, the way you feel a scar in cold weather. Not awake. Not hungry anymore. Just *there*, held, dreaming whatever drowned kings dream, anchored by a bond it could not consume because it was not made of anything consumable.

Behind them, inside, Maren banked the fire. Above them, Wren's breathing slowed toward sleep. Around them, the marsh sang its frog-song, and the beans drank in the east field, and the shop's shelves waited for morning with charms that hummed honestly.

Sebastian Okonkwo, storm-caller, exiled no longer by anything but distance, leaned on the porch rail and watched the stars and thought: *tomorrow there will be nails, and lessons, and a lightning rod to design, and Sefa's beans to answer for, and a leak to argue about, and all of it mine, all of it chosen, all of it real.*

The hollow was filled. Not with thunder, though there would be thunder. Not with glory, though the ballads — terrible, unauthorized ballads — were already circulating. With this. With mornings. With family.

Then, far out over the sea, where no cloud had any business forming on a night this clear, a single small cloud gathered itself out of the dark and began, very quietly, to drift toward shore — and Sebastian smiled, because they recognized the clumsy, eager pull of it, the signature written all over it in a teenager's handwriting.

Some lessons, apparently, could not wait for morning.

They pushed off the rail and went to get their coat.
