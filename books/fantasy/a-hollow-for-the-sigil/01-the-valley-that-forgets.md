## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fog at the ridgeline has a personality, and I've decided it's smug.

I say this with some authority, because I have spent twenty-two years looking up at it. It sits along the crest of the mountains like a cat that owns the house and permits the family to live there, and it does not move. Not in spring, when the meltwater roars down the valley loud enough to shake the shutters. Not in autumn, when every other cloud in the sky packs its bags and goes somewhere useful. The fog stays. It is the oldest member of our guild, in a sense—senior to everyone living, possibly senior to the guild itself—and like all our oldest members, it refuses to explain itself.

My name is Ada Ashgrove, and I was born on the wrong side of that fog.

I know how that sounds. Hollowmere is not a prison. Nobody locked me in. There is a road out—the Ford Road, past the mill, up through the birches to the pass—and it is open most of the year, and no one has ever stood at the mouth of it with a spear telling me to turn back. It's just that anyone who walks up that road from the outside turns around at the top without knowing why. They come as far as the ridgeline, frown at something they can't name, and wander home again muttering about weather. Traders used to try. Pilgrims used to try. Now nobody bothers, because the world forgot we were here, and we got very good at forgetting back.

The elders call this a blessing. I call it a lid.

* * *

That morning in late autumn, I was on the guild house roof fixing slate tiles, because Elder Maren had looked at the sky over breakfast and said, "Snow inside the week," in the tone she uses for prophecies, taxes, and other unpleasant certainties. Maren is eighty-one and can still climb a ladder faster than me, which she considers her personal victory over time and mentions often. She did not climb it that day. She stood at the bottom holding the mortar bucket and supervising my work with the serene confidence of a woman who has never once fallen off anything.

"You're overlapping wrong," she said.

"I'm overlapping fine."

"Your grandfather overlapped fine too, right up until he slid into the gutter and broke his hip on Midwinter's Eve."

"That was ice, not overlap."

"Ice respects good overlap." She thumped the bucket down. "Do it properly or do it twice."

This is the whole catechism of Hollowmere, if you want to know: *do it properly or do it twice.* We are a people of maintenance. The guild house is three hundred years old and mostly held together by stubbornness and my hands, and I have been repairing it since I was tall enough to carry shingles. Roof, gutters, the east wall where the frost gets in, the well chain, the chapel door that swells every wet season and requires a ritual argument involving a mallet and Elder Tam's shoulder. Nine of us live here now. Once—I copied the numbers myself, so I know—there were two hundred and forty.

Nine. And half of those are elders who remember when there were thirty, and who get quietly offended if you point out the arithmetic.

I set another row of slate, overlapping correctly, and let myself look up while I did it. From the roof you can see the whole shape of the valley: the river running silver through the middle, the terraced fields gone gold and stubbled, the village huddled around the guild house like chicks around a hen that has stopped believing in eggs. And beyond everything, the ridge, and the fog sitting on it, white and patient and absolutely uninterested in my opinion.

Somewhere past that fog were cities. Real ones. Places where the roads went somewhere and strangers arrived on purpose and things happened to people that hadn't already happened to their grandmothers. I'd never seen one. None of us had. The maps in the guild library were older than the library—they showed the lowlands in faded ink, towns with names like Ashmarket and Grayharbor, rivers with little ships drawn on them, and then, at the edge of the page, a blank space where our valley should be, as if the cartographer had run out of interest rather than paper.

I used to think the mapmaker just didn't know. Later I understood better: the mapmaker had known exactly, and had drawn the truth. On every map outside, Hollowmere isn't missing. It's *omitted.* There's a difference. Missing things get searched for.

"You're staring at the ridge again," Maren said.

"I'm checking the weather."

"The weather is above you, girl, not beyond you."

"Everything interesting is beyond me," I said, and hammered a nail harder than the nail deserved.

Maren made the small sound she makes instead of sighing—a sort of nasal click, honed over decades of listening to me—and said nothing else. That was its own kind of conversation between us. She knew what I meant. She'd been young once, allegedly, though she treats this as classified information. She also knew what waited outside the fog for anyone foolish enough to go looking, which was nothing, because no one came back to report. Or rather: no one ever went. In three hundred years, the record shows exactly zero members leaving Hollowmere by choice. People die here. They don't depart.

We are not prisoners, I want to say again, to whoever needs it said. We are simply a room that no one has ever opened from the outside, and after enough centuries, a closed room starts to feel like the whole world.

* * *

The snow came that evening, early and soft, fat flakes falling straight down through windless air like the sky emptying its pockets. By full dark there were two inches on the flagstones and the whole valley had gone blue-white and silent, the particular silence of snowfall where you can hear the flakes land if you listen hard enough.

Which is why I heard the bell.

Not the chapel bell—that rings only for deaths and elections, and we'd had neither since spring. This was the ford bell, down by the river crossing, a dented old thing on a post that existed to warn of floods and hadn't rung in living memory. One clang, then another, arrhythmic, desperate, the sound of someone hitting it rather than ringing it.

I was in the scriptorium. I spent my evenings there, most evenings, doing the job nobody had asked me to do and nobody could take away from me: keeping the ledger. The ledger is a great leather-bound book, older than the building, and its purpose is simple and terrible. When a guild member dies, you write their name in it. Full name, dates, and one line about them, because the founders believed—so the frontispiece says—that *a person unrecorded is a person ungrieved,* and grief was supposed to be part of the work. Two hundred and forty names in the front section, in a dozen different hands, going back generations. Then a long stretch of blank pages. Then, lately, my handwriting, adding names one at a time, years apart, like a woman laying flowers on a grave that keeps growing.

Elder Tam. Sister Olla. Brother Fenwick, who taught me letters and died mid-sentence, his last word being "the," which I recorded faithfully because accuracy matters.

That night I had been copying out the year's entries onto fresh pages, because the old ink was fading, and I will admit the thought crossed my mind—briefly, shamefully, honestly—that at the current rate of attrition, I would eventually be writing the book alone. Nine names left. Eight. Seven. Someday mine, in my own hand, unless I found someone to hold the pen after me.

Then the bell rang, and I dropped the pen.

Here is the thing about the ford bell: it should not have been able to ring. Not because of the mechanism—it rang fine; I'd tested it as a child, out of boredom, and caught a week of chores for it—but because of what it was *for.* Floods came from the river, and the river came from the mountains, and both were inside the valley. Nothing outside ever reached the ford. The fog saw to that. The bell was a relic, like the guild itself: an answer to a question nobody asked anymore.

But it was ringing.

I went down the stairs two at a time, took the lantern off its hook in the hall, and let myself out into the falling snow without waking anyone, which was either prudence or selfishness—I've gone back and forth on that ever since, and I still haven't settled it.

The walk to the ford takes ten minutes in good weather. That night it took less, because fear is excellent company and sets a fast pace. Snow came down thick around my lantern light, and the world shrank to a small warm circle with me at the center of it, moving through the dark toward the river, and the whole time I kept thinking: *no one comes in. No one has ever come in. Whatever is ringing that bell, it is not from outside, because outside cannot touch us.*

The fog keeps us safe. That is the first lesson every child in Hollowmere learns, before letters, before prayers. The fog keeps us safe.

Nobody ever asks the obvious follow-up question, the one I was asking myself with every step: *safe from what?*

I heard the river before I saw it, black and quick under the snow. The bell post stood on the near bank, and hanging off it, half in the water, was a man.

He was young—younger than me, maybe, or worn down to look it—with dark hair plastered to his face and clothes that were wrong for here, wrong for anywhere: travel-stained, road-bitten, soaked through. One arm was hooked over the bell rope, and every few seconds his body would convulse and swing, and the bell would toll out over the water, and he would groan like the sound was being pulled out of him.

And he was bleeding. Even by lantern light I could see it—dark stain spreading down his side, dripping off his hem into the shallows. Except the blood wasn't behaving like blood. Where it touched the water, it glowed. Faintly, briefly, a pale gold shimmer, like a coal breathed on, there and gone.

I stood at the edge of the lantern light with my heart slamming and every sensible instinct I owned screaming at me to go back, wake the elders, let the fog handle it, whatever *it* was. The fog handles everything. That's the arrangement. That's the whole covenant of my life: stay inside the lines, and the lines keep the world out.

The man's head lifted. His eyes found the lantern, then me behind it, and they were fever-bright and terrified and unbearably human.

"Please," he said. His voice was wrecked, barely a whisper. "It let me through. The fog—it *let* me through. I don't know why. Please."

He said it like he'd been carrying the sentence a long way, like it was the last thing he had. Then his grip slipped off the rope, and he slid into the water, and I did the thing I would spend the rest of this story paying for, and never once regretted.

I put down the lantern, waded in, and caught him.

The water was cold enough to stop my breath. He was heavier than he looked and lighter than a man should be, all wire and ruin, and as I dragged him toward the bank his hand found my wrist—not grabbing, just resting there, the way a drowning man rests a hand on driftwood—and under my fingers I felt something I had no name for yet. A warmth coming off him that wasn't body heat. A hum, almost, like a struck string still faintly singing.

Later, I would learn what that hum meant. Later, I would learn what he'd carried through the fog, and what the fog had decided, and what my own blood had to do with any of it. But standing knee-deep in a freezing river with a dying stranger in my arms, I knew only three things.

One: the impossible had just walked through our walls.

Two: the elders were going to be furious.

Three—for the first time in twenty-two years, standing in the dark with the snow coming down and a stranger's blood glowing gold against my skin, I felt the lid on my world shift. Just slightly. Just enough to let in a draft.

Something was finally happening in Hollowmere.

And I had opened the door to it myself.
