## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The first snow of the year always arrived in Hollowmere like a rumor nobody had the courage to confirm. One morning the ridge was merely gray; the next, the fog had gone stiff and silver at its edges, and by the time I finished mucking the goat shed, the flakes were coming down in that slow, apologetic way snow does when it suspects it's unwelcome.

I stood in the dooryard of the guild house with a rake in one hand and goat on my boot, watching it fall, and I thought—as I did every year, and as I had promised myself I'd stop doing—*this is it, this is the year I walk out.*

I did not walk out. I put the rake away, scraped the goat off my boot, and went inside to argue with Elder Maren about the roof.

"The east slope," I said. "Three slates cracked since the frost. Another winter and the rain will find the archive."

"The archive is full of things nobody reads," Maren said, without looking up from her darning. She was eighty if she was a day, and she darned the way she did everything: with the serene conviction that the world would eventually agree to hold still.

"That's not the point of a roof."

"It is precisely the point of a roof. A roof keeps weather off. What's underneath is the weather's business."

This is the kind of conversation you have when you live with eight people who have collectively decided that history ended some time around their own mid-forties. I fixed the roof anyway, that afternoon, in the snow, because someone had to and because the alternative was sitting by the fire listening to Tam recite the same story about the bear he'd once seen on the ridge, a bear which, by his telling, had grown larger each autumn for eleven years and was now roughly the size of a chapel.

I want to be fair to Hollowmere. I do. It is a beautiful valley, green in the warm months, hushed in the cold ones, ringed by hills that wear their fog like a woman wears a shawl at a funeral—permanently, and with intent. The fog is the whole trick of the place. Outsiders walk toward the ridgeline and the fog folds around them, and they find themselves, without ever quite deciding to, walking back the way they came. They remember the valley afterward the way you remember a dream of water: something cool, something gone. Nine of us live here, in a guild house built for sixty, with a hall long enough to hold a hundred and the ghosts of all the suppers that used to.

The guild, for those who've never heard of it—which is everyone, by design—is called the Order of the Quiet Hand. We have no sigils, no craft, no purpose that anyone will name aloud. We copy names into a ledger. We keep the fog. We mend roofs.

I was born here. That's the whole of my claim on anything. Twenty-two years, and the sum of my ambition had been to see the far side of a hill.

I tell you all this so you'll understand that when I found a dying man at the ford two days later, my first instinct was not compassion. My first instinct was *how*.

* * *

The ford sits a mile downstream of the guild house, where the Mere goes shallow and wide over gray stones. Nobody crosses it. That's rather the point of the Mere; it comes down out of the fog-country to the north, runs the length of the valley, and leaves by a gorge no one has ever mapped, because the fog doesn't let anyone in to map it. The water is our other wall. The fog keeps the world out; the river keeps the valley honest.

I'd gone down to check the fish traps, because in a house of nine, checking the fish traps is a job you invent for yourself if you want an hour alone. The snow had thickened. The light had that underwater quality it gets before full dark, all gray and green and slow.

He was face-down in the shallows.

For one absurd moment I thought it was a log. Then the log coughed, and I was in the water before I'd decided to be, which is a thing about myself I've never entirely made peace with: my body commits to rescues my mind hasn't approved. The cold went through my coat like a debt collector. I got my hands under his arms and hauled, and he was heavier than he looked, waterlogged and limp, and we both went down once, and came up, and I dragged him onto the stones with my heart slamming and my sleeves frozen to my wrists.

He was young—near my age, maybe a few years older. Dark hair plastered to his forehead. Thin in the way of someone who'd been thin for a long time, not the fashionable kind. And he was clutching something to his chest with both hands, fingers locked, knuckles white even under the skin's gray-blue: a leather tube, the kind mapmakers and messengers use, sealed at both ends with wax.

"Let go of it," I said, prying at one hand. "You'll drown holding it, you great idiot, let go—"

His eyes opened. They were brown, fever-bright, and they fixed on me with a focus that frightened me more than the drowning had, because it wasn't confusion. It was recognition. Or the desperate hope of it.

"Home," he said. "Is this—" and then he was gone again, out under the water of his own body, and I stopped arguing with him and got him up the bank.

* * *

Here is what should have happened, according to every law the guild house has, written and unwritten: I should have left him. Or at most dragged him to the fog-line and let the valley do what it does, folding him gently back out into the world he'd come from. Outsiders don't get in. That is not a rule we enforce; it's a rule the valley enforces, and we are merely its beneficiaries. In twenty-two years, in the whole of my life, no stranger had ever stood inside Hollowmere. The fog did not permit it. The fog had never once, in the guild's memory, permitted it.

And yet.

I knelt on the riverbank with a half-drowned stranger wheezing under a dead alder, and I did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out wrong. The fog had let him through. The fog, which had turned back merchants and soldiers and at least one very persistent surveyor in the years before my birth, had opened like a door for this one man, this one night, and closed behind him.

There are only two explanations for that, and I didn't like either of them. Either the fog was failing—which meant the valley was failing, which meant the roof was the least of our problems—or the fog had *let him in*, which meant something in this valley had wanted him here.

I looked at the leather tube still locked in his grip. I looked at his hands, which were ruined—cracked, blistered, the nails split from cold. Somebody had walked a long way to get to a valley that could not be found.

"Fine," I told him, because he was unconscious and therefore the only person in Hollowmere who wouldn't argue. "Fine. But this is your fault."

* * *

I got him to the guild house by the woodshed door, which was cowardly, but the front hall has windows on all sides and nine pairs of eyes, and I wanted one hour of not explaining before I had to explain. I got him out of the wet clothes—into one of Tam's shirts, since Tam is the only one of us built like a door—and into my own bed, because the guest rooms haven't been heated in thirty years and my room is over the kitchen and the kitchen is the only warm place left in the building.

That was when I saw the wound.

It was on his left side, below the ribs, and I'd have taken it for a knife-cut and nothing more—a bad one, long and deep, the kind that needs stitching and luck—except that it *glowed*. Faintly. The way a coal glows an hour after the fire's dead: not light, exactly, but the memory of light, a dull ember-color sitting in the torn flesh, pulsing slow. When I pressed cloth to it, the glow dimmed, and he made a sound I have not managed to forget since, a sound like a man agreeing to something terrible.

I stitched it. I've stitched goats, geese, Tam (twice), and myself (once, badly), so a stranger was nothing new. But my hands shook the whole time, and not from the cold. The skin around the wound was wrong. Not infected-wrong. *Absent*-wrong. Like the flesh there had forgotten how to be flesh and was only remembering, badly, from a distance.

The fever came on fast after that. He burned. He talked.

Mostly it was nonsense—numbers, names, a phrase he said over and over in a language I didn't know but that made my teeth ache to hear, which is not a thing I have a better explanation for. But some of it was plain, and some of it was worse than the glowing wound.

"—the graycloaks are in the lowlands, they're in the *lowlands*—" he said, once, eyes still shut, hands clawing at the blanket. "Tell them it isn't a map, it's a *key*—"

And later, quieter, almost gentle, the way you'd speak to someone you loved: "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I got it out. I got it out."

I sat by my own bed, in my own room, watching a stranger bleed light, and I did the only sensible thing available to me, which was to think about how much trouble I was in.

If I told Maren, she'd want him out by morning, fever or no. She'd be right, by the guild's lights. The valley keeps itself hidden by keeping itself empty; that's the whole covenant, as far as anyone will explain it to me, which is not far. A stranger inside the fog is a crack in the wall. And this stranger wasn't just a stranger—he was a *messenger*, half-dead, hunted, carrying something he'd called a key, with a wound that glowed like the last coal of a fire the whole world had agreed to let go out.

If I didn't tell Maren, I was lying to my elders. Which, in fairness, I'd done before—about the roof funds, about the still, about the goat—but never about anything that mattered, and never with the specific knowledge that it *would* matter, that this was the kind of lie that doesn't stay small. Lies about a stranger in your bed have a way of becoming stories about a stranger in your valley.

I sat there until the snow stopped, which was past midnight. Then I sat there some more.

He woke once, near the small hours. Not all the way—his eyes found the ceiling, then me, and there was a long moment where he clearly could not work out what he was looking at, and I watched him try, and I watched something in him decide that it didn't matter whether I was real, because he was too tired to check.

"Water," he said.

I gave him water. He drank like it was a duty he'd been neglecting. Then his eyes came back to me and sharpened, just slightly, the fever receding behind them like a tide going out to reveal exactly one clear stone.

"This is Hollowmere," he said. Not a question.

"You know the name of a valley that isn't on any map."

"I drew the map that isn't on it." His mouth did something that wanted to be a smile and only got halfway, which I would learn was typical of him. "Then it's true. The fog let me through."

"The fog has never let anyone through."

"It let me through." He said it the way you'd state the weather. Then his gaze dropped to the leather tube, which I had set on the chair beside the bed, and something went through his face—relief and terror in equal measure, the two of them shaking hands like old colleagues. "It's here. It's still here."

"Whatever it is, it nearly drowned with you."

"It nearly drowned with me a hundred miles back. The ford was just the latest attempt." His eyes were closing again. He fought it, visibly, a man rationing his consciousness like hard bread. "Listen. There isn't time. There's never—when I'm myself again, there won't be time, so listen now, while I'm too stupid to be afraid." His hand found my wrist. His grip was fever-hot and shockingly strong. "The seal is failing. Do you understand me? The old seal. The one your founders didn't flee from. The one they *made*."

"I don't know what you're—"

"You do." His eyes were on mine, and they were clear, horribly clear, clear as ice over deep water. "You've always known. That's why the fog opened. It didn't let me in, Ada Ashgrove." And then he was under again, gone, breathing in that ragged shallow way, the wound at his side pulsing its dull ember-light against the bandages.

I hadn't told him my name.

I sat very still for a long time, listening to the house settle, to the snow starting again against the window, to a stranger breathe in my bed with my name in his mouth and my surname in his prophecy, and outside, up on the ridgeline, the fog stood over the valley the way it always had—patient, silver, keeping the world out.

Or keeping something in.

I looked at the leather tube on the chair. The wax seal at its end was stamped with a mark I'd seen exactly one place in my life: pressed into the frontispiece of the guild's oldest ledger, in the hand of a founder dead two hundred years.

I didn't open it. Not that night. That night I did the cowardly, compassionate thing—the only thing, I told myself, that kept every law intact: I kept him secret, kept him warm, and told no one.

By morning, of course, the snow had stopped, and Tam had found the wet footprints by the woodshed, and the whole house was in the kitchen asking me questions I had exactly one hour to learn to answer.

But that was morning. That night, there was only the fever, the glow, the sealed map with my family's name at its heart—and me, sitting in the dark, holding a stranger's secret in a valley that had never once held a stranger at all.
