## 3. What the Fog Keeps Out

I have died before. Twice, if you count the time in the marshes at Vell, and I do count it, because I remember the cold coming up through my boots and the particular gray quality of the light, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that has never once served me well since: *so this is what the king sees.*

That was before I understood that the king doesn't see anything anymore. That's rather the point of him.

But dying is a habit, and habits are hard to break, and so when the fever took me on the third night in that guild house—third night, or fourth; the fever ate my counting along with everything else—I went looking for the gray light again. And this time, instead of finding it, I found her.

The girl from the ford. Ashgrove. She was sitting beside me with a basin of water and an expression like a woman arguing with a door that refuses to open.

"You're not supposed to be awake," she said.

"I'm frequently not supposed to be things," I told her. "Awake. Alive. In valleys."

"Then stop."

"I've tried. It never takes."

She wrung out the cloth and pressed it to my forehead with the brisk tenderness of someone who resents every ounce of tenderness she's spending. "Your wound is glowing," she said. "I want you to know that I noticed, and that I've decided not to scream about it, and that this decision is costing me a great deal."

"That's very brave of you."

"It's very *tired* of me, is what it is." She dipped the cloth again. "What did this to you?"

"A warden's blade."

"We don't have wardens here."

"No," I said, and the fever made my voice go strange and far away, the way it does. "You have fog instead. Congratulations. It's the same thing, only wetter."

She didn't laugh. I was beginning to understand that Ada Ashgrove didn't laugh at things unless she'd decided they were safe, and nothing about me was going to clear that bar for some time.

* * *

Here is what the fever showed me, in the long hours when she thought I slept:

A corridor. White stone, white light, and me walking down it with a sheaf of papers under my arm, because I was always carrying papers in those days. Scribe to the Privy Seal, third chair from the window, which was the best chair because the window looked out on the inner court where the plum trees grew, and in six years of service I watched those plum trees bloom exactly once.

At the end of the corridor there was a door, and behind the door there was the king, and everyone loved the king.

That was the first thing they taught you, before you ever saw him: *you will love the king.* Not as instruction—as weather. It simply settled over you in the capital, this warm approving fog of love, and you breathed it, and you loved, and it never occurred to you to ask why the love arrived fully formed, without any of the usual labor of knowing someone.

I carried papers to that door for six years. Petitions. Ledgers. A marriage contract once, for a duke's daughter whose name I have forgotten, which should tell you something, because I forget nothing. Forgetting is the one thing I cannot do. It's the crown's great joke: it eats memory, and it left me a man who remembers everything, hoarding it all like a miser in a burning house.

The door opened. The king sat by the window with the plum trees blooming below him, and he turned his face to me, and I loved him.

And underneath the love, small and buried and screaming, something that was me said: *look at his eyes. Look at his eyes. There's no one home.*

* * *

"He talks in his sleep," Ada said to someone, out in the hall. I heard it through the wall, or through the fever, which amounts to the same thing. "He talks in his sleep and half of it isn't words. And the wound—it's not closing right. Maren looked at it and went white and wouldn't say why."

Old woman's voice, dry as ledger paper: "Did you ask him what he did?"

"He says he was a scribe."

"Scribes don't get cut by wardens."

"This one did. He says—" a pause, the sound of someone deciding how much truth a room can hold, "—he says he stole something. From the palace."

"And you believe him?"

Another pause. Longer. "I believe the fog let him in," Ada said. "The fog hasn't let anything in for two hundred years. Whatever he's carrying, it wanted to come home."

* * *

Home. The word followed me down into the fever like a dog that won't be called off.

Because here is the second thing the fever showed me, and this one I will tell you plainly, though it cost me three nights of sweat to say it even to myself:

The king was not always hollow.

His name was Aldric. He had a scar on his left hand from a falcon's talons, from when he was nine years old and insisted on holding the bird himself. He laughed too loudly at his own jokes. He wrote letters—real ones, bad ones, full of crossed-out lines—to a woman in the southern provinces whom the court never approved of, and he kept writing them after the court forbade it, folding them small and passing them to me because I was the scribe and scribes are furniture, and furniture sees nothing.

*I miss being a person,* he wrote, in the last letter I ever carried for him. *I miss it the way you miss a limb. Everyone smiles at me now. No one argues with me. Do you know what it is to give an order and watch two hundred faces agree with you at once, all that agreement arriving like water into a hull? I think something is drinking me. I think it started the day they put the crown on, and I think no one will say so because saying so would require remembering that there was a day.*

I read that letter four hundred times before I delivered it. I could recite it drunk, asleep, bleeding. I can recite it now.

And then the woman stopped writing back—not because she wanted to but because the Graycloaks came to her village one autumn morning, politely, with their warrants and their soft gray wool, and asked her questions until she agreed with them, and by evening she smiled at everything, and by winter she had forgotten there had ever been anything to smile about.

The king stopped receiving letters after that. But he didn't stop sending them. That was the thing no one understood, the thing I didn't understand myself for years: whatever was left of Aldric, buried down deep under the sigil-work and the smiling and the love-that-arrived-fully-formed, that buried man kept writing. Little messages folded into state documents where only a scribe would look. Marginalia. Ciphered nonsense that read like nonsense until you held it against the old guild scripts—and I was a scribe, and scribes are furniture, and furniture reads everything.

*It is still me,* the buried hand wrote. *Find the valley. Find the house with the ledger. Tell them the seal is failing. Tell them I am sorry.*

* * *

"You're doing it again," Ada said. She was there when I surfaced, the way she was always there, basin and cloth and that face like a closed ledger. "Talking. You were saying *tell them I'm sorry*, over and over, and then you said a name."

"What name?"

"Mine. Or—the surname. Ashgrove." Her voice did something careful. "Why does a palace scribe know my name?"

"The map knows your name," I said, because the fever had burned away whatever discretion I owned. "The map is addressed to your name. To the house with the ledger. I wasn't carrying stolen goods, Ashgrove. I was carrying mail."

"Mail," she repeated.

"Two hundred years late, but yes. Mail."

She sat back. Outside the window the fog lay against the glass like something listening. "You said wardens swept the lowlands. Swept them for what?"

"For people who remember." I closed my eyes. Better to say it to the dark. "There's a crown, and there's a thing inside the crown, and the thing eats. Not bread. Not blood. It eats *will*. Memory. The part of a person that says *no*. It started with the court, then the city, then the provinces, and now there are Graycloaks in every market town asking questions, and every answer they hear comes back *yes, yes, we love the king*, and the ones who hesitate—" I touched my side, where the wound sat glowing faintly under the bandage, cold as riverbed stone. "The ones who hesitate get visited twice."

"And the seal? What seal?"

"The founders' seal. The reason your valley exists. Your fog isn't weather, Ashgrove. It's a lock. And locks fail."

Silence. When I opened my eyes she was staring at her own hands, turning them over as if checking whether they belonged to her.

"My whole life," she said quietly, "I thought the fog was keeping us safe from the world."

"It is," I said. "That's the tragedy of good walls. Nobody asks what they're keeping *in*."

* * *

The fever broke on the fifth night, or the sixth, and I woke honest and empty as a drained cup.

Ada was asleep in the chair beside the bed, upright, arms crossed, having clearly lost an argument with herself about leaving. The leather tube lay on the table between us. In the gray pre-dawn light the map inside it seemed almost to breathe.

I should have taken it and gone. Every calculation said go: the Graycloaks were methodical, and methodical things follow lost property. Staying meant the valley learned what the lowlands knew. Staying meant her name, spoken aloud in my sleep, entered into somebody's ledger far less kind than hers.

But I looked at her sleeping in that chair, twenty-two years old and already built entirely out of duty, guarding a stranger she should have turned out into the snow—and I thought about the last line of the last letter, the one I'd never delivered because its recipient was dead:

*Tell whoever finds this that it was not their burden. Tell them anyway.*

"Ada," I said, because honesty was the only payment I had.

Her eyes opened instantly, fully, no drift between sleep and waking. A woman used to watching for trouble.

"The Graycloaks will come here," I told her. "Not maybe. When. They track what I carry, and what you carry now. I need you to understand that letting me across your ford may have been the most expensive kindness of your life."

She considered this with the gravity of someone pricing a roof repair.

"Well," she said at last. "Then we'd better find out what the mail says."

And outside the window, thin as a knife slipped under a door, the fog began—for the first time in two hundred years—to move.
