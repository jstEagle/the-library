## 4. The Ledger of Names

The guild house kept its records the way old men keep their teeth: reluctantly, and mostly for show.

There were three ledgers, technically. The current one, which recorded roof repairs and the dwindling stores of salt pork with the enthusiasm of a eulogy. A middle one, water-stained, that covered a century or so of members whose names I copied out each winter because someone had to and no one else would. And then there was the first one.

Nobody touched the first one. It lived on the top shelf of the scriptorium behind a row of empty ink pots, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, and when I was nine and asked Elder Maren what was in it, she said, "Names," in the tone she used for questions she intended never to answer again. When I asked whose names, she said, "Old ones," and sent me to scrub the hearth, which was Maren's answer to most things, including things that had nothing to do with hearths.

I want it noted that I did not go digging through it because I'd suddenly developed an appetite for my own history. I went digging because Wren's map had ruined my sleep.

* * *

He'd been three days among us by then, drifting in and out of fever in the infirmary cot, and the map—unrolled, weighted at the corners with candlesticks—had taken over the long table in the guild hall like a guest who refused to leave. It was beautiful in the way dangerous things are beautiful. Vellum fine as skin, ink that shifted color depending on the angle of the light, and across its heart a mark I couldn't stop looking at: not a sigil exactly, since Hollowmere had spent two hundred years perfecting the art of having no sigils, but something older. A circle broken by seven strokes, like a wheel with spokes missing, like a cage drawn by someone who wanted you to know it was also a door.

I'd traced it eleven times on scrap paper. On the twelfth, my hand had done something strange—not trembled, exactly. *Remembered.* The way your feet remember stairs in the dark.

That was the part that ruined my sleep.

So on the fourth morning, before anyone else was up, before the frost on the windows had even thought about melting, I took down the oilcloth bundle and unwrapped the first ledger of the Guild of the Hollow Vale.

It smelled the way I imagine the inside of a mountain smells. Cold stone and time. The cover was leather gone black, and there was no title, just that same mark pressed into it—a circle broken by seven strokes—and beneath it, in letters so shallow I had to tilt the thing toward the fire to catch them:

*For the keeping of what must not be forgotten, against the day forgetting is a mercy.*

Cheerful lot, our founders.

* * *

The early pages were written in a hand so fine it looked embroidered rather than penned. I read standing up, then sitting, then hunched so close to the hearth that my shins ached from the heat, and what I read made the hair on my arms stand up and refuse to lie back down.

They hadn't fled magic. Every story we told ourselves—the ones Maren told the children, the ones carved into the lintel over the guild doors, *Here the vale keeps its silence*—every story said our founders were refugees from the wars of craft, simple folk who'd sworn off sigils and been rewarded with fog and forgetfulness. A sanctuary. A hiding place.

But the ledger's first entries weren't vows of renunciation. They were *engineering notes.*

*The binding holds at seven points,* wrote the first hand. *The seventh must be living. We are sorry. There was no other arithmetic.*

And below that, a list of names. Nine names, each followed by a small annotation. *Keeper of the western point. Keeper of the ford. Keeper of the fog's edge.* And the last name on the list, set apart, annotated simply:

*The heart. She has agreed.*

She. Not he. I sat with that a moment, feeling the fire warm one side of me and the cold room take the other, and I thought about how every founding story Maren ever told had men in it. Nine founders, she said, stern gray-bearded men who'd wanted only peace. I'd pictured them my whole life like a row of disapproving uncles.

The first hand of this ledger belonged to a woman. I was sure of it the way you're sure of a voice through a wall. And her name—

Her name was Ashgrove.

Not Ada Ashgrove. *Sera* Ashgrove—which is a strange thing to learn about yourself at dawn, that your name is a hand-me-down two centuries old, that somewhere a woman signed her life away in ink that still hadn't faded and left you her surname like a coat you didn't ask to inherit.

I put the ledger down. I picked it back up. My hands were doing the trembling now, properly, no ambiguity about it.

Because here is the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I was circling the way you circle a wound to see how bad it is. The mark on the map's heart—the broken circle, the seven strokes—I'd found it in the ledger too. Pressed into the cover. Drawn at the head of every page. And beside it, in that first fine hand, a word I'd assumed was decorative:

*Key.*

The map wasn't a map. Or it was a map the way a key is a map of the lock it opens. And the mark at its center matched the mark on our oldest book, the book nobody read, in the guild house in the valley that forgot, and the woman who'd written it all down shared my name.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in the guild hall at dawn, one short ugly sound that scared me more than crying would have. Because there are two kinds of discovering you're important. One feels like being chosen. The other feels like being caught.

This was the second kind.

* * *

Wren found me there an hour later. He was leaning on the doorframe, gray-faced, one hand pressed to the bandage at his side where the wound glowed its faint wrong glow, and he took in the scene—the open ledger, the map, me sitting between them like a defendant—and something in his expression said he'd known this morning would come.

"You read it," he said.

"I read some of it." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "There's more?"

"There's always more." He crossed the room slowly, lowered himself into the chair opposite mine, and for a moment just breathed, riding out whatever the fever was doing under his skin. "Your elders don't know what this house is."

"They know what it's always been. A roof and a rulebook."

"It's a lock," Wren said. "You've been living inside the mechanism your whole life and calling it furniture." He reached across, turned the ledger toward himself with a care that bordered on reverence, and stopped at the page I'd marked. His finger hovered over *Sera Ashgrove — the heart. She has agreed.* "Do you understand what 'the heart' means? In the binding?"

"I understand I don't like the sound of it."

"The seal has seven points. Six are places. Stone and script, they hold on their own." He looked up at me, and his eyes had the fever-brightness that made him look either prophetic or very ill, which with him might have been the same thing. "The seventh is a person. The binding needs a will at its center. Something alive that *chooses* to hold. That's why the valley forgets outsiders, why the fog turns them—it's not a ward, it's a heartbeat. Her heartbeat. Your ancestor's."

"And when she died?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He closed his eyes briefly. "The seal should have failed two hundred years ago. Instead it passed down. Blood to blood. Quietly. The guild stopped being keepers and became—caretakers who'd lost the manual. But the line didn't stop, Ada. The line is *here.*" And he tapped the table, once, meaning the ledger, meaning the valley, meaning me.

I stood up because sitting felt suddenly unsafe, the way standing near a cliff edge feels unsafe once you've noticed the edge. "No. No, that's—you've got the wrong Ashgrove. I fix roofs. I copy dead people's names into a book nobody reads. I'm the least magical person in a valley whose entire culture is the absence of magic."

"Is it?" Wren said softly. "The fog let me through. Dying, bleeding, carrying the one thing in the world it exists to protect—and the fog opened like a door. Why do you think that was?"

"Because I fished you out of the river," I said, and heard it come out too fast, too defensive. "Because I broke guild law and pulled a stranger across the ford. Maybe the valley was just—sloppy."

"Or maybe the lock recognized the key coming home." He said it gently, which was worse. Gentleness from him always landed harder than bluntness; bluntness you could argue with.

I turned to the window. Frost ferned the glass, and beyond it the ridgeline wore its fog like a held breath, and I thought: *two hundred years my family has lived inside a spell and called it weather.* I thought about my mother, dead ten years now, who used to hum while she worked and could never explain why the humming made the bread rise better. I thought about myself at nine, asking Maren what was in the first ledger, and the strange hot certainty even then that the answer mattered to me personally—that I was born on the wrong side of the map, I'd always said, but maybe I'd had it backwards. Maybe the map had been waiting on the right side of *me.*

Vertigo is the honest word for it. Not fear, though fear came later. Vertigo—the floor turning out to be a ceiling, the inheritance turning out to be a debt. You spend your whole life thinking you're poor and then someone shows you the deed to the house and the deed is written in blood and the house is a prison and you're the warden's daughter and the prisoner's granddaughter and possibly both at once.

"I have to show the elders," I said finally.

Something flickered across Wren's face—hope and dread sharing the same expression, like neighbors forced to wave from adjacent porches. "Yes," he said. "You do."

* * *

We convened at midday, which for the guild meant everyone who could walk gathered in the hall while Elder Maren presided from her chair by the fire like a gargoyle that had earned tenure. Nine of us counting Wren, who sat propped in the corner looking like a strong wind would finish him, and counting me, standing at the table with the ledger open and the map spread beside it, feeling less like a presenter and more like a girl showing her elders a dead bird she'd found.

I'd rehearsed. I want that on the record. I'd rehearsed a whole speech about duty and discovery and the weight of history, and I got exactly four words into it before Maren said, "Close the book, Ada."

"Maren, please just look at—"

"We know what's in it."

The hall went quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when everyone realizes the pretense has been dropped and nobody planned what comes after. Old Tam, who'd taught me my letters, found something fascinating in his own hands. Young Pell, who was barely a member, more of a stray we fed, looked from face to face like a boy watching adults pretend not to smell smoke.

"You *know,*" I repeated.

"We know enough." Maren's voice was flat as the frozen ford. "Our grandmothers' grandmothers knew. It's why the book stays wrapped. Some knowledge is a burden, child, and the founders were clear—the less the vale remembers of what it is, the safer it sleeps."

"The vale isn't sleeping safe." I put my palm flat on the map, on the broken circle, and made myself say the next part plainly, because plainly was the only way it would survive their flinching. "There's a king in the lowlands wearing a crown that eats people. There are wardens sweeping the country for this map. Wren nearly died bringing it here—*through* our fog, which shouldn't be possible unless something in this valley is already waking up. And the ledger says the seal that hides us is failing, because its heart—" my voice caught, and I hated it, and pushed through— "because its heart was a person, and the person-line is me, and none of us knew, because knowing was forbidden by people who thought ignorance was a fortress."

Maren's jaw worked. For a moment—one treacherous, human moment—I saw something move behind her eyes that wasn't stubbornness. Fear, maybe. Or grief. The grief of a woman who'd spent sixty years guarding a secret she'd been told was sacred and was now being shown the bill for it.

Then she shut it away, the way she'd shut away my question when I was nine.

"The guild votes," she said. "The book goes back on the shelf. The stranger recovers and moves on. The map—" her eyes cut to Wren, and there was real regret in it, I'll grant her that much— "the map leaves with him. That is the will of this house."

"It's the will of your fear," I said.

"It is the same thing, girl. It has been the same thing for two hundred years, and it has kept every one of us alive." She rose, which ended meetings the way a slammed door ends arguments. "Vote stands. Ada—close the book."

I looked around the hall. Tam studying his hands. Pell staring at me with wide, hungry eyes—he was young enough that the vertigo of the thing hadn't reached him yet; he still thought it was an adventure. The others, faces carefully blank, the blankness itself a vote.

And Wren in the corner, watching me, saying nothing, because he'd learned in whatever court he'd served that a man can't cast another house's votes.

I closed the ledger. I carried it to the shelf. I set it behind the empty ink pots, and I stood there a moment with my hand flat on its cover, feeling the broken circle under my palm like a pulse.

But here's what Maren, for all her sixty years of guardianship, had forgotten about locks: they open from the inside. And here's what I'd learned in four days of reading dead women's handwriting: *she has agreed* was not the same as *she was asked twice.*

The vote stood. Fine. Votes were for roofs and salt pork.

That night, after the house slept, I took the ledger down again, and the map, and a fresh sheet of paper, and I began to copy—every entry, every name, every terrible piece of arithmetic—in my own hand, so that whatever happened next, the knowledge would exist in more than one place, in more than one bloodline, and no elder's fear could wrap it back in oilcloth.

I was halfway down the second page when Pell's voice came thin through the dark from the watch post above the gate, cracked with cold and something else:

"Ada. Ada, there are riders on the ridge road. Two of them. And they're wearing gray."
