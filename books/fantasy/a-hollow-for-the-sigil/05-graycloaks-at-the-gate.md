## 5. Graycloaks at the Gate

I have been many things in my life: a scribe, a thief, a liar of professional quality, and once—for eleven days—the most wanted man in the capital. But I had never before been the reason a sanctuary might burn, and I want it on the record that I did not enjoy the promotion.

It began with bells. Hollowmere's guild house had exactly one bell, cracked down one side so that everything it said came out with a stutter, like a man repeating himself to be believed. *Bran-bran-bran.* Elder Maren's voice beneath it, thin with age and pitched carefully toward calm.

"Visitors," she called into the common room. "At the gate. Under a white flag."

Ada looked up from the ledger she was always copying, ink on her fingers and suspicion already settling into her face like weather. "We don't get visitors."

"No," Maren agreed. "That's rather the point."

I knew what a white flag meant before my fevered brain finished assembling the words. In the lowlands, under the Hollowed King's peace, a flag of parley meant that someone powerful wanted something and preferred to ask first. It meant courtesy. And courtesy from the Graycloaks was like courtesy from a landslide—polite right up until you were buried.

"We should not answer it," I said, and my voice came out rougher than intended. Three days of fever will do that. Three days of Ada Ashgrove changing the cloth on your brow will also do that, though I'd have died before admitting which of the two was harder to bear.

Maren turned her gaze on me. She had the kind of eyes that had spent eighty years watching people and had stopped being impressed by any of them somewhere around year forty. "You know who they are."

"I know what they are." I sat up too fast and the room performed a slow, insolent rotation around me. "Graycloaks. Wardens of the crown. They don't come for thieves, elder. Thieves are beneath them. They come for *things*." I touched the bandage at my side, where the wound still glowed faintly through the linen when the light was wrong. "And they do not leave without them."

"Then we won't give them anything," Ada said.

The certainty in her voice was almost funny. Almost. There was a version of me—a younger version, the one who'd knelt in a marble scriptorium and copied out the king's edicts in a hand so beautiful it made visiting dignitaries sigh—who would have believed that walls and stubbornness were enough. That version had died badly, along with everyone else who'd believed it.

"They asked for parley," Maren said slowly. "Which means they asked. Which means—" she paused, working it through with the care of a woman who had not needed to think tactically in sixty years, "—they think asking might work."

"It means they're being polite because politeness is cheaper than force," I said. "For now. Give them a reason to stop being polite, and the fog outside this valley will learn what fire smells like."

Nobody answered that. The bell kept stuttering its alarm, and outside the window the first snow of the season lay on the courtyard like an unmarked page, and I thought: *this is what it looks like just before something is written on it.*

* * *

They let me watch from the upper gallery, propped against the wall like an invalid at his own trial, which was more or less what I was. Below, the great doors of the guild house stood open for the first time in living memory—I gathered this from the way every elder kept glancing at them as if expecting the wood itself to object—and beyond them, across the snow-dusted yard, two figures waited at the gate.

Graycloaks look exactly as you'd fear. Gray wool, gray hoods, faces like unfinished thoughts. Not masked—that would be dramatic—but somehow unmemorable, the way a wall is unmemorable. You could pass one in a market and by evening be unable to swear you'd seen anyone at all. I had spent four years writing their warrants in a beautiful hand, and even I couldn't have described either of their faces now.

Elder Maren went out to meet them alone, spine straight, hands folded, wearing the expression of a woman greeting distant relatives she did not remember fondly. The taller of the two inclined its head.

"Elder." The voice was mild. Reasonable. The voice of a man asking directions to a well. "We thank you for your hospitality of spirit. We travel under the king's flag of parley, and we will not trouble you long."

"You've crossed a long way to find nothing," Maren said. "Hollowmere keeps no king's business."

"Indeed." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "We seek a courier. Young, dark-haired, wounded. He stole property belonging to His Majesty—a map, in a leather case—and fled north. He is feverish. He may not survive another week in this cold. His family grieves." Another pause. "We would spare them that."

I felt Ada go rigid beside me in the gallery. Her shoulder pressed mine, and I noticed—not for the first time, but inconveniently—that I had begun cataloguing the small facts of her presence the way I used to catalogue royal decrees. The ink stain on her left knuckle. The way she held her breath when she was angry instead of holding her tongue.

"He sounds like a sad story," Maren said below. "But no such man has passed our ford."

*Badly done,* I thought, closing my eyes. *Elder, you lie the way a child hides a stolen sweet—behind your back, in plain view.*

Because there it was: the ford. Everyone in the valley knew the fog let no outsider through; that was the whole architecture of Hollowmere's safety, the founding miracle nobody questioned. If no stranger had passed the ford, then how had the elders known to expect anyone? How had the bell been rung before the visitors were even visible? Maren had told the truth about the wrong thing, and the tall Graycloak tilted its head a degree, and I watched it notice.

"The fog has been strange this season," the Graycloak said mildly. "Thinning at the edges. Our cartographers find it... interesting."

"Our fog is our own affair."

"Of course." The mildness never wavered. It never does; that's the horror of them. "One question more, elder, and we will trouble you no further. The valley has nine souls in its keeping, by old census. Has that number changed?"

Silence. Long enough to count.

"Ten," Maren said finally, because lying twice is easier than explaining why you lied once, and I understood with a sick lurch that she had just handed them the shape of me. A stranger, present, hidden, unnamed. They hadn't known. Now they knew the exact weight of what they were looking for.

"Ten," the Graycloak repeated, tasting it. "Then someone found their way in. Fog or no fog." He reached into his gray sleeve and produced—not a weapon, worse—a small folded paper, sealed with gray wax. "A writ of amnesty. For the thief, should he surrender himself within three days. His Majesty is merciful to those who return what isn't theirs." He set it on the gatepost, weighted it with a stone, and stepped back. "And for the household that shelters him, should he decline. Mercy has a wide margin, elder. But it is a margin, not a country."

They bowed—actually bowed, courteous to the last—and walked back down the road into the fog, and the fog closed behind them like water over stones, and only then did the whole yard exhale.

* * *

They convened the council that evening, all nine of them plus me, which made ten, which made the number true whether I liked it or not. They met in the scriptorium, around the long table where the founders' names were carved into the wood and worn nearly smooth by centuries of elbows. I sat in the corner with a blanket over my shoulders like some grandmother's ghost, and Ada stood behind my chair with her arms crossed, and between us we presented a united front of people who had already decided something the elders hadn't.

"Three days," Elder Coram said. He was the eldest, a stooped man whose hands shook unless he was doing something precise, at which point they became instruments. "Three days is not a threat. It's arithmetic. They'll wait at the ridgeline, count our chimneys, and come back with enough men to make arithmetic irrelevant."

"Or they'll report that the valley shelters a fugitive," said Elder Tam, younger, sharper, "and the crown will decide Hollowmere has outlived its usefulness. The fog thins, they said. Did you hear that? They've been measuring."

"The fog has stood for six hundred years," said Elder Brannoc.

"The fog has never had to stand against *them*," Tam shot back.

I let them go around the table twice. It cost me something to stay quiet—I am, by training and temperament, a man who believes every problem can be solved with the correct sentence—but these were their walls, their dead, their valley, and I had already taken enough from them. So I listened, and what I heard was nine good people discovering, one objection at a time, that their safety had been a loan and the collectors had arrived.

Finally Coram looked at me. "Courier. You've been silent."

"I'm enjoying the debate," I said. "It's very civilized. Where I come from, this conversation happens with the building already on fire."

"Wren." Ada's voice, low, warning.

"No, he's right to ask." I pushed the blanket off and stood, and my side screamed its opinion, and I kept my face pleasant through sheer professional habit. "So here is my sentence, since no one else will say it. You cannot keep me. Every hour I stay, their arithmetic gets worse. They know a stranger is here. They know he's wounded, because wounded men don't move, and they know the valley won't give him up, because if you would have given him up, you'd have done it at the gate with smiles and been done with it. Which means in three days—or sooner—they stop asking politely. And when the Graycloaks stop asking politely, elder, they do not burn the guilty house. They burn the village. It's policy. I wrote the policy." I let that land. "In a beautiful hand."

The silence afterward had texture. Somewhere a log shifted in the hearth.

"You stole from the king," Brannoc said at last. "What could a scribe possibly steal that's worth this?"

"A map," I said. "To the place where the seal was forged. To the truth about why your valley exists at all." I looked around the table, at nine faces that had spent their lives inside a miracle none of them had ever questioned. "Your founders didn't flee magic. They imprisoned it. They built Hollowmere as a lock, and the lock is failing, and the thing on the throne knows it, and that map is the keyhole. That's what I stole. That's what's worth your village."

"And what would you do with it?" Coram asked quietly. "A dying man with a hole in him?"

"Deliver it," I said. "Home."

"To where?"

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was *to the heir*, and the honest answer after that was *I don't fully know who the heir is yet, only that the map's heart bears a founder's mark and the name beside it is Ashgrove*, and I had watched Ada copy that ledger for three days and had said nothing, because some truths need better timing than fever and firelight provide. Guilt, I was learning, comes in layers. First you endanger the sanctuary. Then you hide things from the girl nursing you. The strata accumulate.

"Then it leaves," Ada said.

Every head turned. She hadn't moved from behind my chair, but her voice had changed—it had dropped the valley's softness and picked up an edge I recognized from the lowlands, from people who had decided something while everyone else was still deliberating.

"The map leaves. Tomorrow. Before their three days are half spent." She looked at Maren. "And I take it out, because I'm the one who found him at the ford, and because—" a half-second pause, barely perceptible, "—because I've been reading the ledger, elder, and I have questions the map can answer. Questions with our name on them."

"That's not a decision for—"

"It's exactly a decision for someone young enough to make it," Ada said, "since it's our valley that burns otherwise. With respect."

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and something passed between them that I couldn't read—some old argument, or the end of one. Then the elder said, tiredly, "The council votes at dawn," and I understood that the vote was theater, that it had already been decided in the space between those two women, and that Ada Ashgrove had just volunteered for a journey she could not imagine the size of.

* * *

Afterward, past midnight, she found me in the gallery where I'd gone to cough in private and feel sorry for myself with some dignity. Snow ticked against the shutters. Below, the courtyard lay white and blank again, the writ still pinned under its stone on the gatepost, gray wax catching moonlight like a scar.

"You knew they'd come," she said. Not accusing. Worse—curious.

"I hoped they wouldn't. I knew they might. There's a difference, and the difference is roughly the width of your valley."

"Why didn't you tell us? When I pulled you out of the ford. You could have said *wardens behind me* and we'd have—what, exactly?" She almost smiled. "Barred the door?"

"Yes," I said. "And it wouldn't have mattered, and you'd have hated me slightly less than you're going to."

"I don't hate you."

"You should. It's efficient." I leaned against the sill and looked out at the fog on the ridgeline, faintly luminous even at night, the valley's oldest promise. "Do you know what guilt feels like, Ada? It feels like this. Like being warm in a house you've set alight. Everyone in this valley fed me soup and changed my bandages and voted in committee about my life, and the entire time, I was the reason men in gray wool were standing at their gate doing arithmetic. I've been the crown's pen for four years. I told myself I was just a scribe, that ink isn't a weapon. But I wrote the warrants. I wrote the *policy*. And now the policy is standing in your snow, being polite."

She was quiet a while. Outside, the wind moved the fog, and for a moment the ridge showed clear—black trees, white sky, the whole enormous world I'd dragged to her doorstep.

"Maren lied badly," Ada said at last. "Coram wants to hand you over. Brannoc quoted scripture. And I stood in front of the whole council and announced I'm leaving, and none of them stopped me, and I think—" she exhaled, "—I think that frightened me more than the Graycloaks did."

"Good," I said. "Fear means you understand the stakes. Understanding the stakes is the entire job."

"Is that what you told yourself? In the palace, when you stole it?"

"What I told myself," I said, "was that a dead man I loved used to send letters out through the sigil, little scraps of himself, begging someone to come. And that I was the only one still alive who could read his handwriting." I looked at her. "Everything else followed from that. Including, apparently, your gate."

She studied me with those ledger-keeper's eyes, filing something away. Then she said, "Dawn, then. Pack light. And Wren—if you ever let me walk into a room blind again, I'll finish what the wound started."

"Noted," I said, and meant it, and slept badly, and dreamed of gray wool and white flags and a king's handwriting growing smaller and smaller, like a man waving from a shore that was receding.

In the morning the vote would be held, and lost, and we would go over the ridge into a world that had been waiting six hundred years for someone to open the lock—and I would carry the map, and the guilt, and the growing, inconvenient hope that the girl with ink on her knuckles was exactly who I thought she was.

The fog, when we crossed it, parted like a held breath finally let go.
