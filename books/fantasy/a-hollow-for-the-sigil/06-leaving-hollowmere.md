## 6. Leaving Hollowmere

The elders voted against me by a margin of eight to one, which I considered generous, since the ninth vote was mine.

"It's out of the question," Elder Maren said, in the tone she used for weather and other unstoppable things. "No member of this guild has crossed the ridge in two hundred years. The fog keeps us. That is its purpose. That has always been its purpose."

"The fog kept *him* out," I said, jerking a thumb toward the sickroom where Wren slept through our democracy. "Mostly. It let him through the ford like an invited guest. Whatever it used to do, Maren, it's tired. And if the Graycloaks found the ford once, they'll find it again, and next time they won't send one dying man with good manners."

We were in the guild hall, all nine of us, under the rafters my grandfather had re-thatched and his grandfather before him, around the long table where the guild had presumably once made decisions that mattered. Now we made decisions about roof pitch and goat fencing. The map lay in the middle of the table like a guest nobody would look at directly, leather tube unrolled, its bloodstained corner curling.

Elder Tam puttered his pipe. "Send it back out with no one. Float it down the river."

"And when they catch it?" I asked. "A map with no courier tells them exactly what they want to know: that Hollowmere has it, and Hollowmere can't follow."

"Then we burn it," said Elder Coss, who was eighty-three and had opinions like kidney stones.

"Burn it," I repeated. "Burn the only thing anyone has ever brought to this valley that matters. Burn the answer to whatever Wren is dying of. Burn—" I stopped, because the next word in my mouth was *mine*, and I didn't yet understand why it wanted to be there.

They looked at me. Nine faces, mostly kind, all frightened in the specific way of people who have spent their whole lives being safe on purpose.

Maren sighed the sigh of a woman who had raised me after my parents died and knew precisely how this argument would end because she'd watched me argue my way into trouble since I was four. "Ada."

"I'll take it out myself." I said it before I could sand the edges off it. "I go up through the fog, over the ridge, alone. No wagon tracks, no smoke, nothing for them to trace back here. If they see me at all, they see one girl going one direction, away from you. The valley stays hidden. The valley stays safe. Nobody else risks anything."

"That's not how risk works," Tam said mildly. "Risk pools. It finds the low places. You'd be the low place, child."

"I've been the low place my whole life," I said. "I'm practiced."

Nobody laughed. I hadn't really expected them to, but there was a moment—there always is—where I hoped someone would tell me I was being dramatic, that the world outside the fog was ordinary, that couriers bled ordinary blood, that maps were just maps. Instead Elder Coss looked at her hands, and Maren looked at the window, where the first snow of the season was coming down soft as an apology.

"The vote stands," Maren said finally. "Eight against. You are not to leave."

"You need a two-thirds majority to stop me from leaving," I said. "It's in the charter. Article eleven. I copied it out last winter when I was bored."

"You copied the charter?"

"I copy everything. It's the only thing I'm for."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was not looking at me as an elder looks at an unruly junior. She was looking at me the way you look at a door swinging open onto a room you thought was bricked up. "Two-thirds," she said. "Six votes required to bar your exit. We have eight."

"Then count again," I said, "because I'm telling you now: I'm going. Whether the guild sanctions it or whether the guild wakes tomorrow to find its ledger missing one name. Choose which version goes in the record."

That's when the door opened and Wren leaned in the frame, gray-faced, holding himself upright by what appeared to be spite. His wound had been bound in three layers of linen and he'd sweated through all of them overnight; the faint glow beneath the bandages had dimmed but not gone out, like coals under ash.

"She's right about article eleven," he said. "I read the charter too. While everyone was asleep. You have a surprisingly litigious sanctuary."

"Go back to bed," Maren said.

"In a moment." He came into the room one careful step at a time, and every step cost him, and he paid anyway. "You should know something before you decide. If she goes alone, she dies. Not might. Will. The Graycloaks don't chase lone travelers—they harvest them. Two travelers who look like they know where they're going get followed cautiously. One traveler gets collected quietly and the collection leads back here eventually, because wardens are patient and valleys don't move." He set both hands flat on the table. "I'm going with her."

"You can barely stand," Coss said.

"I can walk downhill," Wren said. "The whole route is downhill. Providence."

"This is madness," said Elder Pell, who had contributed nothing else to the evening and seemed satisfied with it.

"It is entirely madness," Wren agreed. "But it's *aimed* madness, which is more than most madness manages. Elders, I am sorry. I came here to save my life and I will be gone from it within the day, taking the danger with me. That is the best trade I can offer you, and it's better than the truth, which is that staying was never safe. Your fog is thinning. I felt it at the ford, before I ever saw the water. Whatever sealed this valley is failing, and when it fails fully, no vote of yours will matter. Let her go while going still means something."

The silence afterward had texture. Snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, the goats objected to the weather on principle.

Maren looked at me for a long time. "You understand," she said, "that if you cross the ridge, the fog may not let you back."

"I've understood that since I was six years old," I said. "It's the reason I'm going."

* * *

Packing took an hour, most of which I spent arguing with myself about books. I settled on the ledger—not the whole ledger, but the pages I'd copied of the oldest names, folded into oilcloth and tucked against my ribs. If I was going to die in a ditch somewhere, I intended to die knowing whose name was written next to mine in a hand four hundred years dead. Ashgrove. The founder's mark on the map matched it exactly, the little interlocked circles, and no one in this valley would talk to me about it, so the road would have to.

Wren met me at the stable door wearing a guild cloak that hung off him like a tent and an expression of grim competence he was clearly manufacturing on the spot.

"You should be lying down," I said.

"I have been lying down for four days. It's done nothing for the wound and ruined my posture." He swung—swayed—onto the mule I'd saddled for him, a placid animal named Biscuit who regarded him with the resignation of a creature that had survived many owners. "Besides, if I fall off in the snow, you'll feel obligated to bury me properly, and burial takes time you won't have. Incentive structure. I think clearly."

"You think feverishly."

"Fever is just thinking with weather."

Maren was waiting at the edge of the yard with a bundle: bread, cheese, a jar of the bitter green salve the guild brewed for chilblains and heartbreak alike. She pressed it into my hands and then held on.

"When I was young," she said, without preamble, "there was a woman who left. Before your time. Before even Tam's. Her name was Odile and she said the same things you said tonight, almost word for word, standing in almost the same spot. The fog took her and the valley forgot her by spring, the way it forgets everything. But I remembered. I've remembered for fifty years, Ada, and I want you to understand what I'm saying when I tell you—" her grip tightened— "*come back.* Forget the map. Forget the king, forget the seal, forget whatever blood is doing in your veins that makes the elders flinch. None of it is worth what it costs to be forgotten by a whole valley. Come back, or send word, or find some third thing I haven't thought of. But do not make me the one who remembers."

"Maren—"

"Go," she said, and let go, and turned back toward the house before I could see her face properly, which was a mercy she extended often and never acknowledged.

* * *

The climb to the ridgeline took until midafternoon. The snow thickened as we rose, fat flakes falling with the unhurried confidence of weather that has never been in a hurry in its life. Below us Hollowmere shrank to a handful of roofs and threads of chimney smoke, and then the fog took even that, folding the valley away like a letter being sealed.

Wren rode in silence for a long stretch. I assumed he was conserving strength until he said, quietly, "The fog isn't pushing us back."

I had noticed. I'd been trying very hard not to notice. All my life the fog at the ridgeline had been described to me as a wall—a thing you walked into and walked out of again on the far side, turned around, your own footprints mocking you. Every story agreed. Every elder confirmed it. Children were warned off it the way children elsewhere were warned off rivers.

But the fog wasn't resisting. It parted ahead of Biscuit's nose and closed behind us, gentle as a hand smoothing a blanket, and when I reached out—just my bare hand, just to test—it moved *around* my fingers rather than through them, the way water moves around a stone it has decided to keep.

"It knows me," I said. My voice came out strange. Smaller than I meant it.

"Or it remembers you," Wren said. "Those aren't always different things, in old workings. Hold out your arm."

"Why?"

"Humor the dying man."

I held out my arm. He didn't touch me. He held his palm above my wrist, close enough that I felt warmth without contact, and the fog—the fog *leaned*. All around us, the grey air tilted toward my skin like grass toward sun, and where it touched, it did not chill. It hummed. A vibration too low to hear and impossible not to feel, settling into my bones like a tuning fork pressed to the teeth.

"Stop," I whispered, and he withdrew his hand, and the fog eased back, disappointed, a dog told to stay.

"What was that?" My heart was going hard enough that I could hear it in my ears. "What did you *do*?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point." Wren's face, above the borrowed cloak, had gone carefully blank in the way of a man deciding how much truth a situation can bear. "In the lowlands, when a warden walks past a hearth-shrine, the sigils go quiet—hide, the way small animals hide. What you just did was the opposite. The fog came to *you*. Ada, when we're past the ridge, there will be magic in the world. Real magic, working magic, the kind your valley was built to keep out. And it is going to notice you the way the fog just did. Possibly worse."

"Worse how?"

"I genuinely don't know. That's what 'worse' means."

I laughed. It escaped before I could vet it—high and slightly unhinged—and Wren smiled despite himself, and for a moment we were just two idiots on a mule laughing in the snow, and I loved that moment with a fierceness that embarrassed me.

Then we crested the ridge, and I stopped loving anything except the view, because the view was *the world*.

Hollowmere sits in its bowl and I had seen mountains my whole life, the same ringed horizon, the same five peaks with the names the guild gave them. I had not understood—I had genuinely, truly not understood—that beyond them there was *more*. Range behind range behind range, blue-grey into white into a distance that did not stop so much as give up being visible. Valleys stitched with rivers that caught the low sun and burned like spilled metal. Forests going on for days. And threaded through all of it, faint as veins under skin, lines of light that I realized, with a lurch, were not geological. They were *written*. The land itself was inscribed, sigil-work vast enough to be mistaken for nature, and every line of it pulsed—slowly, sleepily, the way a heart beats in sleep.

"Oh," I said. It was not a word. It was the sound a lock makes.

And then the cold water found the crack.

There's no other way I can describe it. All my life I had been dry—dry as the guild hall, dry as the unread ledger, dry as the names of people I'd copied out without ever meeting. I hadn't known I was dry. Dryness doesn't announce itself. But standing on that ridgeline with the world's magic pulsing beneath it like a sleeping tide, I felt the first thread of it slide into me through some seam I hadn't known I had, and the sensation was exactly, precisely, unbearably like cold water finding a crack in a wall you believed was sound.

It hurt. It was wonderful. It was both at once and neither cancelled the other out, terror and exhilaration braided so tightly I couldn't have told you which end was which, and I gripped Biscuit's mane and gasped like a woman surfacing and Wren was saying my name, saying it twice, three times—

"Ada. *Ada.* Breathe. Look at your hands."

I looked. Frost was climbing my knuckles in patterns. Delicate, deliberate patterns—interlocking circles.

The founder's mark. Blooming out of my own skin like the world's most alarming embroidery.

"They'll see that," Wren said softly. He wasn't looking at my hands. He was looking past me, down the far slope, to where the pines broke around a frozen river and the river road ran silver toward the lowlands—and where, tiny with distance but unmistakable, a column of riders in grey was moving along the bank, unhurried, methodical, sweeping the ground the way you sweep a floor you intend to live on.

Graycloaks. A dozen of them at least. Heading, at their own leisure, toward the ford below Hollowmere.

"How fast can Biscuit go downhill?" I asked.

Wren gathered the reins. "Faster than dignity," he said. "Hold on to something. Preferably me."

I dug my heels in, and the frost-flowered hand that grabbed his sleeve was shaking—but only half from fear.
