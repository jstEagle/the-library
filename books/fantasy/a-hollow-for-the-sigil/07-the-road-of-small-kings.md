## 7. The Road of Small Kings

I have walked this road before. That's the first thing you should know about me: I am a man who has walked most roads in this country at least twice, once as a scribe with clean boots and a royal warrant, and once as a thief with neither. The road south out of Hollowmere is the same either way. It doesn't care what you carry. It only cares how fast.

Ada cared about everything else.

"Whose shrine is that?" she asked, an hour past the ridge, when the fog finally let go of us and the world opened up gray and white and endless. She had stopped in the middle of the track, pointing with her chin at a squat stone box beside a farmhouse gate, its little roof furred with snow.

"A hearth-shrine," I said. "Keep walking."

"It has a sigil on it."

"I said keep walking."

She didn't keep walking. She never kept walking. That was going to be a problem, I could already tell—the kind of problem that gets people killed slowly, between one stubbornness and the next. She crouched by the stone, brushed the snow from its face, and there it was: a coiled mark cut deep into old granite, filled with lichen the color of old blood.

"That's outlawed," she said. Not a question. She'd read my letters over my sleeping shoulder more than once since the ford, and I'd stopped pretending I didn't know.

"Outlawed for sixty years," I said. "That one's older than the law. Look at the wear. Somebody's grandfather cut it, and somebody's grandmother fed it, and nobody's told them to stop." I nodded up the lane, where woodsmoke rose straight as a plumb line from the farmhouse chimney. "There are thousands of these. Every village between here and the capital has three or four. The Graycloaks burn the ones they find. The villagers carve new ones. It's the slowest war ever fought, and both sides are losing."

"To who?"

"To winter," I said, before I could stop myself. "To the crown. To time. Pick your poison."

She looked at me then—she had a way of looking at me like I was a ledger with a page torn out—and said nothing, which was worse than anything she could have said. Then she stood, dusted her knees, and touched the sigil with two fingers, light as a woman testing bathwater.

The lichen flushed green.

Not metaphorically. Not a trick of the light. The lichen on a dead stone shrine, in the dead heart of winter, turned green as June, and the frost on the shrine's roof slid off all at once like a dog shaking off rain, and somewhere under the snow a bulb decided, against all sense, to come up.

"Ada," I said carefully, "what did you do?"

"Nothing. I just—" She snatched her hand back like the stone had bitten her. "It was warm. For a second it was warm."

I looked down the lane. The chimney smoke still rose. No door had opened. But I had spent six years learning what power felt like when it moved through the world, back when I wrote warrants for it instead of running from it, and what I felt just now was not a trickle. It was a door thrown open in a house everyone thought was empty.

"We need to teach you to close doors," I said.

* * *

We made camp that night in a shepherd's hut above a village called Brackenlow, which consisted of eleven houses, one shrine-keeper, and a great deal of opinion about strangers. I built the fire small, because small fires are honest, and I took a stick and drew in the ash-floor while Ada watched with her arms crossed like I was about to sell her something.

"Warding is not drawing," I said. "Anyone can draw. Children draw sigils in the dirt and nothing happens, because a sigil without intent is just a scar. Warding is drawing plus wanting. You hold the shape in your mind and you hold the purpose behind it, and you push them into the same place at the same time. Like threading a needle in the dark."

"What does it feel like? The pushing."

I hesitated. Honest answer: it felt like wringing water from a stone, like paying out of a purse that never refilled, which is why most hedge-wards were small and mean and lasted a season. But I watched her fingers, the ones that had turned lichen green with a touch, and I lied a little.

"Cold," I said. "Like reaching into a river. Now try. Small circle. Intent: keep things out. Nothing grander than that."

She drew the circle. She wanted the want. And the ward came up around our hut like a cathedral bell going off.

I don't mean it glowed. I mean the whole valley knew. Snow slid off trees in a fifty-yard ring. The fire bent flat. My teeth ached. And the ward itself—a thing I had expected to be a soap-bubble shimmer, a hedge-witch's trick—rose over us in pale geometry, layered, interlocking, the kind of work I'd seen guild-masters take a week over, done in the time it takes to blink.

Ada sat back on her heels, looking faintly horrified. "Was that too much?"

"The sheep in the next valley felt that."

"Is that bad?"

"Ada." I rubbed my eyes. "Wards are signatures. Every warden's work reads differently, like handwriting. And yours—" I gestured at the impossible lattice humming over our heads. "Yours reads like *her*. Like the founder. If any Graycloak within ten miles has half an education, they now know exactly where we are, and exactly what you are, and 'what you are' is worth more to them than the map."

She was quiet a long moment. The ward hummed overhead, beautiful and damning.

"So I'm a beacon," she said finally.

"You're a bonfire wearing a cloak made of candles."

"And you're teaching me anyway."

"I'm teaching you anyway," I agreed, "because the alternative is you touching shrines by accident and turning whole hillsides green. At least if I teach you, we can aim you."

She almost smiled at that. Almost. "Aim me at what?"

"At closing," I said. "Tomorrow we start with shutting doors instead of opening them. Pulling the power back through the needle. It's harder. Most people can't do it at all."

"Can you?"

"I can do it slowly and badly, like everything else I do."

She lay down by the fire then, and I sat up keeping watch, which was mostly watching the ward and thinking about what it meant that a girl from a valley with no magic in it had produced work like that on her first attempt. I thought about the ledger. I thought about the name beside hers in the oldest hand. I thought about the founder pressing her palm into a seal, though I didn't yet know that part—I only knew the shape of the story, the way you know a storm is coming by the way birds go quiet.

Above us, the ward held. Below us, in Brackenlow, someone had noticed the light on their hillside, and was climbing up to see.

* * *

Her name was Odell, and she was seventy if she was a day, and she carried a lantern and a shotgun older than the kingdom, and she stood outside our hut at dawn and said, "Either you're guild or you're dead, and dead folk don't ward like that."

I got between her and Ada, which was reflex more than strategy. "Ma'am—"

"Shrine-keeper," she said, as if that explained the shotgun. In a way it did; I'd met shrine-keepers before, the underground clergy of a banned faith, the men and women who fed the hearth-sigils and memorized the rites and buried the carved stones in their gardens when the Graycloaks came through. "Sixty years I've kept the Brackenlow stones. Sixty years, and I've seen wards maybe a dozen times, real ones, and none like last night's. So." She lowered the gun an inch. "Who taught the girl?"

"No one," Ada said, from behind my shoulder. "He tried. It just... happens."

Odell looked at her for a long time. Then she did something I didn't expect: she laughed, short and dry as a stick breaking. "Come down to the village and have breakfast, and don't ward anything else until I say so. There's bread. There's always bread; that's the point of me."

We went, because bread is a powerful argument and because refusing hospitality in a village of eleven houses marks you faster than any sigil. Her cottage smelled of yeast and tallow and something underneath both—old magic, banked and patient, the smell of a house that had been keeping secrets for generations. On her mantel sat five small stones, each carved with a sigil, each worn smooth by decades of touching.

"They're warm," Ada said, staring at them.

"They're fed," Odell corrected. "Every morning, first thing, before the kettle. You feed a sigil a drop of blood and a minute of attention, and it keeps the frost off your well and the rot out your beams and the nightmares out your children's heads. Little magics. Kitchen magics. All that's left legal enough to hide." She set down bowls of porridge thick as mortar. "Used to be more. Used to be the guilds taught proper craft, before the crown decided magic belonged to one man and his crown alone."

"The Hollowed King," I said.

"Don't say it loud," Odell said, not looking at me. "Even his name draws ears, these days. But yes. Him. Or the thing that answers when you say him." She finally sat, folding her hands. "Graycloaks came through in autumn. Took the Miller boy. Said he'd been carving. He was twelve. He was carving a *duck*, but they said he'd been carving." Her voice stayed level the way ice stays level—you could walk on it right up until you couldn't. "So understand me, girl. Whatever you are, whatever's in you, you are a danger to every soul in this village while you sit at my table. And I'm feeding you anyway. Because sixty years I've asked the stones for a sign, and last night the whole hillside lit up like a wedding, and I think you're it."

"Think I'm what?" Ada asked.

"The reason," Odell said simply, "the stones haven't gone cold yet."

* * *

The Graycloaks came at noon.

We heard them before we saw them—horses on frozen ground have a particular sound, a muffled drumming, and Odell's head came up from her knitting like a hound's. "Two riders, maybe three, coming up the mill road. Back door. Now."

"How many patrols run this road?" I asked, already moving, because Wren the thief and Wren the scribe both understood that questions were for afterward.

"In winter? None. They don't ride the high roads after first snow." She was shoving bread and cheese into a cloth with the calm of a woman who had packed fugitives before. "Which means they're not patrolling. They're *looking*. For something specific." She fixed me with a look. "For you, I'd wager. Or for her."

Through the back window I saw them: three riders in gray, unhurried, their cloaks moving wrong—too smooth, like the cloth was remembering being something else. Graycloaks weren't men in uniforms, exactly. They were men *worn* by something, the way gloves are worn by hands. Their captain raised a hand, and the three of them stopped at Odell's gate, and the cold came off them even at forty yards. I watched frost bloom across Odell's windowpane in fern-patterns, spreading from the side nearest the road.

"They'll knock polite," Odell murmured. "They always start polite. Under the floor. There's a root cellar, and under the cellar boards there's the old shrine-crypt, and under that—" she almost smiled— "under that there's a tunnel the guild dug when the guild still dug tunnels. It comes up in the mill. Don't come back this way. If you made it, send word that it worked. Whatever 'it' ends up being."

Ada grabbed her hands. "You can't—"

"Girl, I've hidden smugglers, deserters, a runaway bride, and once an entire traveling theater. Two more under my floorboards is Tuesday." She pressed something into Ada's palm—a small carved stone, a hearth-sigil no bigger than a coin. "Feed it when you need light or warmth. A drop of blood and a minute of attention. And—" she glanced at me, and her voice dropped, "—teach her to pull it *in*, scribe. What she did on that hillside wasn't casting. It was leaking. There's a difference, and the difference is whether the crown sends three riders or thirty."

The knock came. Polite. Three raps, evenly spaced, like a clock deciding.

Odell squared her shoulders, picked up her knitting, and became, in the space of a breath, a slightly deaf old woman annoyed at visitors. I pulled Ada down through the trapdoor, into the dark, past the root cellar smells of turnip and earth, down to where boards covered stone older than the cottage, and beneath our feet the guild's old tunnel ran cold and dry toward the mill and the river and the south.

We crouched in the dark and listened. Above us, muffled through floorboards and earth, the polite voices. Odell's, cracked and querulous. The captain's, smooth as poured cream, asking after travelers, after a girl, after *a warding of unusual character observed on the hillside*.

And then silence, the particular silence of a Graycloak listening—not with ears.

Ada's hand found mine in the dark. Hers was burning hot; mine was shaking. Above us, the frost-ferns on the cottage windows would be growing, I knew, reaching toward the heat of what hid below, the crown's hunger reading the air the way a blind man reads a face.

"They felt it," she whispered. "Last night. My ward. They've been riding toward it all morning."

"Yes."

"Then hiding won't matter. They'll follow the leak wherever we go. I'll lead them to every village, every shrine, every—" Her voice cracked, and in the dark I heard the thing underneath it, the thing she'd been carrying since the hillside: not fear of the Graycloaks. Fear of herself. Of being the bonfire, the beacon, the thing that burns everything it loves just by shining.

So I did the stupid thing. The true thing. I squeezed her hand and said, "Then we stop leaking and start aiming. Odell's right—there's a difference. Your power isn't the problem, Ada. It's never been the problem. Unaimed, it's a flare. Aimed—" I grinned in the dark where no one could see it, which is the best place to grin. "Aimed, it's a weapon. And I have been thinking, all morning, about what a weapon like you could do to a crown."

Above us, the captain's voice said something we couldn't catch, and boots crossed the floor toward the kitchen, toward the trapdoor, toward us—

—and Ada, without being taught, without knowing how, pulled.

I felt it happen. Felt the heat of her hand gutter like a candle pinched out, felt the great bright pressure of her vanish—not dimmed, *folded*, tucked away behind something small and dense and utterly silent. The frost-ferns on the windows above stopped growing. The captain's boots paused. And in the dark under the earth, a girl from a valley with no magic in it did in one terrified instant what I had just told her took years to learn.

The boots retreated. Voices faded. Horses, muffled drumming, gone.

We sat in the black a long time, holding hands like children, listening to Odell's knitting needles resume upstairs, steady as rain.

"Well," Ada said at last, unsteady. "Was that—"

"That," I said, "was the first sensible thing your power has ever done. Which means it's learnable. Which means—" and here the thief in me woke up and started doing arithmetic, dangerous, hopeful arithmetic, "—which means by the time we reach the capital, they won't find you by your light."

I felt her turn toward me in the dark. "They'll find me how?"

"They won't," I said. "That's the point. We're going to walk into the Hollowed King's own city invisible, and he is going to have no idea that the key to his cage just knocked on his door."

Somewhere ahead of us, down the cold throat of the guild's old tunnel, water dripped in the dark—the mill, the river, the road south, and beyond them a palace full of smiles and a throne that ate the people who knelt to it.

Ada stood, and lit the little hearth-stone with a drop of blood, and its warmth was small and perfect and ours.

"Then let's not keep him waiting," she said.
