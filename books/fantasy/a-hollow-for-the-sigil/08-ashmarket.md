## 8. Ashmarket

The city announced itself by smell before it showed itself at all: woodsmoke and tar, frying onions, something sweet and rotten underneath that Wren called "money." We came down out of the hills on the fourth morning since the shrine-keeper's barn, following a road that grew steadily more crowded and less honest, until the trees gave up entirely and Ashmarket rose out of the lowland haze like a bad decision made permanent.

I had never seen so many buildings in my life. I want to be clear about that. Hollowmere has forty-one roofs, and I have climbed every one of them, and I thought I understood what a settlement was. Ashmarket had *districts*. It had rooftops stacked on rooftops like crooked teeth, and chimneys breathing in shifts, and a harbor where ships sat so thick you could have walked across their masts to the far shore if you'd been mad enough to try.

"It smells like a tannery married a bakery," I said.

"That's Ashmarket," Wren said. He was leaning on his staff—my staff, technically, requisitioned from the guild house without formal vote—and he had gone gray around the mouth twice on the road already. The wound under his coat had stopped bleeding days ago, which should have been good news, except that the skin around it had gone the color of old candle wax, and sometimes when he thought I wasn't looking he pressed his palm flat against it like a man holding a door shut.

"We find Vess," he said, "we sell nothing, we learn everything, we leave before dark."

"You've said this four times."

"Because you keep looking at the city like it's going to adopt you."

"I'm looking at it because it's enormous," I said. "And because nobody here is staring at us. Do you understand what that's like? In Hollowmere, if I walk past the well, six people know what I bought and three of them have opinions about it."

Wren looked at me for a moment with an expression I couldn't read—something between pity and recognition—and then he said, "Enjoy it while it lasts. Anonymity is just being unknown. It isn't the same as being free."

I didn't know yet how right he was. Give me credit: I learned fast.

* * *

Vess kept her shop in a street called the Tanners' Cut, which was not a street so much as a disagreement between two buildings, and her shopfront was a door with no sign, no handle on the outside, and a knocker shaped like a closed eye. Wren knocked the eyelid once, paused, then three times quick.

The door opened on a chain. One brown eye appeared in the gap, took in Wren, dismissed him, found me, and narrowed.

"You're not his usual sort," the eye said.

"He doesn't have a usual sort anymore," Wren said. "His usual sort are all dead or gray."

A long pause. Then the chain rattled off and the door swung wide, and Vess turned out to be a small woman somewhere between fifty and forever, with silver-streaked hair cut brutally short and hands stained with ink to the second knuckle. She wore an apron covered in map-fragments sewn on like scales. She looked at Wren the way a physician looks at a patient who has come in too late, and then she looked at me again, longer, and whatever she saw made her step back and let us in.

Her shop was a cave of paper. Maps hung from every surface—rolled, pinned, layered—and the smell of ink and lamp oil was strong enough to chew. She cleared two stools with her boot, poured something amber into three cups without asking, and waited until Wren drank before she touched her own. Old habit, I guessed. Poison is a professional courtesy among people like her.

"The Graycloaks swept the river quarter two nights ago," she said. "They're asking after a courier. Feverish. Carrying a tube."

"Are they asking politely?" Wren asked.

"They're always polite. That's the horror of them." She set down her cup. "So. You came to me because you need to move, or you need to know, or you need to disappear. Which is it?"

"Know," Wren said, and nodded at me, and I understood that this part was mine.

I unslung the leather tube and drew out the map with the care you'd give a sleeping animal. I'd gotten better at handling it over the miles; it had opinions about being handled roughly, a faint resistance, like a cat deciding whether to forgive you. I spread it on her worktable and weighted the corners with ink pots.

Vess leaned over it with a loupe she pulled from behind her ear, and for a long moment there was no sound in the shop but her breathing and the creak of the building settling.

Then she went pale.

Not surprised-pale. Not frightened-pale. I watched the color leave her face the way water leaves a sink, all at once and completely, and when she straightened up her hand was shaking and she put it flat on the table to hide it, which fooled neither of us.

"Where did you get this," she said. It wasn't a question. Questions go up at the end.

"A dying man gave it to me," I said. "Why? What is it?"

She didn't answer me. She looked at Wren instead, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from a smuggler scared me more than shouting would have. "Do you know what you're carrying, courier?"

"I know what it leads to," Wren said.

"Do you know what it *is*?"

"Tell us," I said. My voice came out harder than I intended. "You've seen seal-work before. Everyone keeps saying words around me—sigil, binding, key—and nobody will say what they mean. So say it."

Vess looked at me again, and this time she held it, and I felt like a specimen pinned under glass.

"This isn't guild-work," she said. "This isn't even founder-era copywork. This is original. First-hand. Pre-Concordance script, the kind they burned the manuals for." Her finger hovered above the heart of the map, above the mark I'd spent nights cross-referencing against our ledger—the mark that sat beside my own surname in handwriting older than the valley itself. "Every sigil-craft in the world today is a copy of a copy of a copy. Degraded. Safe. *Small.* This—" she tapped the air an inch above the parchment, not touching it, and I noticed her finger trembled even at that distance "—this is one of the originals. A Founder's Key. And there were only ever three."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"Three," I repeated.

"Two to unlock. One to bind." She said it flatly, like reciting a price list, but her eyes never left my face. "The founders split the great working into three pieces so no one person could ever hold it whole. Two keys open the way. The third—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd said one word more than she meant to. "The third completes it. That's all anyone outside knows. That's all *I'm* supposed to know."

"And the Graycloaks," Wren said quietly, "want all three."

"The Graycloaks want what the crown wants." Vess straightened, and some of the color was coming back into her face now, the merchant reasserting itself over whatever the merchant had briefly ceased to be. "Which brings us to terms. I'll pay you for it. Well. Very well. Enough that you could vanish—new names, new faces, papers for any port on the coast. The kind of money that makes a person unfindable."

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the number."

"It doesn't matter. It's not mine to sell."

"Everything is someone's to sell," Vess said. "That's the first law of Ashmarket."

"Then it's the wrong law," I said, and rolled the map up before she could touch it again.

But she wasn't finished, and this was the part I hadn't braced for. She came around the table, slow, and stood close, and spoke low, in the voice of a woman offering not goods but absolution.

"Girl. Look at me. I've been selling escapes for thirty years. I know what people run from, and I know what they run *to*, and I can tell you exactly what you are, because I've sold this exact thing a hundred times." She tapped my hand, once, where it gripped the tube. "You're tired. You've been tired your whole life—in that valley, keeping a dead house alive, copying names into books nobody reads, waiting for something to happen to somebody else. Am I close?"

I said nothing. She was uncomfortably close.

"Take my offer and it ends. All of it. The wardens, the crown, the fog, the ledger, the *duty*. I'll put you on a ship with a new name and in a year you won't remember which parts of your life were real. There are islands where no Graycloak has ever set foot. There are cities where 'Ashgrove' means nothing at all." Her voice softened further. "You could disappear forever. Some people would kill for that. Some people *have*."

And here is the thing I'm not proud of: for about four seconds, I wanted it.

Not the islands, exactly. The *quiet*. The version of me that had never found a drowned courier at a ford, never read her own name in a dead woman's handwriting, never learned that the fog over Hollowmere was a lid on something vast and failing. Four seconds of standing in a shop that smelled of ink and money, holding an exit in my hands, wanting nothing more than to put it down.

Then I thought of Wren pressing his palm against his wound like a man holding a door. I thought of Elder Maren lying badly to two polite monsters. I thought of the ledger, and the empty pages after the last name, and how I'd always assumed the book ended because the guild died—but maybe it ended because it was waiting.

"No," I said again, and this time it came out steady. "I spent twenty-two years disappearing. It turns out I was very good at it and it accomplished nothing. I'm done."

Vess studied me for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, genuine bark, surprised out of her—and stepped back.

"Pity," she said. "You'd have made an excellent ghost." She poured herself another drink, and the merchant mask settled back into place, though her eyes stayed on the rolled map the way a hungry dog watches a table. "Then take advice instead of coin, free of charge. If you're going where I think you're going, you'll need the second key, and the second key is not in friendly country. And girl—" she raised the cup toward me, something unreadable moving behind her eyes, "—people who carry Founder's Keys stop belonging to themselves. The moment word gets out what you're holding, everyone who wants that working finished—or finished *differently*—will come for you. Not the map. *You.*"

"Then word shouldn't get out," Wren said.

"Word always gets out." Vess drained her cup. "It got out two hours ago."

* * *

We were half a street from the Tanners' Cut when I realized what she'd meant.

It started small: a fruit-seller abandoning her stall mid-transaction, gaze sliding past us toward the harbor. Then a pair of dockhands walking with that particular purposelessness that isn't rest—it's waiting. The crowd around us didn't thin, exactly. It *organized*. People drifted to the edges of the lane the way iron filings arrange themselves around a magnet, and the magnet was coming up the street from the water, and it wore gray.

"Wren," I said.

"I see them." His hand found my elbow, steering me left, casual as lovers strolling. "Don't run. Running is a confession with legs."

Four of them, gray cloaks immaculate against the grime of Ashmarket, moving through the crowd without pushing, and the crowd making room anyway. They weren't searching shopfronts. They weren't questioning anyone. They were walking toward the Tanners' Cut with the unhurried certainty of people who already knew the address.

"How?" I breathed. "She swore discretion—"

"She didn't sell us out." Wren's voice was tight. "Look at their route. They're not coming for the shop. They're cutting it *off*." He pulled me into the shadow of a buttress, and I felt him sway slightly, caught himself. "Someone else talked. Someone who saw us enter the city, or saw us on the road, or—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Or someone who's been following us since the monastery road and wanted us to lead them to a buyer first."

The Graycloaks reached Vess's door. One raised his hand—not to knock, I realized, but in a small precise gesture, and the closed-eye knocker began, faintly, to glow.

"They're not asking permission," I whispered.

"No," Wren said. "They never were." He turned to me, and up close I could see how much the last mile had cost him, sweat standing on his brow despite the cold, the candle-wax pallor spreading up his neck. "Back way. Every shop in the Cut has one. Move *now*, Ada, and don't look back at the door—"

Behind us, with a sound like a sigh and a splintering, Vess's front door came off its hinges, and the first true scream of the afternoon rose over the rooftops of Ashmarket.

Wren grabbed my hand, and we ran.
