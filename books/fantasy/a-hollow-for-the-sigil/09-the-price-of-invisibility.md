## 9. The Price of Invisibility

I have been robbed before. I have been lied to, chased, shot at with crossbow bolts by a man whose aim was improved by drink, and once abandoned on a frozen causeway by a mule who showed better judgment than anyone I've since met. But I had never before been betrayed so *politely*.

That was the thing about Vess. She didn't sell us out the way a cutpurse sells out a mark—no furtive glances, no hurried notes slipped to shadows in alleys. She did it over tea.

We'd spent two days in Ashmarket, and Vess had spent those two days being magnificent at us. She had a shop in the Solder District that looked, from the street, like a dealer in lamp oil and bad wine, and inside was a dealer in lamp oil and bad wine, and beneath *that*—down a stair that smelled of tallow and old iron—was the real business: maps. Hundreds of them. Maps of coastlines that no longer existed, maps of cities that had never existed, maps with margins full of annotations in dead hands. She kept them in oiled cases because, she said, paper remembered water the way people remembered grief: badly, and forever.

She'd gone pale when she saw our map. I want to be fair to her here—I genuinely do, because fairness is a luxury I can only afford when I'm not bleeding—and I believe the pallor was honest. A Founder's Key laid open on her table was, for a woman like Vess, roughly equivalent to a god laying open its diary. Her fingers hovered above it without touching, the way you hover near a sleeping animal you both love and fear.

"Three keys," she'd said quietly. "The founders made three. Everyone who knows anything knows there were three." Then she'd looked up at Ada with an expression I couldn't read, and offered to buy it. A sum with too many zeroes. A new name. A house in a city where no one would ever ask Ada Ashgrove where she came from.

And Ada—who had spent twenty-two years wanting nothing more than to disappear into a life that fit—had said no. Said it simply, the way you decline a second helping. I remember thinking, in that moment, something I should probably have said aloud at some point in the weeks since, and didn't, because I am a coward with excellent penmanship.

So instead I lay on a cot in the back room of a smuggler's shop, running a fever that came and went like a dishonest tide, and listened to the two women talk trade in the front room, and told myself that the warmth I felt watching Ada refuse Vess's offer was just the fever.

* * *

The fever dreams were getting worse. That's what I noticed first, before the pain, before everything—the way they'd changed texture. The early ones had been ordinary nightmares: the palace, the king's study, ink on my hands that wouldn't wash off. Now they were quieter than that. In the newest ones I stood in rooms I knew and watched everyone in them smile at me, and their smiles were all the same smile, and I understood in the dream that this was not a nightmare but a memory of things to come.

The wound in my side had stopped bleeding days ago. It had instead begun doing something worse. It glowed—not the bright flare of fresh sigil-work, but a faint gray luminescence, like moonlight through fog, and around it my skin had gone smooth and pale and strangely *uninterested*. When I pressed a finger to it, I felt pressure but not quite pain. It was the difference between reading about cold and being cold.

Sigil-touched. I knew the word for it because I'd written it myself, years ago, in a report nobody important ever read: *wounds sustained from crown-sigil discharge do not heal by natural means; subjects exhibit progressive affect flattening; recommended isolation.* I had written "subjects" as though they weren't people. I had been very good at writing "subjects."

Ada changed the dressing every morning. She pretended not to notice the glow. I pretended not to notice her pretending. Between us we maintained a fiction so elaborate it deserved its own guild charter.

"You're looking better," she said that third morning, which was how I knew I wasn't.

"Liar," I said, and she almost smiled, and I catalogued the almost-smile somewhere safe, the way you bury coin before a war.

* * *

It was Vess's tea that gave her away—or rather, what was in the tea. Or rather, what I realized had been in the tea for three days: questions. Sweetened questions. Where had we crossed the ridge? Which road had we avoided? Did we know the underways beneath the capital? Each one wrapped in sympathy, in professional admiration, in the particular warmth of a woman who deals in secrets and has learned to make curiosity feel like friendship.

On the third night I heard her in the front room after closing, speaking low. Not the low of conspiracy—Vess was too good for that—but the low of commerce. A price being named. I caught only fragments through the floorboards: "...crossed at the western ford... traveling light... the girl carries it..." and then a pause, and then a sound I will hear until I die, which was Vess laughing softly at something a Graycloak said.

I got up. The room tilted politely and let me fall against the wall. I made it to the top of the stairs and stood in the dark of the map room, and Vess turned from the counter, and for one moment our eyes met across a room full of paper that remembered grief badly and forever.

She didn't look guilty. That's what I'll say for her. She looked tired, and sorry, and like a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "You should go," she said. "Now. Out the back, down the tanner's walk, don't take the main stairs. I sold your road, courier, but I didn't sell your faces. There's a difference. It's worth maybe an hour."

"Why?" My voice came out cracked.

"Because you called it home," she said. "When you were feverish. You said the map was going *home*, and I've sold a lot of things in my life, but I've never once managed to sell the idea that someone has one." She turned back to the Graycloak at the counter—a big man, patient, with the gray hood folded neatly at his shoulder—and said, loudly, "As I was saying, the pair went east toward the salt flats," and while his attention was on her lie, I took the back stairs.

* * *

Getting Ada out was the hardest thing I've done, and I once forged a royal decree using the king's own seal while he slept ten feet away.

She wanted to argue. Of course she wanted to argue; arguing was becoming her native language. But I put the tube in her hands and said, "They're coming, and Vess bought us an hour, and I cannot run the way I used to, so tonight you're the courier and I'm the cargo," and something in my voice must have landed, because she went white and started moving.

The tanner's walk behind Vess's shop was a ledge of wet stone above the drainage channel, and Ashmarket at night is a city built entirely of shadows stacked on other shadows, and we went down through them hand over hand along walls slick with old dye. Ada moved better than I expected. Hollowmere had given her roofs to mend and ladders to climb and a body that knew exactly where its weight ended. Twice she reached back for me without being asked, and twice I took her hand, and neither time did either of us say anything about it, because some ledgers you keep in silence.

We nearly cleared the district. I want that recorded. We nearly cleared it.

Then a whistle sounded above us—one note, clean, unhurried—and the rooftops along the tanner's walk grew Graycloaks the way a dead tree grows fungus. Three of them. Four. They didn't shout. Shouting is for people who need help. They simply began to descend, folding off the eaves with that terrible economy the wardens learned from the crown itself, and the leader raised one hand and the air around it shimmered with sigil-light, gray and patient.

"Run," I said.

"I'm not leaving—"

"*Run,*" and I pushed her, and she ran, because Ada Ashgrove was many things but never stupid, and the leader came down the wall toward me like frost coming down a window.

I had one trick left. It wasn't much. Years of serving a crown that ate will had taught me every warding formula the guilds ever banned, and I threw the oldest one I knew at him—a flat shield of force, crude, expensive, the kind of working that costs the caster something. It caught his blade for half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat is a long time when you spend it well.

He came through it anyway. Sigil-work eats sigil-work, and his was fed by the crown, and mine was fed by a man running on fever and stubbornness. But half a heartbeat was enough for me to see his strike was not aimed at me.

It was aimed past me. Down the walk. At Ada's back, where she'd stopped—of course she'd stopped—to turn and fight beside me, because she still didn't understand that she was the map now, that the map could burn and be redrawn, that she was the only original.

There was no decision in it. I want to be honest about that, because everyone likes to imagine heroism is a choice. It wasn't. My body decided the way a body decides to catch a falling cup—some ancient arithmetic about what the world loses if the cup breaks—and I stepped into the line of the blade.

It went in under my ribs, on the left, and the world went very simple and very loud.

* * *

Pain, I discovered, has a color. This pain was white at the center and gray at the edges, and the gray edges were the worst part, because the gray edges were the sigil, drinking. I felt it happen—felt the wound begin its slow work of making me smooth, making me *uninterested*, felt the crown's cold arithmetic reach into me through steel and start subtracting. Somewhere far away Ada was screaming my name, and somewhere closer the warden was withdrawing his blade with a strange gentleness, like a man closing a ledger he'd finished auditing.

Then Ada hit him with a roof tile.

I want to be precise, because history deserves precision: she tore a loose tile off the eave above her, swung it two-handed like a scythe, and broke it across the warden's hooded head, and the crack of it was the most beautiful sound I have ever heard, and I include every bell in the capital. He staggered. She hauled me up with a grip that would have shamed a dock crane, and we ran, and the remaining wardens let us—fell back, actually, unhurried, because they had done what they came to do. They hadn't needed to kill me. They'd needed to *touch* me.

We made it four streets before I collapsed against a shrine wall in a chandler's alley, and Ada lowered me down among the candle-stubs and the wax drippings, and the little outlawed sigil carved above the niche glowed faintly at my presence, or perhaps at hers, or perhaps just at the general emergency.

"Don't you dare," she said. Her hands were pressing the wound and shaking. "Don't you dare do the noble-dying-scribe thing, Wren, I swear by every dead name in that ledger—"

"It's fine," I said, which was the second-worst lie of my life.

"It's glowing. Wren. Why is it *glowing*?"

"Because," I said, and the truth came out of me sideways, the way truths do when you're bleeding, "the crown doesn't kill people like me. It *keeps* them. And I think—" I laughed, and it hurt, and I laughed anyway, because if I didn't laugh I was going to say the other thing, the true thing, the thing I'd been burying like coin before a war since the ford. "I think it recognized me. I used to work for it, you see. I was its scribe. I wrote its reports. I numbered its subjects." I looked at her hands on my wound, gray-lit from below, and thought: *there. Say it now, coward, while you have the perfect excuse to be forgiven.*

What I said instead was: "You should have taken Vess's offer."

"What?"

"The house. The new name. Disappearing forever." My vision was going soft at the edges, the fever and the blood agreeing on terms. "You wanted it. Twenty-two years of wanting it. And you said no in one breath, and I have been thinking about that breath for three days, and I need you to know—" and here the gray came up like fog over the ridgeline of Hollowmere, patient, certain, "—that whatever it hollows out of me, that breath stays. Whatever else it takes. I filed it somewhere it can't audit."

Her face did something complicated. "Wren—"

"Tell me about the valley," I said. "The fog. Tell me what the ridge looks like at first snow. Keep talking. It gives the sigil something to chew that isn't—"

"Wren."

"—isn't the good parts."

She talked. She talked about the ford and the ledger and Elder Maren's terrible lying face, and her voice went thick in the middle and she kept going anyway, and I held onto her voice the way you hold a rope over a drop, and the last thing I thought before the fever took me under was not a grand thought at all. It was small and specific and entirely mine:

*I never told her. And now I might not get the chance. And the crown may take it first.*

Somewhere above the alley, snow began to fall on Ashmarket, the first of the season, and it fell gray, and it did not melt where it touched my wound.

* * *

I surfaced once, briefly, in a room that smelled of tallow and candle stubs—the chandler's cellar, someone had carried me, Ada had carried me, small fierce impossible Ada—and heard voices arguing low above me. Ada's, ragged. And another voice, a woman's, calm and hard as a drawn wire, saying words that cut through the fever-haze like a blade through canvas:

"...the wound is sigil-touched. I've seen it hollow a strong man in six days. If you want him alive past the week, you'll stop hiding in cellars and listen to me, because I know how the crown feeds—and I'm the only Graycloak in this city who's ever walked away from it."

Then the fever pulled me down again, and the last thing I saw was Ada's face above mine, lit gray by my own dying light, wearing an expression I recognized from the map, from the moment she'd refused Vess, from every moment since the ford:

A woman deciding.
