## 10. Wren's Confession

Wren slept like a man who had been running for a year and had finally tripped over the finish line.

I knew this because I watched him do it. One moment he was propped against the wall of the shrine-keeper's back room, muttering something about the second key and drowned chapels; the next his head tipped forward onto his chest and he was gone, out cold, breathing in long slow drags that sounded almost peaceful if you didn't know what was underneath them. Almost. There was a hitch in it, every fourth breath or so, a little stutter where the air caught on something. I'd been listening to that stutter for three days now, ever since Ashmarket, ever since the rooftop, ever since the Graycloak's blade went into him at an angle that should have missed everything important and somehow didn't.

The wound wasn't healing. That was the part nobody said out loud, but everybody thought. Vess had called it sigil-touched before she sold us out, and she'd said it in the tone of a woman naming a disease she'd seen kill people before. The flesh around the cut had gone pale, then grayish, then — this was the part I hated most — faintly patterned, like frost forming on a window, except the frost was under his skin and it moved when he moved. Sera had looked at it once, briefly, before deciding not to look again.

"It's eating him," she'd said. "Slowly. From the edges inward."

"Eating what?" I'd asked.

She hadn't answered. She'd just looked at me like the answer was obvious and terrible, and then walked away to check the perimeter, because that was the kind of person Sera was: she would tell you the truth once, plainly, and then leave you alone with it.

So Wren slept, and I sat across the room from him in a chair with a broken rung, and I did what I do best, which is fuss. I changed the poultice. I fed him willow-bark tea he couldn't drink because he was unconscious. I turned his blanket into a shape that suggested warmth without actually providing any. It was guild work, really — tending, mending, keeping small things from getting worse — and there was a strange comfort in that, doing the one job Hollowmere had ever taught me well while the whole rest of my life burned down around me.

It was while I was wringing out the cloth that I noticed the letters.

They were tucked into the lining of his satchel — the satchel itself was unremarkable, cracked leather, a strap he'd mended twice with different thread — but the lining had come loose at one corner, the way linings do, and inside the gap there was paper. A bundle of it. Folded small, tied with a length of black cord, and hidden with the particular care of a man who has decided that some things matter more than staying alive.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of the evening: I took them out knowing I shouldn't. I told myself it was triage. I told myself that if he died in the night, someone ought to know what he'd been carrying besides a map and a fever. I told myself several other things too, each one flimsier than the last, and none of them survived contact with the actual moment, which was this: I unfolded the first letter because I could not bear another hour of not knowing who Wren was.

There are worse reasons to read a man's private papers. There are also better ones, and I didn't use any of them.

* * *

The first letter was dated four years ago, by a calendar I didn't recognize, in a hand so neat it looked printed. It began:

*To whoever finds these —*

*I am Wren Calloway, scribe of the third rank to His Majesty Aldric Vane, King of the Nine Valleys, and I am writing this because I have begun to forget things, and I want there to be a record.*

That stopped me cold. Not the king's name — though it landed strangely, hearing it at last, the name behind the crown, the name the thing in the throne room never used. What stopped me was the forgetting. Because I had spent two weeks traveling beside this man, and I knew about his memory. I'd watched him lose words mid-sentence, watched him stare at crossroads like they were written in a language he'd once known, watched him describe his own mother's face with the careful, rehearsed cadence of a man reciting rather than remembering. I'd assumed it was the wound. Or the fever. Or exhaustion.

It wasn't. It was four years old. He'd been coming apart since before I was born into this story.

*I serve in the scriptorium beneath the palace,* the letter went on. *My duty is transcription: royal decrees, warden orders, the daily liturgies of the court. Three months ago I was assigned a new task. The King himself — or the thing that speaks with the King's voice — has begun dictating letters. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They are all identical. They say only: I am still here. They are addressed to no one. They are signed with the King's true name, which no decree has carried in years.*

*I copy them because it is my duty. But I have started keeping them.*

*I don't know why. That is the truth. Some part of me — some part that is still mine — knows they are important, and that part does not take orders from the crown.*

I read that paragraph four times. *I am still here.* Hundreds of letters, identical, addressed to no one, dictated by the hollowed king through whatever mouth the sigil-thing allowed him, and copied faithfully by a scribe who didn't know why he was keeping them.

Except he did know why. He just couldn't say it yet. The buried king was sending messages out, and the message was his own existence, and the scribe's buried self — the part the crown hadn't eaten yet — was receiving them. Two hollowed men passing notes through the walls of their own prisons. It was the loneliest correspondence I had ever heard of, and I include in that category the ledger of dead guild members I'd spent six years copying out in an empty house.

I set the letter down. Wren hadn't stirred. The frost-patterns under his skin had spread maybe half an inch toward his shoulder since sundown, which I chose not to think about, and failed.

* * *

The second letter was shorter, and angrier, and dated a year after the first.

*They took more of me today. I know this the way you know a room has been robbed: not by what's missing, but by how much space there suddenly is. I reached for my brother's name this morning and found nothing. I had a brother. I am nearly sure I had a brother. There is a shape in my memory where he should be, the way there's a shape in a bed after the sleeper rises, and I cannot fill it.*

*The crown eats from the outside in. First the future — no one crowned can plan past tomorrow. Then the past, in rings, like a tree. The wardens call this "clarity." The court calls it grace. I have started calling it what it is, but only in these letters, only at night, only in a hand I disguise so the night-scribes won't recognize it as mine.*

*Here is what I know that I am not supposed to know: the letters the King dictates are not madness. I checked. I compared the seal-impressions, the ink, the pressure of the pen. They come from the King's own hand, guided — barely, faintly, like a drowning man tapping on the inside of a coffin lid — by whatever is left of him underneath. The sigil-thing lets him write them because it thinks they mean nothing. A hundred identical notes saying "I am still here" — what threat is that?*

*But it means everything. It means he is still in there. And if he is still in there, then the story everyone believes — that the crown consumed him whole, that there is nothing left to save — is a lie the crown tells to keep anyone from trying.*

I put that letter down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

Because I remembered the throne room. No — I remembered Sera describing the throne room, and the defector village, and the smiling vacant people with their sigil-scars, and I remembered what she'd said in the underways, in her flat warden's voice: *nothing human remains.* She believed it completely. She'd staked her entire defection on it — her bargain, her blade, her demand that we swear to kill the king rather than free him. Nothing human remains, and therefore mercy is a bullet, and the kindest thing you can do for a hollowed man is end him before the vacancy spreads.

And here was Wren, four years ago, holding proof otherwise in both hands. Proof he'd stolen a map to act on. Proof he'd crossed a winter country and a killing fog and a smuggler city full of knives to deliver — and hadn't told me. Hadn't told any of us. Had let Sera build her whole grim ethics on a foundation he knew to be sand, because telling her meant telling me, and telling me meant —

Meant what? I sat with that question for a long time, turning it over, looking for the version of it that made him look best. He'd lied by omission. He'd let me walk toward a choice — reforge the seal, shatter it, kill him, free him — believing the options were simpler than they were. If the king could be saved, then Sera's mercy-killing was murder, and my sacrifice was possibly unnecessary, and every argument we'd had since the monastery had been conducted over a hidden variable.

He'd done it to protect me. That was the obvious answer and I distrusted obvious answers on principle. But sitting there in the broken chair, watching him breathe his hitched, stuttering breaths, I kept arriving back at it. He'd seen what the crown did to people who knew too much. He'd been one of them. And somewhere between the scriptorium and the ford at Hollowmere, he had decided that the map mattered more than his honesty, that the mission outweighed the messenger, that a man carrying the last hope of a buried king didn't get to also carry his feelings around where anyone could drop them.

It was the same logic, I realized, that I'd used on myself my whole life. Duty first. Feelings later, if there's time. Loneliness disguised as responsibility. We were more alike than either of us had ever said out loud, and I found, to my considerable irritation, that this made me less angry rather than more.

* * *

There were eleven letters. I read them all, in order, by the light of a lamp I was careful to keep turned away from his face.

The middle ones charted the erosion in small heartbreaks. A letter about forgetting the taste of plums. A letter about standing in the palace garden and realizing he no longer knew which memories were real and which had been *given* to him — the crown patched its servants with borrowed pasts, stitched-in histories, secondhand childhoods, and by the fourth year Wren could no longer audit his own mind. A letter that was just a list of names, thirty of them, annotated *people I loved, in case I stop loving them* — and beside two of the names, added later in a shakier hand, the word *gone*, meaning not dead but forgotten, meaning he'd lost even the grief.

The tenth letter was dated eight months ago, and it was the one where the map entered the story.

*I have found it. In the deep archive, in the founders' stratum, beneath three floors of the crown's paperwork — the old guild records. The ones the first Hollowed King tried to burn. The ones the fog was built to hide. The valley of Hollowmere is not a refuge that fled magic. It is the lock. And the founder's line did not die out. They went in, and closed the door behind them, and the ledger in that guild house is not a record of members. It is a registry of keys.*

*I cannot read the seal-script. But I know a registry when I see one, and I know what the last entry means: an heir, living, unmarked, unaware. Somewhere in that fog is a person who can open what the founder sealed — or remake it — or break it. The crown doesn't know the valley still exists. The maps were all destroyed. Except this one, which I am holding, which I stole tonight, which makes me the most dangerous man in the Nine Valleys.*

*If you are reading this and I am dead: take it home. Take it home.*

*Take it home.*

The same words he'd whispered into the mud at the ford, feverish, bleeding, while the fog that repels all outsiders parted around him like it had been waiting. *Home.* I'd thought he meant the guild house. He had. But he'd also meant me — the heir, the key, the last entry in a ledger I'd been copying names into since I was sixteen without once understanding what the book was for.

The eleventh letter was undated. It was short. My hands shook reading it, and I'll admit that freely, because the alternative is pretending I'm made of stone, and I've never been good at that lie.

*To the heir, if you exist, if the fog let me through, if you are reading this:*

*I'm sorry I didn't tell you sooner. Every day I waited was a day I told myself you'd be safer not knowing, and every night I knew I was lying. The truth is I was afraid. Not of the wardens. Of you. Of what you'd do when you understood what you are — because the seal offers two doors and both of them cost, and I have watched the crown make people choose between terrible things for four years and I could not bear to watch it happen to you.*

*But you deserve the truth more than you deserve my fear. So: the king lives. Buried, starved, tapped thin, but alive, and asking — in the only way left to him — to be found. I am his handwriting. You are his door. Between us, maybe, we can reach him.*

*Or maybe we can't, and you'll hate me for the hope, and that will be fair.*

*Either way — thank you for opening the gate. Whoever you are. Whatever you decide.*

*— W.C., sometime in the dark, still here*

Still here. He'd signed it with the king's phrase. Four years of copying *I am still here* until the words wore a groove in him, and in the end he'd used them for himself.

* * *

I folded the letters along their original creases, tied the black cord, and sat for a while doing absolutely nothing, which for me is a significant event.

Outside, the winter wind worked at the shrine-keeper's shutters. Somewhere below, Sera was awake — she was always awake; I suspected sleep was a muscle she'd lost — and the house creaked around its secrets the way old houses do. Wren breathed his hitched breath. The frost-patterns under his skin had reached his collarbone. I checked. I wish I hadn't.

Here is what I had thought, going in: that the letters would tell me who Wren was, and that knowing would settle something. Here is what they actually told me: that he was a man who had spent four years being erased and had answered erasure with paper. With copies. With a hidden archive of his own self, maintained nightly, in disguise, at risk of death, because he understood — earlier and better than anyone — that the crown's true weapon isn't killing. It's forgetting. It makes you vacant and calls it peace. And the only countermeasure anyone had ever found was the humblest magic in the world: a person who refuses to stop recording, refusing to stop hoping, refusing to stop saying *still here* into the dark.

He'd carried the king's hope across a country. He'd carried mine, too, though neither of us had known it — because every step of his route ran through the story of what I was, and he'd guarded that secret like a coal cupped in bare hands, letting it burn him rather than let it go out.

I was angry at him. I want that on the record. I was angry for about an hour, thoroughly and righteously, pacing the small room while a sleeping man bled lightless frost. And then the anger did what anger does when it runs up against something bigger, which is dissolve — not into forgiveness exactly, but into resolve. Into a kind of clarity that felt almost cold and was, I think, the warmest thing I'd felt since the ford.

Because here was the arithmetic, finally, laid out plain. Sera wanted the king dead because she believed nothing remained. The crown wanted the keys because it believed the seal could be remade with me inside it. Wren wanted — had always wanted, at ruinous personal cost — the third option: that the buried man could be reached, that vacancy is not the same as death, that *still here* is a promise and not a symptom. And I was the only person alive positioned to test whether he was right.

Not the guild's last member. Its first heir. Its living key. Fine. I had absorbed that much already; it sat in me like swallowed river water. But the letters gave it weight and direction. I wasn't carrying a map anymore. I was carrying a man's last hope — two men's, if you counted the one asleep in front of me, and I did, God help me, I did.

So I made some decisions, there in the lamplight, alone with eleven letters and a hitching breath.

One: Wren would live. This was not a plan so much as a stance, but I've learned that stances come first. The sigil-touched wound would not close on its own, and the second key was a day's walk away in a drowned chapel, and I had noticed — how could I not — that my blood sang near seal-script, that the walls of the underways had resonated with me like struck bells. If the keys were bound to the founder's line, then maybe proximity mattered. Maybe carrying him toward the next seal was the closest thing to medicine I had. It was thin. It was all I had. Thin has carried me this far.

Two: I would not confront him. Not tonight, maybe not tomorrow. He'd confess eventually — men like Wren always do, they leak truth the way his wound leaked frost — and when he did, I wanted to receive it as a choice he made, not a trap I sprang. He'd earned that much. Besides, I needed him steady, and guilt makes people stupid.

Three — and this was the one I held longest, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it — I would watch Sera. Kindly. Carefully. She was brave and honest and half-right, which is the most dangerous combination there is, and if it came down to her blade versus a buried man's last word, I intended to be standing between them with something better than either.

I retied the satchel's lining so the corner hung naturally, tucked the letters back into their dark, and banked the lamp low.

Then, because the night demanded one more dishonesty from me and I was tired of refusing it, I got up, crossed the room, and sat on the floor beside his bedroll with my back against the wall — close enough to hear the stutter in his breathing, far enough that he'd never know I'd been there. The frost-patterns glimmered faintly in the dark, tracing their slow branches up his throat, and I watched them the way you watch a candle you're not allowed to relight.

"You're still here," I told him, very quietly, in case any part of him was listening the way his king listened through coffin lids. "I read your mail. I'm sorry. Also I'm not. Also we're leaving at dawn, and you're going to live, and you don't get a vote."

He didn't stir. But the hitch in his breathing smoothed, just slightly, just for a moment — one clean breath, then another — and I chose, deliberately, with full knowledge of how foolish it was, to believe that counted as an answer.

Dawn came gray and hard. I was packing the satchel when Sera appeared in the doorway, dressed, armed, and already frowning, which for Sera constituted a scream of alarm.

"The water," she said. "At the monastery. It's dropping."

I looked at Wren, still sleeping, frost climbing his neck like ivy with a deadline.

"Wake him," I said. "We're done waiting."
