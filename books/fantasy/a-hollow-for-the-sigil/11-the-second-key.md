## 11. The Second Key

I woke to Ada shaking my shoulder and the word "done" hanging in the air like a blade she'd decided to stop polishing and start using.

Something had changed in her overnight. I'd seen it happen before, in other people, at other thresholds — the moment when fear stops being a wall and becomes a floor you can stand on. It usually took years of grief or a near-death experience. Ada seemed to have managed it between one dawn and the next, which was either admirable or terrifying, depending on how much you liked being near her when she made decisions.

I did not entirely dislike it.

"The water's dropping," Sera said, not looking up from her blades. "Which means either the monastery is surfacing on its own, or something down there wants visitors. Both options are bad."

"Then we should hurry," Ada said, "before it changes its mind."

Sera gave me a look over Ada's head — the look of a woman asking *are you sure about this one* — and I shrugged, because what was I going to say? That I'd known Ada Ashgrove for a handful of weeks and trusted her more than I'd trusted anyone since before the crown ate my king? That trust, once you've watched it hollowed out of an entire kingdom, becomes a thing you hoard and spend recklessly?

"We hurry," I said.

My wound had opinions about this plan. Sigil-touched flesh doesn't heal; it negotiates. Every mile we walked, the frost-veins under my skin crept a little further from the gash, mapping me outward like ivy with ambitions. The cold wasn't pain anymore. That was the worst part. Pain would have meant I was still losing. This felt like a truce, and truces with sigils are just surrenders with better manners.

But I could still walk. So I walked.

* * *

The drowned monastery sat in a valley that the maps called Lake Verren and the old records called, simply, *the mistake*. Two hundred years ago the founders had flooded it deliberately — drowned a whole house of worship rather than let the crown find what slept beneath its chapel. The water had been deep enough to hide a cathedral and cold enough to kill a strong swimmer in ten minutes. For two centuries it had done its job perfectly.

Now it was draining.

We stood on the ridge above it as morning came up thin and white, and watched a lake exhale. Water poured off the valley in sheets, finding cracks in the basin I couldn't see from this distance, roaring away into the earth. The lakebed emerged in stages: first the black ribs of a boathouse, then a causeway, then the tops of arches like the knuckles of a buried hand.

"It's been dropping for three days," Sera said. "Fishermen downstream think it's a blessing."

"Do they," I said.

"They're building a shrine." She almost smiled. "People will worship anything that stops hurting them."

The causeway was exposed enough to walk by midday. We went down single file, boots crunching on silt that had never seen sun, past doorways standing open to nothing, their wooden doors long since rotted to lace. Fish hung drying in the dead weeds. Everything smelled of iodine and old stone — the smell of a secret giving itself up.

"You knew this place," Ada said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"I copied documents here, once. Before." I kept my eyes forward. "The monks were the founders' archivists. When the guild scattered, someone had to keep the dangerous papers somewhere the crown wouldn't look. Under a lake was considered very clever at the time."

"And now?"

"Now the crown has had two hundred years to get cleverer."

The chapel rose out of the mud ahead of us, and I stopped pretending I was fine. I'd sat in that chapel. I'd taken dictation under those windows while the king — the real one, the man, not the thing wearing him — laughed at some joke of the abbot's and asked me to write it down so he wouldn't forget it.

He'd forgotten it anyway. The crown saw to that. But the chapel remembered, and so did I, and neither of those facts helped anyone.

* * *

Inside, the flood-chapel was a cave of dripping dark. The water had only receded to knee-height in the nave, black and mirror-still, holding our reflections upside down like hostages. Light came through the high windows in gray-green shafts, thick with silt, and every sound we made came back to us twice.

Ada waded in without hesitation. Sera and I followed, and I noted — with the detached professional interest of a former scribe — that Ada moved like she'd been here before. Not confidently. *Instinctively.* Her hand found the submerged pew-ends without looking. Her feet avoided the holes in the floor.

"You feel it too," I said. "Don't you."

She didn't answer right away. Then: "It's like walking through a house where someone's humming in another room. You can't hear the song. But you know which way the kitchen is."

That was as good a description of seal-script resonance as any the founders ever wrote, and they'd written several thousand pages on the subject, most of which I'd transcribed and none of which had mentioned kitchens.

The altar stood at the far end, raised on a dais, and the water pooled deepest there — a wide, still circle around it, as if the flood had been reluctant to touch it even at its height. The altar stone was carved all over with script. Even half-buried in two centuries of silt, even drowned, the marks caught the light wrong. They didn't reflect it. They *kept* it, faintly, the way embers keep heat after the fire's gone.

"There," Sera said. "Under the base. See the seam?"

I saw it. A hairline gap around the altar's foundation block, sealed with lead and script. And around the seam, in a ring, the warding — the same pattern I'd spent weeks teaching Ada from stolen practice-sheets, writ large and old and serious.

"This is a Founder's working," I said. "Not a copy. The real hand."

"Can you open it?"

"I can read it. Reading and opening are different professions." I looked at Ada. "Show me your hands."

She held them out, palms up, and I turned them over and felt my stomach drop. Beneath the grime of the road, faint as watermark, the lines of her palms had gone luminous — not glowing, exactly, but *lit from underneath*, and the light moved in patterns. In script.

"When did this start?" I tried to make it a clinical question. It came out closer to a prayer.

"This morning," she said. "When the water started dropping. Is it supposed to do that?"

"No."

"Is it bad?"

"I genuinely don't know, and I've decided to find that reassuring, because everything I've known for certain lately has tried to kill me."

Sera, at the chapel door, said flatly: "Whatever you're going to do, do it faster. There are riders on the ridge."

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next ten minutes, and I remember it the way you remember a fever — in fragments, out of order, with certain moments burned in and others missing entirely.

Ada kneeling in the black water at the altar, sleeves pushed up, both palms flat against the foundation stone.

The script waking.

Not flaring — *waking*, the way a sleeper wakes when someone says their name, a slow brightening along every carved line, and the ring of wards turning like a lock turning, except the lock was the size of a chapel and the key was a twenty-two-year-old woman who'd spent her life copying names into a ledger no one read.

And her voice — quiet, conversational, as if reading aloud to herself — saying words I had no translation for. Seal-script isn't a language, precisely. It's older than language, the grammar magic was written in before anyone thought to speak. The founders learned it the way you learn a mother tongue: by blood, by bone, by inheritance. I had studied it for fifteen years and could parse maybe a tenth of it.

Ada had never studied it at all.

She read it fluently.

The foundation block sank. That was the only word for it — it sank into the dais like a coin into soft wax, grinding, water spiraling down around it into the revealed cavity beneath, and there, in a socket lined with lead and velvet long since rotted to black lace, lay the second key.

It was smaller than I'd expected. Smaller than the map-tube Wren-the-courier had carried across a winter country with Graycloaks at his heels. A rod of dark metal, perhaps the length of a forearm, worked end to end with script that matched the marks on Ada's palms so exactly it might have been cast from them. It didn't gleam. It *waited*.

Ada reached for it.

"Don't—" Sera started.

"—touch it bare-handed, yes, I know," Ada said, and wrapped her sleeve around her fingers, and lifted it out anyway, because apparently my lessons on caution had landed exactly nowhere. "But it's already mine. Look at it. It's been waiting for me the way the kitchen waits for the person who hums."

"That's not — that analogy doesn't hold up to scrutiny—"

"Wren." She held the key up between us, and the light in her palms answered it, line for line, mark for mark. "I'm done running. I've been running since the ford. Running is just duty pointed the wrong direction. This—" she turned the key slowly, and the drowned chapel turned with it, shadows wheeling, "—this is the first thing I've ever chosen. Let me have it."

What do you say to that? I was the King's scribe. Words were my profession, my weapon, my refuge. And I stood in a flooded church holding nothing, watching a woman hold everything, and the only honest sentence available to me was one I couldn't afford yet.

"Careful," I said, which was true, and insufficient, and she smiled like she knew both things.

* * *

Sera's second warning was not a warning. It was a crossbow bolt taking the chapel doorframe a hand's width from her head.

"Graycloaks!" she shouted, already moving, already loosing back into the green murk outside. "Six, maybe eight, coming down the causeway — they waited for us to dig it up, the clever bastards!"

Of course they had. Vess had sold our route, and the Graycloaks hadn't needed to chase us at all. They'd only needed to follow, patient as winter, and let us do the drowning and the diving and the dying. I'd have admired it if admiration weren't currently busy being terrified.

We had one exit: the chapel's rear postern, a low arch half-collapsed, opening onto the monastery's graveyard terraces and from there the western slope. Between us and it: forty yards of flooded cloister, knee-deep water that slowed every step, and whatever portion of eight Graycloaks the causeway party had already flushed inside.

"Give me the key," I said. "You run lighter."

"No."

"Ada—"

"You're wounded, Wren. You run slower. Therefore I carry it, therefore you stay behind me, therefore—" a bolt hissed past her ear and she didn't even flinch, which frightened me more than the bolt, "—therefore stop arguing and move."

So we moved. Sera led, cutting through the cloister arches, picking her shots with the economy of a woman who'd been a Graycloak herself and knew exactly which angles her sisters favored. I followed Ada, which meant following the light of her hands, because the cloister had gone dark as the inside of a closed book.

Halfway across, the water around us began to glow.

Not her doing. The *walls'* doing — the drowned monastery's own script, dormant for two centuries, answering the key's passage like dogs answering a whistle. Lines of faded sigil-work lit along the cloister pillars, along the flagstones, tracing paths through the flood, and Ada — curse her, bless her — read them the way you or I read a signpost.

"Left here," she said. "The bridge is out straight ahead."

"How can you possibly—"

"*Left,* Wren."

We went left. Thirty seconds later, from the darkness ahead, came the splash and oath of a Graycloak stepping into a flooded stairwell that the sigil-lights had warned us around.

"They can't see the script," Ada realized aloud, wonder and horror in equal measure. "They're wading blind. We're not."

"Because you're the heir," I said, and heard the words leave my mouth, and understood that I had just told her something I'd sworn — to myself, to the archive, to a tired man's memory — to manage carefully. "Because the founders built all of this for their blood, and the blood answers, and I will explain *everything*, I promise, but not here—"

A Graycloak came around the pillar.

Close. Too close for Sera's bow, too close for anything. He raised his blade at Ada — at the key, at the light in her hands — and I saw his face under the hood, young, frightened, doing his job.

Ada didn't scream. She raised her free hand, palm out, and said one syllable of seal-script, the way you'd say *stop* to a dog.

The water between them went solid.

Not ice — *stone*, briefly, a wall of transfigured flood rising hip-high between the warden and us, and the young Graycloak stumbled back into his fellows with a shout that had lost all its discipline, and in that shout I heard the thing the crown fears most: a servant realizing the world has rules he was never taught.

"Run," Ada said, to all of us, to the whole drowned monastery, and we ran.

* * *

We came out the postern into gray afternoon light and climbed the western slope with our lungs full of knives. Behind us, the chapel lights died one by one, and the last thing I saw of the drowned monastery was the water beginning to rise again — the basin refilling, slow and deliberate, the lake reclaiming its secret now that the secret had legs and was running away with it.

Sera rearguarded us to the tree line, then caught up, breathing hard, and looked at Ada for a long moment.

"You walled off a Graycloak," she said. "With scripture."

"With *something*."

"With founder-blood." Sera spat. "Do you understand what they'll report? Not thieves. Not couriers. They'll report *her*. By nightfall every warden in the province will know there's a living heir moving west, and the crown doesn't send squads for heirs. It sends—"

She stopped. I finished it for her, because someone had to say it.

"It sends the Hollowed."

The wind came down off the ridge, cold and clean, carrying the smell of the refilling lake. Ada stood with the key wrapped against her chest, and the light in her palms had dimmed but not gone, pulsing gently, in time — I noticed, and wished I hadn't — with my own failing heartbeat.

"Good," she said.

Sera stared. "Good?"

"Let them come." She turned west, toward the capital, toward the third key, toward whatever the founders had built her body to unlock. Her chin was up. Her voice didn't shake. "I'm done running. Let them bring the Hollowed. Let them bring the King himself."

She started walking. After a moment, Sera and I followed, because there was nowhere else to go, and because — though I only admitted it to myself much later — following her had stopped feeling like a choice somewhere back in the flooded dark, and started feeling like gravity.

Behind us, the lake closed over the monastery with a sound like a door shutting.

Ahead, on the far ridge, small and patient against the sky, something that had been waiting for exactly this announcement began to move.
