## 12. What the Crown Eats

The rope around my wrists was good work, I'll give her that. Double-looped, cinched with a slipknot a fisherman would have wept over, and tied by someone who clearly expected her prisoner to be the polite type.

I was tied to a chair in the back room of a miller's cottage that smelled of rye dust and old smoke, and the woman who'd tied me was standing at the window with her gray cloak pushed back off one shoulder, watching the road.

"I didn't even fight," I said. "I want that noted for the record. Wren drew a knife and I told him to put it away."

"You told him to put it away," she repeated, without turning. Her voice was flat as a frozen pond, with something moving underneath it, like a current under ice. "In a village square. In daylight. While he was bleeding through his bandages."

"He was bleeding *slightly* through his bandages."

"He's feverish again. I can hear it in his voice. You two are the loudest fugitives I have ever captured, and I captured a man once who sang the whole time."

I decided not to ask about the singing man. "So you're going to turn us in, then."

She turned. She was younger than the cloak made her look — thirty at most, with a sunburnt, unglamorous face and eyes the gray of river stones, the kind of eyes that had looked at too many things and stopped flinching. The sigil on her collar was the same crown-and-circle the map bore, worked in silver thread gone dull with age.

"Turn you in," she said. "Yes. That is what a warden does with a Founder's Key and the king's own scribe."

"Then why am I in a chair in a miller's cottage instead of a Graycloak wagon?"

"Because I wanted to see your face when I told you." She crossed the room and crouched in front of me, forearms on her knees, close enough that I could see the fine white scarring at her hairline — a thin, pale line, like a crown drawn in old ink. "And because I needed to know if you'd lie to me. You haven't yet. That's rarer than you'd think, in my line of work."

"Your line of work being what, exactly? Because from where I'm sitting it looks like ambushing travelers and tying them to furniture."

"My line of work," Sera said, "is watching people smile."

* * *

She fed us first, which I found more unsettling than the rope. Barley soup, black bread, a heel of hard cheese — set down in front of Wren and me like we were guests and not prisoners. Wren ate like a man who'd forgotten food was a thing bodies wanted and then remembered all at once. His color was bad. The wound in his side had gone from angry red to a strange, bloodless gray at the edges, and when I changed the bandage while Sera watched with her arms folded, she looked at it a long moment and said nothing, which frightened me more than anything she could have said.

Then she took us out the back door and down a lane between bare hedgerows, Wren limping between us, my hands still bound — "for appearances," she said, which appearances, and to whom, she did not explain — until we crested a low rise and she stopped, and said, "Look."

The village below was called Marrow's End, according to the leaning sign at its edge. I don't know how many people lived there. Forty? Fifty? It doesn't matter, because I could not tell you a single thing about any of them except that they were all, all of them, smiling.

It was near dusk. There should have been lamplight, smoke from chimneys, dogs, the clatter of someone drawing water. There was lamplight — every window glowed — and there were people in the lanes, moving slowly, unhurried, going about the small business of an evening. A woman was sweeping her step. A man was splitting wood with the patient rhythm of long practice. Two children chased each other around a well.

And every single one of them was smiling. Not laughing. Not talking. Smiling — the same smile, I realized as my eyes moved from face to face, the same *exact* smile, the mild, pleasant, closed-lipped smile of a portrait sitter told to look content. The sweeping woman swept and smiled. The woodcutter swung and smiled. The children ran and smiled, and the sameness of it in faces so small was the worst thing I had ever seen.

"What am I looking at," I whispered.

"Harvest," Sera said.

Wren made a sound low in his throat. "Marrow's End. It was a weaving village. My mother bought linen here." His voice had gone strange and thin. "Sera. When?"

"Three years ago. I was on the sweep that found it. We were told to look for sedition, hidden sigil-craft, the usual." She didn't look away from the village. "We found everyone at their looms. Smiling. They'd been like that for days, maybe weeks. No one had noticed. The neighbors in the next valley thought Marrow's End had simply gotten *pleasant*."

The woodcutter set down his axe, smiled at the woodpile, picked up the axe again.

"The crown-sigil doesn't kill," Sera said. "Killing is wasteful. Killing is *loud*. It drinks. It takes the will out of a person the way you'd tap a keg — slowly, a little at a time, so the body keeps working, keeps eating, keeps sweeping, keeps smiling, and no one raises an alarm because nothing looks wrong." She finally turned to me, and her river-stone eyes were the eyes of someone who had stopped flinching and then, I think, started again somewhere deeper down. "You've been in Hollowmere your whole life, so let me tell you what the rest of the country knows in its bones. The winter doesn't end. Twenty-two years — that's your whole life, girl, and it's not weather. Frost in high summer. Grain that comes up gray. The long winter is not the crown's punishment. It's the crown's *harvest*. Every season it takes a little more from everyone, and everyone feels it as cold."

I thought of the fog at Hollowmere's ridgeline, the fog that had kept the world out and us in, and for the first time I understood it not as a wall but as a held breath. We had been in a room with the door shut while the house burned slowly, room by room, and we'd called the shut door *peace*.

"Why are you showing me this?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted.

"Because you're carrying a Founder's Key into the capital, and you think you know what you're fighting." Sera untied my wrists. Her fingers were quick and impersonal. "You don't. You think the Hollowed King is a tyrant, and tyrants want obedience, and obedience you can outwait, outwit, outlive. This—" she nodded at the village, at the smiling sweeper, the smiling children, "—is not obedience. Obedience leaves a person inside to chafe. This is *vacancy*. The thing on the throne doesn't want you on your knees. It wants you *empty*, and smiling, and it has all the time in the world, because it made the winter, and the winter does its work for it."

The two children had stopped running. They stood by the well, holding hands, smiling at nothing, at the dusk, at us — I could not tell if they saw us at all. Wren turned away first. I was proud of him and ashamed of myself for being proud.

"The wardens sweep the lowlands for the map, for the keys, for you," Sera went on. "But understand what for. Not to destroy you. The crown doesn't destroy. It *collects*. A Founder's Key is a vessel of will — the founders poured themselves into those. Do you understand what the crown would do with that?"

I did, suddenly, horribly. The same thing it did to everything. Drink it.

"And the king's scribe." She looked at Wren, and something moved under the flat of her voice — I'd have called it pity, except pity was too warm a word for it. "You escaped the palace with your mind. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what the crown does to the ones who get out? It doesn't chase them to punish them. It chases them because a hollowed man who *woke up* is the most dangerous thing in the world to it. You're not a fugitive, scribe. You're a leak."

Wren's hand had gone to his side, to the gray-edged wound. "It's still in me," he said quietly. "Isn't it. The blade that cut me was sigil-touched. It's drinking me the same way. Slowly."

Sera didn't answer, which was an answer.

* * *

We sat that night in the miller's cottage with the shutters closed and a small fire going, and Sera laid her sword on the table between us — not as a threat, I understood eventually, but as a kind of honesty. Here is what I am. Here is what I have done with it.

"I was a warden for eleven years," she said. "I told myself the sweeps were mercy. Find the sedition, spare the village, keep the peace. Then I walked into Marrow's End, and I stood in a weaving shed, and a woman at a loom looked up at me and smiled, and I asked her name, and she smiled, and I asked her children's names, and she smiled, and I realized I was talking to a lit window with nobody home." She turned her sword a quarter-turn on the wood. "I've been a defector for two years. I don't help people. I don't rescue people. I've learned what happens to people who try. But you—" the river-stone eyes fixed on me, "—you walked out of the one valley the crown has never touched, carrying the one thing it fears, and you did it because a dying courier asked you to. That's either stupidity or the beginning of the only thing worth doing, and I've stopped being able to tell the difference, so I'm going to find out which."

"And if it's stupidity?" Wren asked.

"Then I'll have helped two stupid people die, and I'll go back to watching villages smile." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Here is what I know that you don't. The harvest is accelerating. Marrow's End took weeks. The villages along the capital road now take days. The Graycloak command has stopped pretending the sweeps are about sedition — we're *escorting* the harvest now, clearing the roads so the cold can move unimpeded. And in the capital, they've started the preparations for a ceremony the crown-sigil hasn't performed since the founders sealed it away. I don't know its name. I know it needs three keys."

The fire popped. Wren and I looked at each other, and I watched him arrive at the same place I was: the map in its leather tube, the two marks we'd found, the third we hadn't.

"Where," I said.

"The drowned monastery," Sera said. "The second key. The Graycloaks know about it — they've always known, they just couldn't reach it through the flood-chapel's wards. But the wards were built by founders, and founders built them to answer to founders." Her eyes came to me and stayed there, and I felt the weight of it settle on my shoulders like snow. "You walked into Marrow's End and you looked at those people and you flinched. Good. Flinching means there's still someone home in you to flinch. Hold on to that. Because the thing you're walking toward doesn't want you dead, Ada Ashgrove. Dead is easy. It wants what Marrow's End has. It wants you *vacant* — and it would very much like you to arrive that way, carrying the keys, smiling."

I looked into the fire and thought about the ledger in the guild house, the names I'd copied in my neat, dutiful hand, and the founder's name beside my own in the oldest ink, and I thought: *they didn't flee magic. They built a door and stood in it.* And the thing on the throne had spent twenty-two years — no, centuries — trying to drink the door.

"Then we go tonight," I said.

Sera almost smiled. It was nothing like the smiles in Marrow's End. It was crooked and tired and alive. "I was hoping you'd say that," she said, and stood, and began strapping on her sword. "Because the road to the monastery closes at first frost, and the frost is coming early this year."

Wren looked up from the fire, gray-faced, hand pressed to his side. "How early?"

Sera pulled back the shutter and looked at the sky, where the stars were coming out sharp and cold over the hedgerows, over the valley, over the smiling village below.

"Tomorrow," she said.
