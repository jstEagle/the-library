## 13. Sera's Bargain

I had made exactly two oaths in my life before that night.

The first was at fifteen, hand on the gray wool of the cloak, swearing to the Hollowed Crown to guard the realm against sigil-craft and its servants. I remember thinking the words tasted like candle wax. I remember the chaplain smiling at me like a proud aunt. I remember nothing else about it, which in hindsight should have been a warning: the Graycloaks were very good at making you forget things.

The second oath was at nineteen, alone, in the latrine trench behind the garrison at Coldford, where I swore to myself that if I ever saw what I'd seen that day—the village of Aldenmere, all forty-one souls standing in their fields at midnight, faces upturned, *smiling*—I would run, or I would fight, or I would do something other than stand there like them. It took me three more years to keep it. Oaths are like that. They wait for you.

So understand that when I sat across from Ashgrove and her dying courier in that farmhouse loft, with frost climbing the window glass like something trying to get in, I did not offer my help lightly. I offered it the way you hand someone a knife: handle first, but everyone knows what the blade is for.

"Before we go any further," I said, "you should know what you're asking for."

Wren didn't look up from the fire. He'd gone gray around the mouth since Marrow's End—since the blade meant for her had found him instead—and every hour the gray crept a little further, like ink through wet paper. The sigil-touched wound. I'd seen it before. I'd been ordered to make wounds like it before. That's the part I don't tell people at parties, mostly because I've never been invited to one.

"You're asking me to take you into the capital," I said. "Past the checkpoints, past the harvest-wards, down into the underways that half my order doesn't believe exist. And then up into the palace itself. Into the throne room." I let that sit. "There are easier ways to die. Cheaper ones. You could just drink the well water in Marrow's End."

"We're not going to die," Ada said.

She said it with the flat certainty of someone who has never actually watched a plan meet reality. Twenty-two years old, valley-raised, and carrying a Founder's Key like it was a loaf of bread she'd forgotten to put down. I wanted to dislike her for it. The trouble was that she'd walked into a hollowed village that afternoon, seen forty smiling corpses pretending to be neighbors, and hadn't flinched. She'd flinched after—out by the hedge, quietly, where she thought no one could see—but not during. That's rarer than you'd think. Most people do it the other way around.

"No," I agreed. "You'll probably survive the capital. That's not what I'm worried about."

The fire popped. Wren pulled his cloak tighter over his side.

"What I'm worried about," I said, "is what happens when you get to the throne room and find out I'm right."

* * *

Here is what I know about kings, having served one for seven years: the man is long gone. Whatever sits on that throne now wears him the way a glove wears a hand. I stood honor-guard at the winter court four years running. I watched the Hollowed King smile at petitioners while his eyes tracked nothing, watched him sign edicts in his own hand while the hand wrote things he would have burned the kingdom before signing, alive. Once—I have never told anyone this once—he turned to me between audiences, and for perhaps three seconds his face was *his*, terrified, drowning, human, and he gripped my wrist hard enough to leave bruises and said, "It's so quiet in here," and then the crown took him back mid-sentence and the thing behind his eyes smiled at me and asked after my mother.

My mother had been dead six years. The thing knew that. It liked watching me decide whether to answer.

"So when I say kill him," I told them, "I want you to understand I'm not being cruel. I'm being kind. There is no buried king waiting to wake up. There's no true self hiding under the sigil, waiting for someone brave enough to reach him. I've looked. We've all looked. The crown doesn't imprison a man—it digests one. What's left at the bottom isn't a person. It's a shape the magic remembers."

"That's not true."

Wren said it very quietly, to the fire, the way you say something you've said to yourself ten thousand times without ever once believing it would matter.

I turned to look at him properly. Up close the hollowing showed in strange places—a faint translucence at his fingertips, the way his breath fogged even though the room wasn't cold enough for it, as if the wound were already pulling the warmth of him out into the air. Sigil-craft leaves marks. Everything does.

"You knew him," I said. Not a question. I'd guessed it in Marrow's End, from the way he talked about the palace corridors—from the way he *didn't* talk about them, which was louder.

"I was his scribe," Wren said. "Seven years. Before." His hand moved to his side, to the bandage, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "You're wrong, Sera. About the bottom of him. The messages come through. Little ones. A word in the margin of an edict. A hymn hummed off-key in the night watch, a hymn only he knew. A—" He stopped. Started again. "He sends letters out of the dark. I've read them. I carried one for eleven months while I learned how to steal the map. The king is still in there. Buried, starving, but *there*. And every day we spend arguing about whether to kill him, the crown digs deeper."

"The messages," I said, "are bait."

The word landed harder than I meant it to. Ada's head came up sharply; Wren went very still.

"You think I haven't considered it?" I kept my voice level, which cost me. "A scribe who loved his king sees a scrap of the old handwriting and walks halfway across a frozen country with stolen state secrets. You don't think the crown might have *planned* that? It hollowed a whole kingdom, courier. It eats memory and will for breakfast. You think it can't forge a dead man's penmanship?"

"It's not forged." His voice cracked on the second word. "You can't fake the way he crossed his sevens."

"And you know that how? Because you've compared it lately? Against what?"

Silence. The frost ticked against the glass. Somewhere below us the family who owned this farm slept on, unharvested, lucky, temporary.

"I'm sorry," I said, and discovered I meant it, which annoyed me. "But you're not objective. None of us are. That's rather the crown's whole trick—it makes mercy feel like love and suicide feel like rescue. I've buried friends who heard messages too."

* * *

Ada hadn't spoken in some time. When she finally did, it was to ask the question I'd been dreading, and she asked it the way she seemed to ask everything—plainly, without decoration, like a door opened straight onto the cold.

"Why are you helping us?"

Fair. I'd shown up in Marrow's End armed, defected, led them to a hollowed village, and offered passage to the most guarded place in the world, and I'd given them almost nothing of why. Trusting a Graycloak is like trusting a wolf that says it's vegetarian. You check its teeth.

"My sister," I said.

They waited. Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and leaned on the house, and the whole structure groaned like an old ship.

"Mara. Younger than me by two years. She lived in Aldenmere—you know it now, I suppose, the village I showed you. She was there when it happened. The harvest came through in the night, quiet as weather, and by morning everyone was smiling." I found I was looking at the fire. I made myself look at them instead; cowardice is a habit you feed by indulging it. "She wasn't taken to the palace. The lowland harvests aren't for the throne room—they're local. Distributed. The crown likes its food fresh and near. She's still there, probably. Standing in a field somewhere, or setting tables, or whatever the sigil uses her hands for now. Smiling. And here's the thing—" My voice did something I hated, caught and roughened, and I pushed through it. "Here's the thing. Some nights I think: if I break the seal, if the magic comes back wild and free the way your map promises, maybe the sigil-scars burn away and she comes back to herself. Maybe she wakes up in a field at dawn with dew on her and no idea where she's been, and she's *furious*, and she's mine again. And other nights I think that's exactly the same kind of story Wren tells himself about his king. A message from the dark. Bait."

I stood, because sitting felt too much like confession, and went to the window. The stars were out, hard and white. First frost tomorrow, Sera had promised the sky, and the sky was keeping the appointment.

"So no," I said to the glass, to my reflection, to the girl and the dying man reflected faintly behind me. "I don't know if I'm saving her or abandoning her. But I know the crown took her, and I know it will take more, forever, until someone stops it. That part I'm sure of. The rest—" I shrugged, and it must have looked as tired as it felt. "The rest is arithmetic I can't finish."

* * *

We argued until the candle burned down to a thumb's width.

Not shouting—Graycloaks are trained out of shouting, and Wren hadn't the strength, and Ada had that stillness that made raising your voice at her feel like vandalism. It was worse than shouting. It was two people who both believed they were being merciful, explaining to each other how they were planning to commit murder, and meaning it kindly.

"Kill him," I said. "Quickly, cleanly, before the seal-work, before anything else. A knife in the dark and the crown falls off a corpse. No choices inside the sigil, no chances for the thing to bargain, no risk of reforging anything. You want to free a man who may be a mask. I want to bury a mask that used to be a man. Only one of those plans can't go wrong."

"And if you're wrong," Ada said. "If he's in there. Then we murder a prisoner for the crime of his prison."

"Yes," I said. "Welcome to ethics. It's a swamp. Everyone leaves with muddy boots."

"That's not—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles against her mouth for a moment, and I watched twenty-two years of valley kindness collide with everything she'd seen since the ford. When she spoke again her voice was lower. "In Marrow's End you said the enemy wants us vacant. Smiling. Empty. And your plan is to empty him further. To make sure. Permanently."

"My plan is to stop the harvest."

"Mine is to stop the harvester *and* save what it took."

"Those might be the same plan," Wren said suddenly.

We both looked at him. He'd gone paler still, and there was sweat at his temple despite the cold, and I thought—not for the first time—that the wound was eating him faster than any of us were admitting. He levered himself upright against the wall, breathing carefully, like a man carrying something full to the brim.

"Sera's right that the crown lies. Ada's right that the king lives. Both things. That's the trap." He looked at me. "You want certainty. Kill the body, no ambiguity. She wants hope. Break it open, no killing. But the crown survives on exactly that argument—on people choosing safety over rescue because rescue is uncertain. Every person who ever decided a hollowed neighbor was beyond saving, that was the crown winning a little more. Mercy that requires proof first isn't mercy. It's accounting."

"Don't lecture me on accounting," I said, sharper than intended. "Your king signed the harvest orders. In his own hand. You've told us so yourself."

Something went out of his face then. Not anger—worse. Recognition.

"Yes," he said quietly. "He did. Or the thing did, using his hand, and I ground the ink, and neither of us stopped it. I've done my arithmetic too, Sera. I've had seven years to do it." His hand pressed flat against the bandage at his side. "I can't promise you we'll kill him. I won't. He taught me my letters. He gave me my post when I had nothing, and whatever wears him now, the man who did that deserves better than a knife in the dark from people who never heard him laugh. But I can promise you this: if we reach the throne room and the thing in the crown is all that's left—if there's no message, no margin, no hymn, no *him*—" He swallowed. "Then I'll hold him down myself."

I studied him for a long moment. Dying men say generous things; the grave makes great diplomats of us all. But his eyes held steady, and underneath the fever-brightness there was something I recognized from the mirror on bad nights: a person who had already done the arithmetic and hated the total and would pay it anyway.

"All right," I said. "That I can work with."

"That's not a promise," Ada said. "That's a coin toss with extra steps."

"That's politics," I said. "Same thing, better hats." I crouched by the fire and fed it the last of the split logs, buying another hour of light, another hour of argument we didn't have time for. "Look. Here's my offer, final terms. I guide you to the capital—underways, checkpoints, all of it. I get you into the palace warrens, which I know like my own scars. In exchange: when we reach the seal, if there is any moment—any single moment—where freeing him risks the harvest continuing, risks *more* villages, more Mara, then we kill him. Not debate. Not negotiate with the sigil-thing, which I promise you will offer you everything, because it always offers everything, that's how it hunts. We kill him and we sort out the theology afterward."

Ada was quiet a long time. The frost had reached the top of the window now, ferns of ice closing over the last clear patch of sky.

"If it comes to that," she said finally, "we decide together. All three. No one's hand moves alone."

"Majorities move faster," I said.

"Then we'd better make sure we stay a majority," she said, and there was something underneath the weariness that I was starting to recognize as the actual architecture of her: not softness. Load-bearing stubbornness. "Agreed?"

I looked at Wren. He nodded once, small, careful, a man conserving motion.

"Agreed," I said, and stuck out my hand like a smuggler closing a deal, because oaths had gotten me nothing twice, and contracts at least come with exits.

Her grip was stronger than expected. Her palm was warm. I noticed, distantly, that it was the first time in three years I'd touched anyone without checking their eyes first for the shine of the sigil.

* * *

We left an hour before dawn, because the frost keeps no appointments later than the ones it keeps.

I led them out the back of the farmstead, over a stone wall slick with new ice, down into a stream-cut where the old road ran hidden under alder roots. Behind us the valley lay silver and silent. Ahead the land climbed toward the high pass, and beyond the pass, three days east if the weather held and five if it didn't, the capital sat under its dome of enforced springtime like a fly preserved in amber.

Wren managed the first mile on his own feet and the second leaning on Ada's shoulder, and by the third I'd traded places with her without either of them remarking on it, which was its own small treaty. The gray had spread past his wrist now. When I took his arm I could feel it through his sleeve—not cold, exactly. Absent. Like holding a branch where a bird used to sit.

"How long?" Ada asked me, low, when he'd stumbled into the ditch for the second time and we'd hauled him out.

"With the wound open like that? If it hollows him at the rate the lowland harvests go?" I did the arithmetic I'd done a hundred times over other people. "Four days. Five if he fights it. Six if he's stubborn, and he strikes me as stubborn."

"He has to last until the capital."

"Then we walk fast," I said, "and he fights hard, and we don't discuss it in front of him, because patients overhear everything and despair travels at the speed of hearing."

Except he'd heard. Of course he'd heard. He stopped at the next bend, braced against a birch, and looked back down the valley we were leaving—the silvered fields, the far smudge of Marrow's End, and beyond it, invisible but present in all our minds, the village where my sister stood smiling at nothing—and he said, in a voice scraped thin but perfectly clear:

"There's something I have to tell you both before we go another step. About the keys. About what the archive at the monastery is going to show us."

And the way he wouldn't quite look at Ada when he said it—

The way he looked at *me* instead, as if asking permission, or forgiveness, or an accomplice—

Made the frost on the birches seem suddenly the warmest thing for miles.
