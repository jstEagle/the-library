## 14. Underways

The entrance to the underways was not a door. It was a mistake in the city's architecture that only looked like a mistake if you already knew to look for it.

"Here," Sera said, crouching in the mouth of an alley behind a tannery whose smell I will be carrying to my grave. She pressed her gloved hand against a section of wall that was, as far as I could tell, wall. "Founders' work. The stone remembers being open."

"Stone doesn't remember."

"Yours does." She glanced back at me, and even in the dark I could see the arithmetic she was doing—the same arithmetic she'd been doing since the drowned monastery, since I'd read seal-script like other people read their own names. *How useful is she. How dangerous is she. Can I still kill her if it comes to that.*

I was getting better at not flinching from that look. I was not getting better at answering it.

Wren stood at my shoulder, one hand pressed flat against the wall beside Sera's. His wound had been quiet since the chapel—quiet the way a held breath is quiet—and when his palm touched the stone I saw his fingers spread, deliberate, the way a man greets something he's afraid of.

"It's warm," he said.

"It shouldn't be," Sera said. "It's been dead for three hundred years."

"Then it isn't dead."

The wall remembered. I felt it before I understood it—a hum, low and patient, like a note held by someone with very good lungs—and then the stone did something that stone should not do. It softened. Not melted, not crumbled. *Yielded*, the way a fist yields to a hand it recognizes, and a seam of darkness opened in the tannery wall with a sound like a long-held sigh.

Cold air breathed out of it. It smelled of dust and lamp oil gone to wax and, underneath that, something older and stranger: the smell of a room that had been waiting.

"Founders' work," Sera said again, and this time it wasn't an explanation. It was an accusation.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs have any right to go. I counted to four hundred and stopped counting, because the number had stopped meaning anything, and because counting was the only thing keeping the other thought at bay—the one that said *you knew this would happen, some part of you has always known, you were born on the wrong side of the map because the map was always going to come for you.*

The walls were lit. That was the first impossible thing, after the door. No torches, no lamps, nothing Sera's dark-lantern could improve upon. The stone itself glowed—a faint amber rising from seams in the masonry, sigil-work so old it had stopped being writing and become geology. Lines of script ran along the walls at shoulder height, continuous, unbroken, like a sentence someone had started three centuries ago and never finished.

"Can you read it?" Wren asked.

I could. That was the second impossible thing, and I was trying very hard not to look at it directly, the way you try not to look at a wound.

"It's a litany," I said. "Sort of. It's instructions. *Keep the light. Keep the count. Keep the door for the one who comes after.*" I swallowed. "It repeats. Every twenty paces or so. The same words, over and over, all the way down."

"Three hundred years of *keep the door*," Sera said. "For one person."

"Don't," Wren said quietly.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

Sera said nothing more, but she didn't have to. The litany said it for her, patient and amber and endless. *The one who comes after. The one who comes after.* I put my hand against the wall to steady myself on the stairs and the hum rose to meet my palm like a cat leaning into a known hand, and I snatched it back and pretended I hadn't.

* * *

The tunnels opened, after the stairs, into something that was not a tunnel at all.

It was a gallery—vaulted, wide as a chapel, running off into darkness in both directions. The amber light gathered here into proper illumination, and I could see that the walls were carved. Not decorated. *Carved*—the way a confession is carved, the way a warning is carved. Figures in procession along both walls, hundreds of them, deep-cut and deliberate, and Sera's lantern-light and the amber glow moved across them together so that the figures seemed to walk as we walked.

"Founders," Wren breathed. "This is the work record. The guild kept a work record."

He moved along the wall like a man in a dream, reading. "Here—this is the binding of the lowland rivers, see, the flood-work. This is the mending of the blight years. This—" he stopped. His hand came up to his mouth. "Ada."

I went to him. I should not have gone to him. I knew that even as I went.

The carving showed a woman standing before a great ring of stone—a seal, unmistakably, the same ring that sat at the heart of Wren's map, the same ring I had traced with my finger a hundred times in a guild house that had forgotten what it was for. The woman's arm was outstretched. Her hand was pressed flat against the ring's center. And around her, smaller figures knelt—founders, guild members, their faces turned up toward her in attitudes I recognized because I had spent my whole life among them: awe, grief, and the particular helplessness of people watching someone they love do something irreversible.

"She's sealing it," I said. "The original seal. This is—"

I stopped.

The woman had a face.

Not a generic face. Not the smooth anonymous face the carvers had given every other figure on the wall. This woman had a face the way a portrait has a face—specific, considered, *known*. A strong jaw. A mouth that looked like it was more likely to argue than smile. Hair bound back for work, strands escaping at the temple because whoever carved this had seen her on a real day, a working day, not a monument day.

I knew the jaw. I shaved around it every morning. I knew the mouth; the guild elders had spent twenty-two years telling me it was too stubborn for my own good.

"She has my face," I said. My voice came out very flat, very far away. "Wren. She has my face."

"I know."

"You *know*? You knew this?"

"I suspected." He wouldn't look at the carving. He was looking at me instead, and his face had gone the color of the drowned monastery's water. "The map. The ledger. The way the seal-script comes to you. I suspected the bloodline ran true. I didn't know it ran *this* true."

"How close is it?" Sera had come up silently behind us, and her voice had the awful gentleness she reserved for things she was about to use. "How close is she to the woman on the wall? One in ten? One in a hundred?"

"Sera," Wren said.

"Because if it's one in ten, we have a plan. And if it's closer than that—" she touched the carved woman's outstretched hand, just below the five fingers, not quite touching them, "—then we don't have a courier and a map. We have a key walking around in boots."

The amber light hummed. The litany ran on above our heads. *Keep the door for the one who comes after.*

I looked at my own face, three hundred years old, pressed into a seal I was apparently born to refill, and I understood—with the terrible clarity that sometimes arrives too late to be useful—that the fog around Hollowmere had never been a wall. It had been a *cradle*. The guild hadn't forgotten its purpose. It had been keeping me the way you keep a lamp lit: fed, trimmed, protected, and utterly unaware of what you're for.

"Keep walking," I said.

"Ada—"

"I said keep walking. I want to see how it ends."

* * *

It ended in a chamber.

The gallery funneled at last into a round room, and the round room was the reason for everything above it—the stairs, the litany, the three centuries of amber patience. In its center stood the seal. Not a carving of it. The thing itself: a ring of black stone tall as two men, standing upright, its surface covered in script that moved. Not flickered—*moved*, slow as glaciers, the letters traveling the ring's circumference in a procession that had been going, as far as I could tell, since before my valley had a name.

And it was cracked.

The fracture ran from the ring's crown to its base, thin as a hair, and through the crack seeped something that was not light and not dark but a kind of *absence*, a draft of nothing, and where it touched the amber glow the glow flinched away.

"That's the leak," Wren said softly. "That's what's failing. That's what the crown has been drinking through, all this time. The long winter. The hollowing." He turned to me, and I watched him decide—watched the truth come up his throat and get swallowed back down, again, the way it had in the gallery. "The founders built this to hold the crown's hunger. And it's been holding it alone for three hundred years."

"Alone," I said.

"The woman on the wall. She sealed it with—" he hesitated.

"With what?"

"Blood. Will. A life, maybe. The records aren't clear."

"The records aren't clear," I repeated. "Convenient."

"Ada." He crossed to me and took my hands, and his were shaking, and I realized with a distant surprise that it wasn't the seal that frightened him. It was this room, and me in it. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking that the litany says *keep the door for the one who comes after*, and I'm the one who came after, and there's a crack in that ring shaped exactly like a person." I pulled my hands free, gently. "I'm thinking I'd like to sit down, except there's nowhere to sit in a trap."

"It's not a trap."

"Isn't it? Fog on the valley. A ledger with my name in the oldest hand. A map that finds me instead of me finding it. A courier who washes up at the one ford in the country that would let him through." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and glassy. "Wren, I have been *carried* here. Every step of this road, something has been carrying me. And now I'm standing in the belly of it, and the walls know my face."

The seal hummed. And then—because the universe had apparently decided I hadn't had enough for one day—the crack in the ring *breathed*. The absence swirled, and for half a heartbeat it took a shape: tall, crowned, patient. The shape of a thing that had been waiting three hundred years and could wait three hundred more, but would very much prefer not to.

Sera's knife was out. Wren stepped in front of me. And I did the only thing that made sense, the thing my too-stubborn mouth had been arguing toward my whole life.

I put my hand on the seal.

The black stone was warm. Of course it was warm. It knew me. The script rushed up beneath my palm like a tide meeting a shore it had been promised, and the litany in the walls—all of it, every amber word down four hundred stairs and miles of gallery—spoke at once, not in my ears but in my blood:

*The one who comes after. Welcome home.*

I took my hand back. The crack had widened, very slightly, where I had touched it. Or perhaps it had *opened*—the way a lock opens, the way a door opens.

Behind us, far up the gallery, a sound echoed down the stairs: boots. Many boots. And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the dry whisper of Graycloak sigils waking.

"They followed us down," Sera said, and for once there was no arithmetic in her voice. Just the flat calm of a soldier past the point of plans. "How long do we have?"

I looked at the seal. The seal, I would have sworn, looked back.

"Less than we needed," I said. "More than we have."
