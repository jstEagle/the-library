## 16. The Long Winter Falls

The underways ended at a door that wasn't a door.

It was a wall of fitted stone with no handle, no hinge, no seam—until Sera pressed her palm flat against it and spoke a word I didn't catch, and the whole face of it sighed open like a man giving up a secret he'd kept too long. Cold air poured through the gap. Not tunnel-cold, which is still and mineral and patient. This was moving cold. Living cold. It smelled like woodsmoke and horse dung and bread, and underneath all of it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth: frost.

"Welcome to Carreth," Sera said. "Capital of everything worth ruining."

I had imagined this moment for days—the surfacing, the first sight of the city where the king sat wearing his crown like a wound. In my imagination there had been more drama to it. Trumpets, maybe. Or at least a dramatic skyline.

What we got instead was a stairwell that stank of cabbage, and then an alley, and then noon.

Noon in Carreth should have been loud. That's what Wren had told us, back when his voice still had strength in it—that the capital never slept so much as dozed with one eye open, that you couldn't cross a square without tripping over a fishmonger's argument or a beggar's song or some merchant shouting about wool prices as though wool were a matter of state. He'd described it with the particular fondness of a man describing a place he'd loved and lost.

The alley we stepped into was silent.

Not empty—silent. There were people in it. A woman stacking firewood by a doorway. Two men mending a cart wheel, one holding spokes while the other fitted them. A boy crouched over a gutter chasing something with a stick. All of them working. None of them speaking. And every few seconds, all of them—wood-stacker, wheel-menders, boy—would pause at exactly the same moment, tilt their heads slightly toward the palace district at the city's heart, and then resume.

Like a congregation between verses.

"Oh no," Sera said quietly. It was the first time I'd heard anything like fear in her voice, and I found I didn't like it at all. Fear from Sera was like fear from a cliff edge. It meant the ground itself had opinions.

"No what?" Wren asked. He was leaning on my shoulder more than he'd admit, his weight wrong in a way I'd stopped commenting on because commenting didn't fix it. The hollowing wound beneath his bandages had gone quiet lately, which frightened me more than the pain had. Pain fights. Quiet just waits.

"The harvest." Sera was staring down the alley toward the pale smudge of rooftops that must have been the palace. "It's not supposed to start for another month. The crown takes its tithe at midwinter, when the nights are longest—it feeds slow, it feeds *carefully*, because a kingdom that notices what's happening to it is a kingdom that burns its own palace. This—" she gestured at the woman with the firewood, who had tilted her head again, mid-lift, a log balanced against her hip like she'd forgotten she was holding it— "this isn't careful. This is early. Something spooked it."

I thought of the drowned monastery. Of the second key coming up out of the flood-chapel water in my hand, singing along my bones in a script I could read without learning. Of the Graycloaks arriving before the water had even finished draining.

"It knows we're coming," I said.

"It's known since Ashmarket," Sera said. "Maybe since the ford. But knowing and being able to *do* something are different things, even for a crown. Until now it's been playing defense—wardens, patrols, letting the winter do its work at a gentleman's pace." She turned, and her gray eyes had the flat shine of ice over deep water. "Now it's rushing. Rushing means scared. Scared means we've hurt it somewhere it counts."

"Or it means it's decided to stop being gentle," Wren said.

Nobody answered that, which was answer enough.

* * *

We came out of the alleys onto a boulevard called, according to a leaning signpost, the Kingsway, and I finally understood what Wren had been trying to describe and failing to describe, because the thing he'd loved wasn't the city itself but the noise of it, and the noise was gone.

The Kingsway was wide enough for eight carts abreast, lined with buildings four and five stories tall, their upper windows hung with laundry and flower boxes long deadened by frost. Shops stood open. Stalls stood stocked. A baker's window displayed loaves arranged with genuine artistry, golden-brown, dusted with flour—and nobody was buying them. Nobody was selling them. The baker himself stood in his doorway with flour still on his forearms, looking down the boulevard toward the palace with an expression I want to call peaceful, except that peaceful implies someone home behind it.

He was smiling. They were all smiling. Small smiles, closed-mouth, the kind you wear for a portrait.

And they were walking. That was the worst part. Not shambling, not staggering—walking, at an ordinary pace, in ones and twos and little family clusters, drifting down the Kingsway the way leaves drift on a current you can't see. An old man in a fine coat. A girl of perhaps six, holding her mother's hand, both of them crowned with the same faint sigil-scar glowing at their hairlines like a bruise made of light. A butcher still wearing his apron, cleaver tucked into his belt as if he'd simply stepped away from the block mid-work and would be back after.

All of them flowing toward the palace.

"They're not dead," Sera said. She said it fast, like she'd had to say it before, like it was a lesson she'd learned the ugly way. "Don't look at them like they're dead. The crown doesn't kill what it takes. It *keeps* it. Everything they were is still in there, folded up small, and if the sigil-thing dies tomorrow, most of them wake up confused and hungry and furious, but themselves." She paused. "Most of them."

"And the rest?" I asked.

"The rest are the reason I left." She started walking, quick, hugging the shadowed side of the boulevard, and we followed. "Keep your hood up. Don't meet anyone's eyes. The hollowed don't report to wardens—they don't need to—but the *unhollowed* do. Anyone still awake in this city is either hiding, collaborating, or ours. We can't know which until it's too late."

"How can you tell who's still awake?" Wren asked.

"You can't. That's rather the point."

We moved through the drifting crowd like swimmers through kelp. It took everything I had not to grab people by the shoulders and shake them—to shout into the baker's smiling face, to ask the old man in the fine coat what his name was and whether he knew he was walking anywhere at all. Once, the six-year-old looked directly at me as her mother drew her past, and her eyes were brown and bright and utterly vacant, and she smiled at me with her mother's smile, and I felt something in my chest crack along a fault line I hadn't known was there.

This was what the valley's fog had kept out. This was what nine aging guild members in a drafty house had been preserving the world from—or hiding from; I still wasn't sure those were different things. Hollowmere's fog wasn't mercy. It was a locked door in a burning house, and I had spent twenty-two years polishing the lock.

Wren's hand found mine in the crowd. His fingers were cold, colder than the air justified, but they squeezed, and I squeezed back, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the crowd wouldn't hear, and also because some things don't survive being said aloud. They have to stay as pressure. As grip.

* * *

Sera took us off the Kingsway within a quarter hour, down a servants' lane and through a courtyard where laundry froze stiff on its lines like a gallery of ghost clothing, to a building that had been a countinghouse in better years and was now a shell with a good cellar. She barred the door behind us and lit a single candle, and in its light I got my first real look at her face since we'd surfaced, and understood how bad it was.

Sera had planned this infiltration for months. I knew that much—she'd laid out routes and timetables in the underways with the grim precision of a woman defusing something. She had accounted for patrols, for gate rotations, for the palace's servant warrens, for the exact number of hours the frost-tithe would leave the court drowsy and unguarded.

She had not accounted for the harvest starting early, and watching her now was like watching a general realize the map has lied to him.

"Midwinter," she said, mostly to herself. "It always takes midwinter. Weeks yet. The crown builds slowly because it has to—pull too many minds too fast and the strain shows, people collapse in the streets, panic spreads, and panic is the one thing the sigil can't digest." She pressed both hands flat on the dusty counting table. "But this—" She looked up. "This isn't a harvest. Look at the pace. Look at the *smiles*. It's not feeding. It's gathering. It's pulling everyone it can reach into the palace before—"

"Before we arrive," Wren said.

"Before you arrive." Sera's eyes went to me, and I wished they hadn't. "It's not defending the throne room. It's filling it. Every soul it can pull is a body between you and the crown, and every body is a hostage you'll have to walk past. It read your ledger, heir of Ashgrove. It knows you're the sort who counts the cost."

The candle flame leaned in a draft I couldn't feel. Somewhere above us, muffled by floors, the city hummed—not with voices, but with footsteps. Thousands of them. All going the same direction.

"So we go tonight," I said.

Sera laughed, short and without any humor in it whatsoever. "Tonight. Yes. Wonderful. Tonight I was going to spend preparing. Tomorrow night, entering the warrens. Three days of observation before we touched the palace proper, because the servant schedules alone take days to learn, and the wards on the inner doors reset on a cycle that—"

"Sera." Wren's voice was quiet, but it landed. He'd gotten very good at landing quiet words lately; dying men develop economy. "There is no schedule anymore. There's no cycle. Whatever clock this city used to run on, the crown just threw it out the window. Which means every plan built on the old rhythm is already worthless—including yours, including mine, including the one the sigil-thing is running. Nobody knows what happens next." He pushed himself upright off the wall he'd been leaning against, and I moved to steady him and he let me, which told me more about his condition than any complaint would have. "That's terrible news. It's also the only advantage we have left. It's rushing. Rushing things make mistakes."

"Rushing things also make corpses," Sera said. "Usually other people's."

"Then we'll try to be disappointing."

Despite everything—despite the frozen laundry and the vacant child and the humming stairs—I felt my mouth twitch. Wren had that effect. It was infuriating. I suspected it was half the reason I loved him, a fact I had catalogued carefully and intended to inform him of at some point when we were not actively trespassing toward an apocalypse. Possibly over dinner. If dinner existed anymore.

* * *

We spent the daylight hours in the cellar doing the only thing left to do: getting ready, badly, quickly.

Sera drew the palace's layout for us in candle soot on the counting table's surface—the outer walls, the servants' warrens threading through them like veins, the great stair, the throne room at the heart of everything like a spider sitting in a web. Her finger traced our route, and her mouth kept moving through contingencies, and I listened, and I memorized, and underneath the listening a quieter part of me kept doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said aloud, the thing that had been sitting in my chest since the monastery, growing heavier with every mile: the keys were warm. Both of them. The founder's key from the map's heart and the second key from the flood-chapel altar, wrapped in wool at the bottom of my pack, and for weeks they'd been cool as river stones, patient as stones. Today, walking the Kingsway, they had begun to warm. By the time we reached the cellar, I could feel them through the wool against my hip like two sleeping animals dreaming of waking.

They knew where they were going. And whatever lived in the palace knew they were coming, and had decided to stop being subtle about it.

"Ada." Wren, beside me, low. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm here." I watched Sera trace the great stair again. "Just counting."

"Counting what?"

"Steps," I said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, and I hated myself a little for the shape of the lie even though I couldn't yet have said what the truth was. Some inheritances you carry in a pack. Some you carry in your blood. Mine had started whispering to me in the underways, and it hadn't stopped, and the whispers had a direction, and the direction was *in*, always *in*, deeper than the throne room, deeper than the crown—

Later. Whatever it was, it could be later. The clock was audible now in every scene, in every silence, in the hum of ten thousand feet on ten thousand stairs. No more detours.

Dusk came down over Carreth like a lid. Through the countinghouse's broken skylight I watched the sky go the color of a bruise, then the color of ash, and the frost crept visibly across the glass—feathering outward from the edges, midwinter patterns in early winter air, the long winter falling ahead of its season like a curtain rising, if curtains rose on disasters.

Below us, the city's footsteps changed. Slowed. Settled. Ten thousand people arriving wherever they were going, and standing there, and waiting.

Smiling, I'd have bet anything. All of them, smiling in the dark.

Sera snuffed the candle. "Warrens," she whispered. "Stay behind me. Touch nothing. And whatever you see when we get inside—" her voice caught, just once, the smallest hitch, the only honest sound she'd made all day— "whatever you see, keep walking."

The door unbarred. The cold came in like an invitation.

And far off, at the heart of the city, at the top of the great stair, in a room built for a king—a light I couldn't see with my eyes began, very softly, to glow.
