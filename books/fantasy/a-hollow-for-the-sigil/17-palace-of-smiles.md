## 17. Palace of Smiles

I had sworn an oath to the Graycloaks once, with my hand on a sigil-stone and my heart full of certainty. I remembered the words: *We keep the quiet. We carry the crown's peace.* I had believed them the way you believe in weather—because they were simply there, falling on everyone equally.

Now I was leading two fugitives through the servants' warrens beneath that same crown's palace, and the oath felt like something I'd swallowed wrong years ago and never coughed up.

"Stay behind me," I whispered, though whispering was almost unnecessary down here. The underways had their own acoustics—a hush like held breath, broken only by the drip of meltwater from the frost above. "The warrens run under the whole eastern court. Kitchen hands, laundresses, lamplighters. Nobody looks at them. Nobody has looked at them for years."

"That's the part that worries me," Wren said.

He was moving better than he had any right to. The hollowing wound in his side still glowed faintly when his shirt shifted—I'd seen it while he slept, a pale seam of light like a scar made of dawn—and every hour it took something from him. A word forgotten here. A name reached for and missed there. He covered it well. Scribes were good at covering things; it was practically the trade.

Ada said nothing at all. She hadn't spoken much since we surfaced from the deep archive, since she learned—or half-learned, because Wren still hadn't told her all of it, and I could see him carrying the unspoken truth like a stone in his boot—what the third key was. She walked with her jaw set and her eyes doing that thing they did: reading everything, cataloguing everything, refusing to be surprised by anything. Twenty-two years old and she already had the posture of someone who expected the world to lie to her and was keeping score.

The tunnel sloped upward. Old sigil-lamps burned in niches along the walls, blue-white and steady, fed by workings older than the crown itself—the founders' craft, still obedient after all these centuries, lighting the way for whoever needed to move unseen. There was an irony in that which I chose not to examine.

"The servants' door comes up behind the pantries," I said. "From there we cross the gallery to the west stair. The throne room is—"

"I know where the throne room is," Wren said quietly.

Right. Of course he did. He'd spent six years writing this palace's letters before the crown ate him alive and spat him out as a courier with a stolen map and a dying wish. I kept forgetting, because he seemed so *small* now, so worn—but Wren had been the King's own scribe. He had sat three chairs away from the thing we were about to face.

"Then you know the court will be awake," I said. "Midday audience. The harvest makes them—"

"I know what the harvest makes them."

* * *

The servants' door opened onto warmth and noise, and I nearly wept at how ordinary it was.

Kitchens. Steam and clatter and the smell of bread, real bread, brown and heavy, the kind baked for staff rather than lords. A scullery boy ran past us with an armload of kindling and didn't spare us a glance—my warden's grays had been traded in Ashmarket for servant's wool, drab and honest, and Ada and Wren looked like nothing more than a delivery pair trailing the laundress corridor.

But then the boy reached the far end of the kitchen, and a cook called out something cheerful—"Mind the hot plate, love!"—and the boy turned and smiled.

And the smile didn't reach anywhere.

It sat on his face like paint on wood. His eyes stayed flat and busy and empty, and he bobbed his head, and the smile held exactly as long as a smile should hold, and then it was gone, folded away, and he ran on with his kindling.

Ada stopped walking. "Did you see—"

"Yes," I said.

"He smiled like—"

"Like he was told to," I finished. "Keep moving."

Because that was the thing about the harvest that no one warned you about, the thing the defector's briefings never captured: the hollowed weren't sad. They weren't shambling or vacant-eyed in any way that would raise alarm. They *functioned*. They worked and greeted and laughed at the right moments, and if you watched any single one of them for ten seconds you'd swear they were fine.

You had to watch for what was missing. The pause before answering. The flicker of genuine irritation when someone bumped them. The little human stumbles that make a person a person. The hollowed had none of it. They were performances of themselves, scripts running on faces, and the longer you stood among them the more your skin crawled—not because of what they were, but because of how *good* they were at it.

We moved through the kitchen into the laundry passage, and there a woman was folding sheets, humming. Her hum was pitch-perfect. It had no pleasure in it whatsoever. It was the idea of humming, executed flawlessly by someone who no longer knew why humans did it.

Ada touched my arm. "How many?"

"In the palace? Most of the staff below a certain rank. Half the court above it." I kept my voice level. "The king's household went first, years ago. Then the ministers. The crown works inward, like rot in fruit."

"And nobody outside notices?"

"People notice what they're permitted to notice." I thought of my own years in gray, escorting wagons of 'grain' into the capital, never asking why grain wagons needed armed escort and returned empty. "There are forms. Reports. Everything is filed correctly. The kingdom runs beautifully on paper."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been stripped out of him somewhere on the road. "That was my job," he said. "Filing it correctly. Six years of making the horror legible." He pressed a hand to his side, over the glowing seam. "You get very good at not looking at the margins."

* * *

We came up into the gallery behind the throne room through a door meant for cup-bearers and message-runners, and I heard Ada's breath catch, and mine nearly caught too, though I'd seen it before.

The court of the Hollowed King was in session.

The throne room was vast and gold and cold—every window blazing with the strange white light of the long winter, frost ferns climbing the inside of the glass—and it was *full*. Hundreds of nobles in silk and ermine, arranged in their proper ranks, all facing the dais where the throne stood.

And the throne was empty.

Not vacant-empty. Not between-audiences-empty. Empty the way a grave is empty after the funeral, when everyone knows the body is elsewhere but keeps addressing the headstone anyway. Every eye in that room was fixed on the bare seat, every bow calibrated to its invisible occupant, and at intervals—precise, metronomic intervals—a herald would step forward and announce some petition or decree, and the court would murmur approval, and the murmur would rise and fall together, hundreds of voices in perfect unison, like wind through a field of wheat.

No one disagreed. No one spoke out of turn. No one, I realized with a slow cold horror, was speaking *at all*—the murmurs were just that, murmurs, sound shaped like assent without a single word in it.

"They're not even talking to each other," Ada breathed. "Look. Look at the Countess there—she turned to speak to the man beside her and she just... opened her mouth and made the sound."

"She doesn't remember conversation," Wren said. His voice had gone thin. "Only the shape of it. The crown eats the substance and leaves the etiquette." He was gripping the doorframe. The seam of light at his side had brightened; I could see it through his shirt now, pulsing faintly in time with— I looked toward the throne and wished I hadn't. In time with the room. With the murmur. As if his wound were a tuning fork and the whole court were ringing at its frequency.

"We shouldn't stay here," I said.

"One moment." Wren wasn't listening. He was staring across the sea of heads at a woman in dove-gray near the front, standing very straight, her hands folded. "That's Lady Isolde. She used to slip the kitchen boys candied ginger when the king was in one of his black moods. She'd say, *sweetness for the sour days*, and laugh—" His voice cracked down the middle. "She's holding her hands the way she held them when she was waiting for him to finish dictating. She's been standing like that for six years, waiting for a dictation that ended when he stopped being a person."

"Wren."

"He built this." It came out of him low and fierce and aimed at himself. "I wrote the decrees that let the wardens into the provinces. I sealed the orders. I told myself it was law, it was order, it was peace—" He turned to me, and his eyes were wet and burning. "Sera. You wore the gray. Tell me you didn't know. Tell me there was a version of you that didn't know."

I wanted to give him that. I searched for it honestly, the way you search your pockets for a coin you're sure is there.

"No," I said. "There wasn't. We knew something. Not this—never the shape of it—but we knew the peace was bought with something, and we decided not to price it." I nodded at the glittering, murmuring, dead-alive court. "That's what not pricing it looks like after twenty years."

He absorbed that the way a wall absorbs a blow—no motion, all damage.

Ada stepped between us, small and immovable. "Grieve later," she said, not unkindly. "Throne room's empty. That means the thing wearing the crown is somewhere else, and I want to know where before it knows we're here."

She was right, and I hated how right she was, and I led on.

* * *

We crossed the west wing through corridors of increasing silence. The court noise faded behind us until there was nothing but our footsteps and the tick of frost settling in the walls. And then Wren stopped so suddenly I walked into his back.

"This is my corridor," he said.

I looked past him. Tapestries. A window with the winter light coming through it thick as milk. A plain oak door with a scribe's mark burned into the wood—an old guild sign, pre-crown, the kind of thing no one bothered to remove because no one remembered what it meant.

"Your quarters?"

"I had rooms in the east tower. These were—" He pushed the door open.

I have seen shrines. I grew up beside a hearth-shrine with an outlawed sigil carved in its lintel, hidden under whitewash, and I know what devotion looks like when it has nowhere legal to go. What was in that room was devotion with every resource of a palace behind it.

Wren's old quarters—his *real* quarters, the ones he'd kept before elevation, or perhaps after disgrace, I didn't know—had been preserved. Exactly preserved. Ink pots capped and waiting. Quills ranked in their jar. A cloak hung on its peg with the creases still in it from the last shoulders that wore it. On the desk, a letter lay half-finished, pen laid across it mid-sentence, six years stale.

Nobody had dusted. Nobody had *dared*. The room wasn't maintained; it was enshrined. And on every wall, in frames, were copies of documents in a fine, disciplined hand—decrees, correspondence, marginalia—all of it in Wren's handwriting.

"They kept my work," he whispered. He drifted to the desk and picked up the unfinished letter with fingers that trembled. "They kept—"

"Of course they did," I said, and the understanding arrived in me cold and complete. "Don't you see? You were the last person the king trusted before the crown finished taking him. Your quarters aren't a memorial to you. They're bait. Or a reliquary. Something in that thing still remembers you, still circles back to you the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. This room is the shape of the hole you left in it."

Wren stood very still among his own ghost's belongings. Then he turned the unfinished letter over, and went white.

"What?" Ada asked.

"It's addressed to me." He read aloud, barely voicing it: "*Wren—the garden again tonight. It is wearing my mouth to speak to them and I am so tired. If you find this before it finds you—remember the valley. Remember Hollowmere. I put what's left of me in the map. Forgive me. Forgive—*" The sentence simply stopped, mid-word, the ink trailed off in a shaky line, as if the hand writing it had been interrupted. Or reclaimed.

Silence, except the frost ticking in the walls.

"So the messages were real," Ada said softly. "The buried king really has been sending things out."

"For six years this letter has been sitting here," Wren said. "Waiting. Like everything else in this room." He folded it with a scribe's precision and slid it inside his coat, over his heart, next to the glow that was slowly eating him. "He asked my forgiveness. From inside the cage. While the thing was learning to use his mouth."

Ada was looking around the room—at the quills, the cloak, the frozen mid-sentence of it all—with an expression I was beginning to recognize. It was the look she got at the ford, deciding to break guild law for a drowning stranger. Decision assembling itself behind her eyes, plank by plank.

"Sera," she said. "Where does the thing wearing the crown actually sleep? Not the throne. The *thing*."

I thought of the wardens' deepest briefing, the one passed mouth to mouth with the sigil-oath, the one I'd sworn never to repeat. I thought about oaths, and what they were worth, and what I'd already decided they were worth the day I walked out of the gray.

"There's a door behind the throne dais," I said. "Down. Farther than the underways. The wardens call it the Root. We were taught it was where the crown's power was stored." I met her eyes. "We were also taught never to ask what it was storage *for*."

Outside the shrine-room's little window, the winter light thickened, and somewhere far below us—below the court, below the foundations, below sense—I felt it, the way you feel a storm through the floor of a house.

Something down in the dark had just noticed that its scribe had come home.

And it was smiling.
