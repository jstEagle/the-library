## 19. Two Hands on the Seal

I have been a scribe for a king who was not there to read what I wrote, and I have been a courier for a map that wanted me dead, and I have lied to the woman I love with the best of intentions, which is the worst way to do it. So believe me when I say I know how things go wrong in throne rooms. They go wrong quickly, and politely, and then all at once.

The sigil-thing was still talking.

That was the horror of it, really—not the voice itself, though the voice was wrong in ways I could spend the rest of my life describing and never finish. It was that it kept talking. It had Ada's valley in one hand and Wren's-healed-body in the other, metaphorically speaking, and it laid them out like a merchant laying out cloth, patient, unhurried, certain that everything could be bought because everything had a price and it had never once met a price it couldn't pay.

"Your fog," it said through the king's mouth. "I will let your valley keep its fog. A thousand years of hiding, preserved like a fly in amber. And the scribe—" it turned the crowned head toward me, and the eyes were the king's eyes, gray-green, tired at the corners even now, even hollowed, and I felt that tiredness like a hook behind my ribs—"the scribe's wound closed by morning. He has carried it so long. Wouldn't you like him well, heir of Ashgrove? Wouldn't you like him whole?"

And Ada—Ada Ashgrove, who had crossed a winter country with a stranger's blood drying on her sleeves, who had learned warding in three days that took guild members years, who had stood in a drowned chapel and put her hand where her ancestor's hand had gone without being told—Ada looked at the thing wearing her king's face and said, quietly, "No."

Not no, thank you. Not no with trembling. Just: no. The way you'd refuse a second helping of something rotten.

I loved her in that moment with a completeness that frightened me, which is saying something, because I had already loved her at the ford and in the snow and on rooftops while Graycloaks chased us across Ashmarket's slates. But love at the ford had been gratitude. Love on the rooftops had been adrenaline wearing a kinder mask. This was different. This was watching someone stand in front of the largest temptation in the world and yawn at it.

Then Sera moved.

I should have seen it coming. Sera Vane had told us her price in the underways, plain as a knife on a table: we swear to kill the king, or she walks. We hadn't sworn. Wren—who is me, I keep having to remind myself that this narration has an owner—had said, *I knew him. I can't promise you he's gone.* And Sera had looked at me the way you look at a man who insists the house isn't haunted while cold air pours down the stairs, and she had said nothing, and she had come anyway, and I should have understood that a woman who comes anyway is a woman with her own plans.

Her blade cleared its sheath with a sound like a struck bell, and every sigil in the throne room answered it.

* * *

Let me describe the room, because I was its scribe once and I never truly left it.

The throne room of the Hollowed King is long and white and wrong. The pillars are carved with the founders' binding-script, but the script has been eaten from the inside, each character hollowed into a little smiling absence. The windows show a winter that does not end. And the throne itself—obsidian, tall as a gallows—sits under a crown of light that is not decoration. It is the sigil-thing's body. The man in the chair is just where it keeps its hands.

When Sera's blade came up, the light came down.

It came down like weather. Like judgment with excellent manners. I saw it take the air between Sera and the throne and turn it to frost, saw her blade bloom white with rime, saw her—a woman who had walked out of the Graycloaks' ranks carrying nothing but her convictions—stagger as the crown's will pressed on her like a hand on the back of the neck. *Kneel,* said the room, without words. *Kneel and be comfortable. Kneel and stop hurting.*

Sera did not kneel. She screamed instead, which I have always thought takes more courage, and drove forward another step, boots skating on ice that hadn't existed a heartbeat before.

And the sigil-thing didn't even look at her. That was the insult of it. It kept its ruined, beautiful eyes on Ada and said, "Shall I let her die, then? You could stop this. Two keys, freely given, and everyone walks away warm."

"Everyone except him," Ada said.

The pause that followed lasted half a breath and cost the world six weeks of winter, though none of us knew that yet.

"He is not *in* there," the thing said, and for the first time its patience showed a seam. "He is a coat I wear. He is a name I keep for convenience. Whatever you imagine is buried beneath me, heir, it is not a man anymore. It is an echo with opinions."

And I—fool, scribe, liar, courier—did the one thing I had sworn on the drowned altar I would do if it came to it.

I told her the truth.

* * *

"The third key is you."

I said it loud, over the ringing of Sera's blade against the crown's frost, over the wind that was building in a sealed room, and I watched it land on Ada like a dropped roof.

"Two keys unlock," I said. "The third binds. The archive was clear—the founder wrote it herself. The seal was always meant to be remade with her heir inside it. Alive. At the heart. Forever." My voice cracked on *forever*, which was appropriate, since forever was the operative word. "That's what I've been keeping from you since the monastery. That's why Vess went pale. That's why the fog let you leave, Ada—it wasn't done with you. It was never going to be done with you."

The sigil-thing laughed.

I want you to understand what that laugh was. It was the king's laugh—I'd heard it a hundred times in the old days, warm, self-deprecating, the laugh of a man who apologized to furniture he bumped into—but played backward, emptied, worn like a stolen coat. It laughed because I had handed it the argument it had been making all along. *See?* the laugh said. *Even your own side agrees. The seal wants her. Give her to me and call it duty.*

"That's enough," Ada said.

She said it to both of us. To me, kneeling in my confession; to the crown, dripping its patient poison. And then she did the thing that I will be writing about, badly, for the rest of whatever life I'm given.

She walked past Sera's frozen blade. She walked through the frost-wind. She climbed the three steps to the obsidian throne, took the keys from around her neck—the two of them, founder-forged, singing faintly like struck glass—and she seized the king's hand.

The flare knocked every one of us off our feet.

* * *

Here is what I know about what happened next, and I know it the way you know a dream: incompletely, and with terrible certainty.

The keys flared white, and the white was not light but *place*. The throne room folded. The winter folded. And Ada—still gripping the king's hand, still standing, though her hair lifted and her eyes had gone wide and blind—was pulled *inside*.

Inside the sigil. Inside the seal. Into the space between the crown and the man it ate.

I got there the only way I could: I grabbed her other hand.

I am not the heir. I am not a key. I have no blood-right to anything except a very good memory and a talent for being in the wrong room. But I had held her hand across a frozen country, and apparently that counted for something, because the sigil let me in—or maybe it simply didn't notice one more small warm thing being dragged into its machinery, the way a flood doesn't notice a leaf.

We fell together into the white.

* * *

There was a garden.

Of course there was a garden. There is always a garden at the center of these things; I think the soul, pressed far enough, makes one the way a shell makes pearl around a wound. It was the palace garden as it had been twenty years ago, before the frost—wisteria on the south wall, a dry fountain, gravel paths raked in patterns I recognized because I had been the one who described them, once, in a letter the king dictated to his mother.

He sat on the fountain's rim like a man waiting for a coach that is very late.

He was smaller than I remembered. That was the first cruelty of it. In my memory King Aldous Vane had filled rooms; this man seemed apologetic about taking up the bench. His crown was there—of course it was there, fused to his skull like scar tissue, dim now, guttering—and his hands were a scribe's hands, ink-stained at the knuckles, and when he looked up at Ada his eyes were clear.

Clear. After twenty years. That was the second cruelty: they had left him lucid in there, all this time, aware.

"You're the Ashgrove girl," he said. His voice was rusted. "You have her hands. Elyse Ashgrove had exactly that way of standing, like the ground might ask something of her and she intended to be ready." He tried to smile. It worked, mostly. "I'm sorry about the welcome. He does the talking."

"I know what you are," Ada said. Her hand was still in mine; her grip had gone painful. "You're the king."

"I'm what's left." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "A residue. An opinion the crown hasn't gotten around to eating." He looked at our joined hands, and something old and kind moved through his face. "Ah. You brought each other. Good. That's—that's better than I managed, at the end. I sent my messages out alone."

"The letters," I said. "The ones in the map-tube. I found them."

"I know. You were always going to find them. You were the only one who checked the lining." He laughed, a small real laugh, and the garden brightened around it, wisteria shivering in a wind that existed nowhere else in the world. Then the brightness guttered, and his face went grave, and he leaned forward on the fountain rim with the urgency of a man whose time is measured in something shorter than minutes.

"Listen to me. Both of you. She can remake the seal." He nodded at Ada. "My great-something-grandmother's heir, the living key, the whole inheritance. She can bind me back into the crown, seal the sigil-thing around me like a fist around a wasp, and the world goes back to sleep. Safe. Hidden. A thousand more years of fog and frost and villages full of smiling vacancies." His voice dropped. "With her at the heart of it. Alone. Forever. That is the price the founders agreed to, and I have had twenty years inside this thing to decide what I think of the bargain, and I will tell you what I think."

He stood. It cost him. The crown flared and he swayed, and Ada reached for him and he waved her off gently, the way you wave off help with a door that's heavy but yours.

"I think it was a coward's arithmetic," he said. "One life weighed against the world, and the world always wins, and so the same family pays and pays and pays, century after century, and we call it nobility because we've forgotten it's actually just a bill." He took Ada's hand—the key-hand, the founder's hand—and pressed it between his ink-stained fingers. "Don't save me at the world's expense. And don't save the world at hers. There is a third door. There is always a third door; the founders just never had the stomach to look for it, because looking meant risking everything they'd built."

"What third door?" I asked.

But the garden was flickering now, frost climbing the wisteria, the sigil-thing noticing us the way a sleeper notices lice, and the king was fading at the edges like ink in rain.

"Ask her," he said, nodding at Ada. "Elyse left more than a seal. She left a *choice*, written where only her heir's blood could read it. You've been reading it all along, girl—in the monastery, in the underways, in the walls that sang to you. You thought it was instinct." His smile was the realest thing in that false garden. "It was instructions."

The white rushed in. The last thing I saw was the king sitting back down on the fountain rim, arranging himself with a scribe's neatness, a man tidying his desk before the end of the world.

* * *

We came back to ourselves on the throne room floor, and the cold hit like a slap.

Sera was on one knee, blade braced against the flagstones, frost to her elbows, holding the crown's will off by sheer hatred. The sigil-thing had stopped negotiating. The light above the throne had gone from patient gold to a hungry, strobing white, and the whole palace groaned around us like a ship changing course, and somewhere below us—far below, in the deep archive where the founders had worked—the walls had begun to sing.

Ada heard it first. Of course she did.

"It's true," she whispered. She was still holding my hand hard enough to grind bone. Her eyes were wet and furious and lit from underneath, and she was looking at the throne, at the crown, at the flickering shape of a tired man trapped inside his own salvation. "The third door. He wasn't lying. I can feel it—the script under the floor, the founder's hand, it's not a lock, it never was a lock, it's a *hinge*—"

"Ada." I got my knees under me. Every joint I owned filed a complaint. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"You hid the truth from me to protect me," she said, turning, and there was no anger in it, which was infinitely worse than anger. "So here's yours, Wren. I'm not going to reforge the seal. And I'm not going to let him die in there. I'm going to do what the founder wrote and nobody had the nerve to read."

Above the throne, the crown flared, and the sigil-thing's voice came down like an avalanche learning manners:

"*You cannot.*"

Ada Ashgrove smiled—small, tired, absolutely certain—and reached for the third key.

Which was, and had always been, her own hand.
