## 20. Breaking the Seal

Here's what nobody tells you about the end of the world: it's mostly paperwork.

The sigil-script that covered my left hand from fingertip to elbow had been itching for three days, like a scar remembering it used to be a wound. Now, with the third key awake in my skin and the founder's memories sitting in my head like a guest who'd overstayed by two hundred years, I finally understood what the itching meant. It wasn't a warning. It was an invitation. The seal wanted to close. It wanted me inside it, warm and willing and gone, and it had been polite about asking the whole way here.

The sigil-thing wearing the king's crown had made its offer. The sigil-script in my blood had made its counter-offer. And Wren, bleeding on the palace steps with a hollowing wound eating him from the inside out, had confessed his last secret at the worst possible moment, which was, in fairness, the only moment he'd ever been given.

So. Paperwork. Because that's what binding-magic was, when you got down to it—contracts. Clauses. A founder who'd signed her name in blood and then signed her *heir* in the margin, small print nobody had read for six generations, and me, standing in a frozen throne room, about to contest the terms.

"You cannot," the crown had said.

"Watch me," I said, and took hold of the king's hand.

* * *

The world went white, then went *elsewhere*.

I'd been inside the sigil once before, in the underways, when my blood had brushed the walls and the walls had brushed back. That had been like pressing my ear to a door. This was the door opening. This was walking through.

The space inside the crown-sigil was a hall I recognized from my own dreams, which was either flattering or deeply alarming. Pillars of light that had once been words. A floor of script that shifted under my feet like river ice. And at the center, kneeling in a circle of his own unraveling name, the king.

Not the sigil-thing. Not the Hollowed King with his crown of eating light and his voice like an avalanche. A man. Gaunt, gray, dressed in the plain wool of a scholar rather than the gold of a court, with ink stains on his fingers that I knew—*knew*—because I'd spent the last week reading letters written by that same hand.

"You're the scribe's king," I said. "Wren's king."

He looked up. His eyes were tired in the specific way that water is tired when it has been pushing against a dam for a very long time.

"You're the founder's girl," he said. His voice was rough from disuse, from centuries of speaking only through the thing that wore him. "Edith's line. You have her stubborn chin."

"You knew the founder?"

"I knew *of* her. I was crowned two hundred years after she sealed the valley." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "They told me the crown was an honor. They told me it was the guild's greatest work—a king bound to his kingdom, a ruler who could never be corrupted, never be killed, never *leave*. I was twenty-three. I thought never leaving sounded like devotion." He spread his hands, and I saw the sigil-script crawling up his wrists, the same script that crawled down mine, and understood with a lurch that we matched. Two halves of one contract. "It's a cage, Ada Ashgrove. It has been a cage for six hundred years, and the thing that lives in the lock has been eating me one memory at a time, and I no longer remember my mother's face, and I would like, very much, to be done."

The crown flared above us—here, even here, the sigil-thing had reach, a pressure at the edge of the hall like a storm leaning on a window.

"*Girl.*" The voice came through the pillars of light, through the floor, through my own bones. "*You hold the founder's authority. Reforge the seal. Name your price. I will give you the valley forever. I will give you the scribe's life. I will give you anything you can name.*"

"It always offers," the king said quietly. "It offered me too, at the start. That's how it feeds. It doesn't take. It *trades*. And every trade costs the trader."

"I know." I knelt across from him, so we faced each other like two people playing at cards, which, in a sense, we were. "It offered me a life where I could disappear forever. A woman in Ashmarket offered me the same thing. A valley offered it to me for twenty-two years. I've had a lot of offers, and here's what I've learned: everything that promises to keep you safe is really promising to keep you."

"*Sentiment,*" the crown said. "*The founder had none. That is why her seal held.*"

"The founder," I said, "wrote a second clause."

The hall went still. Even the pressure of the crown seemed to lean in.

"I read it," I went on, and my voice didn't shake, which surprised me. "In the underways, on the wall where the carvings show her pressing her hand into the seal. Everyone reads the first clause—*the seal shall hold, and the heir shall hold it.* Nobody reads the second, because the second is written in her own private cipher, the one she only used in letters to her sister, and I only know it because I've spent my whole life copying her hand into a ledger nobody read." I looked at the king. "The second clause says: *if the heir judges the seal a cruelty, she may unmake it, and pour the binding into the thing it binds.*"

The king's breath stopped.

"Into the crown," I said. "Not into me. Not into another girl in another valley, two hundred years from now, itching and waiting. Into *it*. The sigil-thing. The eater. Let it hold what it made. Let the cage close on the cage."

"*You would not dare—*"

"You keep saying that," I said. "It's getting embarrassing for you."

* * *

The trouble with unmaking a six-hundred-year-old seal, I discovered, is that it doesn't want to be unmade. Of course it doesn't. Nothing that has held for six centuries wants to stop. The script in my skin woke up all at once and *argued*, a pressure like drowning in reverse, my own blood trying to close my hand into the shape of the first clause, the safe clause, the clause that ended with Ada Ashgrove as a warm stone at the bottom of a very deep well, keeping everyone safe, keeping everyone out, keeping everyone *nothing*, forever.

The valley had done it to me my whole life, I realized. The fog. The forgetting. Hollowmere hadn't been protecting us from magic. It had been practicing.

"*You will be alone,*" the crown whispered, and it had found the exact frequency of my worst nights, the ledger with its dead names, the roofs I repaired for members who'd forgotten why the house stood. "*You will be safe. No one will ever leave you, because no one will ever reach you. Is that not what you have always wanted?*"

And here's the thing. It wasn't wrong about what I'd wanted. That was the horror of it. I had wanted exactly that, for years, and I had called it duty because duty sounded better than loneliness.

But I'd also crossed a country in winter. I'd learned to ward a doorway in a village of small kings. I'd been hidden by a shrine-keeper who owed me nothing. I'd read a dead man's letters and a dead woman's cipher and a living scribe's terrible, oblique, too-late confession. I had spent a whole winter learning the thing the fog had spent my whole life teaching me to forget:

Alone wasn't safety. Alone was just the seal, and I was not going to be a seal. I was a person. Persons are terrible at holding. Persons are *built* to break.

"No," I said.

I reached for the king's hands.

* * *

Breaking a seal, it turns out, feels like this:

It feels like the moment a fever breaks. It feels like ice going out on the river—all at once, with a sound like the world clearing its throat. It feels like every door in a house opening at the same time in a wind you didn't know was waiting.

I poured the founder's binding through my hands and into the crown, and the crown *screamed*, and the scream was not one voice but thousands—the memories it had eaten, six hundred years of them, my mother's face and your mother's face and every name in every ledger in every guild house, all of them coming home through a crack I'd made with a dead woman's cipher and a stubborn chin.

The hall of light came apart around us. The pillars of script fell like rain. And the sigil-thing, the eater, the thing that had worn a kingdom like a coat—it burned. I felt it burn. I felt it try to let go of the crown and find that the binding had closed around *it*, that the lock had become the cage, that the last clause of the founder's contract was simply this: *what eats shall be eaten by its own shape, and the heir shall judge, and the judgment is final.*

I judged.

The white went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor of the throne room with Wren's hands on my shoulders and Sera's blade somewhere on the ground and the king—*the king*—crying.

Not the sigil-thing. Not the avalanche voice. A man in plain wool, on his knees in a frozen throne room, weeping like a boy, saying "my mother's name was Ilsette, my mother's name was *Ilsette*," over and over, the way you say a thing you've just gotten back, testing it, holding it up to the light.

The crown sat on the steps where it had fallen. It was just metal now. Old, tarnished, slightly dented, the kind of thing you'd find in a pawnshop and haggle down to nothing. The long winter that had stood outside the windows cracked—audibly, a sound like the whole sky shrugging—and through the frost on the glass I saw the first thaw-light in six hundred years come slanting into the palace, gold and ordinary and absolutely shameless about it.

"Ada." Wren's voice, hoarse. "Ada, look at me."

I looked. His wound—the hollowing, the gray-edged gash that had been eating him since Ashmarket—was closing. I watched it happen. The gray retreated like a tide going out, and underneath it his skin was just skin, and his eyes were just eyes, and they were fixed on me with an expression I'd seen once before, on the rooftop, at the worst possible time, when he'd thought he was about to run out of time to say it.

"You did it," he said. "You absolute— you *hid the second clause from me*, you let me think—"

"You hid the third key from me," I said. "We're even. Don't make me keep a ledger, Wren. I'm very good at ledgers."

He laughed, and it turned into something else halfway through, and then I was holding him and he was holding me, and the thaw-light came through the windows and lay across us both like a blanket someone had finally thought to bring.

Sera stood over the crown with her blade drawn, breathing hard, staring at the king as if expecting the monster to climb back out of him at any moment. It didn't. He was too busy weeping over his mother's name. Eventually she sheathed the blade, slowly, like she was setting down something very heavy.

"It's over?" she asked. Not to me. To him.

The king looked up. His face was wrecked and human and sixty years old in the way that faces are when they've been thirty for six centuries.

"It's over," he said. "And I owe a debt to a guild I abolished, a scribe I forgot, a warden who aimed a blade at me, and a girl from a valley I never knew existed." He wiped his face with an ink-stained hand. "I intend to start paying."

Outside, all across the capital, the long winter cracked and cracked and cracked.

And somewhere far north, past the ridgeline, I would have sworn I felt the fog around Hollowmere thin by a single, careful thread.

* * *

We walked out of the palace through streets that were waking up.

That's the only word for it. People stood in doorways blinking, like sleepers called too early, looking at their own hands, at the thaw, at each other, with the slow dawning bewilderment of the unforgotten. A woman dropped an armful of bread and didn't notice. A child laughed at a puddle and the laugh had *weight* in it, real and unperformed, and I had to stop walking for a moment because I hadn't realized until then how quiet the hollowed laughter had been.

Wren walked beside me with his healed hand brushing mine, not quite holding it, in the manner of a man who had confessed on a rooftop and intended to do the rest properly, with time, in daylight, like a person.

"You're going back," he said. Not a question.

"The guild house has nine members who think I left to fix a roof."

"And the ledger."

"And the ledger." I smiled. "Two hundred years of names, Wren. It's time somebody wrote a new one."

Behind us, the palace doors stood open for the first time in living memory. Ahead of us, the road north was mud and meltwater and the first green things deciding, cautiously, to exist.

I had spent my whole life on the wrong side of the map. It was, I reflected, walking out of the capital of a waking kingdom with ink-stained fingers laced through mine, a very good day to redraw it.
