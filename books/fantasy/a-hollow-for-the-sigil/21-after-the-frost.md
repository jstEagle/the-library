## 21. After the Frost

### The King

I do not remember the first thing I did with my own hands.

I remember the second: I wept into them, which is less dignified, and then a young woman with ash-colored hair and the posture of someone who has carried too much for too long said, "You're going to have to stand up eventually, Your Majesty. The floor is very cold."

She was right about the floor. She was wrong about the majesty, but I let it pass, because arguing with the woman who had just burned a god out of your skull seemed ungrateful.

Her name was Ada Ashgrove. I would learn, in the weeks that followed, that she hated being thanked, was suspicious of comfort, and could repair a roof, which struck me as the most kingly skill I had ever heard of. My father had repaired nothing in his life except his reputation, and he'd done that badly.

The first days were a blur of small humiliations. I could not remember my mother's face. I could remember the sigil-script for *obedience* in eleven variations but not whether I liked honey in my tea. (I did not. This discovery delighted me more than any decree I ever signed.) There were gaps where a man should have years — decades, really — and in the gaps there was only the crown's voice, patient as frost, saying *smile*.

Wren found me on the fourth morning, standing in the throne room, staring at the chair.

"You don't have to sit in it," he said.

"I know."

"You're allowed to burn it."

"I know that too." I touched the armrest. It was warm. Everything in this palace had been warm for years, fed on something it should never have been fed. "But if I burn it, everyone will say the madness is still in me. So instead I'm going to sit in it once, publicly, and then give it to a museum with a placard that says *here sat a chair that ate a kingdom*. Let the historians choke on it."

Wren laughed — a rusty sound, like a door opening after a long winter, which I suppose it was. He had been my scribe before. I remembered him now in pieces: ink-stained fingers, a habit of humming when he copied tedious tax ledgers, the way he'd once argued with me for an hour about a bridge toll because a widow in the third quarter couldn't afford it. I had forgotten him. The crown had eaten him out of me like a moth through wool.

"I'm sorry," I told him. "For forgetting you."

He looked at his feet. "You sent letters," he said. "Down inside it. Little ones. I got them all."

"Did I?"

"Every week. For six years." He finally looked up. "That's how I knew you were still in there. A hollowed man doesn't write *the roses by the east wall are lying to us, check the cellar*. Whatever else that thing took, it couldn't take the part of you that noticed things."

I thought about that for a long time afterward. Noticing things. It seemed a small foundation for a soul, and yet it had held.

On the ninth day I stood before what remained of the court — half of them hollowed, half of them terrified, all of them watching me for signs that the thing in the crown had merely changed its hairstyle — and I abolished the Crown's Claim over sigil-craft. Every edict, every registry, every warden's writ. Four hundred years of law, undone in a page and a half, because Wren wrote fast and I had learned from a girl from a hidden valley that the only honest seal is one you're willing to break.

I read it aloud twice. The second time, my voice cracked on the word *free*, and I decided to let it crack. A king who cannot weep in front of his people is a king with room in his head for worse things.

Afterward, an old gardener approached me in the corridor. He didn't bow. He just said, "The roses by the east wall, sire. They are lying. They've been dead two years."

I laughed until I cried, and then I went to check the cellar.

### Sera

They gave me the wardens because nobody else wanted them, and because I asked.

Not asked politely. Asked the way you ask when you've spent nine years enforcing a lie and you'd rather die than watch it be rebranded. I stood in the warden barracks — three hundred Graycloaks who hadn't been at the palace, who'd spent the Long Winter freezing villages and calling it quarantine — and I told them the truth in four sentences:

The crown was a parasite. We were its teeth. The teeth don't get to pretend they were smiling.

Then I offered them a choice, which was more than they'd ever offered anyone: take the gray off, walk out the gate, and live quietly somewhere no one knows your face. Or stay, put on new colors, and spend the rest of your life earning back what we spent. No middle road. No *I was only following the sigil*. The sigil is dead. Following it is not an excuse anymore; it's a confession.

Forty walked. Two hundred and sixty stayed.

I expected more to leave. I think some of them expected me to beg. I have been told I am bad at begging, which is the nicest thing anyone has said about me since the thaw.

Reforming a secret police is like teaching a wolf to garden: possible, slow, and you will be bitten. We started with the hollowed villages. There were eleven of them, scattered across the lowlands like dropped stitches — places where the harvest had come early and left people standing in their doorways, smiling at weather that had stopped listening to the crown. The healers said the vacancy might fade on its own, now that the thing feeding on it was gone. Might. In years. Possibly never, for the oldest cases.

So we did what wardens do, but pointed the other way. We guarded. We carried. We knocked on doors and said *we're the ones who took your sons*, which is a terrible way to start a conversation and the only honest one available to us.

In the village of Brant's Ford, a woman threw a pot of dishwater at me. I deserved it. I stood there dripping and said, "I'll come back tomorrow, if you'll tell me what your son looked like. I took him. I should know his face."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she said, "You'll need the other pot. That one has a chip."

It wasn't forgiveness. It was a chipped pot and a tomorrow. I have built cathedrals out of less.

By midwinter's end we had walked every hollowed village twice, and in three of them, people began to come back. Not all at once. A miller's wife blinked her way out of a smile that had been nailed on for a year and asked, first thing, whether her bread had burned. It had. She wept over the ruined loaf like it was a christening, and her husband held her, and I stood in the doorway feeling something I had no protocol for.

Ada Ashgrove came through on her way home and watched me drill the new wardens in the square at Ashmarket. Afterward she said, "You know they'll hate you. The ones you're training, and the ones you're helping. Maybe especially the ones you're helping."

"Yes."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"It's the only job I'm qualified for," I said. "I spent nine years learning exactly how the harm works. It seems wasteful to throw away the education."

She almost smiled. From her, I've since learned, that's a knighthood.

### Wren

The fever broke on the road home, and I woke to the smell of woodsmoke and Ada arguing with a farmer about the price of eggs.

This is how I knew the world had actually changed and I hadn't died and invented a kinder version: the farmer was winning. Under the old law, selling eggs to a stranger with a guild pin was a flogging offense. Now he was charging her double anyway, because commerce is eternal, and she was paying it while insulting his chickens' ancestry, and neither of them feared anything except each other's stubbornness. It was the most beautiful market transaction I have ever witnessed, and I once watched a duke pay four hundred crowns for a hat shaped like a fish.

My wound had closed. That deserves plainer words: the sigil-touched hole in my side, the one that had been slowly eating me toward the edges for a month, sealed itself the night the crown burned. Sera's healer said the hollowing reversed like water running backward up a hill. I said that wasn't how water worked. She said, "Neither was any of this, scribe. Drink your tea."

I drank my tea.

What do you do, after? Nobody warns you about this part. There are songs about the quest and none about the Tuesday after, when the quest is over and your hands still shake reaching for a doorknob because for a month every doorknob might have been a warden. I kept drafting the official history of the breaking — the king wanted it honest, God help him, honest, with the failures left in — and every afternoon I'd stop mid-sentence and just look at my hands. Whole hands. Ink-stained, shaking slightly, whole.

Ada caught me at it once, in the inn at Millbrook.

"They're just hands," she said.

"They were nearly not-hands."

"Mm." She sat down across from me and stole a piece of my bread, which is apparently how she says tender things. "When I was copying names into the ledger, back home — dead members, generations of them — I used to wonder what they did the morning after whatever their crisis was. Whether they woke up and felt lighter or just felt empty." She chewed. "I think it's both. I think lightness and emptiness feel identical for about a week, and then one of them grows furniture."

"That's not how metaphors work."

"Neither was any of this," she said, and stole the rest of my bread.

She was right, though. It took about a week. Then the lightness grew furniture: the king's ridiculous honesty, Sera's chipped-pot diplomacy, the hearth-shrines in the villages lighting up one by one as people dared, cautiously, to carve a sigil above their fires again — small ones, warding-words, protection-knots, nothing grand. Magic came back to the lowlands the way spring comes: not as a flood but as a rumor, then a green shoot, then a morning when you realize the whole valley hums and you can't name the day it started.

I saw a grandmother in Ashmarket teach her granddaughter a mending-charm — just a little stitch-seal, the kind that was worth a branding when I was the king's scribe. The girl botched it. The button fell off. They laughed and tried again. I stood in the street like a fool with tears running down my face, and a fruit-seller handed me a pear, free, and said, "Rough morning?"

"The best kind," I said, and meant it.

### Ada

We came home slow, because Wren's legs were still learning to trust him and because I had stopped believing in hurry somewhere outside the capital. Hurry had gotten us chased across a rooftop. Slowness got us invited to dinner in four villages and taught me a card game I intend to lose at for the rest of my life.

Hollowmere announced itself the usual way: fog at the ridgeline, thick as spilled milk. But it was thinner. Everyone felt it. Wren said the valley's hiding-magic had been woven from the same binding as the seal, and with the seal broken, the fog was unraveling — gently, thread by thread, like a sweater deciding to become yarn again.

Elder Maren met us at the gate. She looked older. She also looked, for the first time since I'd known her, awake.

"You went," she said.

"You voted against it."

"I voted against it because I was afraid, and I dressed the fear up as wisdom, which is what old people do." She looked past me at the thinning fog, at the lowlands visible through it for the first time in centuries — actual hills, actual sky, a road with actual cart-ruts. "How many came back?"

"All of us that mattered."

She nodded slowly. "Then I owe the ledger an apology. And an entry." She turned and walked inside without another word, which from Maren is a full embrace.

The guild house needed everything. Roofs, glass, the great hall's floorboards, the kitchen chimney that had smoked sideways for a decade out of pure spite. I had missed it. That surprised me — I'd spent twenty-two years restless inside these walls, certain I'd been born on the wrong side of the map, and it took crossing the map twice to see that the house itself had never been the problem. The hollowness hadn't been in Hollowmere. It had been in the seal, in the fear, in the closed doors. The house was just a house. Houses wait.

We worked through the last of winter. Wren re-glazed windows and complained about the quality of Hollowmere putty, which he had decided was a personal enemy. Maren cooked. The other elders pretended to supervise and mostly napped by the fire, which I allowed, because they had earned a nap and I had earned the pleasure of watching them take it.

And travelers started coming.

First one — a tinker who'd heard rumors of a valley where the fog had lifted, asking cautious questions at the ford. Then a family, then a pair of sisters from the hollowed country who wanted to learn warding for their village, then a boy of maybe fifteen who arrived with a bindle and a note from Sera that read, in its entirety: *vouching for this one. If he annoys you, send him to me and I'll find him something worse.*

Maren wanted to vote on each of them. Admit them, refuse them, deliberate, form a committee. I understood. Deliberation had kept the guild alive for four hundred years the way a locked box keeps a letter alive.

But I had seen what locked boxes cost.

"We're not voting," I told her. "The doors are open. Anyone who knocks and means no harm can come in and learn."

"And if they mean harm?"

"Then we'll know," I said. "That's what wards are for. Not walls. Wards. You can see through a ward. You can't see through a wall, so you end up afraid of everything on the other side of it, forever, including the mail."

She grumbled. She always grumbles. But that evening I found her teaching the tinker the guild's greeting-sign, patiently, correcting his fingers like a mother with a knot.

Spring came early to the valley, or maybe it just finally got in. Snowmelt ran loud in the ford where I'd pulled a dying man out of the water a lifetime ago — five months, but it feels like a different geological era, one in which I did not yet know what I was. The first crocuses came up through the guild house courtyard stones, in the exact pattern of the founder's seal, which either means something profound or means the stonework drains that way. With magic loose in the world again, I've decided to stop needing to know which.

Some nights I still wake expecting the fog. Expecting the weight of the old silence, the sense of being a name in a ledger no one reads. I lie in the dark and listen instead to the house: Wren's breathing in the next room, the traveler-boy's snores, wind in a chimney that draws properly now. The house sounds inhabited. It sounds like the opposite of hollow.

I get up, most nights, and go down to the great hall, where the ledger lies open on the table under the window. Founder's name and mine, side by side, in the oldest hand and mine. Below them, space. Pages and pages of space.

I used to think that was emptiness.

It isn't. It's room.
