## 22. A Guild With Open Doors

The fog began to leave on a Tuesday.

I know this because Elder Maren marked it in the kitchen calendar, the one we used for tracking which of us owed which of us for flour, and she wrote *fog thinning* in the margin where the debts usually went. It seemed as good a ledger as any. Hollowmere had spent three hundred years keeping accounts with the mist—what it hid, what it cost, what it took—and now that it was settling its bill, someone ought to note the transaction.

It didn't leave all at once. Nothing in this valley has ever done anything all at once except me, and look how that turned out. The fog thinned the way grief does: imperceptibly day to day, then suddenly you looked up one morning and realized you could see the far ridge, every fold of it, the scree fields and the black pines and the notch where Wren and I had crossed into a country I'd only ever known as *beyond*. The ridgeline had been a rumor my whole life. Now it was just geography. It felt almost rude.

Maren hated it. She stood at the gate with her arms folded like a woman watching a child walk off with her good shawl and said, "We'll be visible now."

"We were always visible," I said. "We just couldn't see ourselves."

She gave me the look she'd been giving me since I was six, the one that said *you were a difficult child and you have become a difficult adult, congratulations*, and went inside to make tea about it. Maren's response to every crisis is tea. Her response to the end of a centuries-long magical quarantine was a full pot and a lecture about how the young mistake openness for recklessness, delivered to an audience of nine guild members who mostly nodded because nodding meant she'd stop talking sooner.

But here is what I noticed, standing at the gate after she'd gone: the air smelled different. Three hundred years of fog will do things to a valley's breath—it goes stale, close, like a room no one opens—and now there was wind in it, actual wind, carrying pine resin and snowmelt and something green and impatient from the lowlands. Spring arriving not as a season but as a decision.

* * *

The first travelers came eleven days later.

I was re-shingling the south roof—the guild house had developed opinions about winter and expressed them through leaks—when Tam came running out with his apron still on, shouting my name in the tone people reserve for fires and births. I nearly put a hammer through my own foot.

"Riders," he said. "On the road. Four of them. They're just—" He gestured helplessly at the whole concept. "They're just *riding up*. Like it's a place."

"It is a place," I said, climbing down. "That was always the problem. We kept insisting otherwise."

They were four, and they were dusty, and they stopped at the ford and stared at the guild house the way pilgrims stare at shrines—which is to say hungrily and with a certain disappointment that it looks smaller than the stories. A woman in the lead wore a hearth-sigil burned into a wooden pendant, the kind Wren and I had seen outlawed in half the villages between here and Ashmarket. She held it up when I approached, not as a threat but as a credential, the way you'd show a scar to prove you'd survived something.

"We heard," she called across the water. "In the lowlands they're saying the valley opened. They're saying the crown fell and the frost broke and there's a guild in Hollowmere that remembers the old craft." She swallowed. "They're saying someone teaches."

I looked back at the guild house. Nine aging members, a leaking roof, a ledger full of dead names, a kitchen that smelled permanently of Maren's tea and Tam's burnt bread. Somewhere behind those walls was a founder's seal-script carved into a beam that I alone could read, and a door that hadn't admitted a stranger in living memory.

"Come across," I heard myself say. "Mind the third step. It's been meaning to give out for years and I haven't gotten to it."

The woman laughed—a startled, cracked thing, like she'd forgotten the shape of it—and led her companions over the ford. When she reached me she gripped my hand with both of hers and said, "My grandmother taught me warding off a stolen book, whisper-quiet, before I was ten. When the Graycloaks came she burned the book and never spoke of it again. She died thinking the craft would die with her generation." Her eyes were wet and furious. "Tell me she was wrong."

"She was wrong," I said. And then, because I am apparently incapable of letting a moment stand at its proper size: "Also your grandmother sounds like she was better at this than half our current membership. You'll fit right in."

* * *

They did not stop coming.

By mid-spring there were fourteen strangers sleeping in the guild house, which meant beds in the scriptorium and bedrolls in the hall and a rotating argument about whose turn it was to chop wood. By late spring there were thirty-one, and Maren had stopped lecturing and started organizing, because if there is one force in nature stronger than an old woman's suspicion it is an old woman presented with a logistics problem. She had a roster. She had a chore wheel. She had *opinions* about the hearth-shrine the lowland folk built in the east courtyard, until she saw them light it at dusk, thirty voices murmuring the old words badly, and then she stood in the doorway with her tea going cold and cried quietly into her sleeve where she thought no one could see.

I saw. I said nothing. Some ledgers are better left unopened.

Wren taught mornings when his strength allowed—sigil theory, the grammar of binding, the history the crown had spent decades burning—and I taught afternoons, which mostly consisted of me demonstrating a ward and then saying "now you try" and then spending an hour putting out small fires. My wards still bloomed too strong, too fast; apparently that doesn't fix itself just because you've saved a kingdom. But I was learning to teach around it, to show students the shape of the mistake instead of hiding it, and I found—I keep finding this, it keeps surprising me—that my worst flaw made me a better teacher than a flawless caster would have been. People trust you faster when you've set your own sleeve on fire in front of them.

Sera wrote twice a month from the capital, where she was rebuilding the wardens into something that guarded rather than harvested. Her letters were terse, dry, and secretly hilarious, which I suspect is how she'd always been and had simply never been allowed to admit. The last one ended: *The king insists on visiting the provinces himself. Mortal kings apparently need to be seen. He asked after you specifically. I told him you were busy with a roof.* We were. The roof remained undefeated.

And the king—Aldric, though he flinched at the name for weeks, wearing it like borrowed clothing—sent word through Sera that the crown's claim over sigil-craft was formally abolished, that the guilds' charters were restored, and that if Hollowmere wished to remain hidden, the crown would honor it forever.

I wrote back myself, one line: *We're done being hidden. Come see us in summer. Bring nothing. We'll feed you anyway.*

Maren nearly fainted. Then she started planning menus.

* * *

But the chapter I actually sat down to write, so to speak, was the ledger.

You should understand what the ledger is. It sits in the scriptorium on a stand older than the building, a book bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth, and for three hundred years its purpose has been elegy. Every member who died entered it in careful ink: name, years, the craft they loved best. Nine names a century, sometimes fewer. A book of endings, kept by people who believed they were the last page.

The founder's name wasn't in it. That had always struck me as strange, even before I knew anything—every guild remembers its founders, it's practically the definition—but the oldest entries simply began, as if the first members had sprung fully formed from the valley fog. Now I understood why. Elara Ashgrove had never been *in* the guild. She had sealed herself into the valley's founding, poured her name into the binding along with her blood, and the ledger—the honest, stubborn object—had refused to record a person who wasn't really there. Who had been spent, not buried. A key doesn't get an obituary.

Until now.

I waited for a quiet evening, which in a house of forty people means I waited for everyone else to be asleep, and I carried the ledger to the long table and lit two lamps because this deserved light, and I turned to the first blank page after three hundred years of deaths.

I wrote her name first. *Elara Ashgrove, founder. First of the guild. Gave everything and was given nothing back, until her heir got stubborn about it.*

Then, beneath it, in the same ink, side by side the way the map had shown them, the way the underways carvings had shown them, the way it had apparently always been meant to be written:

*Ada Ashgrove, twenty-second of her line. Last by inheritance. Not last by choice.*

I sat back and looked at the two names and felt—honestly?—nothing like triumph. What I felt was the vertigo of a debt finally paid, the specific lightness of setting down something you've carried so long you'd mistaken it for your own skeleton. For months I had been the last member, the final entry, the period at the end of a very long sentence. And now the sentence was going to keep going, and it wouldn't be my sentence anymore, and I discovered that this terrified me more than the Graycloaks ever had.

Because duty, I learned, is easy. Duty tells you exactly what you are: the one who stays, who repairs, who copies names. Belonging is harder. Belonging means the door stays open and you don't get to control who walks through it or what they'll make of the place once they're inside. Inheritance is a wall. Belonging is a bridge, and bridges go both ways.

I thought about closing the ledger. Putting it back on its stand, safe, finished, a museum piece.

Instead I left it open on the table.

Let the morning shift find it. Let Tam spill tea near it and panic. Let the lowland woman with her grandmother's burned-out sigil read those two names and understand that the book was waiting for hers. A closed ledger is a grave. An open one is a door, and I had promised somebody a door.

* * *

Wren found me there near dawn, because of course he did. He has this infuriating habit of knowing where I am, which he claims is scribe training—cataloguing people the way he catalogued the king's correspondence—and which I claim is haunting, and neither of us has ever pressed the point because the answer might require conversation, and we are both champions at deferring conversations.

He stood in the doorway in the gray light, thinner than he'd been in autumn, the sigil-scar on his ribs faded now to a pale thread, and he looked at the open ledger for a long time.

"You stayed up all night," he said.

"I was writing two names."

"I can count." He came and sat across from me, and the table between us held the lamps and the book and three hundred years of quiet. "Elara first. Then you."

"That's the order of things."

"Mm." He turned the book slightly toward himself, studying my handwriting the way he studied everything, like a man checking a translation against a memory. "You know what I did the night before I stole the map?"

"You've told me. You sharpened every quill in the royal scriptorium and alphabetized a decade of misfiled petitions."

"I did. Because I thought it was the last night of my life, and I wanted the archive tidy for whoever came after." He smiled, that crooked tired smile that had survived a hollowing wound and a winter and my cooking. "This feels like that. Except backwards. Tidying up for whoever comes *first*."

"The first of many," I said. "Statistically some of them will be terrible. One of them will definitely burn down the kitchen."

"Tam already did that twice this spring."

"Tam is practice."

He laughed, and I laughed, and then we sat there not laughing for a while, in the way of two people who have run out of emergencies and don't quite know what to do with an ordinary morning. That was the strangest part of peace, I decided. Not the absence of fear. The presence of time.

"Wren," I said eventually, because the deferred conversation couldn't defer forever, "the road goes both ways now. Ashmarket. The capital. Vess is apparently telling anyone who'll listen that she met the Founder's Key and lived, which is the most flattering lie anyone's ever told about me. You could go anywhere. Be anyone. The king owes you a life—he said so, in writing, with seals on it. You could have quarters in the palace. A scriptorium of your own. A title."

He was quiet for long enough that I watched the sun come fully up through the window behind him.

"I had quarters in the palace," he said. "I had a title. I had a king who needed me and a kingdom that fed me and I have never in my life been so alone as I was in that beautiful preserved room with my own handwriting framed on the walls like a saint's relics." He looked at the ledger. "Here, I copy names into a book and chop wood on Thursdays and Maren yells at me about my tea-steeping technique, and I have not been alone once. Do the arithmetic, Ada. I'm a scribe. I'm good at arithmetic."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the most honest answer I'm capable of." He paused, and then, because oblique confession had worked so well for him at the worst possible time, he tried it again at the best one: "Besides. Someone has to stay and make sure you don't ward the front gate so strongly the students can't get in. Your wards bloom, remember. Someone responsible ought to be nearby with a bucket."

"That's your reason? Bucket duty?"

"My reasons are extensive," he said, "and catalogued, and available upon request, and the request form is on the table next to the ledger, and the form says—" he reached over and tapped the open page, right beside my name, beside his own handwriting that had appeared there at some point in the night without my noticing, *Wren, scribe, keeper of the archive, staying* —"—this."

I looked at it for a long time. Two names in my hand. One in his. Three hundred years of endings above, and below, the beginning of whatever this was.

"You could have told me you were adding yourself," I managed. "I lived here first."

"You left the book open," he said. "That's an invitation. I'm told you're learning what those mean."

* * *

Summer came the way spring had—gradually, then all at once. The king arrived with no retinue worth the name, a big weathered man who wept at the ford and pretended he hadn't, and Maren fed him until he begged mercy. Sera came with him and immediately organized our woodpile by length, grain, and moral character. Vess sent a crate of paper and ink with a note reading *no charge, consider us even, tell no one I was generous, it will ruin me*. Travelers kept coming—some gifted, some merely hungry for a place with a lit window—and the guild house grew a new wing, and the new wing grew a waiting list, and the waiting list grew Maren's second gray hair, which she displayed to visitors like a war wound.

On the first evening of high summer, I walked out past the east courtyard where the hearth-shrine burned and forty voices stumbled through the old words, and I climbed to the ridge—not beyond it, just to it, the border that had been the edge of my world—and I sat on the warm stone and looked back at the valley.

Fog pooled in the lowest hollows, thin as spun sugar, and even it would burn off by noon. Below, the guild house windows were gold with lamplight, every one of them, doors propped wide against the heat. I could hear the shrine-voices, off-key and earnest. I could smell bread. Somewhere in that tangle of light and sound was a ledger lying open on a table, patient as soil, waiting for the next name.

I had spent twenty-two years certain I'd been born on the wrong side of the map. Standing there, I finally understood the error in the premise. There was no wrong side. There was only the side you left and the side you returned to, and the road between them, and the choice—made daily, made freely, made mine—of which direction to walk it.

Home wasn't the valley. It never had been. Home was the open door, and the hands that kept it open, and the names, still being written.

Behind me, footsteps on the ridge path. Wren, carrying two cups of Maren's terrible tea because he was sentimental like that.

"They're asking for you," he said. "Evening lesson. Someone's attempting a fireward and Tam is supervising, which as we've established is a fire hazard."

"Coming." I took the tea. I let him pull me up. And I turned my back on the view—not hard, not anymore, because I knew it would still be there tomorrow, unfogged and unhidden and mine—to walk back down toward the light and the noise and the open door, where the ledger lay waiting, and the ink was not yet dry.
