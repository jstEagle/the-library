## 1. Small Charms

The city of Vell kept its secrets the way a drunkard keeps his coins — badly, and only until he needed them.

Marguerite Montrose had learned that in his first week, five years ago, when he'd arrived at the Southgate with no name anyone could verify, no trade anyone could question, and a face that opened doors the way rain opens shutters. He had learned it again every night since. Vell was a city of forty thousand griefs, and grief was the one commodity its people would pay to be rid of.

He worked the Lamplight Quarter on nights when the fog came off the canal thick enough to hold its shape. The fog mattered. Not for hiding — Marguerite had never needed hiding; he had a way of standing in a doorway that made him look like he belonged there more than the door did — but because fog muffled sound, softened lamplight into haloes, and made the whole city feel like something remembered rather than something lived in. A city half-remembered was a city easy to steal from.

His first stop of the night was the widow Anselm's house on Candlemaker's Row, though she didn't know yet that it would be his first stop. He knew it because he'd been watching her for six days, and six days was his rule: never less than six days of watching before you took anything. Six days told you where a person kept their sorrow, how heavy it sat on them, whether they'd miss it or whether they'd thank you for carrying it away.

Widow Anselm — Tomasine, though nobody in Vell used her Christian name anymore; widowhood had eaten it — kept her sorrow in a blue glass jar on the mantel above her hearth. Not literally. The jar held dried lavender and three copper buttons and a twist of wedding ribbon gone grey with age. But sorrow needs a vessel, and hers was that jar, and every evening at dusk she took it down and held it in both hands the way other women held sleeping cats, and her mouth did something complicated, and then she put it back.

Six days of that was enough. On the seventh night, Marguerite went to work.

* * *

The trick of taking a memory is not what people imagine. There is no pain, no tearing, no dramatic gasp. The Spire's artificers — if any of them still lived, which the Crown insisted none did — would have told you it was closer to lifting a ring off a sleeping hand. The finger doesn't wake. The hand doesn't close. And afterward there is a pale band of skin where the ring sat, invisible to everyone except the person who knows to look for it.

Marguerite looked like a debt collector, which was funny, because he collected nothing but what people gave him freely. He knocked on Widow Anselm's door at the hour when candles gutter low and pride goes with them, wearing his second-best coat and an expression of professional sympathy, and he said, "Madam, I'm sorry to call so late. I represent the Guild of Small Mercies."

There was no such guild. There had been once, perhaps, before the Purge, before the wards went up and magic was declared a rumor spread by traitors and madmen. Now there was only Marguerite, and men like him — though there were very few men like him, and he intended to keep it that way.

"Small mercies," she repeated. Her voice had the flatness of someone who has been disappointed by institutions.

"We provide consolations," Marguerite said. "For widows. For the bereaved. It's a charitable office. We've helped four households on this street already." This was true in the sense that all his best lies were true; he had indeed visited four households on Candlemaker's Row, and sold each of them a small glass vial of "blessed oil" that was really canal water and rosemary, and each of them had slept better for believing it. The lie was only in the word *helped*, and lies built on true bones stood up straightest.

She let him in because her fire was dying and his coat was good and loneliness is a door that opens inward.

He sat where she indicated, accepted tea he didn't drink — never drink anything in a mark's house; it slows the hands and warms the face — and let her talk. That was most of the work, honestly. People want to talk. Forty thousand griefs in Vell, and almost nobody listening. She talked about Tomas Anselm, dead eleven years now, drowned in the canal with a belly full of gin and someone else's quarrel, and how the magistrates called it misadventure, and how his mother had spat at her funeral and how the neighbors had stopped meeting her eye after the third year, as if grief were contagious past a certain date.

"He was a fool," she said, "but he was my fool."

And there it was. Marguerite felt the memory before he saw it — you feel them the way you feel heat from an oven door, a warmth behind the eyes — the wedding, the good day, the day before the drowning, the day she was young and he was sober and the whole street had thrown rice and someone had played a fiddle badly and she had laughed until her ribs ached. It sat in her like a coal banked under ash. Twenty-two years married and the only unspoiled thing left in the whole ruin of it was one afternoon.

"I can offer you a charm," Marguerite said gently. "A luck-charm. Blessed against further misfortune. Against drowning." He let the last word sit between them like a coin on a table.

Her eyes came up. "Against drowning," she said.

"The canal takes what it takes," Marguerite said, with the mild fatalism of a man quoting scripture. "But a proper charm turns aside what's aimed at you. Your remaining years, madam — they needn't go the way your first ones did."

He watched her do the arithmetic that poor women do, weighing coin against hope, and he hated himself with a clean and practiced hatred, the way a man hates a scar, and he did not stop.

"It costs," she said.

"A trifle. What you can spare."

What she could spare was nine coppers and a silver button, and she counted them out of a cracked teapot with the shame of the poor who are always being asked to prove their poverty, and Marguerite took them with a bow, and then, while she turned to fetch the ribbon she wanted tied around the charm — her ribbon, the wedding ribbon, she wanted luck tied in something of Tomas's — he leaned forward as if to warm his hands at her hearth, and he took the memory.

It was exactly as gentle as lifting a ring. Her back stayed turned. Her shoulders never tensed. Somewhere behind her eyes a pale band of skin formed where a bright afternoon had been, and she carried on talking about ribbon, and she did not know, and she would never know, that the reason she could no longer quite picture Tomas's face on his wedding day — the reason it would slide away from her whenever she reached for it, like a fish in dark water — was that a stranger in a good coat had just put it in his pocket.

She turned back with the ribbon. He tied it around a vial of canal water and rosemary, murmured something in a language that didn't exist anymore, and told her to hang it above the door where the fog couldn't reach it.

"You'll sleep well tonight," he said, and meant it, because the memory he'd taken was the one that woke her.

At the door she pressed his hand. "Bless you," she said. "I feel lighter already."

That was the worst part. Not the theft. The gratitude.

* * *

He walked home through the fog with nine coppers, a silver button, and a wedding in his head that belonged to a woman named Tomasine Anselm, née Vasseur, who had laughed until her ribs ached while a man murdered a fiddle and rice fell like weather.

This was the part of the night he hated, and the part he lived for, and he had long ago stopped trying to reconcile the two. In the privacy of his skull, where no magistrate could reach and no priest could follow, he unwrapped the stolen thing and looked at it. That was the rule beneath all his rules, the one he'd carved first and deepest: *never steal anything you can't sell twice.* A memory taken and hoarded was a corpse in the walls — it soured, it stank, it drew attention. A memory taken and sold was inventory. You could sell a woman her own wedding back as a dream-charm, a sleep-charm, a charm against nightmares, and she'd pay gladly, because the dream would feel like a gift rather than a loss, and she'd never know the gift had been picked from her own pocket.

But there was a second rule, older, unwritten, that he broke every single time and would not stop breaking: *look at what you take.* Really look. Because the alternative was to become a man who carried forty griefs and felt none of them, and Marguerite had met men like that, in the years he couldn't remember, and he would rather hang.

So he looked. He stood on the bridge over the canal — the same canal, God help him, that had taken Tomas Anselm — and he let the memory play out in full, generous, guilty detail. The fiddle. The rice. Tomasine at nineteen with flowers going brown in her hair because the day had been too hot and she hadn't cared. Tomas lifting her over the threshold of a house they'd rent for thirty-one years and lose to a bottle. The look on his face. God, the look on his face — a fool entirely, gloriously, permanently in love, certain as sunrise.

Marguerite stood on the bridge until the fog beaded in his hair, holding another man's joy like a hot coal passed hand to hand, and he thought, as he always thought: *this one I'll sell carefully. This one I'll sell to her gently. Some night when the dreams are bad, I'll come back with it dressed as mercy, and she'll buy her own afternoon back without knowing, and she'll sleep, and it will almost make up for—*

Almost. Never quite. That was the shape of his penance: he got to keep the guilt and give away the absolution.

He was halfway across the bridge when the first wrongness came.

It was small. That was what unsettled him — how small it was. A lamp on the far bank guttered, steadied, guttered again, and its flame burned a color he had no name for, a greenish white like foxfire, like the light off deep ice. Then it righted itself and burned ordinary yellow, and Marguerite told himself it was the fog playing tricks, fog and fatigue and a head full of somebody else's happiness.

But as he walked on toward his rooms above the chandler's shop, he noticed other things. A cat sitting perfectly still in the middle of an empty street, staring straight up at the sky with its ears flat. A church bell that rang the hour and then, faintly, a second later, rang it again, as if uncertain. And overhead, through a tear in the fog, stars — and one of them, just for a moment, seemed to him to flicker out and flicker back, like a lamp deciding whether to stay lit.

Marguerite Montrose, who had survived five years in Vell by noticing things, stood in the middle of Chandler's Lane and noticed that the city felt wrong the way a room feels wrong when someone has moved a chair in the dark.

And underneath the wrongness, deeper, in a place in his mind he had never had occasion to visit because he had never known it was there, something stirred. Something turned over. Like a sleeper hearing a bell.

He stood very still in the fog, and listened to his own head the way you listen to a house at night, and for the first time in five years of careful, profitable, guilty living, Marguerite Montrose was afraid of what might be living in the walls of himself.

Then the feeling passed, the way such feelings do, and he laughed at himself — softly, so the fog wouldn't carry it — and climbed the stairs to his rooms, and slept, and dreamed of a tower he had never seen, white against a sky with no stars in it, and a child's hand, small and cold, slipping out of his.

He woke before dawn with the taste of snow in his mouth, though it was high summer, and outside his window the fog over Vell was burning off, and the bells were ringing wrong.
