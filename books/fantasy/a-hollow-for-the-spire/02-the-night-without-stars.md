## 2. The Night Without Stars

The market at Vell kept its own hours, and its own weather. By the time the true night came down over the rooftops, the Night Market had already lit itself — a hundred paper lanterns strung along the Butcher's Row awnings, tallow candles guttering in jars of salted oil, and above it all the great iron chandeliers of the old customs house swinging on their chains like slow bronze moons. Marguerite Montrose had worked this market for five years, and he had learned to read it the way sailors read the sea: by smell, by sound, by the particular quality of greed in the air.

Tonight the air smelled of roasting chestnuts and wet wool and, underneath it, faintly, of ozone — the sharp clean bite that meant someone nearby was working magic they shouldn't have.

He moved through the crowd with his hood back and his hands loose at his sides, wearing the face he wore everywhere: pleasant, forgettable, a little tired around the eyes. A man you'd trust with your purse and never think to count what else he'd taken. The rule lived in him like a second heartbeat, learned early and paid for dearly: *never steal anything you can't sell twice.* A memory lifted once was a coin spent. A memory sold as a charm, then sold again as its absence — *I can restore what was taken from you, madam, for a modest fee* — that was a living.

He paused at a stall where an old woman sold candied violets under a banner of faded blue silk, and let his fingers brush the rim of her basket as he passed. Just a touch. Just long enough.

Her evening came to him whole: a kitchen with copper pots, a man's laugh, rain on a window, the specific weight of a wedding ring turning on a finger during the first nervous year. He folded it small, the way he'd taught himself, the way no one had ever taught him because there had been no one — or so he believed, on nights when he thought about it at all, which he tried not to — and pressed it into a scrap of waxed paper with a pinch of dried lavender.

"Charm against sorrow," he said, when she looked up. "Three coppers."

She bought it, of course. They always did. She'd wear it at her throat until the luck ran thin, and in a month or two he'd find her again at some quieter stall and sell her back her own wedding, and she would weep with gratitude and never know she had wept twice for the same afternoon. He told himself the guilt was a kind of tax, paid in full each night, and that taxes were honest.

The chandeliers swung. The lanterns burned. Somewhere a fiddler tuned up for the dancing that would start after moonrise, if there was a moonrise, which there always was, which there had always been, every night of Marguerite's five remembered years.

That was the thing about five years. It was enough time to build a life. It was not quite enough time to build one so solid that a man stopped noticing the mortar was strange.

* * *

It happened at the hour the fiddler began to play.

Marguerite was leaning against the customs house wall with a cup of mulled wine he had no intention of finishing, watching a pickpocket younger than he'd ever been work the crowd with clumsy, hungry hands. He was composing, idly, the little speech he'd give the boy later — *you lift the purse, but you leave the hand feeling lighter; that's how they remember you* — when the music stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped. As though cut with shears.

Every head in the market turned upward at once, the way birds turn, the way sheep turn, some animal consensus older than any of them. Marguerite looked up too, wine slopping cold over his knuckles, and saw that the sky was going out.

It did not cloud over. Clouds have edges, texture, movement. This was subtraction. The stars vanished first — not dimming, not occluded, simply *gone*, each one snuffed like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the darkness where they had been was somehow darker than the dark around it, holes punched through the night into something behind the night. Then the moon. He watched it happen. He watched the moon's pale face erode from the east inward, not shadowed but *unmade*, until there was nothing above the city of Vell but black, absolute and close, a lid rather than a sky.

The lanterns guttered all together, though there was no wind.

And then came the sound.

Later, people in Vell would describe it a hundred ways — a crack of ice on a frozen river, a scream made of metal, the world's spine breaking — but Marguerite knew what it was within the first half-second, knew it in his teeth and the roots of his hair, the way you know a voice even before you place the face. It was a bell. An enormous bell, struck once, at the far edge of hearing and the far edge of the country, and instead of ringing it *broke*. The tone climbed and climbed past what a bell should bear and then shattered into a thousand discordant shards that fell across the land like glass rain, and Marguerite felt each shard land somewhere inside him, precise as needles finding the marks on a chart.

The border wards. Every ward, all at once, along the whole circumference of the nation. Falling.

For three heartbeats the market held perfectly still — a thousand people listening to the silence where the wards' low hum had lived beneath everything, unnoticed as blood in the veins, for longer than anyone could remember. Then the hum was gone, and everyone felt its absence at once, the way a room feels the moment a clock stops, and the Night Market of Vell came apart like a knot pulled loose.

The screaming started small — a child, then a woman, then the fiddler, who dropped his instrument and stood clutching his head as though the silence were louder than the music had been. Lanterns went over. The candied-violet woman was on her knees, praying aloud to saints whose names hadn't been spoken in this country for a generation, names that had been *discouraged*, names that belonged to the time before the Spire taught everyone that magic was a rumor poor people told. A merchant began beating his own stall to splinters with a tent-pole, methodically, weeping, and no one stopped him because no one could think of a reason why not.

Marguerite did none of these things.

Marguerite stood with his back against the customs house wall and both hands pressed flat over his ears, and felt something open inside his skull.

* * *

It is difficult to describe a door opening in a place where you had never noticed there was a door. There was no pain. That was the worst of it — no pain, only a sensation of *release*, of pressure held so long it had become indistinguishable from the shape of his own mind suddenly let go, like a fist unclenching after years, like a held breath finally leaving a body he'd forgotten was holding it.

A room unlocked inside him.

He had no other word for it. Not a memory — memories arrive with their own furniture, their own light. This was the antechamber to a memory, the sealed vestibule, and through its newly cracked door he caught smells without images: chalk dust and hot metal and the green tang of preserved things. He heard, without hearing, a rhythm of footsteps on stone stairs, many floors of them, descending. And underneath it all, faint as a watermark, a voice saying words he could not parse but whose *cadence* he knew, whose patient, clipped, kindly cadence he knew the way you know a lullaby sung before you could speak —

— and terror went through him, yes, cold and total, but threaded through the terror, unforgivably, unmistakably, was recognition.

*Oh,* said something deep in him, in a voice that was his and yet older than his five years, older than the name Marguerite Montrose, which he had chosen himself off a gravestone in the Salt Quarter because it sounded like nobody. *Oh. It's still there. I wondered.*

*I?* Who was *I*?

The market chaos moved around him like weather. Someone knocked his shoulder hard enough to spill the wine entirely; he didn't feel it. Above, where stars should be wheeling over Vell, the blackness seemed to press lower, and he understood — with the same terrible fluency, the knowledge arriving pre-installed, native-tongued — that the wards had not merely failed. Wards did not fail. Wards were *maintained*. Someone had stopped maintaining them, or someone had broken them deliberately, and either way the country's long pretending was over, and the magic that had been pressed out beyond the borders for a generation was going to come home now like floodwater finding a dry valley.

And he — whatever he was, whoever had built the walls of the locked room in his head — he had been part of the architecture. He knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The bell-shards landing on the chart-marks inside him: those marks were *his*. He had put them there. Or they had been put there in him, using instruments he understood intimately, and he could not tell which horror was worse.

Around him, Vell was remembering how to be afraid of the dark. Around him, people who had spent their lives being told magic was a fairy tale were discovering that the fairy tale had been waiting patiently outside the walls the whole time, and that the walls were down. A lamplighter stood in the middle of Butcher's Row with his pole raised, lighting nothing, staring up at the starless black and whispering, "It's coming back, it's coming back," over and over, in the tone of a man greeting either a god or a debt collector.

Marguerite pushed off the wall and walked — steadily, he was proud of how steadily — toward the edge of the square, away from the fires starting in the overturned stalls, away from the crowd pressing toward the cathedral steps where the priests were emerging with torches and faces like men asked to explain the tide to fish.

At the mouth of Tanner's Lane he stopped. Behind him, the Night Market burned and sang and prayed. Ahead, the lane ran narrow and black toward the river and the old gatehouse district, where the city's own minor wards — the ones on the gates, the bridges, the tax-houses — must be failing too, must be falling silent one by one like a row of candles.

In the locked room behind the cracked door, the footsteps on the stairs were still descending. Patient. Unhurried. Whoever walked them had all the time in the world, and had known, all along, that the door would open eventually. Had perhaps *designed* it to open tonight.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to a stranger whose file he had once read.

"Get off the streets," a watchman was bellowing somewhere, uselessly, wonderfully. "Curfew! Curfew by order of the Council!"

Marguerite did not go home. Home was a rented room above a dye-shop with a false floorboard and forty-one stolen charms wrapped in oilcloth, and it was the only true thing he had, and he found he could not make his feet carry him toward it. Because the door in his head was open a crack, and through the crack came the smell of chalk dust and hot metal, and beneath the fear, beneath the vertigo, something in him was *leaning toward it* — listening for the descending footsteps the way you listen for a name called in a crowd that might be yours.

He turned instead toward the river gate. Toward the sound, just beginning, of shouting at the walls — the disciplined, frightened shouting of soldiers, of ward-keepers, of people whose job it was to stand between a city and the dark.

He told himself it was instinct. A thief runs toward exits; gates are exits.

But even as he thought it, he knew it was a lie, and he knew the lie was not his own construction either. Something in him wanted the gate. Something in him had always known the gate mattered.

Above the drowned stars, the black sky pressed down like a held breath, and in the lane below the gatehouse, torchlight bloomed around a knot of grey-uniformed figures dragging something — someone — out of the rubble of the collapsed watchtower, and Marguerite, moving toward them through the dark with his heart hammering a rhythm he almost recognized, did not yet know that his five years were about to end.

He only knew that the room in his head had opened its door.

And that someone was coming up the stairs to meet him.
