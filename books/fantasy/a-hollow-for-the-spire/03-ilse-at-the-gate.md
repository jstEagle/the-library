## 3. Ilse at the Gate

The bell broke at dusk.

Ilse Varn was on the wall when it happened, which was the only reason she survived the first hour. She had been counting ward-stones — a punishment detail, a disgrace dressed up as duty, three hundred and eleven stones along the western curtain of Vell's gatehouse, each one to be checked for hairline fractures and logged in her own cramped hand. She had counted them every evening for two years. It was work for a woman who had once kept the entire northern circuit of wards alive through the Long Frost, and who now checked stones because no one trusted her with anything that could break.

Then the bell broke. Not rang — *broke*. A sound like a cathedral bell struck too hard from inside, a note that went wrong halfway through its own voice, and every ward-stone on the wall sang back at once, a chorus of hairline fractures opening into honest ruin.

Ilse was thrown against the parapet. The stones under her palms went hot, then cold, then simply dead, like coins pulled from a drowned man's pocket. Above her the sky — which had been the flat grey nothing it had been all her life, the starless lid the Spire had bolted over the country a generation ago — shivered. And in the shiver she saw, for half a heartbeat, what the sky used to hold. Lights. Cold white lights scattered like salt. She had never seen stars. She knew them anyway, the way you know a face from a description.

Then the dark closed again, thicker than before, and the gatehouse came down.

* * *

She did not remember deciding to go toward the collapse. That was the thing about the old training; it lived below thought. She was moving before the dust finished falling, boots finding the rubble-rhythm, one hand out, breath held against the chalk-and-iron smell of broken wards. Broken wards smelled like that — like a smithy drowned in milk. She had smelled it once before, years ago, on the worst night of her life, and her body remembered it before her mind could stop it.

The gatehouse had folded inward, the old tower slumping onto the barbican like a drunk against a doorpost. Wards that should have caught the fall — cushioning glyphs, load-sharing sigils, a dozen small mercies woven into the masonry by keepers long dead — had not fired. Had not even tried. They were empty. Every ward in Vell was empty, Ilse realized, standing in the ruin with her hands open at her sides like a woman who has forgotten what hands are for. For thirty years the city's magic had been siphoned north to feed the border seals, and now the seals were gone, and the siphons ran backward, and there was nothing in the pipes at all.

Except—

There. Under a slab of cornice the size of a coffin-lid, a hand. Not moving. Fingers pale as candle-wax, curled loosely around something small and dark that glinted when the lantern-light found it.

Ilse got her shoulder under the slab and lifted with her legs and her anger, which had always been the strongest part of her. The stone ground aside. The man beneath it was maybe thirty, thin in the way of people who eat last, dressed in a thief's drab browns gone grey with dust. Blood had painted a careful line down his temple, precise as a scribe's rule. His chest moved. Barely. Shallow, uneven, the breathing of a man whose body was arguing about whether to continue.

She knelt in the rubble and put two fingers to his throat and felt his pulse stutter like a moth against glass.

And then he opened his eyes.

They were grey. Not the grey of weather or iron but the grey of a room seen through frosted glass — a grey with something behind it, something sealed, something that looked back at her from a great depth and recognized her before she recognized it.

Her whole body went cold.

She knew that face.

Not from the wall. Not from the market, or the muster rolls, or any of the ten thousand faces a ward-keeper learns to file without seeing. She knew it from paper. From a folder bound in grey leather with the Spire's seal stamped in wax the color of dried blood, a folder she had carried down seven flights of stairs into the furnace-room of the Hall of Records with orders to burn it unread, and had read, because Ilse Varn had been many things by then but obedient was not one of them, and because the folder had been thinner than the others and she had wanted to know why.

It had held eleven files. Eleven names, eleven faces, eleven men and women who had walked into the Spire's inner tower as artificers and clerks and come out — or not come out — as something else. The eleventh face had been this one, younger by some years, cleaner, wearing the white coat of a senior artificer, looking at the camera of whatever device the Spire used with an expression she had never been able to forget: not fear, not defiance. *Recognition.* As if he already knew what they were going to do to him and had agreed to it anyway.

Under the photograph, in the tight clerical hand she had learned to read in the dark: **VESSEL ELEVEN. SEAL INTEGRITY: TOTAL. DO NOT APPROACH. DO NOT AWAKEN. REPORT SIGHTING TO THE SPIRE DIRECTLY.**

She had burned ten files that night. She had kept the eleventh inside her coat, against her heart, where it had grown warm and soft with her sweat until the ink began to ghost through the pages, until she finally buried it in a lead box under the floor of her mother's house in the Tanner's Quarter, where it might still be sitting if the Tanner's Quarter still existed.

That had been six years ago. Two months later she had refused an order herself — a real order, a ward-order, the kind that gets people killed — and the court-martial had taken her rank, her post, and her name from the rolls, and given her instead the wall, and the stones, and the evenings.

Now the eleventh vessel lay bleeding in the ruins of her gatehouse, and his eyes were open, and behind the frost on them something was stirring.

"Hey," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she meant. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His lips moved. No sound. His hand tightened around the small dark thing he'd been holding — a charm, she saw now, a cheap tin luck-charm of the kind sold at every market stall in Vell, the kind that were supposed to be empty, supposed to be nothing but tin and superstition. This one was glowing faintly, a pulse of light like a slow heartbeat, and Ilse felt the hair rise on her arms because charms were not supposed to glow. Charms had not been able to glow for thirty years.

Magic was coming back. Magic was coming back, and this man was full of it, sealed to the brim, and every order she had ever sworn to — even the ones they'd stripped from her — said the same thing now: report the sighting. Report it directly. Do not approach. Do not awaken.

Boots on the rubble behind her. Lantern-light swinging. Voices calling the tally of the dead and the dug-out.

Ilse made her choice the way she made most of them: fast, angry, and certain to cost her.

* * *

Sergeant Brakke found her twenty minutes later, kneeling in the wreckage with a stranger's head cradled in her lap, pressing a strip of her own shirt against his temple.

"Varn." He said her name the way he always did, like a chore. "Who've you got?"

"Digger," she said. "From the Lower Ward crews. He was checking the drain-gates when the tower came down."

Brakke swung his lantern lower. Light crawled across the stranger's face. Ilse kept her body loose and her eyes bored and her heart hammering against the place where a grey leather folder had once sat.

"Doesn't look like a digger," Brakke said.

"He's got the mud on him. Check his knees." Which was true, because she had dragged him through the flooded barbican channel while the lanterns were still swinging elsewhere, and mud was the one lie that told itself. "He'll live. Cracked head, cracked ribs. I'm taking him to the undercroft infirmary."

"You're a stone-counter, Varn, not a—"

"I was a keeper for nine years," she said, and let the old anger into her voice, because Brakke flinched from her anger the way horses flinch from fire, and flinching men agree to things. "I know triage better than anyone on this wall tonight, and you need every pair of hands that isn't pulling bodies. Unless you want to log that you argued with me over a dying man while your section bled."

Brakke looked at her. Looked at the stranger. Somewhere above them another piece of the gatehouse shifted and groaned, and everyone within fifty feet ducked, and when the dust settled Brakke had already lost interest in anything that wasn't structural.

"Log him," he said. "Name?"

"Didn't give one. Out of his head." She paused, and the pause was the riskiest thing she'd done all night. "Might not wake up. Head wounds like this—" She shook her head, letting the sentence die the way soldiers let sentences die, meaning *don't get attached.*

"Fine. Unnamed male, gatehouse collapse. Get him out of my sight."

She carried him herself. He weighed less than he should have, as though some part of him had already been taken away and never returned. In the shadow of the barbican arch his hand found her wrist — weakly, blindly, the grip of a drowning man — and his lips moved again, and this time sound came out, one word, barely voiced:

"...wrong..."

"Yes," Ilse said, to him, to the night, to the broken bell-note still hanging in the air like a smell. "Everything is. Hold on anyway."

* * *

The undercroft infirmary had been a wine cellar before the Spire decided cities needed fewer cellars and more surveillance posts. Now it was forty pallets and a brazier and Sister Annet, who was seventy and deaf in one ear and the only person in Vell's garrison Ilse trusted further than she could throw.

Annet looked at the stranger, then at Ilse, then at the stranger again, with the one good eye doing all the work.

"Not a digger," she said.

"No," Ilse said.

"Trouble?"

"The worst kind." Ilse set him down on the pallet farthest from the door, in the corner where the lamplight didn't reach well. "The kind that comes with paperwork."

Annet grunted, which from her was a treaty. She probed the scalp wound with fingers like tree roots, clucked over the ribs, held a cup of willow-bark and something stronger to his lips until he swallowed. Through all of it he drifted, surfacing and sinking, and twice he said words that made no sense and once he said a word that made terrible sense:

"—the children—"

Ilse went still. "What children?"

But his eyes had rolled shut again, and Annet was looking at her over the brazier with an expression that asked questions Ilse couldn't afford to answer.

Outside, the city was screaming in the way cities scream when the rules stop working. Bells — real bells, unbroken ones — rang the emergency peal over and over. Somewhere a crier was shouting the word *curfew* until his voice shredded. And under it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, Ilse could feel it: the hum. The wards were waking. Empty for thirty years, drained dry to feed the borders, and now the borders were ash and the power was running home through pipes that had forgotten their own shape, and every ward-stone in Vell was beginning, very slowly, to remember how to sing.

She had spent nine years learning to hear those songs. Two years being punished for hearing them wrong, or right, depending on whom you asked. And what she heard now, rising under the city's panic like a note under a note, was wrong in a way that made her teeth ache. The returning magic wasn't settling. It was *searching*. Moving through the walls like water through a flooded mine, looking for low places, looking for vessels, looking for —

Looking for him.

She turned. On the pallet, in the bad light, the stranger's charm — the little tin thing he'd clutched through the collapse — was glowing brighter. And beneath his skin, along the line of his jaw, along the backs of his hands, faint traceries of light were appearing and fading, appearing and fading, like veins of frost forming and melting on a window. The seal. Total integrity, the file had said. But total integrity had been written six years ago, and the bell had broken tonight, and seals were wards, and wards were waking.

He was going to open. Here, in a cellar full of witnesses, under a garrison that would send word to the Spire's remnant before dawn — because the Spire had a remnant now, everyone whispered it, inquisitors who had survived the fall of the towers and gone underground, still filing reports to a headquarters that no longer existed, still hunting.

*Do not approach. Do not awaken. Report directly.*

Ilse sat down on the stool beside his pallet and looked at his face for a long time. Younger in the file. Cleaner. Same grey eyes, same stubborn line of the mouth. In the file, under his photograph, there had been a name, and she had read it, and she had promised herself she would forget it, and she had failed, because she forgot nothing, that had always been her curse and her craft.

"You're going to get me killed," she told him quietly.

His eyelids fluttered. The frost-traceries pulsed.

"And I'm going to do it anyway," she said. "Because somebody owes you. Because I owe you, and I don't even know your name, and that's the stupidest debt I've ever heard of." She laughed, short and bitter, and wiped her hands on her ruined shirt. "Six years I kept that file. Six years I told myself it was evidence. Insurance. Something I'd need someday." She leaned closer. "It was a promise, wasn't it? I just didn't know to whom."

Sister Annet's footsteps crossed the cellar and stopped. "Girl," the old woman said, quietly, for her, "whatever you're going to do, do it before the sergeant comes back. He comes back at the change of watch."

Ilse nodded. Change of watch. One hour, maybe less.

One hour to decide how to lie to an institution she no longer belonged to, about a man she had never met, to protect a secret she had kept so long it had grown roots through her like a ward-stone's anchor-chain.

She looked at the stranger — at the vessel, at the eleventh file, at the man the Spire had sealed and the bell had shaken — and she began, in the low steady voice she had once used to talk panicking apprentices down off walls, to plan.

First: a fever. Fevers explained everything — delirium, the wrong words, the glowing skin if anyone saw it, which no one would, because Annet would hang blankets and Ilse would stand guard and fevers were the one mercy garrisons believed without question.

Second: a story. A digger with no family, no name, no one asking questions. Diggers died anonymous all the time.

Third — and here her hands went cold on her knees, because third was the hard part, third was the part that cracked her duty clean down the middle like the bell —

Third: when he woke, really woke, she had to get him out of Vell. Past the curfew, past the remnant, past whatever was coming down out of the broken north. Because the file had said *do not approach*, and Ilse Varn had built her whole ruined life on approaching exactly the things she was told not to, and because she had seen his eyes open for one second in the rubble, and in that second, behind the frost, she had seen not a weapon, not a vessel, not a report waiting to be filed.

A prisoner. Looking at the first free air he'd seen in six years.

The change-of-watch horn sounded, far above, thin through the stone.

Ilse stood, rolled her shoulders, and went to meet Sergeant Brakke with a fever-story ready and a lie in her mouth like a drawn knife.

Behind her, on the pallet, the man who had been Vessel Eleven murmured something in a language she almost knew, and the frost-veins under his skin pulsed once, twice, like a heart remembering its rhythm — and somewhere across the darkening city, in the flooded undercity where the Spire's remnant kept its candles lit, someone began, at last, to write his description down.
