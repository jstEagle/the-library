## 5. The Remnant

The undercity of Vell had drowned a century ago and no one had mourned it. Its streets ran beneath the living streets like veins beneath skin, and when the Spire fell — when the last of its towers cracked and folded into the winter sky like a kneeling giant — the inquisitors who survived did what inquisitors had always done. They went down. They went where the light did not follow.

Corvane came down by the old cistern stair behind the tanneries, wading through water that reached his thighs and smelled of iron and drowned candles. Above him, through grates and broken vaults, he could hear the city screaming about the stars. The whole sky had gone black at midday, mid-market, and Vell — a city that had spent thirty years perfecting the art of not noticing things — was finally noticing.

He let it scream. Screaming cities were honest cities. Screaming cities told you where the fear lived, and fear was where the unregistered vessels hid.

*Unregistered vessels.* The phrase had been coined by a dead man whose name Corvane had been ordered to forget, and it meant this: a person whose mind held sealed magic, planted there by the Spire's artificers in the years before the Fall, growing in the dark of them like a seed in a locked drawer. The Spire had made hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. Vessels for a power the country had decided, all at once and very politely, had never existed.

And now the wards were down, and the seals were waking, and every vessel in Vell was a lit candle in a house the Spire had once owned.

Corvane intended to blow them all out.

* * *

The chapter house — they still called it that, though it had been a wine cellar once, and before that, if the oldest maps were true, a chapel to a god whose name had been harvested — was lit by tallow and by the pale, sourceless glow of the last working reliquary lamp. Nine inquisitors had made it down. Nine, out of a chapter of sixty. Corvane counted them the way a man counts his fingers after a bad winter, and found the arithmetic wanting, and said nothing about it.

They had gathered around a table made of a door laid across barrels, and on the door were spread the things they had saved: ledgers, seal-irons, a jar of memory-salt gone hard as gravel, and the great register of vessels, its pages swollen with damp, its ink bleeding like wounds.

Sister Anthe was reading names aloud. She was the youngest of them and had the particular ferocity of the young who have lost a father-figure and do not yet know what to do with the anger, and so she read the register as though reciting a war-roll.

"Vessel forty-one, a cooper on Tanner's Row. Vessel forty-four, a midwife, deceased. Vessel forty-seven —" she paused, wet her thumb, turned the page — "forty-seven is unaccounted for. Marked *transferred*."

"Transferred where?" asked Brother Lask, who had a soldier's face and a clerk's patience.

Anthe looked up at Corvane. Everyone, eventually, looked at Corvane. He was not the senior inquisitor by rank — that had been Prelate Ossory, who was now a smear of ash and doctrine on the Spire's third landing — but rank was a thing the Spire had made, and the Spire was a thing the country had unmade, and what remained was authority, and authority was Corvane.

"Transferred," Corvane said, "to the deep archive. Which no longer exists." He let that sit in the wet air a moment. "Which means forty-seven is either dead or walking around Vell with a sealed mind full of Spire-work and no handler. Find which."

Anthe wrote it down. Lask went back to his maps. And Corvane stood at the edge of the lamplight with water seeping cold into his boots, and did the thing he did every night now, which was to look at the reliquary lamp and think about mercy.

The lamp burned on rendered memory. That was the secret at the heart of the Spire that the country had never learned, or had learned and buried under thirty years of polite denial: that memory could be rendered, like tallow; that a life could be burned down to light. The inquisitors had always known. It was why they had been feared, and then hated, and then — when the Reconciliation came, and the wards went up, and the country agreed to forget — quietly pensioned off, scattered, renamed *archivists* and *records-keepers*, given small offices and smaller dignity, until the Spire itself was a story told to frighten children about the dangers of remembering.

Corvane had never stopped being an inquisitor. He had simply stopped saying so.

* * *

The runner came up out of the water at the second bell of the night, gasping, a boy of perhaps fifteen with the grey lips of someone who had swum the flooded galleries. He carried a leather satchel against his chest like a wounded bird.

"From the gatehouse watchers," he said. "From before the — before it all went wrong." His eyes were still wide with the day's catastrophe. "It got buried under the collapse reports. It only just —"

"Give it to me."

The satchel held the ordinary detritus of a gatehouse watch: duty rosters, a requisition complaint about mold in the barracks bread, and then, folded inside a ward-keeper's incident log, a single sheet of paper that made Corvane go very still.

It was a watch report, dated three nights before the Fall. A body pulled from the rubble of the old barbican — no, not pulled, *found*, found walking, half-dead, found with no papers and no name and a head wound that should have killed him. And beneath the watch-sergeant's blunt hand, in a different, finer script — a woman's, he judged, a ward-keeper's trained hand — a description.

*Male, late twenties, dark hair, grey-eyed. No identification. Unusual: subject shows no memory of how he came to be in the rubble. Claims five years' residence in Vell as a charm-seller. Claims the name Marguerite Montrose.*

Corvane read it twice.

The name meant nothing. The description meant nothing. Five years of charm-selling in the night markets meant nothing — the city crawled with petty memory-thieves, moths who had learned to sip at the edges of the Reconciliation's great forgetting, selling widows their own weddings back as luck. The Spire had never bothered with them. They were beneath doctrine.

But the *location* meant something.

The old barbican. The rubble of the gatehouse — and Corvane knew, because he had been there, thirty years ago, on the last night the Spire had still been the Spire, what lay beneath the old barbican. Not stores. Not armory. The transfer tunnel. The route by which the vessels had been moved, in their white coats and their soft shoes, from the tower to the archive and out of the world.

And a man found walking out of that rubble, with no memory of how he came there, in the same week the border wards fell — the same week the seals all over the country had begun, like sleeping eyes, to open —

Corvane set the paper down on the wet table and found that his hands were not quite steady, and was displeased with them.

"Anthe," he said. "The register. Read me the entries from the last year of operations. The transfer cohort."

She turned pages, frowning. "They're sealed entries, sir. Prelate's seal. We were ordered —"

"I know what we were ordered. Read them."

She hesitated. Lask looked at the lamp. In the flooded dark of the undercity, with the drowned chapel breathing around them and the city above screaming about a sky with no stars in it, the old orders felt suddenly like clothes from a dead man's wardrobe — still tailored, still fine, and no longer belonging to anyone living.

Anthe broke the seal. The register's last pages were written in the Prelate's own hand, and the Prelate had written small, the way he did everything, as though ink were a doctrine to be conserved.

"Transfer cohort, final entries," she read. "'Vessel forty-five, sealed and archived. Vessel forty-six, sealed and archived. Vessel forty-seven —'"

She stopped.

"Read it," Corvane said.

"'Vessel forty-seven,'" Anthe read, and her voice had gone careful, the voice of a woman walking on ice, "'sealed and archived. Note: subject self-sealed. Artificer's own work, turned inward, by his own hand and at his own request. Memory of self and office removed entire. Subject released to the city under a working identity. May he find what he is looking for.'"

The water dripped somewhere in the dark. The reliquary lamp burned its steady, cannibal light.

"Artificer," Corvane said quietly. "The artificer."

There had been only one. Everyone who had served in the last years knew it, though no one said it: the man in the white coat who had built the seals themselves, who had devised the whole architecture of the Reconciliation's forgetting, who had sealed the vessels' minds and then, in the final days, when the Spire was falling and the country was closing like a fist — had sealed his own. Had taken out his name, his office, his thirty years, and locked them behind a door only he could build, and walked down into Vell to become a charm-seller named Marguerite Montrose.

They had all assumed him dead. It was the comfortable assumption. It was the assumption Corvane had signed his name to in the Reconciliation's final audit, and had believed, because believing it was the only way to sleep.

And now the wards were down. And now a man with the artificer's face — though Corvane had never seen the face, only heard the voice through a tower door, only read the hand in the margins of a hundred seal-diagrams — was lying in a gatehouse infirmary, and a ward-keeper had written his description down instead of burning it.

"Who wrote this report?" Corvane asked.

Anthe checked the log. "A watch-sergeant. And the annotation — the description — is initialled. *I.V.*"

"Ilse Varn," said Lask. He said it flatly, but everyone at the table heard the weight under it. "The Varn girl. The one from the burning."

*The burning.* Two years ago, when the Reconciliation's final phase had ordered the destruction of the Spire's remaining records — the last act of a country burying its own past — it was ward-keepers who had fed the archives to the fires, because inquisitors were too watched, too hated, to be seen near a flame that size. And one of those ward-keepers, a young woman with a spotless record and a reputation for exactness, had stood at the pyre for six hours and fed every file in, and had been decorated for it, and had been quietly broken a year later on charges no one could quite name — negligence, they said, dereliction — and stripped of rank, and left to rot in the gatehouse watches where no one would look at her.

Corvane had always wondered what she had failed to burn.

"Find out where she is posted," he said. "And find out what happened to her court-martial. The full record."

"And the vessel?" Anthe asked. "Forty-seven?"

Corvane looked at the report again. *Claims five years' residence in Vell as a charm-seller.* Five years of small thefts, of widows' weddings and soldiers' homecomings sold back as luck. Five years of a man being no one, carefully, diligently, with an artificer's discipline.

The seals were waking. The country's great forgetting was cracking like river ice, and every sealed mind in Vell was a room whose door had begun, very softly, to swing. Somewhere in the gatehouse, behind a head wound and a false name, the man who had built all those doors was waking up inside his own.

If he woke all the way, he would know things no living person knew. He would know where the vessels slept. He would know how the seals were made — and how they were unmade. He would know, perhaps, what the Hollow Door was, and whether it could be opened, and what it would cost.

The Remnant could not allow that. Not out of cruelty — Corvane had never been cruel, and resented the word — but out of mercy, which was harder. A vessel that woke unguided burned. He had seen it. The power came back into a mind that had forgotten how to hold it, the way a flood comes back into a house with its doors painted shut, and what was left afterward was not a person. The Spire had sealed those minds to save them. Corvane had helped. He had carried the sedatives himself, down the white corridor, and held the artificer's instruments, and believed — still believed — that it was the kindest thing anyone had done in that tower.

The artificer had thought so too. That was the terrible symmetry of it. They had both, in their ways, been saving him.

"Vessel forty-seven is not to be harmed," Corvane said, and saw Anthe's surprise. "He is to be recovered. Gently. He is the last artificer and the only man alive who can unseal the others without killing them, and the country above us is full of vessels whose doors are already opening. We are going to need him." He folded the report and put it inside his coat, against his heart, where he kept the things that had become doctrine. "And if he cannot be recovered gently —"

He did not finish it. He never finished it. The unfinished sentence was the whole of inquisitorial mercy, and every person at the table understood it, and no one flinched, because they were the Remnant, and the Remnant did not flinch.

"Watchers on the gatehouse," he said. "Quietly. If Varn moves him, follow. If anyone else moves him —" a pause, the water dripping its patient count — "report to me before you act. No one touches the artificer but me."

* * *

Alone, afterward, Corvane sat at the edge of the lamplight with the report in his hands and let himself, for exactly as long as it took the reliquary lamp to consume one measured grain of rendered memory, remember.

Not his own memory. The lamp's. It was the discipline he had kept for thirty years: each night, one grain, one borrowed life burned down to light, so that he would never forget what the Spire's work actually was. Tonight's grain was a baker's — a woman, Vell-born, who had sold her childhood for bread-money in the famine years and whose memory, rendered, smelled of yeast and river-mud and a mother's voice singing something in a dialect that no longer had speakers.

He listened to it burn, and thought about the man in the white coat, and about the last time they had spoken.

It had been through a door. It was always through a door, with the artificer; even at the end, even on the last night, when the towers were coming down and the sky was filling with the first unnatural snow, the man had stood on the far side of a sealed door and spoken to Corvane through the gap in a voice that was perfectly calm.

*You'll keep the register,* he had said. *Someone has to remember what we did, even if no one else is allowed to.*

*And you?* Corvane had asked. *What will you remember?*

And the artificer had laughed — softly, the laugh of a tired craftsman putting down his tools — and had said: *Nothing. That's rather the point. I've made myself the last vessel, Corvane. The master key, locked inside its own lock. If the country ever needs me again, it will have to come and find me — and I won't know whether to open the door.*

The lamp consumed the last of the baker's childhood. The light steadied. Corvane rose, and took up his coat, and gave the order that set the Remnant moving — up through the flooded galleries, out into a city screaming under a starless sky, toward a gatehouse where a disgraced ward-keeper was, at that very hour, holding a lamp over a man with no name and saying *choose fast*.

The hunt had begun. It did not feel like a hunt. It felt, to Corvane, like a rescue — and that was the most dangerous thing about it.
