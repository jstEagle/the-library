## 6. A Bargain in Candlelight

### Ilse

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Ilse worked up the nerve to knock.

She had told herself she was only checking on him. That was the story she gave the duty sergeant, and it was even true, in the way most lies are true — a truth wearing a coat that didn't fit. The prisoner in the east cell of the gatehouse had taken water. He had kept down bread. His wounds were closing clean. There was no medical reason to visit him again before morning.

She went anyway.

The corridor smelled of wet stone and old iron, of the particular cold that lives inside walls built to keep things out and ends up keeping things in. Her boots rang against the flags. Somewhere below, the river muttered against the foundations of Vell, and above her — through arrow slits meant for archers' eyes, not stargazers' — there was nothing at all where the sky should have been. No stars. No moon. A black so complete it felt less like night than like a held breath.

Three days since the wards fell. Three days since the bell-sound broke over the city and every ward-keeper in Vell had stood at their posts with their hands pressed flat against stone that no longer answered them. Three days since Ilse had looked into the face of a half-dead stranger pulled from the rubble of the north gatehouse and known him.

Not remembered him. *Known* him. The way you know a word you were ordered to forget and forgot only on the surface, the way ash remembers fire.

She had lied for him already. She had written in her report that the man pulled from the gatehouse was nameless, unregistered, concussed past questioning — a vagrant, probably, one of the hundreds the collapsing wards had shaken loose like coins from a torn pocket. She had watched the clerk file it without reading it closely, because clerks in Vell had learned not to read too closely; reading closely was how you ended up assigned to the flooded districts, or worse, to nothing at all.

Now she stood outside his cell with a candle stub in one hand and a key in the other, and understood that the lie had a shape, and the shape had consequences, and she was standing inside all of them.

She unlocked the door.

He was sitting on the cot with his back against the wall, and he did not look surprised to see her. That was the first thing she noticed, and it unsettled her more than surprise would have. Surprise is what innocent men feel. He simply turned his head, slowly, the way a man turns who has spent years being looked at by people deciding what he was worth.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. His voice was rough but whole now, mended at the edges. "If they find you in here after curfew—"

"They won't." She set the candle on the floor between them, deliberately, the way you set down something you intend to talk across rather than talk over. "I bribed the watchman with a bottle I've been saving for five years. It seemed fitting."

"Fitting."

"I was saving it for the day I got my commission back. I'm beginning to suspect that day isn't coming."

He studied her. In the candlelight his face was all planes and hollows, the gauntness of a man who had been starved recently and carefully, though he didn't look like a man who knew why. There was a scar behind his left ear, thin as a hair, that she recognized because she had seen it described once, in a document, in a room that had since been emptied of everything except its smell.

"Marguerite," she said.

His eyes flinched. Not much. A millimeter of movement, the kind you'd miss if you hadn't spent your career watching faces for exactly that kind of movement. But she caught it, because catching it was what she was for, once. Reading wards. Reading people. The Spire had never seen much difference between the two.

"That's not my name," he said quietly.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. But it's the name you answer to, and I've been thinking about why a man would keep a false name the way other people keep religion."

He laughed — short, dry, involuntary, the laugh of a man who has just been seen and hates it. "You've been thinking," he repeated. "Ward-keepers think?"

"Ward-keepers used to *matter*," she said, and heard the bitterness come out sharper than she'd intended, and let it stand, because bitterness was honest and honesty was the only currency she had left. "Five years ago I kept the wards along the river district. Two hundred yards of stone and sigil-work that held back nothing anyone could see and everything everyone feared. Then the Reconciliation came, and the Spire fell, and we were told the wards had never been necessary. That magic had never been real. That the things we'd spent our lives holding back were stories we'd told ourselves to feel important."

"And you believed it?"

"I obeyed it. Believing was optional. Obedience wasn't." She crouched, finally, bringing herself level with him, close enough that the candlelight touched us both — no. Close enough that the candlelight touched them both, one small island of gold in a room made of dark. "They burned the files, Marguerite. All of them. Every record of what the Spire did, every registry of the sealed ones, every page with names on it. I carried the baskets myself. I fed the furnace myself. I stood there and watched twenty years of my own work turn to smoke, and I saluted while it happened, because that's what a good soldier does when the order comes down."

The prisoner said nothing. But he had gone very still, the stillness of a man listening for something underneath the words.

"There was one file I didn't burn," she said.

Stillness deepened into silence.

"It had a face on it. Yours. Or — a face that became yours, somewhere between the paper and the gatehouse rubble. I don't know how to explain that. I've been trying to explain it to myself for three days and getting nowhere. The file said you were an artificer. It said you'd been sealed. It said the seal was scheduled for review, and then the Spire fell, and reviews stopped happening, and men like you stopped existing on paper and started existing only in places like this one — cells, ditches, the spaces between categories."

"Why did you keep it?" His voice was careful. Curious and afraid in equal measure, which she was learning was his native condition.

"Because I'm a coward," she said. "Or because I'm not. I've never worked out which. Burning it would have been obedience. Keeping it was the first disobedient thing I ever did, and I did it quietly, in the moment between the basket leaving my hands and the page reaching the flames — half a second, no more. Half a second is enough, if you're fast and ashamed and paying attention."

"What was in it? Besides the face."

"Names. Dates. A seal designation." She paused. "A list of components."

He waited. She let him wait, because the next part was the part that mattered, and matters deserved their silence.

"The file listed eleven components," she said. "Memory fragments. Sealed fragments, harvested and stored, each one keyed to a specific purpose. The notation was incomplete — whoever wrote it had been interrupted, or had been ordered to stop writing, or had run out of time. But the last line was legible. The last line said: *all eleven required.*"

"All eleven for what?"

Ilse looked at him, and then, deliberately, at the darkness beyond the arrow slit — the skyless, starless, moonless dark that had swallowed the country three nights ago — and back at him.

"For a door," she said.

* * *

### Marguerite

Marguerite had sold memories long enough to know when someone was selling him one.

Everything Ilse said had the texture of merchandise. The words came measured, portioned, offered and withdrawn — a spoonful, a pause, another spoonful. She was feeding him information the way he fed charms to customers: enough to create hunger, never enough to end it. He recognized the technique because he had invented refinements of it. And yet — and this was the thing that kept him listening instead of laughing — underneath the merchant's pacing, her voice was doing something his voice had never done. It was *aching*.

Merchants don't ache. Merchants calculate. This woman calculated and ached in the same breath, and the combination was either the most dangerous thing he'd encountered in five years of thieving or the most honest, and he genuinely could not tell which.

"A door," he repeated.

"The Hollow Door." She said it the way believers say the names of saints they aren't supposed to believe in anymore. "In the old registries — the ones that survived in fragments, in copies people hid in floorboards and hymnals and the linings of coats — it's called different things. The Under-Gate. Winter's Mouth. The Door Beneath. But the oldest copy I ever saw, before it went into the furnace, called it the Hollow Door, and said it was the last passage out of the country that didn't go over the wall."

"Over the wall." Marguerite tilted his head. "There's a wall around the whole country?"

"There's a border. There always was. For a generation it was invisible — wards, sigils, agreements, the kind of border that exists in ledgers and in the memory of what the land is permitted to remember. Now—" She gestured at the ceiling, at the blackness pressing down through the slits. "Now the wards are down, and the border is remembering itself. And it is remembering itself as winter."

He had felt it already. Everyone in Vell had felt it, though the feeling defied description — a cold that came not from the air but from under it, rising through the stones like water finding cracks. The market stalls had frozen shut two nights ago. The river was skinning over at the edges, in high summer, or what passed for summer in a country that had forgotten its own seasons along with everything else.

"People are leaving," Ilse said. "Or trying to. The roads north are filling with carts and families and whatever can be carried. But the roads are wrong. Villages that appear on no map still stand on the road, full of people who don't know what country they're in. Signposts point at towns that burned down thirty years ago. And the passes through the mountains—" her voice tightened, "—the passes closed four days ago. Snow. Out of season. Out of nowhere. As if the country itself had decided to shut its doors and count who was still inside."

"And the Hollow Door doesn't close."

"The Hollow Door was never a door *out*, exactly. That's the part nobody understood, the part the Spire understood and never said out loud. It's a door *through*. Through the border. Through the winter. Through the forgetting. And it opens from the inside only, and it opens only for a key, and the key—" she tapped the flagstone between them, twice, gently, like knocking on a neighbor's wall, "—the key is not a thing. It was never a thing. It's a pattern. Eleven pieces. Eleven memories, sealed in eleven vessels, scattered when the Spire fell so that no single survivor could walk out with the whole country's escape route in his head."

Marguerite felt the sentence land in him like a stone dropped into a well whose depth he had never properly measured.

Eleven pieces. Scattered. Keyed. Required — *all eleven required.*

He thought of his ledger. He thought of the widow in Tanner's Row whose wedding memory he had stolen and sold back to her twice. He thought of the ferryman, the midwife, the old soldier on Candle Street, the choirboy with the voice like thawing ice — every theft catalogued in his own hand, every charm priced and sold, every buyer walking around Vell carrying a piece of someone else's past inside their heads like a coin sewn into a hem.

Five years. He had counted the entries once, drunk, on the worst night of the worst winter, and stopped counting at ninety because ninety was bad enough and the truth was worse.

"Ninety," he said aloud, before he could stop himself.

Ilse blinked. "Ninety what?"

"Memories. Stolen. Sold. Ninety buyers, give or take the ones who resold." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it in the open air. "You're telling me my petty little criminal career — the widow's wedding, the soldier's discharge, the midwife's first delivery — you're telling me those weren't crimes. They were *components*. Someone designed me. Someone seeded me into this city with a thief's trade and a thief's instincts and a false name, and let me do the scattering for them."

The candle flame leaned in some draft from under the door, and both their shadows swung across the wall like figures in a shadow-play, and for a moment neither spoke.

"Do you know that?" Ilse asked. "Know it, I mean — or suspect it?"

"I suspected it three nights ago." His voice came slower now, heavier, each word dragged up from somewhere below the floor of his ordinary speech. "When the wards fell. When the bell broke. Something opened in my head — a room I didn't know I had. I've been living in the rest of the house for five years, renting out its furniture piece by piece, and there was apparently always a locked room at the center of it, and the lock just — failed. Not broke. *Failed.* Like a mechanism completing its cycle."

"What's in the room?"

"Fragments. A tower. White coats. A child's hand — small, cold, trusting, holding onto my finger with a grip that meant something I can't translate anymore. Smells I can't source. A corridor I've walked in dreams for years and always assumed I invented." He laughed again, that same dry involuntary sound, and this time it hurt. "I assumed I invented a great many things. That's the trick of it, isn't it? You build a life out of whatever's left after someone takes the original, and you defend that life, and you call it yours, and eventually you'd kill for it. I would have killed for mine. I nearly did, twice, over smaller things than this."

Ilse was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"The room. What you can reach of it." She shifted forward on her heels, candlelight climbing her face, and her expression changed — the merchant's mask slipping, the ward-keeper's discipline taking its place, and beneath both of them something rawer than either. "I spent ten years learning to read sealed minds. Not break them — read them. There's a difference, and the difference is the only reason I'm still alive to sit in this cell. I can tell you what I see. I can't take it, and I wouldn't. But I can tell you whether the fragments match the file."

"And if they match?"

"Then I'll tell you what I came here to tell you, and we can stop wasting each other's courage on preliminaries."

So he showed her. He didn't know how else to describe what happened — he closed his eyes, and he opened the failed lock as far as it would go without breaking, and he let her look. It was not like being read by a customer. Customers took. She only stood at the threshold of him with her hands open, saying *show me*, the way you'd say it to a frightened animal, and what she saw she narrated back to him in a low, steady voice, like a woman reading instruments in a storm.

Tower. Confirmed. East range, third level, according to the file's marginalia.

White coats. Confirmed. Laboratory staff, experimental division.

The child's hand. She hesitated over that one. Long hesitation. Then, quietly, in a voice that had gone somewhere past professional: "Confirmed. And worse than confirmed. The file lists the children separately. There were nine. Their designations were letters. Their names were never written down anywhere, which was itself a decision someone made, and signed."

Marguerite opened his eyes.

The candle had burned another inch. The room was colder than it had been. He could not have said whether the cold came from the failing wards, the coming winter, or the specific temperature of hearing a stranger confirm the worst thing you carry.

"Now," Ilse said, "the part where I stop being careful."

* * *

### Ilse

She had rehearsed the offer eleven times on the walk over. She knew because she had counted, the way she counted everything now — steps, breaths, the seconds between lightning and thunder in a sky that had no right to hold lightning anymore. Eleven rehearsals, and every one of them had collapsed the moment she imagined his actual face instead of the imagined one, because the imagined face was a file photograph and the actual face was a starving man sitting six feet away from her, watching her the way you watch someone who might be about to save you or sell you, and not knowing which.

So she abandoned the rehearsal. She said the true thing instead, plainly, badly, the way true things get said when the speaker has run out of elegant options.

"I can get you out of this cell tonight," she said. "Not officially. Officially you're a vagrant pending processing, and processing in Vell currently means a holding pen in the undercity where the remnant's people are sorting the population into categories, and one of the categories is *unregistered vessel*, and I have reason to believe — strong reason, file-based reason — that you belong in it. If Corvane's people run you, they will find the seal. They will find the fragments. And they will not ask you questions. They will put you in a wagon and take you south, and you will never come back, and neither will the eleven pieces, and the Door will stay shut, and everyone inside this country will spend the rest of however-long-this-lasts inside a winter that is eating the country's memory one village at a time."

"You want me to run."

"I want you to *walk*. There's a difference. Running leaves traces. Walking, done correctly, looks like a guard escorting a prisoner to a latrine and coming back alone, and reporting that the prisoner slipped into the flood tunnels, and expressing appropriate regret." She paused. "I've written reports like that before. I'm good at them. It's the one skill the Reconciliation left me."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange—" Here she stopped, and made herself finish, because stopping here was the coward's option and she had promised herself, somewhere in the ashes of her career, that she was done choosing the coward's option. "In exchange, you carry what you carry to the Hollow Door. North. Through the winter. Through the erased villages and the dead signposts and the passes that no longer exist. I know the road. Not the map-road — the *real* road, the one underneath, the old ward-line that runs from Vell to the Door like a buried spine. Ward-keepers walked it for two hundred years. I walked it for ten. The maps burned, but the line is still in my feet. I can find it blind, in snow, in the dark that's coming. And you—"

She made herself look at him while she said the next part.

"—you are carrying a key in your head that you did not choose and cannot use and that will get you killed the moment anyone competent looks at you sideways. Alone, you are the most dangerous object in this country and the most fragile. With me, you are a passenger with cargo. Without me, you are cargo that hasn't met its courier."

Silence. The good kind — the kind where both parties are actually considering.

Then Marguerite said, "You want something. Everyone wants something. Five years of buying and selling has taught me that the only people who claim to want nothing are the ones charging the highest price. So. Name it."

And Ilse, who had rehearsed eleven times and abandoned the script, found that the true answer was also the hard one, and said it.

"I want my signature off the burning," she said. "Metaphorically. Actually, I want—" and here her voice did the thing voices do when the speaker suddenly hears her own depth, "—I want to have been the kind of person who keeps one file out of ten thousand. I was, once, for half a second. I want the half-second to have meant something. I want to stand at that Door when it opens and know that the reason it opened runs through me. Through the cowardice or the courage, whichever it was, whichever it is. I want to watch the winter lose."

"That's it?"

"That's it. No money. My pension died with my commission. No absolution — I don't believe in it, and neither, I suspect, do you. Just the Door, and the opening of it, and the right to say afterward, to whoever asks, that I carried the courier to the lock." She hesitated, then added, quieter: "And one more thing. Small. Selfish. I want to know what happens to the nine children. The ones with letters instead of names. If any piece of this key touches their lives — if opening the Door gives anything back to them — I want to be there when it does. I fed their records to a furnace. I'd like to feed something back."

Marguerite sat very still for a moment. Then he said, "The child's hand. In the fragment. The one holding my finger."

"Yes."

"I think one of them was mine. Not biologically. I mean — I think I was one of the nine. I think the hand was one of theirs, and I think the reason I can't translate what the grip meant is that translating it requires knowing which child I was, and that knowledge is on the far side of the seal." He exhaled, slow and controlled, a man managing his own vertigo. "Which makes your ninth condition redundant. If I'm one of the nine, then knowing what happens to the nine is knowing what happens to me."

Ilse considered this. Then she said, with the particular gentleness of a woman who has decided to stop protecting him from the truth: "Then we want the same thing, and neither of us can say honestly what it is, and that's the best foundation I've ever been offered for anything."

* * *

### Marguerite

He almost laughed. Almost. What stopped him was the recognition — sudden, total, unwelcome — that she was right.

Two survivors negotiating intimacy as a transaction. That was what this was. He had watched it happen a hundred times in the market, in the taverns, in the gray hours when Vell's desperate traded warmth the way they traded everything else: cautiously, in installments, with exits pre-built into every promise. *I'll show you mine if you show me yours. I'll trust you as far as tomorrow. I'll need you until I don't.* He had always watched from slightly above, the merchant's vantage, amused and untangled.

Now he was inside the transaction. Sitting on a cot in a gatehouse cell with a disgraced ward-keeper crouched across a candle stub from him, negotiating terms for a journey neither of them expected to survive, and discovering that the merchant's vantage was gone — dissolved, or confiscated, or simply outgrown — and that from down here, inside the deal, everything cost more and meant more and hurt more.

"So we understand each other," he said. "Terms."

"Terms," she agreed.

"One: you don't report me. Not to the remnant, not to the garrison, not to the clerk with the filing cabinet and the tired eyes. Whatever you decide I am, I stay a vagrant on paper until we're past the walls."

"Done. Already done, as of three days ago. I'm just making it retroactively sincere."

"Two: you don't open the seal. You said you can read it. Reading is fine. But if you ever decide you can *break* it — faster, safer, kinder — you tell me first. It's the only property I have left. The only self I have left, maybe. I decide when it breaks."

Ilse nodded slowly. "That's fair. And it's also—" she paused, honest even now, "—probably the wrong instinct, eventually. But it's yours, and I agree to it. Tell me first. Always tell you first."

"Three: the ledger. My ledger. Ninety entries, ninety buyers, ninety pieces of other people's lives circulating in this city because of me. If the Door needs eleven components and my thefts were the scattering mechanism —" he stopped, because finishing the thought out loud made it real, and real things demanded decisions, and he wasn't ready. "We'll come back to three."

"We'll come back to three," she said, and did not push, and he noted that — noted it the way he noted everything now, with the wariness of a man relearning how to trust and the hunger of one.

"Four," he said. "When this goes wrong — and it will go wrong, Ilse, something always goes wrong, I've survived five years on the certainty of it — when it goes wrong, we don't trade each other. Not to the remnant, not to the winter, not to whoever offers warm soup and a dry bed in exchange for the other one's location. We can fail together. We don't survive apart. Agreed?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Candlelight moved on her face. Somewhere above them, through the arrow slit, the black sky pressed down like a lid, and the wind coming through it carried the smell of snow from mountains that were not supposed to have snow.

"Agreed," she said. "Though I'll note, for the record, that you just proposed the exact clause a man proposes when he already suspects he's going to be the one worth trading."

"I know what I'm worth," Marguerite said. "I've priced it daily for five years. I'm worth whatever the market says the key in my head is worth, and the market is about to get very interested." He turned the candle stub a quarter-turn, idly, a thief's habit with his hands. "Which brings us to the last term. Mine, not yours. The one I almost didn't say."

Ilse waited.

"When we reach the Door — if we reach the Door — and it opens, and the key does what keys do, and everything I am gets poured into the lock the way the file says it has to be poured—" he made himself hold her eyes while he said it, "—I want you to remember that I chose it. Not that it was taken. Not that the seal broke and the Spire's machinery completed itself and I was consumed by my own design. That on some specific night, in some specific place, a man who had spent five years being a forgery looked at the chance to become real again and decided the price was acceptable. Remember that. Whoever I am after — whoever's left when the lock is full — I want one witness who knows the choice was mine."

Ilse was silent for the space of ten heartbeats. He counted them. Counting was becoming a habit; the country was teaching him to count things that might not be there tomorrow.

"I can be that witness," she said finally. "But I want it understood — recorded, even, in whatever private ledger you keep — that I intend to argue with you about it. The whole way north. Every cold camp, every dead signpost, every night the winter takes another village off the maps. I intend to argue that the key doesn't require your *willingness*, only your presence, and that willingness is a luxury survivors can't afford, and that you should keep as much of yourself as the lock will leave you." She paused. "I'll lose the argument. I already know I'll lose it. But I'm going to have it, because you deserve to be argued with by someone who wants you to survive, and I've spent five years surrounded by people who wanted me compliant."

Something in Marguerite's chest shifted — a small structural event, load-bearing, irreversible. He had spent five years being wanted the way merchants want customers: precisely, temporarily, in amounts calibrated to profit. Being wanted the way this woman wanted him — argumentatively, stubbornly, against her own interests — was a currency he had never handled, and his hands were clumsy with it, and he set it down on the cot beside him and resolved to pick it up again later, when no one was watching.

"Then we have a bargain," he said.

"We have a bargain," she said. "Fragile, mutual, and almost certainly doomed. Which is the only kind worth signing."

She stood. The candle was nearly gone — a quarter-inch of tallow and a blue-hearted flame fighting the draft under the door. She gathered the stub with the care of a woman conserving resources, tucked it into her coat pocket, and straightened to her full height, which was considerable, and which filled the small cell in a way that reminded him she had once stood at gates and been obeyed.

"Sleep," she said. "Eat what they bring you. Tomorrow I move you. The flood tunnels run north under the tanneries, and the grate at the third junction is mine — has been mine since before the Reconciliation, when keeping private grates was merely eccentric instead of treasonous. We go at the change of watch, when the corridors are emptiest and the sergeants are dullest. Wear the blanket as a cloak. Look concussed. Concussion explains everything — slow steps, blank eyes, bad answers. I've used it before."

"For yourself?"

"For others. Never for myself. I was always too proud." She smiled, briefly, crookedly, a smile with damage in it. "Pride is expensive these days. I'm liquidating."

Marguerite watched her go — watched her unlock the door from the inside, check the corridor with a ward-keeper's glance, two seconds of trained attention that read the hallway like a page, and step out into the dark with the candle stub in her pocket and his future folded into her coat alongside it.

The cell went black. Truly black, for the first time since the stars had vanished — no candle, no window, no glow from the city, because the city's lights were going out one district at a time as the winter ate the fuel and the fear ate the nerve to burn it. He lay on the cot in the absolute dark and listened to his own heartbeat, and thought about the eleventh component.

Ten, the file had listed. Ten fragments, ten vessels, ten pieces scattered among the living. *All eleven required.*

Ten accounted for. One missing.

He lay in the dark and turned it over, the way he turned everything over now, compulsively, a man shuffling a deck with cards missing. Ten fragments in ten strangers' heads. The eleventh — the keystone, the piece that made ten pieces into a key — unlisted. Unlocated. Unwritten, perhaps, in the interrupted margin where the notation ran out.

Or not unwritten. Perhaps only unwritten *down*.

Perhaps the eleventh component had never needed storing. Perhaps it was the one piece that couldn't be harvested, because it wasn't a memory of anything — it was the memory of the *maker*. The artificer who built the seals. Who designed the lock. Who scattered the other ten and then, in the last act of a falling tower, sealed himself — his name, his history, his self — into the one vessel that had to remain attached to its contents.

Himself.

He sat up in the dark, abruptly, heart hammering, and put his hand flat against the cold stone wall as if steadying himself against a conclusion.

The eleventh component was in his head. Had always been in his head. Was the seal itself — was the thing Ilse had read around, the thing she could see the edges of but not the interior of, the locked room at the center of the house. The ten fragments were pieces of the key. The eleventh fragment was the *hand that turned it*.

And it was scheduled — the file had said so, in that interrupted margin — for review. Review meant examination. Examination meant, eventually, *release*. The Spire had always intended to open him. The fall of the Spire had interrupted the schedule, and the interruption had
