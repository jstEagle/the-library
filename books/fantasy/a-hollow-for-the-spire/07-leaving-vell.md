## 7. Leaving Vell

The riot began at the ninth bell, which was fitting, because everything in Vell that had gone wrong lately had begun with a bell.

Marguerite heard it from the window of Ilse's rooms above the disused tannery: first the bell itself, cracked and toneless since the night the stars went out, and then the sound of people answering it — not with silence, as curfew demanded, but with voices, hundreds of them, rising off the streets like steam off a scalded thing. He pressed his palm to the cold glass and looked down into the Lanternway and watched a city decide, all at once, that it was done being afraid of the wrong things.

They were afraid still. That was the terrible grace of it. They were afraid of the dark sky, of the wards that had begun to sing in their sleep, of the inquisitors' white hoods moving through the smoke — but they had finally stopped being afraid of *each other*, and so they poured out of their houses with lamps and hammers and kitchen knives and stood shoulder to shoulder in the streets, shouting at the Spire's remnant as if shouting could hold back winter.

"Curfew's broken," Ilse said behind him. She was dressing by feel in the dark, buckling her old ward-keeper's coat over layers of wool, and she spoke the way she always did when something mattered — flatly, quickly, as though emotion were a toll she refused to pay twice. "It won't hold. The garrison will clear the Lanternway within the hour, and after that they'll lock the gates against their own citizens, and after *that*—" She pulled the strap tight. "After that there is no leaving Vell at all. So we leave now."

Marguerite turned from the window. In the guttering light of a single candle he looked at her and thought, not for the first time, that he did not trust her, and that he would follow her anywhere, and that these two facts had somehow stopped contradicting each other.

"The north gate," he said. "Through the tanners' quarter."

"The north gate is warded."

"The north gate is *singing*," he corrected. "You've heard it. Half the city has heard it. Whatever the wards were built to keep out, they've stopped keeping it out and started keeping time instead." He shrugged into his own coat, the one with the deep pockets where his charms lived, each one wrapped in wax paper like a sweet. "Wild magic doesn't bar doors, Ilse. It forgets they're doors."

She gave him a look — the look she reserved for his charm-work, which was equal parts professional contempt and unwilling fascination — and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. Five days ago he had been a thief with a false name and a sealed room in his skull. Now he was a thief with a false name, a sealed room in his skull, and a file full of someone else's handwriting explaining what the room was for. The name on the file had been burned before she could read it. The face on the file was his own, younger, unafraid, wearing the white coat of a Spire artificer.

He had not slept since. He suspected he would not sleep again for some time.

* * *

The tanners' quarter smelled the way it always had — of lime and rot and the river's slow dying — but tonight it smelled of smoke too, woodsmoke and something sharper underneath, the ozone bite of magic running loose through channels that had been dry for a generation. Marguerite felt it on his skin like weather. It raised the hair on his arms. It made his teeth ache in a way he recognized without understanding, the way you recognize a song from a country you've never visited.

The wards ran along the tops of the quarter walls in old iron conduits, green with verdigris, dead as drainpipes for as long as anyone could remember. Except they weren't dead now. As they came up the tanners' stair, keeping to the shadow of the drying sheds, Marguerite saw light moving inside the conduits — not the steady gold of maintained wardlight but something restless, many-colored, flickering like fish under ice. And he heard it. Everyone in Vell had been hearing it for five days and pretending not to: a hum, a chord, almost a melody, rising and falling with no pattern any musician could find.

"It's beautiful," he said, before he could stop himself.

"It's dangerous," Ilse said. But she had slowed, and her head was tilted, and he saw her hand rise unconsciously toward the nearest conduit the way a hand rises toward fire. She caught herself. "Ward-keepers used to say wild magic was just magic that hadn't been given a job yet. My instructors said that was sentimental nonsense." Her voice did something complicated on the word *instructors*. "I'm starting to think my instructors were afraid of it because it doesn't need them."

The gatehouse rose ahead of them, black against the blacker sky, its two towers leaning together like conspirators. Between them, the north gate itself — oak and iron, forty feet of it, built to stop an army — stood closed, and along its whole breadth the wardlines blazed now, visible even from here, running through the iron hinges and the iron studs and the great iron ring of the locking bar, and the light in them was not gold at all but every color at once, shifting, and the sound coming off it was nearly loud enough to feel in the chest.

And around the gatehouse, between them and it, the crowd.

Not rioters — the riot was still back in the Lanternway, three quarters away, a glow and a roar like a distant sea. These were the ones who'd come ahead: families with bundles, carters with their carts, a knot of dockworkers still in their aprons, all of them pressed up against the gate's outer bars or standing back from the wardlines with the wary reverence of people near a shrine. Waiting. Hoping the singing gate would open. Knowing it wouldn't.

"We go through them," Ilse murmured. "Slow. Heads down. We're nobody — a clerk and his wife going to check on property in the country. If anyone asks—"

"No one will ask," Marguerite said. "Everyone's looking at the gate."

He was right, and he hated being right about it. The crowd parted around them without seeing them, eyes fixed upward on the burning colors in the ironwork, faces lit blue and violet and a green that didn't belong to any lamp. A child on her father's shoulders reached toward the light and her father pulled her hand down, gently, the way you'd pull a hand from a stove. An old woman was weeping without noise. Somewhere a man kept saying *it's opening, look, it's opening* to no one, over and over, and it wasn't opening, and everyone let him say it because tonight the lie was kinder than the truth.

Ilse led them along the wall's base to the postern — the small door set into the gatehouse's flank, the one the watch used, the one whose ward-seal she had spent two days studying from memory of a profession she'd been stripped of. She knelt. Drew chalk. Began the old gestures, quick and precise, hands moving through patterns her body remembered better than her mind did.

The seal resisted. Then it shivered. Then, with a sound like a struck glass, it simply — stepped aside. Not broke. *Stepped aside*, the way a servant steps aside for a master, and the postern swung inward on darkness, and the wild light in the great gate flared once, all colors at once, bright enough to throw their shadows long behind them across the cobbles.

Every head in the yard turned.

For one heartbeat the whole crowd looked at them — at Ilse kneeling in the doorway, at Marguerite standing half-in, half-out, at the small dark gap of the postern that had not opened in twenty years. Marguerite saw hope move through the crowd like wind through wheat, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what they thought they were seeing.

"They'll follow," he breathed. "Ilse, if we open this door, they'll all—"

"I know." She was already through, pulling him after her. Behind them the crowd surged forward, and someone began hammering on the postern, and someone else began shouting for the watch, and above it all the great gate sang louder, wilder, as if answering. "Run."

They ran.

* * *

Outside the walls, Vell fell away behind them in terraces of rooftops, and the north road unrolled pale under a starless sky, and the country beyond it lay dark in every direction, waiting.

They walked fast for an hour, until the gate's singing faded to a thread behind them and the riot's glow became a smudge on the underside of the clouds, and only then did Ilse call a halt in the lee of a milestone whose inscription had been chiseled away sometime in the last generation — deliberately, thoroughly, the way the Spire had chiseled away everything. She checked the road both ways. Set wards of her own, small ones, honest ones, the kind that only told you if someone crossed them. Sat down against the stone with the finality of a woman putting down a weight she'd carried too far.

Marguerite sat beside her. The silence between them had changed texture over the past five days; it was no longer the silence of strangers but the silence of two people who had each seen the other's worst room. He found he didn't mind it. He found, in fact, that he minded how little he minded it, and that thought frightened him more than the inquisitors did.

"You should eat," she said.

"In a moment."

"Eat *now*. You go somewhere else when you don't eat. Someplace behind your eyes." She dug in her pack without looking at him, produced bread and hard cheese, put them in his hands with the impersonal authority of a quartermaster. "Whatever the seal is doing, starving it won't help."

He ate. The bread tasted of nothing, which was normal lately; most things tasted of nothing, as if his tongue had been included in whatever bargain had been made with his memory. He chewed and watched the dark road and felt the sealed room in his skull sit where it always sat, patient as a spider, and he thought about the file, and the schedule, and the words *for review*, and the ten fragments scattered through the heads of buyers in six cities, and the eleventh fragment that was himself.

And then — because he was tired, because the bread was nothing, because the wild magic still hummed faintly in the air around them like a tuning fork that wouldn't die, and because he was, beneath everything, beneath the guilt and the fear and the forged life, a thief, and thieves steal when they are frightened — he did the thing he had sworn, four days ago, alone in Ilse's rooms with the burned file smoking in the grate, never to do again.

He stole from Ilse.

It was barely a decision. That was the horror of it, looking back; that was the part he would turn over and over in the years after, the part that never got smooth no matter how he wore it. There was no moment of choice. There was only the habit, moving through him like water finding a channel worn deep by five years of rain: her attention on the dark road, her breathing slowed toward dozing, her guard down for the first time since he'd known her — and his hand drifting up, casual, scratching his jaw, fingers settling against his own temple in the old gesture, and the charm-craft turning over inside him, silent, practiced, *hungry*.

He took the surface memory nearest the top. It was all he meant to take — a crumb, a coin off a table, nothing. Just to quiet the hunger. Just to have something in his hands that wasn't dread.

Her court-martial came away with him like a leaf off a branch.

* * *

He knew immediately. Of course he knew immediately; that was the cruelty of the craft, that the thief always knows, in the first instant, exactly what he's taken, even when the victim doesn't know yet what she's lost. The memory unfolded in him whole and vivid and terrible, and he sat very still against the milestone with the bread going stale in his hand and let it happen, because stopping halfway through a theft damages the stolen thing worse than finishing does, and he had already done enough damage.

The hall at the Ward-Keepers' Bastion. The long table of judges. Ilse at twenty-six, standing straight in a uniform she would never wear again, while a man with a dry voice read the charges: negligence in the maintenance of the Ashford line. Failure to report anomalies. The deaths, indirectly, of eleven people when a ward failed during the flood year — a ward that the record showed, and the record showed it, showed it plainly, showed it in her own signed reports, had been scheduled for replacement for two years and defunded each time, defunded by the same tribunal now judging her, by men who would sign her disgrace and keep their own seats.

He watched her defend herself — not for pardon, he realized; she had already understood there would be no pardon, and her defense was not for the judges at all but for the record, for the junior keepers sitting along the wall, so that someday someone reading the transcript would know the truth. He watched her present the reports. Watched the dry-voiced man accept them, glance at them, and set them aside unread. Watched her understand, in real time, in front of two hundred colleagues, that the truth was not a defense here, that the institution did not make mistakes, it made *examples*.

And then the sentence. Stripped of rank. Struck from the rolls. Exile from the Bastion. And the last thing, the small thing, the thing that broke something in him that he suspected would not mend: the moment after, in the corridor, when a young keeper she had trained — a girl with ink-stained fingers — started to speak to her, started to reach out, and Ilse shook her head once, barely, and walked on alone, and did not cry until she was outside the walls, and even then only for a minute, checking the road first, the way she checked everything, the way she would check everything for the rest of her life.

*Five years,* the memory said, in its wordless way. *Five years carrying a disgrace that belonged to other people. Five years watching the Spire burn records and rewrite maps and pretend, and knowing — knowing — that the rot went all the way up, and having no one left to tell.*

That was why she'd kept his file. That was why she'd lied to Corvane's people at the gate. Not debt, or not only debt. Recognition. She had looked at a stranger's face on a page marked for destruction and seen another person the institution had decided to erase, and she had burned everything except him, because burning him would have made her complicit, and she had spent five years learning exactly what complicity cost.

Marguerite came back to himself with the taste of ash in his mouth that had nothing to do with the bread.

Beside him, Ilse sat with her eyes closed, her breathing unchanged, her face in the starless dark smooth and unknowing. She didn't know yet. She might not know for days — stolen memories left a soft place, a blank with edges, and most people papered over the blank without ever examining it. She would feel it eventually. Something missing. A room in her own house she couldn't enter. And she would know, the way he had known, that someone had been there.

*I am the same thing they are,* he thought. The realization arrived without drama, flat and complete, like a ledger balancing. *Corvane. The Spire. The ones who sealed me. I take what isn't mine from people who trust me, and I call it craft, and I call it survival, and I sell it twice.* He looked at his own hands in the dark, the thief's hands, quick and clever and suddenly obscene. *The file says I built seals. It says I helped design the taking of minds. Maybe that's why I'm good at this. Maybe I never stopped.*

No. That was the seal talking, or the fear talking; he couldn't tell anymore which voices in his head were his own. But it didn't matter whether the thought was true. What mattered was that he had done it *again*, after swearing, after knowing, after five days of clarity — and that the doing had felt like relief, right up until it felt like rot.

He could give it back. That was the one mercy of the craft: what was taken could be returned, if the thief was skilled, if the thief acted soon, before the memory settled into new soil. He knew the return-gesture. His fingers knew it better than his name.

He looked at her sleeping face and did not move.

Because giving it back meant touching her mind again. Because returning a theft required admitting the theft, waking her, saying the words aloud: *I stole from you, the one person in this country who owes me nothing and gave me everything, and I did it because I was frightened and hungry and because stealing is the only prayer I know.* Because some cowardly arithmetic in him whispered that she would forgive him — that she had already forgiven larger things — and that forgiveness was a currency too, and he had lived too long poor to spend honestly.

He sat with the stolen memory folded in him like a letter he had no right to read, and the mile-stone cold against his back, and the wild magic humming far off in the walls of Vell like a conscience he couldn't shut out.

"Ilse," he said.

She woke instantly, completely, the way soldiers wake — hand already moving toward her knife, eyes already cataloguing the dark. Then she saw it was only him, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch, and he understood that even that small loosening was a gift she had decided to give him, and that he was holding a theft of it in his pocket.

"What is it?" she said. "Trouble on the road?"

"No trouble on the road." His mouth was dry. "There's something I have to tell you. About what I am. About what I did tonight, before we stopped." He made himself meet her eyes. "About what I did *to you*."

Behind them, far to the south, the bells of Vell began to ring — all of them at once, cracked and tuneless, urgent — and the sound rolled up the dark road toward them like a wave, and Ilse went still, listening, and whatever confession he had gathered in his throat froze there, because those were not riot bells.

Those were alarm bells. And they were ringing for the north gate.

Ilse was on her feet before the second peal, staring south at a glow that was growing, colorfully, impossibly, along the skyline of the city they had just escaped — the wild light in the wards, flaring higher and higher, all colors at once, like a fire that burned in every hue but red.

"Oh no," she said quietly. "Oh, Marguerite. They're all trying to leave. And the gate is *letting them*."
