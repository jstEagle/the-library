## 9. The Memory Market at Ashford

Ashford announced itself by smell before it appeared at all — woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath both, something sweeter and stranger: the faint burnt-sugar scent of memories being handled too often. Marguerite knew that smell the way a coin-counter knows the taste of old copper. He had carried it on his own fingers for five years.

The town sat in a bowl of hills like a cupped hand, walled not with stone but with lanterns — hundreds of them, strung on rope and wire and fishing line between crooked poles, every one burning a different color according to what it held. Amber for childhoods. Green for griefs. A few rare violet ones whose contents no one would name aloud. The light they threw was not warm; it pooled strangely, as if the air itself were remembering being water.

"Refugees," Ilse said, reining up beside him on the frozen road. Her breath came out in white ribbons. "Half the north's funneling through here since the wards fell."

"And the other half is selling to them," Marguerite said.

He could see them already from the ridge — the market sprawled through Ashford's one long street like spilled ink, stalls of canvas and cart-tarpaulins, buyers and sellers huddled over small flames, and everywhere the trade itself happening in the oldest way: palm to palm, temple to temple, a thumb pressed briefly against the soft skin below a customer's ear. No coin changed hands that Marguerite could see. Coin was paper now. Paper burned. Memory kept.

His hands ached suddenly, an old ache, the ache of craft. He pressed them flat against his thighs and made himself look away.

"We need food," Ilse said. "And information. And neither of those is free anywhere anymore."

"They're free here," Marguerite said quietly. "They're just paid for in the only money left." He turned his horse toward the lantern-gates. "Stay close to me. And whatever you see me do down there — remember I've done it before."

* * *

Inside the walls, Ashford was louder than any city he had ever known, though half its people did not speak. They stood in doorways with their mouths slightly open, faces slack as unwritten pages — the freshly harvested, the ones who had traded everything and were still deciding whether they regretted it. Around them moved the others: the sellers, the brokers, the pickpockets-of-the-mind, all of them touching their own temples reflexively, the way a man who has lost a tooth keeps finding the gap.

Marguerite walked through it like a man walking through his own reflection.

Here a woman sold the smell of her mother's kitchen to three buyers in turn, each paying a different color of charm-light. There a broker weighed two memories on a little brass balance — not physically, nothing physical about it, but everyone pretended there was, because pretending made the trade feel honest. A boy of perhaps ten offered passersby the memory of falling out of a pear tree, unbroken bones and all, four years old, going cheap.

"The rule," Marguerite murmured, without turning his head. He felt Ilse tense behind his shoulder.

"What rule?"

"The one I told you once. Never steal anything you can't sell twice." He nodded at the pear-tree boy. "That memory's been sold six times. You can tell by how thin the edges are. First sale, it's sharp — you can feel the bark under your palms. Sixth time, it's like holding smoke. But someone will buy it anyway, because smoke is better than nothing, when winter's eating your whole country."

Ilse said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone careful. "You talk about it like a trade. Like it's respectable."

"It isn't. That's why I'm good at describing it."

They found a stall selling bread that tasted mostly of ash and bought it with a memory Marguerite had been carrying for two days — the recollection of a stranger's dog, retrieved off a dying man on the road north. It hurt less than he'd expected to give it up. That frightened him more than anything else he'd seen in Ashford.

* * *

It was Ilse who saw the stall first. She caught his sleeve — she almost never touched him — and turned him by the arm as if he were a slow child.

"Look," she said. "There."

The stall stood apart from the others, back against the ruined wall of what had been a chapel, lit by a single lantern of deep, saturated blue — a color Marguerite had never once seen in five years of the trade, and recognized instantly anyway. Institutional blue. Archive blue. The Spire sealed its deepest records in glass dyed that shade, or so the rumors went, back when the Spire was only rumors.

The seller was an old man wrapped in three coats, none of them his, sitting very straight on a stool as if posture were the last thing he owned. Before him lay a velvet cloth bearing exactly one item: a small glass phial, no longer than a finger, filled not with liquid but with light — a light that moved, that shifted, that showed, when the eye caught it right, the suggestion of stairs winding upward into darkness.

Marguerite stopped breathing.

"Memory of the inner tower," the old man said. His voice was dry as moth wings. He wasn't calling out to the crowd; he didn't need to. "One owner only. Verified provenance. Taken inside the Spire itself, before the fall." His eyes, pale as skimmed milk, lifted and found Marguerite across twenty feet of crowded market, and stayed there. "You'll want to handle it carefully, sir. Some contents are heavier than they look."

"Ilse," Marguerite said. His voice came out strange, flattened, as though heard through a wall. "Walk past. Don't stop. We're leaving."

"You're staring at it like—"

"*We're leaving.*"

But his feet had already betrayed him. Later he would not be able to say which of them crossed those twenty feet first — only that suddenly the stall was in front of him and the phial was in his hand and the cold of the glass bit through his glove like teeth.

The tower rose in the light. Not an image, not a picture — a *memory*, pressing against the walls of the phial the way a living thing presses against a cage. Stairs of black stone worn pale at the centers by centuries of feet. A landing where the wind always smelled of snow, even in summer. A door banded in iron, and beyond the door —

Beyond the door was a room he knew.

Not remembered. *Knew.* The way a hand knows itself. A workbench under a high window. Shelves of sealed glass, row upon row, each labeled in a neat hand he somehow understood to be his own. A white coat hung on a peg by the door. And on the bench, half-finished, a seal — the same seal, the exact same geometry, that sat coiled and silent somewhere behind his own eyes.

The market noise fell away. Somewhere very far off, Ilse was saying his false name — the false name, Montrose, the name that was a coat he wore over a nakedness he'd never seen — and her voice sounded like someone calling across a frozen lake.

"How much," he heard himself ask.

The old man smiled with too many teeth for an old man's mouth. "For this? Not silver. Not charms." He leaned forward, and up close Marguerite could see that the milky eyes weren't blind at all. They were focused, patient, and terribly awake. "For this I want a look inside the buyer. Standard practice for archive-grade goods, sir. I have to know my customer isn't carrying anything... contagious."

Every instinct he owned screamed at once.

Because that was the trick — of course it was the trick — and it was a good one, elegant, the kind of trap he himself would have built in the old days of Vell's night markets: bait a man with his own face, then demand to search his head for the thing everyone in this broken country was now hunting. Unregistered vessels. Sealed minds. The Remnant's phrase, passed down the refugee road like a fever, whispered in bread lines and latrine queues for weeks now. *They can tell. They touch your temple and they know.*

"No," Marguerite said. He set the phial down. His hand shook doing it, and the shaking felt like the truest thing about him. "I'm not buying."

"A shame." The old man's voice dropped, pleasant and precise. "Because you've already handled it. And handling leaves traces, sir. Everyone knows that." Those milk-pale eyes flicked, deliberately, past Marguerite's shoulder — to where a knot of men in gray travel-cloaks had paused at the mouth of the lane, lanterns of institutional blue hanging from their staves, sweeping the crowd with slow, methodical turns of their heads. "In fact, I'd say you've been handling things your whole life. Haven't you?"

Marguerite turned, unhurried, a man who had merely glanced at a stall and lost interest — and walked into the crowd with Ilse's hand hard around his wrist.

"Don't run," she breathed against his ear. "Running marks you faster than anything. Walk."

They walked. The gray-cloaked men drifted along the market's edge, and wherever their lantern-light touched, people flinched and covered their temples, and once, horribly, a woman simply sat down in the trampled snow and wept without sound while the light passed over her and moved on. Searching. Sweeping for what was left.

A shout went up behind them. Marguerite didn't turn to see whether it concerned the old seller or some other unlucky soul; turning was running, and running was dying. He fixed his eyes on the back of a fishmonger's cart and steered them both after it, through the press of bodies, past stalls of amber and green and grieving violet, until the chapel wall was behind them and the lantern-gates glowed ahead.

Then a hand closed on his collar and spun him around, and it was not a gray cloak but Ilse, her face white as the road, her ward-keeper's training finally cracking through her soldier's discipline.

"That room," she whispered. "In the phial. You knew it. Marguerite — *Montrose* — whoever you are — you looked at that tower the way I looked at your file. Like reading your own death warrant."

Behind her, at the far end of the street, one of the blue lanterns stopped moving.

It hung in the air above the crowd, patient as the lights that had followed them through the snow, and began — slowly, gently, with the courtesy of a man entering a sickroom — to drift their way.

"Later," Marguerite said. "Please. Whatever this is, later."

"There won't be a later if—" She saw his face and stopped. Five days on the road had taught her that much about him: when his voice went quiet and flat as it was now, he had already counted the exits.

"Roofline," he said. "Left of the chapel. There's a gap between the granary and the wall — I used to run gaps like that in Vell, before I learned to walk instead." He pulled her toward the shadowed alley, and behind them the blue light brightened against the backs of the crowd like dawn coming the wrong way. "And Ilse — when we're clear — I need to tell you what I saw in that phial."

"You don't owe me explanations."

"I know." The gap was ahead, dark and narrow, snow squeaking under their boots. "That's what frightens me. I *want* to."

They went up into the dark together, and below them the blue lantern reached the empty stall, and paused, and tilted — like a head, considering — over a velvet cloth where a single memory of the inner tower still waited, still glowing, still for sale.
