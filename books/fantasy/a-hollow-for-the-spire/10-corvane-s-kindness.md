## 10. Corvane's Kindness

The cell beneath the flooded customhouse had once been a counting room. Corvane had chosen it for that reason. Ledgers were still bolted to the walls behind their iron grilles, their columns of figures swollen and illegible with damp, and there was a comfort in that — in being surrounded by the remains of an institution that had believed, as the Spire had believed, that everything could be counted, and that what could be counted could be controlled.

He arranged the room himself. A table, two chairs, a lamp of Spire-make that burned without smoke and gave light the color of late afternoon. A carafe of water, not wine — wine loosened tongues too fast and left them sore by morning, and Corvane had never approved of soreness. A plate of bread and hard cheese, because the thief had been in the holding pens for two days and fear digested poorly on an empty stomach.

And on the table, squared to its edge with a precision his subordinates found faintly alarming, a single sheet of paper bearing a description.

*Male. Mid-thirties. Dark hair going grey at the temples. Answers to "Marguerite Montrose," which is not his name. Seal-work of the third order, self-applied, seven years stable. Do not damage the vessel.*

Corvane read it again while he waited, though he knew every word of it by heart. He had written most of it. The last line was not his; it was older, copied from a file he had carried across half a drowning country in a oilskin wallet against his heart, through the fall of the Spire, through the years when carrying such a file was death by fire if anyone read it. *Do not damage the vessel.* He had obeyed that instruction longer than he had obeyed any commandment of the order that wrote it, and he intended to go on obeying it, whatever the remnant said, whatever the flood said, whatever the darkening sky said.

There came the sound of boots in the standing water of the corridor, and then his prisoner.

They brought her in wet to the waist, because everything in the undercity was wet to the waist now, and Corvane rose to meet her the way he would have risen to meet a colleague — which, in a sense, she was. She was perhaps twenty-five, sharp-faced, river-thin, with the particular wariness of someone who had learned early that doors open from both sides. Her wrists were bound with cord, not iron. He had given that instruction too.

"Sit, please," he said, and drew out her chair.

She looked at the chair as though it might be a trap, which was intelligent of her, because most things in this country had become traps. Then she looked at the bread, and something in her face betrayed her — hunger is honest even when nothing else is — and she sat.

"Untie her hands," Corvane said to the guard at the door. "Wait outside."

"Master Inquisitor—"

"She's a cutpurse from the Ashford market, not a ward-breaker. If she runs, the water will slow her more than your rope would." He smiled at the girl. "Won't it."

The guard hesitated, then cut the cord and withdrew. The door closed. The lamp hummed its small contented hum. Somewhere above them, muffled by three floors of drowned stone, the winter wind moved through the streets of the dead city, and Corvane heard in it that new note that had been growing all month — a note like a voice trying to remember a word.

"Eat," he said. "Please. Interrogation goes better when no one is hungry. I've found that people lie mostly with their stomachs."

The girl watched him for a long moment. Then she took the bread, and ate the way the starving eat — carefully, as if it might be taken back, as if kindness itself might be a hook.

"You're not what they said," she said, around the second mouthful. "The others in the pens. They said the inquisitors would be—" She stopped, recalculating. People always recalculated, in front of Corvane. It was one of the reasons he preferred speaking to them alone.

"What did they say we'd be?"

"Tall," she said finally. "And cold. And wearing those grey coats with the eyes on them."

"The coats burned well, at least. That was something." Corvane sat down opposite her and folded his hands. Up close, in the lamplight, he was not tall — a spare, stooped man of fifty-odd with ink-stained fingers and the mild face of a provincial schoolmaster — and this, too, was useful. Men who expected monsters often talked freely to schoolmasters. "My name is Corvane. You may use it or not, as you like. I'm going to ask you some questions about two travelers who passed through Ashford nine days ago. Before I ask them, I want you to understand something, so that you can decide how much of your fear to keep and how much to set down."

She stopped chewing.

"I am not going to hurt you," Corvane said. "Not today, not tomorrow, not ever, if I can help it. Whatever you've heard about the Spire — much of it is true, and I grieve for the parts that are true, and I will not waste your time denying them. But my work has always been a different kind of work. I mend things. I put back what was taken out. And the man I'm looking for is the most important thing I have ever tried to mend."

The girl swallowed. "You want the charm-seller."

"Marguerite Montrose. Yes."

"He didn't do anything. He bought a memory off Tallow-Jen, that's all. Off the rack, like anyone. Paid silver like anyone."

"And then?"

"Then the wardens came through the market asking after him, and he and his woman went out the north gate before dawn, and that's all I know, I swear it on—"

"On whatever you swear on. I believe you." And he did, which surprised her visibly; disbelief was cheaper and she had budgeted for it. "But you knew him before that. You sold beside him, or near him. Charm-sellers talk to each other. They compare techniques. They complain about customers." He leaned forward slightly, and softened his voice into the register he used for confidences. "I don't want to punish him, child. I want to save him. And I need to know where he's gone so that I can reach him before the winter does, or before the remnant's other hunters do — and I promise you, I am by far the gentler of the things following that road."

* * *

It took an hour, and it took almost no cruelty at all, which was Corvane's craft and his pride.

He let her finish eating. He asked her name — Sefa — and used it, sparingly, the way you touch a nervous animal. He asked about the market at Ashford, and listened with genuine interest to her complaints about the toll-wardens and the price of tallow, because listening was not a performance for him but a discipline, and because a person who feels heard will eventually hear themselves say more than they meant to. He asked what charms sold best that season, and she said luck-charms, always luck-charms, and he nodded as though this confirmed something he'd long suspected about human nature, which it did.

Then, very quietly, he asked what Marguerite's charms felt like.

Sefa blinked. "Felt like?"

"When you held one. The good ones — the ones he must have made before the wards fell, when the magic was thin and only a born artificer could coax anything out of it at all. What did they feel like in the hand?"

She frowned, reaching. "Warm," she said slowly. "No — not warm. Like... like a room where someone was just sitting? Like the warmth was left over?" She looked up, startled by her own answer. "Jen said they felt like being remembered. That's why they sold. Everyone's so scared of being forgotten now, with the roads going and the towns going. His charms felt like proof you'd happened."

Corvane closed his eyes briefly.

*Like being remembered.* Seven years of sealed silence, and the boy's work still spoke in his own native tongue. Memory was the only language Marguerite had ever been fluent in; the Spire had seen to that, carving away everything else until only the gift remained, clean and terrible and usable. They had called it refinement, in the tower years. Corvane had stood in the white room with his hands folded and his doubts folded smaller inside them, and he had watched, and he had told himself it was necessary, and the telling had worked for a very long time.

It worked still. That was the thing no one understood about him — not the remnant, not the frightened girl across the table, not the drowned city overhead. They thought a man who hunted fugitives through a dying country must be either a fanatic or a coward, running toward the old order because he couldn't survive the new one. But Corvane was neither. Corvane was a man who had made a promise in a white room, to a boy strapped gently into a chair with his own consent, and promises were the only scripture he had ever kept.

"We were sealing him for his own protection," the boy had said, before the first incision. Nineteen years old, brilliant past all decency, his forearms already mapped with the fine silver scarring of the artificer's trade. "If the wards ever come down — if the magic comes back the way the models say it will — anyone carrying unsealed capacity will burn out. Or be taken. Or be used." He had laughed, a little. "Or all three. Better a locked room than an open wound, Master Corvane. Isn't that what you teach the second-years?"

*Isn't that what you teach the second-years.* And Corvane had said yes, because it was what he taught, because it was true, because the alternative — that they were not protecting the boy but amputating him, that the seal was not a shield but a door with the hinges removed — that alternative had been unthinkable, and so he had declined to think it.

The wards had come down. The magic had come back. And somewhere on the north road, walking through a country that was forgetting its own name, was a young man with a locked room where his life should be, and every unregistered vessel between Vell and the border was already burning, or being taken, or being used.

All three. Just as the boy had said.

"Master Inquisitor?" Sefa said. "You went somewhere."

"I did." He opened his eyes and smiled at her, and it was a real smile, tired at the edges. "Forgive me. Old memories. Now — the north gate. Who else went out that night? Caravans, pilgrims, anyone heading up the old causeway road?"

And she told him. Because he had fed her, and untied her, and listened to her grievances, and because he had never once raised his voice, and because — this was the part Corvane understood better than any torturer who had ever worked for the Spire — because she wanted to help him. Kindness extracted more surely than pain. He had learned that in the tower years too, watching the senior inquisitors work, and he had hated it then, and he used it now without hating himself, or telling himself he didn't.

A tinker's cart. Two families of refugees. A lame horse sold at the gate. And a woman in a ward-keeper's coat with the insignia torn off, walking beside a charm-seller who kept looking back at the town as though it owed him something.

"The causeway road," Corvane said. "North past the drowned chapel?"

"They'll have turned off by now. Everyone turns off. The causeway's..." She hesitated, and lowered her voice as though the walls might repeat it. "The causeway forgets you. People come back the wrong length. There's a ferryman at the marsh edge who takes silver and doesn't ask where you've been."

"Thank you, Sefa." He rose, and poured her a cup of water from the carafe, and set it before her like a sacrament. "You've been more help than you know. More than you can know."

"Am I free to go?"

"In a little while. There's a boat leaving for Ashford at dawn and you'll be on it, with silver in your pocket and no record anywhere of this conversation." He moved to the door, then paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at her — and for a moment the schoolmaster's mildness fell away entirely, and she saw what was underneath, which was not cruelty but something worse: certainty, worn smooth as a river stone by decades of use.

"One question more, and then you rest. The charm-seller's companion. The woman. Did she seem afraid of him?"

Sefa considered. "No," she said slowly. "That was the strange thing. Everyone's afraid of everyone now. But she looked at him like—" She searched. "Like he was something she'd lost and found again. Like she was afraid *for* him. Of the whole world, but not of him."

"Yes," Corvane said softly. "Yes, that's what I feared."

* * *

He dismissed her to the pens with the promised silver and sat alone in the counting room while the lamp hummed and the drowned city dripped.

From the wallet against his heart he took the file — the one file, the one he had saved from the fires when the order came down to burn the memory archives, the order he had executed faithfully except for this. He had told himself then that he kept it as evidence. He had told himself many things, in those years. The paper was soft as cloth now from handling. On it, in a clerk's neat hand, was a list of names, and beside each name a seal designation, and beside one name — the fourth from the top, the only name Corvane had ever memorized — a note in his own younger writing:

*Seal holds. Subject cooperative. Subject believes.*

*Subject believes.* That had been the triumph of it, the thing the architects of the program had prized above all. Not a prison the mind fought, but a story the mind lived in. Give the vessel a false history it can love — five years of small thefts, small guilts, a manageable life — and it will defend that history against the truth with everything it has. No chains required. The boy had designed the technique himself. He had been so proud of it.

And now the wards were down, and the seal was fraying — Corvane could feel it from here, the way you feel a tooth working loose — and piece by piece the locked room was giving up its contents into a mind that had spent seven years believing itself a petty thief named Marguerite Montrose. Every fragment that surfaced would hurt. Every fragment would contradict the forgery. And when the last of it came home — the tower, the children, the white coat, the name — the boy would understand what had been done to him, and by whom, and he would hate.

Unless someone reached him first. Unless someone who had been there in the white room, who had held his hand before the first incision and promised — *this is to save you, this is to keep you whole* — unless that someone arrived in time to frame the truth correctly. To kneel. To say: *we did this out of love, and here is why, and here is what you must do now, because the key in your head is the only thing keeping this country from finishing its own erasure, and you were always meant to carry it, and I have come to help you carry it.*

Corvane believed every word of that. That was the horror of him, if horror it was: there was no lie anywhere in his intention. He loved the boy in the way a surgeon loves, which is the coldest love and the only kind that cuts.

He repacked the file. He snuffed the lamp, because Spire-lamps were rationed now, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the water rise, inch by patient inch, up the walls of the drowned customhouse.

Then he called for his lieutenant and gave the orders: six riders, the marsh ferry, the causeway road. Quietly. No grey coats. No eyes on the sleeves.

"We're not taking him," he said. "We're meeting him. Remember that, all of you. If any hand is laid on that man before I reach him, I will take the hand."

And in the dark of the counting room, among the ruined ledgers, Corvane whispered the name — the true one, the sealed one, the name that had not been spoken aloud in seven years — practicing it, the way you practice a prayer you intend to say over a grave, or over a resurrection. He wasn't sure yet which it would be.

Neither, he suspected, was the country.

Far to the north, on a road that was busy forgetting itself, a fire burned small in the dark, and two people sat close to it, and one of them was beginning, at last, to remember.
