## 11. What Ilse Burned

The fire Ilse built that night was too small for the cold, and she knew it, and she built it small anyway.

Smoke was a beacon. Smoke was the thing that had killed three of the last four ward-keepers she'd trained with, back when there were ward-keepers and back when there was training and back when the word *back* still meant something other than a country that had stopped existing behind them. So she fed the fire one stick at a time, patient as a woman feeding a bird she intended to eat, and she kept it low and red and mean, and she sat with her back against a fallen wall and her sword across her knees and watched Marguerite Montrose — or whatever his name was, the name under the name — sleep like a man who had learned that sleep was just another kind of theft you committed against yourself.

He slept badly. That was new. In Vell he had slept like the dead, or like the professionally guilty, which amounted to the same posture: one hand loose near his knife, breath shallow, ready to be gone. Out here, under a sky with nothing in it, he turned. He flinched. Twice she had heard him say words in a language she didn't know, and once — this was the one she kept turning over, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth — once he had said *no, not the children, take mine instead,* in a voice that was not a thief's voice at all.

She should have woken him. Ward-keepers woke sleepers who spoke in old languages; that was in the manual, in the chapter she'd never been tested on because the manual had been burned the same winter everything else was. Instead she sat and watched him and let the fire eat her one stick at a time, and she understood that she was going to tell him. Not tonight. But soon. Because the road north was going to get worse — she had seen the way the signposts had begun to point at nothing, the way the villages had gone off the maps like ink lifted by water — and a woman could not walk into a country that was forgetting itself while carrying a secret that heavy. It would pull her down like a stone in a river. It had already begun to.

The secret was a piece of paper. That was the absurdity of it. Five years of her disgrace, her court-martial, her name struck from the rolls of the Vellish ward, the whole slow humiliation of her exile to gate duty — and under all of it, at the bottom of it, like a coin at the bottom of a well, was a single piece of paper that she had not burned.

* * *

The order had come down in the last spring of the old world, when the Spire was still standing and the wards still hummed and nobody in Vell said the word *magic* above a whisper, and never indoors.

Ilse had been twenty-six and a keeper of the third rank, which meant she had authority over nothing but her own hands, and her hands were good hands. She could feel a ward the way a midwife feels a pulse. They had told her once, at her certification, that she had the finest touch the examiners had seen in a decade, and she had gone back to her barracks and gotten quietly drunk on that sentence, and she had believed — this was the part that still burned, this was the coal she carried — she had believed that the Spire's work was clean. That the sealing of unregistered minds was a mercy. That the people who went into the memory-wards came out lighter, calmer, better. That the white coats in the inner tower were healers of a kind, and the children she sometimes saw being walked through the lower galleries, small and silent and holding their escorts' hands too tightly, were being *helped.*

The order was delivered by a man named Vess, who had a voice like wet paper and a rank that outranked explanation. Eleven crates of records, he said. Experimental logs. Subject files. To be destroyed by fire, witnessed, certified. The Spire was closing a program. The program had never existed. She would sign the certification, and she would never speak of it, and her silence would be noted favorably.

She remembered the smell of that burning. Everyone who had ever burned a library remembered the smell, and it was not the smell of paper, it was the smell of *years,* of ink and vellum and the particular sweetness of a mind's worth of notes going up — because that was what the files were, she understood as she fed them page by page into the incinerator in the Spire's sub-basement. They were people. Each file was a person reduced to a sheaf: subject number, intake date, seal specifications, decay projections, a name in the margin where a clerk had been careless. She read them as she burned them. She couldn't stop herself. It was like watching a drowning and looking away; she had never learned how.

Subject 9. Subject 14. Subject 22, *female, age approx. 7, seal integrity excellent, subject no longer responds to given name.* She burned a child's name. She burned forty names. She burned drawings that some clerk had preserved because they were evidence of success — a child's sketch of a house, done before sealing, and the same house done after, and the after-house had no windows, and the after-child had drawn no one standing in it.

And then there was the last file. The one at the bottom of the eleventh crate, the one that was thicker than the others, the one with the seal specifications in a hand she recognized because she had copied that hand for three years in her apprenticeship — the precise, beautiful, terrible hand of the program's chief artificer, the man who had *designed* the seals, the man whose name was on the title page of every technical manual she had ever studied.

The file was his own.

She had sat with it for a long time. The incinerator roaring. Vess somewhere above her, waiting for the certification. The sub-basement lit orange and shuddering with heat, and her hands — her good hands, her certified hands — holding the life of the man who had built the machinery of forgetting, and the certification form blank in front of her, and the word *destroy* sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She read it. That was her crime, if you wanted the narrow version. She was ordered to burn and she read first, and what she read was this: the chief artificer had applied his own seal to himself. Voluntarily, according to the file, though the word *voluntary* was crossed out and rewritten twice. The seal was the deepest ever attempted — not a removal but an *architecture,* a false life built over the true one like a painted wall over a door. Five years of false memory, specified in advance, page after page of it: a city, a trade, a name. *Marguerite Montrose. Thief. Small charms. Never steal anything you can't sell twice.* She had read the rule in his own handwriting, in the file, five years before she ever heard him say it — because he had said it to her, the first night, bleeding on the collapsed gatehouse, and she had known it, and that was the moment she had frozen, that was the moment she had lied to her superiors with a steadiness that had frightened her.

The file said why, too. Or it started to. The last pages were the artificer's own, a memorandum to whoever found them, and the ink had gone strange, pressed too hard, the hand less beautiful: *if the wards fail, if the stars go out, the seal must break from the inside. The key is the whole life. Do not restore him in pieces. He will know what he built. He will know what it was for.* And then a line she had copied out later, from memory, in her own hand, on a scrap she kept inside her gauntlet for five years until the sweat and the rubbing wore it to lace: *He asked me to make him forget so that he could bear to be the one who opens it.*

She had not burned that file. She had burned the certification instead — signed it, rather, *eleven crates, total destruction, witnessed* — and she had walked out of the Spire with the file inside her coat, against her heart, like a coal, like a brand, and she had told herself she would decide what to do with it later.

Then the wards fell. Then the stars went out. Then the gatehouse came down on a half-dead stranger with a face she had studied by firelight for five years, and there was no *later* anymore. There was only a road north, and a man who did not know he was a key, and a country dissolving behind them like sugar in rain.

* * *

The fire had gone down to a red eye. Marguerite had stopped flinching. Ilse fed it one more stick — the last of the small ones — and made the decision the way she made all her decisions, badly and all at once.

"Wake up," she said.

He surfaced the way he always did, instantly, hand to knife, eyes finding hers in the dark with that flat merchant's assessment that she had long since stopped finding funny. "If you're going to rob me," he said, voice rough with sleep, "at least let me count what's gone first."

"Your ledger's short one item," she said. "I'm returning it."

He went still. She had learned his stillness by now, the particular quality of it — not the stillness of a man afraid, but of a man listening with his whole body, the way she listened to wards. "Ilse," he said carefully, "if this is about the memory I took at the ford—"

"I know you took it. We'll deal with that. This is different." She turned her hands palms-up over the coals, feeling the last of the heat, because she could not say this looking at him. "I'm going to tell you a story. I want you to let me finish it. All of it. Even the parts that are going to make you hate me. Especially those."

"The parts that make me hate you," he said, "are usually the parts you leave out. I've noticed. It's a tell."

Despite everything — despite the dark, despite the road, despite the frozen town they had walked through that morning with its population standing in the fields like a harvest no one had come to bring in — despite all of it, something in her almost smiled. "Then here's the part I left out."

She told him. Not the way she had rehearsed it, not the clean version with the edges sanded off, but all of it, in order, the way you walk a wound: the sub-basement, the smell, the eleven crates, the children's drawings with the windows gone. She told him about Subject 22 and the name in the margin. She told him about Vess and the certification and the word *destroy.* She told him about the last file, and she watched his face while she did it, and what she saw there was not horror, not yet — it was recognition, arriving in pieces, like furniture being carried into a dark room. He knew the sub-basement. She could see him knowing it. He knew the incinerator's roar and the orange light, and when she said *the chief artificer's own hand,* his hand went to his temple, to the place behind his ear where he said the sealed thing lived, and he pressed there as if holding shut a door with wind behind it.

"You read my file," he said. His voice had gone somewhere she hadn't heard it go before. Not the thief's smoothness. Not the gatehouse weakness. Something older, and very quiet.

"I read it until I couldn't," she said. "Then I lied about burning it. Then I carried it for five years. Then the gate fell, and there you were, with your face, saying a rule you wrote in your own blood and ink before I was ever assigned to burn it." She finally made herself look at him. The fire was nearly dead; his eyes caught what was left of it. "You asked me once why I came with you. You said nobody follows a thief north for free. You were right. Nobody does."

"The file," he said. "Where is it now?"

And there it was. The coal, the brand, the thing she had carried against her heart through a court-martial and an exile and a winter that had eaten the stars. She reached into her coat, into the inner pocket where the scrap of lace had been, where the file had been wrapped in oilcloth through everything, through the flooded undercity and the ambush at the ford and the long wrong road — and she brought it out and held it between them, and the oilcloth was dark with five years of her sweat, and her hands were not quite steady, and she let him see that they were not quite steady.

"I never decided what to do with it," she said. "I told myself I was keeping it as evidence. Then as leverage. Then as penance. The truth is I kept it because burning it felt like the second-worst thing I could do, and I'd already done the worst thing, which was burn everyone else." She set it in his hands. It was lighter than it had any right to be. A life, an architecture, a false city built wall by wall — and it weighed less than a charm. "It's yours. It was always yours. I just — I needed you to hear it from me first. Before you read what you asked someone to make you forget."

He didn't open it. That undid her more than anything — that he sat with it in his hands, unopened, in the dark, and looked at her instead, with an expression she had no name for, an expression that was not forgiveness and was not accusation and was maybe the country between them, the one they were both standing in.

"You lied to the Spire for me," he said. "You didn't know me. You knew a page."

"I knew enough." She looked at the coals. "I knew a man who sealed himself to carry something no one else could carry. I knew what it cost, because the file listed the costs. Every one." Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She let it. "I've spent five years telling myself I owed you a debt. It was easier than the truth."

"Which is?"

"Which is that I read those files and I burned them anyway, all but one, and there is no debt that covers forty names." She said it flatly, because it was flat, because it was bedrock. "But if the road north needs me, and the man who built the lock needs an escort to the door he made himself forget — then I can be that. Not as payment. There's no payment. Just as — the thing I do next."

The fire died. In the dark she heard him turn the oilcloth package over once, twice, the small sounds of a man holding his own life and not yet daring to unwrap it. Then, quietly, in the voice of the chief artificer rather than the thief — she heard the difference now, she would always hear it now — he said:

"There's a tower. North of here. I don't know how I know that." A pause. "I know every room in it."

Ilse went cold all over, and not from the winter coming down behind them like a lid.

"Then we'd better reach it," she said, "before whatever else is in your head gets there first."
