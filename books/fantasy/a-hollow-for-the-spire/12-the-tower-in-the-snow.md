## 12. The Tower in the Snow

The snow began an hour before they saw the tower, and it fell wrong.

Marguerite had watched snow all his life — five years of it, at least, five years he could account for, and before that a fog where a life should have been. Snow fell in Vell the way snow falls anywhere: drifting, indecisive, settling on shoulders and awnings like a thing that has forgotten why it came. This snow did not drift. It fell straight down, in silence so complete that Ilse's breathing sounded loud beside him, and each flake seemed to know exactly where it intended to land.

"It's being *aimed*," Ilse said, for the third time since noon. She kept saying it as though repetition might make it less true.

"I heard you."

"Then say something back, because if I'm the only one who thinks the weather is aiming at us, I'd like to be corrected."

He couldn't correct her. The road north had been unspooling wrongness for two days now — villages that appeared on no map, signposts whose arms pointed into empty fields with the confidence of things that had once pointed somewhere real — but this was different. This was not the country forgetting itself. This felt deliberate. Directed. As though something vast had turned its attention toward the two small figures trudging through the white and begun, with terrible patience, to close around them.

And beneath the dread, worse than the dread, ran the other thing: the knowing.

He knew this snow. Not snow in general — *this* snow. He knew that if they crested the next rise they would see the tower's silhouette against the grey, a broken tooth on the ridge, and that its western face was sheared away at the fourth floor and never repaired because the Spire had judged the cost of repair against the tower's remaining usefulness and found the sums wanting. He knew that the sums had been his own work. He did not know how he knew that. He knew it the way you know your own hands.

"There," he said, when they crested the rise, hating himself a little for the relief in it. "The watchtower. We can shelter there tonight."

Ilse looked at him sideways. She had been looking at him sideways more and more, and he understood why. Every day the fog in his head thinned a little further, and every day the man emerging from it was less the thief she'd dragged off the gatehouse and more someone else entirely — someone with a chief artificer's cadence and a catalog of rooms behind his eyes.

"You've never been here," she said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"But you know the tower."

"I know which stair creaks," he said. "I know the third-floor hearth still draws, if the flue hasn't collapsed. I know there's a cache of lamp oil in the cellar behind a false wall, because I'm the one who—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Because someone put it there who wanted a place to hide. And I think that someone was me."

Ilse said nothing for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she didn't brush it off. Then: "Lead on, then. Since you're the one giving the tour."

* * *

The tower rose out of the whiteness like a memory of a building rather than a building itself — which, Marguerite thought grimly, was probably what had saved it. Everything else on this stretch of road had been unwritten or half-written, farmhouses dissolving at their edges, a chapel whose bell tower existed only from certain angles. But the Spire had built its watchtowers to *endure*, and endurance, it turned out, was its own kind of forgetting-proofing. The wards woven into the mortar were dead — he could feel their absence like a missing tooth — but the stone remembered its own shape with a stubbornness that nothing else in this country could manage.

The door was oak, iron-banded, and locked.

Marguerite stood before it in the falling silence and found his hand already moving, dipping into a seam between the second and third stones to the left of the lintel, where his fingers closed around a key that should not have survived twenty years of winters but had, wrapped in oilcloth and wax, cold as a coin from a riverbed.

"You hid a key," Ilse said.

"I hid six keys." His voice sounded far away to himself. "One for each entrance. In case I ever needed to come back in a hurry, or leave in one." He turned the key. The lock gave with a sound like a held breath released. "I don't remember doing it. I remember *that* I did it. There's a difference, and the difference is going to drive me mad."

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and old tallow and, faintly, of something chemical and sweet that made the back of his teeth ache. Lamp oil. Alchemical reagents. The particular smell of a working room left mid-thought.

He lit the lamps without searching for them. That was the worst part, he decided later — not the knowledge itself, but the *ease* of it. His hands moved through the tower's rooms like water finding channels cut long ago. Third floor, hearth, flue intact; he built a fire while Ilse checked the windows with a ward-keeper's thoroughness, her fingers trailing the sills, reading whatever dead traces the old wards had left. The fire caught on the first strike. Of course it did. He had chosen that hearthstone, or the man he used to be had, for its draw.

"Marguerite." Ilse crouched by the fire, thawing her hands, watching him with those level grey eyes. "Say the name out loud. I want to hear whether it still fits you."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Somewhere below them, wind found the sheared western face of the tower and made a low, mournful note of it, like a bottle blown across.

"It fits," he said finally. "That's what frightens me. It fits like a coat fits a mannequin. Well enough that no one questions it. Well enough that *I* didn't question it, for five years." He sat down across from her, hard, the way a man sits when his legs decide the matter for him. "Ilse. When we were in Ashford, and I bought that memory of the inner tower — I told you it was fragments. A white coat. A child's hand. A staircase. I told you that was all."

"You told me that was all."

"It wasn't. I've been holding the rest of it back because holding things back is what I do, it's how I've stayed alive, and because the rest of it is—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. The fire painted the inside of his lids orange. "There were children, Ilse. In the tower. Not students. Subjects. And there was a room on the seventh floor where the seals were made, and I—"

The word *I* snagged on something. He felt it happen — felt the seal, that beautiful, seamless thing, flex under the pressure of the approaching truth the way ice flexes under a boot. For five years the seal had done its work by misdirection: not walling memories off so much as *routing around* them, letting him live a plausible life in the spaces between. But he had spent five years doing the one thing the seal's designer could not have anticipated.

He had spent five years handling memories. Stealing them, carrying them, selling them twice. A thousand foreign recollections passing through his mind like cargo through a port, and every one of them wearing grooves into the same channels the seal used. He hadn't broken the seal. He had *worn it thin*, the way a thief wears thin the lock he means to come back to.

"The seals were mine," he said. It came out flat, factual, the voice of a ledger. "I designed them. I built them. There were eleven of us in the artifice wing and I was the best of us, and when the Spire needed a way to take a mind apart without breaking it — to remove what it held and leave the vessel walking, functional, *deniable* — I was the one who solved the problem." He laughed, and the laugh was awful, dry as the dust on the stairs. "Do you understand? Everyone in this country whose head has a sealed room in it — everyone Corvane's remnant is hunting, every 'unregistered vessel' — they're walking around inside my craftsmanship. I built the cage. I just didn't expect to wake up in one."

Ilse had gone very still. Firelight moved on her face and she let it. "The child," she said carefully. "In the fragment. Whose hand?"

"I don't know." True, and not the whole truth, and he watched her see that and choose, for now, to let it pass. "There were children brought up from the low towns. Families paid, or were told their children had been taken for apprenticeship, or weren't told anything at all. We called them vessels. That was the word we used so we wouldn't have to use another one." His hands had begun to shake, and he looked at them with a kind of clinical disgust, these competent thief's hands, these artificer's hands. "And then the purges came, and the Spire decided the artifice wing was too dangerous to leave scattered about the countryside with its knowledge intact, and someone — Corvane, I think, or whoever Corvane answers to — ordered the wing sealed. All of us. Our own technique, turned on its own makers. Neat. Economical. I would have admired it, once."

"And they dumped you in Vell with a false name and five years of fog."

"Not false." He shook his head slowly. "That's the elegant part, and I hate that I can see the elegance. They didn't give me a new identity. They grew one. Seeded the empty space with a plausible history — an orphan, a talent for small magics, a knack for theft — and let it set. Marguerite Montrose isn't a mask over someone. He's a *casting*, poured into the mold the seal left. Which means—" and here his voice did something he didn't authorize, something cracked and young, "which means I don't know which of us has been living my life. Him or me. Whether the man who felt guilty stealing a widow's wedding was the forgery feeling guilt on schedule, or whether the guilt was the last true thing in me, leaking through."

The fire popped. Above them, the wind changed its note.

Ilse crossed the space between them and took his shaking hands in hers, and her grip was a ward-keeper's grip, steady, grounded, the grip of a woman who had held a collapsing gatehouse together with her will alone. "Listen to me," she said. "I burned files for the Spire. Dozens of them. Names, experiments, people. I obeyed, and I have hated myself for it every day since, and I am *here*, in this tower, in this winter, because of it. So believe me when I tell you the difference between the thing you did and the thing you are." Her eyes held his. "You built cages. Fine. You've spent five years opening other people's heads and handing them back pieces of themselves — badly, for money, with guilt chewing on you the whole time, but handing them back. That's not nothing. That might be the only penance anyone in this story is actually performing."

"That's generous."

"That's accurate. I don't have room in my life for generous." She squeezed his hands once, hard, and let go, and the absence of her grip felt like cold air on a wound. "Sleep. Or pretend to. Tomorrow we go through this tower properly, because if you built it, there are things in it we need before Corvane finds it — and he will find it. Towers that survive erasure get noticed."

She rolled into her bedroll facing the fire. Marguerite sat a while longer, watching the flames, listening to the wind sing against the sheared face of the tower he knew room by room.

Then, quietly, so as not to wake her, he went down to the seventh floor.

* * *

The stairs to the seventh floor were narrower than the others, and he knew — knew without counting — that there were sixty-one steps, and that the forty-third creaked, and that he had once meant to fix it and never had, because a creaking stair on the seal-room approach was a warning system that required no maintenance. The thought arrived whole, unbidden, wearing his own voice. He climbed anyway.

The door at the top was plain grey steel, unmarked, and it opened to his touch — not locked, not warded, because who would steal from a room no one remembered existing?

Inside, the seal-workshop lay exactly as it had been abandoned. Benches in rows. Tools racked with obsessive precision. Jars of reagents gone to crystal and dust. And along the far wall, shelf upon shelf of the seals themselves: small discs of pale material, bone-colored, each one etched with filigree patterns fine as frost, each one a finished, waiting instrument for taking a life apart.

His breath fogged in the colder air of the upper room. He walked the shelves slowly, reading his own signatures in the maker's marks — a tiny spiral within a spiral, stamped into each disc's rim. Eleven artificers in the wing. One mark. This one.

At the end of the last shelf, apart from the others, stood a single seal larger than the rest, mounted in a frame of black iron, and beneath it a placard in his own handwriting:

**PROTOTYPE IX — TOTAL CONTAINMENT. SUBJECT: VOLUNTARY.**

Voluntary. He stared at the word until it stopped resembling language. Someone had volunteered. Someone had lain down on the bench in this room and asked him — asked the man whose hands trembled now in the exact shape of these memories — to seal everything away. And prototype nine, the total containment, the one that left no seams, no routing, no way back in—

Except there was a way back in. There had to be. He had worn it thin himself, five years of stolen cargo grinding the channels wide. But a good artificer always built a second door, a failsafe, something even the Spire's auditors wouldn't find, because a good artificer trusted no one, least of all himself.

He reached out and touched the frame of Prototype IX, and the seal-disc within it shivered — a faint, high note, felt in the teeth rather than heard — and for one instant the workshop was not empty. For one instant he smelled tallow and antiseptic and fear-sweat, and heard a voice, young and terrified and resolute, saying words he couldn't yet hold onto: *do it, do it now, before I lose the courage — and whatever you do, don't tell me my own—*

The vision snapped shut. Marguerite found himself on his knees on the cold flagstones, blood on his lip where he'd bitten it, the great seal silent again on its shelf.

From below, muffled through sixty-one steps and twenty years, Ilse's voice called up the stairwell, sharp with alarm: "Marguerite! What was that? The whole tower just hummed!"

He wiped his mouth. Looked at his red fingertips. At the placard. At the word *voluntary*, which now meant something entirely different than it had a minute ago — because you did not volunteer to forget your crimes, not unless the crimes were the lesser horror, not unless there was something in your own head so dangerous that burying it was mercy.

Or unless you were hiding the key where no inquisitor would ever think to search.

"Nothing," he called back, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. "It's all right. I found the workshop."

A pause. "And?"

He stood. His legs held. Outside the narrow window, the aimed snow fell straight as plumb-lines, and far to the north, past the ridge, past the drowned causeway, past everything, something waited that his own hands had helped to build and his own mind had been built to forget.

"And," he said, "I think I need to tell you who sealed my memory. Because it wasn't the Spire, Ilse. The order was theirs. But the hand on the bench—" He swallowed the taste of blood and truth together. "The hand was mine. I did this to myself. And I'm starting to understand why."
