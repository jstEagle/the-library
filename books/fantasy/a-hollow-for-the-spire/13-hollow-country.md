## 13. Hollow Country

*Ilse*

The road down into the valley had been singing for an hour before they saw the town.

Ilse noticed it first as a wrongness in her teeth — a hum like a struck tuning fork lodged behind her molars, rising through the soles of her boots with every step. Ward-song. She had kept wards for eleven years and been court-martialed out of them, and she knew every note a ward could sing: the bright triad of a healthy border, the flat drone of maintenance, the thin shriek of failure. This was none of those. This was low and slow and satisfied, the sound of something enormous feeding.

"Marguerite." She put a hand on his arm. "Do you hear it?"

He had gone very still on the frozen ruts of the road, his breath hanging in the grey air. He was looking past her, down the long slope to where the valley opened like a cupped hand, and his face was doing something she had never seen it do. Not fear. Not the quick merchant's calculation he wore like armor. Something older and more terrible — recognition, the kind that comes from below memory, from the body's own archive.

"Ilse," he said quietly. "How many people lived here?"

She followed his gaze. The town sat in the valley floor the way towns do — a church spire, a mill wheel stopped mid-turn, chimneys, crooked lanes, smoke-stained roofs. Smoke still curled from two of those chimneys. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the people.

They were everywhere. In the fields, in the lanes, at the well, at the doors of their houses. A woman stood at a wash-line with a wet sheet half-pinned, her arms raised, her face turned up toward a sky that had not held stars in weeks. A man knelt in the frozen turnip rows with his hands buried to the wrist in black earth. Three children stood in a ring around a puddle that had iced over, holding hands, mid-game, mid-laugh — their mouths open around laughter that would never finish. None of them moved. None of them shivered, though Ilse's breath froze on her scarf and her fingers ached inside her gloves. They breathed. She could see the small fog of it, each one, rising and falling in perfect unison, like a tide.

"They're alive," she whispered.

"They're empty." Marguerite's voice had gone flat, stripped of everything. "Look at their eyes."

She didn't want to look. She looked anyway. The woman at the wash-line had brown eyes, ordinary eyes, and they were looking at nothing at all — not unfocused but *uninhabited*, windows in a house from which all the furniture had been carried out. Behind them, deep down, something flickered. A light going about its work. The ward-song hummed up through Ilse's boots, patient and immense.

"The harvest," she said. "This is what the harvest looks like."

"Now you know." Marguerite did not step closer to the town. He stepped back, one careful pace, as if the valley were a fire. "The Spire never killed anyone, Ilse. Killing wastes the vessel. They just take everything out and leave the shell standing, and the shell keeps breathing because breathing doesn't require a soul. It only requires lungs." His voice caught, and he covered it with coldness. "We should go around. North along the ridge."

"Go *around*?" She heard her own voice crack against the word. "There are four hundred people down there—"

"There are four hundred *shells* down there. There is no one to help."

"You don't know that." She was already unshouldering her pack, already kneeling in the snow, pulling out her kit — the chalks, the iron filings, the little brass compass that had been the last thing they let her keep when they took her commission. "I've held wards my whole life. If I can find the anchor point, the feeding line, I can pinch it. Slow the draw. Maybe wake them—"

"With what?" For the first time since she'd known him, Marguerite raised his voice. It came out raw. "Ilse. Listen to me. I know how this works. I *know*. Whatever you think you remember about keeping borders, this isn't a border. This is a tapeworm. You can't pinch it without—" He stopped himself, jaw tight.

"Without what?" She stood up slowly. "Finish the sentence, thief."

He looked at the town. At the children in their ring around the ice. When he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Without giving them something to fill back up with. And we don't have anything. We have a bag of charms and half a loaf and a road to reach, and if you walk into that valley you will stand there until you freeze, because you will not be able to stop trying, and it will not work, and it will take you too."

* * *

*Marguerite*

He had lied, and she had seen him lie, and that was the worst part — worse than the valley, worse than the shells with their synchronized breath.

Because it wasn't entirely a lie. It was a lie wrapped around a truth so heavy he could barely lift it: he knew exactly what that humming was. He had helped design it. Somewhere beneath the seal in his head, under five years of stolen charms and false names, a man in a white coat had drawn the schematics for the great slow harvest — had calculated how much memory a vessel could hold before the vessel collapsed, had specified the resonance frequency that would keep four hundred bodies breathing while four hundred lives were piped north into the Spire's reserves. That man's hands were Marguerite's hands. That man's handwriting was on the drawings. He didn't remember it. He remembered *around* it, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be.

And now Ilse was looking at him across the snow with her ward-keeper's gray eyes, and she said, "You're lying to me."

"I'm protecting you."

"No." She shook her head once, deliberate. "You're lying. I've watched you lie to buyers for a month. Your left hand does a thing — you rub the scar on your thumb. You're doing it now." Her breath smoked between them. "So try again. What aren't you telling me?"

The wind came down off the ridge and moved the frozen wash-sheet on its line. The woman's arms stayed raised. Marguerite found he couldn't look at her anymore; the sight of her went through him like a splinter working toward the heart.

"It doesn't matter," he said.

"It matters."

"It doesn't *matter*, Ilse, because knowing won't change what's true! The only thing that changes anything down there is—" He bit it off.

"Is," she repeated softly.

He closed his eyes. When he opened them she had not moved, had not blinked, and he understood with sudden clarity that he had lost this fight some time ago — perhaps the day she'd dragged him off the gatehouse, perhaps years before either of them was born, in whatever ledger the world kept of debts between people who refuse to name them love.

"The key," he said. "The Door needs a key. I told you that much. What I didn't tell you is that a key this size can't be carried — it has to be *paid*. Memory for passage. That's the old law, the law underneath the wards. The Door doesn't open for coin or blood or promises. It opens for what you give up." His voice steadied itself on the shape of the truth, the way a hand steadies on a banister. "And a whole town's harvested life — that's a payment. That might be enough payment to open the Hollow Door for everyone still inside this country. But it's locked in that feeding line, flowing north to the Spire's reserves, and I don't know how to redirect it, and even if I did—" 

"Even if you did," Ilse finished, "it wouldn't be yours to spend."

"It wouldn't be mine," he agreed. "That's the point. That's always the point. Everything I have ever taken belongs to someone else. I'm a courier, Ilse. A courier who steals. And couriers don't get to open the packages."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Down in the valley the children stood in their ring, mouths open around unfinished laughter, and Marguerite thought: *I built the arithmetic that keeps them standing.* He thought: *there is a version of me, sealed away, who could look at them and feel nothing at all.* He thought: *which one am I?*

Ilse picked up her pack. Not to leave — he saw that immediately, the set of her shoulders, the way she settled the straps like a soldier taking up a post. She picked it up and walked three steps down the slope toward the town and stopped, and turned, and the words came out of her the way water comes through a cracked dam — not fast, but with the full weight of everything behind it.

"I spent six years after the court-martial telling myself duty was a costume I'd been foolish to wear. That obedience was the real country and I'd been exiled from it, and exile meant freedom." Her voice didn't rise; it sank, which was worse. "Then the stars went out, and the wards fell, and I learned that the obedience I gave my life to had been harvesting *this* the entire time. Four hundred people in a valley, standing in the snow like laundry on a line, so that a tower in the capital could have full shelves." She pointed down the slope, arm steady. "So no, Marguerite. I am not walking around this valley. I don't care if it costs us the Door. I don't care if it costs us the *road*. Some things are cheaper than becoming the kind of person who walks around."

"And if helping them costs the Door?" His voice was quiet. "If pinching that line alerts the Spire, if Corvane finds us because you wanted to play savior to people who are already dead—"

"They're not dead!"

"They're *gone*, Ilse!" And then it was out of him, all of it, the flood he'd been holding back since the gatehouse. "Gone, do you understand? Their weddings, their mothers' voices, the names of their children, the taste of their own cooking — drained north and shelved. Even if you cut the line, even if you woke every body in that valley, you'd be waking emptiness. You'd be performing a resurrection with nothing inside to resurrect. There is nothing down there to save except meat and breath, and I know—" his voice broke and he hated it, hated her for hearing it, "—I know *because I helped build the thing that emptied them*."

The silence afterward was enormous. Snow ticked against snow. Somewhere above them a crow crossed the white sky like a signature on a blank page.

Ilse came back up the slope until she was close enough that he could see the wind-chapping on her cheeks, the exhaustion in the corners of her eyes, and something else — something that frightened him more than Corvane's whole remnant, because it was aimed at him and it was gentle.

"I know," she said.

"You don't—"

"I read the file, Marguerite. The one I didn't burn. I've known since the night I pulled you out of the gatehouse rubble. Artificer-class. Seal division." She said it plainly, without cruelty, and somehow that was crueler than any accusation. "You built the locks. I burned the records. Between us we've got a complete crime." Her mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "The difference is I've made my peace with what I owe. You're still pretending your debt is someone else's handwriting."

"It *is* someone else's handwriting. They took my name, Ilse. They took five years and wrote a thief over the space—"

"And whose schematics were in the drawer?" she asked softly. "Whose initials on the resonance tables? Whoever you were before the seal — he wasn't a victim, love. Victims don't get offices in the inner tower."

*Love.* She had said it the way you'd say *thief* or *ward-keeper*, tossed off, tactical, and it went through him anyway, a hot wire in the cold. He stepped back from it as if from the edge of the valley.

"Don't," he said.

"Don't what?" But her voice had gentled further, and she took one step with him, closing the distance he'd opened. "Don't say the true thing? We've got a darkening country behind us and an inquisitor ahead of us and four hundred people standing in the snow, and you want to negotiate over which truths we're allowed?" She reached out and took his wrist — the left one, the one with the tell — and held his thumb still against the scar. "I am telling you, openly, with witnesses of snow and crows: I will not walk around suffering I can lessen. Not for the Door. Not for the plan. Not even for you. That is who I am. If that makes our bargain worthless, then it was always going to be worthless, and better to learn it here."

Marguerite stood very still. Her grip on his wrist was strong and chapped and warm, and he thought about the sealed room in his skull, and the man in the white coat, and how that man had certainly never been held by anyone who refused to leave.

"If you go down there," he said finally, hoarsely, "you'll need someone who knows the architecture of the harvest line. Where the junction valves sit. How to make it choke without snapping." He swallowed. "Because if you snap it, the backlash kills every shell in the valley. All four hundred, in one pulse. Choking it is slower. Choking takes an artificer."

Her eyes searched his face. "You'd come with me."

"I said couriers don't get to open the packages." He turned his hand over and, before he could lose his nerve, laced his fingers through hers. "But nobody said anything about rerouting the mail."

* * *

*Ilse*

They went down into the valley together as the light failed, which Ilse would remember afterward as the moment the bargain between them died and something else, unnamed and heavier, was born in its place.

The ward-song grew until it filled her skull. Up close she could finally parse it — not one sound but thousands, each harvested life a single sustained note, braided into a chord so vast and contented that it seemed less like theft than like music. That was the horror of it, she thought. The Spire had always understood that atrocity performs best in harmony. No one screams in a chord this beautiful.

The people did not react to them. She passed within an arm's reach of the woman at the wash-line and the woman's eyes slid across her like light across glass. The sheet in her raised hands had frozen stiff, a sculpture of interrupted labor. Ilse touched her shoulder — gently, uselessly — and felt warmth under the cloth. Warm skin. Steady heartbeat. Breathing in time with every other shell in the valley, a tide of lungs.

Marguerite knelt at the wellhead in the center of the village square, gloved fingers pressed to the stone, eyes half-closed. She watched him listen the way she herself had once listened to border wards, and realized he was doing it from memory — from the sealed room, from the man he'd been — and that every second of it cost him something visible.

"Junction's under the chapel," he murmured. "Of course it is. They always liked the irony." He looked up at her, and in the dying light his face was stripped to its foundations — no charm, no merchant's gloss, just a tired man in the snow about to commit treason against his own designs. "Ilse. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does, you run north. You don't stop. You don't come back for me. You carry what I told you about the Door and you finish it alone." His hand found hers again in the blue dusk. "Swear it."

Down in the valley the four hundred breathed together. Above them the sky was a lid of iron. And somewhere far to the south, Ilse knew without knowing how, men in flooded cellars were reading their names by candlelight.

"Swear it," Marguerite said again.

"I swear," she lied — and squeezed his hand, and led him toward the chapel, where the harvest sang.
