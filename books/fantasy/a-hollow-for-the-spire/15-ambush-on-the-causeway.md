## 15. Ambush on the Causeway

The causeway appeared out of the drowned country like a spine out of flesh.

Ilse had walked it once before, in another life, when she still wore the grey coat of a ward-keeper and the wards along the Mere still held. It had been a road then, raised above the marsh, paved with stones laid down by engineers who believed in permanence. Now the water had come up around it on both sides, black and patient, and the stones were slick with a frost that fell even in daylight, because the winter behind them did not keep hours. It fell when it liked. It fell the way erasure fell — without malice, without attention, the way a hand brushes crumbs from a table.

She counted the arches as they came to them. Eleven arches, her old maps had said, and she had learned long ago not to trust old maps, but the count held, and there was a small comfort in that: eleven arches, and at the twelfth span the causeway climbed to a stone island where a toll-house had once stood, and beyond it the road went north into the hills, and the hills went to the Hollow Door.

"Twelve," Marguerite said beside her, and she realized he had been counting too.

"You remember the maps?"

"I remember the road." He said it lightly, the way he said most things, but she heard the seam in it. He remembered the road the way a man remembers a scar — not because he studied it, but because something had cut it into him. She did not press. She had learned that about him in the weeks since Vell: press the seal too hard and it frayed, and when it frayed, he went somewhere she couldn't follow, his eyes fixed on the middle distance, his lips moving around a word he didn't know he knew.

The water on either side was not moving. That was the wrongness she kept circling back to, the thing that made her hand drift to the ward-knife at her hip every hundred paces. Marsh water breathed. It shifted with wind, with the passage of fish, with the slow tides of the deep fen. This water was still as poured glass, and under its skin, when the light came down through the torn sky at the right angle, Ilse could see shapes. Rooftops. A church spire, leaning. A whole town, drowned but not ruined, standing under the flood with its windows dark and its doors shut, as if the water had been poured over it like wax over a finished letter.

"Don't look at it," she said.

"I'm not looking at it," Marguerite said, looking at it.

* * *

They made camp at the toll-house because there was no choice. The causeway was a day's crossing at least, and the winter behind them had eaten the last village on the map two nights ago — not burned it, not flooded it, but *unmade* it, the houses going soft at the edges like wet paper, the names of the streets lifting off their signs and drifting upward as grey motes. They had walked out of it at dawn with the smell of forgetting in their noses, a smell like cold iron and old paper, and Ilse had not slept since.

She built no fire. Marguerite sat in the toll-house doorway with his back against the jamb, turning a charm between his fingers — one of the last he had, a widow's wedding from Vell, wrapped in red thread. He did that sometimes now. Held the stolen things and looked at them like a man counting debts.

"Say it," she said.

He glanced up. "Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not saying since the drowned town. You've been carrying it in your shoulders since the drowned town."

The corner of his mouth moved — not quite a smile, the ghost of the market-charm smile he wore like a coat. "You'd have made a good thief, Ilse. You read faces."

"I read faces for a living. It was the whole of my living, near enough. Reading faces and knowing when the wards were about to fail." She sat down across the doorway from him, knees up, knife across her thighs where it had lived for three weeks. "Say it."

He was quiet a while. The frost ticked on the stones. Far out in the drowned town, something shifted — not moved, *shifted*, the way a sleeper shifts, and neither of them looked at it, by mutual and unspoken agreement.

"The key," he said at last. "The pieces. I told you they were scattered in the buyers' heads — every charm I ever sold, a fragment of the Door's lock, seeded out across five years of small thefts." He turned the red-wrapped charm over. "I've been counting them. The ones I remember. There's a gap, Ilse. A big one. The first year, maybe the first two. I can account for maybe sixty charms. My ledger — the one in my head, the one I built so I'd never steal anything I couldn't sell twice — it starts in the spring of the year the stars went out. Before that, nothing. Before that, Vell, and a room over a dyer's shop, and a name I gave myself."

"And you think the missing pieces—"

"Are in the part of me that was sealed." He said it evenly, and she hated him a little for how evenly he said it, and admired him for it too. "Corvane won't want the charms back off the buyers' heads. He can gather those at his leisure, and he's had time. What he can't gather is what's behind my seal. So he doesn't need to catch the key. He needs to catch *me*."

"Then we don't sleep," Ilse said. "And we cross the causeway at first light, and we're through the hills before—"

She stopped. Because Marguerite had gone very still, the way he went still when a memory surfaced, except this was different. This was the stillness of a man hearing a sound he knows.

Out on the water, the frost was falling upward.

It came off the flood in fine bright motes, rising instead of sinking, and where it rose the water's skin clouded, and under the clouding Ilse saw the drowned streets begin — not to move, but to *arrange*. Doors turning on their hinges to face the causeway. Windows lighting, one by one, with a light that was not lamplight and not memory and not anything she had a name for, though she had spent her whole life naming lights.

"Ward-keeper," Marguerite said quietly. It was the first time he'd used her old title, and he used it now the way you'd hand someone a blade. "How long do we have?"

She was already on her feet, reading the water the way she'd read the wards of Vell's gate for eleven years, reading the pattern of the rising frost, the spacing of the lit windows. "They're not here yet," she said. "They're using the town. The drowned dead — whatever's left of them — the Spire's remnant can wear them like gloves. It's a scrying. They're finding us." She looked east, where the causeway ran back toward the drowned country they'd crossed, and west, where it ran into the hills. "But scrying needs a focus close by, and a focus needs a body on this road. Which means they're already on this road."

"How long?"

"Until the twelfth arch lights up. When the last window lights, they're here."

Marguerite stood. He had the charm in his fist, the red thread cutting a line across his palm, and for a moment — a long moment, three heartbeats, four — Ilse watched him do the arithmetic she knew he was doing. The causeway was a mile of stone with water on both sides and no cover and no exit. Two of them. One knife, one thief's tricks, and whatever half-waking things lived behind his seal.

"Run for the hills now, in the dark, off the causeway—" he began.

"—is drowning," she said. "The water's not water anymore. You'd go under and the town would keep you. I've seen what it keeps." She had. She did not describe it. "We hold the toll-house. It's stone, it's got a threshold, and thresholds are the one thing the old wards and the new wild magic still agree on. We hold until the light, and then we run the last span in the open, fast, together."

"Together," Marguerite said. He said it strangely — not questioning, not agreeing. Tasting it.

"Together," Ilse said, and went to check the door.

* * *

They came at the hour before dawn, when the frost was thickest and the dark was at its most complete, and they did not come quietly, because they had never needed to be quiet and did not know how to begin.

Ilse heard them first as a wrongness in the frost. The ticking on the stones — that small dry percussion she had listened to for three weeks, the sound of the winter falling — stopped in a moving circle, a circle of silence coming up the causeway from the east, and inside the silence she heard footsteps. Many. Unhurried. The footsteps of people who have already won and are simply arriving.

"Twelfth arch," she whispered.

It was lit. All the windows of the drowned town were lit, and their light stood up out of the water in columns, and in the columns, shapes were walking — not on the water, *in* it, upright beneath the surface, and where they passed, the drowned houses leaned toward them like flowers toward a sun.

"Thresholds," Marguerite said. He was inside the toll-house, both hands flat on the doorframe, and his hands were *burning* — not with fire but with a faint blue script that rose out of his skin like heat-shimmer, letters in no alphabet she knew, and she understood with a lurch that this was the seal, the great work of the Spire's artificers, waking on the surface of him because the thing it had been built to contain was leaning against its door. "The threshold will hold. It was built to hold things like me. Get behind it."

"I'm the ward-keeper," she said. "I hold thresholds. That's the whole of my living, near enough." She stepped out onto the causeway stone, alone, and put her back to the toll-house door, and drew the ward-knife.

They came into the circle of the frostless silence and stopped, maybe forty paces out, and the frost fell upward around them, and she saw them clearly for the first time.

Not soldiers. Not quite. There were perhaps twenty of them, in the grey remnants of Spire livery, and half of them were walking with the loose-jointed patience of the drowned dead wearing borrowed flesh, and the other half were living — she could tell, because the living ones breathed white and the dead did not — and at their center, on a pale horse that should not have been able to stand on water but stood anyway, rode a man in a black coat with a silver collar.

Corvane. She had never seen him, but she knew. Every ward-keeper in Vell had known the name the way you know the name of a disease.

"Ilse Varn," the man on the horse said. His voice carried across the still water without effort, without echo, the way a voice carries in a room where nothing else is alive. "Ward-keeper, third gate, Vell. Discharged. Court-martial pending, indefinitely pending, pending forever, because the Spire that would try you no longer exists." He said it without cruelty. That was the horror of it. He said it the way you read a label. "You are harboring an unregistered vessel. You are carrying the last piece of the Hollow Key. I am authorized to offer you—"

"You're authorized nothing," Ilse said. "There's no Spire left to authorize you."

The man tilted his head, and in the light of the drowned windows his face was gentle, and tired, and utterly without doubt. "The Spire is not a building, ward-keeper. The Spire is a duty. Buildings burn. Duties wait." He raised one hand, two fingers, a small gesture, almost a blessing. "Step aside. You will not be harmed. You were a good keeper — I've read your file. The one that wasn't burned."

And Ilse felt it go through her like a blade of ice: *he knows about the file.* He knows what she kept. He knows why she is here, on this road, in this winter, and he is saying it aloud in front of Marguerite because he wants Marguerite to hear it, because a key turns more easily in a lock that's been shaken.

Behind her, through the door at her back, Marguerite said, very quietly: "Ilse. What file?"

"Later," she said.

"Ilse."

"*Later.*" And then, because she owed him more than a deferral, because she had owed him for eleven years and had been paying it down in installments of silence: "I'll tell you everything when we're through the hills. I swear it on the gate I kept. But right now the only thing I have to tell you is that you run when I say run, and I say run *now* —"

Because the dead had started forward.

They came up the causeway in a line, unhurried, frostless, and Ilse did the thing she had been trained for and had never once done in earnest: she raised the ward-knife and *cut* — not flesh, but the air, the threshold-line of the toll-house extended outward along the causeway stones, a ward drawn in a single stroke, and she spoke the old words of the third gate, the words for *you shall not pass, you shall not pass, you shall not pass*, and the wild magic that had come back into the world heard her.

The causeway lit. Blue script ran down the stones from her feet to either horizon, the same script that burned on Marguerite's hands, and the first of the dead struck it and came apart — not into blood, into *letters*, unravelling into the grey motes of unmade names, and for one breath, one glorious breath, the line held, and the living remnant stopped, and Corvane's gentle face showed something that was almost surprise.

"Ward-keeper," he said, and there was something in it now that was not a label. Respect, maybe. Or appetite. "Hold it, then. Hold it as long as you can."

"Marguerite." She did not turn her head. The knife was shaking. The script was dimming at its far ends already; she was one woman with one blade and the wards of the world were waking wild and half-blind, and she could feel them slipping through her fingers like water. "The hills. The last span. *Run.*"

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are, though." And she heard her own voice go steady, the way it went steady in the gatehouse when the bell broke, the steadiness that was not calm but was all that was left when calm burned off. "You're the key, you idiot. You're the last piece of the Door. You run, and you get through, and you open it, and everyone still inside this country gets out — the frozen towns, the drowned, all of them. That's the whole arithmetic. One of me against all of them." She laughed, short and sharp, and it came out almost like a sob. "It's the best trade I've ever been offered."

"Ilse—"

"*Run.*"

She felt him hesitate — felt it through the door at her back, the way you feel a person through a wall, the heat of him, the terrible pull of him, the thing between them that they had carried for three weeks unspoken and unnamed, the thing that was not a bargain anymore and not yet anything else, the thing that had no name because neither of them dared to give it one. She felt him take one step toward her.

And then the script on the causeway guttered, and the dead came on, and there was no more time for anything.

She held the third gate's words in her teeth and the ward-knife in her fist and she held the line — held it while the script died span by span, held it while the dead broke against her like grey water, held it while she heard, behind her, running footsteps on the causeway stones, going *north*, going *away*, going toward the hills and the Door and the country that no longer existed, and the sound of those footsteps was the worst sound she had ever heard and the best, and she held the line until the knife snapped, and until the words ran out, and until hands — living hands, she was almost grateful for that — took her arms and the frost fell upward around her and the last thing she saw was the dark shape of the hills, and the last thing she heard, faint under the silence, was Corvane's gentle voice, saying:

"Well done, ward-keeper. Now — let's go collect the key."

And the drowned town, under its lit water, turned every window toward the north.
