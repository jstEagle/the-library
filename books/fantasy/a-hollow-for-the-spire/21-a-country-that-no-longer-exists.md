## 21. A Country That No Longer Exists

The Door did not open outward.

Ilse had expected it to — some great swing of iron and starlight into a country beyond the winter, a road south or east or anywhere that was not here — but instead it opened *inward*, the way grief opens: not onto another place but onto everything you had failed to look at directly. Light poured out of it in long slow ropes, like honey off a knife, and where the light touched, the world remembered itself.

She stood at the threshold with her hand still raised from a knock she had never needed to give, and she watched a nation wake up.

The first thing to come home was sound. For weeks the north had been muffled, as though the whole country were wrapped in wool, as though winter had pressed its thumbs into everyone's ears. Now the silence tore along a seam and out of it came birdsong — real birdsong, not the clockwork imitation the remnant had seeded through the dead districts — and beneath it, farther away, a river running where no river had been on any map she'd burned. The Hollow Country's rivers had been among the first things unwritten. They came back all at once, roaring, like men surfacing from deep water who take their first breath as a shout.

Then the light reached the frozen towns.

Ilse saw it happen to the nearest one — Harrowmere, if the old signs could be believed, though the signs had been blank for a month, their letters dissolved back into raw wood. A town of perhaps four hundred souls, standing in the streets and fields exactly as the harvesters had left them: a woman mid-stride with two buckets, a boy on a fence rail, an old man bent over a door he'd been about to open for what might have been years. Snow to their waists. Frost in their eyelashes. Statues of themselves, patient as saints.

The light went through them like dawn going through glass.

The woman with the buckets moved first. Her shoulders dipped, her arms remembered their weight, and the water — which should have been ice after so long — sloshed warm and living against the wood. She set the buckets down very carefully, the way people do when they have woken somewhere they do not recognize, and looked at her own hands, and began to weep without knowing why. Behind her the boy slid off the fence and stood swaying, and the old man pushed his door, and it opened onto a kitchen where a fire leapt up unasked because the house, too, was remembering how to be inhabited.

All across the valley, in every direction Ilse turned, the same small resurrection. Smoke beginning to rise from chimneys that had been cold since before the wards fell. Dogs barking — dogs, alive, muddy, ordinary dogs — racing between the waking figures as if they alone had kept the time while everyone else slept. Somewhere a bell rang out of tune, and no one minded.

And threading through all of it, streaming down out of the Door like snow falling upward: memories.

That was the only way she could describe it. She felt them pass — she couldn't help it; ward-keepers are built porous, that had always been her gift and her disgrace — felt them brush against her mind like fish against a swimmer's legs. Not hers. Never hers. But she caught glimpses as they went home to their owners: a wedding in a cider orchard. A mother's voice singing a lullaby in a dialect that hadn't been spoken aloud in twenty years. The smell of tar and apples. A man's whole childhood in Vell, the good parts and the shameful parts given back together, because Marguerite had never sorted them, had carried everything.

*Everything,* Ilse thought, and put her hand flat against the Door's frame, and the frame was warm.

He had poured himself into the lock. She had stood there and watched him do it — watched him open his hands like a man releasing a bird he'd kept too long — and the last thing he had said, before saying ended, had not been her name. It had been *thank you*, which was worse, because thank-you was a word you gave to strangers and debtors and he had meant it as both.

Now she searched the crowd.

She didn't decide to. Her body decided for her, the way it had decided things her whole life — to lie to Corvane's clerks, to keep one file from the fire, to hold a causeway alone against six inquisitors with a broken warding-stave and nothing else. Her feet took her down off the threshold into the thawing street, and her eyes went from face to face among the waking, and she hated herself for it even as she did it, because she knew — she had known since the cell, since the seal broke, since the night by the fire when he'd told her what giving back would cost — that there was no face to find.

A man does not pour five years of stolen memory plus one name plus one childhood tower plus one white coat into a door and remain the man who walked in.

But she looked anyway. That was the hook the Door had left in her, and she was honest enough, finally, to admit it was also a hook she had hung herself.

* * *

She found him near noon, or what passed for noon under a sky that was still deciding whether to have stars again.

He was in the market square of Harrowmere, helping raise a tent pole. That was all. That was the whole of it: a nameless man holding a pole upright while three farmers argued amiably about the angle of the guy-lines, and laughing — laughing at something the boy from the fence had said, a short startled laugh, like a man discovering the mechanism still worked.

Her heart did the stupid thing hearts do. It leapt. And then it did the crueler thing hearts do, which is to look closely.

It was his face. Of course it was his face; the body stays, the body is load-bearing. The dark hair going grey at the temple, the crooked nose he'd once told her he broke stealing a memory out of a drunkard's head in the flooded undercity — all of it present, all of it intact. But the face wore itself differently now. Marguerite Montrose — the false name, the false man — had worn his face like a mask fitted over something sealed, and behind his eyes had always been that locked room, that vertigo, that terrible careful watchfulness of a man guarding a vault whose contents he feared more than death. She had seen him sleep twice on the road and both times his face in sleep had been worse than his face awake: clenched, braced, a sentry dreaming of siege.

This man's sleeping face, if she ever got to see it, would be nothing at all. This man's face was just a face. He looked *lighter*. There was no other word. As though someone had carried him in from a long drowning and wrung him out, and what remained was less than what had gone into the water and somehow more.

He glanced up — felt her looking, maybe; some habits survive the erasure of everything else — and smiled at her.

Not Marguerite's smile. Marguerite's smile had been a merchant's smile, quick and priced, deployed to sell a charm or defuse a question. This was slower. Unpracticed. It arrived on his face the way spring was arriving on the country: late, unevenly, with patches still frozen, but arriving.

"You're hurt," he said, and crossed to her, and took her arm where the inquisitors' staves had marked her, and turned it gently toward the light. His hands were the same hands. She had to look away from them. "Sit down. There's a woman here — Marta, I think her name is Marta, it came back to her an hour ago and she's been telling everyone — she has a salve made of comfrey and tallow. It smells appalling. It works."

"Ilse," she said. "My name is Ilse."

"I know." He said it simply, without ceremony, and then, after a moment, with a small rueful tilt of the head: "I know it the way you know a word in a language you used to speak. I'm sorry. You said it enough, on the road. Some of it stayed in the walls of me, I think, even after the rest went out."

"Do you remember—" She stopped. Started again, because a ward-keeper learns to finish her sentences or die of them. "Do you remember the fire? What you told me? About the file?"

"The file, no." He guided her to a bench outside the tent — someone had brought bread, someone was boiling something that smelled like actual tea, and Ilse sat down heavily because her legs had decided the day's heroism was spent. "You told me about the fire, afterward. I have your account of it. It's strange," he added, and this he said looking at the square, at the waking town, at the woman with the buckets now laughing and crying in equal measure as her neighbors crowded around her, "it's strange to have a self made entirely out of other people's tellings. You gave me myself in pieces, these last days. You and the Door between you. I'm mostly hearsay."

"That's not funny."

"No," he agreed. "But it's true, and truth is allowed to be strange." He handed her tea. It was terrible and hot and she held it in both hands like a coal. "Ask me what you actually came to ask."

She looked at him. He looked back, and there it was — not the vault, not the watchfulness, but something plainer and harder to bear: patience. A man with nothing hidden has nothing to defend, and nothing to defend turns out to be its own kind of armor.

"Are you still in there?" she asked. "Anywhere. Under the hearsay. Is any part of—"

"Marguerite?" He said the name carefully, like a man testing ice. "No."

The word went into her like a stave-point. She had prepared for it — God knew she'd had miles of road to prepare — and it went in anyway, all the way, and came out her back, and she sat with it transfixing her and drank her terrible tea.

"But," he said, and waited until she looked up, "I know what he chose. I know the shape of the choosing, the way you know the shape of a room you've never entered, from the light under the door. He chose to stay. He chose to give everything, including himself, rather than keep one piece and let the Door stay shut on all of you." He paused. "I don't think I can be sorry about that. I've tried, since I woke up enough to try. I can't manage it. It seems to me the finest thing anyone ever did, and it seems to me it was done by a thief, and it seems to me—" and here the new smile flickered, uncertain, human, "—that I am what was left over when the finest thing finished. And I find I'd like to see what gets built next. Here. In this country that isn't supposed to exist anymore."

"You could leave," she said. "Through the Door. Whatever's on the other side of it now. You're empty — you owe this place nothing. No one would stop you."

"No one would stop me," he agreed. "And there's nothing in me that remembers why I'd want to go. Isn't that remarkable? Everyone else is getting their pasts back today. I'm the only man in the valley with no past at all, and I'm the only one who isn't trying to leave it behind." He drank his own tea. "Besides. Someone has to learn charm-craft properly. Without the theft, I mean. The widow in Vell — the wedding — he kept one, you know. Stole it and sold it back to her as luck and kept a copy, and the copy went into the Door with everything else, and I hope—" he stopped, and corrected himself, and Ilse realized she was watching a man build a soul in real time, plank by plank, out of whatever lay to hand, "—I hope she has it back now. All of it. I'd like to go and see. When the roads remember where they go."

* * *

Evening came, and the stars came with it — shyly at first, a handful in the east like coins dropped in dark water, then dozens, then the whole spilled treasury of them. The wards were gone; there was nothing left to keep magic out, and so magic came home the way the memories came home, in streams, and the sky filled up with it.

Ilse stood at the edge of Harrowmere and did what she had done all her life, which was tend boundaries. Only now the boundary she tended was the edge of the firelight, where the waking town met the dark fields, and what she kept out was nothing more dangerous than the cold. Children ran past her with sparklers someone had conjured from pure returned delight. An old woman was teaching the fence-rail boy a song, and the boy was singing it wrong, and the old woman was letting him, because that was how songs survived.

He found her there. He brought a blanket and, without asking, put it around her shoulders, and stood beside her looking up.

"They'll need ward-keepers again," he said. "Real ones. Half the old craft is in heads like yours — heads that were ordered emptied and weren't. You kept one file from a fire, Ilse. Imagine what else is still buried in this country. Whole guilds' worth of remembering, scattered in disgraced and dismissed people. The Spire tried to erase the keepers and failed, and now the keepers are the map."

"I'm not going back to keeping," she said. Too fast. She heard herself say it too fast.

"No," he said mildly. "I didn't think so. But someone will ask you. Word travels fast on a night like this." He nodded up at the stars. "And when they ask — whatever you answer — I'll be around. If it matters. I seem to be staying."

She turned and looked at him fully then, this man wearing the face of the man she had — loved? The word had been waiting all chapter, all book, all war, patient as the frozen woman with her buckets, and now it woke too, and she let it stand there in her without picking it up. Grief and grace in the same hand. You carried them together or you put both down.

"What do I call you?" she asked. "I can't keep thinking of you as—"

"Nothing," he said. "For now. Names come back too, apparently — the whole country's proof of that. Mine just hasn't been made yet. It feels right to wait for it. To choose it the way he chose, at the end. Deliberately." He smiled the new smile, and above him the stars thickened, and down in the valley the fires of the waking towns burned in a long constellation of their own, answering the sky. "Give me a season. Give me until the roads are sure of themselves. And then — walk with me a way, when I go to Vell? There's a widow there who's owed a wedding, and I'd like to return it in person. Properly. With my hands."

Ilse looked north, where the Door stood open and quiet, pouring its last light into a country that no longer existed on any map and was, tonight, more itself than it had been in a generation.

"Yes," she said.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, beneath the bells and the birdsong and the boy singing wrong, she heard — or thought she heard, or perhaps the Door left it in her ears deliberately, the way it left the search — a sound like a ledger closing. Like a debt, at last, paid in full.

Somewhere south, in Vell, a widow was about to get a visitor.
