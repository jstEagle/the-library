## 22. What He Sold Twice

Spring came late that year, and it came strangely — not all at once, as spring is meant to arrive, but in pieces, like a memory returning to a mind that had been made to forget it.

First the ice on the Ashford millpond went soft at the edges and stayed stubbornly whole at the center for three weeks, so that the children who skated there learned to read the water the way their grandparents had once read weather: a pale ring here, a dark seam there, danger wearing the face of safety. Then the first green came up through the frozen market square of a town whose name no one could agree on — some said it had always been Harrow's End, some said Marrow's Fen, and an old woman with a new-old face wept when she remembered it was both, before the marriage of two families joined them into one word. The name came back to her the way names come back after long illness: suddenly, and with a weight she hadn't known she was carrying.

The country was rebuilding its memory. That was the phrase people used, half-laughing, half-afraid, in the queues outside the granaries and the reopened chapels and the houses where doors stood open now because there was nothing left inside worth locking away from anyone. *Rebuilding its memory.* As if memory were a bridge or a wall, something with stones and mortar, something you could lay your hands against and feel whether it would hold.

In some ways, it was.

* * *

The man who set up his little table at the edge of the Harrow's End–Marrow's Fen market did not call himself anything. When people asked — and they asked less often than you might think, because in a country that had spent a winter being unwritten, a man without a name was only one more thing the cold had taken — he would smile in a way that used up most of his face and say, "I sold my name. It went to a good cause."

They assumed he was joking. He let them.

He was there by the second week of thaw, when the mud was still deep enough to swallow a cartwheel and the traders were laying out their wares on trestles with the particular reverence of people handling things they had feared they'd never see again: real wool, real salt, apples from a cellar that had kept its cold honestly instead of magically. His table was smaller than anyone else's. On it sat a wooden box, and in the box, nested in wool, were small charms.

Not the charms he had once made. Anyone who had lived through the years of quiet magic — the years when Vell's wards hummed so low you forgot they were singing, when a luck-charm cost a copper and contained a stranger's wedding day — anyone who remembered those years might have expected the old trade to come back like a weed. Magic had returned to the country; why shouldn't its oldest black market return with it?

But these charms were different, and the difference showed even to people who couldn't have said how. They were warm without being feverish. They sat in the palm like a bird that had chosen to stay. And they were free.

"Take one," the nameless man said, to everyone, all day long. "Take one. They're paid for."

Paid for. The traders shook their heads over it. A boy of maybe ten, gap-toothed and solemn, stood at the table for most of an afternoon turning a charm over in his hands — a smooth river stone wrapped in copper wire, nothing more to look at — and finally asked what it did.

"It remembers," the man said. "That's all. It holds a piece of somebody's remembering, and if you ever lose yours — if the winter comes back, or anything like it — you put your hand around it and you'll know you were someone. That you loved someone. That someone loved you." He paused. "It won't make you lucky. Luck was always just other people's stories anyway."

The boy took the stone home to his mother, who had spent the winter unable to remember her own sister's face and had woken in March weeping because the face had come back to her overnight, complete, down to the chipped tooth. She walked to the market the next morning to thank the charm-seller, and found him already packing up, and asked him where he'd be next, and he said he didn't know yet. Wherever the forgetting had been worst, probably. There was a list. He kept it in his head, which was, he liked to say, the largest empty room in the country.

* * *

He traveled light. He had almost nothing, and what he had he gave away on a schedule, like a man bleeding out politely.

There was the coat — good wool, Ilse's doing, bought off a trader at the causeway crossing with money Ilse insisted he take because "you can't walk into towns looking like a beggar, people won't take charms from a beggar, they'll think it's a trick." There was the box. There was the ledger.

The ledger was the only thing he owned that he guarded. It was a plain book, cheap paper, sewn binding gone crooked, and inside it was written, in a hand that grew steadier as the pages turned, every memory he had ever stolen and given back — or tried to. Five years of thieving, itemized. The widow's wedding. The cooper's grief for his drowned son. A soldier's last morning before a war nobody remembered fighting. Page after page, each entry crossed through in ink when the return was confirmed, left open when it wasn't.

Most were crossed through. The Door had seen to that, in its strange, sideways mercy: everything he had poured into the lock had not been destroyed but *released*, scattered back across the country like seed, finding its way home to the heads it had been taken from. He had felt them go. Standing before the Hollow Door with Ilse's hand fisted in his sleeve, he had felt five years of other people's lives leave him all at once, and behind them, last of all, his own name — and he had thought, in the final moment before the lock drank him down, that it would feel like dying.

It had felt like being born, if being born were something you could remember afterward.

Some entries remained uncrossed. A handful of buyers had died over the winter, or been harvested by the remnant before Corvane's order collapsed, or simply could not be found in the vast reshuffling of a country that had forgotten its own roads. For those, the ledger stayed open, and the nameless man kept walking.

And then there was the last page. One entry, never crossed through, never offered to anyone.

A wedding. Vell. A widow named Sera Voss, married on a grey morning in the chapel of Saint Aldhelm with rain on the windows and her sister laughing too loudly in the third pew. Marguerite Montrose had lifted that memory from her head on a night of petty thefts five years ago, sold it back to her as a luck-charm, and told himself the rule that had kept him alive: *never steal anything you can't sell twice.*

He had sold it twice. He had sold it a dozen times, over the years, to buyers who wanted the feeling of being loved without the labor of loving. And every time, some part of him had flinched, though he hadn't known why until the tower in the snow, until the seal broke, until he understood that the flinch was the ghost of a conscience he'd been made to misplace.

Now the memory sat in the last page of the ledger, uncrossed, and the widow Sera Voss was somewhere in the drowned quarters of Vell, alive — he had checked, through traders' gossip, through letters carried north — and she did not know that the morning she treasured above all others had spent five years in a thief's pocket.

He kept it. He knew it was wrong. He kept it anyway.

* * *

Ilse found him in June, the way she always found him, which was to say by not looking.

She came down the Harrow road with a ward-keeper's staff on her shoulder and frost still melting off her boots, though frost in June was nearly a courtesy now, a habit the winter was slow to break. She had a new commission — the provisional council in Vell had reinstated her, rank and all, with a ceremony so brief it was practically an apology — and she had taken a month's leave to walk a route she refused to call checking on him.

"You look terrible," she said, dropping onto the bench beside his table.

"I'm told I don't have a face," he said. "Just a place where a face was."

"You look like a man who slept in a barn."

"The barn was very comfortable. The farmer's dog disagreed about my right to be there." He slid a charm across the table to her — she took it without looking, the way you accept bread from family — and watched her turn it in her fingers. "Harrow's Fen," he said. "Or End. The town can't decide. The charm holds the argument. Both names, holding hands."

"That's not how names work."

"It's exactly how names work. Ask any married couple."

She laughed — short, surprised out of her, the way her laughs still were, as if she hadn't fully forgiven herself for having them. Then she looked at the table, at the box, at the line of townsfolk drifting past and taking charms and saying thank you to a man with no name, and her expression settled into the particular gravity she saved for things she'd rehearsed saying.

"You could stop," she said. "You know that. The Door is open. The key is spent. Nobody's ledger is calling you anywhere except your own."

"My own calls quite loudly."

"Then cross it out." She tapped the book where it lay under his hand. "All of it. Every page. Walk away. Come to Vell. They're rebuilding the Spire — not the Spire, the *site*, they haven't decided what goes there — and they want ward-keepers who remember what wards are for, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I could use a partner who knows what memory costs. Someone who's paid it."

He looked at her for a long time. Her hair had gone half white over the winter, along one side, where the causeway water had frozen in it; she'd stopped hiding it sometime in April. Her hands were scarred where the remnant's bindings had burned her, and she moved her left shoulder carefully in the cold, and she was, he thought, the single most honest thing in a country busy learning honesty again.

"Ilse," he said. "Do you know what I am?"

"A thief."

"Do you know what I *was*?"

"An artificer. A builder of seals. A man who helped design the machine that emptied a valley." She said it steadily, without cruelty, because they had done this work together, the saying of it, all through the winter flight and the cell and the breaking. "And a man who chose, at the end, to give it all back. That's also what you were. You don't get to keep only the first part. You especially don't get to keep only the first part when you're the one telling the story."

"And if the story changes?" He turned the ledger toward her, opened to the last page. "If the man who tells it keeps one thing back?"

She read the entry. He watched her read it — the widow's name, the chapel, the rain on the windows — and saw her understand, and saw her not flinch, which was its own kind of mercy.

"You kept her wedding," Ilse said.

"I've kept it since Vell. Before the Door. I poured everything else into the lock and I held this back like a coward holding a coin." He closed the book. "Every charm I give away, I mean. Every one is a debt paid. Except this. This one I stole properly, all the way down, and I've never returned it, and I tell myself it's because I owe her a personal apology, and I know that's a lie. I keep it because it's the only proof I have of what I was willing to do. The worst thing I ever did, and it fits in my pocket, and it's warm."

Ilse was quiet for a while. Down the row, a trader called out the price of early onions as if onions were a miracle, which, this year, they were.

"Then go pay it," she said at last. "Go to Vell. Find her. Give it back with your own hands and hear whatever she says to you — curse you, forgive you, spit on you, all three. And then come find me, and we'll see what's built on the Spire's grave, and whether it needs a thief in it." She stood, shouldering her staff, and looked down at him with the expression he had learned, over one terrible winter, to read as tenderness. "I'm not asking you to be new, nameless. I'm asking you to be finished."

* * *

He reached Vell in the autumn, when the city was scaffolding and argument.

The walls still stood, but the gates stood open now, all of them, day and night — a policy the council had debated for a season and adopted on the grounds that a city which had nearly been erased should probably stop practicing. The flooded undercity had been pumped and was being rebuilt by crews who worked to the rhythm of songs they were inventing as they went, because the old work songs had belonged to people who hadn't all come back. The Spire's stump rose over the eastern quarter like a broken tooth, and around its base someone had planted a garden. It was mostly weeds. It was fiercely tended.

He found the widow's street by asking, and asking again, and a third time, because Vell had changed its own streets in the rebuilding — not erased them, this time, but *chosen* them, lanes widened and renamed by vote, the city editing itself on purpose instead of being edited. There was something in that, he thought, standing in a lane that had been two lanes when he'd last stolen through it. Something the country was learning that he hadn't managed yet.

Sera Voss lived above a dyer's shop, in rooms that smelled of madder and walnut hulls. She was older. Everyone was older; the winter had taxed them all. She answered the door with dye-stained hands and looked at the nameless man on her step with the flat patience of a woman who had survived too much to be afraid of strangers.

"I knew you once," he said. "Under another name. I took something from you, a long time ago, and I've come to give it back."

People had come to her door with strange offers since the Door opened — penitents, prophets, remnant deserters, a man who claimed to be her husband's ghost and was removed by the watch. She folded her arms. "What did you take?"

"Your wedding morning. The rain on the windows. Your sister laughing in the third pew." He held out the charm — the original, the first one, the one he had sold her for a copper and bought back from a dead man's estate two winters later, the one thing from his thieving life he had never let out of his reach. It sat in his palm, warm, humming faintly, like a bird deciding. "It's yours. All of it. It was always yours. I only kept it because I needed to remember what I was capable of, and I don't need that anymore."

She looked at the charm for a long moment. Then at him.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. "From the markets. People talk. They say you give them away, and that they work, and that you have no name." Her eyes narrowed — not unkindly, but thoroughly, the way a dyer inspects cloth for flaws. "They also say you used to sell them. In the bad years. Before."

"I did."

"And mine?" She nodded at the charm in his hand. "Was mine one of the ones you sold?"

"Twice," he said. "Maybe more. I stopped counting, which was the worst thing about me."

Sera Voss was silent. Somewhere below, the dyer's apprentice dropped a skein and swore with impressive creativity. The autumn light came down the lane the color of walnut stain.

"Keep it," she said.

He blinked. "What?"

"Keep it." She unfolded her arms. "I have my wedding back — all of it, since the spring, since the Door. It came home like a bird. I wake up with the rain on the windows and Marta laughing too loud and I don't need a charm to hold it. But you—" She pointed at him, one dye-stained finger, and her voice gentled in a way that undid him more than anger would have. "You gave away everything. Even your name. Even to the Door. And a man can't live on empty, however clean the empty is. So keep it. Not as theft. As a gift. From me. The only man in Vell who attended my wedding twice."

He stood on her step with the charm warm in his hand and found, to his astonishment, that his eyes were wet.

"Come inside," Sera Voss said, opening the door wider. "Tell me your worst thing over tea. I've got all afternoon, and I promise you it isn't worse than mine."

* * *

He stayed in Vell through the winter — the ordinary kind, the kind that merely fell instead of erasing — and in the spring he set up his table again at the edge of the market, in the shadow of the Spire's garden of weeds.

The ledger's last page was crossed through now, in ink, in a steady hand. Not struck out. Marked *returned*, with a date, and beneath it, in smaller letters, a note he had added the night he wrote it and had not shown anyone:

*Given freely. Received more generously than deserved. Some things you steal twice — once from the person, and once from yourself. Only the second theft can be undone.*

The charms on his table caught the light. Above him, in the garden on the Spire's foundations, the weeds were flowering — somebody had identified half of them as rare and saved them, and now the council argued about whether it was a garden or a memorial, and the flowers, indifferent to the debate, went on opening.

A child stopped at the table, gap-toothed, solemn, and pointed at the stone charms in their wool nest.

"What do they do?" she asked, the same question, the eternal question.

"They remember," the nameless man said, smiling the smile that used up most of his face. "Take one. They're paid for."

And in his pocket, warm as a living thing, a wedding he had never attended went on quietly loving him back.
