## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

Midwinter's eve in the Hollow was always a quiet affair. The guild kept no grand observances—there were only three of us left, and one of those was Elder Maren, who considered celebration a form of extravagance and extravagance a form of sin—but we had our small rituals all the same. Bramble-warden Tamsin baked honey bread that tasted mostly of duty. Old Corvin polished the guild's single silver candlestick until you could see your own worried face in it. And I climbed to the boundary stone at the valley's northern lip to watch the stars come up over the ridge, because someone had to, and because I liked it.

That last part mattered more than I ever admitted aloud. In a valley where magic didn't work—where a candle flame sat perfectly still no matter how you coaxed it, where rain fell straight down like it had somewhere better to be, where my own attempts at the simplest cantrip produced nothing but a faint smell of scorched wool and profound personal embarrassment—the stars were the one piece of wonder left to us. They came through. Whatever the wards kept out, they let the sky in.

I used to think that was an accident of design. I would think a great many things before dawn.

* * *

The evening began normally, which is to say it began with Elder Maren telling me, for the fourth time that week, that the boundary stones were not my responsibility anymore.

"You tend them out of habit," she said, watching me pack my kit by the hearth. "The wards are dead, Silas. Have been for years. You're maintaining a fence around an empty field."

"The field isn't empty," I said. "I'm standing in it."

She gave me the look she reserved for my more tiresome arguments—the one that said she agreed with me but wished I'd stop being right about things that made her life difficult—and turned back to her ledger. The Hollow Guild's ledger was a sad document these days: three names, dwindling stores, and a column of ward-stones marked *failing* in Corvin's increasingly shaky hand going back eleven years. Eleven years of writing *failing*. You'd think at some point he'd have written *failed*, but Corvin believed optimism was a professional obligation, and I loved him for it the way you love a man who keeps bailing a boat with a thimble.

Tamsin pressed a heel of honey bread into my hands as I left. "For the walk," she said. "It's cold enough tonight to crack teeth."

"It's midwinter," I said. "Cold is the whole point."

"Cold is never the point. Cold is the punishment for living somewhere beautiful." She said this with real feeling. Tamsin had come to the Hollow forty years ago fleeing something in the lowlands—I never learned what; she had a gift for closing doors on her own history—and she spoke about beauty the way other people spoke about debts.

The climb to the northern stone took most of an hour in the dark, along a path I could have walked blindfolded, and probably had, several times, when I stayed out too long and the moon went down on me. The stone itself stood hip-high, a grey fang of granite carved with the guild's sigil—a hollow circle, which the founders had apparently thought was poetic and which I had always thought looked like a doughnut with delusions. The sigil-grooves still held traces of old silver leaf. When I was a child, Corvin told me the wards used to hum here, a note so low you felt it in your back teeth rather than heard it. He demonstrated once, pressing his palm flat to the stone and humming himself, since the stone had stopped cooperating. It was the saddest magic I have ever witnessed, and I include my own.

I reached the top as the last light drained off the western ridge, spread my cloak on the frost-hard ground, and settled in for the show.

And the stars came out. That was the thing—they came out, one after another, punctual as guildsmen, and for perhaps half an hour everything was exactly as it had been every midwinter's eve of my twenty-six years. The Hunter leaned over the southern crags. The Sisters trailed their long veil of dust. I found the star Corvin had named for me when I was seven—Silas's Star, he called it, though officially it was something with far too many Greek letters in it—and I made my private wish, the same wish I made every year, which was that next year there would be four people at the guild table instead of three.

Then Silas's Star went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. It was simply *gone*, the way a candle is gone when you pinch the wick—one moment burning, one moment black. I actually laughed at first, because I assumed it was me. My eyes did strange things in the cold; I'd once spent a full quarter hour convinced I was being followed by a bear that turned out to be a stump. I rubbed them, waited, looked again.

Still gone.

And then, in ones and twos, in no order I could find—as if each star were making its own private decision, or losing its own private argument—the others began to go out too. A bright one near the zenith. Two of the Sisters. The Hunter's belt, buckled then unbuckled, star by star, like a man undressing in the dark. I stood up. I shouted at the sky, which is not a thing a sensible person does, but I had been raised by a guild whose entire purpose was shouting at things that couldn't hear you, so arguably sensibility was never on the table.

"Hey!" I said. "Hey! Come *back*!"

They did not come back. By the time I had counted to a hundred, the sky above the Hollow held perhaps a dozen stars, scattered and lonely, and by the time I had counted to three hundred it held none at all. None. I lay flat on my back on the frozen ground and stared straight up into a darkness so complete it felt less like night than like a lid. Like the underside of something. Like being inside a closed hand.

I want to be honest about what I felt, because what I felt was not fear. Not at first. What I felt was vertigo—that lurching sensation of the floor tilting, except the floor was the sky, and it hadn't tilted so much as *stopped existing*. Every night of my life, the stars had been the fixed points. You navigate by fixed points. You trust them. And they had gone out like lamps in a house whose owner had decided, without consulting anyone, to move.

I don't know how long I lay there. Long enough for the frost to work through my cloak and into my bones. Long enough to notice, eventually, that the silence had changed quality—not just the absence of stars now, but an absence underneath the absence. The wind had died completely. The pines on the ridge below had stopped their constant dry whispering. Even the cold felt different, a still, held-breath cold, the cold of a room where something has just stopped moving.

And then I felt it—the thing I would spend the rest of the night failing to explain properly to anyone. A pressure. Faint, like the change in your ears when you descend into a cellar. As if the whole valley had, in the space of an hour, become a room with the door shut. As if something enormous had taken a breath and was holding it.

I ran home. There's no dignified way to say that. Twenty-six years old, last apprentice of the Hollow Guild, and I ran down that mountain path like a child running from the dark, honey bread abandoned on the stone, boots skidding on frost, arriving at the guildhall door breathless and half-frozen and pounding on it hard enough to wake Corvin from the light doze he denied having.

"The stars," I gasped. "The stars are *gone*."

Corvin opened the door, looked at the sky over my shoulder, and said, with the calm of a man who has survived forty midwinters in this valley, "Well. That's new."

* * *

Elder Maren took some waking, and took more convincing. She stood in the dooryard in her shawl, squinting upward with the expression of a woman being asked to believe in weather she couldn't feel, and pronounced, after due consideration, that it was probably cloud cover.

"There are no clouds," I said. "There is no *anything*. I watched them go out one at a time. Stars don't do that, Elder. Stars go behind clouds all together or not at all."

"Stars also don't go out one at a time under any circumstances," she conceded, "which is why I'm choosing cloud cover. It's the least alarming explanation available."

"That's not how explanations work."

"It's exactly how explanations work in the Hollow," she said, and there was something in the way she said it—a tiredness, a deliberate flatness—that I noticed at the time and failed, spectacularly, to understand.

Because here is what happened next, and I want it on the record that I behaved reasonably, given what I knew, which was nothing. I proposed riding for the boundary stones—all twelve of them, ringing the valley—to check the wards. Maren said no. I proposed sending word to the ward-keepers' chapter in the lowlands, who still answered our letters, barely. Maren said no. I proposed, at minimum, climbing the bell tower with a lantern and confirming that the darkness extended beyond our little bowl of the world, that this wasn't somehow a local phenomenon, a valley-sized pocket of unusual night. Maren said no to that too.

"The wards are dead," she said, for the second time that day, in the same tone. "Nothing is getting in. Nothing is getting out. Nothing has gotten in or out for a hundred years. Whatever the sky is doing, it isn't doing it *to* us."

"But that's just it," I said, and my voice cracked in a way I hated, "we don't know that nothing gets in. We know that nothing *has*. Those aren't the same fact, Elder. Those aren't even cousins."

Maren looked at me for a long moment across the dooryard, in that starless dark, and I saw something pass over her face that I had never seen there before—or rather, I saw something *leave* her face, some set of her jaw, some old and practiced steadiness, and what remained underneath looked briefly, terribly young. Then she squared herself, the way you square yourself when you've decided to carry something heavy a while longer, and said:

"Go to bed, Silas."

"I'm not a child."

"No," she said. "You're not. Go to bed anyway."

So I went to bed, furious, and lay awake listening to the silence, which was so total I could hear my own blood moving, and somewhere past the deepest hour of the night I got up, quietly, and went to the archive.

Every guild has its archive. Ours occupied the old chapel undercroft, a low stone room that smelled of tallow and mouse and time, where the guild's records went back four hundred years in ledgers and folios and, in the oldest cases, things that were technically books only if you were generous about the definition. Corvin catalogued it badly and lovingly. I had been sorting it, slowly, for six years—an inheritance project, really, begun the winter my mother left and never finished, though I'd have cut out my tongue before saying that sentence aloud to anyone, including myself.

I took a lamp, though the lamp was pointless—in the Hollow, flame burned steady and gave exactly the light it gave, no more, no coaxing, no enchantment—and I started where I always started when something frightened me: with the founding documents. If the valley had a rule, the rule would be written down. The guild wrote everything down. It was the one virtue we had never lost, possibly because it required no magic.

The founding charter was a fat vellum roll, brittle as autumn leaf, sealed in wax the color of dried blood. I had read it before, years ago, in Corvin's company, and remembered it as a dull legal hymn to boundaries and good order. I unrolled it now and read it again, properly, alone, at three in the morning, with the sky outside holding not one single star, and I found—halfway down the third panel, in a clause I swear on my mother's name I had never seen before—a passage that stopped my breath.

*...and in consideration of the invisibility granted herein, the undersigned do covenant and deliver unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one soul, held in trust, renewable upon each centennial turning...*

I read it four times. My hands were shaking so badly by the fourth reading that I had to weight the vellum down with Corvin's inkstone. *One soul, held in trust.* Not a tithe. Not a service. Not a symbolic offering of grain or a pledge of good faith. A soul. An actual soul, delivered, held, *kept*—by whom, for whom, the charter did not say, or rather the charter said exactly what such documents always say, which is the minimum required to make the signature legally binding and the signer morally comfortable.

And beneath the clause, in ink gone brown as old rust, the signatures. I knew them all by heart from previous readings—founders' names, stern and flourished, the kind of handwriting that suggests a person who was certain about things. But at the bottom, apart from the rest, smaller, pressed hard into the vellum as if the pen had been gripped too tightly, was a signature I had never once noticed in all my years of sorting this room.

*Elowen Ashgrove.*

My mother's name.

My mother, who had left the valley twenty years ago on a morning in late spring, who had kissed me goodbye at this same door and said she was going to the lowlands to visit her sister and would be back before the bracken turned, and who had not come back, and about whom the elders had told me precisely nothing except that some people leave and some people don't return and it was best not to ask questions, because questions in the Hollow were like striking flint indoors—you might get a spark, and sparks were dangerous.

I had been nine years old. I had asked questions anyway, for years, quietly, into the archive, into the ledger margins, into the spaces where Corvin's catalogue said *see: unfiled*. And now, at three in the morning on the night the stars went out, the unfiled box gave up its answer.

At the bottom of the chest, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, I found a fragment—scorched at one edge, as if it had been pulled from a fire, or saved from one, or both. A corner of a contract. Heavy paper, foreign stock, watermarked with a device I didn't recognize: a circle of thorns enclosing a single star. And across it, in a formal hand entirely unlike anything the guild ever wrote, the words:

*...one (1) soul, being Elowen Ashgrove of the Hollow, received and held in trust against the renewal of the century, per the covenant of the undersigned...*

The scorch mark had eaten the signatures. Whoever had burned this document had burned the names and saved the terms—which meant, if I was thinking clearly, and I was not remotely thinking clearly, that someone had wanted the terms known and the parties hidden. Someone had wanted a future reader to know *what* was done without knowing *who* had done it. Or someone had wanted the opposite, and the fire had disagreed with them.

I sat down on the archive floor among four hundred years of careful records and held that scorched fragment in both hands, and I understood, in the slow sickening way you understand a thing that rearranges every other thing, what the valley was.

Not a sanctuary. Not a refuge. Not a place the world had mercifully forgotten.

A cage. A purchased cage. Our invisibility—the blessed, boring, suffocating safety I had chafed against my whole life—was rent paid to something beneath the mountain, and the currency of the rent was a person, and the person was my mother, and she had not left. She had been *taken*, or she had *gone*, or—this was the thought that finally broke me open—she had gone *knowing*, walked out that door and down out of the valley with her eyes open, so that the contract would hold and the rest of us would never have to see the bars.

The stars hadn't fallen. The stars had been *switched off*. The wards hadn't failed tonight. The wards had done exactly what they were built to do, one last time, on schedule, at the centennial turning—and what they were built to keep out, or keep in, or keep *unseen*, had included the sky itself.

I don't remember walking back up the stairs. I remember standing in the guildhall doorway at dawn, the fragment in my hand, watching a sun rise into a sky that was still, impossibly, empty—no stars, and now no darkness either, just a blank pale nothing from horizon to horizon, a sky like unwritten paper—and I remember Elder Maren coming down the hall in her dressing gown, seeing my face, and stopping as if she'd hit glass.

"You found it," she said. Not a question. She knew what I'd been doing. She had maybe always known.

"Elder." My voice came out strange, flattened, like a voice heard through the wards themselves. "What did Grandmother sign?"

And Maren looked at me with that young, unsteady face of hers showing through, and said the only honest thing anyone in the Hollow had said to me in twenty years:

"Not me, Silas. Ask Corvin. Corvin remembers."

Corvin, who had been humming at dead boundary stones for forty years. Corvin, who named a star after me. Corvin, who was already awake, already dressed, already standing in the kitchen with his hat in his hands and a look on his face like a man who has been waiting all night for someone to finally ask.

He set the hat down very carefully on the table, the way you set down a thing you may need to pick up and run with.

"Come sit down, lad," he said. "There's a story, and it's worse than yours."
