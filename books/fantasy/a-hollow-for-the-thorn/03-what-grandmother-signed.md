## 3. What Grandmother Signed

Corvin's story took all night and most of a pot of barley tea, and it was worse than mine, exactly as promised.

He told it in the old way, the way guild stories were meant to be told—slowly, with pauses for refilling cups, so that the listener had time to dread each part properly before it arrived. He told me about his own master, Aldous Fen, who had been the guild's archivist before Corvin was anything at all. He told me about the night in Aldous's study when he was seventeen and curious and had gone looking for the founding charter because a boy will do that sort of thing, and Aldous had caught him with it open on the desk.

"He didn't shout," Corvin said. "That's what I remember most. I'd expected shouting. Instead he just looked at me for a long moment and said, *Now you know why we sweep the stones.* And then he made me tea, of all things, and we sat there until dawn while he explained to me what I'd read."

"And you never told anyone?"

"Who would I tell? The valley has three hundred souls in it, Silas, and every one of them sleeps better believing the wards are a fence against wolves." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Aldous used to say the truth was like a lantern in a hay barn. You can carry it carefully your whole life, or you can drop it once."

Outside, the sky stayed dark where stars should have been. I kept not looking at it, which is its own kind of looking.

"What did you read?" I asked. "Exactly."

Corvin set down his cup. "Come to the archive," he said. "It's better if you see it yourself. Some things a man shouldn't have to take on faith."

* * *

The Hollow Guild's archive occupied the lowest floor of the guildhouse, which was itself the oldest building in the valley, which meant the archive was older than memory. To reach it you went down two flights of stairs that complained differently underfoot—the first flight creaked, the second groaned—and through a door whose lock Corvin opened with a key he wore on a cord around his neck, next to his heart, though he'd deny it if you asked.

Inside smelled of paper and cold stone and, faintly, of thyme, because Corvin believed dried thyme discouraged damp and had been tucking bundles of it into every corner for forty years whether it worked or not. Shelves climbed the walls into shadow. Boxes upon boxes, ledgers upon ledgers, the whole sedimentary record of a guild that had once mattered and now mostly swept.

Corvin lit lamps. Three of them, spaced evenly, and then a fourth he carried with him as we walked, because he was that kind of careful man.

"The founding documents are here," he said, stopping at a shelf near the back, behind the tax records from an era when the guild had apparently collected taxes, which I found almost harder to believe than the rest of it. "And here—" he drew out a flat box wrapped in oilcloth, "—is the part that matters."

He unwrapped it the way you unwrap something you've unwrapped many times but still respect: corner by corner, no rushing. Inside lay a sheaf of vellum pages, browned at the edges, tied with a ribbon gone the color of weak tea.

"One hundred years ago," Corvin said, "the valley was dying. Not dramatically—nothing in this valley has ever been dramatic—but dying all the same. Bad harvests, sick herds, children born thin. And the guild of that era, your grandmother's generation, went looking for help in the only direction left to them. Down."

"Down?"

"Under the mountain." He tapped the floor with one toe, twice, as if knocking. "There are courts beneath the world, lad. Everyone knows the stories and everyone agrees they're stories, right up until their harvest fails. Your grandmother—Maren Ashgrove, guild-mother, a woman I remember as being approximately the size and temperament of a well-fed badger—led a delegation down through the old gate. Nine of them went. Six came back."

I looked at the vellum. My hands, I noticed, were very steady, which felt like someone else's hands.

"Read it," Corvin said gently. "I'll only spoil it."

I untied the ribbon. The first page was a preamble, written in a formal hand with flourishes that tried very hard to be beautiful and succeeded in being ominous:

*Be it known to all parties, mortal and otherwise, that the undersigned do enter into compact freely offered and freely taken...*

The second page was where it stopped being preamble.

*In consideration of the Court's favor, namely: that the Valley of the Hollow shall pass unseen, unmarked, and unremembered by all powers beyond its borders; that blight shall not find it, war shall not seek it, and magic shall not enter it, save by invitation of the Guild;*

*In exchange, the Valley offers in pledge: one soul, held in trust, renewable each hundredth year upon the winter solstice...*

"Held in trust," I said. My voice came out strange. Flat, like a coin dropped on stone.

"A courtly way of saying *kept*," Corvin said quietly.

I turned the page. There was more—clauses about boundaries and renewal, about penalties, about terms I couldn't yet parse—but my eyes had already jumped ahead, hunting, the way eyes do when they already suspect what they'll find. Past the list of signatories. Maren Ashgrove's name was there, signed with a fierce downward stroke, and six others, and then—

Then the pledge itself. Not a name of a person to be chosen later. Not a slot to be filled. A specific soul, named in ink a century old:

*Elowen Ashgrove.*

The lamp flame didn't move. Nothing in the room moved. Somewhere above us, the guildhouse settled against the cold with a sound like a long sigh, and I stood in the archive holding a piece of vellum and understood, in one white-hot instant, that my mother had not left.

Twenty years ago, Elowen Ashgrove had walked out of our house on a grey morning in late autumn and never come back, and the whole valley had nodded knowingly and said *these things happen*, and my father had grieved the way quiet men grieve, badly and alone until he didn't have to anymore, and I—I had been seven years old, and I had waited at the window every evening for a month, and then I had learned to stop waiting, because that is what children in safe valleys learn to do.

She hadn't left. She'd been *collected*. She'd been the payment, called due on the solstice of some year I'd been too young to mark, and the contract had been sitting down here in the dark the entire time, patient as stone, naming her in beautiful handwriting.

"Silas." Corvin's hand was on my shoulder. When had that happened. "Sit down, lad."

"I'm fine."

"You're white as the vellum. Sit down anyway."

I sat. On a crate of thyme, as it happened, which crushed a little and perfumed the moment absurdly, and I think that small ridiculousness is the only reason I didn't come apart entirely.

* * *

Here is what I remembered afterward, unbidden, the way grief sends its invoices:

My mother teaching me letters by writing them in spilled flour on the table, and how she always wrote the *E* last, saving it, she said, because every word worth writing had an E in it somewhere and it was good to end on something certain.

My mother humming while she mended, a tune I could never find again anywhere, and when I asked her once where it came from she'd smiled in a way I now understand had been sad, and said, *"Under the mountain, love. It followed me out."*

Wait. No. That wasn't right. It hadn't followed her out. It had been hers before, or—

Or she had known. Even then. Twenty years ago, walking out our door with her shawl pinned wrong because her hands were shaking, she had known exactly where she was going, and she had hummed that tune so I would carry a piece of it without knowing I was carrying anything at all.

My mother, the last morning, kneeling to button my coat even though I was seven and perfectly capable of buttons, taking longer than buttons require, and saying, "You'll look after the stones for me, won't you? All of them. Even the ones nobody remembers."

I had thought she meant the boundary markers. I had thought it was a mother's ordinary request.

*Even the ones nobody remembers.*

She had known which stones hid what. She had grown up the daughter of Maren Ashgrove in this same guildhouse; she had read these same documents, or been told, or guessed. And when the debt came due, she had walked out the door herself rather than let them draw lots, rather than let them take some farmer's daughter or miller's son who'd never consented to any of it.

She hadn't been stolen. She'd been *volunteered*. By blood, by name, by a signature her own mother had set down a hundred years ago—and she had gone quietly, so the valley would keep sleeping, so I would keep sleeping, so that no one would ever have to know what their safety cost.

I put my face in my hands and the archive swam.

* * *

When I could see again, Corvin had the contract spread across the reading table and was standing over it with his spectacles low on his nose, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hates.

"There's a renewal clause," he said. "Page four. Read it aloud. I want to hear it in your voice, because mine will shake."

I read it aloud. My voice shook too, but I got through it:

*"Upon each hundredth year, the pledge shall fall due anew, and the Guild shall render up a soul of equal weight, freely given, in place of that held; failing which, the Valley's concealment shall lapse, and the Court's claim shall extend to whatsoever the Guild holds dear."*

"Equal weight," I repeated. "What does that mean?"

"Soul-weight. Courts don't count years, they count... substance. What a person is made of, underneath." Corvin took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, though they weren't dirty. It's what his hands do when his mind is elsewhere. "Your grandmother's bargain bought a century. That century ended—" he looked up at me, and his eyes above the spectacles were terribly kind, "—three nights ago. Midwinter's eve. The stars didn't go out because the wards failed, Silas. The wards failed because the bill came due, and there was no one here to pay it."

"No one volunteered."

"No one knew. Aldous died with the secret; I kept it; and then there was only me sweeping stones and telling myself the next generation would sort it out." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Forty years of telling myself that. And now the Court has simply reached in and taken what it was owed—or is in the process of taking it—and the valley sits in the dark wondering why the sky looks wrong."

"But my mother." I touched the name on the page. The ink was cool. Everything down here was cool; nothing in the archive had been warm in a hundred years. "If she was the pledge—if she was *held in trust*—then she wasn't consumed. She's not gone. She's..."

"Kept." Corvin said it softly. "Yes. If the old law holds, and Court law is nothing if not old, then Elowen Ashgrove is alive beneath the mountain. Unaged, likely. Undisturbed. Held in trust, exactly as written." He paused. "Which means, lad, that the question in front of us isn't whether your mother can be saved. It's whether anyone can get to her, and what the Court will demand when they catch them trying."

I stood up. The crate of thyme exhaled its crushed scent around me, and I found that the grief I'd been drowning in had changed texture somehow—it had cooled, and hardened, and taken an edge. Grief curdling into purpose, if purpose can curdle. I suppose anger is just grief that's decided to stand up.

"Then someone gets to her," I said.

"Silas—"

"She went quietly so the valley wouldn't have to know. She paid for three hundred people who never learned her name beyond 'that Ashgrove woman who left.' She's been in the dark under a mountain for twenty years because of a signature her mother set down before she was born." I picked up the contract, carefully, the way Corvin handled it, because I was learning. "Nobody's coming for her, Corvin. Nobody knows. Which means the answer to your question is yes—someone goes, and it's me, and I'd like to know everything this archive knows about the road under the mountain before dawn."

For a long moment Corvin just looked at me. Then he did something I'd never seen him do: he took off his hat entirely, held it against his chest, and bowed his head briefly, like a man at a graveside.

"Aldous told me once," he said, "that someday the bill would come due and there'd be no one left with the stomach to pay it. I've been dreading that day for forty years." He straightened, and set the hat back on, and there was something new in his face—something that might, in a younger man, have been called hope. "I'm too old to walk to the mountain. But I'm not too old to empty this archive onto a table and tell you every story in it. Sit down, lad. We'll need the whole night."

We worked until the lamps burned low. Boundaries and gates, toll-roads and rules, names of exiles who traded with the Court and lived—which is to say, survived, which is not the same thing. And near the bottom of the last box, wrapped in its own oilcloth, Corvin found a ledger page he stared at for a long time before handing to me without a word.

It was a record of debts. Guild bookkeeping, decades old. One entry, dated twenty years past the autumn my mother left, in Aldous Fen's precise hand:

*Elowen Ashgrove — rendered up per compact. Witnessed. Paid in full.*

And beneath it, smaller, added later in different ink, in a hand I recognized as Corvin's own young scrawl:

*Not paid. Deferred. Find the difference.*

I looked up. "What difference?"

"That," said Corvin, "is the question that's going to kill us both. But there's a woman I've heard of—a ward-keeper, royal-trained, disgraced, currently making a nuisance of herself somewhere on the north road—who asks exactly that kind of question for a living. And if anyone outside this valley knows how Court arithmetic works..."

He trailed off, listening. So did I. Because far overhead, faint through two floors of old stone, came a sound neither of us had ever heard in the Hollow before, and would not mistake for anything else:

Someone was knocking at the guildhouse door.
