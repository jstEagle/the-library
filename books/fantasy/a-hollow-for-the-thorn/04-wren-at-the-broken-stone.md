## 4. Wren at the Broken Stone

Wren had been walking for eleven days when the valley finally let her in.

She hadn't expected it to be difficult. The Hollow was famous, in the way small places are famous—famous for being unremarkable. A green fold of land between two grey hills, a river that ran slow and polite, farms that produced exactly enough and never a bushel more. The royal surveyors had mapped it twice and found nothing worth mapping either time. No ley lines. No crossings. No history of anything stranger than a dispute over a millpond.

But wards, Wren knew, were not walls. Walls kept people out. Wards kept *things* out, and things could be very particular about who they allowed to approach their door.

So it was that on the twelfth day, as she crested the last ridge before the valley mouth, she felt something push back against her like a hand flat against her sternum. Not hostile. Not even unfriendly. Just a firm, patient *no*, the way a good sheepdog refuses a stranger at the gate without ever barking.

She stood there for the better part of an hour, testing it. She tried approaching from the north. The no moved with her. From the east, along the river—the same. It was elegant work, whoever had woven it. Old work, too; she could feel the age of it in the way the threads sat, dense and settled, like a tapestry that had hung so long in one place it had taken the shape of its own wall.

Which was precisely what made her job impossible.

Wren was a ward-keeper. Third circle, Royal College of Boundaries and Edges, which sounded grander than it was. In practice she spent her days walking the borders of the realm, reading the invisible stitching that held the kingdom together against the things that lived outside it—and there were things outside it, whatever the comfortable folk in the capital liked to believe. The Thorn Court alone accounted for half her caseload: a court of the old blood, seated beneath a mountain three valleys east, that had spent four centuries perfecting the art of the bargain. They did not invade. Invading was vulgar. They *contracted*. They waited for desperate people at desperate moments and offered them exactly what they needed, priced in exactly what they could least afford to lose, and then they held the paper forever.

Her orders, signed by the Master of Edges himself, were simple: the Hollow's outer wards had failed on midwinter's eve. Stars had gone out over the valley—not dimmed, not clouded, *gone out*, which was not a thing stars did—and the boundary weave had collapsed within the hour, as though the two events were threads of the same knot. Someone or something had sabotaged the oldest quiet place in the province. Find out who. Assess the damage. And above all, find out whether the failure was local—a rotten beam, a negligent keeper—or whether the Thorn Court's fingerprints were on it.

Because if the Court had reached into the Hollow, then the Hollow wasn't a valley anymore. It was a doorway.

The problem, of course, was that the wards wouldn't let her in to inspect them.

She camped that first night on the ridge, wrapped in her cloak, eating hard bread and arguing with herself. A ward-keeper of the third circle had authority to cross any boundary in the realm on the King's business. But authority, she had learned the hard way, was a document. Wards didn't read documents. Wards read intent, and weight, and the particular hum a person carried in their bones. Her authority hummed of the Crown, and the Crown meant nothing here. This valley had been refusing the Crown politely for two hundred years.

*Well,* she thought, chewing. *At least we have something in common.*

Because Wren had been refused politely herself, once, by an institution rather than a valley, and it had ended her career. She did not think about it often. She thought about it every night, actually, lying awake in whatever ditch or waystation the road provided, but she had gotten skilled at not thinking about it during daylight hours, which was the important part.

Two years ago she had been the youngest third-circle keeper in the College's history, tipped for fourth circle, tipped for a posting in the capital, tipped for everything. Then she had been assigned to audit the wards of Harrow Fen, a marsh-keep on the southern edge, and she had found what she found: that the keep's master, a court favorite, had quietly sold passage-rights through his land to a broker who sold them onward to the Court of Thorns. Not treason, technically. The contracts were beautifully drafted. Every clause legal. Which was, she argued in her report, exactly the problem—the law itself had become the breach, and the wards were failing because the land itself had been bargained away beneath them.

Her report had been accurate. It had also named names, and one of the names had friends, and the friends had asked why a third-circle keeper imagined she understood the economics of borderlands better than the men who administered them, and the answer, after six months of inquiries, had been: she didn't, apparently, and neither did anyone else, and the matter was resolved by reassigning everyone involved to postings far from anything that mattered. Wren had gone from tipped-for-everything to keeper-of-nothing in a single season. Her new assignment, until this one, had been auditing the wards around a gravel quarry.

So she understood refusal. She understood institutions that would rather blame the messenger than look at the wall. And she understood, with the particular clarity of the professionally ruined, that when a valley's ancient protections failed on the same night its stars went out, someone somewhere had signed something—and whoever signed it would very much prefer that nobody came asking.

The wards pushing her back weren't protecting the valley from her. They were protecting somebody's signature.

That was her theory, anyway, on the first night. By the third night she had refined it considerably, because on the second day she had met a shepherd.

* * *

The shepherd's name was Doss, and he grazed his flock on the high pasture just inside the valley's northern shoulder, and he was the first person in living memory, he said, to walk right up to the boundary stones and touch them.

"They used to sing," he told her, with the mild wonder of a man describing something he still wasn't sure he'd believed even while it happened. "My grandfather said. You put your ear to a stone on midsummer's eve and you could hear them humming, like bees in a wall. I always thought it was nonsense. But my da said his da swore it true."

"And now?"

"Now they're just rocks." He turned one over with his boot—a waist-high pillar, lichen-crusted, carved with a spiral worn nearly smooth. "Except. Hm." He squatted, rubbed at the base with his thumb. "There's this."

Wren knelt. What Doss had exposed beneath the lichen was a scorch mark. Not weathering, not staining—*scorching*, a fan-shaped burn radiating up from the stone's root into the soil around it, black as a smithy floor. And the pattern of it was wrong for lightning. Lightning shatters. This had seared, evenly, in lines that followed the grain of the stone like flame following a wick.

"That happened—" Doss began counting on his fingers, gave up, and settled on "—sometime after the stars went funny. Before that, never. My dog won't come near the stones anymore. Used to nap against them all summer. Now he goes wide around the whole line, like there's a smell."

There *was* a smell. Faintly, under the lichen and the cold earth: burnt sugar and old smoke and something green underneath, something like crushed nettle, something that Wren recognized because she had smelled it twice before in her career, and both times it had been attached to paperwork bearing the seal of a certain mountain court.

Thorn-fire. The Court's preferred instrument for unwinding what it could not otherwise reach. It burned cold where it touched living wood and hot where it touched worked stone, and it left that sweetness behind like a calling card, because the Court believed—correctly—that most people would never know what they were smelling, and the few who did would understand it as a message: *we were here, we will be here again, do not make us explain ourselves.*

Someone had walked the boundary line of the Hollow with thorn-fire and opened it stone by stone. And the wards hadn't resisted, because—

"Because," Wren said aloud, to nobody, crouched in the wet grass, "the wards were already dead."

Doss looked at her sideways. "You say something?"

"I said thank you for your time." She paid him in dried apples, which he accepted with the grave courtesy of a man being overcharged and knowing it.

Here was the thing she could not stop turning over: sabotage should have left resistance. A living ward fights. Even a failing ward fights—it frays, it sparks, it takes chunks out of whatever's cutting it, and the scars show. These stones showed no scars. The thorn-fire had passed through the boundary weave the way light passes through open water. Either the wards had been dismantled carefully from the inside beforehand—which required access, which required trust, which required *someone inside*—or the wards had never been guarding against the Court at all.

Or, third option, the one she liked least: the wards had been built *by agreement*. A boundary that parts willingly for one specific party isn't a broken wall. It's a door with a key, and the key has been in the Court's pocket the whole time.

Which raised the question she'd been sent to answer in a form her superiors had not anticipated: if the Hollow's invisibility had been purchased—if the whole valley was sitting inside a bargain—then the wards weren't defenses. They were the *terms*. And terms don't fail. Terms get *fulfilled*.

She spent that afternoon walking the line south, mapping scorches. Eleven stones, eleven burns, spaced with a regularity that spoke of method rather than malice. At the twelfth stone the line turned toward the river, and at the bend she found the thing that made her stop sleeping properly: a fragment of vellum, wedged in a crack, charred at the edges, ink ghosted but legible if you tilted it against the low sun.

It was a contract fragment. She was almost sure. The hand was formal, the kind taught in chancery schools, and the surviving phrases included *"...held in trust..."* and *"...renewed upon each hundredth winter..."* and, most tellingly, a witness clause referencing *"the sky entire, starless, as sign and seal."*

Starless sky as a *seal*. Wren had read hundreds of Court contracts. They loved conditions like that—conditions that sounded like poetry and functioned like padlocks. If the founding bargain of the Hollow had been witnessed under a sky with no stars, then the vanished stars weren't a side effect of the wards failing.

They were the wards *working*. The sky going dark was the contract closing its own envelope.

And someone had just opened it.

She copied the fragment twice, photographed it once with the little brass-and-glass field camera the College had issued her and which she trusted slightly less than a pencil, and buried the original in a lead-lined tube in her pack, because she had learned what happened to inconvenient documents that stayed in convenient places.

Then she went down into the valley to find whoever was left.

* * *

The Guildhall sat at the valley's heart like an old dog in the sun: too big for its yard, proud of its bones, visibly dying. Wren saw it first from the ridge road—grey stone, slate roof patched in three different colors of slate, a tower that leaned two degrees off plumb with the confidence of a building that had been leaning for a century and intended to continue. The guild sign above the door read THE HOLLOW GUILD, KEEPERS OF THE BOUNDARY, and beneath it, smaller, newer, painted by someone with a sense of humor and dwindling paint: *est. unknown, members: 3.*

Three. The greatest ward-keeping institution in the province, once forty strong, now three. She'd known the numbers from the census rolls. Knowing a number and standing in front of it were different things. The courtyard flagstones were swept clean—someone still swept—but the well had a wooden lid nailed down, and the herb garden had gone to seed in a way that suggested the person doing the sweeping had opinions about herbs and no time to act on them.

She knocked. After a while, a woman answered the door: seventies, maybe, spine straight as a rule, grey hair pinned with what appeared to be a bone knitting needle. She looked at Wren the way gatekeepers everywhere look at petitioners—with an immediate, pre-verbal assessment of threat level—and then, catching sight of the College badge on Wren's collar, said something under her breath that Wren chose to interpret as unflattering.

"You're the one from the capital," the woman said. "Maera Tetch. Senior keeper, such as that means anything now. You'll want the archive. Everyone wants the archive. Come in before the draft gets ideas."

Inside, the Guildhall was cold in the way of buildings being heated by stubbornness rather than fuel—one fire, in one room, everything else shut up and sheeted. Maera led her down a corridor lined with empty weapon racks and past a hall whose long table was set for thirty and laid for none, to a small warm room where a young person was bent over a table covered in stone rubbings.

"This is Silas Ashgrove," Maera said. "Apprentice. Last one. Silas, this is the King's snoop."

The introduction had the flavor of an insult offered on both sides simultaneously, and Wren found, to her surprise, that she respected it. Institutions that still had the energy to be rude to inspectors were institutions that hadn't entirely given up.

Silas Ashgrove stood up and Wren revised several assumptions at once. Young—early twenties, perhaps. Careful hands, ink-stained at the edges. Eyes that went to the lead tube in Wren's pack before going to her face, which told Wren two things: that Silas had already found fragments of their own, and that Silas understood what such fragments were worth and to whom.

"You've been at the boundary stones," Silas said. Not a question.

"Eleven of them. Scorched. Cold-burned, thorn-signature, no defensive scarring." She watched Silas's face as she said it and saw the words land like weights on a scale—each one confirming something Silas had clearly already feared. "And I found this." She drew out her copy of the vellum fragment and laid it on the table between the rubbings.

Silas went very still.

"We found pieces too," Silas said finally. "In the archive. Burned sections of a founding charter. I've been trying to reconstruct the wording for—" a glance at Maera, a hesitation, "—for some weeks. Since the stars went out. Since before you arrived."

"Some weeks," Wren repeated. "The wards fell on midwinter's eve. It is now—" she counted, "—the twenty-third of First-Thaw. Two months."

"I started looking the morning after the stars vanished," Silas said. "Before the wards failed. Because the stars vanishing was wrong in a way I couldn't name, and everything I'd been taught said that when something is wrong in a way you can't name, you go to the records." A pause, and then, with the bluntness of someone deciding to spend trust like coin: "The records led me to a bargain. A soul, held in trust. Renewed each hundredth winter. And the hundredth winter is *this* winter."

Wren sat down. This was bigger than her commission. Her commission assumed saboteurs. Saboteurs implied the valley's protections had been attacked. Nothing about a soul held in trust implied attack. Everything about it implied *payment coming due*.

"The elders know?" she asked.

"The elder knows," Silas said, with a precision that told Wren exactly how many elders remained and how much Silas thought of the distinction. "He knows. He says the bargain must stand. He says if we provoke the Court, they'll take another soul instead, and better the devil's price than the devil's visit." Silas's voice did something complicated on those words—went flat, then tight, then flat again, like a rope taking strain. "I disagree."

"With respect," Maera interjected from her chair, where she was mending something that appeared to be a net, "the apprentice disagrees loudly, daily, and at length, and I have begun keeping a tally."

"How do you tally disagreement?" Wren asked, genuinely curious.

"Notches," Maera said. "On the doorframe. We're at forty-one."

*Forty-one arguments in two months.* Wren did the arithmetic and realized Silas had picked this fight nearly every single day since finding the charter, against the last authority in the valley, alone, with no evidence beyond charred paper and a bad feeling. There was a word for that kind of persistence and the word wasn't recklessness. Reckless people stopped when it stopped being fun.

"What I can't work out," Silas continued, pulling a rubbing toward them—a spiral, matching the boundary stones, "is the direction of it. Everyone assumes the Court broke in. But your stones show no fight. Mine either. And if the wards were built as part of the bargain—as the *terms*—then they didn't fail. They were *released*. On schedule. By design." Silas looked up. "The valley wasn't invaded. It was *collected*."

Wren felt the hair rise on her arms, and not from the cold. Collected. A hundred-year lease expiring. The valley's invisibility running out like a lamp running out of oil, except the oil had been a person.

"Do you know whose soul?" she asked, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Silas's face answered before Silas did.

"Yes," Silas said quietly. "I believe I do."

And then, because honesty apparently ran in the family or the training or wherever it ran: "I'm not ready to say more. Not yet. Not until I'm sure. If I'm wrong and I say it aloud, I've handed the Court a name for free. If I'm right and I say it aloud in this room, I don't know what Maera's tally does, but it does something."

Maera, from her corner: "Forty-two."

Wren laughed before she could stop herself, and the laugh did something useful—it cracked the room open half an inch. Silas's shoulders came down. Even Maera's mouth twitched, briefly, in the manner of a woman conceding a point to an opponent she intended to defeat later.

"All right," Wren said. "Here's where I stand, since we're spending trust. My commission says find the saboteur. I no longer believe there is one, in the sense my masters mean. What I believe is that the Thorn Court holds a contract on this valley, that the contract has matured, and that the Court executed it with a thoroughness that looks less like malice and more like *bookkeeping*. Which is worse. Malice you can fight. Bookkeeping you have to *unwrite*."

"And you want to help?" Silas said. The skepticism in it was gentle but real.

"I want to *read*," Wren said. "Everything you've reconstructed. Everything you haven't. And in exchange—" she tapped the lead tube, "—you get the only copy of my stone findings that exists outside my head, plus everything I know about how the Court drafts, seals, and enforces. Which is more than the College teaches and exactly as much as they'd deny teaching me."

Silas considered her for a long moment. Wren had been considered by many people in her career—superiors considering her usefulness, enemies considering her danger, colleagues considering her odds of advancement—and she had learned to distinguish consideration from appraisal. This was appraisal. Silas Ashgrove was weighing her, item by item, the way you weigh a tool you might have to bet your life on.

"Why should I trust you?" Silas asked at last. "You work for the Crown. The Crown sold out Harrow Fen. I've read about it. It's in the College's own disciplinary record, if you know where to look, which I did, the night the stars went out, because I wanted to know what kind of kingdom keeps our valley 'safe.'"

The hit landed. Wren let it land. "Harrow Fen is why you *should* trust me," she said, "if you're going to trust me at all. I'm not here because the Crown trusts me. I'm here because the Crown doesn't know what to do with me and this posting was the shape of that. I'm the keeper they sent to the quarry. They sent me to a quarry for telling the truth about a bargain, and now they've sent me to a valley that turns out to be sitting on top of a bargain, and I intend to be equally truthful about this one, and they can send me to a quarry again." She paused. "I'm aware this is not a glowing recommendation. It's the honest one. Those are different documents."

Something in Silas's face shifted—the appraiser recognizing, in the goods, a familiar flaw. "You were punished for naming names in a bargain," Silas said slowly. "And now you're standing in a valley full of people who don't want any names named."

"Occupational hazard," Wren said. "I seem to collect them."

Maera, watching from her chair with the net in her lap, delivered a verdict that surprised all three of them: "Take her to the archive, Silas. I'll make tea. If she's a spy, she's the worst one I've ever seen, and I've seen two."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the evening, and it was strange, and it was good, and neither of them said the other thing that was happening, which was that two people trained from childhood to distrust outsiders were discovering they trusted each other's *methods* completely, which is the load-bearing version of trust, the kind friendships are secretly built on.

Silas spread the reconstructions across the big table: rubbings, transcriptions, guesses marked in a different-colored ink so honest that Wren felt a professional pang of admiration. Wren added her stone data. Together the picture assembled itself with the grim satisfaction of a puzzle completing: a founding bargain, purchased invisibility, a soul held in trust, renewal each hundredth winter, sealed under starless sky.

"One thing nags at me," Wren said, deep in the evening, tea gone cold in her cup. "The witness clause. *Starless sky, as sign and seal.* Contracts need witnesses. Who witnesses a sky?"

"Whoever the contract gives power over it," Silas said. "Or—whoever the contract *binds* to produce it." And then Silas went quiet in a way Wren was learning to read as the sound of a thought arriving too large to speak comfortably.

"What?"

"If the starless sky is the seal," Silas said, "and the seal is holding—then the stars aren't gone. They're *withheld*. By the contract. Which means somewhere in the wording, someone agreed to hide the sky. And if someone agreed to hide the sky, then someone can agree to *stop*." Silas's finger came down on a charred margin, on a phrase they'd both been treating as decorative boilerplate. "*...until the sky be honestly shown...*" Silas read it aloud. "Honestly shown. Not restored. Shown. As though the hiding were ours to end."

Wren stared at the fragment. Somewhere in the back of her mind, the quarry posting stung afresh, because this was the work she'd been trained for and exiled from—the reading of edges, the finding of the seam—and here it was, happening in a dying guildhall over bad tea, with an apprentice who had never been trained at all and was somehow out-reading her.

"There's a courier road east of here," she said, to change the subject to something actionable, because the alternative subject was too big for one evening. "Runs toward the mountain. If the Court executed a collection, there'll be traffic. Riders, notices, maybe a herald. Courts of Thorns don't send armies; they send *paperwork with legs*."

As if summoned, there came a knock at the Guildhall door. Three knocks, precise, evenly spaced, the knock of someone who had never in their life needed to knock twice.

Maera went to answer it, knitting needle still in her hair, and came back thirty seconds later wearing an expression Wren had last seen on a customs officer announcing an inspection.

"There's a gentleman at the door," Maera said. "Very polished boots. Says he represents interested parties from the east. He's brought a letter." She set the letter on the table: heavy paper, sealed in green wax pressed with a sprig of something thorny, addressed in a hand so fine it looked engraved.

Addressed to *Silas Ashgrove, Apprentice of the Hollow Guild.*

Wren read the address upside down, as inspectors do, and felt her stomach drop, because the letter wasn't addressed to the elder, or the guild, or the Crown's inspector.

It was addressed to the apprentice. Personally. By name.

And Silas Ashgrove, sitting beside her, had not yet told anyone whose soul the contract held.
