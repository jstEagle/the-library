## 5. Bramble Owes a Debt

Bramble had been locked out of the Hollow for twenty years, and he'd like it noted that he took it rather well.

He'd taken it so well, in fact, that he'd built a life on the wrong side of the boundary stones. He sold ribbons that changed color to match your mood (mostly sulk, in these parts), luck-charms that worked about one time in nine (he was honest about the odds, which was more than most charm-sellers could say), and a small but reliable trade in gossip, which never failed at all. He wintered in the tollhouse of Small Mercies and summered along the valley's rim, calling up cheerfully at the invisible wall that would not let him in, and every year the answer was the same: nothing. Not a ward-flare, not a polite refusal, not even the satisfying *thunk* of a well-made barrier. The Hollow simply declined to acknowledge his existence, which Bramble considered the rudest form of magic there was.

And then, three nights past midwinter's eve, the wall had gone.

He'd felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave your mouth — a strange lightness where a pressure used to be, and an immediate, unshakable urge to poke at the gap. He'd walked right up to the boundary stone at Miller's Bend, the one Elowen Ashgrove used to sit on when she thought no one was watching, and he'd put out one green-fingered hand, and nothing had stopped him.

He still hadn't crossed. That was the thing nobody understood about half-fae folk, and what they got wrong about bargains generally: a door opening is not the same as an invitation. Bramble stood at the edge of the Hollow like a man at the lip of a pool in early spring, knowing perfectly well the water was cold, knowing perfectly well he was going in anyway, and hating every second of the waiting.

Because here was the arithmetic of his life, plain as a peddler's ledger:

One life-debt, owed to Elowen Ashgrove, incurred some twenty-three years ago, outstanding.

One Elowen Ashgrove, vanished from the world for twenty years, presumed gone.

One valley, suddenly open, in the exact season when debts come due.

Bramble did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what the Thorn Court called its paperwork.

* * *

He found the guild house by following the smell of woodsmoke and stubbornness, both of which hung over the last lit window in the village like a banner. He knocked the way he knocked on all doors — a cheerful triple rap, then a pause, then two more, in case the first set had been offensive.

The door opened on a chain. Through the gap: a young woman with ward-keeper's calluses and a face like a closed ledger, and behind her, half-shadowed, someone younger and thinner who looked, to Bramble's considerable shock, like a portrait of Elowen that had been left out in the rain and drawn again from memory by someone who loved them both.

"Well," said Bramble. "That's uncanny."

"State your business," said the woman with the calluses.

"My business," said Bramble, "is that I've been standing outside your valley for twenty years selling mood-ribbons to people who are mostly sulky, and now your door is open, and I would very much like to know why, because I have a suspicion, and my suspicions have a distressing habit of being correct."

"We're not discussing guild matters with strangers."

"I'm not a stranger." He fished in the many pockets of his coat — a coat that contained, by conservative estimate, forty pockets and at least eleven things that were technically alive — and produced a ribbon. Pale blue, frayed at one end, faded almost white. "Recognize this? Sold it myself, twenty-two years back, to a woman with ash-brown hair and a laugh like a door hinge. She paid me double and told me to keep the change and buy something for myself, because 'no one should walk into winter empty-handed.' She tied it round my wrist herself." He held up his wrist, where the ghost of a knot still dented the skin, though the ribbon itself had long since gone the color of old ice. "Elowen Ashgrove. And before you ask — yes, I remember exactly what she bought it for."

The younger one moved forward. Up close, the resemblance was worse. Better. Worse.

"You knew my mother," Silas said. It wasn't a question, but their voice cracked in the middle of it, which made it one anyway.

"I knew her," Bramble said, and let the cheer drain out of his voice, because there were moments for cheer and moments for honesty, and he'd learned to tell them apart if nothing else. "I owed her my life. And I think she may be the reason your stars went out."

* * *

They let him in, eventually, after Wren had searched him twice (once for weapons, once for charms, and once, he suspected, purely out of professional spite) and made him swear on his own name — *Bramble Underbough, son of Nettle of the Autumn Road*, a name he gave up reluctantly, since names were currency and he hated spending his — that he meant no harm to anyone under this roof.

The guild house was warm and shabby and smelled of tea gone bitter from steeping too long, which told Bramble everything about how the last few days had gone. An elder sat by the fire radiating disapproval with the efficiency of a stove. Wren sat where she could see the door and the window and, apparently, the chimney. And Silas sat across from him with a scrap of scorched paper flat on the table between them, guarded like a candle in wind.

"So," Bramble said. "Tell me what you know, and I'll tell you what I suspect, and we'll see whose night gets worse."

Silas told him. The stars going out one by one, like lamps snuffed down a corridor. The wards falling without a fight. The contract fragment: *one soul, held in trust, against the renewal of the vale's concealment.* The signature in the archive — Grandmother Ashgrove's hand, neat as stitching. The name attached to the soul.

"Elowen," Silas finished, and their voice held steady through the whole word, which cost them something; Bramble could see the cost sitting on their shoulders like a yoke. "She didn't leave. She was *taken*. Twenty years ago. As payment."

The elder made a sound like a man swallowing a sermon. Wren didn't make any sound at all, but her jaw did something complicated.

"And here's my part," Bramble said, and turned his teacup in his hands, because it was easier to say to a cup than to a face. "Twenty-three years ago — before you were born, young one, so mind the timeline — I was caught in a thorn-wight's snare on the autumn road east of here. You know what those are? No? Good. Keep it that way. It doesn't kill you. It *keeps* you. Feeds slow. I'd have lasted, oh, a decade, maybe, getting thinner and thinner and more and more forgettable, until I was a story peddlers told to frighten each other.

"Your mother cut me out of it. Took her most of a day and both her hands bled to the wrist doing it, because wight-snares don't cut for steel, only for offered pain. She never asked me for anything. Never named a price. Just wrapped my hands in her scarf and said, 'There. Now we're even,' and laughed that hinge-laugh of hers." He looked up. "But that's not how debts work, not deep ones. A life for a life isn't settled by a scarf. Somewhere in the old accounting, I still owe her. And debts like mine don't rest quiet. They wait. They find their moment."

"The wards fell," Wren said quietly. "The debt found its moment."

"The court came collecting," Bramble agreed. "Or its paperwork did. And here's the part I suspect, and hate suspecting: the Thorn Court doesn't send bailiffs for souls already held. If they've come knocking now, it's because something's *due*. Renewal time. The century turns, the bargain comes up for its signature again, and somebody has to stand in the old spot." He tapped the scorched fragment gently with one green finger. "Or somebody new does."

Silence, except for the fire.

"That's why you're here," Silas said slowly. "Not to sell ribbons."

"I'm always here to sell ribbons," Bramble said. "A man must eat. But yes. Also this." He leaned forward. "I know the way beneath the mountain. All the ways in, and three of the ways out, and one of those might even be true. I've traded at the tollhouse of Small Mercies for twenty years; I know what names cost and what memories fetch and which of the court's rules can be bent and which will bend *you*. Your mother pulled me out of a snare when every sensible creature would have walked on by. So." He spread his hands, forty pockets and all. "I'm offering passage. Guide, translator, professional liar-for-good-reasons, at your service. Payment: none. The debt settles itself."

Wren's eyes narrowed to inspecting-slits. "Nobody works for free."

"Nobody *honest* works for free," Bramble agreed pleasantly. "Fortunately, I'm a peddler. We operate on an entirely different moral system. Mine says: a debt unpaid is a stone in the boot, and I have walked twenty years with this stone, and I would very much like to take my boot off."

* * *

The elder forbade it, of course. Bramble gathered that forbidding things was roughly ninety percent of the elder's remaining duties, and he performed this one with real feeling: the court would simply take another soul if provoked; the valley had been safe for a hundred years; the dead contract should be burned and forgotten; and above all, no Ashgrove should set foot beyond the stones, because the court would smell the blood on them like a butcher's shop.

"They already know the blood's here," Silas said. "They addressed a letter to me. By name." They folded the contract fragment with great care and put it inside their coat, over the heart, which Bramble noted and filed away: this one carried their grief where other people carried knives. "If the debt is due, hiding doesn't discharge it. It just means nobody's home to argue the terms."

"Arguing terms with the Thorn Court is how souls get *held*—"

"Then I'll argue carefully."

It was, Bramble thought, watching the young one pack by candlelight — a spare kit, a coil of good rope, the contract fragment, and, tucked into a side pocket with the tenderness people usually reserved for infants, a tin of their mother's favorite tea, still sealed after twenty years — it was exactly the sort of thing Elowen would have done. Walked toward the thorns with a tin of tea. He felt something in his chest turn over, an old hinge remembering its swing.

Wren came out to the yard at dawn, where Bramble was pretending to check his cart's axle and actually feeding a biscuit to a small sentient hatbox he kept for company.

"You're coming too," Bramble observed. "Don't look surprised. You've had your bag packed since the letter arrived. Ward-keepers pack light and mean it."

"Someone has to keep this one alive," Wren said, nodding at Silas in the doorway. "And someone has to find out who sabotaged the wards. Those may be the same job." She paused, and then, grudgingly, the way other people hand over hostages: "You know the roads. I'll allow that."

"*Allow,*" Bramble said, delighted. "Oh, we're going to get along famously."

"We are not."

"We absolutely are. I can tell. You have the face of a woman who secretly enjoys peddlers."

"I have the face of a woman deciding whether to arrest you."

"Same thing, in my experience."

* * *

They left at first light, three of them and a cart that looked like it had been assembled from the contents of a magpie's divorce. The valley lay gray and starless around them, and the boundary stone at Miller's Bend — Elowen's stone — stood dark and ordinary, just a rock now, holding up nothing.

Bramble paused there a moment with his hand on the cold granite, the way you'd touch a grave or a threshold, and said nothing, because some accounts you settle out loud and some you settle by walking.

Then he clicked his tongue at the cart, and the three of them went out through the gap where the wall used to be, onto the road where nothing grew, toward the mountain that had eaten a mother and, if Bramble's suspicions were right — and his suspicions had a distressing habit of being correct — was expecting company.

Behind them, far off, the first snow of the long winter began to fall.

Ahead, on the road, something was already waiting: a figure at the edge of sight, small and patient, beside a lantern that burned green.

"Now *that*," said Bramble, reaching calmly into pocket number thirty-one, "wasn't in my ledger."
