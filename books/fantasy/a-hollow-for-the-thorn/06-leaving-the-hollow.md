## 6. Leaving the Hollow

Elder Maren Thistlewood had three volumes of guild law memorized, a walking stick she used primarily as a pointing instrument, and the unshakable conviction that any problem could be solved by refusing to acknowledge it loudly enough.

She demonstrated this last skill the morning after I told her I was leaving.

"You are not leaving," she said, from her chair by the guildhall hearth, in the tone of a woman announcing that it was not raining.

"I am," I said. "At dawn tomorrow."

"Dawn is a superstition." She jabbed the stick at the window, where the sky was doing its new trick of being nothing at all—no stars, no moon, just a smooth grey ceiling like the inside of a closed fist. "There's no such thing as dawn anymore. There's only dimness and slightly less dimness. You can't leave at a time that doesn't exist."

"I'll leave when it's marginally less dim, then."

"Silas." She set the stick down, which was how I knew she was serious. Maren without her stick was like a storm without its thunder—quieter, and far more alarming. "Sit down."

I sat. The guildhall smelled of cold ash and old paper and the rosemary sachets she tucked into everything, convinced that herbs could hold back decay the way they held back moths. In a valley where magic couldn't work, we had all become very devoted to small practical superstitions. Rosemary against rot. Salt at thresholds. Habits shaped like prayers.

"The court beneath the mountain," Maren said, "is not a place. It is a *condition*. Like winter, or grief. You do not walk into winter, child. Winter walks into you."

"That's very poetic for someone who also insists I mend my own socks."

"I contain multitudes." She leaned forward. "Here is what your archive-reading hasn't taught you yet, because you're young and archives flatter young people: bargains like this one don't have doors. They have *appetites*. If you go down there and rattle the cage, the Thorn King will not hand over Elowen Ashgrove and wave goodbye. He will decide the debt has grown. He will take interest. And interest, in his ledger, is measured in souls."

"So we do nothing."

"We do nothing *loudly*," she corrected. "We do nothing quietly, and carefully, and for another eighty years until the renewal comes due, and then we negotiate from a position of—"

"Of what? We have three members, Maren. You, me, and Old Tam, who mostly talks to the well. Our wards fell because the stars went out, and the stars went out because our founders sold my mother to buy us invisibility. What position exactly are we negotiating from?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, the roof settled with a groan, the sound the guildhall made every night now, like an old man turning over in bed.

Maren looked at me for a long moment, and something in her face shifted—not softening exactly, but settling, the way snow settles before it slides.

"Do you know why I stayed?" she said. "Forty years ago, when I was younger than you and twice as angry. Do you know why I never walked out of this valley to find out what the world was hiding from us?"

I didn't answer. She hadn't ever talked about before. Before was a country Maren kept locked, and I'd assumed the key was lost.

"Because my grandmother signed too," she said. "Not the founding bargain. A smaller one. A *supplementary* one, if you like. She traded the guild's courage for the guild's safety. Every Ashgrove since has been brave enough to tend stones and stubborn enough to stay, but not one of us has been able to walk past the boundary with intent. Try it. Go on. Walk to the eastern stone and mean to cross it."

I had tried it. Of course I had tried it—I was twenty-three and had spent my whole life in a box, however gilded with rosemary. My feet had turned me around at the boundary stone every time, gentle as a parent steering a toddler from a hot stove, and I had always assumed it was cowardice. My cowardice. A private, shameful flaw.

"It's in the blood," Maren said. "It's in the *bargain*. And here is the part that should frighten you more than thorns: if you defy it—if you somehow drag yourself over that line—the contract reads it as breach. Breach invites collection. Collection means the King doesn't take one soul from the Ashgrove line. He takes as many as he likes, starting with whoever's nearest." Her eyes were steady and terribly kind. "You wouldn't be saving your mother, Silas. You'd be delivering the rest of us to her cell."

I sat with that. Outside, the nothing-sky pressed down on the valley like a lid.

"And if I do nothing," I said finally, "the wards stay dead, the stars stay gone, and the court keeps her anyway. That's your plan. Eighty years of nothing."

"My plan," said Maren, "has kept this valley alive for four hundred years."

"Your plan," I said, "is why my mother has been in a cage for twenty."

The words came out harder than I meant them, and I watched them land. Maren took them the way she took everything—straight-backed, unblinking, like weather.

"Yes," she said quietly. "It is."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I spent the night in the archive instead, with a candle and the contract fragment flattened under glass, and I did what I had been doing for three days: reading it again, looking for the door Maren insisted wasn't there.

The fragment was scorched along one edge, rescued from the founders' fire two centuries ago by some archivist with better instincts than obedience. Most of it was formula—the usual bone-dry language of bargains, all *whereas* and *hereinafter* and *in perpetuity*. But contracts are maps, Wren had said, back when she'd still been speaking to me in full sentences rather than clipped tactical updates. Maps have edges. Edges have gaps.

And there, in the gap between the clause of payment and the clause of silence, a phrase in the founder's cramped hand:

*...witnessed under the open sky, and binding so long as the sky remains...*

So long as the sky remains. Not so long as the mountain stands. Not so long as the blood endures. The *sky*.

The stars had gone out when the wards fell. Everyone in the valley had read it as catastrophe—the court reaching in, snuffing our lights, announcing itself. But what if it was the opposite? What if the wards, dying, had done their last duty? Had hidden the sky—the witness—to keep the contract alive a little longer? To buy us time?

Or to give someone a chance to break it honestly.

I copied the phrase onto a scrap of vellum, folded it into the fragment, and sat back. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, Old Tam was talking to the well again, low and rhythmic, and for once I thought I caught actual words: *go on then, go on.*

Even the well was mutinying.

* * *

Maren was waiting for me at dawn—which existed after all, a thin bruised light seeping through the nothing-sky—and she was waiting with a pack.

I stopped on the guildhall steps and stared at it. Wool cloak, oilskin, a coil of good rope, dried apples wrapped in cloth, and a small iron knife that I recognized as the one she used to trim candlewicks and, once, memorably, to defend the cheese cellar from a badger.

"That's mine," I said stupidly.

"It is now." She held it out. "Take it before I come to my senses."

"Maren—"

"Don't." The stick came up, pointing, though there was nothing to point at but me. "You'll say something grateful and I'll say something gracious and then we'll both stand here until the light fails, and I have letters to write. Take the pack."

I took the pack. It was heavier than it looked, the way all parting gifts are.

"You understand I'm still forbidding this," she said. "For the record. For the charter. When they ask—and someone will ask, eventually, when the valley wakes up and notices its sky is missing—I need it written that Elder Thistlewood forbade the expedition in the strongest terms."

"Forbidden in the strongest terms," I agreed. "You were very stern. I trembled."

"You did not tremble even slightly. That's the problem with you Ashgroves." But her voice cracked on the name, just barely, and she covered it by rummaging in her sleeve and producing a folded paper. "The renewal schedule. The names of every signer, going back. And—" she hesitated, then pushed it into my hands whether I wanted it or not— "a letter. For Elowen. If you find her. Don't read it. It's forty years late and none of your business."

My throat did something complicated. "Maren, the boundary. You said the bargain won't let me cross. That defying it invites collection."

"It does." She said it flatly, no comfort in it. "If you cross alone, angry, trying to sneak—that's breach. That's a thief's crossing, and thieves pay." She tapped my chest with the stick, once, gently, right over the heart. "But a bargain is a living thing, Silas. It watches *intent* the way the well watches rain. You're not sneaking out to steal. You're walking out the front gate, in daylight, with your name in your mouth and your purpose on your shoulders, to collect what was taken. That's not breach. That's *claim*. Different word entirely. Different weight."

"You're guessing."

"I'm hoping," she corrected. "Guessing is what the founders did. Hoping is all that's left to the honest." She stepped back and squared herself, and for a moment I saw her as she must have been at twenty—young and furious and trapped behind a wall her grandmother built. "Now go, before I remember I'm a coward too."

* * *

Old Tam was at the eastern boundary stone, which was unusual, because Tam's rounds had ended years ago and these days he mostly sat by the well explaining things to it. He stood beside the leaning marker with his hands folded, dressed in his good coat, the one with the brass buttons polished to little suns.

"I'm not stopping you," he said, before I could speak.

"I know."

"Good. Because I've a speech prepared for stopping you, and it's rubbish. I wrote it last night and the well laughed at me." He patted the stone the way you'd pat an old horse. "Wards are dead, then."

"They're dead."

"Shame. I liked the wards. Kept the tax collectors out." He squinted at me. "You'll want to know what's past the ridge. Nothing grows on the north road anymore. Hasn't for years—we just told ourselves it was poor soil. Poor soil doesn't spread, Silas. Whatever's up there has been leaking through the cracks for a long while, and now the cracks are doors." He dug in his pocket and pressed something into my palm: a smooth grey pebble, river-worn, ordinary as bread. "From the well. Don't ask me what it's for. The well says you'll know."

"Tam, does the well actually talk to you?"

"Every day," he said serenely. "Mostly about drainage. But lately it's had opinions." He looked east, toward the ridge and whatever waited beyond it, and his voice dropped into something older. "Twenty years ago a woman walked this same direction with a pack on her back and told me she'd be home by spring. I believed her, because I wanted to. So I'll tell you what I should've told her: don't come home by spring. Come home. However long it takes, however much of you it costs. Just come home."

Then he hugged me, quick and fierce and smelling of well-water and pipe smoke, and turned me around by the shoulders, and pointed me at the gap in the hills like a man loosing an arrow.

* * *

The boundary stone passed on my left, and I crossed the line of the old wards, and nothing happened.

That was the strange part. I had braced for the turn of my feet, the gentle parental steering, the wall of my own cowardice. Instead my boots simply carried me forward, one step and then another, over ground that had been forbidden to my family for four hundred years, and the only thing that changed was the wind—it found me properly for the first time, full of pine and distance and cold, and pushed at my back like a hand.

I stopped ten paces past the stone and made myself look back.

The Hollow lay below in the grey half-light: the guildhall with its sagging roof, the crooked lane, the well with Tam beside it, Maren's silhouette in the doorway, not waving, absolutely not waving, one hand raised in what was either farewell or a final prohibition. The fields, stubbled and sleeping. The valley walls rising green and familiar on every side, the walls I had mistaken, my whole life, for the shape of the world.

It was smaller than I remembered. That was the thing nobody tells you—that cages shrink when you finally stand outside them. From beyond the boundary, the Hollow looked like something you could cup in two hands. Something that had been holding its breath for four hundred years.

Something worth coming back to.

I touched the contract fragment through my coat, where it rode against my ribs—map and weapon both, the founders' confession folded into a rectangle small enough to hide. Somewhere under that mountain, in a hall of held things, my mother had been keeping a cell warm for twenty years so that this valley could pretend to be safe. The least I owed her was the truth: that the pretending was over, and someone had finally come.

The road bent north, into country where nothing grew, toward a mountain wearing a crown of cloud.

I walked.

Behind me, faint on the wind—or perhaps only in my head, or perhaps in the well's low voice carrying further than wells should—came the sound of the valley exhaling at last.

By midmorning the road had narrowed to a track, and the track had begun to die. Grass gave way to grey moss, moss to bare earth, earth to something pale and brittle that crunched underfoot like old porcelain. Frost climbed the fenceposts in July-thin patterns though the season was autumn. And ahead, where the track crested a rise, a figure stood waiting in the middle of the dead road—a woman in a ward-keeper's coat, arms crossed, watching me come with the expression of someone who has been standing there long enough to compose several speeches and abandoned all of them.

"You're supposed to be behind me," Wren called. "Trailing me. Suspiciously."

"You told me to meet you at the ridge."

"I told you to meet me at the ridge *tomorrow*. I wanted a day's head start to scout." She uncrossed her arms. There was frost on her shoulders she hadn't bothered to brush off. "Instead you leave at dawn, alone, without telling anyone, across blighted ground, carrying the single most dangerous piece of paper in the valley."

"I told Maren."

"Maren doesn't count; Maren forbade you. That's practically a blessing." She looked past me, down the long grey road, then back, and something in her jaw tightened—the reluctant arithmetic of a woman deciding, again, to trust. "Well. You're here now, and the road's worse than I reported, and there's something moving in the treeline that I'd very much like to not explain to your corpse." She jerked her chin northward. "Keep up, Ashgrove. And walk where I walk."

"Why?"

"Because where I walk," said Wren, already moving, "is still alive."
