## 7. The Road Where Nothing Grows

Wren had walked the ward-roads of the capital for eleven years, and she had learned to read land the way other people read letters. Good land spoke plainly: hedgerow, hedgerow, field, stream, the faint hum of old boundary-work beneath it all, like a song hummed by someone who'd forgotten the words but not the tune.

This land said nothing at all.

Three days out of the Hollow, and the silence had grown from a whisper to a shout. The grass came up grey-green and brittle, like hair on a corpse. The hedgerows stood black and leafless in what should have been late autumn's gold. And the birds — Wren kept counting them, the way she'd once counted ward-stones on her rounds, and the count kept coming up the same.

Zero.

"It's the winter," Bramble said, for the fourth time that morning. He walked with his hands in his sleeves and his peddler's pack riding high on his shoulders, and he had the particular cheerfulness of someone determined not to look at anything too closely. "The court's winter. It spreads ahead of the mountain like spilled ink. My grandmother used to say you could tell how angry the Thorn King was by how far the frost reached."

"Your grandmother," Wren said, "was a fae who married a turnip farmer and then sold the turnip farm for a hat. Forgive me if I don't cite her as an authority."

"She got a *very* good hat."

Behind them, Silas said nothing. That was the thing about Silas Ashgrove that Wren couldn't stop noticing, the thing that kept snagging on her professional judgment like a burr in wool: they were quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Not calm. Not composed. Just *waiting* — for the world to prove as cruel as they'd already decided it was.

Wren had come out here to arrest someone. That was the truth she kept folded up small and flat, the way you'd carry a knife. The Hollow's wards hadn't failed on their own. Wards didn't fail; they were *made* to fail. Someone had done it, and the someone was almost certainly one of the last three members of a guild that had spent two centuries buying invisibility with other people's souls. She'd rehearsed the accusation on the road out of the capital. She'd imagined Silas in irons, imagined the smug elder's face when the royal writ came down.

Instead she'd found a scorched contract fragment, a half-fae peddler with a life-debt and a suspicious amount of luggage, and an apprentice who had packed one bag, kissed a crumbling boundary stone goodbye like it was a grandmother's cheek, and walked out of the only safe valley they'd ever known to go bargain with a king of thorns for a mother they'd mourned twenty years ago.

Suspects didn't do that. Wren knew suspects. Suspects lied about small things and got defensive about large ones and always, *always* kept an escape in reserve.

Silas kept checking on Bramble. Kept counting the birds too, Wren had noticed. Kept walking on the outside of the road, between the other two and the treeline, without seeming to realize they were doing it.

That wasn't a saboteur. That was a shepherd.

Wren hated how much it mattered to her.

* * *

They made the village at dusk, if you could call it a village.

It had been one, once — Wren could see the bones of it under the blight. A well with its roof caved in. A smithy with no smith. A dozen houses whose thatch had gone the color of old teeth, and whose windows were dark in a way that dark windows shouldn't be, because dark windows at dusk usually meant candlelight behind them and these meant nothing behind them at all, just rooms holding their breath.

"Everyone's gone," Silas said.

"Not everyone." Bramble had stopped in the middle of the road, and his ears — Wren had finally gotten used to the pointed ears, mostly — were angled forward like a hound's. "Smoke. Faint. Two chimneys down, maybe three. And—" His nose wrinkled. "Bread. Somebody's baking bread."

"Somebody's baking bread," Wren repeated, "in a village the court's winter ate alive."

"People are stubborn," Silas said quietly. "You'd know that if you'd grown up in a valley that spent two hundred years pretending the world didn't exist."

Wren decided, with effort, not to rise to that. "We should keep moving. We're losing light, and I don't like the look of the—"

The scream came from the east end of the village. A child's scream, high and cut short, the kind of sound that emptied Wren's mind of strategy in half a heartbeat.

She was running before she'd decided to. She heard Bramble's pack hit the ground, heard Silas's boots behind hers, and then she was through a collapsed garden fence and into a yard where the last light showed her the whole terrible picture at once.

A girl. Eight, maybe nine, in a coat three sizes too big, pressed against the wall of a bread-smelling house. And between the girl and the world stood a thorn-wight.

Wren had read about thorn-wights. She'd read the capital's field manuals, the ones with tidy woodcuts and tidy warnings: *fae-adjacent construct, territorial, avoid engagement.* The woodcuts had not prepared her. The thing stood seven feet tall and was built the way a briar patch would build itself if you gave it a hundred years and a grudge — black limbs like braided branches, joints that bent the wrong way, and a face that wasn't a face at all, just a knot of wood with a single split in it that opened when it moved. When it opened, Wren could see inside.

There were things inside it. Small things. A bird's wing. A doll's arm. A lock of hair, going grey.

It was a *hoard*. It was a walking, hungry hoard, and it had come for the child the way a magpie comes for something bright.

"Hey!" Wren shouted, and threw the only thing she had, which was a rock, which was, in fairness, a very well-thrown rock. It cracked against the wight's knot-face and the split snapped open with a sound like green wood tearing, and the thing turned toward her with the whole horrible patience of winter.

"Wren—" Silas was at her shoulder now, and Wren saw them do something she'd remember for the rest of her life: they stepped *between* the wight and the child, hands out, empty, the way you'd calm a spooked horse. "It's a collector," they said, fast and low. "The guild archives had a chapter on them — the court's winter doesn't just kill, it *takes*. It sends these out to gather. Small things. Bright things. Things with life still in them." Their voice didn't shake, but Wren could hear the effort holding it flat. "It's not hunting her. It's *shopping*."

"Wonderful," Wren said. "I'm being shopped for by a hedge."

"It takes the brightest thing it can reach," Silas said. "So give it something brighter."

Bramble, from the fence, had already understood. He came over the collapsed wall like a man diving into a river, and he was *glowing* — not metaphorically; the fae blood in him had lit up along his forearms and the edges of his ears, a soft gold shimmer, the most alive thing in that grey yard by a mile. He held his arms wide.

"Oi! Over here, you great walking compost heap! Half-fae, lightly used, excellent provenance!" He waggled his fingers. "Look at all this *life*!"

The wight's split-face turned. The whole body turned with it, branches groaning, and for one heartbeat Wren thought Bramble had simply volunteered himself as dinner.

Then Silas moved.

They snatched the iron fire-poker leaning against the bread-house wall — iron, Wren realized a beat later, of course, the village was old enough to remember what the fae feared — and drove it into the wight's back, into the knot where the branches braided together, and *twisted*.

The sound the wight made was not a scream. It was the sound of a forest in a hard frost, a whole winter of small deaths compressed into one note. The thing came apart the way frost does in sunlight — not falling so much as *unbecoming* — branches slackening, hoard spilling, until all that was left on the frozen ground was a scatter of black splinters and a bird's wing and a doll's arm and a lock of grey hair, and a silence that rang.

Silas stood over it, breathing hard, the poker still in their hands.

"That," Bramble said faintly, glow fading, "was the single most violent thing I have ever seen you do, and I once watched you argue with a toll-keeper for an hour."

"It was going to take her." Silas's voice cracked on the last word, and Wren watched them hear it crack, watched them press their mouth shut against whatever else wanted out.

The child hadn't run. She stood against the wall with her arms wrapped around herself, and she was looking not at the splinters, not at the glowing half-fae, but at the lock of grey hair on the ground. She walked over — small steps, deliberate, the way you approach a grave — and picked it up.

"That's Nana Sef's," she said. "It came out of the *wall* of our house last month. We thought she'd just... gone."

Wren felt something cold slide down her spine that had nothing to do with the winter.

* * *

The child's name was Tilly, and the bread was real, and so were the nine people left in the village, huddled in the two houses whose chimneys still drew. They fed the travelers stew that tasted mostly of turnip and gratitude, and Tilly's mother — a broad, exhausted woman called Sera — talked while the fire did its best.

"It started small," Sera said. "Frost in high summer, we thought nothing of it. Then the birds left. Then the well water went bitter. Then people started *losing* things. Not losing — being *robbed*, but there's no thief, see? A man's shadow went missing off his feet at noon. Old Sef's hair, a whole handful, right out from under her while she slept — and her *color* with it, she went grey as ash in a week. My husband's laugh." She stirred the stew without looking at it. "He still laughs. It just doesn't sound like him anymore. Sounds like someone doing an impression of my husband, from memory, badly."

"The court feeds," Bramble said quietly. He'd gone still, which for Bramble was its own kind of alarm. "I told you the winter spreads. I should have told you what it spreads *for*. The Thorn King holds what he takes. Every stolen shadow, every laugh, every lock of hair — it all goes under the mountain, into the vaults. It's not cruelty. It's *provision*. A court in winter stocks its larder." His ears had drooped. "My grandmother's stories had this part right, curse her and her hat."

"And the wights?" Wren asked. "The collectors?"

"Those are for the big shopping." Bramble looked at Silas, and Wren watched him decide to say it anyway, because Bramble was a coward about pain and honest about everything else. "Souls. Bright souls, especially. A child's soul is the brightest thing in any village. The wight wasn't passing through. It was *sent*."

Silas had stopped eating. Wren saw the stew go untouched, saw their hands go still around the bowl, and understood — really understood, for the first time since the broken stone — what Silas had been carrying since the night the stars went out.

They'd grown up inside a bargain. Their whole valley, their whole *life*, had been purchased with their mother's soul, and every safe, magicless, invisible day of it had been paid for by a woman who had gone quietly so the rest of them wouldn't be taken loudly. And now the wards were down, and the collectors were out, and Silas was walking toward the mountain carrying a contract fragment like a torch — and every mile, the road was showing them exactly what their mother had been protecting everyone from. What she'd chosen. What she'd sacrificed.

*You're not a suspect,* Wren thought, and the thought landed in her chest with the weight of a confession. *You're a hostage's child, walking into the hostage-taker's house with your chin up and your hands empty.*

She'd come out here with a writ and a theory. She'd rehearsed the arrest. And now she was sitting in a dying village eating turnip stew, watching the person she'd meant to arrest give their own supper to a hungry child, and the writ in her pack felt less like justice every day and more like the stupidest piece of paper she'd ever carried.

"Sera," Silas said. Their voice was steady again, but Wren heard the seam in it. "The wight came from the east?"

"East and north. Everything bad comes from the mountain now. It's like the winter's... leaning. Like it's reaching for something."

Silas nodded slowly. "It's reaching for the Hollow. The wards are down. The valley's visible for the first time in two hundred years." They looked up, and their eyes found Wren's across the fire, and what was in them wasn't fear exactly. It was arithmetic. "A valley full of bright, sheltered, undefended souls. We didn't break the wards and expose ourselves, Wren. We broke the wards and rang the dinner bell."

The fire popped. Tilly had fallen asleep against her mother, one hand still curled around nothing, dreaming of a grandmother whose hair sat in a wight's hoard under a mountain.

Wren set down her bowl. "Then we walk faster," she said.

Silas looked at her — really looked, with that held-breath attention of theirs — and something between them shifted and settled, the way a ward settles when it finally finds its true line.

"Tomorrow at first light," Silas said. "The mountain's four days out if the road holds."

"The road won't hold," Bramble said glumly, from the corner where he was pretending to sleep. "Roads never hold. In my experience, the worse the weather, the worse the news, and the worse the news, the worse the—"

He stopped.

His head came up, ears pricked, and Wren saw him look toward the eastern window, toward the dark, and the gold shimmer rose along his arms unbidden.

"What," Wren said. "What is it."

"There's a light on the road," Bramble said slowly. "Coming this way. Carried very high." He swallowed. "In the court's colors. Green and black."

And far off down the frozen road, small and steady and wrong, a lantern-light came swaying toward the village through the blight — and it did not flicker, and it did not waver, and it did not behave like any fire Wren had ever seen.
