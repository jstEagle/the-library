## 8. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

Bramble had a theory about doors, and it was this: any door worth knocking on had a smell to it. Woodsmoke meant welcome. Wet stone meant trouble. And the particular scent now drifting down the mountainside—bread, beeswax, and something faintly metallic, like an old coin held too long in the mouth—meant *mercy with conditions*.

He smelled it before he saw it.

"Up ahead," he said, and pointed his walking stick at a crooked shape wedged into the pass like a splinter in a thumb. "Tollhouse."

Wren squinted against the wind. "I don't see anything."

"That's because you're looking with your eyes instead of your nose. Eyes lie. Noses only exaggerate." He tapped the side of his own snout, which was, admittedly, more fox than anything else, and gave her what he hoped was a reassuring grin. It came out as mostly teeth. It usually did. "There's a waystation ahead run by exiles from the Thorn Court. People who got out. They'll feed us, warm us, and tell us exactly how doomed we are—for a price, but a fair one. Fairness is practically their religion. It's what happens when you've lived somewhere that has none."

Silas said nothing. They'd said very little since the letter. Bramble had watched them carry it for two days now—not in a pocket, but pressed flat against their chest inside their coat, over the heart, as though paper could be kept warm enough to stay alive. He knew better than to comment. Some things you carried loudly by carrying them silently.

The tollhouse revealed itself as they rounded the shoulder of the pass: a squat stone building grown half into the mountain, its roof shingled in slate and, inexplicably, one copper kettle. Chimney smoke leaned hard eastward in the wind. A sign hung above the door on two chains, and the wind swung it so it knocked against the wall in a slow, patient rhythm—*tok, tok, tok*—like something waiting to be let in.

Painted on the sign, in letters that shimmered faintly when you weren't looking directly at them (a trick of fae paint; Bramble's mother had used it to label her jam jars, which was why no thief ever stole her damson preserve twice), were three lines:

**ALL WHO FLEE THE COURT MAY ENTER.**
**ALL WHO ENTER MUST PAY.**
**NO BARGAIN MADE HERE BINDS BEYOND THIS DOOR.**

"Read the last line twice," Bramble advised. "It's the important one. It's also the one people forget, because hope makes readers lazy."

---

Inside, the tollhouse was warm in the way that only buildings maintained by the desperately kind can be warm. A fire burned low and steady in a hearth built from stones that had clearly been carried up the mountain one at a time by someone with strong opinions about hearths. The air smelled of onion soup and tallow candles. Along one wall stood shelves crammed with mismatched mugs, each one chipped, each one clean.

Three figures sat within. An old woman with iron-grey hair braided through with wire—actual wire, Bramble noted, the kind ward-keepers used—mending a sock with the ferocity of someone stabbing an enemy. A broad man stirring a pot, humming. And a third figure who did not move at all: a tall person seated in the corner shadow, hands folded, face smooth as river stone, eyes closed.

The old woman looked up. Her gaze went first to Wren—the wire in her hair seemed to sit up straighter—and then to Silas, and then, last and longest, to Bramble.

"Well," she said. "A disgraced keeper, a Hollow child, and a fox-blooded peddler walk into my kitchen." She set down the sock. "If the next words out of anyone's mouth are 'we're not here for trouble,' I'm turning the soup off and we all suffer together."

"We're here for soup," Bramble said promptly. "Trouble is optional and we'd prefer to decline."

That earned him a short laugh from the pot-stirrer. "Maren," the woman said, jerking her chin at herself. "That's Odo, he cooks. That—" she nodded toward the still figure in the corner, "—is Pell. Pell doesn't speak anymore. Sold the words. Kept everything else, more or less."

Bramble felt Silas go still beside him. He understood why. *Sold the words.* In the Hollow, such a thing would be nonsense—a story told to frighten apprentices straight. Here, it was Tuesday.

"Pell will write, if it matters," Maren continued. "Mostly it doesn't. Mostly people just need to sit somewhere warm and be told the rules by somebody who survived learning them." She ladled four bowls without asking whether they wanted any. Nobody had ever come to the tollhouse wanting anything but soup; she'd simply stopped asking. "Sit. Eat. Then we talk terms."

They sat. They ate. The soup was potato and leek and something wild and green Bramble couldn't name, and it was the best thing he'd tasted since a honey-fair in Rillwater six years ago, and he told Odo so with his mouth full, which Odo appeared to regard as the highest form of compliment available to a customer.

It was Pell, of all people, who began the lesson. When the bowls were empty, the silent figure opened their eyes—pale grey, unblinking—and raised one hand. Maren handed them a slate and chalk, and Pell wrote in a small, precise hand:

**YOU ARE GOING UNDER THE MOUNTAIN.**

"How—" Wren started.

Pell underlined the sentence. Then wrote beneath it:

**EVERYONE WHO COMES HERE IS GOING UNDER THE MOUNTAIN. OR RUNNING FROM IT. YOU SMELL OF INTENT, NOT FLIGHT.**

Bramble snorted. "He's right. I've been running from that court for thirty years and I still smell like a man packing a picnic. These two smell like a funeral they intend to crash."

Maren pulled a chair to the fire and sat, and the wire in her braid caught the light. "Then listen, children, because I paid full tuition for this education and I resent how few students enroll." She held up one finger. "First rule. **Bargains bind absolutely.** Not mostly. Not unless. Absolutely. You say 'I promise' to one of them and mean it even a little, you have signed something. They don't need ink. Your intent is the ink. Your breath is the seal."

"Even a joke?" Silas asked quietly.

"*Especially* a joke. Humor is how they get you relaxed." Maren's mouth twisted. "The Court's whole architecture is contract. Their roads are clauses. Their seasons are payment schedules. You cannot fight a bargain with a sword. You can only read it better than they do—which is nearly impossible—or find the flaw in it, which they employ entire departments to prevent."

Second finger. "**Names are currency.** Not metaphorically. If they learn your true name, they own a piece of you the way a landlord owns a room. Speak carefully. Introduce yourself by trade or road-name. Never give your mother's name aloud below the mountain—it's the key to your own door, and they collect keys."

Bramble watched Silas's face at that, and saw the color drain out of it, and understood before either of them spoke. Elowen Ashgrove. Their mother's name was already in the Court's ledger. Had been for twenty years. Which meant—

"Which means the Court doesn't just hold her," Silas said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "It holds the key to *me*."

Maren didn't flinch. "Now you understand why nobody rescues anybody from that place. You can't steal back a hostage when the captors hold deeds to your own house." She poured tea from a dented pot. "Third rule. The hardest one. **The Thorn King never releases what he holds willingly.** Never. Not for love, not for justice, not for a hundred years of good behavior. Whatever leaves that court leaves because the contract says it must, or because something was traded that the King values more. There is no fourth option. People waste years searching for mercy in him. There isn't any. There's only arithmetic."

The fire popped. Pell wrote one more line on the slate and turned it around:

**THE KING IS ALSO BOUND. REMEMBER THAT.**

Maren frowned at it. "Pell believes that. I think it's wishful thinking." She shrugged. "But Pell paid more than I did to learn what he knows, so weigh it accordingly."

---

The talk of terms came after, and it was shorter than Bramble expected, because Maren's terms were simple: nothing. Soup, bed, bread in the morning, free. "Exiles don't charge exiles," she said. "We charge the Court, in our hearts, daily, with interest."

But there was one matter she would not let pass, and it was the passage itself.

"The gate beneath the mountain opens onto the Long Stair," she said. "And the Long Stair has a tollkeeper. Not one of the King's folk—an independent contractor, if you like, a creature called the Ferryman of Small Debts. He takes payment for crossing his stretch of the dark. Coin means nothing to him. Names he has plenty. What he wants—" she looked at Silas with something like pity, "—is weight. Something you carry that you'd rather not. A memory does nicely. Grief works. Regret is premium stock."

"And if you refuse to pay?" Wren asked.

"He lets you sit at the edge of his dark until winter seals the mountain," Maren said. "Which, given the year, is perhaps nine days away. So: refuse, and freeze politely. Pay, and lose a piece of yourself. Those are the options. Welcome to the foothills of the Thorn Court."

Silas set down their cup. Bramble watched them do it—watched the deliberate care of it, the way you set down something fragile when your hands want to shake.

"Which memory?" Silas asked.

"That's between you and the Ferryman. But I'll say this much, freely, no charge: choose something true but survivable. Everyone tries to pay with their worst memory, thinking pain equals value. It doesn't. Pain is common. What he prizes is *sweetness*. A sweet memory given up freely hurts the giver far more, and the Ferryman knows it, and respects it, and charges less for it." Maren smiled, and it was an old, tired, kind smile. "Mercy, in that place, is mostly a matter of good accounting."

---

Bramble slept badly, which was normal, and woke before dawn, which was also normal, and found Silas already awake, sitting on the doorstep with the letter against their chest and their breath fogging in the blue air.

He sat down next to them. For a while neither spoke. The sky was doing that pre-dawn thing where it couldn't decide on a color and committed to all of them badly.

"I've been meaning to ask," Bramble said, "and I keep not asking, because asking felt like prying. But we're about to do something stupid together, and I find stupidity goes easier with honesty. Why did you come for me? At the border, when the wards fell. You didn't know me. You could have left me standing outside your valley like a cat at a window."

Silas considered. "You knocked," they said finally. "Everyone else ran. You walked up to the broken stone and knocked on it, like the valley was a door and maybe someone was home."

"It was a very good knock. I've practiced."

"It was a ridiculous knock. I liked it." Silas almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in two days, and Bramble treasured it the way you treasure a coin you're pretty sure is counterfeit but beautiful anyway. "Also—you owed my mother a life. I wanted to know what kind of person owes my mother anything. Turns out: a peddler with bad boots and worse jokes."

"My boots are excellent. They've died twice and been resurrected once." He nudged them with his elbow. "For what it's worth—I never met her, your mother. I met a girl caught in a flood on the Thorn Road, twenty-odd years ago, who pulled me out of the water and asked for nothing. I only learned her name later, from a merchant who'd known her family. Elowen Ashgrove. I've been carrying the debt since. Debts are heavy, but they're honest weight. Better than most luggage."

Silas looked at him sideways. "Is that why you're really coming? The debt?"

"Partly. Partly because the Court took thirty years of my mother's life before she got us out, and I've never once gone back to look it in the eye. Partly because you two are hopeless without me—you'd trust the first charming liar who offered directions, and Wren would arrest him, and then where would we be?" He stretched his legs out. "And partly—don't repeat this—because I'm tired of running. Thirty years is a long time to be a rumor with a pack."

Silas was quiet a moment. "I won't repeat it," they said. "But I'll remember it."

From Bramble, that was a benediction.

---

They reached the Ferryman near midday.

The Long Stair descended into the mountain through a cleft in the rock, and at its head stood a structure that was not quite a bridge and not quite a door: an archway of black thornwood, grown rather than built, its branches woven so tightly that no light passed between them. Beyond it, the stair fell away into a darkness that had texture—that had, Bramble thought, *appetite*.

The Ferryman stood beneath the arch. He was difficult to describe, which Bramble suspected was intentional. Tall, robed in grey, his face a smooth oval with the suggestion of features the way clouds suggest ships. Around his neck hung hundreds of small glass vials on fine chains, each one holding a curl of smoke or a drop of light or a sound, and they chimed softly when he moved, a hundred tiny griefs ringing like a wind-bell shop.

"Crossers," he said, and his voice was pleasant and terrible, like a lullaby sung slightly too slow. "Three souls, one debt-bound, one disgraced, one hollow-born. I know what you are. Do you know what I take?"

"A memory," Silas said. "We were told."

"Told, yes. Told is cheap. Let me be precise." The vials stirred. "I take one memory, freely given, fully surrendered—you will not miss it vaguely, you will not dream around its edges. It will be *gone*, root and branch, as though it never happened. Choose well. Choose honestly. I will know if you try to pay me with something you've already half-discarded. Discarded goods fetch poor rates, and I add a surcharge for insult."

Wren stepped forward first. "Mine," she said, before either of them could speak. "Whatever the rate—I'll pay for all three."

The Ferryman regarded her for a long moment. "Generous. Also forbidden. Each crosser pays their own toll. That is the oldest clause on this stair, older than me, and I am older than the King's crown." His suggestion-of-a-face turned to Bramble. "Peddler. Your toll."

Bramble reached into himself the way he reached into his pack, feeling along the shelves of his own head. He chose quickly, because hesitation made the choosing worse. "The smell of my mother's kitchen," he said. "Bread and juniper and woodsmoke, mornings, before the Court took her. All of it—the whole memory, every morning of it."

The Ferryman lifted a vial from his neck, uncorked it, and held it out. Bramble breathed into it—one long exhale—and felt something leave him with the breath, some warmth going out of his chest like a coal passed from hand to hand. The vial glowed faintly amber, then dimmed to grey.

He tried, afterward, to remember the kitchen. He remembered that there had been one. He remembered the facts of it the way you remember a town you've only read about. The smell was gone. Just—gone. He stood there a moment feeling the shape of the absence, which was the worst part: absence has edges, and edges cut.

"Paid," the Ferryman said, and sounded, for just a moment, almost gentle. "Warden. Yours."

Wren paid a memory of her sister's voice singing, and came away from it white-faced and silent, gripping her own hands.

Then the Ferryman turned to Silas, and the vials all stilled at once, as if the whole necklace were listening.

"Hollow-born," he said. "Your reputation precedes you. The valley that forgot itself. You should be empty already—easy pickings. And yet." The suggestion of interest moved across the smooth face. "You carry something dense. I can feel it from here. What will you pay?"

Bramble watched Silas close their eyes. He saw their lips move—not speaking to the Ferryman. Speaking to themselves. Counting, maybe. Weighing, in Maren's phrase, sweetness against survivability.

When they opened their eyes, they said: "My grandmother teaching me to tend the boundary stones. The last summer she was well. Her hands over my hands on the stone, showing me the old words. All of it."

Bramble's chest tightened. Of everything—of the guild's decay, of the elders' cowardice, of the wards falling—he hadn't guessed they'd choose that. The one warm thing left from before everything curdled.

"Why that one?" he couldn't help asking.

Silas looked at him. "Because it's the sweetest thing I have," they said simply. "And because she signed the contract. And because—" their voice caught, steadied, "—because if I'm going to spend twenty years learning to hate what she did, I'd rather do it honestly. Without the part of me that loved her softening the truth. The Ferryman can have the love. I'll keep the anger. Anger travels lighter."

The Ferryman extended a vial of clear glass, finer than the others, older. Silas breathed out.

What left them wasn't visible. But Bramble heard it—a sound like a bell struck once, far away, in a valley at evening. The vial filled with a faint gold light, and the Ferryman corked it with a reverence he had shown nothing else, and hung it at the center of his necklace, where it chimed against its neighbors like a coin dropped in a church box.

"Paid in full," he said. "All three. Pass."

The thorn arch yawned open. Cold air rolled up the stair from below, smelling of stone and frost and something sweeter underneath, like flowers forced to bloom in the wrong season.

They descended. Bramble went last, as was his habit—rear guard, best view of retreats—and paused at the arch to look back up at the thin blade of daylight.

"Maren," he murmured, to no one, to the mountain, to whatever kept accounts in places like this. "She said mercy is good accounting. I hope she's right. Because I just checked my pockets, friend, and I'm running low."

Below, in the dark, the stair waited. And somewhere beneath them—Bramble could feel it now, a pressure like weather—a court of thorns was keeping a soul alive in a cell, unaged and furious, counting the days until winter sealed the mountain and the century turned.

Nine days, the tollhouse had said.

Eight, now.

The dark swallowed them gently, the way debts do.
