## 9. A Letter Twenty Years Late

The courier had been dead a long time.

Silas knew it the way you know bread has gone stale—by the smell, mostly, and by the way the body had settled into the snow like it was furniture. He lay in a hollow beneath an overhang of black rock, half-buried, his satchel still buckled across his chest with the neatness of a man who had expected to get up again soon. His coat was court-gray, trimmed in thorn-silver, and frost had grown through him in fern-patterns, delicate as lace, as if winter had decided to keep him rather than kill him.

"He's been here since before the tollhouse," Wren said. She crouched at the edge of the hollow, one hand hovering over the frost without touching it. "Since the first snows. Maybe longer."

"Then why hasn't anything eaten him?" Bramble asked from behind them, arms crossed against the cold. "Out here? A dead man in a nice coat is practically a dinner bell."

Wren gave him a look. "Because nothing that eats would touch him. Look at the frost."

Silas looked. The frost-ferns didn't stop at the coat. They grew *out* of it, spreading across the rock in careful spirals, and every spiral turned the same direction, like handwriting. Like a seal.

"He's carrying something," Silas said. "Something the court doesn't want found."

They dug him out together, which felt wrong and necessary in equal measure. Wren insisted on doing most of it—"You don't rob the dead with four hands when two will do"—and she worked quickly and gently, the way she did everything that mattered, as though efficiency were a form of respect. She unbuckled the satchel and laid it open on a flat stone.

Inside: a wax cylinder stamped with the Thorn Court's sigil, unbroken. A purse of coins that were warm to the touch, which none of them remarked on because remarking would have required thinking about it. A tin of travel biscuits, gone to dust. And, wrapped separately in oilcloth and tucked into an inner pocket like something he'd meant to deliver by hand—

A letter.

"It's addressed to the Hollow," Silas said, and their voice came out strange, flattened, the way sound goes when your ears have stopped working properly. "To the guild. To—to anyone. It says 'to whoever keeps the stones now.'"

Bramble went very still. Even Wren's hands paused over the satchel.

"You don't have to read it," she said quietly. "Not here. Not yet."

"Yes I do." Silas turned the letter over. The paper was heavy, good stock, the kind the guild used for contracts—the kind their grandmother had used to sign away a soul. On the back, in a different hand, someone had written: *Undeliverable. Hold.* And below that, in a third hand, older and angrier: *Hold until the century turns.*

Twenty years of *hold*. Twenty years of some clerk in a frozen office deciding that a mother's words were a filing problem.

Silas broke the seal.

* * *

The fire that night was small, because small fires were all the blighted land allowed—wood out here burned gray and gave off heat like an apology. They made camp in the lee of the courier's rock, and if it seemed grim to shelter beside a dead man, well. He'd kept the wind off for twenty years. Silas figured he wouldn't mind the company.

They ate the tollhouse's bread, which tasted faintly of rosemary and of the memory they'd traded for it—Silas could no longer quite recall the smell of the guild kitchen on baking day, only that there had been one, and that it had mattered. Grief by subtraction. You didn't notice what was gone until you reached for it.

Silas read the letter aloud. It seemed important that it be heard. Their mother had waited twenty years in a pocket; she could wait no longer than this.

*My name is Elowen Ashgrove,* it began, *and if you are reading this, then I did not come home, and someone has finally been brave enough or foolish enough to look for me. I am sorry for what you are about to feel. I remember feeling it myself, once, reading my mother's papers. It does not soften. But it does become useful, if you let it.*

Silas's hands shook. Wren moved closer—not touching, just near, the way you stand next to someone in high wind.

*I was taken on the eve of the renewal. I want you to understand that I knew. That is the first thing, and maybe the hardest: I knew. Grandmother told me everything the autumn before, all of it—the bargain, the soul, the price, the fact that the soul was mine. She wept when she told me. I had never seen her weep. I think she had been saving it up for sixty years.*

*She wanted to fight. She wanted to break the wards herself and let the whole valley stand naked before the court rather than pay. I talked her out of it, and I want you to know that it was not cowardice, mine or hers. I sat up all night with the contract and I read it the way she taught me to read, slowly, twice, aloud—and I understood what she could not, because she loved the valley too much to do the arithmetic.*

*If I run, they take someone else. That is the clause. There is always someone else. The court does not want me specifically; it wants an Ashgrove, because an Ashgrove signed, and debts follow signatures like wolves follow blood. If I run, it is you, or Aunt Sef, or the miller's daughter who happens to share our name three generations back. If I go quietly—if I walk down myself, on the appointed night, with my chin up—they take only me. One soul, held in trust. That is what the contract says, and for once the lawyers told the truth.*

*So I went. And it was fine. I mean that. I was frightened, but the road down was stranger than it was terrible, and the King—*

Here the writing stopped. When it resumed, the letters were smaller, pressed harder into the page.

*The King is not what the stories say. I will write more of him when I understand him myself. But I will say this much, because whoever finds this letter deserves one true thing: he looked at me when I was brought in, and his face—I have thought about his face every day for the length of time it took me to write this sentence, and I still cannot describe it except to say that it was the face of a man watching someone else be locked in his cell.*

*Tell the valley nothing. That is my last instruction and I need you to obey it, even though I suspect you are the sort of person who does not obey things. If they know, they will come, and if they come, the court will invoke the replacement clause, and I will have died for nothing in a cell under a mountain while everyone I love marches down to join me. Let them believe I left. Let them be angry with me. Anger keeps people alive better than grief does; grief makes them reckless.*

*But if you are reading this anyway—if twenty years passed and someone came looking regardless—then hear me, my darling, my stubborn one, whoever you grew into:*

*I am not sorry. Do not carry that for me. I bought you twenty years of sky with my walking, and I would buy you forty more. What I am sorry for is the birthday I missed, and the ones after it, and the fact that you learned to cook from Maren, whose idea of seasoning is salt and despair. I am sorry that I did not get to see whether you got Grandmother's stubbornness or your father's, assuming you ever learn which of the two men who claimed the title was actually—*

The letter devolved here into a paragraph of crossed-out lines, and then, at the bottom, in a final burst:

*It doesn't matter. None of the old business matters. Here is what matters: I loved you before I left and I have loved you every day since, and the court can hold my body and my name and my hundred years, but it cannot hold that, because I did not sign it over. Check the contract. I checked. Love is not in the inventory.*

*Come find me if you must. But come angry, not grieving. Angry people check the clauses.*

*Your mother,*
*Elowen*

*P.S. If it is Silas reading this—you were six. You bit a fae peddler at the boundary stone and I laughed so hard I cried. I hope you're still biting people who deserve it.*

* * *

Silas did not cry, exactly. What happened was more like weather—a pressure behind the eyes, a tightness in the chest, a long moment where the fire and the rock and the dead courier all went slightly out of focus, and Wren's hand arrived on their shoulder and stayed there, warm and steady, asking nothing.

"Bramble," Silas managed finally. "You were the fae peddler."

"I was *a* fae peddler," Bramble said, with tremendous dignity, from his side of the fire. "There are many of us. We are a whole profession."

"You sold Grandmother a cursed hat."

"I sold her a *novelty* hat, and the curse was load-bearing, and she got eleven excellent years out of it." He paused. "Also, for the record, the child who bit me drew blood. I have thought about that bite for twenty-five years. Half-fae healing, thank you, but still. You were a feral little thing."

"I was protecting the boundary stone."

"You were protecting a *rock*, and you were very brave about it, and that is why I owe your mother a life-debt instead of merely a grudge. Because she apologized for you so sincerely, and fed me so well, and asked me such good questions about the roads, that by the time I left your valley I had already decided that if her family ever needed anything, I would come running." He poked the fire. "I assumed it would be money. It's never money. It's always a mountain full of thorns."

Silas laughed—it came out wet and cracked, but it came—and that broke something loose, and then the crying happened after all, quiet and undignified, into Wren's shoulder, while the fire burned its apologetic gray and the dead courier kept the wind off.

Wren said nothing for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough in a way Silas hadn't heard before.

"My disgrace," she said. "The ward-line at Carrowmere. Everyone thinks I took bribes to let it fail." She watched the fire. "What actually happened is that I found a contract in the ward-stone's foundation. An old one. Royal signature. And I reported it, and the report went up the chain, and three days later the line failed and I was the one holding the paperwork." Her jaw tightened. "I've spent two years believing the court bought someone above me. Sitting here, listening to your mother—" She shook her head. "She's right, you know. About checking the clauses. Nobody checks the clauses. That's the whole trick. The entire architecture of these bargains is built on the assumption that grief makes people illiterate."

"She wrote it like a map," Silas said. "Come angry. Check the clauses. Love isn't in the inventory." They wiped their face. "She's been down there twenty years, composing arguments."

"Of course she has," Bramble said softly. "What else is there to do in a cell? She's been rehearsing for you. Every day. Twenty years of rehearsal." He looked up, and for once there was nothing flippant in his face at all. "Silas. Whatever's waiting down there—she has already argued it. You just have to arrive."

* * *

They buried the courier at dawn, or did the closest thing the frozen ground allowed: stacked stones over him, and Wren cut the court's sigil from his coat so that whatever accounts were kept, he'd be filed as a man and not a message. Silas tucked the letter inside their shirt, over their heart, where it weighed approximately nothing and approximately everything.

"One question," Wren said, shouldering her pack. "Why hold the letter? Why not destroy it?"

Silas considered. "Because destroying it wasn't in the instructions. The court binds people with rules. Maybe its clerks are bound the same way." They looked north, toward the mountain, where the sky had begun doing the thing it had done for days now—thinning, graying, shedding light like old cloth. "Or maybe somebody down there wanted it delivered eventually. Somebody who couldn't say so out loud."

"The King," Bramble murmured.

"The King," Silas agreed, and found that the word had changed shape overnight. It was harder to hate a man your mother pitied. Harder—but not impossible, and Silas held onto the impossible part carefully, the way you hold a knife.

Eight days, the tollhouse had said. Seven, now.

They walked. Behind them the courier's cairn shrank to a speck, a small human stubbornness against all that white. Ahead, the mountain had begun to show itself between the ridgelines—black and vast, wearing clouds like a hood pulled low—and as the light strengthened Silas saw, or thought they saw, a thin red gleam along its base, like a coal refusing to die.

Like a door, standing very slightly open.

And on the wind, faint enough to be imagination, came a smell that did not belong to any winter: woodsmoke, and roses, and underneath both, unmistakably, the specific burnt-sugar sweetness of a fae bargain being struck somewhere far below.

Bramble stopped walking. His ears—those clever, mobile, traitorous ears—went flat.

"That's not supposed to happen yet," he said. "That's a *court* smell. That's the court awake and dealing." He turned to them, and his face had gone the color of old ash. "Someone's bargaining down there. Right now. And there's only one person it could be."

Silas's hand went to the letter over their heart.

Their mother had been rehearsing for twenty years.

Apparently, she'd decided to stop waiting.
