## 10. The Court's Herald

Wren had learned to read weather the way other people read letters—quickly, and with an eye for what was being hidden. The sky that morning was doing something she didn't like at all.

It wasn't snowing. That was the problem. For six days the winter had chased them up the old courier road like a debt collector, flurries at their heels, frost climbing the fenceposts behind them faster than they could walk. Now, abruptly, it had stopped. The air sat still and pale as a held breath, and the clouds overhead had arranged themselves into a ring, a neat gray circle with a hole in the middle through which no light came.

"Nobody's going to say anything about the sky?" she asked.

Bramble glanced up from where he was re-lacing his boots on a milestone. "I say things about the sky constantly. You've complained about my commentary twice before breakfast."

"I mean something useful."

"It's a court sky," Silas said quietly. They'd stopped walking without seeming to decide to, the way you stop when you hear your name called in a crowd. Their hand had gone to the contract fragment inside their coat—the scorched scrap of parchment they'd been carrying like both compass and wound since the archive. "I've never seen one. But I've read about them. When the Thorn Court wants to talk to someone, it clears a space first. Tidies up."

"Tidies," Wren repeated. "The sky is being *tidied* for us."

"For one of us." Silas's jaw did the thing it did when they were frightened and refusing to be. Wren had catalogued all of Silas's faces by now: the stubborn one, the stubborn-but-trying-to-be-polite one, the one that meant they hadn't slept. This was a new one. This was the face of someone standing in a doorway they'd only ever seen in nightmares.

Bramble finished his bootlace with unnecessary flourish and stood. "Right. Well. In my professional experience—and I have been intercepted by no fewer than four courts, two guilds, one very possessive river spirit, and a tax collector from Marrowdale—when the sky makes a room for you, the polite thing is to wait in it. Running just means they make the room bigger."

"I hate that you're right," Wren said.

"I know. It's one of my best qualities."

They waited in the tidied sky.

It took less time than Wren would have liked. She'd hoped, briefly and unprofessionally, for hours—a chance to eat, to argue about the route, to not think about the fact that three days ago Silas had fallen asleep against her shoulder on watch and she had let them stay there far longer than any sensible truce required. Instead the road ahead simply *changed*. Frost crawled backward off the stones. Dead gorse along the verge shivered and put out blossoms of black glass. And down the road, walking toward them at the pace of a man arriving slightly early for an appointment he'd scheduled himself, came the herald.

He looked almost ordinary, which was the worst thing about him. A tall figure in a coat the color of wet bark, buttons of pale wood, a scarf wound high enough to hide everything below his eyes. His skin, where it showed, had the faint grain of birch. He carried no weapon. He carried, instead, a ledger bound in green leather, and a small silver bell that made no sound when it swung.

"Good morning," he said, and his voice was pleasant, dry, exactly the voice of a clerk reading terms aloud. "Warden Ashgrove. Keeper Vane. Master Bramblethistle." He inclined his head to each of them in turn, and Wren felt her spine go cold at the last name. Bramble had never once given them his full name. She'd assumed he didn't have one worth giving.

"Bramble is fine," Bramble said, in a voice that was not fine at all.

"Naturally." The herald smiled with the lower half of his face only. "I am Corven, herald of the Thorn Court, and I come bearing good news. Genuinely. I'd ask you to check my expression, but I'm told it doesn't move much in this weather."

"You'll forgive us," Wren said, stepping half in front of Silas, "if we don't celebrate."

"Warden Vane." He said her name like he was setting it down carefully on a shelf. "Royal Ward-Keepers' Guild, third circle, until the incident at Cael Ford. I've read your file. It's thinner than your reputation suggests, which I suspect will please you."

"My file is sealed."

"The Court reads sealed files the way you read open ones. It's one of our few advantages over the living." He turned the ledger open against his forearm with a practiced flick. "But I'm not here for you, Warden, or for Master Bramblethistle's colorful history. I'm here for the Ashgrove matter. And I am authorized—I want that word understood, *authorized*, under seal, witnessed—to offer a discharge."

Silas went very still beside her. Wren watched their hand press flat against their coat, over the contract fragment, as if holding it shut.

"A discharge," Silas said.

"The debt held against Elowen Ashgrove may be discharged by any blood of her line." Corven's voice stayed level, kind, almost gentle. "Any. Not the named party. Not the eldest. Any descendant of the founding signatory's line who stands willing before the Court. You are, as far as our records show, the only candidate remaining—which simplifies matters wonderfully. One signature, freely given, and your mother walks free. Today. Before the deep winter seals the passes. She goes home, Warden. Isn't that what you crossed the blighted country for?"

The silence afterward was enormous.

Wren had spent eleven years training herself to notice the shape of traps—not the teeth, which everyone sees, but the shape: the way a snare is always positioned where the game already wants to go. And every instinct she owned stood up and started shouting. Because here was the thing about mercy offered by a court of thorns: it arrived precisely, perfectly, at the moment you wanted it most, wearing exactly the face you'd been praying for.

She looked at Silas and saw the trap working. Not because Silas was foolish—Silas was many things, brave and stubborn and maddeningly literal, but not foolish—but because Silas was *tired*, and grieving, and had spent twenty years believing their mother had simply walked out of the valley and left them. Of course they wanted to believe the door out was this easy. Anyone would.

"That's remarkably generous," Wren said, keeping her voice flat and official, warden-to-clerk. "The Court isn't known for generosity."

"The Court is known for honoring its contracts," Corven corrected mildly. "Generosity is merely what honoring looks like from the outside, sometimes." He produced a pen from somewhere inside his coat—it seemed to grow out of his sleeve, a slender white thing, quill or thorn, and Wren decided firmly it was a thorn—and laid the ledger on the milestone between them. The page was already written. Of course it was. Everything but the signature.

"Before anyone signs anything," Wren said, "I'd like to read the full text. Aloud. All clauses."

Corven's smile didn't waver, but something behind his eyes did—a flicker, quick as a fish turning. "That won't be necessary. The operative clause is the one I've quoted."

"In my experience," Wren said, "the operative clause is always the one nobody quotes."

"Warden." He closed the ledger gently, keeping one long finger in the page. "You are not party to this instrument. With respect, its terms are not yours to audit."

"Then you won't mind if she reads it anyway," Silas said.

Wren blinked. So, visibly, did the herald.

"They're right," Bramble added, recovering fast, arms crossing. "Terrible habit of theirs. Reading things. Asking questions. Honestly, it's exhausting traveling with them, but there it is."

Corven regarded Silas for a long moment. Then he reopened the ledger and turned it, obligingly, toward them. "Of course. We have nothing to hide, Warden Ashgrove. Read whatever comforts you."

Which was, Wren thought grimly, exactly what a man says when he knows the trap isn't on the page.

* * *

Silas read. Wren read over their shoulder, because she was going to read it whether invited or not, and after a moment Bramble crowded in too, so that the three of them stood bent over the milestone like conspirators over a stolen letter.

The text was beautiful. That was the first thing Wren noticed, and it set her teeth on edge. Court drafting always was—clauses nested like flowers, language so clean it seemed to shine. Discharge of the debt of Elowen Ashgrove upon the voluntary undertaking of any of her blood. Release of the named party, unconditional, immediate. Safe passage home for the released party, guaranteed by the Court's own word.

And then, near the bottom, in a section titled *Incidental Provisions*, in letters no larger than any other:

*...the discharging party shall enter into such custody, term, and condition as the discharged party did hold; the debt being perpetual, and the vessel interchangeable.*

There it was. Not hidden. Not even disguised. Just buried—set among provisions about witnesses and weather and the return of personal effects, in prose so smooth your eye would skate across it, especially an eye full of tears, especially an eye that had just read the words *your mother walks free.*

Wren looked up. Corven was watching Silas, not the page. Watching Silas's face with an attention that had nothing clerical in it at all. And she understood, suddenly and completely, that the loophole was real—that every word of it would be honored to the letter, because the letter was the whole point. The Court didn't need to cheat. It needed Silas to *want* this. It needed the wanting to do the work.

Because a contract signed under duress could be challenged. A contract signed in ignorance might be. But a contract signed *knowingly*—signed by someone who had read the interchangeable-vessel clause with their own eyes and put their name beneath it anyway, out of love—

That was iron. That was the kind of bargain even stars couldn't unmake.

"Well," Bramble said into the silence, very lightly, "that's a load of fertiliser, isn't it."

"Master Bramblethistle—"

"Don't." All the peddler's warmth had gone out of his voice, leaving something older underneath, something with bark on it. "You dressed it up as mercy and you brought it to a child. Twenty years old and you brought a thorn-pen and a pre-written page and a sky with a hole in it, and you call that good news. My grandmother dealt with your lot. I know the smell of a fair-sounding knife."

"The clause is disclosed," Corven said. "Freely disclosed. The Warden has read it. There is no deception here—only choice. Which is more than most mortals get." His gaze slid back to Silas, and softened, and that softness was the cruelest thing he'd done yet. "Think of her, Warden. Twenty years in the dark, unaged, unforgotten. Think of her face when the cell door opens. Think of her walking home under a clean sky. All of it costs you one signature. Refuse, and you cost her another century—for pride? For procedure? Warden Vane can recite clauses at the mountain gate. Your mother cannot wait for poetry."

"Stop," Wren said.

Corven stopped. Even the wind, such as it was, seemed to stop.

"Not because you're wrong about the clause," she said. "You're not. It's airtight. I've drafted enough wards to know airtight when I see it." She stepped forward, putting herself squarely in the tidy space the sky had made, and let eleven years of guild discipline settle over her voice like armor. "But I've also seen what happens to people who sign your instruments. I investigated the Cael Ford collapse, herald. Do you know what I found at the bottom of it? A bargain. Freely entered. Knowingly entered. Every clause disclosed. And a village that stopped existing." She let that sit. "So here's my counteroffer. Nobody signs today. The Warden takes the night to consider—which, unless your contract forbids it, is customary."

For the first time, Corven hesitated.

It was small—a half-breath, a fractional tilt of the birch-grain head—but Wren had interrogated smugglers and worse, and she knew hesitation when she saw it. He didn't want the night. The offer had a shelf life. Whatever was coming, whatever the Court feared or awaited, it wanted the signature *now*, warm and desperate, before anyone slept on it.

"One night," Silas said quietly.

Corven turned to them. "Warden—"

"One night," Silas repeated, and there was something in their voice Wren hadn't heard before—not the stubbornness, not the fear. Something steadier. "You said freely given. Freely means I choose when. Take your ledger back to your king and tell him the Ashgrove blood read every word, and will give its answer at dawn. If that displeases the Court, it may take the matter up with the clause about voluntariness. Section four. I checked."

A long moment. Then Corven bowed—deeper than before, and somehow that was worse—and closed the ledger. "Dawn, then. I will await your answer at the tollhouse road." He straightened, and his eyes found Wren's, and for an instant the pleasant clerk fell away and she saw something else looking out: tired, old, and—she would swear this later, and no one would quite believe her—*sorry*. "Keeper Vane. A word of professional courtesy, since you enjoy reading things. When you examine the founding contract—and you will—you should look at how it was witnessed. Not what it says. How it was witnessed." A pause. "The Court's hands are tied. Yours needn't be."

Then he walked back down the road the way he'd come, and the black-glass blossoms fell off the gorse, and the ring in the sky closed like an eye, and the snow began again as though it had never stopped.

* * *

They didn't speak for a mile. Then Bramble said, "Well. I need a drink," and Wren said, "We don't have a drink," and Bramble said, "Then I need to complain at length about the absence of drink," and the ordinary rhythm of them reassembled itself, piece by piece, like a dropped stitch worked back in.

It was Silas who finally broke, at the next bend, stopping in the middle of the road with snow gathering on their shoulders.

"He was right," they said. "That's the horrible part. If I'd signed—if either of you hadn't been there—I'd have signed. I *wanted* to sign. I read 'interchangeable vessel' with my own eyes and some part of me was already reaching for the pen, because twenty years, Wren. She's been in the dark for twenty years and I get to fix it with one signature. What kind of person does the math like that and hesitates?"

"The kind who has friends standing next to them," Wren said.

Silas laughed, wetly, and wiped their face with a sleeve. "Is that what we are? Friends?"

"I'm still deciding. You snore."

"I do not snore."

"On watch, Tuesday. Like a millwheel."

"That was Bramble."

"It was definitely you," Bramble said comfortably, from behind. "I sleep beautifully. It's one of my best qualities."

But Wren wasn't really listening anymore, because her mind had snagged on the herald's parting words and refused to let go. *Look at how it was witnessed.* A clerk of the Thorn Court—an instrument of the thing that had eaten twenty years of Elowen Ashgrove—had gone out of his way, at risk, to hand them a thread. Which meant either it was bait, layered inside the trap like a hook within a hook.

Or the Thorn Court contained at least one prisoner who wanted the same thing they did.

"Dawn," Silas said, squaring their shoulders against the snow. "One night to find an answer that isn't a signature."

"No," Wren said, and dug the contract fragment from her satchel, holding it up so the firelight of their little camp-catch caught the scorch marks along its edge. "One night to find out why a contract needs a witness at all—and what happens when the witness stops watching."

Above them, through the thinning cloud, one star appeared.

Just one. Faint, trembling, and utterly impossible.

All three of them stared at it, and none of them moved, and somewhere beneath the mountain, Wren would have sworn she heard something begin—very slowly, very quietly—to count.
