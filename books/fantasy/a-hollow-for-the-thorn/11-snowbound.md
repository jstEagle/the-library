## 11. Snowbound

Bramble had survived two hundred years of roads by following one rule: never sleep anywhere the wind could find you. So when the white wall came down off the northern crags like a curtain falling on a bad play, he was already shouting at the other two to move, and when they didn't move fast enough, he was already dragging them by their collars toward the black seam in the rockface that his nose—half-fae, half-hound in spirit if not in fact—had caught a quarter mile back.

"Inside," he said. "Inside, inside, *inside*."

Silas went first. Wren went second, because Wren was the sort of person who argued with weather and lost but insisted on arguing anyway. Bramble came last, hauling their packs, and the winter arrived behind him like a creditor.

The seam opened into a cave that smelled of old stone and older cold, and then into a chamber beyond it, and then—when Bramble struck a light and held it up—a chamber tall enough that the flame couldn't find its ceiling. Stalactites hung down like the teeth of something patient. Along one wall ran a shelf of stone, smooth as a bench, as though someone had once meant this place for sitting.

"Somebody lived here once," Silas said, voice echoing.

"Somebody froze here once, more likely," said Wren. She was already circling the chamber with her ward-keeper's habits, trailing one finger along the walls, checking for cracks, for drafts, for anything that might be a door wearing a disguise. "It'll hold. Probably."

"Probably is my favorite word," Bramble said, setting down the packs. "Right after *free* and right before *wine*."

* * *

They built what fire they could from what they carried: a stub of tallow, a bundle of pine knots Bramble had been hoarding since the blighted road, and—after some negotiation—a chair leg from the tollhouse that Wren had pocketed without asking and that nobody asked her about now. The fire caught reluctantly, threw more shadow than heat, and settled into a sullen orange glow that made the stalactites look less like teeth and more like, say, the fingers of something patient instead.

Outside, the wind found its voice. It was not a howl, exactly. It was worse than a howl—it was conversational. It rose and fell in long sighs, and every so often it seemed to pause, as if listening, before continuing whatever it had been saying.

"The court's winter," Wren said. She'd taken up her post facing the entrance, sword across her knees, because apparently even in a cave during a blizzard she intended to be useful. "It moves ahead of us. Herding us."

"Toward the mountain," Silas said.

"Toward the mountain," Wren agreed.

"Well," said Bramble, settling himself on the stone bench with the air of a man arranging his own funeral, "I've been herded by worse. There was a woman in Gildermere who herded me into marrying her for nearly a season. The winter at least won't ask me to fix her roof."

Silas laughed. It was a small laugh, tired, but real, and Bramble noted it the way a merchant notes a coin of unexpected weight. He had been watching these two for days now—the way they orbited each other, all sharp edges and borrowed glances, two people so busy being guarded that neither had noticed they'd stopped guarding against each other. Half-fae saw such things clearly. It was practically a professional obligation.

He decided, in the way of half-fae everywhere, that the storm outside was an excellent excuse to meddle.

"So," he said, rubbing his hands over the fire. "Trapped. Possibly for days. No wine." He sighed theatrically. "We shall have to make our own entertainment. Truth-telling. It's free, it's warm, and unlike wine, it doesn't give you a headache."

"No," said Wren immediately.

"Wonderful, I'll start," said Bramble, because he had learned long ago that the fastest way to get humans to do a thing was to forbid them from watching you do it first. "You know I owe Elowen Ashgrove a life-debt. You know I'm here to pay it. What you don't know—" he paused, enjoying himself, letting the firelight do dramatic things to his face, "—is *how* I owe it."

Silas leaned forward despite themselves. Bramble counted that a victory.

"I was young," he began. "Younger than you, Ashgrove, and twice as foolish, which given your recent decisions is saying something. I'd run away from my mother's people—they didn't care for my human half, and my father's people didn't care for my fae half, and I decided the solution was to belong to nobody, which is a decision that sounds very romantic until you're hungry. I was stealing apples from an orchard that turned out to belong to a hedge-witch with strong opinions about theft and stronger hands. She had me by the collar, and she had a pruning hook, and I was composing what I believed would be my final witticism, when a woman walked out of the trees."

He let the silence sit. Outside, the wind sighed.

"She wasn't armed. She wasn't large. But she looked at the witch the way a teacher looks at a child who has just broken something precious, and the witch—this woman who feared nothing, I'd seen her hex a tax collector—put me down. Apologized. To *me*. And Elowen Ashgrove paid for the apples, sat me on her kitchen step, fed me bread and honey, and asked me exactly one question: *Are you running from something, or toward something?*"

"And you said?" Silas asked softly.

"I said 'away,' which was honest, and she said, 'Then run toward my valley for a while. Nobody will chase you there.'" Bramble spread his hands. "She saved my life twice that day, once from the pruning hook and once from myself. I never repaid it. I told myself I would, and then the wards closed, and the valley vanished from every map I knew how to read, and twenty years went by the way years do when you're ashamed—quickly, and always in the wrong direction."

The fire popped. Neither of the others spoke, which Bramble took as the particular silence of people who understood shame professionally.

"Now you know mine," he said briskly, before the moment could get any heavier than he liked. "Wren. Your turn. And don't say 'there's nothing to tell,' because I've traveled with disgraced soldiers before and they all have the same look, like a dog that's been shouted at by someone it loved."

Wren's jaw did something complicated. For a long moment Bramble thought she'd refuse—and then, perhaps because the wind chose that moment to press against the cave mouth like a hand testing a latch, she looked at the fire instead of at them and began.

"You know I was a royal ward-keeper. You may not know what that means. It means I kept the borders of the realm—not walls, lines. Agreements written in stone and salt and starlight, the old kind, the binding kind. Every ward has a keeper, and every keeper swears the same oath: *the line holds while I live, and I live while the line holds.*" She said it flatly, the way you recite something you've said too many times to feel anymore. "Four years ago, the eastern line failed. The Fenmarch crossing. Something came through—something that shouldn't have been able to, because the ward was sound, because *I* had checked it myself, twice, the week before."

"What came through?" Silas asked.

"A thorn-wight." Wren's voice didn't change, but her knuckles whitened on her sword hilt. "It took three villagers before we brought it down. A boy of eight among them." She was quiet a moment. "The inquiry wanted to know how a sound ward failed. I wanted to know too. I tore apart every record, every supply ledger, every maintenance log. And I found it. The heartstone for the Fenmarch line hadn't degraded. It had been *replaced*—swapped for a flawed twin, months earlier, by someone with access to the deep stores. Someone inside the order."

Bramble felt the cold come off her like a second draft.

"I reported it. That was my mistake. Not finding it—reporting it. Because the trail led upward, to a quartermaster, and the quartermaster led to a name I wasn't permitted to speak, and within a month the inquiry reversed itself, declared the failure natural, and declared me unstable. Grief-stricken. Unfit." She smiled, and it was not a smile Bramble would have liked to receive. "They didn't strip my rank. They let me keep the title and took everything under it. A keeper with no line to keep. Do you know what that is? It's a bell with no rope. They hang you where everyone can see you and no one can ring you."

"And so you hunt whoever broke the wards," Silas said slowly. "Not to clear your name."

"My name is gone. Names are currency, remember? Mine was spent." Wren finally looked up, and her eyes were steady and furious and very tired. "I hunt because somewhere there is a person who sold a hole in the world for whatever price they were paid, and a boy of eight went through it. Somebody has to be the one who doesn't stop looking. If not me, who?"

Silence again—but a different silence, the kind that comes after something true. Bramble nodded slowly to himself. He'd suspected as much; half-fae always suspect, it's cheaper than trusting. But suspicion confirmed is its own small gift.

"That's why you believed me," Silas said. "At the broken stone. When I said the wards didn't fail—they were *let* fail."

"I know what a sabotaged ward looks like," Wren said simply. "I've spent four years learning to see nothing else."

* * *

The fire burned lower. Bramble fed it another knot of pine and decided the evening had reached precisely the temperature of honesty at which the third truth should be invited out of hiding. He did it gently, or as gently as a man shaped like a hedgerow can.

"And you, Ashgrove," he said. "You've heard ours. Fair trade says—"

"There's nothing fair about trade," Silas said, but without bite. They pulled their cloak tighter and stared into the flames, and for a while Bramble thought that would be all. Then:

"I'm afraid I'm not rescuing her."

The words came out small. Smaller than anything Bramble had heard from Silas since the valley shrank behind them.

"I've been turning it over since the tollhouse. Since I traded the memory of my mother's voice singing—for safe passage, which was stupid, I know it was stupid, but I panicked and the toll-keeper's eyes were so *patient*—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "Since then I keep thinking: what if the herald was right? What if the court doesn't want to punish me. What if it wants to *collect* me. What if I'm not coming to take her place back. What if I'm coming to fill it. What if everything I do—the contract fragment, the questions, walking through that door at the mountain's mouth—all of it is just the bargain digesting. Pulling me in politely."

"Silas—"

"Think about it." Their voice cracked and steadied, cracked and steadied, like ice underfoot. "Grandmother signed. Mother was taken. Now the debt falls to the last Ashgrove, and the last Ashgrove walks willingly into the dark, alone, answering honestly, exactly as the contract requires. If I were writing a trap, Wren, I would write *this*. And I can't tell—from the inside—whether I'm the rescuer or the payment. That's what frightens me. Not the thorns. Not the King. That I might walk all this way and never once have been free to turn around."

The wind outside rose, sighed, and fell. The fire spat. Bramble watched Wren, and watched her decide something.

She got up. Crossed the small space between them—four steps, maybe five, though Bramble had watched those steps take three weeks to become possible—and sat down beside Silas on the cold stone, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"Listen to me," Wren said. "I kept a line for eleven years. I checked it twice a week. I slept on the wrong side of it in all weathers, and I never once doubted it would hold, until the day it didn't—and here is what four years of disgrace taught me." She turned, and Bramble saw her take Silas's chin in her hand, gently, turning their face from the fire to her. "A trap doesn't need you to believe you're free. But *you* don't need to believe it either. You need to act free. The difference between a prisoner walking a corridor and a person walking a corridor is nothing—nothing except what they intend at the far end. So intend. Walk in intending to walk out. Let the court think what it likes. Let the contract think what it likes. *You* know why you came."

"To bring her home," Silas whispered.

"To bring her home," Wren agreed. "And anyone—including yourself—who tells you otherwise is standing between you and the door."

Silas made a sound that was half laugh and half something wetter, and leaned into her, forehead against Wren's shoulder, and Wren—stiffly at first, like a woman remembering an abandoned language—put her arm around them and held on.

Bramble looked discreetly at the ceiling. Well. As discreetly as a man shaped like a hedgerow can, which is to say he grinned at the ceiling, but silently, and with great warmth.

"For what it's worth," he offered, after a decent interval, "I've dealt with the Thorn Court's agents my whole life, and I'll tell you a secret about traps: they only work on people who go quietly. You, Ashgrove, argue with weather. You argued with a toll-keeper over a memory you'd already lost. You'll argue with a contract. Courts hate that."

"They really do," said a new voice.

All three of them started hard enough to scatter sparks. Bramble's hand went to his knife; Wren was on her feet with her sword drawn before the echo settled; Silas scrambled upright between them.

The voice had come from deeper in the cave—from the dark past the fire's reach, where the passage bent away into the mountain. And now, out of that dark, unhurried, pale as paper and dressed in winter-gray, stepped a figure they had last seen on the blighted road with a herald's chain of black iron and a smile like a locked door.

"The mountain's mouth is two days' walk," said the herald of the Thorn Court, "and the snow will not lift. Fortunately for you, I know a shorter way." His smile widened by a single tooth. "Shall we talk terms?"
