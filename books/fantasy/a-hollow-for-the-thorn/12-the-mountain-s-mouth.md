## 12. The Mountain's Mouth

The mountain did not look like a prison from the outside. It looked like a grandfather asleep in a chair—broad-shouldered, snow-dusted, radiating the kind of patience that made you want to whisper.

Silas felt no comfort in it whatsoever.

They had climbed for two days through country that grew stranger the higher they went. The blighted fields of the lowlands had given way to forests of black pine, and the pines had given way to something worse: trees of actual black iron, their branches hung with frost that glittered even though there was nothing left overhead to glitter at. The sky had been a smooth grey lid for three days now. Wren kept glancing up at it the way a person checks a locked door they already know is locked.

"There," Bramble said, and pointed with his chin, because his hands were full of rope, cheese, and a lantern he refused to explain.

Below them, where the mountain's roots met a valley of stone, stood a door.

It was not set into a wall. There was no wall. The door simply stood upright in the open air, framed in silvered wood tall as three men, and behind it—this was the part that made Silas's stomach turn over—behind it was *stone*. Solid mountainside, unbroken, going up forever. The door led nowhere except into the mountain itself, which meant it led somewhere after all, which meant everything Silas thought they understood about doors needed revising.

"It's smaller than I imagined," Wren said.

"You imagined it?" Silas asked.

"I imagine every door before I meet it. Professional habit." Wren crouched, brushing snow from the threshold with a ward-keeper's careful fingers. "No wards. No locks. No hinges on this side." She looked up, and for once her face held none of its usual armored dryness. Just wariness, and under it, unwillingly, awe. "It doesn't keep people out, Silas. It decides who comes in."

"Then we knock," Bramble said cheerfully, "and lie beautifully."

"No," said the door.

All three of them jumped. The voice came from everywhere—the frost, the iron trees, the space between Silas's ribs. It was not loud. It was the voice of someone who has been asked the same question ten thousand times and has never once been surprised by the answer.

"I beg your pardon," Bramble said, recovering first, because of course he did. "We hadn't actually knocked yet."

"You intended to. You intended to knock and say you are a tinker named Goodfellow Peddleson with a delivery of copper nails for the court below." A pause, delicate as frost forming. "You are not. You have no copper nails. Your name is Bramble, you are half-fae by your mother's side, and you owe a life-debt to Elowen Ashgrove, which you have mentioned eleven times in nine days, usually when you wanted something."

Bramble opened his mouth. Closed it. Set down the cheese with great dignity. "Well," he said. "This is going to be more difficult than advertised."

* * *

They made camp out of the door's earshot—or what they hoped was out of its earshot, though Silas suspected the concept didn't apply—and argued by firelight.

"It only spoke to Bramble," Wren said, feeding sticks to the flames. "Maybe it takes visitors one at a time."

"Or maybe it's softening us up," Bramble said. He was uncharacteristically quiet, turning his lantern over in his hands. "Doors like that one don't ask riddles to be clever. They ask because the thing behind them can't afford mistakes. Whatever's down there—" He stopped himself, glanced at Silas, and finished differently: "Whatever's down there, it wants to be sure."

"My mother's down there," Silas said. It came out flatter than they intended, so they said it again with the feeling intact: "My mother is down there, and winter seals this mountain in—I don't know, weeks? Days? We don't have time for me to develop a rapport with a talking door."

Wren snorted. "You developed a rapport with me in an afternoon, and I was actively trying to arrest you."

"That was different. You were armed and rude. This door is unarmed and rude."

"Rude?" Bramble looked wounded. "I thought it was rather elegant. Cruel, but elegant. Like my aunt."

They laughed—briefly, gratefully, the way people laugh when the alternative is sitting with the fear. Then the laughter ran out, the way fuel runs out, and the fire crackled into the silence, and Silas looked at the two people who had walked out of the world and into the winter for a woman they'd never met, and felt the weight of what tomorrow would ask of them settle onto their chest like a stone laid there by a careful hand.

"Wren," Silas said. "If it only lets one of us through—"

"Don't."

"—then you take the contract fragment and go back down. Get word to the guild. Make the elders—"

"*Don't.*" Wren's voice cracked like a whip, and then she winced at herself, and then, visibly deciding honesty was cheaper than pride, she said: "I spent six years being the person who follows orders and goes back and reports. It got my whole watch killed and my name struck from the rolls. I am not going back down this mountain to write a report, Silas Ashgrove. If you go through that door alone, I will find another way in if I have to dig."

"She means it," Bramble said. "She told me she'd dig. She's terrible at digging. I've seen her dig."

"Thank you, Bramble."

"Her technique is all shoulders."

"Bramble."

Silas smiled despite everything. It hurt to smile, the way cold hands hurt when they warm. "Okay," they said softly. "Nobody goes back. Nobody digs. We figure it out together."

But lying in the frozen dark afterward, wrapped in blankets that had long since lost their argument with the cold, Silas stared up at the blank grey sky and knew, with the certainty that lives in the body rather than the mind, that the door was going to take them alone. Doors like that always did. They took the piece of you that was truest and shut the rest outside.

They slept badly, and dreamed of stars.

* * *

Morning came without a sunrise, which by now felt less like an omen and more like a routine insult.

They broke camp quickly, ate standing up, and walked down into the stone valley in a silence none of them quite knew how to break. The door grew as they approached—not larger exactly, but *more*, the way a storm is more than clouds. Up close, Silas could see that the silvered wood was carved all over with writing in no alphabet they knew, and that the carvings moved, very slowly, like things written in sleep.

Bramble stopped twenty paces out. Wren stopped beside him.

Neither of them had discussed it. They just stopped, as if some instinct older than either of them told them this was the line.

Silas walked on alone.

Up close, the door smelled of cold iron and, strangely, of plums. Of a pantry in autumn. Of somewhere warm and specific and gone. Silas's throat tightened and they couldn't have said why, except that they could: it smelled faintly, impossibly, of home. Of the guild kitchen. Of the valley that had been bought with their mother's soul and sold to them as safety.

"Elowen Ashgrove's child," the door said. Not asking. Knowing.

"Yes."

"The court below holds her. The court below holds many. The court below does not wish to be disturbed, and neither do I, and yet here you are." The carvings rippled. "I am the Mouth of the Mountain. I do not keep passwords, because passwords can be stolen. I do not keep keys, because keys can be copied. I ask questions, and I weigh the answers, and I admit or refuse. Three questions. Answer honestly. Lie to me once and you will walk away from this door and forget why you came, and you will live a long and reasonably pleasant life, and you will never find this valley again. Do you understand?"

Silas's heart was hammering somewhere up around their ears. "Yes."

"Liar," said the door, almost gently. "No one understands until afterward. But it will do. First question."

The frost on the ground stirred. The iron trees leaned in.

"Why have you come?"

Such a small question. Such an enormous one. Silas opened their mouth to say *to rescue my mother*, which was true, and heard beneath it the other truths crowding up: *because the guild lied to me my whole life; because she went quietly so I could stay safe and I cannot bear that she paid for my safety with herself; because if I don't go I will spend the rest of my life being the person who stayed.*

They said all of it. Stumbling, half-coherent, tears freezing on their cheeks in the cold, they said all of it, and the door listened without interruption, and when Silas finally ran out of words the silence stretched so long that Wren, twenty paces back, took an audible breath.

"Honesty," the door said at last, "is rarely tidy. That answer was a disaster. It was also entirely true." The carvings shivered, and one line of silver script sank into the wood and vanished. "Second question."

Silas wiped their face with a sleeve already stiff with frost. "Ask."

"What will you sacrifice?"

And there it was. The real question. The one underneath every question since midwinter's eve, since the scorched contract fragment, since *one soul, held in trust*. Silas thought about the tollhouse, and the memory they'd traded—a whole afternoon, their ninth birthday, their mother's hands flour-white, laughing at something Silas couldn't picture anymore. Gone. Sold for safe passage. They thought about how much of themselves they had already been spending, coin by coin, on this road.

"I don't know," Silas said honestly, because they didn't. "I'd like to say nothing. I'd like to say I'll be clever enough that nobody has to pay. But I know how bargains work now—Bramble taught me, and the tollhouse taught me, and my grandmother's signature taught me. Somebody pays. So: whatever it costs to bring her out, I'll pay it. My memories. My years. If it comes to it—" Their voice caught. "If it comes to it, myself. I'm not saying it bravely. I'm saying it terrified. But I'm saying it."

The silence again. Longer. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the iron trees with a sound like slow applause.

"Terrified truth," the door murmured, "outweighs brave lies. Always has." Another line of script sank away. "Last question. And hear me, child of the Hollow: this one I ask for myself, and I have asked it for nine hundred years, and no one has ever answered it well."

The frost went utterly still. Even the wind stopped.

"If you pass through me, you may find what you seek. You may free your mother. You may burn the bargain to ash and walk out under returned stars. And you may fail, and be taken, and be held in a cell of thorns for a hundred years while everyone you love grows old above you, believing you abandoned them. Knowing all of this—knowing it truly, not as a story with a guaranteed ending—"

The door's voice dropped, and for the first time it sounded old. Ancient. Tired in the bones.

"—why come anyway?"

Silas stood in the cold and thought about their mother's letter. *I went quietly so the valley would be spared. Don't be angry with them. Be angry with the bargain, not the people afraid of it.* Twenty years dead-and-not-dead, and her first thought in the letter had been to protect the people who let her be taken.

Because love wasn't a wager, Silas realized. Love didn't check the odds first.

"Because she's my mother," Silas said. "That's the whole answer. Not because I'll win. Because she's my mother, and she's down there, and I'm up here, and the distance between those two facts is the only thing in the world I can't live with. If I fail, I fail *there*, close to her, instead of here, comfortable and hollow. I'd rather be taken than be the person who calculated her way out of loving somebody."

The stillness held for one heartbeat. Two. Three.

Then every carving on the door blazed silver at once, and the great panels swung inward—not onto stone, but onto a corridor of living thorn and pale lamplight, descending into the mountain's heart, breathing out air that smelled of plums and cold iron and something older than both.

"Enter, Silas Ashgrove," said the Mouth of the Mountain. "Alone. That is the shape of the passage. What waits below does not permit witnesses to bargains—it learned that lesson long ago, and it learned it badly." A pause, and then, in a tone that might, in a lesser door, have been called apologetic: "Your friends will find another way. They are resourceful, and one of them owes debts in inconvenient places. Tell no one I told you. I have a reputation."

Behind Silas, Wren made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to boil.

Silas turned. Bramble was grinning—that wild, crooked grin that meant he already had four plans and trusted none of them. Wren's jaw was set hard enough to crack walnuts, and her eyes were bright and furious and, underneath, afraid.

"You heard it," Silas said. "Another way."

"We'll find one," Wren said. "We found you, didn't we?"

"That's not reassuring, you arrested me."

"And I'd do it again," she said, and then, quieter, stepping forward until she stood at the invisible line the door had drawn, close enough that Silas could see the frost in her lashes: "Come back out, Silas. Both of you. That's not an order. I don't have the rank. It's just—come back out."

Silas wanted to say something worthy of that. Something that would hold them both through whatever came. What came out instead, wobbling, was: "Save me some cheese."

Bramble burst out laughing, and pressed the whole wheel into Silas's arms. "For luck," he said. "Also I've grown tired of carrying it."

So Silas stepped through the door of the Mountain's Mouth with a wheel of cheese under one arm and terror under their ribs, and the panels closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting, and the last thing they saw of the world above was Wren's face—set, stubborn, and refusing, absolutely refusing, to look worried.

The corridor breathed around them. The lamplight bent toward the depths.

Somewhere far below, Silas felt—rather than heard—a second heartbeat that was not their own, patient and furious and unmistakably alive.

*Mother,* Silas thought, and began to descend.
