## 14. Audience with the Thorn King

They came for her at the hour that had no name.

Elowen had learned to measure time by the thorn-light — a greenish glow that seeped through the walls of her cell like water through old mortar, brightening and dimming on no schedule she could trust. Twenty years of it. Twenty years of counting her own heartbeats because nothing else in this place would hold still long enough to count.

The door did not open so much as *unclench*, thorns withdrawing into the stone one finger-length at a time, and two figures stood in the gap. They wore the shapes of courtiers, mostly. One had too many joints in his fingers; the other's smile was set slightly too high on her face, as if it had been installed by someone working from memory.

"Lady Ashgrove," said the jointed one. "The King requires your presence."

"Does he," Elowen said. She rose from the cot — her one piece of furniture, and she'd spent years training the thorn-woven frame not to bite her — and smoothed her dress with the particular care she reserved for occasions when she wanted very much to seem unbothered. "Twenty years he could have required my presence. I'd begun to think he was shy."

Neither courtier laughed. Courtiers here never laughed; Elowen suspected the court had confiscated their laughter along with everything else they'd arrived carrying. She'd once asked a servant what happened to a fae's sense of humor after a century underground, and the servant had looked at her with genuine, heartbreaking confusion, as though she'd asked where they kept the sun.

She followed them anyway. What else was there to do? She had spent twenty years being patient, and patience, she had learned, was just anger wearing its good coat.

* * *

The throne room had been beautiful once. That was the cruelest thing about the Thorn Court — you could still see the beauty underneath, the way you could see the shape of a face through frost on a window. Great pillars of black wood arched overhead, carved — or grown — into vaults that should have caught starlight from windows that were now bricked over with woven bramble. The floor was living root, polished smooth by centuries of feet, and down its length burned rows of thorn-lanterns whose light was the color of a bruise deciding whether to heal.

And at the far end, on a throne of white thorn that had grown up through the floor like a bone through skin, sat the Thorn King.

Elowen had seen him only twice before: once on the day of her capture, and once, years ago, through the bars of her cell window during some procession or other. He was not what stories promised. Stories gave him antlers and a crown of fire and eyes like winter. In truth he looked — tired. Tall, yes, and terrible in the way that old trees are terrible, all shadow and grain and slow weight, but there was something about the way he held himself. Something she recognized.

He sat the way her father had sat in the last year of his life, when the illness had settled into his bones and every chair became an enemy he was losing to politely.

"Well," Elowen said, stopping at what she judged to be a respectful distance and then taking one step closer, because respect was a currency she intended to spend carefully if at all. "You wanted me."

"I wanted your child." The King's voice was low and dry, like wind moving through a hollow reed. "Instead I am told the wards failed upward. That the sky went dark over your valley. That someone has come beneath the mountain who does not appear in any ledger I keep." His eyes — grey, she noted, grey as river stones, not winter at all — fixed on her. "You know something about ledgers, Lady Ashgrove. You signed one."

"My grandmother signed it. I merely inherited the debt." She folded her hands. "Though I'll admit I've had twenty years to read it more carefully than anyone in this room apparently has."

Something flickered across the throne. Not quite surprise. Interest, perhaps — the first honest expression she'd seen on a face in this court since the day she arrived.

"Carefully," the King repeated.

"Clause by clause. I've had nothing else to do." She let that land. Let him see the twenty years in it. "Your herald found a loophole last month — any Ashgrove blood discharges the debt. Very generous. Almost suspiciously generous. And yet nowhere in the founding bargain does the word *discharge* appear. Not once. I checked. Repeatedly. I had, as I say, the time."

The jointed courtier made a small sound, like a hinge remembering it could rust.

"You are impertinent," the King said.

"I'm bored," Elowen corrected. "Impertinence is just boredom with nowhere to go. You sealed me in a cell with a view of a wall for two decades. You're lucky I haven't taken up poetry."

For a moment — a single, unguarded moment — the corner of the King's mouth moved. It might have been the ghost of a smile. It might have been pain. With him, she suspected, the two had grown indistinguishable.

* * *

It was during the third hour of negotiation — because the Thorn King insisted on negotiating properly, with terms read aloud and witnessed, as though ceremony could make captivity respectable — that Elowen understood.

She had been steering the conversation deliberately toward the edges of the contract. Asking about renewal dates. About the original signatories. About what, precisely, the valley's invisibility purchased, and from whom, and why a court buried under a mountain needed a human soul in the first place. Each question he answered with the weary precision of a man reciting terms he had not chosen.

And each answer fit together wrong. Or rather — right, in a way that revealed the shape of the trap.

"The bargain binds the holder of the debt," he said, when she asked directly. "That is the law of contracts. The holder serves the terms as surely as the debtor."

"And who holds the debt?"

A silence. The lanterns guttered, all of them at once, as if the room itself had flinched.

"I do," the King said.

"Yes," Elowen agreed pleasantly. "But who held it *first*?"

His hands — long, bark-grey, elegant — closed on the arms of the throne. And Elowen watched, with the strange clarity that comes after twenty years of watching a wall, as the truth assembled itself in front of her out of pieces she'd been carrying all along without knowing they were pieces.

The founding bargain had been signed by her grandmother. But bargains require two parties. Someone on the other side had signed first. Someone had offered the valley its invisibility, had named the price, had written the clauses — including the clause about renewal, including the clause about discharge, including every clause designed to keep the debt alive forever.

Someone had built a cage and then locked themselves inside it.

"You never wanted my mother's soul," Elowen said quietly. "Or mine. You wanted *out*. And the contract won't let you take it — only receive it. Only wait for someone to walk in and hand it to you." She took another step forward. The guards — such as they were — did not move. "You're not the jailer, are you. You're the first prisoner. The crown is just the lock they dressed up."

The throne room was very still. Somewhere above, water dripped through the mountain's bones, counting seconds in a language older than either of them.

"You presume a great deal," the King said at last, and his voice had gone quieter, which she noticed, and which mattered.

"I presume nothing. I read." She spread her hands. "Here is what I think, Your Majesty. I think whoever wrote that contract — the true author, the one whose name isn't on it — bound you to collect souls because a collector can never leave. A debt-holder must stay and hold. Forever. And I think you have been sitting on that throne for longer than your court can remember, waiting for someone clever enough or desperate enough to find the crack in it, because you cannot look for it yourself. Looking would be *seeking release*, and seeking release is breach, and breach forfeits—"

"Forfeits everything," he finished. Flatly. "Yes."

"So you waited for a human. Humans are so wonderfully reckless about reading things." She smiled, and it wasn't entirely kind. "Did my grandmother know what she was doing to you, when she signed? Or did she think she was buying a valley and paying a soul, fair and square?"

"She thought," the King said, "she was speaking with a god." Something moved behind his eyes — old, and cold, and terribly sad. "I let her think it. It was easier than explaining that I had been sent to do this. That refusal was not among my options." He looked down at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "Do you know what it is, Lady Ashgrove, to be the monster in a story you did not write?"

And there it was. There was the thing she recognized — the thing she'd seen in her dying father, in the mirror during her worst years in the cell, in every creature in this court shuffling through its duties with its laughter confiscated.

Elowen thought of Silas. Of the letter she had written and never sent, the courier dead in the snow, the words that had traveled twenty years to arrive in the wrong hands — or exactly the right ones. She had gone quietly into this mountain so that a valley full of strangers could keep pretending it was safe. She had called it sacrifice. Standing here, looking up at a king who had done the same thing for ten times as long and been hated for it, she wondered if there was another word. *Hostage.* *Fellow traveler.* *Fool.*

"There is a clause," she said. "In the founding bargain. Nobody reads it aloud — I think whoever wrote it hoped nobody ever would. It concerns the witnessing."

The King's head came up slowly.

"A contract witnessed under a starless sky," Elowen said, "is void when true stars return." She let the words hang in the bruise-colored light. "Your court hid the stars over my valley. But the wards didn't fail downward, Majesty. They failed *upward*. Someone broke something honestly — or is trying to — and the sky went dark because the lie couldn't hold both directions at once."

For the first time in twenty years, Elowen saw the Thorn King afraid.

Not of her. Never of her. Afraid of *hope*, which is a far more dangerous thing to carry into a prison, because hope can be confiscated too, and losing it twice kills something that death does not touch.

"If that clause wakes," he said, "the contract burns. Everything in it. Every holding. Every—" He stopped.

"Every prisoner goes free," Elowen finished softly. "Including the one on the throne."

"That is treason against the court."

"The court," Elowen said, "is a cage that learned to decorate itself. Forgive me if I don't weep for it." She stepped closer still, close enough now that she could see the fine cracks running through the white thorn of the throne, hairline fractures, the kind that spread. "My child is somewhere in this mountain. They came for me, which was foolish and brave and exactly what I raised them to be, and I will burn this whole court down myself before I let it take them. But it occurs to me, Majesty, that I may not need to burn anything. It occurs to me that you and I want precisely the same thing, and that between us we might finally be clever enough to get it."

The King regarded her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he did something no chronicle of the Thorn Court had ever recorded.

He leaned forward, wincing — actually wincing, as though the throne bit him when he moved, and perhaps it did — and spoke in a voice pitched for her alone.

"The contract-vault lies below the east root-hall. Warded, but the wards answer to the court's own seals, and the seals answer to me — when I am permitted to act, which is rarely, and never openly." His grey eyes were fierce now, alive, a river breaking ice. "There are others in your party. The peddler and the ward-keeper. If they reached the vault—"

"They'd need someone to open it."

"They would need someone to *distract a court* while they opened it." And here, at last, unmistakably, the Thorn King smiled — a thin, rusted, magnificent thing, like a blade kept oiled in the dark for three hundred years. "Fortunately, Lady Ashgrove, I am about to convene a formal audience regarding the discharge of the Ashgrove debt. Full attendance. Every courtier. Every guard. Every eye in this mountain fixed upon the throne — where, I am assured by ancient law, all attention must remain."

Elowen felt her breath catch. Twenty years of patience, and it came down to this: a prisoner-king preparing to make noise, and a mother praying her child was clever enough to use the dark.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When the stars come back — and they will — you walk out of here *with* us. No lingering. No noble last stand among your thorns. I've done the quiet-sacrifice thing, Majesty, and I promise you the reviews are terrible."

The King studied her. And then he laughed — short, startled, rusty, as if the sound had escaped custody — and for one impossible moment the lanterns flared gold instead of green, and the throne room remembered what it had been built to be.

"It is a bargain," he said.

"No," Elowen said, turning toward the doors, toward the sound — faint, distant, unmistakable — of alarms beginning to wake in the roots below. "Bargains are how we all got here. Call it a plan."

Far beneath the throne, in the dark of the east root-hall, something began to break.
