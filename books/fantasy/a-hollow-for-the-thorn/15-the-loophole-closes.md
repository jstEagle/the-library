## 15. The Loophole Closes

Wren had broken into exactly three vaults in her career as a royal ward-keeper, and she had been caught at two of them. She considered this a respectable average, given that the third time she'd been drunk on plum wine and it was technically her own vault.

"Fourth time's the charm," Bramble said cheerfully, crouched beside her in the shadow of a buttress that looked like it had been grown rather than built. Everything in the Thorn Court looked like that—the architecture of something that had never been taught patience. The walls were black wood shot through with living thorn, and here and there a bud swelled against the stone like a knuckle under skin.

"It's not charming," Wren muttered. "It's a felony."

"In your kingdom, perhaps." Bramble adjusted the collar of his peddler's coat, which he'd insisted on wearing because, quote, *a man should be dressed for the job he wants*. "Here it's called initiative."

The contract-vault sat at the heart of the court, past the Hall of Held Things where Silas was even now walking corridors lined with caged names and bottled years. Wren tried not to think about that. Thinking about it made her want to turn around and put her body between Silas and every door in this place, which was not tactically useful and also, she suspected, not entirely professional.

She thought about it anyway.

*Focus.* The plan was simple, or as simple as anything got when half of it depended on a half-fae who treated danger like weather. While Silas searched for Elowen above, Wren and Bramble would descend to the vault, find the founding bargain—the one Grandmother Ashgrove had signed—and amend it. Not break it. Amend. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point: a broken contract voided the valley's protection instantly and left three hundred people standing naked under a hungry sky. An amended one could be rewritten so that the debt named no soul at all. A loophole closed from the inside.

"You're certain about the warding?" she asked for the fourth time.

Bramble patted his coat pocket, where a small tin rattled. "Crushed moonwort, grave-moss, and a pinch of ash from my grandmother's hearth. It won't disable the wards. It'll make them confused. They'll see us and decide we're probably supposed to be here."

"That's insane."

"That's fae bureaucracy," Bramble said fondly. "I grew up in it. The trick is never to lie to it outright—just to be so unremarkable that lying becomes unnecessary." He grinned, sharp and sweet at once. "Ready?"

Wren checked her knife, her chalk, and the small iron stylus she'd carried since her disgrace—a ward-keeper's tool, useless here except as a reminder of what she used to be. "No," she said honestly.

"Wonderful. Neither am I. Off we go."

* * *

The descent took them down a spiral stair that had opinions. The steps were warm underfoot, faintly pulsing, and twice Wren felt the stair *consider* them—a slow attention moving up through her boots like water rising in a well. Both times Bramble hummed a little tuneless song and the attention moved on.

"What is that?" Wren whispered.

"Lullaby my mother sang. The mountain likes it. Or it finds it irritating enough to ignore us, which amounts to the same thing." He glanced back, and for once the flippancy fell off his face. "Wren. If this goes wrong—if the herald's little gift turns out to be what I think it might be—I need you to know I don't regret coming. Even if it costs me. A debt paid clean is worth more than a long life owed."

"Don't talk like that."

"I'm a peddler. Talking is the entire trade." But he squeezed her hand once, brief and hard, before pushing open the door at the stair's foot.

The vault was smaller than Wren expected, and colder. A round chamber, ceiling lost in dark, its walls lined floor to dome with niches—and in each niche, a document. Not paper. Contracts here were written on things: strips of birch bark gone silver with age, a length of ribbon that still moved faintly, as if breathing, a shard of ice that had not melted in what looked like centuries. Some were bound in chains of woven hair. One, near the door, was simply a child's shoe with writing inked on the sole, and Wren made herself look away from it.

"There." Bramble pointed to a niche near the chamber's heart, set slightly apart, honored—or isolated—by a frame of black iron roses. Inside lay a scroll of vellum so old it had gone the color of old teeth, sealed with wax the deep red of arterial blood.

The founding bargain.

Wren approached it the way you approach a sleeping animal: slow, low, hands visible. The wards around the niche were invisible until she was close enough to feel them—a pressure against her skin like the moment before a storm breaks. She scattered Bramble's mixture in a ring, spoke the words he'd drilled into her on the road (*we are expected, we are expected, we are expected*), and stepped through.

Nothing happened. The pressure parted around her like a curtain drawn by a bored servant.

"Huh," she said. "That worked."

"Don't sound surprised, it hurts the mixture's feelings." Bramble joined her, and together they lifted the scroll from its cradle. It was lighter than it should have been. It hummed, very faintly, against Wren's palms—the way a struck bell hums after the sound has supposedly ended.

She unrolled it across a reading table that rose obligingly out of the floor, which she chose not to think about either. The script was dense, formal, and written in an alphabet she recognized from her training: court-hand, the legal script of the fae, every letter a small locked door.

"Read it to me," Bramble said. "My court-hand is passable but slow, and my eyes are decorative."

Wren read. The opening was as Silas had described: the valley of the Hollow, its borders, its invisibility, purchased and renewed each century. Then the consideration clause, and her stomach dropped even though she'd known it was coming.

*One soul, held in trust, as surety for the term.*

And beneath it, in a different hand—shakier, human, pressed hard enough into the vellum to tear in places—a signature.

*Maren Ashgrove.*

Silas's grandmother. And below the signature, in the same desperate hand, a line Wren had not expected:

*The soul named herein is freely given, being mine own, until such time as it passes beyond my keeping; thereafter the debt shall rest upon my blood, that none other need bear it.*

"She tried," Wren said quietly. "Maren Ashgrove tried to take the debt herself. To keep it in her own body so it would die with her."

"And the court let her sign it," Bramble said slowly, "and then took Elowen anyway."

"Because—" Wren scanned the clauses, faster now, anger making her quick. "Because of this. Clause nine. *Should the named soul be unavailable at renewal, the debt shall fall to the nearest of the signer's blood.*" Her jaw tightened. "They waited. Maren died, and they waited twenty years, and then they took her daughter. That's not a bargain. That's a snare with paperwork."

Bramble had gone very still. In Wren's experience, Bramble going still was roughly equivalent to anyone else screaming.

"Wren," he said. "Read clause eleven."

She found it. Read it. Read it again, because the first reading refused to mean what it said.

*Should any holder of the debt accept formal discharge of their obligation within these halls, the discharge shall be entered as a new binding, and the accepting party shall stand as surety for the full remaining term.*

"Ashgrove blood," Bramble whispered. "Any Ashgrove blood. The herald told Silas the debt could be discharged by any of their line. And clause eleven says that accepting the discharge doesn't end the debt—it *transfers* it. Fresh binding. Full term. A hundred more years, starting tonight."

The cold in the vault suddenly seemed very deliberate.

"The herald wasn't offering mercy," Wren said. Each word came out flat and precise, the way her voice went when her hands wanted to shake. "He was selling a trap and calling it a door. If Silas accepts—even to save their mother, especially to save their mother—they walk out of here free and Elowen walks back into the cell, and the contract gets a hundred-year renewal with Silas's name on it. Signed by acceptance. No coercion. Perfectly legal." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "It's elegant. I hate how elegant it is."

"We have to warn them." Bramble was already reaching for the scroll. "Now. Before the herald finds them."

"Yes. But first—" Wren spread her fingers over the vellum, over clause nine, over the words *nearest of the signer's blood*, and took out her stylus. "First we close every door we can. If we strike the transfer clauses, the trap has nothing to spring *into*."

Amending a court contract was not like editing a letter. Every alteration had to be witnessed, justified, and paid for; the contract itself would resist, the way a living thing resists. Wren had trained for this. She had trained for this in marble halls with instructors and second chances, and then she had thrown that life away, and now here she was in a vault under a mountain, scratching amendments into a soul-bargain with an iron stylus and a heart full of fury.

She began with clause nine. *Nearest of the signer's blood* — she scored through *nearest* and wrote, in careful court-hand, *no one; the obligation ends with the signer's death*. The vellum shuddered under her hand. Somewhere far above, a bell she couldn't hear began to ring.

"Keep talking to me," she said. Her voice needed an anchor.

"Right. Yes." Bramble braced the scroll flat. "Did I ever tell you how I met Elowen? Properly, I mean. Everyone assumes it was some grand rescue, because I'm so obviously the damsel type."

"You are absolutely the damsel type."

"Thank you. It was a market day, actually. Terrible rain. She found me half-drowned under my own collapsed stall, and instead of taking the free pick of my goods—which any sensible person would have—she held the tarp over my head for two hours and complained about the rain in a way that made it funny." His voice wobbled, just slightly. "She stayed until I stopped shivering. That's the debt. Two hours of shelter. She probably doesn't even remember it. That's the kind of person she is, and that's the kind of person her child is, and I will burn this whole mountain down before I watch that family get sold twice."

The amendment took. Clause nine rewrote itself in a ripple of pale light, the old words sinking into the vellum like stones into water. Wren exhaled and moved to clause eleven.

Her stylus touched the parchment—

And the door opened.

Not loudly. That was the worst part. After all the stairs and the humming walls and the wards that had decided to look away, the vault door simply swung inward without a sound, and the herald stood in it.

He was exactly as Wren remembered from the road: tall, courteous, dressed in winter colors, his face pleasant the way a frozen lake is pleasant. Behind him, filling the corridor, stood six of the court's guard—thorn-and-iron shapes that did not move and did not blink and did not need to.

"Keeper Wren," the herald said warmly. "And the peddler. How industrious. Most visitors merely steal. You've come to *legislate*."

Bramble stepped in front of Wren, arms spread, which was touching and also completely useless. "Now, friend—"

"I'm not your friend, but I admire the instinct." The herald drifted forward, unhurried, and his gaze settled on the scroll, on the pale scar of the amended clause, and something in his pleasant face flickered—not anger. Recalculation. "Ah. You've been reading ahead. That was not in the spirit of your invitation."

"We weren't invited," Wren said. "We broke in. Which you knew, because you let us."

"I let the *other* one in," the herald said mildly. "The Ashgrove child. Their admission was arranged; the door asked its questions and was answered honestly, and honest answers open doors—that's law, not kindness. You two were meant to be occupied elsewhere. But no matter." He folded his hands. "You should know that your amendment is noted, witnessed, and—pending review—binding. Well done. Genuinely. It's rare to see such work outside the college." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "Clause eleven, however, remains intact. You'll find I protected it personally. It is, after all, the reason any of you were permitted this far."

Wren's blood went cold in a new way. "You *wanted* us in the vault."

"I wanted the vault *busy*," the herald corrected. "There is a difference. While you were down here improving the paperwork, my colleagues upstairs were concluding a separate negotiation." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "The Ashgrove child has found their mother. Reunions are so moving. And in the warmth of that reunion, a proposal was made—an offer of discharge, generous, immediate, requiring only a yes."

"No," Wren said. She was moving before she finished the word, toward the door, toward the stairs, and the guards shifted to block her with the smooth inevitability of a tide.

"They'll say yes," the herald said, not unkindly, which was the cruelest register he owned. "Of course they will. They came all this way. They'll say yes to save her, and clause eleven will do the rest, and the valley will keep its invisibility, and everyone will be satisfied—except the one in the cell, and the one who takes her place, and you two, who will be charged with tampering, which carries a sentence I'm told involves becoming part of the architecture." He tilted his head. "Unless."

"Unless," Bramble repeated dangerously.

"Unless someone in this room can produce a reason why the discharge cannot legally be accepted. A flaw. A prior claim. A clause." His eyes glittered. "I do so enjoy watching clever people search for exits that I have already measured."

Wren stared at him, and understanding arrived like ice water: he wasn't threatening them. He was *stalling* them. Every polite word was another second ticking off the clock upstairs, another breath closer to Silas saying yes.

"You're buying time," she said aloud. "You're not here for us at all. We're the diversion *now*."

The herald's smile widened by a single degree. "Clever keeper. Do run along and check."

They ran. Bramble went through the window—there was a window, a slit of night high in the vault wall, and he went through it like a cat through a hedge, shouting something about meeting at the gate. Wren went through the door, straight at the guards, and discovered that her knife, her chalk, and twenty years of training bought her exactly four seconds of glorious, hopeless violence before thorn-arms closed around her like a book shutting.

She fought anyway. She fought while they bound her wrists in braided hair that tightened when she pulled. She fought while the herald watched with his head politely tilted, murmuring, "Do conserve your strength, Keeper. You may yet need it."

And through the stone under her knees, up through the bones of the mountain, she felt it begin: a slow, deep vibration, like a great lock turning. Like a contract accepting a signature.

Somewhere above them, Silas Ashgrove was being offered everything they'd come for.

And Wren, kneeling on the cold floor of the vault she'd broken into to save them, realized with perfect clarity that the loophole hadn't closed at all.

It had closed *around* them.
