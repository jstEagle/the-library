## 16. Thorn and Iron

Bramble had been in a great many bad rooms in his long and moderately dishonest life. He had been thrown out of a duke's banquet for eating the centerpiece. He had once spent a night in a jail cell that turned out to be a very large owl's stomach. He had, on one memorable occasion, been married by accident.

None of it prepared him for the Hall of Judgment of the Thorn Court.

It was not a courtroom, precisely. It was a garden grown indoors — or a forest grown into a courtroom, the distinction having eroded sometime in the last thousand years. Pillars of blackthorn rose from a floor of packed white frost, and their branches wove overhead into a ceiling dense enough to be a sky, except that no sky Bramble knew had thorns for stars. The benches where a jury might sit were roots, and the roots were occupied: figures in winter-grey, faces like bark that had thought about becoming faces and then lost interest. They did not speak. They did not need to. Their attention had weight, and the weight was currently distributed across three prisoners kneeling in the frost.

Well. Two kneeling and one standing, because Wren had never in her life done what she was told, even when told by furniture.

"Up," she'd hissed at Bramble as the thorn-soldiers marched them in. "We're not kneeling to this."

"We are absolutely kneeling to this," Bramble whispered back. "Wren. Sweetheart. Colleague. I have read about this court. The last person who refused to kneel is *part of the floor now.* Look at the floor. Look at how much floor there is."

She looked at the floor. She stayed standing. Bramble, who owed a life-debt to one Ashgrove and had recently and inconveniently acquired feelings of the protective sort toward another, knelt with the grace of long practice and pretended he'd always intended to.

Silas knelt between them, wrists bound in braided hair that had gone pale as birchwood and tightened until the skin beneath showed red. They were not fighting. That frightened Bramble more than anything else in the room. Silas Ashgrove, who had walked into a door that asked questions and answered honestly; who had traded a memory of their own birthday without blinking; who had crossed a blighted country on stubbornness and grief — Silas was looking at the throne with an expression Bramble recognized because he wore it himself whenever he counted up his debts.

The arithmetic face. The face of someone totaling what they could afford to lose.

And on the throne —

Ah, but you couldn't call it a throne, really. It was a tree. A vast, black, leafless thing grown through the heart of the mountain, its trunk rising through the dais and vanishing into the thorned dark above, and seated within it — grown *into* it, Bramble realized, the way a nail grows into a tree that has been left too long — was the Thorn King.

He was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful, which is to say entirely and dangerously. His crown was the tree's own branches, and they moved when he moved, and when he did not move — which was most of the time — they held still with the terrible patience of something that has nowhere else to be.

"Elowen Ashgrove's child," the King said. His voice was quiet, and the whole hall leaned in to hear it, roots and all. "And a keeper of broken wards. And—" his gaze found Bramble, and something ancient and amused flickered behind the frost of it, "—ah. The peddler. I know your mother's people."

"Everyone says that," Bramble said, "and no one ever sends word ahead."

The amusement did not spread. It simply went out, like a candle pinched between two fingers.

"You entered my court under false colors," the King said. "You broke into my vault of contracts. You conspired to amend a bargain older than any of your kingdoms." He lifted one hand — slowly, the way glaciers lift themselves — and the braided bindings on Silas's wrists creaked. "In the mortal world these would be crimes. Here they are merely *rude.* And rudeness, little thieves, has a price schedule."

*Here it comes,* Bramble thought. He had sat through enough negotiations to know the shape of this one. The King would itemize. The King would offer choices, each worse than the last, each dressed in reasonable language. And somewhere in the middle of it, the trap that had already sprung around them — the herald's sweet loophole, the discharge that made the acceptor collateral — would be presented as mercy.

He glanced sideways at Silas. Still counting. Still silent.

He glanced at Wren. She was glaring at the throne with the expression of a woman composing a formal complaint to whatever authority existed above frozen kings, and Bramble loved her a little for it, in the way one loves a comrade-in-arms, which is to say briefly, fiercely, and with full knowledge that it will complicate everything.

"The terms," the King said, "are as follows."

*There it is.*

"The child of Elowen Ashgrove has accepted discharge of the debt. The contract recognizes this. The contract does not care that the acceptance was baited; contracts do not concern themselves with bait. An Ashgrove soul stands ready to enter trust." The branches of the crown stirred. "However. I am told the court may hold only what is freely given, and so we arrive at the question of *freely.*"

His eyes came down to Silas like a lid closing over a jar.

"Choose," said the Thorn King. "Enter the trust willingly, and your companions walk out under my peace, unharmed, unmolested, and free. Refuse, and I will take the trust by force — and force, as every soul in this hall knows, spoils what it touches. Your mother has kept well for twenty years because she was given gently. You will not keep well at all. And neither will they."

The silence afterward had texture. It had grain and weight and temperature. Somewhere high in the thorned ceiling, something small and blind shifted in its nest.

Bramble's heart was doing something complicated and undignified.

Because here was the thing about Bramble — the thing he generally kept folded away under the peddler's smile and the patter and the suspiciously good prices: he had come down this mountain owing a life. Not metaphorically. Not in the vague way merchants owe favors. Owing a *life*, the old kind, the kind that binds, the kind his mother's people took seriously enough to carve into bone. Twenty-three years ago, in a village that no longer appeared on maps, a woman named Elowen Ashgrove had pulled him out of a river he had no business being in, breathing warmth back into a body the water had already begun to claim, and she had asked nothing for it, and her asking-nothing had hung around his neck ever since like a stone with a name on it.

He had told himself the debt was to Elowen. He had told himself rescuing her discharged it neatly, ledger balanced, everyone goes home.

But debts, he had learned at the Tollhouse of Small Mercies along with everyone else, bind to the *shape* of what was given, not the letter of the name. And what Elowen had given him in that river was not a favor to a stranger. It was a life handed back to someone she had no reason to love. The debt's shape was that. Its shape was *her child.*

So when the Thorn King said *choose*, and Bramble watched Silas Ashgrove begin — visibly, terribly — to choose, some old carved-bone law in Bramble's chest stood up and cleared its throat.

"No," Bramble said.

Every root in the hall turned its attention toward him. It felt like being regarded by a library.

The King's brows — if those were brows, and not frost formations — drew together a fraction. "Peddler. You were not addressed."

"Funny thing about life-debts," Bramble said, and got to his feet, because apparently today he too had decided to stop doing what he was told, and wasn't that just marvelous, the whole company of them catching courage like a cold. "Everyone thinks they belong to the debtor. They don't. They belong to the *debt*. And the debt doesn't care what the King offers or what the herald baited or what poor Silas here is about to volunteer for, because the debt was incurred before any of you wrote your clever contract, and it outranks you. It outranks everybody. My mother's people wrote the first versions of things like yours, Your Majesty, back when your tree was an acorn with ambitions. So believe me when I tell you the paperwork on this is *immaculate.*"

"Bramble," Silas whispered. "Don't."

"Oh, I'm going to. I've been planning not to do this for twenty years and it turns out all that planning was wasted." He turned to Silas, and let the peddler's smile fall off his face entirely, because this part deserved a real one. "Elowen Ashgrove gave me back my life when I was nobody, before you were even a rumor. I came down this mountain thinking I'd square it by finding her. But the debt isn't paid by finding her, is it? It's paid by giving what she gave. A life, freely, for her blood." He spread his hands. "So. Here's my counteroffer, Your Majesty. Take mine instead. One life-debt, formally discharged, in front of witnesses—" he gestured grandly at the roots, "—and in exchange, both Ashgroves walk. The mother *and* the child. And the keeper walks too, since she'll only follow us home otherwise and make noise the entire way."

"I will *not*—" Wren began.

"—she'll make noise the entire way," Bramble repeated over her, "so you may as well release her while she's still bound."

The hall was very still. Even the blind thing in the ceiling had stopped shifting.

The Thorn King looked at Bramble for a long moment, and Bramble had been alive long enough and dishonest long enough to recognize what moved behind the King's face, and it chilled him worse than anger would have. It was *hunger*. The hunger of a man offered exactly what he wants by someone who hasn't realized what they're holding.

"A life-debt," the King said softly, "freely tendered. Yes. That is... admissible."

"Of course it's admissible," Bramble said briskly, before his courage could hear itself and reconsider. "I told you. Immaculate paperwork."

"Bramble." Wren's voice had gone low and strange. "Bramble, you absolute *peddler*, you don't get to—"

"It's my debt, Keeper. Mine to spend. That's rather the point of owning one." He winked at her. "Besides, think of the story. 'The Peddler Who Bought a Warden.' They'll sing it in taverns. Terribly off-key, probably, but sung."

"Don't you dare make me cry in front of a tree."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

The King raised one hand, and the frost on the floor between Bramble and the throne began to move — drawing itself up into lines, into script, into the opening clauses of a new instrument, writing itself in real time, and Bramble felt the pull of it in his chest like a hook set behind the sternum. Old magic. Honest magic, in its horrible way. It wanted only his yes.

He opened his mouth to give it.

"*Wait.*"

Silas's voice. Small, cracked, and carrying anyway — the way it had carried across the Road Where Nothing Grows, the way it had carried in the dark of the snowbound cave. Bramble turned. Silas had risen to their feet without seeming to decide to, bound wrists and all, and was looking past the King, past the throne, up into the black thorned canopy with an expression Bramble had seen exactly once before: at the tollhouse, the moment after they'd traded the memory, when Silas had gone quiet in a particular way and then said, *wait, what did I used to do on my birthday?*

The arithmetic face. But the sum had changed.

"Before anyone signs anything," Silas said, "I invoke the clause."

The King went very still. Stillness, on him, was different from stillness on other creatures. It was the stillness of a mountain deciding whether to avalanche.

"There is no clause," the King said.

"There's always a clause," Silas said. "You taught me that yourself, Your Majesty — or rather, your herald did, when he offered me the loophole. Everything in this court is contract. Every kindness, every cruelty, every mercy is a clause somewhere. Which means the founding bargain — the one my grandmother signed, the one that holds my mother — has clauses too. All of them. Even the ones nobody read aloud." Silas's breath was shaking. Their voice steadied itself by main force. "I've had twenty years of guild archives and three weeks of your corridors, and I read *all of it.* Every page. Including the witness conditions."

Something changed in the hall then. Bramble felt it before he understood it — a shift in the pressure, the way air moves before weather. The roots on the benches had stopped attending like a jury and started attending like *witnesses*, which, Bramble thought with a lurch, was possibly the same thing here, and possibly the whole point.

"The contract was witnessed under a starless sky," Silas said. "That's in the preamble. Witnessed and sealed beneath the Hollow's hidden heavens, where no star could see the signing. And the same preamble — clause one, sub-section four, in the oldest hand in the archive, the hand nobody bothered to translate because it was *boring* — says that should true stars ever witness the parties again, the instrument is void. Void ab initio. Void as if it had never been." Silas turned, slowly, to face the throne, and their bound hands trembled and their voice did not. "Your Majesty. When did the stars go out?"

The silence stretched.

"They went out," Silas said, "the night the valley's wards fell. Everyone assumed your court hid them — that you snuffed the sky to work in the dark. But the wards didn't fail downward, into the valley. Wren proved that. They failed *upward*. They were hiding the sky from *you*. From the moment the founders signed, the wards' true purpose was to keep the stars from witnessing what had been done — to keep the void-condition from ever coming due. And the night the wards broke..." Silas's voice dropped to almost nothing. "The sky didn't vanish. It was *unveiled*. What we saw wasn't darkness. It was the court's shadow, drawn up over the valley like a sheet, because for the first time in a hundred years, the stars could see in. And they are seeing *now.*"

Bramble looked up.

High in the woven thorns of the ceiling, through gaps he had taken for mere damage, light was falling. Thin, cold, silver-white, unmistakable. Starlight, threading down through the black canopy of the Thorn Court itself, pooling on the frost in small bright coins.

The roots on the benches were turning their faces toward it.

"No," the Thorn King said. It was not a command. It was not even loud. It was the voice of a man watching a tide come in over something he cannot lift. "No. The condition requires the *parties* under true stars. The parties are—"

"Present," said Silas. "All of them. Signatory's heir, held soul, and the crown that benefits. Under open heaven. As witnessed." They lifted their bound wrists, and the braided hair binding them — hair, living things' hair, *contract-stuff* — began, very quietly, to unravel.

The Thorn King rose from his tree, and the tree rose with him, groaning, and for one heartbeat Bramble saw what lived underneath the crown: not a tyrant. A prisoner, watching the walls of his cell finally, blessedly, begin to crack — and terrified, because he had worn the cell so long he no longer knew who he was without it.

And the starlight came down like a verdict.
