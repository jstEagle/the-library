## 18. Breaking the Bargain

The court had come to watch.

That was the thing Silas noticed first, standing on the dais steps with the burned fragment of the founding contract in their hand: the Thorn Court had *turned out*. Not the hollow-eyed prisoners in their cells, not the nameless servants who moved through the halls like smoke, but the court proper—the barons of briar and the ladies of hoarfrost, the herald with his too-white smile, ranks of thorn-knights whose armor was grown, not forged. They lined the walls of the great hall in their hundreds, and every one of them was looking at Silas Ashgrove the way a hungry family looks at a pie.

"Cozy," Wren murmured at Silas's shoulder. Her voice was steady, but her hand had found her knife hilt, and her knife was iron, and the nearest baron had edged a full step away from her because of it. "Remind me to throw better parties."

"You weren't invited to this one either," Silas murmured back.

"Details."

Bramble stood on Silas's other side, and he was not joking at all, which was somehow worse. He had paid his life-debt an hour ago in a corridor of screaming iron—had spoken the words that freed him of Elowen Ashgrove's saving, that made him nothing to anyone, owing nothing, bound to nothing—and then, when Wren had been given her liberty and told to walk out of the mountain and never look back, he had planted his feet on the black ice of the floor and said, "No."

Just that. *No.* Bramble, who had spent the whole journey insisting that debts were debts and bargains were bargains and a half-fae who broke his word was no better than the thorns, had looked the Thorn King's chamberlain in the eye and refused his own freedom.

"Sentimental fool," the chamberlain had said.

"Absolutely," Bramble had agreed. "It runs in the family. Well. It would, if I had one."

And so all three of them stood here, at the foot of the throne, in the hall where the founding contract had been signed a hundred years and a hundred years ago, and the Thorn King waited on his throne of black briar with the patience of something that had been waiting for a very long time.

"Little heir," the King said. His voice filled the hall the way cold fills a valley at dusk. "The court is gathered. The terms are plain. Renounce the Hollow's protection, and the sky will judge."

Silas looked up at him. It was hard, looking at him. In the throne room's light he was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, all clean lines and silver and the suggestion of terrible depth beneath. But Silas had seen the vault. Had seen the original contract, the one the herald's trap had been built to spring, and had read—with Wren's ward-keeper's eye and Bramble's fae blood both bent over the parchment—the clause that no one had read aloud in two hundred years.

*A contract witnessed under starless sky is void when true stars return.*

The vanished stars had never been the court's doing. That was the joke of it, the awful, wonderful joke. The valley's wards, failing upward, had hidden the sky from everyone—had hidden it from the *court itself*. The Thorn King had never seen true stars since the bargain was struck. He had sat beneath a false and starless dark for two centuries, a prisoner wearing a crown, bound by a contract whose own escape clause hung above him every night, invisible, waiting.

He didn't know. He couldn't have known. And Silas was about to tell the whole court at once.

"Before I renounce," Silas said, and their voice cracked on the first word, so they stopped, and breathed, four counts in, eight counts out, and started again. "Before I renounce, I want everyone to hear what I read in the vault. All of you. Every clause."

Murmur ran through the briar-court like wind through a hedge. On the throne, something flickered in the King's eyes—not fear, Silas thought. Not yet. Something older and more tired. *Hope's cautious cousin.*

"Read, then," the King said.

So Silas read.

They read the founding bargain in full, the whole rotten architecture of it: the valley's invisibility purchased with a soul held in trust, renewed each century, witnessed—*witnessed*, the word rang out through the hall and came back changed—*witnessed under starless sky, and void when true stars return.*

The murmur became a roar.

Silas had expected that. What they hadn't expected was the *shape* of it. It wasn't anger. It wasn't the hungry glee of a court about to be cheated. It was—Silas searched for the word and found it and didn't believe it—*relief*. A baron of briar pressed a hand to his chest. A lady of hoarfrost made a sound like thawing ice. Somewhere in the ranks of thorn-knights, one of them laughed, a bright cracked sound, and didn't stop.

"You didn't know," Silas said to the King, and it wasn't a question anymore. "You've been bound by a contract you could have walked out of any night, if you'd only looked up."

"I *did* look up." The King's voice was quiet now, and the whole court leaned in to hear it, the way you lean toward a fire. "For the first hundred years, I looked up every night. There was nothing to see. The wards hid the sky from the valley, little heir, but the valley is small, and the wards were old, and old magic is *generous* with its blindness." He rose from the throne, and the black briar of it creaked like a ship. "I stopped looking. That is what a prison does. It does not lock the door. It teaches you to stop checking."

Silas's throat ached. They thought of the valley, of the guild's three remaining members dusting wards no one believed in, of twenty years of quiet dread, of a mother who had gone quietly so the valley would be spared. All of it, every piece of it, built on a contract that contained its own funeral and nobody had read far enough to attend.

"Then look up now," Silas said.

And they raised the burned contract fragment above their head, and spoke the words.

* * *

Later—much later, in a world that had changed shape—Silas would try to remember exactly how they said it, and would fail. Wren would insist it had been a formal renunciation, clause and counter-clause, a ward-keeper's masterpiece. Bramble would swear it was three words and a great deal of shouting. Elowen, when she finally told the story to the rebuilt guild, would say only: "They were frightened, and they did it anyway. That's the whole of it. That's always the whole of it."

What Silas remembered was the cold.

Not the court's cold, which was a held breath, a preserved thing. This was a *living* cold, pouring down through the mountain's roots as the words left their mouth—*I, Silas Ashgrove, of the blood of the Hollow Guild, renounce the valley's protection, root and ward and wall; let the bargain stand or fall as the sky remembers*—and with the cold came a sound like the world inhaling.

Then the ceiling of the great hall, which was living rock veined with thorn, went *transparent*.

Not broken. Not opened. Simply—honest. As if the mountain had been holding its breath for two hundred years and had finally, at Silas's ridiculous request, let the sky in.

And the stars came back.

They did not trickle. They *blazed*. A whole sky's worth, wheeling and burning, constellations Silas had only read about in the guild's oldest star-charts—charts drawn before the valley forgot itself, before the wards went up, before the bargain. The court made a sound Silas would carry for the rest of their life: a single vast indrawn breath, a thousand voices at once, and then a silence so complete that Silas could hear the stars themselves, or thought they could, a high thin ringing like the edge of a bell.

The contract fragment in Silas's hand caught fire.

Not burned—*caught*, the way a wick catches, cheerfully, as if it had been waiting. The old ink glowed gold and then lifted off the parchment in threads of light, and the threads rose, and the parchment crumbled, and the light went up and up and joined the stars, and Silas felt the bargain *end*—felt it the way you feel a splinter leave a finger, a small sharp sting and then an ache and then, unbelievably, nothing. Nothing at all. The valley's invisibility, the debt, the held soul, the whole two-hundred-year architecture of it: gone, like frost in morning sun.

The Thorn King stood beneath the true stars with his face turned up.

He was weeping. Silas hadn't known the fae could. The tears came without ceremony, without shame, cutting bright lines down a face made of winter, and the court watched their king weep at the sky and no one laughed, and no one looked away.

"Two hundred years," the King said. His voice had lost its throne-room weight; it was just a voice now, tired and wondering. "I built a court. I built *laws*. I took souls and held them and told myself it was the contract's doing, the contract's hunger, and all the while—" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, and then it was not terrible at all. "All the while the door was the sky."

He lowered his gaze to Silas.

Silas's whole body braced. This was the moment, they thought. The trap's last spring. The King was freed, and the King was hungry, and here stood an Ashgrove, unguarded, unclaimed, *available*—the court's law said the debt must be discharged by blood, and even a dead contract's habits die hard.

The hall held its breath. Wren's knife was out. Bramble had stepped half in front of Silas, which was absurd, Bramble weighed about as much as a bundle of kindling, and Silas loved him for it with a fierceness that surprised them.

The Thorn King looked at Silas Ashgrove for a long moment.

Then he knelt.

The sound of it—the King of the Thorn Court, kneeling on his own black ice before a guild apprentice in a coat two sizes too big—went through the hall like a bell through still water. The barons of briar knelt. The ladies of hoarfrost knelt. The herald, last of all, with the expression of a man whose entire career had just been cancelled, knelt.

"I have taken a replacement every century," the King said. "It is what I am. It is what I *was*." He looked up, and his eyes were the color of the space between stars. "I choose not to. Let it be recorded in whatever honest book survives this night: the Thorn Court took nothing. The Thorn Court *released* everything. Let the first act of my freedom be a refusal."

"Recorded," said a voice, and it was Bramble, and his voice shook, and he was grinning like a fool, "by whom, exactly? Because I know a guild that's about to have a vacancy in the honest-records department."

The King almost smiled. It looked unfamiliar on him, like a coat that hadn't been worn in a century, but it fit better than the crown had.

* * *

Afterward there was chaos, the good kind, the kind Silas had never known existed.

The vaults opened. All of them. The hall of held things, with its corridors of caged names and stolen years, unsealed at the King's word, and the court's servants—most of them prisoners themselves, bound by bargains as rotten as the founding one—went through the cells unlocking, unlocking, unlocking. Elowen Ashgrove walked out of her cell of twenty years under her own power, unaged and furious and free, and the first thing she did was find her child in the crowd and hold on so hard that Silas's ribs creaked, and the second thing she did was hold on harder.

"You *idiot*," she said into Silas's hair. "You magnificent, impossible idiot."

"You went quietly so the valley would be spared," Silas said. "I read your letter."

"And look how well that turned out." She pulled back, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "Never again. Do you hear me? No one in this family goes quietly ever again. We go loudly. We go *annoyingly*."

"That's the new guild charter," Bramble called from somewhere. "Article one: go annoyingly."

Wren found them by the great doors, where the mountain's roots were beginning to groan—not in collapse, Silas realized, but in *thaw*, two centuries of held winter loosening all at once, ice singing as it became water. She had soot on her face and her knife still in her hand and she was looking at Silas with an expression Silas had last seen in a snowbound cave, in the dark, when admitting things was easier.

"You voided a two-hundred-year contract with one speech," she said.

"It was mostly quoting."

"It was *insubordinate, reckless, procedurally indefensible*—" She stopped. Shook her head. And then, in front of a recovering court, several hundred kneeling fae nobles, and one newly freed Thorn King, she took Silas's face in both hands and kissed them, thoroughly enough that Bramble started a slow clap that several barons of briar, having no idea what else to do, joined.

"Procedurally indefensible," Silas agreed, when they came up for air.

"We'll workshop it," said Wren.

Above the mountain's mouth, the stars were still blazing, and the long winter that had crawled across the world was breaking—Silas could feel it, a loosening in the air, a smell of wet earth and growing things where there had been only frost. Somewhere out there, villages were waking to thaw. Somewhere out there, a valley that had forgotten itself was about to remember, all at once, with no walls left to hide behind.

And somewhere out there, Silas knew, the people of the Hollow were looking up at a sky full of stars for the first time in their lives, and they were afraid.

They'd need someone to tell them what the stars meant. Someone with an honest book, and open gates, and a charter that began, in a half-fae's handwriting, *Article one: go annoyingly.*

"Come on," Elowen said, taking Silas's hand as the mountain's doors swung wide onto a world being remade. "Let's go home and scare them."

They stepped out beneath the returning stars, and the long walk home began.
