## 19. The Long Winter Breaks

Wren had spent eleven years as a royal ward-keeper, and in all that time she had never once run *toward* a collapsing structure. Warders ran away from collapsing structures. It was practically the first thing they taught you, right after "don't lick the sigil-ink" and "if the ward hums in B-flat, leave."

And yet here she was, sprinting back into the Hall of Held Things while the ceiling wept meltwater and the pillars groaned like old men getting out of chairs, because Silas Ashgrove had looked at her with that stubborn, hollowed-out face and said, "There are others. We can't just leave them."

"We absolutely can," Wren had said.

"They're *people*, Wren."

"They're people who signed things," she'd said, which was the wrong thing to say, and she'd known it even as it left her mouth, because Silas had gone very still in the way of someone whose entire family history was a series of signatures other people regretted.

So now: running toward the collapse. The Thorn Court was thawing around them, and thawing, for a court built on frozen bargains, meant dying. Ice that had held for centuries was sloughing off the walls in sheets. The corridors of preserved contracts—those long galleries where stolen years hung in glass like pickled fruit—were shattering one by one, and every shatter released something. A sigh. A scream. Once, horribly, applause.

"Left!" Bramble shouted from somewhere ahead, his voice carrying that particular peddler's cheer he deployed during disasters, which Wren had learned to read as terror wearing a hat. "The gallery on the right is already flooding! Also I would like to register formally that this is the worst delivery route I have ever taken!"

"You're not delivering anything!" Wren shouted back.

"I'm delivering *us*! It counts!"

* * *

They found the first cell by sound—a thin, rhythmic tapping, like someone politely knocking on a door that hadn't opened in eighty years.

The door was thornwood, black and glossy, and it had begun to bud. Actual green shoots were forcing their way through the grain, curling away from the lock as if the wood itself wanted no part of what it had been holding. Wren put her palm flat against it and felt the last threads of the old contract sizzling out through the thorns like wire going cold.

"It's open," she said. "Or—it will be. The binding's dead. The wood just hasn't figured it out yet."

Silas stepped up beside her. They had been quiet since the throne room—quiet since the burning, since the stars had come roaring back overhead through the mountain's cracked crown, since the Thorn King himself had stood amid the ashes of his own contract and said, in a voice like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he carried, *"Go. All of you. Take what the fire freed."*

Wren kept expecting Silas to say something about it. They didn't. They just worked.

"Bramble," Silas said. "Your hands are smallest."

"My hands are *elegant*," Bramble said, but he was already picking the thorn-lock, muttering to it in the coaxing tone he used on stubborn cart wheels and stubborn customers and, once, memorably, on Wren herself. "There we go. There we go, lovely. Nobody's hurt. Everyone's free. Well—not everyone. But you specifically."

The door swung.

Inside sat an old woman on a stool, hands folded, spine straight, dressed in the fashion of a century ago. She blinked at the light. Then at them. Then at the green shoots retreating up the walls.

"Oh," she said. "Am I done, then?"

Wren's throat did something complicated. "You're done," she said. "The contract burned. The whole court's coming down."

The old woman considered this. "Pity. I'd nearly finished learning the flute."

She stood, wobbled, and accepted Silas's arm without comment, as though being rescued from a fairy prison by three strangers was simply the sort of thing that happened after enough decades.

That was the first one. There were forty more.

* * *

By the twelfth cell, Wren had stopped counting the freed and started counting the exits, because the mountain was not merely thawing—it was *deciding*. The frozen foundations of the court had been laid over living roots, she realized; the whole place was a graft, thorn-grown over something older and greener, and now that the bargain's cold was gone, the older thing was waking up under the floorboards. Water ran in channels that hadn't flowed since before Wren's grandmother was born. Mushrooms the size of dinner plates erupted overnight—literally overnight, she watched it happen—in the gallery where the stolen names had been kept. The air smelled like spring trying to remember its job.

It was also, structurally speaking, a disaster.

"The east stair is gone," Bramble reported, sliding into view around a corner with the particular lope of a man who had recently been running. "Gone gone. Not blocked—gone. There's a waterfall where the corridor used to be, and honestly? Improvement. But we can't get the slow ones down it."

"How many can't travel fast?" Wren asked.

Bramble's ears drooped, just slightly—the only honest part of him, Wren had learned. "Most of them. Some of them haven't walked in longer than I've been alive. And the ones from the deep cells—" He shook his head. "Some of them don't remember how to want anything. You have to tell them where to go, and they go, but there's nobody home doing the steering."

Silas, hauling a young man who might have been thirty or might have been three hundred, said nothing. But Wren saw them look at the deep-cell door, and she saw the flinch, and she knew exactly which cell Silas was thinking of. The one they'd found empty. The one their mother had already walked out of, on her own two feet, twenty years too late and somehow exactly on time.

"She went ahead," Silas said quietly, to no one. "She said she'd guide them out from below. She's been awake in there for twenty years. She knows these halls better than the King did."

"Then we trust her," Wren said.

"That's the plan?"

"That's the plan. It's a terrible plan. All the good ones are."

* * *

The bottleneck came at the Tollhouse—that same waystation of exiles they'd passed on the way in, though it had changed. The tollkeeper, a broad woman named Maren with iron rings in her ears and a ledger she guarded like a firstborn, met them at the threshold with her arms full of blankets.

"Well," Maren said, surveying the ragged procession of the freed. "You've brought me forty-one guests, a flooding mountain, and no payment whatsoever."

"Maren—" Bramble began.

"I'm joking," she said. "Partly. Get them inside, all of you. The hearth's lit. First real fire this place has seen in a hundred years—I'd forgotten how much smoke stings." She pressed a blanket into Wren's arms and, lower, added: "The deep ones won't sleep unless someone sits with them. Court-taken. They wake up reaching for bars that aren't there."

So Wren sat. She, who had once been the Crown's finest ward-keeper, who had drawn circles of protection around fortresses and duchies and one extremely nervous archduke, spent the night sitting on a stone floor holding the hand of a farmer who had been traded for a good harvest in 1743, murmuring, "No bars. No bars. You're out," every time he woke.

Somewhere past midnight, Silas sat down beside her, soot-streaked and hollow-eyed, and leaned against her shoulder without asking, and Wren found she didn't mind. She minded so little it frightened her.

"You should sleep," Wren said.

"So should you."

"I'm on duty."

"There's no guild anymore, Wren. No Crown posting. No wards to keep." Silas's voice was gentle, which made it worse. "What exactly are you on duty *to*?"

Wren looked at the farmer, sleeping now, his hand slack and trusting in hers. At the forty-odd souls breathing in the dark of the tollhouse. At Bramble, asleep sitting up against the doorframe with his pack still strapped on, because apparently even in freedom he expected to be sent somewhere.

"Dunno yet," she said. "Ask me tomorrow."

* * *

Morning—or what passed for morning, down here, now that the mountain had windows of daylight cracking through its shoulders—brought the farewell.

Wren knew it was coming the way you know rain: Bramble had repacked his bag three times, which for Bramble was the equivalent of writing a will. He fussed with buckles. He redistributed dried plums between four pouches for reasons known only to him. He polished a brass button that had no business being polished.

Finally Silas said, "Bramble. Say it."

He winced. "Say what? I'm saying nothing. I'm packing. Packing is a normal activity for people who are leaving."

"You're not packed," Wren said. "You're *arranged*. I've traveled with you for weeks. When you're actually leaving, you throw everything in and complain about the weight. This is different. This is ceremonial."

Bramble set the bag down. He looked at it. He looked at the doorway beyond, where the mountain's new green was climbing the walls in earnest—ivy and thorn together now, growing into each other instead of strangling—and where, further down the passage, the freed souls were gathering for the walk to the surface.

"The debt's paid," he said. His voice had lost the patter entirely; underneath it was something older and quieter. "Elowen Ashgrove pulled me out of a bog when I was seventeen and stupid, and I have owed her my life for thirty years, and yesterday she waved at me like I was a neighbor and said 'that's settled, then,' and I—" He stopped. Started again. "A life-debt doesn't just evaporate. It transfers. That's how it works. Everybody knows that."

"Transfers to whom?" Silas asked, though Wren could see they already knew.

"To whoever needs keeping." Bramble gestured vaguely at everything—the halls, the cells, the thawing dark, the forty-one people upstairs learning to sleep without bars. "This place is going to need a keeper. Not a king. A *warden*. Someone who knows the rules well enough to break the bad ones. Someone half of both worlds, who can talk to thorns and to farmers." He grinned, and it wobbled at the edges. "Someone annoying enough that nobody gets comfortable, and kind enough that nobody freezes. I checked the qualifications list. It's very short. It's basically me."

"You hate commitment," Wren said. "You once fled a town because a woman asked you to stay for supper."

"I fled because her stew was a *threat*, Wren, that's different." But he laughed, and then the laugh caught on something, and then he wasn't laughing, and none of them pretended otherwise for a moment.

Silas crossed the room and hugged him. It was brief and fierce and slightly awkward, because Silas had been hollowed by the bargain and was still learning where all their own edges were, but Bramble held on like a burr, and when they separated his eyes were wet and he pretended magnificently that they weren't.

"You'll write," Silas said.

"I'll do better. I'll visit. Every midwinter, at your valley, and I'll bring terrible gifts and worse stories, and if the stars ever flicker—even once—you send for me and I will come so fast I'll leave a Bramble-shaped hole in the air."

"Deal," Silas said.

"And *you*—" He rounded on Wren, jabbing a finger. "Take care of them. I know, I know, you're both adults, you don't need looking after, you've survived a fairy court, blah blah. Take care of them anyway. That's not a request. That's a peddler's curse. Refuse it and you'll have bad luck with hinges for the rest of your life."

Wren snorted. "Hinges."

"*Hinges,*" he confirmed solemnly. "You have no idea how much of your happiness depends on hinges until they fail."

She hugged him too, briefly, because she was not made for long farewells and neither, she suspected, was he. "Fairer court," she said against his shoulder. "You said a fairer court. Make it true. Don't let it grow teeth just because teeth are traditional."

"No teeth," Bramble promised. "Well. Decorative teeth. For ambiance."

"Bramble."

"Fine. *One* tooth. Ceremonial."

She pulled back and looked at him—at the half-fae peddler who owed a life and had paid it twice over, once in blood and once in staying—and decided that was as good as oaths got.

They walked him as far as the great root-hall, where the way split: up and out toward the grey morning, or deeper, toward the green dark that was becoming something new. Elowen Ashgrove stood at the fork, unaged and unbroken, directing the freed souls upward with the calm competence of a woman who had spent twenty years memorizing every corridor of her prison precisely so that someday she could march everyone out of it. She kissed Bramble's cheek. She said something to him too quietly for Wren to hear, and whatever it was, it made him stand up straighter than any blessing could have.

Then he turned, shouldered his bag, and walked down into the green, humming something tuneless and cheerful, and the ivy closed behind him like a curtain, gently, the way a house settles when someone comes home.

Nobody spoke until they were well up the switchback stairs.

"He'll be good at it," Silas said finally.

"He'll be *terrible* at it," Wren said. "He'll be terrible at it for ten years and then he'll be the best warden that mountain ever had, and he'll complain the entire time, and it'll be perfect."

Silas huffed—almost a laugh, the first one Wren had heard from them since the burning—and reached over without looking and took her hand.

Wren let them. She was, she reflected, getting alarmingly used to it.

* * *

They came out of the mountain into afternoon light so clean it hurt, forty-one freed souls blinking like newborns, and the world outside was already changing. The blight was breaking up across the lowlands—Wren could see it from the ridge, the grey winter-rot retreating in patches, grass coming up green through it in ragged, triumphant islands. Somewhere below, a village bell was ringing, and ringing, and ringing, and she couldn't tell whether it was alarm or celebration, and she supposed it might be both.

Elowen paused at the head of the trail and looked back at the mountain. Snow slid from its shoulders in long white ribbons. Underneath, impossibly, the rock showed green.

"Come along," she said to Silas, in the voice of a mother who has waited twenty years and intends to waste not one more minute of the walk home. "I want to see what your valley looks like with the sky back."

They started down. And Wren, falling into step beside Silas, felt the question rise in her that had been waiting since the tollhouse floor, since the throne room, since the moment the stars came back—

Home. They were all going home. But Silas's home was a valley that had spent a century hiding, and hers was a capital that had struck her name from the rolls, and neither of those places was quite home anymore, and—

Ahead, Elowen stopped dead on the trail.

"Wren," she said, without turning around. Her voice had gone very careful. "How far is it to the valley, by the road?"

"Four days. Three if we push."

"Then we should hurry." Elowen pointed down into the valley's direction, where the horizon should have been ordinary hills and evening cloud.

Instead, far off, above the place where the Hollow lay hidden, the sky was glowing. Not starlight. Not dawn.

Firelight.
