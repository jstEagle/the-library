## 20. Homecoming

The valley did not look different from above, and that was the cruelest part of it.

Silas stood at the ridge where the boundary stones began their slow descent toward home, and the Hollow spread out below them exactly as it always had: the crooked rooftops of Thistle Row, the mill pond holding a coin of grey sky, the guild hall with its sagging porch where Silas had spent twenty years learning to be small. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, obedient lines. Nothing burned. Nothing had fallen.

Except, of course, everything had.

"You're staring," Elowen said beside them. She said it lightly, but her hand found Silas's shoulder and stayed there, as if she needed proof of weight and warmth the way Silas needed proof of her—constantly, hungrily, reaching out to touch her sleeve or wrist just to confirm she was real and not another trick of the thorn-light.

"I'm trying to decide what I'm going to say to them," Silas admitted.

"To whom?"

"Everyone." Silas laughed, and it came out cracked. "Twenty years I lived down there thinking my mother abandoned us. Then I learned she was sold. Then I went under a mountain and unmade a bargain older than the guild, and now I have to walk into Thistle Row and explain that the valley's been lying to itself for three hundred years, and oh, by the way, magic works here now, please don't panic."

Elowen considered this. "You could lead with the crops."

"The what?"

She nodded downhill, and Silas followed her gaze properly for the first time—and stopped breathing.

It was late winter. It should have been mud and bare branches and the grey stubble of frozen fields. Instead, green ran down the valley sides like spilled ink. Winter wheat stood ankle-high in fields that should have been sleeping, impossibly thick, impossibly bright. The orchards along the mill stream were in blossom—*in blossom*, in the last week of winter—as if someone had told the trees spring was coming and they'd decided not to wait. And threading through it all, faint as breath on glass, was something Silas had never felt in their life: a hum at the edge of perception, warm and alive, like standing near an oven door.

Magic. In the Hollow. Gentle, diffuse, seeping up through the soil like meltwater.

"It's everywhere," Silas whispered. "I thought—I don't know what I thought. That it would take years. That the land would be angry."

"The land was never angry," Elowen said. "The land was *holding its breath*. Three hundred years, Silas. Everything this valley ever wanted to grow, it grew in secret, underground, waiting." Her voice caught on the last word, and Silas understood without asking that she was not only talking about wheat.

They started down. The path wound between boundary stones that no longer glowed with wardlight—no longer glowed at all, in fact, just sat in the grass like old men dozing in the sun. Silas touched one as they passed, out of habit, and felt nothing but cold granite and, beneath that, the faint pulse of the valley's new warmth. Markers now. Not walls.

Halfway down, they met the first people.

It was the Fenwick twins, hauling a cart of early greens toward the village, and they took one look at Elowen Ashgrove walking down from the mountain and dropped the cart entirely. Turnips rolled everywhere. Neither twin moved to retrieve them.

"That's—" the elder twin began.

"Elowen," the younger finished. "Elowen Ashgrove. From the *grave*."

"I was never in a grave," Elowen said, with the mild patience of someone who had recently negotiated with a king made of thorns and found him more reasonable than most villagers. "I was in a cellar. A very large cellar. Under a mountain."

The twins stared. Then stared at Silas. Then at Elowen again. Then the younger one sat down abruptly on the cart's shaft, missing it slightly, and landed in the mud.

"We thought you were dead," the elder managed. "We thought—twenty years, we thought you'd run off. Old Maren used to say you'd been taken by the fair folk, and everyone laughed at her, because there's no such thing—"

"There is such a thing," Silas said quietly. "And she was right."

The silence that followed had texture. Silas could feel the whole valley's disbelief pressing down the slope toward them, arriving ahead of them like weather.

By the time they reached Thistle Row, half the village had turned out—not in welcome, Silas realized, but in fear. People stood in doorways with iron pokers in their hands. Someone had brought out the old guild bell, the one meant to be rung only when the wards failed, and it hung silent from its beam while the crowd muttered beneath it. At the front stood Elder Corvane, who had forbidden the journey, who had insisted the court would simply take another soul if provoked, who had watched Silas walk out of the valley at dawn four months ago with the expression of a man watching someone step off a cliff.

He looked at Silas now, and then at Elowen, and his face did something complicated—grief and vindication and horror all fighting for space.

"You went," he said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I went."

"And the wards are gone."

"The wards were never protection," Silas said. "They were a payment plan. We weren't hidden from the world, Elder. We were *paid for*. Our invisibility was bought with my mother's soul, renewed every century, and the bill came due this winter. The stars didn't vanish because the wards failed. They vanished because the wards finally told the truth."

Corvane's jaw worked. Behind him, the crowd murmured like a hive. "And now?"

"Now the contract is ash. Now the Thorn King is free, and he chose—of his own will, with every right to do otherwise—to take no replacement. Now Bramble keeps the court beneath the mountain, and he keeps it kindly. And now—" Silas gestured helplessly at the impossible blossom-heavy orchards, the riotous green fields, the warm hum in the air. "Now this. Whatever this is. I didn't ask for it. None of us signed for it. It's just what happens when a valley stops being a cage."

Nobody spoke. A woman near the front—the cooper's widow, Silas thought, though she looked younger than memory suggested—slowly lowered her iron poker. She was staring past them all, at her own garden, where something was happening.

Where, in fact, her winter cabbages were growing.

Not fast, exactly. Not unnaturally. Just... steadily, visibly, the way things grow in a dream of summer, leaves unfurling with a soft rustle that had nothing to do with wind. The woman reached out and touched a leaf and made a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh.

"It doesn't feel wrong," she said wonderingly. "That's the thing. Magic always felt wrong, in the stories. This feels like—" She pressed her hand flat over her heart. "Like being *thanked*."

That broke the dam. People surged forward—not with pokers now, but with questions, dozens at once, hands reaching for Elowen as if to confirm she cast a shadow, had a heartbeat, could be wept on. And she let them. Silas watched their mother absorb the whole village's twenty years of guilt and grief and joy at once, watched her hold the cooper's widow's face in her hands and say, "You kept my garden. All these years. You watered it," and watched the woman come apart entirely.

Silas stepped back. This part wasn't theirs. This reunion belonged to the valley.

But grief has a way of ambushing you in the gaps between other people's joy, and it found Silas at the edge of the crowd, standing alone in the blossoming orchard, looking up at branches heavy with flowers that shouldn't exist. Twenty years of believing a lie. Twenty years of setting an extra place at the table out of habit before catching themselves. Twenty years of tending boundary stones for a bargain built on their mother's captivity, and calling it duty.

They didn't hear Wren approach. Of course they didn't; she moved like a ward-keeper even now, even disgraced, even carrying a satchel full of stolen contract fragments and a limp she'd earned breaking out of a fae vault.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand apart and catalog your feelings instead of having them." She came to stand beside them, shoulder almost touching. Above them, petals drifted down like lazy snow. "Your mother's home. The valley's saved. The sky has actual stars in it again—you checked, I watched you check, twice. And you're standing in a flowering orchard looking like someone handed you a bill."

Silas huffed. "Someone did. Twenty years of it."

"Mm." Wren picked a blossom off a low branch and turned it in her fingers. "You know what I keep thinking about? The tollhouse. The memory you traded. Small mercies, big price. You gave up—" she hesitated, "—what was it? Your tenth birthday? The one with the honey cake?"

"The one where Mother came home early and we ate the whole thing before Father got back and swore never to tell him." Silas smiled despite themselves, then felt the smile crack. "Yes. That one. Gone. Traded for a night's passage under a mountain."

"And how does it feel, having a hole where it used to be?"

Silas considered honestly. "Lighter. Which is the worst part. Like I lost something and gained room and can't tell which one happened."

Wren nodded slowly. "That's hollowing. Everyone thinks it's about loss. It's not. It's about *space*." She tucked the blossom behind Silas's ear, matter-of-fact, as if she'd done it a thousand times, and Silas's heart performed an unscheduled maneuver. "What you fill it with is the part nobody tells you. So." She jerked her chin toward the crowd around Elowen. "Go get filled. I'll mind the stones."

"You don't have to—"

"I know." She was already walking away, limping slightly, pausing to exchange a nod with the elder twin, who was still sitting in the mud next to his turnips and appeared to have accepted this as his permanent station in life. "Boundary stones don't tend themselves. Habit, remember? You taught me that one."

Silas watched her go and understood, with the particular clarity that comes after nearly dying together, that they were going to keep this person. Whatever shape that took. Whatever the guild became. Some debts you pay. Some you choose to carry.

* * *

By evening, the guild hall's common room held more people than it had in decades.

Elder Corvane sat at the head of the long table, which was either tradition or surrender—Silas suspected both. The fire was lit. Someone had brought honey cakes, which Silas chose not to read into, and failed completely, because Wren caught their eye across the room and mouthed *honey cake* with an expression of pure mischief.

Elowen held court at the far end, telling the story of the descent for the fourth time, each version slightly longer than the last. The villagers listened the way people listen to scripture: leaning in, flinching at the thorn-wight, gasping at the vault. When she got to the part about the Thorn King—about a prisoner wearing a crown, bound by the same bargain he enforced—a hush fell that had real weight to it.

"He *chose*," the cooper's widow said. "He could have taken Silas, and he didn't."

"He'd been taking replacements for three hundred years," Elowen said. "Every century, a new hostage, because the contract demanded one and he had no more choice than we did. When the contract burned, so did his chains. And the first free act of the Thorn Court's king was to refuse." She looked around the room. "Remember that, when you're afraid of what's under the mountain. Fear teaches you what a thing is. But choice teaches you what it *wants* to be."

Corvane cleared his throat. The room quieted. He looked old tonight, Silas thought—older than he had at midwinter, as if the intervening months had been spent carrying something heavy uphill.

"I owe the hall an accounting," he said. "I told this apprentice not to go. I argued the valley's safety lay in keeping the bargain, in paying what was asked, in not provoking powers beyond our understanding. I was wrong." He said it plainly, without theater, and Silas felt the words land somewhere old and bruised. "I was wrong about the danger. Wrong about the price. Wrong about what we were protecting. We weren't protecting a valley. We were protecting a lie, and the lie cost us Elowen Ashgrove twenty years, and it would have cost us Silas forever." He turned to Silas and inclined his head—the first time, in Silas's entire apprenticeship, that the elder had bowed to anyone. "The Hollow Guild died this winter. What rises in its place is yours to build. I'll fetch wood and mend roofs until you tell me otherwise."

Silas's throat tightened. "Elder—"

"Don't argue with your elders," Corvane said, and for one moment the dry humor underneath his gravity showed through. "It's the only tradition I intend to keep."

Laughter moved through the room, loosening it, and the evening tipped over into something warm and ordinary: food passed hand to hand, children chasing each other between benches, the twins arguing over whose turnip harvest was more miraculous. Wren ended up beside Silas at the bench by the window, close enough that their shoulders touched, neither of them moving away.

"Look at that," she murmured, nodding at the window. Outside, the first stars were emerging over the ridge—real ones, honest ones, wheeling slowly into place. "Still there."

"Still checking?" Silas asked.

"Every night since the mountain." She said it simply, without embarrassment. "I think I'll check for the rest of my life. You spend enough time around bargains, you learn not to trust anything given freely. But those—" she pointed with her spoon, "—those were never given. They were just always there, behind the wardlight, waiting for someone to break the bargain honestly." She glanced sideways. "That was you, by the way. In case the history books get it wrong. Which they will, because you'll insist on writing them modestly."

"I make no promises about the history books."

"Liar. You've already started drafting. I saw the ink on your fingers."

Silas laughed—really laughed, the hollow place inside them filling a little more—and leaned into Wren's shoulder, and outside the stars wheeled on, patient and true.

Later, when the fire had burned low and the villagers had drifted home and Elowen had fallen asleep in a chair with a blanket over her knees and a look on her face like a woman finally allowed to set down a century of weight, Silas climbed to the guild hall's upper window and looked out over the valley one more time.

Green in the dark. Blossoms in the cold. Warmth rising from the earth like a heartbeat that had been muffled for three hundred years and was now, finally, allowed to be loud.

Tomorrow there would be work. A charter to rewrite, gates to open, records to make honest, a guild to rebuild on terms no one had signed and everyone would have to choose. Tomorrow Corvane would fetch wood, and Wren would argue about ward-keeper duties she technically no longer held, and the valley would have to learn what it was without its walls.

But tonight—

Tonight, Silas noticed it only because the firelight shifted: a folded paper on the desk below, sealed with plain wax, addressed in a hand they didn't recognize. No courier had come up the valley road. No traveler had arrived with the evening crowd.

The seal was unbroken. The paper smelled faintly of green things, and underneath that—faintly, unmistakably—of thorns.
