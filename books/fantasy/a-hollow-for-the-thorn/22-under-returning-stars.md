## 22. Under Returning Stars

The frost had come early that year, but gently — the way a guest arrives when they've been invited.

Silas Ashgrove stood at the first boundary stone with a lantern in one hand and a rag in the other, wiping lichen off the old carved sigil the way you'd wipe dust from a portrait of someone you'd finally forgiven. The stone itself hadn't changed. It was still squat and gray and older than the guild, older than the valley's name, older probably than every argument anyone had ever had about it. What had changed was what it meant. A year ago it had been a wall. Now it was a signpost: *You are entering the Hollow. Mind the turnips.*

That last part Silas had added themselves, chiseled small beneath the sigil in letters that were, Wren said, an insult to stonemasons everywhere. Silas disagreed. The turnips deserved recognition. They were having an extraordinary year.

They finished polishing the sigil, stepped back, and looked up.

The stars held.

They had held for a full year now — every night, through storms and cloudless cold and one memorable evening in spring when half the valley had stood in the lanes pointing upward like people who had never seen weather before. Which, in fairness, most of them hadn't. Not real sky. Not the whole unbroken spill of it, wheeling overhead without a single star going dark like a candle pinched out by a careful hand. For two hundred years the wards had hidden the heavens to hide the valley, and the valley had learned not to look up. Now looking up was just something you did. Silas still did it too often, still caught themself standing in doorways at midnight with their neck craned like an owl, but they had decided this was not a flaw. It was a habit of gratitude.

"Talking to rocks again?"

Wren came up the path with her collar up and her hands in her pockets and her ward-keeper's badge — newly reissued, newly earned, and this time honestly — glinting at her throat. Her hair was shorter than it had been on the mountain. That had been Elowen's doing, with kitchen scissors and no apology, after Wren fell asleep by the fire one evening in March and woke up shorn. She claimed not to mind. She claimed, in fact, that it was the best haircut of her life, which Silas suspected was love talking rather than honesty, since the back of it remained genuinely alarming.

"I'm *maintaining* the rocks," Silas said. "There's a difference."

"Is there."

"There is now. Before, I maintained them because I was afraid of what would happen if I stopped. Now I maintain them because—" Silas patted the stone, "—because someone has to introduce visitors to the turnips."

Wren leaned against the marker beside them, shoulder to shoulder, breath fogging in the lamplight. Below, the valley lay scattered with windows already gold against the dusk. The Hollow Guild hall had its lights on too, all of them, which would have been unthinkable once — three members hoarding candles like dragons. Now there were eleven guild members, and two of them were children who had wandered in off the road last summer asking if magic was real, and Elder Maren had looked at Silas over their heads with an expression of profound surrender and said, *Apparently we're doing this.*

"Midwinter's eve," Wren said. "One year."

"One year." Silas turned the rag over in their hands. "I keep waiting for it to feel different. The anniversary. Everyone kept telling me it would feel different."

"Doesn't it?"

"No. It feels like tonight. It felt like tonight yesterday." They shrugged. "Maybe that's the point. Maybe peace isn't supposed to announce itself. Maybe it's supposed to be so ordinary you have to be reminded it was ever otherwise."

Wren considered this for a moment, then said, "Or maybe you're just bad at anniversaries," which was also fair.

* * *

The festival had grown teeth over the year — good ones. Last midwinter's eve, the valley had spent huddled indoors waiting for the sky to fail again, and when it didn't, the celebration had been less a festival than a collective exhale. This year people had planned. There were lanterns strung from the well to the guild hall. There was a bonfire being built in the square by apprentices who had never seen a thorn-wight and hopefully never would, and who therefore built their bonfires with the reckless confidence of the blessed. There was a table of food that would have been impossible even five years ago, when the valley's soil gave exactly what it had always given and nothing more.

Now things grew that shouldn't. Not wildly — the mountain's new warden was careful, and the magic that seeped into the Hollow came in like water through cloth, slow and gentle and asked-for. But the apple trees had fruited twice. The winter wheat came up green where it had always come up gray. Old Tam's rheumatism had quieted, though he insisted this was the wool socks and Silas saw no reason to argue him out of it.

And the mountain itself — Bramble's mountain now — breathed warmth down the valley on the coldest nights. Faintly. You had to stand at the northern stones to feel it, a soft pressure against your face like standing near a sleeping animal. The first time Silas had noticed it, they'd cried, which they told no one except Wren, who told everyone, because she was like that.

"He keeps his word," Silas said now, tilting their head toward the dark bulk of the mountain against the stars. "Every night. I can feel it."

"You check every night?"

"Every night."

"That's not checking, that's visiting."

Silas opened their mouth to object and found they couldn't. Because it was true, wasn't it. Somewhere under all that stone, in a court that had thawed from a prison into — whatever it was becoming, a school, a sanctuary, a very strange household — a half-fae peddler with mismatched eyes and a laugh like cracking ice sat on a throne of living thorn and did his job. He sent letters. Terrible letters, dictated apparently to a scribe who hated him, full of court gossip and complaints about his advisors and postscripts that made Silas laugh out loud in front of witnesses. The last one had included a pressed flower from a garden that hadn't existed a year ago, and a note reading: *First bloom in four hundred years. Do not tell the rose-warden I touched it. B.*

Bramble, warden of a fairer court. He'd chosen to stay when the debt was paid — chosen it, freely, which was the only currency the new court recognized. Some nights Silas missed him so much it was a physical ache behind the ribs. Most nights they just felt lucky. Lucky to have known him. Lucky that he was out there, keeping watch, keeping word, warming a valley he'd only ever passed through.

"Come on," Wren said. "Maren will send someone after us if we miss the toast. And by 'someone' she means those children, and by 'send' she means 'release like hounds.'"

* * *

The guild hall smelled of woodsmoke, spiced cider, and the particular scorched-sugar smell of Elowen's baking, which was legendary and slightly dangerous. Silas's mother moved through the crowded room like a woman making up for twenty lost years one loaf at a time. She had aged, this year — really aged, catching up all at once, silver coming into her hair like frost claiming a field. She'd said once, laughing, that she intended to age twice as fast as everyone else until she caught up, and then stop, and everyone had pretended this was a joke while privately suspecting it was policy.

She caught Silas's eye across the room and raised her mug, and something passed between them that needed no words. It had taken months to get here — to the place where looking at each other wasn't an accusation. In the cell beneath the mountain, Elowen had been furious, and she'd been right to be: her child had walked into the Thorn Court to trade themselves for her, and no mother wants her rescue purchased with her child's freedom. They had fought about it, afterward, properly, loudly, in the guild kitchen with flour on both their faces. And somewhere in the middle of the fighting Elowen had stopped and said, *You went in alone. You answered the door alone. You were brave alone,* and her voice had cracked on *alone*, and Silas had understood that the anger had been fear wearing armor, and after that it got easier.

Now she crossed the room, kissed the top of Silas's head — she could still reach it; Silas had decided to find this charming rather than humiliating — and said, "Your bread's rising badly. You've been distracted."

"I've been *polishing rocks*."

"Mm. Distracted." She squeezed their shoulder. "Go on, then. The toast's starting."

Elder Maren climbed onto a chair, which for a woman of her dignity was roughly equivalent to climbing a mountain, and raised her cup. The hall went quiet.

"A year ago," she said, "I told this fool—" she pointed at Silas with her mug, affectionately, "—that provoking the Thorn Court would cost us everything. I told them the valley survived because it hid. I told them some bargains are best left unread." She paused. "I was wrong about all of it, and I am going to say so out loud, once a year, on this night, for as long as I live, so that no one ever again mistakes caution for wisdom. To the Hollow — visible, open, and free. To the ones who broke the bargain honestly. And to the stars—" she tipped her cup toward the open window, toward the wheeling sky, "—which held."

The hall drank. The children drank cider and immediately regretted nothing. Someone started a song, badly, and was joined by forty voices determined to make it better through sheer volume, which failed, gloriously.

Silas stood near the fire with Wren, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation. They had gotten good at that this year. Good at silence, good at shared work, good at the small grammar of two people who had learned each other in the worst possible circumstances and then, remarkably, been given the chance to learn each other in the ordinary ones. Wren knew how Silas took their tea (badly — too much honey). Silas knew that Wren hummed when she worked a difficult ward, tunelessly, like a kettle deciding whether to sing. They had argued about the guild charter's punctuation. They had planted an apple tree together in the spring and then argued about whose fault it was that it leaned. (It was Wren's fault. She knew it.)

"Next year," Wren said quietly, "same stone. Same time."

"Same stone," Silas agreed. "Same time."

"And the year after."

"The year after."

"And—" Wren hesitated, which she almost never did, and then didn't hesitate, which was more characteristic: "and the same house, eventually. If you want. I've been looking at the cottage past the mill. It leans worse than our apple tree but it has good light."

Silas looked at her. The firelight did something generous to her face, or perhaps her face did something generous to the firelight; Silas had stopped trying to determine which. "You hate that cottage. You said the floor sloped like a ship in a storm."

"I've reconsidered. The slope builds character."

"It does not build character, it breaks crockery."

"We'll nail the crockery down."

Silas laughed, and the laugh turned into something warmer, and they took Wren's hand, and Wren let her, and outside the window the stars burned on, indifferent and abundant, the way stars are supposed to burn.

"Yes," Silas said. "All right. Yes."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the bonfire had gone down to embers and the children had been carried home asleep and the hall finally stood empty — Silas walked out alone to the first stone one more time. It was a thing they did now on midwinter's eve, a private observance nobody had assigned them. They brought the lantern, though they barely needed it. The sky did the lighting.

They set their palm flat on the stone, on the old sigil, on their own crude turnip inscription beneath it, and they felt it: the faint, steady warmth off the mountain, the warden's promise arriving on schedule, patient as tide. *Keeping my word*, it said, in the only language stone understands. *Still here. Still fair. Still yours.*

A year ago, on this night, Silas had stood at this exact spot and watched the last star go out, and had believed — had *known*, the way you know things when you're afraid — that the world was a cage with the paint peeling. That safety was a lie bought with a soul. That every kindness in the valley rested on a debt no one would name.

And it had been true. All of it. That was the terrible part. It had all been true, and the truth had nearly eaten them.

But it hadn't been the *whole* truth. That was the other part, the part Silas carried now like a coal in the chest: that cages can be broken honestly. That contracts written in bad faith contain, folded small in their margins, the clause nobody read. That a mother can carry her hollowed child home through a collapsing court. That a disgraced ward-keeper can refuse to leave. That a peddler who owed a life can pay it and then choose to stay anyway, not because he must, but because the staying was the point all along.

Silas looked up. Every star present. Every star accounted for.

"Goodnight, Bramble," they said softly, to the mountain. "Goodnight, Mum. Goodnight, Wren — don't wait up."

Then, because the moment demanded it and because they had earned the right to be ridiculous at their own boundary stone, they added:

"Mind the turnips."

The warmth pulsed once against their palm, faint as a heartbeat, amused as a friend. And Silas Ashgrove — last apprentice of the Hollow Guild, first member of whatever it was becoming, keeper of markers instead of walls — shouldered their lantern and walked home under the returning stars, which held, and held, and held.
