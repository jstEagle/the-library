## 1. Salt on the Scales

The night market of Corvane opened the way all good lies do: slowly, and then all at once.

By the time the sun had finished drowning itself in the harbor, Sebastian Villalobos had already claimed her corner beneath the leaning awning of a spice-seller who charged her too much rent and pretended otherwise. Her stall was a plank table, a strip of oilcloth, and a wooden scale whose brass pans had gone green at the edges from ten years of sea air. On one pan she stacked her wares: knotted cords dyed the red of cheap wine, river-stones polished smooth and painted with eyes that never blinked quite right, little tin fish on leather thongs that were supposed to keep nets full and husbands faithful. On the other pan she set a lump of salt the size of a fist, because Corvane's market law required every charm-seller to display *the honest weight*—a token of the city's founding creed that nothing sold here should promise more than it could carry.

Corvane was a city without magic. That was its pride, written into its charter in letters of iron and repeated by its magistrates like a prayer they'd forgotten the meaning of. No temples to the old gods stood within its walls. No hedge-witches hung their bundles in doorways. The Concord had burned all that out two generations ago, or so the story went, and what remained was a city of scales and ledgers and hard bargains struck under lantern-light—clean, the Corvanese liked to say, clean as salt.

Sebastian had spent ten years learning to be small enough to live inside that word.

She arranged her charms with the care of a woman arranging alibis. Each knot she tied with deliberate clumsiness. Each painted eye she left slightly crooked, because perfect work invited questions and questions invited attention, and attention was the one currency she could not afford. A charm-seller's magic was supposed to be entirely imaginary—the customer supplied the belief, the seller supplied only the object—and in ten years she had never once let anything real pass through her hands at that table.

The suppression tattoos ran from her wrists to her shoulders, inked in patterns that looked, to a casual glance, like ordinary sailor's marks: waves, anchors, a gull with its wings spread. They were not ordinary. They were a cage stitched into her skin, forty-one needles' worth of binding script that sat against her bones like a hand pressed flat over a screaming mouth. She had paid for them in a border town whose name she refused to remember, paid everything she had carried out of the fire, and every morning since she woke and pressed her thumb to the gull at her collarbone just to feel the dull, blessed nothing underneath.

Nothing. Ten years of nothing. She had learned to love it the way you learn to love a scar: not because it didn't hurt, but because it meant the wound had closed.

"Charm for the road?" she called to a fishwife hauling baskets past the stall, her voice pitched to the flat, cheerful monotone of the utterly harmless. "Keeps the gulls off your catch. Three coppers."

"Gulls don't listen to tin," the fishwife said, but she bought one anyway, because that was the night market's true religion—not faith, exactly, but the refusal to leave any luck untried.

That was the bargain Sebastian had made with this city. It believed in nothing, and so it asked nothing of her, and in exchange she sold it little lies about luck and kept the enormous truth folded up inside her arms like a blade wrapped in cloth.

* * *

The crowd thickened as the lamps came up. Corvane's night market smelled of frying onions and tar and the green rot of the tideflats, and it roared with the particular argumentative joy of people who had survived another day and intended to be compensated for it. Sebastian worked her customers the way she'd been taught by Odile, who had taught her the trade back when Sebastian had arrived in the city with burned hands and no name anyone could pronounce: smile with your mouth, count with your eyes, never touch the customer's palm unless they pay extra for the fortune-telling, and even then tell them something boring. Boring fortunes kept people alive. *You will have a dry winter and an enemy who talks too much.* Nobody ever came back to argue with boring.

She was mid-haggle with a rope-maker's apprentice over a knotted cord when she saw the woman.

She noticed her the way you notice weather—a pressure change, a wrongness in the air before the rain. The woman stood at the edge of the lamplight holding a bundle wrapped in a grey shawl, and she wasn't browsing. She was watching Sebastian with the fixed, hungry stillness of someone who has already decided and is only gathering the courage to spend what the decision will cost.

The bundle shifted. Made a sound. Not a cry—a sound smaller than a cry, a wet, rattling effort at breath, the kind of sound that lives in the space between coughs and never quite becomes a voice again.

Sebastian knew that sound. She had heard it in another city, another life, coming out of the throats of children in a street where something had fallen from the sky and left the air itself sick. She had been able to do something about it then. That was the whole of why she was here now, selling tin fish under a false name in a city that had outlawed the thing she was.

The woman approached the stall like a swimmer fighting a current.

"You're the charm-seller." Her voice came out scraped. She was young—younger than Sebastian, maybe twenty-three—with chapped hands and the gray, unwashed look of someone who had stopped sleeping. "They said—the fishwives said you're the one who does the real ones."

"I do knots," Sebastian said pleasantly. "Knots for luck, knots for love, knots to keep your mother-in-law from visiting. Four coppers."

The woman did not laugh. She set the bundle down on the edge of the table, and the shawl fell open, and there was the child.

A boy, perhaps three years old, with the terrible translucent quality that children get when fever has been burning through them long enough to start consuming the fat and then the muscle underneath. His lips had gone the blue-gray of deep water. His breath came in shallow pulls, each one a small labor, his whole chest working like a bellows operated by a dying man. Sweat had glued dark curls to a forehead far too hot. He was asleep, or in the place past sleep where the body goes to negotiate.

"He's been like this nine days," the woman said. "The physiker says it's the lung-rot. He says—" Her voice caught and she dragged it back like a net. "He says I should make him comfortable. That's what he said. Comfortable. He's three."

Around them the market roared on, buying luck, haggling over onions, laughing at a juggler's dropped knife. Sebastian looked at the child and felt the tattoos wake along her arms—not pain, never pain, the bindings were too well-made for pain—but a kind of pressure, a tide pushing against a seawall. Beneath the gull and the waves and the anchor, under ten years of careful nothing, the thing she had buried stirred and turned over in its sleep.

*Don't,* she told it.

"I can sell you a cord," she said aloud. "Red thread. Tie it at his wrist. It won't cure him—it's not that kind of charm—but it's for strength, and strength matters, and it costs four coppers which is four coppers you need for medicine, so honestly, madam, save your money."

The woman stared at her. Something behind her eyes was breaking in slow motion, and Sebastian watched it happen and hated herself with a completeness that surprised even her.

"They said you were different," the woman whispered. "They said some of the sellers—if you begged—they'd know a prayer. A blessing. Anything. Please. I'll pay anything. I'll work for you. I'll—" She was crying now, silently, the way poor women cry, without the luxury of noise. "His name is Tam. He was walking nine days ago. He chased a gull on the seawall. He laughed. And now he makes that sound when he breathes and I sit up all night counting his breaths and praying he keeps making it."

*Praying.* The word went through Sebastian like a fishhook.

There were no gods in Corvane. That was the charter, that was the law, that was the entire architecture of the peace she had hidden inside for a decade. But the woman was praying anyway, right there in front of her, in a city where prayer was supposed to have been burned out of the ground like a stump. Praying to whatever might be listening, because her son was dying and the physikers had given up and the city offered her tin fish and red thread and called it mercy.

And Sebastian Villalobos—who had once walked through a storm like a doorway, who had spoken to thunder and been answered, who had carried in her body a power that had burned cities and drowned fleets and started a war that had rewritten the maps—stood behind a plank table and offered this woman a knot.

"No," she said, and her own voice sounded foreign to her, thin and small. "I'm sorry. I don't know prayers. I sell cord."

The woman took the red thread because it was all there was to take. She counted out four coppers with shaking fingers, tied the cord around her son's thin wrist herself, tucked the shawl closed around him, and picked him up, and turned away into the crowd.

Sebastian watched her go. The market swallowed her the way the sea swallows things—no drama, no pause, just the closing of bodies and lantern-light, and then she was gone, and somewhere across the city there was a room where a woman would sit up all night counting breaths, holding a red thread that held nothing.

Sebastian stood at her stall for a long moment, hands flat on the plank, breathing carefully. The scale beside her sat perfectly balanced: salt on one pan, tin fish on the other, the honest weight and the honest lie, weighing nothing against nothing.

*Ten years,* she thought. *Ten years of being small. Ten years of choosing it, every day, choosing it again like a vow.*

But vows are made by the person you were, and the woman watching a dying child disappear into a crowd was not entirely that person anymore. Something had cracked. She felt it the way you feel ice shift underfoot—one hairline fracture, quiet, total.

She packed up early. Told the spice-seller she was ill. Walked home the long way, through the tanner's rows, checking behind her twice, three times, the old habits of a hunted woman, while all the way the pressure built along her arms like a storm reading the coastline.

* * *

Odile's lantern-house stood at the end of Rope Walk, the last building before the mudflats gave up pretending to be land. It leaned. Everything about it leaned—the stairs, the chimney, Odile herself, a small round woman of indeterminate age who moved through the world at the speed of syrup and missed nothing. She had taken Sebastian in ten years ago with no questions, which Sebastian had understood even then was its own kind of question, patiently unanswered.

Sebastian came in through the kitchen, and Odile looked up from a pot of fish stew, took one look at her face, and said, "Sit down before you fall down," which was how Odile said almost everything.

Sebastian sat. The kitchen was warm and smelled of bay leaf and lamp oil. Through the window, out past the mudflats, the harbor light turned its slow circle, sweeping the water like a lighthouse keeping watch over nothing in particular.

"A mother came to the stall tonight," Sebastian said, to the table, because she couldn't say it to Odile's face. "With her son. Lung-rot. The physikers gave him nine days and they've used eight of them."

Odile stirred the pot. Said nothing. Waited, the way she always waited, with the patience of a woman who had raised four sons and buried two and understood that some silences are doors.

"She wanted a blessing." Sebastian's hands were flat on the table, and she noticed they were trembling, and she pressed them harder. "In Corvane. In the city with no gods. She wanted a blessing for her child and I sold her a red thread and told her to save her money."

"And?"

"And I followed her."

Odile's ladle stopped moving.

"Tannery Row, then the weavers' stairs, then a room above a sail-loft with a window that doesn't close. I told myself I was just going to walk past. I told myself I'd stand in the street and look at the lit window and do nothing, because doing nothing is safe, doing nothing is the whole architecture of my life—" Sebastian heard her own voice climbing and forced it level. "The window was open. I could hear him breathing. That sound. You know the sound."

Odile knew the sound. Her face did something old and private and then went still.

"The tattoos hold," Sebastian said. "They've always held. Forty-one needles, ten years, they hold. But I put my hand on the windowsill, and I didn't try to do anything, I swear to you I didn't try—I just put my hand there, and something came *through*. Not much. A trickle. Like a crack in a dam the width of a fingernail. Warmth, down my arm, out through my palm. I don't even know what I did. I don't know if I did anything at all. Fever breaks on its own sometimes. Children turn corners in the night for no reason anyone can name."

"But?"

"But his breathing changed." Her voice failed on the words and she said them anyway. "Before I left the street. Before I was even down the stairs. It changed. Slower. Deeper. The rattle going out of it like silt settling in water. And I stood in that alley with my heart slamming and my arm burning under the ink and I thought: *ten years. Ten years of nothing, and the first thing that gets through is for a stranger's child, because a woman prayed in a city where prayer is illegal.*"

The stew bubbled. The harbor light swept its circle. Somewhere out on the flats a curlew cried, three notes, falling.

Odile ladled stew into a bowl and set it in front of Sebastian and put a spoon in her hand and said, "Eat."

Sebastian ate, mechanically, tasting nothing, while the tremor in her hands slowly stilled.

"You know what happens now," Odile said quietly. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know what happens now." Sebastian did know. A healed child was a miracle, and miracles had mothers, and mothers talked, and talk in Corvane traveled faster than plague. *The charm-seller on Rope Walk. The one who does the real ones.* Two more desperate parents would come, then ten, then a rumor shaped like a whisper shaped like a report, and reports in this city went to the magistrates, and the magistrates answered to the church, and the church had inquisitors whose entire profession was finding embers in ash. "I should burn the stall. Leave tonight. Renn could get me out on the tide."

"You could," Odile agreed. "Or."

"Or what?"

"Or you stay, and you keep being small, and you pray—whatever's left in you that prays—that one trickle through a cracked wall doesn't become a flood." Odile sat down across from her, folding her chapped hands. "Or you decide that ten years of nothing was long enough, and you find out what you actually are now, instead of what you were running from. I'm not telling you which. I've watched you carry this for ten years, girl, and I've never once asked you to put it down or pick it up. I'm not starting tonight."

"That's not fair," Sebastian said, half-laughing, half-broken. "You're supposed to tell me what to do. That's what you're for."

"I feed you," Odile said. "Telling you what to do is above my station." She reached across the table and tapped Sebastian's wrist, right over the anchor mark, with one blunt finger. "But I'll say this. Whatever's under that ink—it knocked tonight. First time in ten years. Things that knock once tend to knock again, and louder, and they don't much care whether the door is bolted."

Sebastian looked down at her arm. The gull at her collarbone, wings spread, frozen mid-flight over a sea of binding script. For ten years it had been a tattoo. Just ink. Just a cage.

Tonight, for the first time, she had felt it as something else: a shape drawn over a living thing, waiting.

Outside, the fog was coming in off the flats, the way it did most nights, swallowing the harbor light and the mudflats and the seawall where a boy had chased a gull nine days ago. Somewhere across the city, in a room above a sail-loft, a woman was counting breaths that had gone slow and deep, and whispering thanks to whatever had listened.

*Whatever had listened.*

Sebastian lay awake in her narrow bed under the eaves long after the house went quiet, thumb pressed to the gull, feeling the nothing underneath—and for the first time in ten years, the nothing was not empty. Far down, beneath the ink and the silence and the careful smallness of her constructed life, something vast had turned in its sleep, and answered a prayer it should never have heard, in a city where no gods were supposed to exist.

She fell asleep near dawn with her hand still curled over her collarbone, and dreamed of standing on a seawall with a storm-front on the horizon, and woke with salt on her lips though the window had been shut all night.
