## 2. The Prayer That Answered Back

The child's name was Wren.

Sebastian learned it three days after the healing, when the mother came back to the market—not to her stall, never to her stall, but to the shadowed mouth of Tanner's Lane where Sebastian took her supper of fried eel and cheap wine, standing, like a woman who had nowhere to be and wanted to keep it that way. The mother's name was Sefa. She pressed a wrapped parcel into Sebastian's hands with the fierce, embarrassed gratitude of someone who had nothing left to give and gave it anyway: honey-cakes, still warm, and a scrap of blue cloth embroidered with a gull in flight.

"She sleeps through the night now," Sefa said. "She breathes like—like there's nothing wrong at all. Like she was never sick."

*She was never healed,* Sebastian did not say. *She was healed by something I am not supposed to have.*

She smiled instead, the small closed smile she had practiced for ten years until it fit her face like a well-worn glove, and said the things charm-sellers said: *the luck was already in you, madam, I only helped you find it.* Sefa laughed, wiped her eyes, and went, and Sebastian stood in the lane holding warm honey-cakes and feeling the way she always felt after—one hand heavier than the other, as though she'd stolen something from herself.

The blue cloth she kept. She told herself it was for the gull. Gulls were common in Corvane; they wheeled over the salt-works all day, screaming their greedy joy. Nobody would look twice at a gull. She sewed it inside the lining of her coat, over her heart, where the oldest scar sat—the one shaped like a forked line, like lightning deciding where to strike—and told herself that was coincidence too.

That was on the third day. On the fourth night, the prayer came looking for her.

* * *

It began, as most terrible things in Corvane began, quietly.

Sebastian lived above Odile's lantern-house, in a room barely wider than her cot, with a window that looked out over the harbor basin where the fishing boats knocked their hulls together in their sleep. The room smelled of lamp oil and old wood and, faintly, of the sea-tang that got into everything in Corvane and never quite left. She liked it because it was small. Small rooms were hard to fill with weather.

She woke past midnight with her scars burning.

Not aching. *Burning.* All of them at once, the lattice of them that ran from her left wrist up under her arm and across her shoulder blade and down her spine like a river system drawn on skin by someone who hated rivers—every suppression tattoo she wore was inked to cross an old scar, sealing it, and tonight every seal was hot as a brand. She sat up in the dark with her breath coming fast and her hands flat on the cot, waiting for the pain to crest and pass, because in ten years it had always crested and passed.

It didn't pass. It *spoke.*

There is no honest way to describe what happened next except to say that a voice came through her scars the way water comes through a cracked wall—not heard so much as *seeping*, pressure finding the flaw, filling it, rising. It did not use her ears. It used the wounds. And it said:

***Little vessel.***

Sebastian's whole body went rigid. The word landed in her like a hook landing in a fish—she felt it set, felt the barb of it, felt the line go taut somewhere very far away and very deep down.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, like a fool. "No, no—you're dead. You're all dead. I watched it happen."

The voice did not answer immediately. That was worse. The silence had texture to it, a patience, the way the sea goes flat and glassy before it remembers what it's for. Then, seeping again, slow as tide:

***The child prayed.***

"I know." Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which surprised her. Ten years of practice. "Children pray. They pray to your replacements, to the Concord's painted saints, to their mothers, to the moon. It doesn't mean anything."

***She prayed to the one who mended her.*** A pause, long enough for Sebastian to hear her own heart slamming. ***And the one who mended her answered. And the answering made a sound. And the sound traveled. And we heard it.***

"Who is *we*?"

The silence that followed had a different quality now. Not patience. *Recognition.* Something vast turning its attention, the way a whale turns toward a swimmer's splash—unhurried, enormous, and suddenly, terribly aware.

***You do not remember my name,*** the voice said, and it was not a question, and it was almost gentle, which frightened her more than anger would have. ***But your blood does. Say the word rain.***

"I won't."

***Say it.***

"You can't make me—" But even as she said it, the word rose in her unbidden, the way a sneeze rises, the way a sob rises, and she thought *rain*, just the shape of it, just the syllables, and every scar on her body lit white-hot and the window rattled in its frame and outside, above a harbor where no cloud had been an hour ago, a single thunderhead assembled itself out of clear night air, silent, coiled, patient, like a fist deciding whether to close.

Sebastian threw herself off the cot and onto the floor and pressed both palms against the boards and *pushed*, pushed everything down, all of it, the way she had taught herself in the first year of exile when the storms tried to follow her out of the war-lands like dogs that had lost their master. Push. Breathe. Be small. Be a charm-seller. Be nobody. Be a woman named Sebastian who sold luck and believed none of it.

The thunderhead hung there for the length of nine heartbeats. Then, grudgingly, grain by grain, it unraveled. The stars came back. The harbor went dark and ordinary. And the voice in her scars withdrew—not gone; she could feel it the way you feel a splinter, a wrongness seated in the tissue—but withdrawn, receding down whatever deep channel connected her ruined veins to the drowned places of the world.

***I will not ask again tonight,*** it said, at the very edge of hearing, and the tenderness in it was the cruelest thing about it. ***Sleep, little vessel. You have burned so bright for so long alone.***

Then nothing. Just the creak of boats and Odile's lantern turning below and the ordinary breathing of a city that did not believe in gods and was about to be proven catastrophically wrong.

Sebastian lay on the floorboards until dawn without sleeping, one hand pressed flat over the hidden gull, counting her scars like a miser counts coins he knows are cursed.

* * *

In the morning she did the arithmetic.

Ten years ago, in the last days of the god-war, she had done something—she had never let herself finish the memory, had walled it off behind a door in her mind painted to look like a wall—and afterward she had run, and paid a tattooist in a border town everything she had to write the suppression marks over her scars, and the tattooist had taken one look at what lay beneath and refused to meet her eyes ever again. *Whatever you were,* the woman had said, inking the first seal, *stop being it. These will hold if you stay small. Stay dry. Stay quiet. Never heal anyone. Never bless anyone. Never answer.*

*Never answer.*

A child had prayed. And Sebastian, soft-hearted, stupid, ten years careful undone by one feverish little girl with gray lips—Sebastian had *answered*. And the answering had been a bell rung in a drowned cathedral, and everything that slept in the deep water had turned toward the sound.

They were awake now. Or one of them was. And it knew her address.

She spent the morning doing what she always did: grinding charm-powder, cutting cord, knotting the little false promises she sold for coppers. But her hands shook, and the knots came out wrong, and by midday she had ruined more stock than she'd made. So she stopped, and sat in the back of Odile's lantern-house among the oil tins, and looked at the basket of finished charms—forty of them, maybe fifty, each one a tiny braided lie, a knotted string and a bead and a whispered nothing—and understood, with the cold clarity that comes after a sleepless night, what she had to do.

If the voice could reach her through a prayer answered once, it could reach her through anything she touched with intent. Every charm she had ever sold was a thread. Thin threads. But she was a beacon now whether she wished it or not, and beacons did not get to choose which ships came toward them.

She carried the basket up to the seawall at dusk, to the stretch behind the salt-works where the wind came off the water clean and no one walked after dark because the ward-lamps there had been broken since the usurper's tax collectors stripped the district.

She meant to throw them into the sea. That was the plan: drop them in the water, let the tide take the threads out beyond the harbor wards, scatter them past any hope of gathering. She stood at the wall with the basket on its crooked handle and the wind pulling strands of hair loose from her scarf, and she looked down at the black water, and she could not do it.

Because they weren't just stock. They were the market. They were the widow who bought a knot for her son's boat. They were the dockhand who swore the red-beaded one kept his hands from shaking. They were small, harmless, fraudulent comforts—and they were also, God forgive her, the only magic she had permitted herself in ten years, the one place where the woman she'd buried got to move her fingers.

So she burned them instead.

She built a little fire in the lee of the wall, cupped from the wind, fed it with driftwood and tar-paper, and fed it her charms one by one. The beads cracked in the heat. The cords curled and flared green and blue, copper salts burning color into the flames, and the smoke went straight up in the windless dusk like a column, like incense, like something being offered rather than destroyed. She worked methodically, throat tight, saying nothing, because there was no rite for this, no words for a woman unmaking the only altar she'd allowed herself.

When the last knot caught fire—a plain one, undyed cord, the kind she sold children for a farthing—she held it a moment longer than she needed to.

"They're just charms," she told the smoke. "They're nothing. Let them be nothing."

The firelight moved across her scars, and for half a heartbeat the burn-marks seemed to shift, to rearrange themselves into a pattern she almost recognized—a coastline, a river delta, a word written in a language older than Corvane—and then the flame guttered and the pattern was gone and she told herself she had imagined it, the way she had told herself things for ten years.

She stayed until the fire died to embers and kicked sand over them, and walked home along the seawall in full dark with the harbor lights strung out ahead of her like fallen stars, and she made herself count them, all forty-one, because counting was a mortal thing to do and tonight she needed to be mortal very badly.

At the door of the lantern-house, Odile was waiting up, as she sometimes did, a shawl around her shoulders and her silver-streaked hair pinned carelessly, her sharp face softened by lamplight into something almost maternal.

"You smell of smoke," Odile said.

"Driftwood fire," Sebastian said. "Behind the salt-works."

Odile looked at her for a long moment—looked the way she occasionally did, that look Sebastian had never fully decoded, the look of a woman weighing something on scales only she could see—and then she simply stepped aside and held the door.

"There's stew," she said. "And Renn's back tomorrow. He'll want his eel pies, so eat some before he gets them all."

Sebastian went in. Behind her, out over the open water, far past the harbor wards where no fisherman sailed at night, the sky flickered once—softly, silently, a heat-lightning pulse with no storm attached, no cloud, no sound, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep and dreaming of her name.

She did not see it. She was eating stew, and lying about how fine she was, and being, for a few hours more, only a woman.

But the lightning saw her. And it remembered the way home.
