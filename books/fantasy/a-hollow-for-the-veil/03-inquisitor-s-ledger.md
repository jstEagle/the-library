## 3. Inquisitor's Ledger

The ledgers of the Church of the Concorded Flame were kept in a room with no windows, on the theory that paper, like faith, should not be exposed to weather.

Inquisitor Dain Marrec had spent eleven years in that room, and in eleven years he had learned three things. First, that the ink used for official records was mixed with ash from the burned temples of the old gods, so that every line written in the Church's service was, in a small and deniable way, an act of erasure. Second, that the clerks who copied those lines never asked what was being erased, and that this was not ignorance but discipline, and that discipline of that kind was the most frightening thing in the building. Third, that the numbers always told the truth, which was why so few people bothered to read them.

He was reading them now, by lamplight, at an hour when the rest of the Bastion of Records had gone home to wives and wine and the comfortable fiction that the world was governed from the palace above and not from this basement beneath it.

The column of prayer-tithe receipts from Corvane had done something impossible.

He set his finger on the line and made himself look at it the way he would look at a wound. The tithe was the Church's oldest instrument: every consecrated prayer-stone in every chapel of the Concorded Flame drank a measure of the faithful's devotion and passed it up the chain, chapel to chapterhouse, chapterhouse to the Bastion, and from the Bastion to the Queen's garden, where it was, according to doctrine, "consecrated to the Flame's eternal keeping." Marrec had never seen the garden. He had seen the receipts. Devotion, quantified, was the true currency of the realm, and it flowed one direction only—upward, always upward, into the dark of the Bastion's vaults and out through the sealed aqueduct-tunnels toward the palace hill.

Corvane's tithe had been falling for nine years. That was normal. The free cities had never loved the Flame the way the heartlands did; Corvane especially, with its magic-free streets and its merchant councils and its stubborn, unconverted habit of praying to nothing at all. A slow decline was the expected shape of the line. Marrec had charted it himself, three years ago, in a report no one had read.

Two nights ago, the line had jumped.

Not risen. *Jumped*—a vertical stroke on the ledger, as if a clerk had dropped his pen and dragged it down the page in panic. The prayer-stones of Corvane's three licensed chapels had drunk, in the space of a single night, more devotion than they had in the previous four months. And then, the next night, more again. And last night, more again, each surge a little larger, each one arriving at the same hour—well past midnight, in the dead dark when honest people slept.

Marrec sat back and rubbed his eyes with a thumb and forefinger, pressing until he saw ghosts of light. He was forty-six years old and he had the face of a man who had spent his life being underestimated, which was a professional asset he maintained with care. He was not tall. He was not imposing. He wore the grey cassock of the inquisitorial order without ornament, and his boots were scuffed, and clerks who met him in corridors assumed he was an auditor of tallow-weights or some other dull and harmless thing.

That was fine. Dull and harmless men got to keep reading.

He pulled the raw stone-reports toward him and checked the surge against the chapel logs. The chapels themselves reported nothing unusual. No revival meetings, no festival, no visiting prelate whose charisma might have whipped the congregations into fervor. The devotion had arrived *unattached*—not gathered through the licensed rites at all, but simply *appearing* in the stones, the way water appears in a cellar when a river has changed course somewhere you cannot see.

Which meant it had not come through the Church.

Which meant it had come from outside the Church.

Which meant—

Marrec closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and sat for a long moment in the lamplight doing nothing at all, because the next thought was a dangerous thought and he wanted to be certain he was alone with it.

*The stones are drinking from a well the Church did not dig.*

There was a word for devotion that reached the stones without passing through the Flame's rites. The old word. The forbidden word. It had been struck from the liturgy forty years ago, in the Reformation of the Concord, when the Church had declared—by decree, with armies—that there were no other gods, that there had never been other gods, that the prayers of the faithful had always and only ever risen to the Flame, and that anyone who said otherwise would be educated at the stake.

*Prayer,* the old word was. Unmediated prayer. Prayer that went up like smoke from an open fire instead of down a sanctioned flue.

Marrec had been a boy of six when the Reformation reached his town. He remembered very little of it: a smell of smoke, his mother's hand tight on his shoulder, a priest's voice saying *there is only the Flame, little one, there has only ever been the Flame,* and the strange, hollow feeling of being told what he had always believed, as if belief were something that could be installed in a person like a roof tile. He had believed it. He had believed it so thoroughly that he had given his life to the institution doing the telling.

He was still not sure he had stopped.

He opened the ledger again and began, methodically, to do his job.

* * *

The hunt, when the Church opened one, began with a classification, and the classification was everything. A heretic could be reasoned with, or broken, or burned, and the paperwork for all three was well established. A relic could be seized and locked in a lead box. An anomaly, though—an anomaly was worse, because an anomaly had no doctrine attached, no procedure, no shelf in the archive where its file was meant to live. An anomaly frightened the people above Marrec, and frightened institutions did not think clearly, and unclear thinking in the Church of the Concorded Flame had a historical body count that Marrec could recite from memory.

He drafted the classification himself, because he knew that if he left it to the clerks, the word *heresy* would appear by morning and the Bastion's answer to everything would appear by evening.

He wrote: *Anomalous devotion-surge, Corvane chapterhouse district. Source unlocated. Nature unlocated. Recommend designation: ember.*

An ember. Not a fire—a fire could be fought, and naming it a fire would summon the Knight-Preceptors with their censers and their chains and their appetite for spectacle. An ember was small. An ember was *contained*, in theory. An ember could be found by someone patient, someone quiet, someone dull and harmless, and snuffed before anyone above the third floor needed to be troubled.

He wrote the recommendation for himself into the file without irony and without pride: *Assign: Inquisitor D. Marrec, sole investigator, discreet mandate.* It was not modesty. It was containment of a different kind. If he went alone, then whatever he found, he would find it before the machinery of the hunt woke up, and he would have—briefly, privately—the choice of what to do about it.

He was honest enough with himself, in the windowless room, to admit that he did not know what he would choose.

He sealed the file with the plain wax of internal correspondence, not the red of escalation, and carried it up three floors to the Office of Mandates, where a clerk with ink-stained cuffs stamped it without reading it, because Marrec had timed his visit for the clerk's supper hour, and because in eleven years he had learned that the Church's greatest weakness was not heresy but hunger.

By the time the bells rang compline, Inquisitor Dain Marrec had a mandate, a purse, a warrant written in language so vague it could mean anything, and a berth on the morning packet-boat to Corvane.

He spent his last night in the capital doing what he always did before a hunt: he read the old files.

* * *

There was a section of the Bastion archive that did not officially exist, which in the Church's grammar meant it existed very much indeed. It occupied a sub-basement below the sub-basement, behind a door whose lock had been made by a locksmith who had then, Marrec was fairly certain, been locked behind it. Marrec had the key because the previous occupant of his office had died without heirs and without tidiness, and Marrec had inherited the key along with the desk, the scuffed boots' predecessor, and a low chronic headache that he had come to think of as professional.

The files down here were the Reformation files. The god-war files. The things that had been burned everywhere except here, because the Church, whatever else it was, had never been able to bring itself to burn its own records.

He did not take anything down. He never took anything down. He read, and he put things back exactly as he had found them, and he carried the contents out in the only vessel the Church could not search: his own memory, which was excellent, and which he kept dull-looking on purpose.

He was looking for one thing: precedent. Somewhere in forty years of ash, there had to be a case of devotion arriving in the stones from outside the rites. The Reformation had not been fought against armies, whatever the murals said. It had been fought against *this*—against prayers that would not stay in their sanctioned channels, against wells the Church had not dug, against a world that kept, stubbornly, answering to more than one name.

He found what he was looking for in the third hour, in a folder with no title, only a date: the year the last storm-temple fell.

The document inside was not a report. It was a deposition, taken from a stone-warden of the old Veyran rite—a woman who had surrendered at the fall of the temple and been questioned, at length, before her execution. Most of the deposition was the usual thing: names, locations, refusals, the warden's voice growing thinner page by page. But near the end, the questioning inquisitor—some ancestor of Marrec's office, writing in a hand that had begun confident and ended hurried—had asked her a question that was not in the approved list.

*Where does the devotion go, when we burn your temples?*

And the warden had answered—Marrec could almost hear the tiredness in the transcription, the patience of a woman explaining rain to a fish:

*It doesn't go anywhere. It waits in the vessels. You can burn every temple on the coast, inquisitor. As long as one vessel breathes, the god has an address.*

The questioning inquisitor had underlined *vessels* twice, and written in the margin, in that hurried hand: *Find the vessels.*

Someone, forty years ago, had been given the same hunt Marrec had just assigned himself. And the file ended there—no follow-up, no closure, no list. Either they had never found the vessels, or they had found one, and what had happened next had been moved somewhere even deeper than this room.

Marrec sat in the dark of the sub-basement with the deposition open on his knee and felt the flicker arrive—the one he had been holding at arm's length since the ledger, the one he had hoped the work would drown.

He had spent his life serving an institution that told people what they had always believed. He was good at it. He was *respected*, in the way a well-made lock is respected. And now he had read a dead woman's sentence—*as long as one vessel breathes, the god has an address*—and instead of the righteous cold that was supposed to fill a servant of the Flame at such words, he had felt something else.

He had felt *curiosity*.

Curiosity was the first crack. He knew that. He had watched it open in others, in younger inquisitors who had asked one question too many at the right dinner party and vanished into a posting on the ice-coast. Curiosity, in the Church, was not a virtue. It was a symptom.

He put the deposition back in its folder, the folder back in its place, and climbed the stairs with the lamp held steady, thinking: *I am going to Corvane to find an ember. I will find it. And then I will decide, alone, before anyone can stop me, whether it is a thing to be snuffed or a thing that was never supposed to be put out.*

He did not pray. He had stopped, some years ago, without announcing it to himself. But on the packet-boat the next morning, standing at the rail with the grey capital shrinking behind him and the free cities' flat green coast rising ahead, he caught himself doing something with his hands—rubbing his thumb over his knuckles, over and over, a rhythm without words.

It took him most of the day to recognize the motion for what it was. His mother had prayed like that, in the last year of the Reformation, when the churches were closed and the stones were confiscated: hands moving, no words, devotion with nowhere sanctioned to go.

He stopped at once. Then, after a decent interval, watching the water, he started again.

* * *

Corvane did not look like a city that was lying. That was the first thing Marrec noticed, and he distrusted it immediately.

He had prepared himself for a city in the grip of something—a fever of whisper, a hush, the telltale flinch of a population that knew what moved among them. He had expected shuttered faces and chapels full to bursting with frightened new converts. Instead he found the night market in full riotous operation, eel-smoke and lantern-oil and a charm-seller's row where a broad-shouldered woman with a scarred jaw sold luck-knots to fishermen with the bored fluency of a person who had done it a thousand times and believed in none of it. The licensed chapels stood quiet and half-empty. The prayer-stones in their niches glowed with the fat, overfed light of stones that had been drinking hard.

Nobody looked afraid. Everybody looked *busy*. And that, Marrec knew from eleven years of ledgers, was the most sophisticated disguise there was. Fear could be found and levered open. Contentment had to be taken apart rivet by rivet.

He took a room above a chandlery, told the landlady he was an assessor of tallow-weights, and began the slow work.

He walked the market for three nights. He attended the licensed rites and watched the congregations, and found the devotion there thin and dutiful, a trickle, nothing that could account for the ledger's vertical stroke. He interviewed the chapel wardens, who were honest men and knew nothing. He checked the harbor wards, the tide-records, the storm-registry—because the old files had taught him that the Veyran rite had always been a coastal thing, a thing of salt and thunder, and if a vessel breathed in Corvane, the sea would know before the stones did.

The storm-registry gave him nothing. That was the strange part. Corvane's coast had been flat-calm, windless, unremarkable, for the exact nights of the surges. No storms. No lightning. The sea had not stirred.

And yet the stones had drunk like dying men.

On the fourth night, standing at the edge of the charm-seller's row with a cup of bad wine, watching the broad-shouldered woman make change for a fisherman and tuck a knot of red thread into a child's fist without charging for it, Marrec felt the flicker again—sharper now, less like doubt and more like a splinter working toward the surface of a finger.

The city was lying. He was sure of it. Not the people—the *city*, the stones and the salt air and the harbor itself, all of it lying together with the placid competence of a thing that had been lying for a long time and had gotten good at it. The surge had come from here. The ember was here. He could feel it the way he could feel weather in his knee: not as evidence, but as certainty.

And beneath the certainty, quiet as a second heartbeat, the question he had carried out of the sub-basement and had not managed to put down on the boat or the road or in the chandlery's narrow bed:

*If I find it—what is it that I'll be snuffing?*

Across the row, the charm-seller looked up from her table—just for a moment, just the way anyone looks up when a stranger's gaze gets too steady—and her eyes met his across the lantern-light and the eel-smoke.

She looked away first. She went back to her knots and her customers, bored, harmless, small.

Marrec finished his wine and did not write anything down, because he had learned long ago that the most important entries in an inquisitor's ledger were the ones he kept only in his head—and in his head, in a hand no clerk would ever copy, he wrote the first line of a file that had no official shelf:

*The ember sells luck-charms. The city lies for her. God help me, I think I understand why.*
