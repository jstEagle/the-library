## 4. The Gull and the Lantern

The raid came on a morning of gulls and low tide.

Sebastian had been at her stall since before the fishmongers finished arguing over the day's catch, arranging her charms in their careful rows—knots of red thread, river-stones drilled through with holes, little tin gulls that caught the light and threw it back in fragments. Harmless things. Things that worked because people believed they worked, which was the only honest magic left in Corvane. She had spent ten years learning to sell belief without touching the real thing beneath it, and she had gotten good enough at it that most mornings she could almost forget what slept under her skin.

Then the bells started.

Not the harbor bells that meant weather coming in off the Veiled Sea, not the temple bells that marked the hours for a church Sebastian had never once entered. These were the other bells—the ones hung from the customs house, the ones that only rang when the inquisitors came up from the docks with their ledgers and their long gray coats and their faces like men reading a list of names they intended to shorten.

She knew the sound the way sailors know the first cough of a storm.

Around her, the market did what markets do when authority arrives: it performed innocence. The pickpockets vanished into the crowd like smoke pulled up a chimney. The unlicensed fortune-teller flipped her tablecloth to its plain side and became, instantly, a woman selling dried lavender. The oyster-seller's boy stopped his singing and began sweeping with tremendous, conspicuous diligence. Everyone bent themselves into shapes that said *nothing here but honest trade*, and everyone wore the same tight smile, the smile of people who have all broken some small law or other and are praying the gods of bureaucracy are feeling merciful today.

Sebastian's hands went still over her charms.

They always searched her stall. Not because of anything she'd done—because of what she refused to be. Ten years ago, when she'd come down out of the hill country with nothing but a false name and a body full of suppression tattoos inked by a woman who hadn't survived the winter after, Corvane had been the one city in the free coast where magic was not merely forbidden but *uninteresting*. The Concord had stripped it out of law and then out of memory. Here, a charm-seller was a curiosity, a street-corner entertainer, no more dangerous than a puppeteer. It was the safest lie she had ever lived inside.

But safe lies require maintenance, and maintenance required that when the inquisitors came, they find exactly what they expected: a tired woman, a table of trinkets, receipts stamped by the harbormaster, and nothing else.

*Nothing else.* Her eyes flicked, despite herself, to the underside of the stall—the narrow gap between the planks where she kept her satchel. Inside the satchel: three charms she had made last winter during a bad night, charms with actual working in them, small ones, warming-spells against the damp, nothing that would raise an eyebrow anywhere magic was legal. But this was Corvane. In Corvane, a warming-charm was evidence. Evidence was a ledger entry. A ledger entry was a cell, and a cell was a question, and questions were how people like her ended up on the slow boats going east, the ones that never docked again.

She reached beneath the table, casual as a woman adjusting her hem, and closed her fingers around the satchel strap.

That was when she saw them come around the corner of the chandlery.

Four of them. Gray coats, gray hoods pushed back, and the lead one carrying the ledger itself—a thick black book bound in something that wasn't leather, clasped shut with a seal she'd only ever seen described in rumors: the inverted tower of the usurper's church. Behind them walked two city watchmen who looked profoundly unhappy about their assignment, the way local dogs look when strangers put leashes on them.

"Charm-sellers," the lead inquisitor announced, in the flat voice of a man reciting a grocery list. "Licenses and stock. All of it."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and afterward Sebastian would turn it over in her mind a hundred times trying to decide whether it had been kindness or calculation or simply the reflexes of people who had done this before.

Odile got there first.

The lantern-keeper came down from the seawall path with her cart—the rattletrap wooden cart she used to haul lamp-oil up to the beacon every dusk—and she parked it directly across the mouth of Sebastian's stall lane, wedged between two posts, at precisely the angle that made it impossible to bring the cart through without unloading it. Then she began, loudly, to complain about her axle.

"You there!" she called to the inquisitor, with the particular fearless irritation of a woman in her sixties who has buried a husband, outlived two kings, and stopped being impressed by uniforms somewhere around the second coronation. "You're blocking my route. That axle's held together with prayer and tar, and if I have to move this cart twice before noon it will come apart right here, and you can explain to the harbormaster why the beacon goes dark tonight."

The inquisitor looked at her the way officials look at old women who cause problems: with the weary recognition that problems caused by old women tend to multiply. "Move your cart, madam."

"It won't move," Odile said cheerfully. "That's rather the point of an axle breaking."

While this negotiation unfolded—slowly, loudly, drawing the inquisitors' attention like flies to honey—Renn appeared from the direction of the quay, moving with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once hurried in his life and considered hurrying a confession of guilt. He was carrying a crate of eels. This was important. Renn was always carrying something when trouble started, because Renn understood, in the bone-deep way of smugglers, that a man holding cargo is a man with a destination, and men with destinations don't get questioned.

He set the crate down beside the inquisitors. Close enough. Deliberately close enough.

"Mornin'," he said. "You'll want to mind those. They bite."

"We're conducting an inspection," the lead inquisitor said.

"Eels don't care much for inspections." Renn nudged the crate lid with his boot, and the lid shifted, and something wet and furious surged against the slats with a sound like a sackful of angry rope. One of the watchmen took a full step backward. "Nor do I, honestly, but we've all got our burdens."

It should not have worked. It was absurd. Four armed agents of the usurper's church, delayed by a broken axle and a crate of hostile eels, while behind them a charm-seller slid a satchel of contraband warmth into the bottom of a basket of dried kelp and passed it, with both hands, to a fisherman's wife who owed her a favor from three winters ago and asked no questions now, only nodded once and walked away into the crowd like a woman carrying exactly what she'd come to buy.

But it worked. It worked because Odile and Renn had done this dance before—not for her, she realized later, staring at the ceiling of her room above the lantern-house; she had simply never been able to see the pattern until she was looking for it. For others. For years. The cart that broke down in front of raids. The crates placed just so. The whole quiet choreography of a neighborhood that had decided, without ever discussing it aloud, that the inquisitors would be slowed wherever they went, that paperwork would be misfiled, that witnesses would remember wrong, that Corvane's famous indifference to magic would be enforced from below whether the Concord liked it or not.

By the time the inquisitors finally reached her stall, waving away the eels and stepping around the cart, Sebastian's table held nothing but licensed stock and stamped receipts. The lead inquisitor turned her charms over with gloved fingers, one by one, tin gulls and red thread and river-stones, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find, and wrote something in the black ledger anyway—she never learned what—and moved on down the row to the fortune-teller, who had by now become so thoroughly a lavender-seller that even Sebastian half-believed it.

The bells rang once more, farther away, and were done.

* * *

She didn't go back to selling. She closed the stall early, tied the canvas down with hands that wouldn't quite stop shaking, and walked the long way home along the seawall, where the wind came in off the water and scrubbed the smell of the market out of her hair and gave her face something to feel besides fear.

The lantern-house sat at the end of the wall like a stone finger pointing at the sky, older than the city around it, older maybe than the Concord itself. Nobody remembered who had built it. The church claimed it was consecrated ground; the harbormaster claimed it was municipal property; Odile claimed it was hers, and Odile had outlasted everyone who might have argued.

Sebastian climbed the spiral stair and found Odile in the lamp room, trimming wicks, her silver hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fishhook, oil to her elbows, humming something tuneless and old.

"I saw the whole thing," Sebastian said, from the doorway. "From the stall. The cart. The eels."

"What eels?" Odile said, not looking up.

"That's my point. There were no eels. You staged a cart breakdown and Renn produced a crate of eels he definitely did not catch this morning, and I want to know—" She stopped. Her voice did something she hadn't planned. "Why."

Odile set down her trimming knife. She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, thoroughly, the way she did everything, and then she looked at Sebastian with those pale harbor-gray eyes that had watched ten thousand ships come home and a few hundred not come home at all.

"Sit down, child."

"I don't want to sit down. I want an answer."

"You'll get one sitting. Standing answers go to the head and make people stupid." She nodded at the bench beneath the great lens housing, and Sebastian, because it was Odile, sat.

For a moment the only sounds were the sea below and the tick of cooling brass. Then Odile said, "Ten years ago, a woman came up my stairs at night, soaked to the skin, carrying a girl who couldn't have been more than twenty and looked younger and was burning up with fever. Scars all over her arms. Ink scars. I'd seen that kind of work once before, decades back, on a storm-priestess the Concord dragged through the harbor in chains." She paused. "I knew what the tattoos were. Suppression work. You don't put that much ink in a person's skin unless there's something underneath worth burying."

Sebastian's breath went thin. "You never said."

"No. I didn't."

"Ten years, Odile. Ten years I've lived in your house. You fed me. You taught me the tides. You let me think I'd fooled you."

"You didn't fool me," Odile agreed mildly. "You fooled everyone else, which is harder, and you did it well, and I was proud of you for it. And I never said anything because saying something would have been the most dangerous thing I could possibly have done." She picked up her knife again, turned it over, set it down again. "A secret spoken is a secret owned. As long as I never told you I knew, you never had to decide what to do about me knowing. You just got to be a girl selling charms. I bought you that. Every year, I bought you another year of it."

"Why?" The word came out cracked. "You didn't know me. I was nobody. A stranger with fever and scars. You owed me nothing."

Odile looked at her for a long time, and something in her face softened into an expression Sebastian had never seen there—not pity, not fondness, something older and wearier and more stubborn than either.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "the old king's men burned a witch in the market square. Right where your stall is. I stood in the crowd and I watched, and I did nothing, because doing nothing was safe, and I was young, and young people are very good at confusing safety with virtue." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had gone still in a way that was worse than shaking. "She screamed for about four minutes. I've had forty years to count. So no, child, I didn't owe you anything. I owed *her*. And everyone like her since. You were just the first one who landed on my stairs instead of in a fire."

The lamp room seemed very bright suddenly, and very high above the water, and Sebastian found she couldn't speak. Ten years of gratitude reorganized itself in her chest into something heavier and less comfortable: guilt. Because Odile had carried this alone for a decade—had known, every single day, that the woman eating at her table was a lit fuse walking around in human shape, and had said nothing, asked nothing, demanded nothing. Had offered shelter without conditions Sebastian hadn't earned, hadn't even known she needed to earn.

"The raid today," Sebastian managed. "The cart. Was that for me, or—"

"For whoever needed it that morning. Today it was you. Last month it was the herb-woman off Tanner's Lane. Before that, a boy who could quiet animals, poor lamb, didn't even know it was unusual." Odile shrugged. "Corvane pretends it has no magic. Half the city knows better. We just keep the pretending alive so the church doesn't have to notice the truth. It's a communal fiction. Very civilized. Very fragile."

"Fragile how?"

"Fragile the way all fictions are fragile." Odile finally looked back up at her, and her eyes narrowed slightly—not in suspicion, Sebastian realized, but in the sharp protective way of a woman reading weather. "Fragile the way a lantern flame is fragile. One hard wind." She tilted her head. "You've been strange lately, child. Since the night market. Since that mother and her boy. You burn your charms?"

Sebastian's stomach dropped. "How—"

"Oil smell. Three nights running, from your window. Charm-wax burns sweet; whatever you burned was bitter." She said it simply, without accusation, and somehow that was worse. "Something's changed in you. I don't know what. I'm not asking. But the inquisitors were at your stall today, and they don't come to stalls that interest them nothing. Somebody filed something. Somebody noticed something."

"They search every charm-seller."

"They search every charm-seller *once a season*. This is the third time this month." Odile folded her arms. "So here is what an old woman who keeps a light burning knows about storms, child: you feel them coming in your knees long before the clouds show. And my knees have been aching for a week."

Below them, far out past the harbor mouth, a single gull turned against the gray water, white as a scrap of paper, and Sebastian thought of the tin gulls on her stall, the harmless ones, the ones that were only light and tin and nothing else, and wondered how much longer any of it—stall, name, city, self—could hold.

"Whatever's coming," Odile said quietly, "you won't face it alone in this house. That's not a promise I'm making lightly, and it's not a promise I'm asking you to explain yourself to keep. When you're ready to tell me, you'll tell me. Until then, the door stays barred at night, and Renn sleeps in the boathouse with his knives, and I trim these wicks same as I have for forty years." She picked up her knife and went back to the wicks, and the conversation was over, because Odile's conversations were always over exactly when she decided they were over.

Sebastian stood on the gallery outside the lamp room a long while after, watching the water darken toward evening, watching Odile's light kindle in its great lens and sweep out across the Veiled Sea like a slow arm reaching for ships she'd never meet.

Warmth. That was what she felt, mostly. Warmth, and under it guilt like a stone swallowed whole—guilt that these people had spent ten years building a wall around her life without her knowledge, spending their own safety as mortar, asking nothing, and that she had repaid them with silence stacked on silence. She had thought her secret was hers alone to carry. She understood now that it never had been. Odile had been carrying it too, all this time, in the place where old women carry the screams they failed to answer.

And under the warmth and the guilt, faint as a smell of bitter smoke from a window three nights cold, something else stirred. Odile's aching knees. The inquisitors' third visit. The ledger entry she would never read.

The wind was changing. Somewhere out past the harbor wards, past the gray line where the sea met a sky that had been empty of gods for ten years, something had begun, very quietly, to take an interest in Corvane.

That night, Renn knocked on her door. He had a letter in his hand, salt-stained, wrapped in oilcloth, and a look on his face she had never seen there before—the look of a man holding something he wasn't sure he wanted to have found.

"You need to see this," he said. "It came upriver with a drowning man."
