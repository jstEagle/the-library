## 5. What the Tide Brought In

The river at night was a different animal than the river by day. By day it was commerce—barges and fish-traps, the smell of tar and gutted catch, the whole gray machinery of Corvane's survival grinding along its banks. By night it was older. By night the water moved with a patience that made Renn Voss feel like a boy again, poling his uncle's skiff through fog so thick he'd had to navigate by the sound of church bells he wasn't supposed to be able to hear.

He was thinking about that boy as he poled the smuggler's flat toward the old tannery dock, because thinking about the man slumped in the bow was unbearable.

The stranger hadn't spoken since Renn hauled him out of the reeds below Grayfall Weir. He'd groaned once when they pulled him over the gunwale—a wet, tearing sound, like cloth giving way—and then gone quiet in a way that quiet men went quiet, which was to say deliberately, holding himself together by will alone while blood seeped through the sacking Renn had thrown over him.

Three knife wounds, by the look of it. Maybe four. Someone had worked on him with care and then left him in the river to finish the job, which told Renn everything he needed to know about who they were. Amateurs didn't bother finishing.

"Almost there," Renn said, though the man showed no sign of hearing. The fog took the words anyway, folded them into the mist off the water. "Easy now. Easy."

The tannery had been dead for twenty years—closed the season after the Concord, when the guilds purged anything that smelled of the old ways, and tanning had smelled of nothing but itself. Its rotting pilings still stood, though, and its loading dock still held, more or less, and more or less was the standard Renn had built a life on. He nosed the flat under the sagging roof of the boathouse and tied off, and for a moment he just stood there in the dark, breathing, listening to the water slap the hull.

Ten years of clean living. Well. Clean-ish. Ten years since the last time he'd run something upriver that could get a man hanged, ten years of honest smuggling, which was to say wine without tax stamps and silk without provenance—things that hurt no one, things that kept Odile's lantern-house warm and Sebastian fed and the whole crooked little family of them afloat. Ten years of telling himself the dangerous part of his life was behind him, sunk with the *Kestrel's Grace* off the Teeth, along with two crewmen whose names he still said to himself on the bad nights, like penance, like prayer.

And then tonight, walking home from the salt-market with a crate of untaxed brandy on his shoulder, he'd heard the splashing.

Not drowning-man splashing. Drowning men didn't splash; they just stopped. This had been deliberate—a body dragging itself through the shallows on purpose, toward the one stretch of bank where a light would show if any light was going to show. Toward him. Which meant the man had known who walked this bank at midnight, or had been told, or had paid someone to tell him, and all three of those possibilities sat in Renn's stomach like swallowed hooks.

"Come on," he said, and got his shoulder under the stranger's arm.

* * *

The boathouse smelled of creosote and old rope and, faintly, of the lamp oil Renn kept there against cold nights. He lit the stub of a candle rather than the lamp—less light to leak through the boards—and eased the man down onto the pile of sailcloth that served as a cot.

Up close, the wounds were worse. Two across the ribs, shallow enough that they'd bled more than they cut. One lower, in the meat of the thigh, deep and ugly, the kind that festered if it didn't kill you. And the man's hands—Renn had noticed them on the water and tried not to think about them, and now, in the candlelight, he couldn't not think about them.

They were burned. Not scalded, not singed. *Burned*, in patterns—the backs of both hands marked with raised white scars in shapes too regular to be accident. Circles within circles. A design like a wheel, or an eye.

Renn knew that design. Every child in the free cities knew it, from the picture-books the Concord had left unburned precisely so children would know what had been defeated: the storm-wheel of Veyra, the drowned god, the god of the sea-wrack and the thunderhead, she whose temples had gone into the harbors when the veil came down.

Except the books said her priests had all died. That was the whole point of the books. *Here is what we burned, and here is why it deserved burning.*

The stranger's eyes opened.

They were gray, sea-colored, and they found Renn's face with a steadiness that had no business belonging to a man four wounds into a river. His lips moved. Renn leaned close.

"Not the church," the man whispered. His voice was gravel dragged through brine. "Swear it. Swear you're not theirs."

"I'm nobody's," Renn said. "I'm a smuggler. I move things people don't want counted."

Something like relief crossed the ruined face—or maybe it was just the pain finally getting permission to arrive. The man's head fell back against the sailcloth. "Then listen," he breathed. "Because they'll come asking. And what you tell them decides whether I'm the only one who drowns."

"I don't make deals with half-dead strangers in boathouses."

"No." The gray eyes closed again. "You make them with friends of the lantern-keeper. Odile of the harbor-light. She held my passage once, years ago, before your time. Ask her."

The candle guttered. Outside, the river kept up its soft, indifferent conversation with the pilings, and Renn stood very still, feeling the night rearrange itself around him the way a deck rearranges itself in a swell—nothing moving, exactly, and everything changed.

Odile. Of course it came back to Odile. Everything in his life, sooner or later, came back to that woman and the lighthouse she tended like a hearth.

"Who did this to you?" he asked.

"The church's questioners. Inquisitorial retainers, out of Vhalen." The man said it the way sailors named a reef—not angry, just precise. "I was carrying letters. They wanted them. I put them in the river instead."

"And they let you live?"

"They think I drowned." A ghost of a smile. "I've been drowning for eleven years. I'm very good at it."

Renn crouched, tore a strip from the sacking, and started binding the thigh wound without quite deciding to. His hands knew this work from another life, another deck, another set of bleeding men. "Letters," he said. "For whom?"

"For a person." The stranger's breath caught as the bandage tightened. "A person in this city. And before you ask—I don't know their name. I know their title. The letters are addressed the way such letters must be addressed, by office, not by name, because names can be tortured out of a man and offices cannot."

"What office?"

The man looked at him for a long moment. Water dripped somewhere in the dark. Somewhere farther off, a night-bird cried over the reeds, thin and lonely.

"You're going to want to put me back in the river," the stranger said quietly, "when I tell you."

"Try me."

* * *

It took him another hour to say it, and most of that hour was Renn working—boiling water over the candle-flame in a tin cup, cleaning the rib-wounds, feeding the man sips of brandy from the untaxed crate, ordinary tasks performed with extraordinary care because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

The stranger gave his name finally, or a name: Brother Aldous, late of the Temple of the Deep Font, which had stood where the customs house stood now, which had gone into the harbor with forty of its clergy on the Night of Chains, which was a story every Corvane child learned and every Corvane adult repeated without thinking, the way you repeated that the moon rose, that taxes came due, that the gods were gone.

"We didn't all die," Aldous said. "Some of us swam. Some of us were already away. The Concord burned the temples above the waterline. It never thought to look below it." He flexed his scarred hands, wincing. "There have been faithful in the drowned places ever since. Keeping the rites. Keeping the *names*. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the veil to wear thin. For the prayers to start being answered again." His eyes fixed on Renn, and there was something in them that Renn recognized from the worst nights of his life, from the *Kestrel's Grace*, from men who had seen the wave coming and understood there was nowhere to run to. "They are being answered, friend. Have been, these past weeks. Something old has turned its face back toward the world. And the church felt it, and the church is afraid, and when the church is afraid it sends out men with knives and ledgers."

"Inquisitors."

"Inquisitors." Aldous nodded slowly. "Hunting what their own doctrine calls impossible. An ember. A source. Because the prayers aren't scattering—they're *converging*. On one place. On one—" He stopped. Breathed. Started again. "We spent years finding out where. Years. And when we knew, we wrote. Because whatever comes next, she deserves to hear it from us first, and not from them."

"She." Renn's mouth had gone dry. The candle flame bent in a draft from the river, and the shadows swung. "Who is *she*?"

Aldous reached inside his ruined coat—slowly, showing his empty hands the whole way, a man who knew how movements read to jumpy ones—and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with wax stamped by a seal Renn didn't recognize and yet somehow did, some deep animal part of him flinching from it before his mind caught up. Storm-wheel. Circle within circles.

"There are three letters," Aldous said. "One for the priesthood's own, wherever they hide. One for the council of the free cities, which will do nothing with it, because councils are how cities lie to themselves. And one—" he held it out, and Renn found he could not take it, could not make his hand move, "—one for the vessel herself. Addressed by office, as I said."

The oilcloth was stained with river water and darker stains besides. The writing on it had been done in a careful hand, ink that had half-run and been traced over again:

*To the Storm-Caller of Corvane.*

The words seemed to sit on the air between them. Renn heard the river. He heard his own heart. He heard, absurdly, the memory of Sebastian's laugh from three nights ago, when Odile had burned the stew and Renn had eaten it anyway and pronounced it an improvement.

Sebastian, who sold luck-charms at the night market and laughed at her own jokes and flinched, always flinched, whenever lightning walked the far horizon—flinched not like fear, he'd thought, not until this moment, not until now, holding a dead god's letter with a dead temple's seal.

Flinched like recognition.

"No," Renn said aloud. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened, far away. "No. You've got the wrong city. There's no storm-caller here. There's a charm-seller who waters Odile's herbs and cheats at cards and hasn't got a drop of—"

He stopped. Because he was lying, and he suddenly understood how long he'd been lying, and to whom. To himself, mostly. Ten years of small mercies and harmless charms and a woman who never once, not ever, in all the years, had asked him to carry so much as a whisper of real magic in his boat—and he'd told himself it was principle, it was caution, it was the discipline of a reformed man. When all along, some part of him had known. Some part of him had watched her stand at the seawall in storms and had seen the way the wind leaned toward her like a dog hearing its name.

"Friend," Aldous said gently, "you knew. Don't shame yourself. The ones who love them always know first, and admit it last."

Renn looked down at the letter. The river-water stain had spread across the word *Storm-Caller* like a hand trying to cover it, and failing.

If he handed it over—if he carried this up the hill to the lantern-house and put it in her hands—then it became real. The church was hunting an ember; Aldous had said so; men with knives and ledgers were already in the city or coming, and the letter in his hands was a lit torch in a drought. Handing it to Sebastian meant painting her. Meant painting Odile. Meant painting the whole crooked little family, the lantern-house, the market stall, all of it, with a mark no amount of honest smuggling would ever wash off.

And if he didn't—if he fed this letter to the stove, and Brother Aldous to the river, and went home and never spoke of it—

Then she'd face what was coming blind. Because it *was* coming; he believed that the way he believed in weather. The church had felt what she'd done—healed a child, Odile had told him weeks ago in a voice carefully stripped of meaning, and he'd filed it away the way he filed away everything dangerous, in the drawer that didn't open—and the church did not stop feeling things. It followed them. It followed them with knives and ledgers and patience.

The choice wasn't whether the storm arrived. The choice was whether she saw it coming.

"Take me to her," Aldous said. "Or take the letter. Either way, friend—but decide soon. The men who cut me weren't sent to fail."

Renn stood there a long time, the letter in his hands, the candle burning low, the river saying what rivers say, which is nothing, which is everything.

Then he wrapped the oilcloth tight, tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, where the weight of it sat like a stone, and reached for the pole.

"One condition," he said. "You tell her nothing about the rite. Nothing about vessels, nothing about what she's *for*. She hears that from the priesthood itself or not at all. She gets to be a charm-seller for one more night."

Aldous studied him with those sea-gray eyes, and something moved in them—pity, maybe, or its cousin.

"You're buying her a night," he said, "with a war."

"I know," Renn said, and pushed off from the dock, and poled for the lantern-house through the fog, past the drowned foundations of the Temple of the Deep Font, where the black water lay still as glass over everything the city had sunk and called it peace.
