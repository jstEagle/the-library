## 9. The Ember Hunt

The fog came off the harbor at dusk the way it always did in Corvane—thick as spilled milk, tasting of brine and old rope—and Sebastian had learned to love it the way a fugitive loves anything that hides her: completely, and with a constant awareness that it could lift.

She had not meant to be out tonight. Every instinct she owned had been screaming *stay indoors* for three days now, since Renn had pressed the drowned priest's letter into her hands and her whole careful architecture of smallness had come down around her ears like wet paper. Stay inside. Lock the door. Let Odile's lantern burn in the window and pretend it was only a lantern.

But Odile was low on lamp-oil, and the oil-seller who didn't ask questions worked the far end of the fish-rows, and fear was a luxury measured in oil. So Sebastian wrapped her scarf to her chin, pulled her hood low over the suppression tattoos that crawled up her left wrist like black frost, and went out into the white.

The market was emptying. Vendors folded their stalls with the particular hurry of people who wanted to be behind shutters before the fog got truly stupid. A boy hauled a barrow of eels past her, wheels squeaking. Somewhere a gull complained about the century. Corvane's magic-free streets were never entirely silent, but they were quiet in a way that felt rehearsed—the city performing its own innocence, night after night, for an audience of inquisitors who were no longer fooled.

Sebastian bought the oil. She counted out coppers with hands that did not shake, because she had spent ten years teaching them not to, and she turned for home.

That was when she saw him.

He stood at the mouth of Tanner's Lane where it opened onto the fish-rows, half-dissolved in fog, and he was not pretending to browse. Inquisitor Dain Marrec wore no vestments—she'd seen enough of his kind in the early years to know the grey coat and the iron pin were deliberately absent—but he wore the posture, the stillness, the way his eyes moved across a crowd like a man reading a ledger written in faces. He had let every other body pass him by. He was waiting for hers.

Her heart did not pound. That was the strange thing, the thing she would remember later. Ten years of hiding had trained her body so thoroughly that terror arrived as stillness, as a cold spreading outward from the spine, as the sudden hyperclarity of a world gone sharp at the edges. She could feel the oil bottle against her ribs. She could feel the fog beading on her lashes. She could feel, faintly, horribly, the thing beneath her skin that she had spent a decade drowning—the storm-god's ember, banked under ink and will, stirring the way a fire stirs when a door opens somewhere in the house.

*Run*, said the part of her that had survived the burning of Veyra's last temple.

*Don't*, said the older part, the one that knew running was a confession, and that a man like Marrec had chosen this lane, this hour, this fog, and that if he had wanted soldiers he would have brought them.

She walked toward him.

It was the bravest or the stupidest thing she had done since the night she'd walked out of the ashes of her old life, and she could not have told you which, even under torture. She simply kept her pace even, kept her eyes down as any charm-seller would before a stranger, and passed within six feet of him—

"Sebastian Villalobos."

Not her name. Not really. She hadn't been called that aloud since Corvane's gates had swallowed her, and hearing it now was like hearing a bell rung underwater—muffled, wrong, and impossible to unhear. She stopped. The fog closed around them both like a held breath.

"That's not my name," she said quietly.

"No," Marrec agreed. "I don't imagine it is." He stepped closer, and she made herself look at him properly for the first time. He was younger than his reputation—forty perhaps, grey coming in early at the temples, a face built for endurance rather than command. There were lines around his eyes that looked less like cruelty than like insomnia. His hands hung loose at his sides, empty. No writ. No irons. No escort.

"You're alone," she said.

"I'm alone."

"That's either very confident or very foolish."

"It's usually both." He glanced up the lane, down it, an old habit of a man checking whether the walls had ears. "Walk with me. The fish-rows are foul but the fog is honest company, and I'd rather not conduct this conversation where a lamplighter can count our shadows."

Every sane voice in her screamed refusal. But refusal was a door slamming, and slammed doors invited boots. And beneath the fear, treacherous and warm, curiosity had already lit its little lamp: *he knows my name. He hasn't called the hunt. Why?*

She walked.

They went along the rows, past shuttered stalls and coiled rope and the great dark shapes of hulls drawn up for winter repair, their tarred flanks weeping in the damp. Their footsteps sounded different from each other—his measured, hers lighter—and the fog took both and gave back nothing.

"Do you know what the church calls you?" Marrec asked, after a while. "In the reports I'm supposed to be writing?"

"The ember," she said. "I've heard the word."

"The ember," he repeated, tasting it. "As if you were a spark that wandered out of a hearth. As if you were small." He shook his head slowly. "I've read the surge-ledgers from three nights ago. Whatever answered that child's prayer in the night market, madam, it was not an ember. It registered on instruments in the Citadel that haven't moved in a generation. One of my colleagues fainted. Another resigned by morning." He looked at her sideways through the fog. "So before we go one step further, I'd like to stop pretending we're discussing a luck-charm seller."

"And what are we discussing?"

"A woman who healed a dying child in a city where healing is a hanging offense," he said, "and then, instead of running—which any sensible fugitive would have done—stayed. Kept her stall. Sold her charms. Walked home through the same streets." He paused. "Either you're the greatest fool in the free cities, or you have nowhere else to go. I've spent two weeks deciding which, and I find I care about the answer more than I expected to."

"Care," she echoed. The word came out with more edge than she intended. "Is that what the church calls it now? When the hunter develops a fondness for the quarry?"

Marrec stopped walking.

For a long moment he said nothing, and the fog pressed in, and somewhere out on the water a buoy-bell tolled once, twice, patient as a heartbeat. When he spoke again his voice had dropped its institutional polish, and what lay underneath was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"My daughter died four years ago," he said. "Fever, in the spring. I sat by her bed for nine days in the Citadel infirmary while the church physicians bled her and prayed over her and bled her again, and I am a senior inquisitor, madam—I have access to the best their medicine offers, which is to say the best that a church which has outlawed everything else permits itself to offer." His jaw worked. "On the third night, an old woman came to the ward gate begging to see her. A hedge-healer. They turned her away. I signed the order myself, because refusing would have cost me my post, and my post was how I fed my other children." He turned to face Sebastian fully, and his eyes in the fog were the color of wet slate. "She died on the ninth day. And every day since, I have wondered what one act of forbidden mercy might have bought, weighed against all my lawful obedience. So yes. Care. Call it what you like."

Sebastian stood very still.

She had prepared, in the long walk from the market, a dozen deflections—a false name, a false history, a bribe, a threat, a sprint into the maze of the fish-rows where she knew every dead-end better than any man born outside the tide-lines. She had not prepared for grief offered plainly, without leverage, like a coin laid on a table.

"That's a fine story," she said at last, because trust was a luxury she had buried with her old name. "And stories are how your order works. You find what a person loves, and you build the cage around it."

"Yes," Marrec said simply. "We do. It's the first thing they teach you." Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile. "Which is why you should notice that I'm telling you mine first."

* * *

They walked on, out past the last of the stalls, to where the fish-rows ended in a rickety railing above the mudflats. The tide was out; the flats breathed their cold mineral smell; the fog here was total, a white country with no borders. Marrec set his hands on the rail and looked out at nothing.

"Three days ago," he said, "I filed a report stating that the surge in the night market was a localized resonance event caused by faulty ward-stonework in the temple district. My superiors accepted it, because it was boring, and boring reports get archived." He glanced at her. "You're welcome."

"Why?" The question tore out of her before she could dress it in caution. "Why falsify it? What do you want? Everyone wants something. The resistance wants a weapon. The priesthood wants a vessel. Your church wants—" She stopped herself, but the shape of the sentence hung there between them, and Marrec finished it without flinching.

"Our church wants you burned, or worse. There are men in the Citadel who would cut the source of that surge out of you while you lived, purely to study it." He said it flatly, without drama, the way a physician describes a tumor. "Yes. I know what they want. I've read the directives. I've carried some of them." He turned from the rail. "And three weeks ago I would have carried this one too, and slept soundly after, because that is what obedience does—it launders the hands. But then I interrogated a priest."

Sebastian's breath caught. "The drowned temple. The man in the regalia."

"You know about him." Marrec studied her face, and whatever he found there seemed to confirm something. "Then you know more than my superiors believe exists. Good. That saves time." He reached into his coat—slowly, telegraphing every inch, and she watched his hands the way a hawk watches a field—and drew out not a writ but a slim packet wrapped in oilcloth. He set it on the railing between them.

"What is it?"

"Copies. Testimony, ledger fragments, a fragment of the Concord itself—or what passes for it in the Citadel vaults." He tapped the packet once. "Do you know what the Concord of Ashes is, madam? The covenant that ended the god-war? The foundation your usurper queen's entire church stands upon?"

"Everyone knows the Concord. The gods withdrew. The veil was raised. Magic was surrendered so the burning would stop."

"That is what is preached." Marrec's voice went quiet and hard. "Here is what the interrogation taught me. The Concord was not a treaty the gods agreed to. It was a theft. Isolde's grandmother did not negotiate the gods behind the veil—she *bound* them there, using a covenant stolen from their own priesthood, and she bound it to her bloodline, and her church has spent ninety years feeding on the seam. Every prayer whispered in the free cities goes to the throne. Every plea, every grief, every desperate bargain struck with a heaven that no longer answers—they all flow to Isolde's garden, and she drinks them, and calls the resulting silence 'peace.'" He exhaled. "The priest wept while he told me. Not for himself. For the prayers. Thousands of years of human longing, piped into one woman's root-cellar like water into a cistern."

The fog seemed colder suddenly. Sebastian thought of the mother in the night market, clutching her coin, begging for a true blessing. Thought of the prayer the child had whispered afterward, the one that had *answered back*. All those voices, she thought, screaming into a pillow. All that hope, harvested.

"Why show me this?" she asked. "Why not take it to the council of the free cities yourself? You're a senior inquisitor. Your testimony would—"

"Would be weighed against my order's," Marrec said. "One voice against a cathedral. And the moment I testified, every asset the church has would turn on me and everyone I've spoken to, and the evidence would be buried under my corpse." He shook his head. "No. This needs something the council cannot dismiss. This needs the ember itself. The living proof that the old gods are not myths, not memories—*present*. Answering. Because the stolen covenant only holds while the gods sleep behind the veil, and you, madam, are the crack in the wall."

"So that's the bargain." She folded her arms against the cold and against him. "Say it plainly. I've had enough of priests and smugglers and inquisitors speaking in circles. What do you want from me?"

"Testimony," Marrec said. "Yours. Under seal, before the council, when the time comes—that the surge was real, that the gods stir, that the throne's foundation is theft. In exchange: passage out of Corvane. Clean papers, a ship that doesn't ask questions, enough silver to vanish so thoroughly that even I couldn't find you." He paused, and something almost gentle entered his voice. "You could be nobody again. Truly nobody, in a place where nobody has ever heard the word Villalobos. Isn't that what you've been buying with ten years of smallness? I'm offering to let you stop paying."

For one vertiginous moment, she wanted it.

Gods, how she wanted it. A coast far south of here, a stall in some fogless sunlit market, charms that were only charms, a room with her own lock on the door, Odile's letters arriving slow and safe, Renn's jokes secondhand. An ordinary life, purchased honestly at last, with no ember ticking under her skin like a debt collector's knock.

But wanting, she had learned, was the hook the world used. And underneath the wanting, other things stirred: the memory of the child's fever breaking under her palm; the voice that had spoken through her scars, vast and patient and *hers*, in the way a birthright is hers whether she claims it or not; the letter in Renn's cellar with its summons addressed to a title she had killed a decade ago and could not, apparently, keep dead. *Storm-Caller of Corvane.* The city had named her already. The city had decided.

"If I testify," she said slowly, "your church learns I exist beyond doubt. Every Hollow Knight in the realm comes here. Not for me—for the example. Do you understand what you're asking me to light the beacon for?"

"I understand exactly what I'm asking." Marrec met her eyes. "I'm asking because the alternative is worse. Isolde's armies march within the season—my colleagues' own dispatches say the free cities are next, that the throne means to harvest *these* prayers directly, wards and all. The council dithers because it doesn't believe the danger is real. You are the proof, madam. Uncomfortable, dangerous, possibly fatal proof. But the only kind that moves councils." He spread his empty hands. "I won't lie to you: I may be offering you a longer road to the same pyre. But it's a road where the burning might mean something."

The buoy-bell tolled again out on the flats, and Sebastian realized her hands had gone cold on the railing, and that beneath the suppression tattoos the ember was awake and listening, the way it always listened now, and that some deep stratum of her had already answered before her mouth opened.

"You're lying to me about something," she said.

"Yes," Marrec agreed, without hesitation, and the honesty of it startled a short laugh out of her, the first in days. "I'm lying about how much I trust you. I've known you two weeks and you are, by my order's own definition, an existential threat to everything I've served my whole life. Trust would be insanity." He picked up the oilcloth packet and held it out. "And you're lying about how much you trust me. You're wondering right now whether this packet is evidence or bait, whether the ship I promise is real, whether the moment you touch this I'll whistle and the fog will fill with knights."

"Am I wrong to wonder?"

"No. Keep wondering. Wondering keeps us both alive." He did not lower the packet. "But here is what isn't a lie, madam, and you can weigh it however you like: I falsified my report before I ever dreamed of bargaining with you. Before I knew your name. I did it because a child lived who should have died, and because I have buried one child already and found I could not dig the grave for another. Everything since has been built on top of that one unlawful mercy. If it was folly, it was at least *my* folly, chosen with open eyes." His arm stayed steady. "Take it or leave it. But decide tonight. The hunt doesn't wait for comfortable weather, and neither do armies."

The fog drifted between them. Sebastian looked at the packet—plain oilcloth, soldier's knots, no sigils—and thought of Odile's lantern burning in the window of a house that had sheltered her without conditions she hadn't earned. Thought of Renn bleeding somewhere in a cell for a route he'd invented to protect her. Thought of the thin places, the summons, the god coiled in her marrow like a second heartbeat.

Ten years ago she had run from a war and called it survival. She was done calling it that.

She took the packet.

Their fingers did not touch; Marrec released it the instant her grip closed, precise as a man passing a live coal. She tucked it inside her coat, against the oil bottle, against the heartbeat that wasn't entirely hers.

"The lantern-house on Candle Row," she said. "Two nights from now, after the third bell. Come alone, come unarmed, and come knowing that if this is bait, I will not be taken quietly—and what I am, inquisitor, does not get taken quietly at all."

"Noted," Marrec said dryly. "For what it's worth, I believe you."

"One more thing." She turned to go, then stopped, and the words came out before she could weigh them, soft and lethal in the fog. "The directive you mentioned. The one about cutting the source out alive. If your order ever issues it—if it ever lands on *your* desk with your signature line beneath it—burn it. Burn it and walk away, or I swear by the god you don't believe in anymore, Dain Marrec, there will be no report to file, no archive to bury it in, and no Citadel standing to file it in."

The fog held them both for a long moment. Marrec's expression did not change, but something behind his eyes did—the flicker of a man realizing the quarry has teeth, and finding, to his own visible surprise, that he respects the teeth.

"Understood," he said.

He turned north along the railing and was gone into the white in six steps, silent as the institution that made him. Sebastian stood alone above the breathing mudflats with the stolen truth pressed against her ribs, and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for ten years.

Then she ran for Candle Row, because the third bell was already ringing somewhere behind the fog, and because the packet against her heart was heavier than paper had any right to be—and because, two streets away and closing, she could hear the measured tread of boots that were not Marrec's, and a voice calling through the murk for all units to converge on the fish-rows.

Someone had followed him after all.

The hunt had begun—and she was carrying its evidence straight through its arms.
