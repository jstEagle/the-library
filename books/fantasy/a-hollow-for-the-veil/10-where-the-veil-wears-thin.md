## 10. Where the Veil Wears Thin

The map was drawn on skin.

Not tattooed—*written*, in ink so old it had gone the color of dried blood, on a rectangle of vellum that Brother Cassian swore had been cut from the shoulder of the last high priest of Veyra's drowned temple. He had pressed it into Sebastian's hands two nights ago with the gravity of a man handing over a relic and the desperation of a man handing over his last coin. *Follow the salt line past the third cairn,* he'd said. *Where the tide goes out farther than it should, you'll find it.*

She had not wanted to take it. She had taken it anyway, because the alternative was to keep pretending she was only a charm-seller with a talent for guessing which wrist a fever would settle in, and she had run out of strength for pretending somewhere around the time she burned her own charms in a brass pan and watched the smoke spell nothing at all.

Now she walked the salt marsh north of Corvane before dawn, when the fog lay thick as wool and the gulls hadn't yet found their voices. The path was not a path. It was a suggestion—the kind of suggestion the land makes when it wants you to believe you chose it yourself. Mud sucked at her boots. Reeds bowed under a wind that came from nowhere, that moved against the direction of every other wind she'd ever known, as if the air itself were being drawn inward, breathed toward something.

She felt it before she understood it: the pull. Like standing too close to an open window in winter, except the cold wasn't cold. It was *attention*. Something on the other side of the world's skin had noticed her walking and was watching her come.

Ten years of suppression tattoos crawled along her spine like a chain of small fires banked low. They had been quiet for a decade. Lately they itched. Lately they *answered*—a flicker beneath the ink whenever thunder rolled in off the sea, a warmth when the child she'd healed had prayed over his mended leg, a voice like a struck bell ringing through her scars at hours when no bell rang. Veyra, stirring. Veyra, patient. Veyra, who had waited inside her body like water waiting inside a sealed jar, and whose jar was beginning, seam by seam, to leak.

The third cairn rose out of the fog on her left—a heap of grey stones stacked by hands long dissolved, furred with lichen the color of old bronze. Past it, the ground changed. She couldn't have said how. The mud stopped gripping. The reeds grew sparser, then wrong—bent all in one direction, away from the water, as though fleeing something inland. And the tide—

The tide was out. Farther out than tides go. A mile of seabed exposed and gleaming under a sky still dark enough to hold its stars, pools of trapped seawater lying flat as mirrors, each one reflecting not the sky above it but a sky that wasn't there: unfamiliar constellations, a moon with a bite taken from it, clouds lit gold from below by a sun that had set hours ago.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the drained flats and felt her heart try to climb out through her throat.

*Here,* said the map, in its dead priest's handwriting. *Here the veil wears thin. Here the gods still listen. Kneel where the water would be, and speak.*

She did not kneel. Not yet. First she did what ten years of exile had taught her to do first: she checked the horizon for inquisitors' boats, scanned the fog for grey cassocks, listened for the particular silence that meant someone was holding their breath nearby. Nothing. Only the gulls, late to wake, wheeling overhead with a confusion she recognized—they kept landing on water that wasn't there, folding their wings onto air, crying out at the trick of it.

Only her. Only the pull. Only the thing underneath, listening.

She knelt.

The mud was warm. That was the first wrongness that made her breath catch—not cold, not neutral, but *warm*, blood-warm, as though the earth itself had a pulse and she had knelt directly upon it. She pressed both palms flat. Beneath the warmth, faint as a heartbeat heard through three walls, something rhythmical moved. Slow. Enormous. Patient beyond any patience a living thing could afford.

"Veyra," she said aloud, because the map told her to speak, and because saying the name felt less like prayer than like knocking on a door she'd nailed shut behind herself a decade ago.

The flats answered.

Not with thunder. Not with the storm-voice she remembered from before—the one that had spoken through her at nineteen, the one that had made the sky split open over the temple district and taught her what her gifts cost other people. This was quieter. This was worse. The pools began to ripple, each mirror-surface trembling with a vibration that had no wind to explain it, and from the ripples rose sound. Voices. Hundreds of them. Then thousands, layered, braided, arriving all at once the way light arrives—all together or not at all.

Prayers.

Sebastian went very still on her knees in the warm mud and listened to the prayers of Corvane.

They came in every register a human voice owns. An old man asking—not begging, just asking, politely, the way you ask a neighbor—for rain on his barley before the blight reached it. A girl of maybe twelve, her words half-swallowed by tears, praying that her father's cough would stay a cough. A fisherman's wife naming her sons one after another like beads on a string, asking the sea to give them back. A boy who had never learned to pray properly, who simply repeated *please, please, please* until the word wore smooth as river stone. A woman praying for her husband's safe return from a war the usurper called a policing action. Another woman praying that no one would ever find out what she'd done to survive it.

They rose out of the thin place in a flood, and Sebastian understood, with a lurching, sickening clarity, that these prayers were not rising *up*. They were rising *out*—drawn sideways, siphoned, pulled through the thin place the way water is pulled through a crack in a dam. And they were not arriving anywhere holy.

They were arriving somewhere hungry.

Because threaded through the voices—under them, around them, woven into the fabric of the sound like rot woven into cloth—was another presence. She felt it before she could name it: a vast, smooth attention, cold and deliberate, moving through the prayer-flood the way a trawler moves through a school of fish. Gathering. Sorting. *Taking.* It didn't listen the way gods listen. Gods, even cruel ones, even Veyra in her most terrible aspect, listened because hearing is what divinity *is*. This thing consumed. It swallowed prayers whole and converted them into something else—she could feel the conversion happening, feel the raw desperate human longing being refined, compressed, transmuted into power that tasted of incense and iron and candlewax.

The church's power. The usurper's throne. Isolde's garden.

She had heard rumors, of course. Everyone in Corvane had heard rumors—that the Queen's Vigil burned hotter than any faith had a right to burn, that the cathedral's lights never dimmed, that Isolde's soldiers fought with a vigor no rationing and no pay could explain. Renn had joked once, drunk on cheap plum brandy, that the Queen must be eating prayers for breakfast. Odile had laughed. Sebastian had not laughed, because she had been raised in the old temples and knew what jokes about eaten prayers sounded like to people who remembered the god-war: not funny. Prophetic.

But knowing a rumor and kneeling in the warm mud of a drained sea while thousands of stolen voices poured through your hands—those were different countries entirely.

She tried to follow one prayer. Just one. The old man and his barley. She fixed her attention on his voice, tracked it into the current, watched it move—and saw, in the mirror-pools, images surface like fish rising to feed. The prayer passed through the thin place. It entered a channel she could not see but could feel, a vein running beneath the world's skin, and traveled—south, always south, toward the city, toward the cathedral hill, toward a garden she had never seen but now understood she had been dreaming fragments of for weeks without knowing why. Black roses. Soil that steamed. A woman's hands in white gloves turning the earth.

And there the prayer ended. Not answered. *Eaten.* Ground up into fuel for wards and blessings and the particular vigor of Isolde's Hollow Knights, and the old man's barley would blight, and he would die believing the gods had simply not been listening, because for ten years the gods had not been permitted to listen. The veil between worlds had been hammered shut by the Concord—not to protect mortals from divine war, as the church taught, but to give the church a monopoly on heaven. To make the usurper's altar the only door, and to stand at that door taking a toll paid in human hope.

Sebastian knelt there until her knees ached and the tide-turn came, and the sea came crawling back across the flats like a slow silver animal reclaiming its territory, and the voices thinned, and the hunger-thing receded southward with its belly full, and still she knelt.

The child's prayer had woken Veyra. She had thought that was the horror of her life: that a god lived in her, that her existence was a beacon, that everything she loved would burn for it. She had spent ten years believing her secret was the worst thing in the world. That if it got out, if the inquisitors found the ember, if the priesthood claimed their vessel, the result would be catastrophe—her catastrophe, her fault, her burden to prevent.

She had been thinking too small.

Her secret was a single ember. The church's secret was a furnace. Her sin—the storm she'd called, the temple district she'd leveled, the exile engineered around her—had killed hundreds, maybe thousands; she had never let herself count. Isolde's sin was measured in years and millions: a decade of stolen prayers, a nation of people praying into a mouth that chewed and never spoke, faith harvested like wheat and milled into tyranny. Every candle lit at the Queen's Vigil was a withdrawal from an account no worshipper had opened. Every miracle the church performed was bought with miracles it prevented.

The enemy's secret was worse than hers. The enemy's secret made hers look almost innocent—an accident beside an industry, a wildfire beside a plantation.

And something in Sebastian, some part of her that had spent ten years practicing smallness, practicing apology, practicing the art of being harmless—something in her stood up inside her chest and refused to kneel anymore.

* * *

The walk back took until full morning, and she made it in a daze, the prayer-flood still ringing in her skull like a bell that wouldn't finish fading. By the time Corvane's walls rose out of the haze—smoke-stained granite, gull-white ledges, the cathedral spire thrusting up at the city's heart like a needle sewing the sky shut—her thoughts had begun to arrange themselves into something harder than grief.

She climbed the seawall steps slowly, leaning on the salt-crusted rail, and looked out at the city she had hidden in for a decade. From up here it looked exactly as it always had: rooftops tumbling down toward the harbor like brown and grey waves, laundry lines strung between chimneys, the market already roaring, children darting through alleys after gulls. Ordinary. Precious. Built entirely out of people who prayed without knowing their prayers were being farmed.

Odile would be lighting the lantern at dusk. Renn would be arguing with a harbormaster about mooring fees. The mother from the night market would be telling her son the story of the charm-seller who fixed his leg, embellishing it a little more each time, until eventually the story would become a legend and the legend might become a prayer, and the prayer would rise, and be eaten, and feed the machine that had made such stories necessary in the first place.

Sebastian pressed her palms against the seawall stone and felt the suppression tattoos flare—one pulse of heat, spine to wrists, like a warning shot.

"You knew," she said quietly, to the god inside her, to the scars that carried the god's voice, to whatever part of Veyra had been awake long enough to watch the flats with her. "You knew what the church was doing. You've known for ten years. You let me hide."

The answer came not in words but in weather: a pressure change, a shift in the wind off the harbor, a taste of ozone at the back of her throat like the moment before lightning. And beneath it, faint, patient, unmistakable—the bell-tone through her scars, the voice that had first spoken to her the night the child prayed.

*I waited,* it seemed to say. *You were not ready to hear it. Grief makes poor soil for resolve.*

She almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was closer to a snarl, aimed at herself, at the decade of careful smallness, at the beautiful lie she had built her exile on: that hiding was the same as keeping people safe. That if she stayed small enough, quiet enough, harmless enough, the world's hungers would pass over her street and find softer prey.

There was no softer prey. There was only prey that hadn't been counted yet.

She thought of Marrec, the inquisitor with the tired eyes and the ledger full of names, who had looked at her across a market stall and seen, somehow, not a suspect but a question. She thought of the summons she still carried folded against her ribs, the exiled priesthood's claim on her body, the rite they wanted to perform that would open her like a door. She thought of Isolde in her garden of black roses, gloved and patient, tending soil made rich by a decade of devoured hope.

Three powers wanted her. The church wanted her extinguished. The priesthood wanted her opened. The resistance—Renn's friends, Odile's debtors—wanted her weaponized. And she had spent so long refusing all three that she had forgotten there was a fourth option, the one nobody offered because nobody profits from it:

She could choose. Herself. On her own terms, for her own reasons, in service of the people whose prayers she had just heard pour through her hands like a river of small unbearable lights.

Not as Veyra's vessel. Not yet. Maybe never on their terms. But as Sebastian Villalobos, charm-seller, exile, the woman who healed a child in a night market because she could not bear not to—as the one person in Corvane who had knelt in the thin place and *heard*, and therefore could not unhear, and therefore could not, in good conscience, remain a spectator to her own life.

Resolve settled into her the way ballast settles into a hull. Not fire. Not fury. Something heavier and more durable: the decision to stop drifting.

She descended the seawall steps and turned toward the lantern-house, and halfway down the harbor road she became aware that she was being followed—or accompanied, rather, in the way weather accompanies a traveler. A wind off the sea that bent in her direction. Gulls gathering above her in a loose revolving crown. The faint, persistent itch of the tattoos, no longer painful, no longer suppressed, more like a hand resting lightly on the back of her neck.

Guidance, or surveillance. With Veyra, she was learning, the distinction was academic.

The lantern-house came into view at the end of the crooked lane, its windows catching the afternoon light, smoke rising thin and domestic from the chimney. Odile's silhouette moved behind the glass, steady as a metronome. Home. The word still fit, barely, the way a coat fits after you've grown but haven't admitted it.

And then Sebastian noticed the boat.

It sat at the end of the pier below the house, low in the water, riding at anchor with the particular stillness of a vessel that has nowhere urgent to be. Grey hull. No colors flying. And standing in the bow, motionless, watching the lantern-house with the patience of a man who has done this watching many times before—inquisitor's cassock, ledger under one arm, tired eyes fixed on the door she was walking toward.

Dain Marrec had found her address.

He had probably always known it. That was the thought that arrived, cold and clarifying, as she stood in the lane with the harbor wind at her back and the thin place's voices still echoing in her bones. Inquisitors did not find addresses by accident. He had let her live in plain sight for reasons of his own, and now, with the anomalous surge logged in his reports and the hunt for the ember officially open, those reasons were expiring.

She could run. Renn had boats. The priesthood had sanctuaries. The resistance had routes through the marshes that even the tide didn't know.

Or she could walk down the pier and meet him—meet the hunter, carrying the one piece of knowledge in all of Corvane that could break his order apart from the inside. She knew what his church ate for breakfast. She had knelt in the field where it grazed.

Sebastian stood at the top of the pier steps for the length of three slow breaths, and the wind off the water stirred her hair, and somewhere far beneath the harbor's grey chop, faint as a heartbeat through three walls, the thin place pulsed.

Then she started walking down.
