## 11. The Cellar Beneath the Lantern

Renn had always known this night would come. He'd simply imagined it arriving with more warning—drums, maybe, or a scream from the seaward watch, some courtesy of noise before the world broke in. Instead it came as a smell: lamp-oil and wet wool, drifting up through the floorboards of the lantern-house while he was mending a net that didn't need mending.

He set down the net. He listened.

Three sets of boots on the pier below, moving soft. A fourth holding at the head of the stairs—the lookout, patient as a heron. And beneath it all, fainter than the oil-smell, something Renn had learned to dread in ten years of running cargo past the wrong harbors: the particular silence of people who had already decided what they were going to do.

"Seb," he said quietly, to the empty room, because she wasn't there. She'd gone down to the pier an hour ago to meet Marrec, and Odile had gone with her, and the house had felt too big ever since.

The knock came anyway. Three raps, polite as a tax collector.

Renn crossed to the window. Below, in the fog-thinned lamplight, three figures stood at the foot of the lantern-house stairs wearing the grey oilskins of harbor laborers, which fooled no one, because real harbor laborers walked like their backs had given up years ago and these three walked like coiled wire. The one at the stairs wore his hood back. Young. Clean hands. A resistance signet hidden under his left glove—he thought Renn couldn't see the outline of it, but Renn had spent a decade learning to read what people carried against their skin.

Resistance. Not inquisitors. That was almost worse. Inquisitors you could hate cleanly.

He blew out the lamp, took the stairs down into the cellar two at a time, and put his shoulder to the old salt-crate stacked against the rear wall. It scraped aside. Behind it, the low door Odile had never explained and he had never asked about opened onto a crawl-tunnel that smelled of brine and older stone—a smuggler's vein running out beneath the pier pilings toward the marsh side of the harbor. Sebastian could be halfway to the thin place before these fools finished kicking in the front door.

Except she wasn't here to save. That was the joke of it. They'd come for a woman who was currently standing on a pier negotiating with an inquisitor, and all they'd find down here was him, a lantern-keeper's stores, and every secret route out of Corvane carved into Renn's own stubborn skull.

He could open the door and be gone in ninety seconds. He'd done it before, in ports where staying meant dying.

Instead he dragged the crate back over the door and sat down on it.

Because if they found the tunnel, they'd find the routes. And if they found the routes, they'd find the marsh sanctuaries, and the priesthood's landing coves, and the half-dozen families who fed runaways along the river—and somewhere in that ledger of places lived a woman who had just walked down a pier to sell her soul to a hunter so that none of them would burn.

Renn Voss had been many kinds of coward in his life. He'd abandoned cargo, abandoned debts, once abandoned a wife in Tessel Bay whose face he still couldn't fully remember. But he'd made himself one promise the night Odile pulled him half-drowned out of the shallows: not this house. Never this house.

The front door above splintered.

* * *

They came down the stairs fast, lamps high, and Renn gave them exactly what they expected: a big man rising slow from a chair by the cold hearth, hands loose and open, face arranged into the dull affront of someone woken at the wrong hour.

"Looking for the charm-seller," said the young one with the ring under his glove. His voice had the rehearsed flatness of a man reciting orders he hadn't written. "Villalobos. Where is she?"

"Sold out of luck-charms weeks ago." Renn shrugged. "Business dried up. People round here figured out they were just painted wood."

"Where is she."

"You're standing in her bedroom, friend, and I'm telling you she isn't in it."

One of them—a broad woman with a scar splitting her eyebrow—moved past him without hurry, checking the walls with her knuckles, listening for hollows. Good, Renn thought. Listen there. Just not behind the salt crate. He shifted his weight, casual, a man annoyed rather than afraid, keeping his body between her and the rear wall by the simple honest geometry of being large and in the way.

"She came here in the night, four days ago," the young one said. "Wet to the knees. Someone saw her."

"Half of Corvane comes here wet to the knees. Odile keeps a kettle on."

"Odile." Something flickered across the young man's face—not cruelty, worse, a kind of apologetic regret. "Where is she?"

"With a friend."

The scarred woman stopped at the salt crate.

Renn saw it happen the way you see a wave rise that you already know will break over you. Her knuckles rapped the wood twice. Hollow. Of course hollow—it was a door. Ten years of hiding a tunnel behind a crate and he'd never once thought about how a crate sounds when you knock on it, because nobody knocks on crates.

"Well," Renn said, and moved.

He got the young one first—drove him into the shelving hard enough that jars of Odile's pickled eels burst like small green bombs—and had the second man's wrist bent back before the man's knife cleared its sheath. For perhaps four seconds it went the way fights go in the stories: Renn's size, Renn's fury, the narrow room working against them.

Then the scarred woman hit him across the kidney with something weighted, and the room went white, and the fight became what fights actually are: short, graceless, and paid for afterward.

He went down among the broken eel jars. Boots arrived around him like a verdict. Through the ringing in his skull he heard the rear wall scrape—the crate dragged aside, the low door swinging open onto the dark of the tunnel—and heard the young one exhale, long and satisfied.

"A route. Under the lantern-house." A pause, and then, quieter, almost gentle: "You could have just told us. We're not the church, Voss. We know who you are. We know what she is. We're trying to *save* her."

Renn spat blood onto the flagstones and laughed, which cost him two cracked ribs' worth of pain and was worth every copper.

"That's the difference, then," he said. "You want to save her. We just want her to *be*."

* * *

They tied him to a chair in the cellar, which showed a certain lack of imagination, and then they waited, which showed a certain lack of urgency, and both of those things told Renn more than any question did. You don't wait out a prisoner unless you're waiting for something else—orders, or a superior, or the return of whoever had gone to collect the actual prize.

The fog thickened outside the high little window. Somewhere across the water, Corvane's evening bells rang the hour, and Renn counted them the way he counted everything now: how many hours had Sebastian been gone, and was she back yet, and if she came back to a gutted house would she do something none of them could take back?

The scarred woman worked him over twice. She was professional about it—no rage, no relish, just questions laid out like items on a list, each one following the last with the patience of tide. Where did the tunnel surface. Who else used it. What did the charm-seller carry when she traveled. What names did she use on the river.

Renn gave her weather. He gave her the price of eels. He gave her a long, loving description of the rot in the lantern-house's eastern joists and his opinions on who should be blamed for it. Between answers he bled from his split lip and thought about Odile's hands, steady on a lantern-wick, and about the promise he'd made in the shallows ten years ago, and about how promises were easy until they were made of your own teeth.

"You're loyal to someone who lied to you for a decade," the scarred woman said at last, crouching to his eye level. There was no contempt in it. That was the terrible part. She sounded tired, like a woman reading a bill she'd seen too many times. "She let you risk your neck for her without ever telling you what the neck was for. And you're sitting in your own blood for her anyway. Why?"

Renn looked at her for a long moment.

"Because she healed a stranger's kid and then burned everything she owned so no one could trace it," he said. "Because Odile knew what she was for ten years and kept her kettle warm anyway. Because somebody in this city has to be decent on purpose, and apparently it's us." He grinned through the blood. "Also because you're asking questions you already know the answers to, which means you don't have them, which means she got away clean. So really, thank *you*."

The blow that followed was harder than the others. He decided, as the dark came up to meet him, that it had probably been earned.

* * *

He woke to lamplight and a different silence.

The scarred woman and one of the others were gone. The young one remained, sitting across the cellar on an upturned barrel, hood down now, looking younger without it—younger and sicker, a boy who'd signed on to rescue a saint and found himself guarding a beaten smuggler instead. He'd lit one of Odile's spare lamps. He'd even, Renn noted blearily, propped him upright in the chair so he wouldn't choke on his own blood. Small mercies from the people breaking your house.

"They're bringing someone," the young one said. "From the cell. Someone who can tell when people lie. So I'd think carefully about whatever you say next."

"Kind of you to warn me."

"It's not a warning. It's—" He stopped. Rubbed his face. When he spoke again it was barely above the lamp-hiss. "I joined up because they told us the ember was the answer. One person, one miracle, and the usurper's whole rotten church comes down. Nobody said anything about burning lantern-houses. Nobody said anything about *him*." He gestured at Renn with something like apology. "Is it true? What they say she is? That she's the vessel?"

Renn considered him. Boyish jaw, clean hands, a signet he kept turned inward like a guilty conscience. Somewhere out in the fog, Sebastian was either dead, fled, or doing business with an inquisitor, and Renn would not know which until morning, and this child wanted theology.

"She's a woman who sells painted wood," Renn said, "because she thinks people deserve a little luck even when luck isn't real. Whatever else is true about her, start there or don't bother."

Boots on the stairs again. Two new figures descended into the lamplight—one heavyset, grey-robed, carrying a leather case that Renn disliked on sight; the other tall, dry-voiced, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had done this in many rooms.

The tall one studied Renn the way a buyer studies a horse with a limp: assessing what was still sound.

"The tunnel runs west under the pilings," the tall one said. It wasn't a question. "It surfaces in the marsh channels. There are six safehouses between here and the river mouth. We need the order they're used in, and we need it tonight, because in the morning the inquisition burns the marshes regardless, and everyone in those houses dies whether you talk or not." He crouched, forearms on knees, voice dropping into something almost reasonable. "Your silence doesn't protect them, Voss. It just decides whether we get there first."

It was a good speech. Renn had heard better ones from slavers.

But here was the thing about the routes—they lived in Renn's head not as a list but as a living thing, tides and timings and the moods of specific reed-beds, and a lie planted in that soil could grow deep before anyone found it. He let his eyes glaze. He let his breathing go shallow with pain. He let the silence stretch until the tall one leaned in another inch.

"There's a channel," Renn rasped. "Past the drowned mill. Marked by a leaning post with a red band."

"Yes."

"It floods at half-tide. You have to go at slack water, third bell after midnight, or the mud takes the boat." He coughed, wetly, selling it. "First house is the smokehouse on the fen edge. Second's a chapel ruin. Third—" he closed his eyes, summoning the memory of a place that did not exist, "—third is a stone bridge with a keystone carved like a fish. Knock twice, then once. Ask for Maren."

The grey-robed one opened his case and began, softly, to work. Renn felt the touch of it like cold fingers inside his skull, turning over what he'd said, weighing each word for the hollow ring of invention.

He held the image of the false bridge in his mind the way Odile held a flame in wind: cupped, sheltered, absolutely refusing to gutter. A bridge he'd invented an hour ago. A name he'd invented an hour ago. Knock twice, then once. Ask for Maren.

The grey-robed one frowned. Turned something over. Frowned again.

"He believes it," he said at last, sounding faintly puzzled, as though belief were a mechanism that had failed to catch. "Or he's built the lie so deep even he can't find the seam."

"I'm a smuggler," Renn said, and smiled with ruined lips. "Lying's the honest part of the trade."

* * *

They left him tied in the dark when they went—out into the fog, toward the drowned mill, toward a bridge with a fish carved on a keystone that existed nowhere but in the space between Renn's ribs where he kept the people he loved. He heard the boat push off. He heard the fog close over them.

Then he heard the other thing. The thing that turned his blood to harbor-water.

Across the black water, from the direction of the citadel, horns. Not the harbor bells' polite grammar—war-horns, three notes rising, the signal Renn had heard only once before in his life, the night a fleet burned at Tessel Bay. And beneath them, rolling in from the open sea, a pressure in the air like the intake of breath before a shout, like the sky gathering itself—

Like a storm deciding.

Renn strained against the ropes until his wrists ran slick, staring through the little window at a horizon where lightning stood up out of the sea in a column, silent and impossibly far and impossibly near, and he understood with perfect clarity that Sebastian had stopped hiding.

"Oh, Seb," he whispered to the empty cellar, to the broken jars, to the house that had been his one promise kept. "What did you do?"

The ropes bit. The storm stood on the horizon like a god getting to its feet. And somewhere between them—in a cell, in a citadel, in chains he could only imagine—was the only person alive who might call it back down.
