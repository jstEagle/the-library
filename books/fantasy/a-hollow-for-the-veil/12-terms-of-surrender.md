## 12. Terms of Surrender

The cells beneath the Bastion of the Ledger had no windows, which Dain Marrec had always considered a mercy. A man could lie to himself about a great many things in daylight, but in the dark, with only the sound of dripping stone and his own breathing for company, the lies wore thin.

He stood outside cell nine and listened to Renn Voss breathe.

It was not a peaceful sound. The smuggler had taken a beating during his capture—two cracked ribs by the sound of it, the wet hitch in each inhale—and he had given up nothing in three days of questioning except a route that led to a drowned quarry full of nothing but eels and old rope. Marrec had checked. Of course he had checked. He was thorough; it was the one virtue his order had never managed to beat out of him.

Thoroughness was also how he knew that everything else about this arrest was wrong.

* * *

Three days earlier, the directive had come down from the Bastion's spire in the usurper Queen Isolde's own cipher, sealed with the garden-sigil that made Marrec's skin crawl every time he saw it—a rose whose petals were rendered as open mouths. It was brief, as her directives always were. Brevity, in Isolde's church, was what cruelty looked like when it had been polished for a generation.

*The ember is to be taken whole. The vessel is not to be harmed. The man is irrelevant.*

Marrec had read it four times by candlelight, and each reading had taught him something new about the shape of the trap he was standing in.

*Whole.* Not alive—whole. As though Sebastian Villalobos were a set of dishes that must arrive unchipped at some table he was not invited to see. And he had seen enough in his years as an inquisitor to know what *whole* meant when the Hollow Knights were the ones doing the taking. He had seen the prisoners returned from the Knights' custody who were technically, anatomically whole, and who screamed at the sight of their own hands.

*The vessel is not to be harmed.* Which meant the church believed she was a vessel. Which meant the church believed in Veyra—not as superstition, not as the heresy they burned people for denying, but as a fact, a resident fact, something living inside a charm-seller from the night market like a swallow nesting in a wall.

And *the man is irrelevant.* That was the part that had kept him awake. Because Marrec had spent eleven days interrogating Renn Voss before the directive arrived, and he had learned two things: first, that the smuggler would die before giving up the lantern-keeper Odile or the woman he called Seb; and second, that Marrec did not want to be the instrument of either lesson.

He had begun falsifying reports a week ago. Small things. A date moved. A witness described as unreliable. The anomalous surge in Corvane's wards attributed to tidal interference rather than divine resonance. Each falsification had felt, at the time, like a pebble rolled into a stream—insignificant, deniable, easily explained if anyone ever asked.

But streams became rivers, and Marrec had just realized he was standing in the river up to his chest, and the water was still rising, and behind him the church was building a dam out of burned priests and stolen prayers, and ahead of him—

Ahead of him was a cell door and a broken smuggler and a choice he had already made without admitting it.

* * *

He went in alone. No clerks, no acolytes, no record-taker. In the Bastion, going in alone was itself a transgression; every interview was meant to be witnessed, every word weighed against the ledger. Marrec had stopped believing in ledgers somewhere around the year he'd found one that recorded prayers being *withdrawn* rather than heard.

Renn Voss sat on the cell's stone bench with his back against the wall, which Marrec recognized as the posture of a man conserving breath because breathing hurt. His face was a study in bruises going yellow at their edges. He looked up when the door opened, and his eyes—quick, irreverent, the eyes of a man who had talked his way past a hundred harbor guards—did something Marrec had not expected.

They went flat. Deliberately empty. The look of a man deciding in advance that whatever came next, he would give it nothing.

"You can save us both the performance," Marrec said quietly. "I'm not here to question you."

"Shame." Renn's voice was gravel and smoke. "I had some new material."

"I imagine you did." Marrec pulled the second stool from the corner—it was there for interrogators, bolted too low to be a weapon—and sat, which put them nearly eye to eye. Up close, the smuggler smelled of salt and blood and the particular staleness of a man who had been in one room too long. "I'm here to make you an offer. Before I do, I want you to understand exactly how much trouble I'm in for making it, so you can calibrate whether to believe me."

Renn said nothing, but the flatness flickered. Curiosity was a hard thing to kill in a man like that.

"The order received its directive three days ago," Marrec said. "From the Queen's own hand. You are to be declared irrelevant. Your fate is to be left to the discretion of the local office." He let that sit. "The local office is me. Do you understand what discretion means, in an institution like mine?"

"It means whatever keeps the paperwork clean."

"It means," Marrec said, "that no one above me cares whether you live or die, and someone below me would very much prefer you died, and the only thing standing between those two facts is my signature on a transfer order." He leaned forward. "Here is the offer. You tell me where Sebastian Villalobos is—or better, you carry a message to her, once you're free—and I release you. Not escape. Release. Papers, a route out of the city, a head start measured in hours, not minutes. In exchange, she comes to me. Voluntarily. To talk."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them, the Bastion's bells marked the hour—Vigil, the dead hour after midnight—and the sound came down through the stone like water through cloth.

"You're the one hunting her," Renn said finally.

"I was."

"And now you want a meeting."

"I want," Marrec said, and heard his own voice go rougher than he intended, "to show her something. And then I want her to decide what to do about it, which is more than the church will ever give her, and more than I can give her for much longer." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper—not the directive itself; he had memorized and burned that—but a copy of the troop movements that had crossed his desk that evening. "Because the timetable changed today, smuggler. What we all thought was months is weeks. Maybe less."

Renn took the paper. Marrec watched him read it—the columns of regimental designations, the supply manifests, the dates—and watched the smuggler's jaw tighten as the arithmetic landed. Four armies converging on the free cities' coast. Harbor fleets requisitioned in three ports. A quartermaster's note, dry as ash: *provisions calculated for a campaign of six weeks.*

"They're not posturing," Renn said.

"No."

"They're coming to burn us."

"They're coming to take her," Marrec corrected. "Burning is what happens to everything standing near her when they miss." He paused. "And they won't miss twice. The Knights are already in your city. You know that. They burned a lantern-house on the Salt Row two nights ago looking for her."

Something moved through Renn's face then—grief, quick and brutal, gone almost before it arrived but leaving wreckage behind. Marrec had interrogated enough men to read the aftermath even when the expression itself was denied him.

"Odile," Renn said. Just the name. Nothing else.

"She held the door," Marrec said quietly. "That's in the report. She held it long enough that the house was empty when they came through it. And she burned something before they reached her—I don't know what. The Knights are furious about it. Furious men make mistakes, and I intend to be nearby when they do." He met the smuggler's eyes. "I am sorry. For what it's worth, which I know is nothing."

Renn looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed—a short, wrecked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man finding one dark joke in a collapsed mine.

"Nothing," he echoed. "You keep saying that word like it costs you something." He folded the troop movements with careful, deliberate slowness, wincing as his ribs objected, and tucked it inside his shirt. "All right, inquisitor. Here's my counter-offer, and it's not negotiable. You get me out of this hole tonight. Real papers, real route, and you personally walk me past your own checkpoints, because I don't trust a single soul in that spire above the third floor, and I've decided I trust you exactly one floor higher than that."

"That's not a counter-offer. That's my offer with the risk moved onto me."

"Yeah." Renn's grin was a ruin. "That's the point. You want her to trust you? Start by betting something I can see."

* * *

They went out through the records level, because Marrec had spent two years learning which corridors the cameras of clerical habit ignored. He had forged the transfer order himself—an administrative release pending further questioning, routed under the seal of a superior who was conveniently traveling—and the forgery was good, because Marrec had been forging small mercies into the church's paperwork for longer than he liked to admit, and a man gets fluent in the language of his sins.

At the third checkpoint, a young acolyte with ink-stained fingers looked at the order, then at Marrec, then at the prisoner swaying between restraints that Marrec had deliberately left loose.

"This is irregular, Inquisitor."

"Everything worth doing is," Marrec said. "Initial there."

The acolyte initialled. Later—when the reckoning came, and Marrec had stopped pretending it wouldn't—he would remember the boy's face and hope the boy's career survived what Marrec's would not.

They passed the last barrier into the fog-thick streets of Corvane, and Renn stopped, and for a moment simply breathed. The salt-fog wrapped around them both, muffling the city into shapes and suggestions. Somewhere a gull complained at the night. Somewhere far off, a patrol's lanterns swung like slow fireflies along the seawall.

"Why?" Renn asked. He didn't look at Marrec. He looked at the fog, as if the answer might be written there. "Eleven days you worked me over. You could have broken me eventually—you know it, I know it. Instead you throw away your career on a smuggler and a charm-seller. Why?"

Marrec considered lying. It would have been easy; it was practically his trade now. But there was something about the fog, and the hour, and the particular exhaustion of a man who has finally stopped pretending to himself.

"Because I read the ledger," he said. "The real one. The one underneath the one they show novices." He turned to face the smuggler fully. "Do you know where the church's power comes from, Voss? Not faith. Not discipline. Prayers. Millions of them, harvested through the ward-network, devoured at the source before any god can hear them. Every soul in the free cities who folds their hands at night is feeding the throne. And the throne is afraid—truly afraid—for the first time since Isolde took it, because one woman in one market stall is a crack in the pipe, and things are leaking through the crack that haven't leaked in thirty years." He exhaled. "I served that throne for nineteen years because I believed it kept the god-war from happening again. I was wrong. It *is* the god-war. It's just been fought slowly, prayer by prayer, so no one noticed the world bleeding."

Renn was quiet for a while. The fog drifted between them.

"Seb used to say something like that," he said at last. "Not the same words. She'd say the city was holding its breath, and someday it would have to exhale, and nobody was going to like what came out." He looked at Marrec then, and there was something new in his face—not trust, not yet, but the scaffolding of it, raised fast and crooked in an emergency. "She'll come. If I get word to her, she'll come. Not because she trusts you. Because of the armies. She'll do the math the way I just did." His voice caught, steadied. "But inquisitor—if this is a cage with nicer bars, if you hand her to the Knights, if one hair of her head—"

"I know."

"—because Odile died for her, and I'll be honest with you, I don't have much left to lose, and men with nothing left are—"

"*I know,*" Marrec said, and the force of it surprised them both. He mastered his voice. "I have spent nineteen years learning exactly what my order does to the people it takes whole. I am not handing her to it. I am handing her the knife, and the map to the throat, and then I am getting out of her way." He hesitated, then added, because it was true and because truth was the only currency he had left: "And I am asking her, when this is done, to remember that not everyone in the church chose it. Some of us were just born inside the walls."

Renn studied him. Then he nodded, once, the smallest possible contract.

"There's a woman on Gull Street," he said. "Sells mended nets, third door past the chandlery, blue shutters. Tell her the eels got loose. She'll know."

* * *

Marrec walked back to the Bastion alone through streets that felt, for the first time in his nineteen years of service, like enemy territory—not because the city had changed, but because he had. The fog thinned near the harbor, and he could see the seawall, black against the blacker water, and beyond it the open sea where four armies were already loading.

Six weeks, the quartermasters had calculated. But Marrec had read the vanguard schedules, and the vanguard did not wait for quartermasters. By his own count, the first outriders would sight the free cities' watchtowers in eighteen days. Perhaps fifteen, if the weather favored them, and the weather had been strange lately—calm where storms should be, and a pressure in the air like the sky holding its breath, like the whole horizon waiting for someone to speak first.

Eighteen days to find a charm-seller who did not want to be found. Eighteen days to convince her that the hunter was now the hunted's ally. Eighteen days to build, out of a falsified report, a released prisoner, and a lifetime of complicity, something that could stand against a queen who ate prayers.

His report to the spire was due at dawn. He climbed the Bastion's outer stair to his office, lit the lamp, and sat before the blank page for a long time.

Then he wrote: *No significant progress in the Corvane matter. The ember trail appears cold. Recommend continued passive surveillance.*

A lie. His largest yet. It went into the dispatch tube with all the others, and Marrec watched it rattle down the chute toward the spire, and understood that he had just signed his name to the other side of a war.

Outside, far off over the water, thunder rolled—though the sky was clear, and had been clear for days, and would stay clear until it didn't.

Marrec found, examining himself honestly, that he was not afraid.

He was committed. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. Fear wanted a way out. Commitment had already burned the bridges and was now, rather pleasantly, rather terribly, looking forward to seeing what was on the other side of the river.

He snuffed the lamp and began, in the dark, to plan a meeting between a hunted woman and a traitor—and wondered which of them the word fit worse.
