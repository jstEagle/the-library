## 14. The Usurper's Garden

The garden did not grow in soil.

Queen Isolde had made that decision thirty years ago, in the first spring of her reign, when the architects proposed importing loam from the river valleys and she refused with a smile that ended the conversation. Soil was honest. Soil fed things without asking anything back. She wanted nothing honest anywhere near her.

So the garden grew in prayer.

Beneath the flagstones of the Winter Court's innermost courtyard, beneath layers of consecrated lead and blessed iron, ran a lattice of hollow channels that her engineers called the *roots*—and through those roots flowed everything the Church of the Concord gathered from its ten thousand altars across the usurped provinces. Every whispered plea for rain. Every mother's bargain for a sick child. Every dying man's apology to a god who no longer answered. The prayers came down the channels like water through an aqueduct, thin and silver and faintly warm, and where they pooled, things grew that had no business growing under any sun.

Isolde walked among them at dawn, as she did every dawn, barefoot on the warm stone. Her gardeners were not permitted to see her do this. Her priests were not permitted to know she did it at all. The garden was hers alone—the one honest accounting in a kingdom built on a lie, because the garden never pretended to be anything but what it was: a ledger written in living green.

The prayer-vines climbed the eastern wall in braided columns, their leaves veined with a pale luminescence that brightened when they were fed. The blossoms opened only at night, and each one held, for a single hour before wilting, the exact shape of the voice that had grown it. Isolde had learned to read them the way other monarchs read reports. A blossom with a cracked throat of petals meant a prayer offered in desperation. A blossom curled tight as a fist meant a prayer offered in anger. And once—only once, years ago—a blossom had opened shaped like a child's mouth mid-laugh, and Isolde had stood before it for a long time, feeling something she declined to name, before she let it die on the vine like all the others.

She paused now beside the oldest plant in the garden: a black-branched tree that bore no leaves at all, only fruit like clenched grey knuckles. It had grown from the first harvest of devoured prayers, thirty years past, taken from the temples of Veyra when the storm god's priesthood was broken and scattered and their altars pulled down stone by stone. The fruit was bitter. She had tasted one, once, early in her reign, out of curiosity or arrogance—she no longer distinguished between the two—and the taste had stayed with her like a splinter behind the tongue. It tasted of being small. Of standing in the rain and knowing it did not fall for you.

She had built an empire so that no one would ever make her feel that again.

"Your Majesty."

The voice came from the colonnade, soft as a footfall on carpet. Isolde did not turn. Only three people were permitted to speak to her here, and two of them were dead.

"High Cantor." She continued along the row of vines, trailing one finger above a blossom still furled in its daylight sleep. "You've come quickly. That usually means the news is bad, and you hope I'll be gentler if you catch me among the flowers."

"The rite has failed," said High Cantor Ovelin, keeping his distance from the beds as though the plants might recognize him. He was a narrow man, grey as ash, dressed in vestments so heavily embroidered they seemed less clothing than armor. "At Corvane. The vessel resisted. The priesthood's exiles are... alive, we believe, but scattered. And the vessel herself remains intact. Both of them. God and girl."

Isolde stopped walking.

For a moment she simply breathed, letting the garden's warmth move through her, and when she turned her face toward Ovelin it wore the expression her courtiers called *the Queen's patience* and her enemies, in the last minutes available to them for such observations, called something else entirely.

"Tell me exactly what happened," she said. "Not the summary. The shape of it."

Ovelin consulted a slip of paper he did not need—he remembered everything; it was why she kept him. "The exiled priests performed the awakening rite in a salt-cellar beneath the harbor district. Our listening posts detected the surge immediately—an unmasking of divine resonance unlike anything since the Concord. For eleven minutes, Your Majesty, we registered a full theophonic signature. Veyra's own voice, present in the world." He hesitated. Isolde watched him hesitate and found it almost charming. "Then it stopped. Not faded. Stopped. As though something had reached into the rite and closed its hand around it."

"As though the vessel refused her," Isolde said.

"That is how our readers interpreted the pattern, yes."

"And the girl survived the refusal." Isolde resumed walking, slower now, and Ovelin fell into step at the respectful interval. "Eleven minutes carrying a god, and then setting the god down again, and walking away whole. Do you understand what that means, Cantor? Every vessel in the histories was consumed. Every single one. The body burns out, or the god pours in until there is nothing left of the person but a shape wearing their face. This one held the line. She stood inside her own skin and said *no* to something older than the sky, and it obeyed her."

Ovelin's voice thinned. "It may have been exhaustion. The rite itself—"

"It was will." Isolde said it without heat, which somehow made it worse, and Ovelin flinched as though the word had been struck off him. She smiled at the flowerbeds. "I admire it. You look shocked, Cantor. You shouldn't. I have spent my entire reign admiring will in others—from a safe distance, and always briefly. Will is the only thing I have ever feared, and therefore the only thing worth studying. A woman who can refuse a god is either the most dangerous creature alive or the most useful. Possibly both. We will determine which."

They walked in silence for a time, past the fountain that did not hold water but held light—a captured fragment of dawn, purchased at obscene cost from a dying sorcerer, that rose and fell in the basin like slow breathing. Isolde had bought it because her garden needed a heart, and hearts should beat even when no one watched.

"What does the Council of Readers advise?" she asked finally.

"They are divided. Some counsel assassination. Kill the vessel while she is mortal enough to bleed, and the god sleeps another century."

"Mm. And the others?"

"Some counsel..." He swallowed. "Some counsel negotiation. If she carries Veyra willingly, she could be bound by covenant rather than destroyed. An alliance of crowns and gods."

Isolde laughed. It was a genuine laugh, low and warm, and it startled a finch out of the prayer-vines. "Negotiate. With a storm. Cantor, you must tell me sometime what they put in the Readers' tea." She plucked a dead leaf from a vine and let it crumble between her fingers, releasing a faint scent of incense and salt tears. "No. Assassination is waste, and negotiation is surrender. There is a third option, and I am surprised none of your Readers found it, since it is the obvious one."

"Your Majesty?"

"We take the god out of her." She said it the way she might have announced a change in the harvest schedule. "Alive. Intact. Without killing the girl—or rather, without killing her *first*. The order matters, Cantor. If the vessel dies, the god disperses, and we spend another hundred years sweeping divinity back into bottles. But if we open her carefully—if we cut the way the old priests used to cut, before cutting became unfashionable—we can lift Veyra out whole and pour her into a vessel of our own choosing. One with a compliant temperament. One raised in my household from birth, perhaps. There are children in the palace orphanage who pray very beautifully."

Ovelin had gone the color of his vestments. "Your Majesty, the extraction rites are forbidden. They were forbidden by the Concord itself—"

"The Concord," Isolde said pleasantly, "is a document I wrote. Do you imagine I forbade the rites because they are wicked? I forbade them because they are *difficult*, and difficult things in careless hands become loose ends." She turned to face him fully, and the morning light caught the circlet at her brow—black iron set with a single stone that had once been the eye of a temple statue, back when statues were permitted eyes. "Gather the Readers tonight. Tell them the Queen has heard their counsels and weighed them, and that the vessel of Veyra is to be taken alive and delivered to the Winter Court. Tell them nothing else. What I intend to do with her once she arrives is a matter between me and the garden."

* * *

The Hollow Knights assembled in the Undercroft at moonset, as they always did, though the Undercroft had no windows and the moon was a courtesy paid to habit rather than necessity.

Isolde watched them from the gallery above, alone, her hands folded on the railing. Below her, forty figures stood in perfect silence on a floor of black glass, and the silence they made was different from other silences. Ordinary soldiers rustled. Ordinary soldiers breathed, shifted, dreamed with their eyes open. The Hollow Knights did none of these things, because there was nothing left in them to do it with.

She had made them herself, in the early years, when the throne was new and the old gods' loyalists still fought in the hills. Each Knight had been a person once—captured zealots, mostly, priests and paladins who would not renounce their gods. She had not tortured them. Torture was crude; torture left scars, and scars were doors through which the self could climb back in. Instead she had used the quiet process, the patient one: prayer by prayer, memory by memory, she had fed them to the garden's roots until nothing remained but the shape of obedience filled to its edges. What stood below her now were not men and women. They were vessels in the other sense—containers emptied and then sealed, holding nothing, wanting nothing, capable of anything.

People in the free cities told stories about the Hollow Knights. That they cast no shadows. That mirrors refused them. That they could walk through a battlefield untouched because death could not find anything in them to take. The stories were wrong in the details and right in the substance, and Isolde permitted the stories, because fear that arrives pre-embroidered saves the crown the cost of thread.

Their commander stood a half-step before the ranks: Knight-Captain Serevan, first of the Hollow, the man who had been her husband's brother in another life, before she had unmade him so gently that he thanked her afterward. Even emptied, some residue of the old devotion remained in him, redirected like light through a prism. He had loved her brother-in-law. Now that love had nowhere to go, so it went to her, because she was what remained.

"Captain," Isolde said, and her voice carried down into the black glass room without effort, the way it always did, as though the architecture itself had been trained to listen. "You have heard the Readers' report."

"We have, Majesty," Serevan said. His voice was beautiful and terrible, a voice with all the grain sanded out of it.

"A woman in Corvane carries a god. The exiled priesthood tried to wake it and failed—not because their rite was flawed, but because the woman refused. Understand this, Captain: I do not want her dead. Dead, she is a rumor. Alive, she is a door." Isolde leaned forward slightly, and forty blank faces tilted up toward her in unison, a movement like a field of flowers turning toward the sun, and she felt—as she always felt in this room—that particular cold satisfaction that had sustained her through thirty years of stolen prayers. "You will take her alive. You will take the god out of her alive. The surgeons of the Old Rite will accompany you; they remember the techniques, whatever the Concord pretends. When the god is removed, the vessel comes to me breathing. If she dies on the table, you will bring me the surgeon who failed, and I will explain to him personally what was lost."

"Yes, Majesty."

"One more thing." She straightened. "Corvane is a free city. Its walls are warded, its harbor sealed, its council fat and cowardly and convinced that neutrality is a strategy. You will demonstrate otherwise. Burn the lantern district. Break the harbor wards. Let the city learn what its neutrality purchases." She paused, and something almost tender crossed her face. "And Captain—there is a house by the water with a lantern in its window. The vessel's friends keep it. Take the house first. People protect what they love, and I want her unprotected when you come for her. Grief makes such a clean wound. So much easier to cut through."

Serevan bowed. Forty gauntlets struck forty breastplates in a single sound, one sound, like a door closing.

Isolde remained at the rail long after they had marched out, watching the empty black floor, thinking about the girl in Corvane who had looked a god in the face and said *no*.

She should despise her. Part of her did—the part that remembered kneeling in a burning temple at nineteen, praying to Veyra for deliverance and receiving only rain, rain, indifferent rain, until she understood that the gods had never been listening at all and that anyone who believed otherwise deserved to be ruled by someone who knew better. That part of her wanted the girl's defiance ground fine and scattered on the garden beds, one more prayer devoured, one more blossom fed.

But another part—the part she showed to no one, the part that walked barefoot among the flowers at dawn—was curious. Thirty years she had harvested the prayers of the faithful, and never once, not in ten thousand altars, had she heard a prayer answered. The gods were gone. She had proven it. She had built the proof into flagstones and crowned herself with it. And now one stubborn charm-seller in a fog-bound city had gone and found a god anyway, held it, argued with it, and put it down again like a sword too heavy to carry.

*If she can refuse a god,* Isolde thought, turning from the gallery, *then perhaps a god can be refused. Perhaps it can be owned.*

The thought pleased her more than any victory in a decade.

In the morning, the garden would need feeding. In the evening, the Knights would ride. Between the two, the Queen of the Concord would sit in her courtyard among the luminous vines and listen to the prayers come down through the roots, thousands upon thousands, rising from cities that did not yet know they were already lost—and somewhere in that silver flood, fainter than the rest, newer than the rest, she would listen for one voice in particular.

A voice that had prayed once, recently, in a salt-fog, and been heard.

*Let her pray again,* Isolde thought, pressing her palm flat against the warm stone above the roots. *Let her pray all she likes. Everything that answers a prayer in my kingdom answers to me.*
