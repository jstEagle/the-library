## 16. Everything I Buried

She felt the lantern-house before she saw it.

That was the thing about grief, Sebastian thought, or perhaps it was the thing about Veyra—there was no longer a clean line between what she suffered and what she was. The storm that had carried her across the harbor came up through her bones like a second heartbeat, and every heartbeat struck the city's stones and echoed back wrong. The wards along the seawall sang their thin defensive hymn. The rain fell in sheets that tasted of smoke.

And beneath it all, threaded through the downpour like a black wire through white silk: burning. Old wood, old paper, old lavender—the smell of Odile's drying racks, the smell of the tallow Odile had rendered herself because she said the market stuff smelled like a chandler's arse. Ten years of mornings. Ten years of Renn's boots on the stairs and Odile's voice calling *the kettle's on, don't let it boil angry* as if a kettle could be scolded into patience.

The Hollow Knights had left the house standing, barely—a charred ribcage of it, walls gone to black lace against the rain—but they had made sure she would know exactly what had been taken. They'd left the door. Odile's door, blue once, painted over so many times no one alive remembered the original color. It stood alone in the wreckage like a tombstone someone had forgotten to lay down.

Sebastian landed on the street without deciding to land there. The storm set her down the way a hand sets down a cup.

The Knights were still there. Six of them, hollow where men should be, their armor drinking the rainlight and giving nothing back. They had not fled. They had been waiting for her—they had burned the house as bait, and now they stood in the ruins of everything she loved with the patience of things that did not breathe, and somewhere inside her the pressure built and built and built.

*There,* Veyra said, and it was not a voice anymore, not since the rite, not since she had stopped being only Sebastian Villalobos. It was weather given language. It was the sky leaning close to whisper. *There they are. Let me.*

The suppression tattoos on her arms—Renn had them now, he kept them folded in oilcloth against his heart, her mortal name written in ink and scar—those tattoos were gone from her skin, but she could still feel the shape of them, the shape of the smallness she had worn for ten years, and the smallness was screaming.

*Let me,* Veyra said again. *Let me and it will be quick. Quicker than they deserve.*

And that was the horror of it, she realized distantly, the way one realizes one is falling only after the ground has already begun to rise: it would be quick. That was the terrible arithmetic of a god's anger. Six Knights, and behind them the garrison barracks, and behind the barracks the tenement rows where the fisher families lived, and behind those the orphanage on Tanner's Lane where Odile had once bought forty pounds of wool at midwinter because, she'd said, children should have something soft that wasn't each other. A storm meant to erase six hollow men did not stop at six. A storm did not check its ledger. She knew this. She had known it since she was nineteen years old and the world had taught her what her power cost when it went off like a wound instead of a choice.

The god inside her did not care. The god inside her had watched temples burn for a thousand years and had learned nothing about restraint, because gods are not people; gods are forces wearing names, and the name Veyra wore was *storm*, and storms do not grieve. Storms only fall.

*They took your mother,* Veyra said, using Odile's face inside her mind, Odile's laugh, Odile's hands kneading bread dough while she talked about the debt she owed and never explained. *They took your brother. They took the child who prayed and the prayer that answered. Let me make it even.*

*No,* Sebastian said.

But her hands were already rising. That was the treachery of it—that she said *no* and her body said *yes*, that the rage in her chest was not hers alone anymore and could not be argued with by the part of her that still remembered how to be careful. Lightning gathered in the clouds above Corvane like a held breath. The air went the color of a bruise. Somewhere below, in the streets, people were looking up, and she could hear them through the rain—not words, just the sound of a city inhaling.

*You want this too,* Veyra said, gentle now, which was worse. *Don't lie to me. I am you. I have been you since before you had a name for yourself. You want the world to hurt the way you hurt.*

"Yes," Sebastian whispered, and the word came out as thunder, and windows rattled down three streets, and the first of the Hollow Knights raised its head toward her like a hound scenting meat.

*Then open.*

Her fingers uncurled.

* * *

Renn had run until his lungs gave out, and then he had run anyway, because that was the kind of man he was—smugglers learn early that the body's complaints are negotiable—and so he arrived at the foot of the storm half-dead already, which meant he had nothing left to lose, which meant he was, in his own grim accounting, the freest man in Corvane.

He had seen her once before like this. Only once. Nineteen years old and the sky split open above the Godsfen and he had learned that night what a person looked like when they stopped being a person and became an argument between heaven and earth. He had spent ten years telling himself he'd imagined the rest of it—the light, the silence afterward, the girl kneeling in the mud with her hands pressed over her mouth like she could push the thunder back in.

He had not imagined it.

"Sebastian!" His voice came out shredded. He tried again. "SEBASTIAN!"

She turned—or the storm turned, carrying her attention the way a current carries a leaf—and looked down at him, and his heart broke cleanly in two, because her eyes were silver. All silver, edge to edge, no brown left, no warmth, no *her*. And yet her mouth was her mouth, and it was saying, in a voice like wind through a broken window:

"Run, Renn."

"No." He walked forward. Rain hammered him flat; he walked anyway, bent into it like a man wading a river. "Not doing that."

"You have to run. I can't—" Her jaw clenched. The lightning overhead crawled, hungry, looking for a way down. "I can't hold it if you're standing under it."

"Then hold it." He stopped ten feet away, close enough to see the storm moving under her skin, veins lit like rivers on a map. Close enough to see that she was crying, though the rain hid it, though maybe the rain *was* it. "Hold it, Bas. That's all. Just hold it."

"I can't." The words tore out of her. "They killed her, Renn. They killed her holding the door, she died for *me*, for my secret, for a ledger full of names she burned rather than give up, and they're standing there, they're *standing there*—" The clouds above her began to turn, slow, vast, a wheel of darkness. "I can end them. I can end all of it. Isolde, the church, the whole rotten covenant—I can burn it out of the world tonight."

"Yeah," Renn said. "And Tanner's Lane goes with it."

The wheel of cloud stuttered.

"The orphanage," he said, quieter. "Forty pounds of wool. Midwinter. You remember. She told us the story every year like it was scripture." He took another step. The nearest Knight shifted, and he ignored it, because there was only one thing in the world right now and it was the woman in the sky's grip who was forgetting how to be one. "The fishers on Gull Row. The Marlowe kid who buys your luck-charms with coins he finds in gutters. The kettle, Bas. Who's going to scold the kettle?"

"Don't." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Don't you dare make me laugh right now."

"Wouldn't dream of it." But his mouth twitched, bloodied and ruined as it was, and something in the terrible symmetry of the moment—the two of them, ash and rain, the end of the world gathering overhead—bent slightly, the way a blade bends before it either snaps or turns aside.

The Hollow Knights began to move. Not toward Renn. Toward her—all six, closing in a slow crescent, and Renn understood with a lurch that they didn't care whether she unleashed the storm or not. Either outcome served the throne. If she destroyed the city, the free cities would beg Isolde for protection. If she didn't, they'd cut the god out of her and take it home in a jar.

"They're not afraid of you," he said quickly. "Look at them. That's the trick, Bas. That's the whole trick. They *want* you to be the monster the church has been preaching about for ten years. Every sermon, every burning, every name on every list—it was all building to this. You, in the sky, leveling a city full of innocents. Proof. They don't need to beat you. They just need you to *confirm* them."

"I don't care."

"You do." Another step. He was inside the crescent now, between her and the nearest Knight, a soaked, bleeding man with his arms spread like he could block lightning with his ribs. "Because if you didn't care, you'd have done it already. You've been holding it since you landed. Minutes, Bas. You've held a god's fury for minutes when most people can't hold their temper through a market argument. That's not nothing. That's *you*. That's the girl who healed a stranger's kid and then burned her own charms so no one could trace it back. You've always paid the price quietly. Don't stop paying it quietly now, when it matters most."

"She's dead, Renn." And now the storm in her voice broke, and it was only Sebastian, only a woman, only grief with nowhere to go. "Odile is dead and I wasn't here. I'm never here. That's what hiding costs. Everyone I love dies at a door while I'm being careful somewhere else."

"I know." His voice went rough. "I know. I carried her out. Or—what was left to carry." He swallowed. "She was smiling, Bas. Did you know that? Door frame came down and she was smiling, like she'd finally paid off that debt she never talked about. Like she'd been waiting ten years for the bill to come due so she could settle it." He wiped his face with a filthy sleeve, which changed nothing. "So here's what I think. I think if you bring the storm down right now, you're not avenging her. You're making her death mean what the church wants it to mean. You're letting them write her ending. And Odile Vane did not spend sixty-eight years being impossible to anyone who tried to write her ending."

The rain fell. The clouds churned. The lightning waited, coiled and enormous, a scream not yet screamed.

And Sebastian hung in the middle of it, between the god's yes and the man's no, between *let me* and *hold it*, and understood—finally, horribly, clearly—that this was the choice she had been running from for ten years. Not whether to use her power. Whether to be ruled by it. Whether grief got a vote. Whether pain got to pick the target.

Veyra surged against the inside of her skull like a tide against a sea-wall.

*They deserve it.*

"Some of them do," Sebastian said aloud, and her voice was double now, mortal and thunder both. "That's what makes it a trap. It only works because some of them deserve it."

*The city will rebuild.*

"Tanner's Lane won't."

*You will feel better.*

"For about a minute." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, wet and cracked. "And then I'd be Isolde. A queen of ashes, tending a garden of dead prayers. That's what she is, Veyra. That's what she made of a god's hunger. And you're asking me to become her with better aim."

The storm went very quiet.

Not calm—quiet, the way the sea is quiet before it decides. And in that quiet, for the first time since the rite, Sebastian did not wrestle the god. She simply stood inside her own skin and refused to move, the way Odile had stood in a doorway, the way Renn stood now in the rain with his arms spread, and she understood that this too was power—not the calling-down but the holding-up, not the strike but the stay.

*You cannot keep me leashed forever,* Veyra said. There was something almost human in it now. Almost wounded.

"I'm not trying to leash you." Sebastian lowered her hands slowly, slowly, the lightning following them down like a dog reluctant to heel. "I'm asking you to wait. To choose with me. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I think—you've been caged for a thousand years, so maybe no one ever told you—but the difference is *everything*."

The clouds groaned. Shifted. And then, inch by inch, degree by degree, the great wheel of darkness began to turn away from the city—from Tanner's Lane, from Gull Row, from the orphanage with its forty pounds of wool—and toward the harbor, where Isolde's warships sat at anchor like knives in a drawer, and toward the garden-throne beyond them where a stolen covenant fed on stolen prayers.

Sebastian descended. Her feet found the flooded cobblestones, and she was only a woman again, shaking, silver-eyed, soaked through, alive.

Renn reached her at a stagger and caught her arms, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there were no words large enough and also because the smallest ones were the truest: *you're here, you're here, you're here.*

"You don't get to avenge us by erasing us," he said, hoarse, into the rain between them. "Ever. Say it back."

"You don't get to avenge us by erasing us," she repeated, and the thunder repeated it after her, rolling out over the darkened city like a promise, or a law.

Then her eyes moved past his shoulder, to the six Hollow Knights frozen in their crescent—and to the seventh figure standing behind them, unhurried, dry beneath the storm as though the rain itself had agreed to fall around him.

Inquisitor Dain Marrec, with a sealed letter in his hand and an expression on his weathered face that she had never seen there before.

It looked, impossibly, like hope.

"We need to talk," Marrec said, "about the council's terms. And about what Isolde's armies will do to this city by dawn if we refuse them."

Behind him, far out in the harbor, the first of the usurper's signal fires bloomed red against the water—one, then two, then a dozen—the march of the Hollow begun in earnest, the last hours of the old world ticking down like a lamp running out of oil.
