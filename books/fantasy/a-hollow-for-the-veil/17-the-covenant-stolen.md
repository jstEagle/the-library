## 17. The Covenant Stolen

The rain had followed Marrec all the way from Corvane.

It had started somewhere past the salt marshes, a thin gray drizzle that thickened as he rode north, and by the time the spires of Vellamare rose out of the morning haze it had become something with intent behind it—a storm that seemed to know his name. He told himself it was coincidence. He had spent eleven years telling himself things were coincidence, and it had kept him alive, if not clean.

He rode through the Harbor Gate at dawn with the proof wrapped in oilcloth against his chest: three documents, a fragment of consecrated lead, and a dead priest's confession written in a hand that shook. The confession was the least of it. Anyone could write a confession. It was the covenant itself that mattered—the original accord between the free cities and the old gods, signed in blood and stormlight two hundred years ago, before Isolde's grandmother's grandmother had learned that divinity could be farmed like any other crop.

Marrec had read it by candlelight in a safehouse cellar while Renn's people watched him from the shadows, arms crossed, waiting to see what he would do. He had read every line twice. He had read the clause near the end—the one about the vessel, the veil, the price—and when he finished he had sat in the dark for a long time, listening to water drip through the ceiling, feeling the shape of his whole life rearrange itself around a single terrible fact.

He had served a lie. Faithfully. Thoroughly. For eleven years.

* * *

The Council of the Free Cities met in the Amber Hall, a chamber built to make men feel small. Its dome was painted with the constellations as they had been before the god-war, back when the sky still answered questions, and the floor was a mosaic of the nine cities joined hand to hand around a central sun rendered in gold leaf so old it had gone green. Delegates sat in a horseshoe of desks worn smooth by two centuries of elbows. Marrec had testified before them twice in his career—once on grain tariffs, once on harbor piracy—and both times he had left feeling like a man who had shouted into a well and heard only his own echo come back.

Today the well was full.

He stood at the center of the mosaic sun with the oilcloth bundle on the table before him, and he could feel the attention of forty delegates pressing down on him like weather. Chancellor Ovidra of Vellamare presided, a narrow woman with silver-threaded black hair and eyes like ledger entries. She had been stalling the war question for eight months. Everyone knew it. Everyone also knew she was the only person in the chamber capable of holding nine fractious cities together long enough to lose a war properly, which was its own kind of achievement.

"Inquisitor Marrec," she said. "You request the floor under emergency provisions. You have twenty minutes."

"I'll need less." He unwrapped the bundle. His hands were steady; he had made them steady through practice, the way a man learns to hold a blade without flinching. "What I'm about to show you comes from inside the Church of the Concordant Throne. I took it myself. I'll answer for how, afterward, if anyone cares."

He laid the documents out one by one, and let the clerks carry copies down the horseshoe, and watched the faces change.

"The document before you is the Concord of Salt and Sky," he said. "Executed in the Year of Falling Ashes between the nine free cities and the pantheon of the old gods. You'll notice the seal—it's authenticated by the Temple Archive at Solmere, or what's left of it. This is the original covenant. Not a copy. The original."

"Where has it been?" someone called from the third tier. A merchant-prince from Brassa, by his rings.

"For the last sixty years," Marrec said, "in the private vault beneath Queen Isolde's garden-palace at Corvane. Along with this." He held up the second document, thinner, its vellum dark with age. "A supplementary instrument. Unratified by any council. Signed by one monarch and sealed with the Concordant Throne's authority—which, I remind you, was created *by* this covenant, not above it."

He let the silence stretch. He had learned from Dain Marrec's long career in interrogation that silence did more work than words; you simply opened the door and let them walk through it themselves.

"The supplementary instrument," Chancellor Ovidra said slowly, "does what, exactly?"

"It transfers the covenant's obligations to the throne and voids the gods' claims upon the cities—in exchange for certain considerations." He turned the page toward her, though she had her own copy now. "Specifically, Article Four. Read Article Four aloud, Chancellor. I think it should be heard."

Ovidra read. Her voice was dry and precise, and it cracked only slightly, on the last line:

"*...the Throne shall receive, in perpetuity, the devotions of the faithful, to dispose of as the Crown sees fit, unbound by the covenants of hearth, harvest, or sea.*"

"Devotions," said the delegate from Brassa. "You mean prayers."

"I mean prayers," Marrec said. "Every prayer spoken in the Concordant territories for sixty years has been collected. Diverted. Consumed. There is a mechanism beneath the palace—I've seen the records of its construction, I have engineers' reports—that draws devotion the way a millrace draws water. The Queen's power, her armies' endurance, the wards on her warships, the Hollow Knights themselves—all of it is fueled by the stolen faith of millions of people who believed their prayers were going somewhere." He paused. "Who believed, most of them, that no one was listening at all. That was the lie we were taught too. That the gods were dead, or gone, or never there. The church didn't kill the old gods, delegates. It *starved* them and sold the meat."

The chamber erupted. He let it. He stood in the middle of the noise with his hands flat on the table and waited, because there was more, and the more was worse, and they needed to burn through the first outrage before they had room for the second.

It took four minutes. Ovidra restored order with a gavel older than the mosaic.

"You said there was more."

"There is." Marrec touched the third document, the confession. "This is the testimony of Father Ellery Vance, Keeper of the Inner Vault, taken shortly before his death. In it he describes the mechanism, the diversion rites, and—" here he made himself slow down, made himself say it plainly, because plainly was the only way it would land "—and the reason the church has launched a continent-wide hunt for a single woman hiding in Corvane."

"The ember," said the delegate from Solmere. Word traveled fast among the fearful.

"Sebastian Villalobos. Formerly of the Storm-Clergy, formerly an exile, currently a charm-seller in the night market." He heard how absurd it sounded and let it sound absurd. "She is the last living vessel of Veyra, goddess of storms. The covenant names such vessels as the living seal upon the veil between gods and mortals. As long as a vessel endures, the old gods cannot fully return—but neither can the Throne's mechanism operate at full capacity. Her existence is a leak in Isolde's engine. That is why the Knights are hunting her. Not because she's dangerous to the throne's armies." He looked up at the horseshoe, at forty faces lit lamplight-gold under the painted stars. "Because she's dangerous to the throne's *food supply*."

* * *

They broke for recess, which Marrec understood to mean that the factions needed to meet separately and decide what they already believed. He used the hour badly—walking the colonnade outside the Amber Hall in the rain, smoking nothing, tasting nothing, watching gulls fight the wind over the harbor. Somewhere down the coast, Corvane was burning or about to be. Odile's lantern-house was ash; Renn was in chains or out of them; Sebastian was standing on a seawall somewhere between the city and the storm-front, and Marrec had put her there, or helped, or failed to stop it—he hadn't yet decided which verb fit, and suspected he would spend the rest of his life deciding.

An aide found him at the colonnade's end. "Chancellor Ovidra will see you privately. Now."

Her office smelled of ink and cold tea. She dismissed her secretaries, poured two cups without asking, and pushed one across the desk.

"You knew," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I suspected for years. I confirmed it six weeks ago." He drank. The tea was bitter enough to strip paint. "I needed proof that would survive contact with this council. Proof that couldn't be dismissed as treason, or madness, or the fabrications of a defector buying his life."

"And do you consider yourself a defector?"

"I consider myself a man who found out the house was on fire and stopped arguing about whose fault the lamp was."

Ovidra studied him for a long moment. "Inquisitor, I'm going to tell you something I haven't said aloud in this building in nine years. When I was young, my mother prayed. Every night, at the hearth, to whatever was left to listen to. She prayed for my father's ship, and my brother's lungs, and rain for the barley. And nothing ever came. And I grew up believing that was the truth of the world—that the sky was empty and prayer was a habit grief wears when it has nowhere else to go." She set down her cup. "Now you're telling me the sky wasn't empty. It was *robbed*. Do you understand what that does to a person? To nine cities of persons?"

"It's why I came here instead of going to the press," Marrec said. "People will forgive a queen who taxes them. They will not forgive a queen who ate their grief."

"No," Ovidra agreed. "They won't. Which means we have perhaps one season—not even that—to turn this into policy before it turns into riot. Nine cities, Inquisitor. Nine councils, nine militias, nine sets of grudges, and one shared border with a throne that has been drinking our prayers for sixty years and is now marching an army that doesn't tire, doesn't fear, and doesn't eat." She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "And you want us to declare war."

"I want you to survive. Declaring war is optional. Losing is not."

* * *

The afternoon session ran until the lamps were trimmed twice. Marrec sat through it in the visitors' gallery, watching the covenant's contents pass from hand to hand like a live coal, watching alliances form and dissolve in the space of a single speech. The Brassa delegation wanted reparations first, war second. Solmere wanted the temple archives restored and the mechanism destroyed by sappers, not soldiers. The river cities wanted guarantees for the grain routes. Someone from the eastern marches proposed, with a straight face, that they open negotiations with Isolde, and was laughed at for exactly as long as it took everyone to remember that Isolde's ambassadors were already in the city, lodged at the Gilded Anchor, and had been for a month.

By dusk a resolution had taken shape. Marrec read it in draft form, passed to him by a clerk who wouldn't meet his eyes, and felt the floor tilt under his chair.

It declared the Concordant Throne illegitimate. It invoked the covenant's mutual-defense clauses. It authorized a unified command, pooled treasuries, embargoed Corvane's shipping. All of it—everything he had ridden through a storm to deliver, everything he had burned his name and order and future to make possible—was there, in language older and finer than anything the council usually produced.

And then there was Article Seven.

*Whereas the covenant's restoration requires the presence and cooperation of a lawful vessel, and whereas the vessel known as Sebastian Villalobos remains within territory contested by the enemy, the Council hereby requests that the said vessel present herself to the Council's custody at the earliest opportunity, there to be held under protective guarantee pending the disposition of hostilities.*

*Custody.* Marrec read the word again. *Protective guarantee.* He had written interrogations orders with softer teeth.

He went down to the floor during the recess, found Ovidra at the chancellery table, and set the draft in front of her.

"Article Seven."

"What about it?"

"You know what about it."

She didn't flinch. He had expected her not to flinch; it was one of the reasons he'd trusted her, and one of the reasons he should have trusted her less. "The smaller cities wouldn't sign without it, Dain. Brassa, Solmere, the Marches—they've spent two days hearing that one woman carries a god who can crack their harbors open. They will not authorize a unified army while that question is unanswered. Article Seven is the price of the other six articles."

"She's not a hostage."

"She's a *guarantee*," Ovidra said. "There's a difference. Custody under protective guarantee. Food, lodging, dignity, counsel of her choosing. We are not handing her to anyone. We are asking her to stand where nine nervous cities can see that the storm has a face and a name and a voice, and that the voice is willing to talk."

"You're asking her to walk into a cage and wait to find out whether it's a cell or a throne room."

"I'm asking her to do what every person in this war has been asked to do since the first wall fell," Ovidra said, and her voice went quiet and very tired. "To give up something she can't spare so that other people can keep what they can't defend. I don't like it. I wrote it at three in the morning and I hated every word. But you brought us a fire, Inquisitor, and fire doesn't care what we like. Either the council holds together or the council dies divided, and Article Seven is the stitch that holds it. Take it out and watch Brassa walk, then Solmere, then the Marches, and then explain to me how the free cities survived."

Marrec looked at the draft a long time. Outside the tall windows the rain had stopped; the clouds were breaking apart over the harbor in ragged gold, and the light lay across the mosaic floor of the Amber Hall like something spilled.

"She saved my life," he said finally. "Three weeks ago, in the salt-fog. She had every reason to let the storm take me and she pulled me out of it. Then she gave me the truth that gutted her own history, because she thought the cities deserved it more than she deserved her privacy." He straightened the pages, squared their edges against the table, a small useless act of tidiness. "And your answer is to summon her like a defendant."

"Our answer," Ovidra corrected gently, "is to ask her like a citizen. Whether it feels like a summons will depend, I suppose, on how we deliver it." She met his eyes. "Which is why I'm asking you to deliver it. You're the only messenger she might believe."

* * *

The session resumed at moonrise. The vote was called at the second bell after midnight. Marrec stood in the gallery and listened to the roll: Vellamare, aye. Brassa, aye—after a quarter hour of procedural throat-clearing that fooled no one. Solmere, aye. The river cities, aye, grudgingly, their delegate staring at Article Seven the whole time he spoke. The eastern marches abstained, which everyone understood to mean *aye, but we'll deny it later*. Eight of nine. The ninth, little salt-poor Kestrel, voted nay on principle and was thanked for its principle.

Carried. Unanimous in effect, fractured in spirit, exactly as designed.

The chamber emptied slowly, delegates clustering in doorways, speaking low and fast, the covenant copies vanishing into satchels and sleeves. Marrec stayed until the floor was nearly bare, standing at the rail of the gallery, looking down at the mosaic sun where he had delivered the truth and watched it get weighed, measured, and priced.

Ovidra found him there. She handed him a folded letter, sealed with the council's wax, bearing the summons in formal language and, beneath the formal language, a single handwritten line in the chancellor's own ink: *We ask, we do not compel. Come and speak for yourself.*

"You leave at dawn," she said. "There's a fast courier boat. You can reach the coast in two days if the weather holds."

"The weather," Marrec said, "has not been holding."

"No," Ovidra agreed. "I've noticed. I've been trying to decide whether that's an omen or a message." She paused at the gallery door, silhouetted against the lamplight of the corridor. "For what it's worth, Inquisitor—I believe you. About all of it. And I believe she'll come. People who have already given up everything rarely hesitate to give up the last thing, if you ask them honestly. It's the ones protecting something who refuse."

"She's protecting a city," Marrec said. "Full of people who love her."

"Then let us hope," Ovidra said, "that the city loves her back loudly enough to matter. Because if the council's letter reaches her and she reads it as a warrant—if she decides the free cities are just another throne wearing a kinder mask—then we will have done Isolde's work for her. We will have taken the one person the storm trusts, and taught the storm that trust is a trap." She pulled her cloak close. "Deliver it well, Dain. Everything depends on how it sounds when it lands."

He stood alone in the gallery a while longer after she was gone, the letter heavy in his coat pocket, heavier than the covenant had been. Through the high windows the broken clouds were sliding off the moon, and below him the harbor lights of Vellamare glittered like a net thrown over dark water.

Two days south, a woman stood on a seawall between a drowning city and a sky full of answers. By the time his boat cleared the breakwater, the Hollow Knights would already be moving.

He had carried the truth north and watched it become a bargain. Now he had to carry the bargain south and watch it become a choice—one that belonged to someone else entirely, made in the shadow of an army, with the whole weight of nine cities dressed up as a polite request.

He thought of her in the salt-fog, pulling him from the water, asking nothing.

*God forgive us,* he thought, and did not specify which god, because he no longer knew how many were left to hear him.
