## 18. March of the Hollow

The garden had never looked so beautiful, Isolde thought, and that was how she knew the end was near.

Beautiful things required tending, and tending required attention, and all her attention had turned east these last weeks—to the free cities, to Corvane, to the ember that had dared to burn in her carefully darkened world. So the garden had grown wild in the way only her garden could grow wild: roses the color of arterial blood, hedges that murmured in their sleep, vines heavy with fruit that wept softly if you listened long enough. Each plant had been grown from a prayer. Each prayer had been taken from someone who had once believed the gods were listening.

She walked the rows barefoot, as she always did, her hem dark with dew that was not entirely water. Behind her, at a respectful distance, the Hollow Knights waited in their silence. They were good silence, her Knights. They did not rustle, did not breathe audibly, did not dream. She had made sure of that. Everything in her kingdom made sure of nothing, and that was the whole of her genius.

"Your Majesty." The voice came from the garden gate, thin and careful. A courier, gray-robed, gray-faced, holding a gray dispatch as though it might bite. "Word from the vanguard. The armies reach the outer walls of Corvane at moonrise."

"And the harbor?"

"The wards still hold, Majesty. But the Knights report—" the courier swallowed— "they report the wards are *singing*."

Isolde smiled. It was a genuine smile, which made it worse. "Of course they are. Something on the other side is pushing." She plucked a rose from its stem and turned it slowly in her fingers. It whimpered. "A vessel who will not be emptied. A god who will not stay buried. Do you know what I was doing thirty years ago, courier?"

"No, Majesty."

"Neither do I, some days." She let the rose fall. It landed without sound. "I was a girl in a temple that no longer exists, watching old men argue about a covenant they were too cowardly to keep. They said the gods were too dangerous to leave among mortals. So we took the listening and called it safety. We took the prayers and called them fuel." She looked east, past the walls of her garden, past the walls of everything. "I have never once believed we were wrong. But I have always known we would have to do this again. Someday. To someone."

The courier waited. The Knights waited. The garden wept its soft, fruit-heavy weeping.

"Tell the vanguard to hold at the walls until the harbor is broken," she said at last. "I want Corvane to watch the sea open before I let them watch their gates. Fear in sequence, courier. It is the only mercy I have ever been accused of." She turned back to her roses. "And tell the Knights: the girl is to be taken *whole*. The god comes out of her alive, or not at all. If they must choose between her life and the god's—"

She did not finish the sentence. She did not need to. The Knights were already moving, soundless as a held breath, and the garden gate closed behind them with the softness of a coffin lid.

Isolde stood alone among her devoured prayers and, for one unguarded moment, pressed her palm flat against the soil.

*Forgive me,* said the last prayer she had ever owned, the one she had never fed to the garden. *Forgive me. Forgive me.*

No one answered. That, too, was by design.

* * *

From the eastern tower of the Corvane customs house, Marrec could see the fires of the usurper's camp, a scatter of orange eyes blinking in the dark hills like a constellation that had fallen and decided to stay.

He had been standing there for three hours. His report sat unfinished on the desk behind him—*no anomalous activity, ember presumed fled the city, recommend standing down the hunt*—the fourth such lie he had written, each one a little larger, each one a little harder to carry. Somewhere below, in the cells he had once filled, Renn of the lantern-house sat in one, and Marrec had spent a week pretending he did not know exactly which cell, exactly which guard, exactly which hour the rotation left a door under-watched.

"You could still stop," he told himself quietly. "You could write the true report. You could hand her over and go back to being a man who sleeps at night."

But he had seen the thin place. He had stood where the veil wore thin and heard what the church did with the listening—thousands of voices, folded and pressed and burned for fuel, prayers that had gone up in hope and come down as power in Isolde's veins. He had interrogated a priest of the old gods and watched the man speak of the stolen covenant with such grief that Marrec had needed to leave the room, and had stood in the corridor afterward, a hunter of thirty years, understanding for the first time that he had been the hound of thieves.

Below him, Corvane was doing what Corvane did on the eve of everything: arguing about prices. The night markets still ran, the fishwives still shouted, the harbor still stank of tar and salt and honest labor. They did not know. Or worse—they half knew, and had decided that not-knowing was a kind of armor.

A knock at the tower door. One of his own, a young inquisitor named Pell, breathless from the stairs.

"The harbor wards," Pell said. "They're—Inquisitor, you should see it. They're glowing. The old harbor-wards, the ones nobody's maintained since the Concord. They're lighting up like—like the sea is full of lanterns."

Marrec closed his eyes. *The sea is full of lanterns.* He had heard that phrase before, in a lantern-house on the river, from an old woman who kept her lamps lit for reasons she had never fully explained.

"Show me."

From the seawall side of the tower, they saw it together: a line of pale fire running along the water where no fire should be, the drowned wards of old Corvane waking one by one, singing a note too low to hear and too large to ignore. And beyond them, out on the black water, shapes. Silent. Patient. The vanguard of the Hollow, come ashore like a tide that had learned to walk.

"They'll breach within the hour," Pell said. "Maybe less. The wards were built to hold back *gods*, sir. Not whatever those are."

"No," Marrec said. "They were built to hold back exactly what those are. That's the problem." He pulled his cloak tight. "Get word to the council. Not the full council—half of them are Isolde's already. Get word to the harbor-master, the night-watch, anyone still loyal to the city and not to their own skin. And then—"

He stopped. Far off, at the eastern edge of the seawall, a figure had climbed up onto the old stone where no sentry was posted. Small against the vast gray sky. Standing very still. Facing the storm-front that had gathered on the horizon like a debt come due.

Marrec knew that silhouette. He had cornered it in salt-fog and made it a bargain he still wasn't sure he'd kept his side of.

"Never mind," he said. "I have one more letter to write. And this one's going to be true."

* * *

Renn had counted the hours by drips. The cell leaked; everything in Corvane leaked; it was one of the things he loved about the city, and he loved it now with a ferocity that made his ribs ache where the resistance agents—or the church's men, it hardly mattered, a fist was a fist—had worked him over.

They had asked him where the storm-caller would run. He had given them the drowned road to Vell's Landing, which was not a road at all but a channel that flooded at half-tide, and he had given it to them with such convincing terror that they'd left within the day, six boats and forty men chasing water that would swallow them by morning. It was, he reflected, the finest lie of his career, and no one would ever know, and it might not matter anyway.

Because the walls were burning. He could smell it through the grate—the outer wall, the eastern quarter, wood and pitch and something underneath that smelled wrong, like burnt sugar, like prayers. The Hollow Knights had reached the city, and the Hollow Knights did not fight the way men fought. The guards who came down the corridor to check on him had gone pale and stopped meeting his eyes.

"Hey," he said to the youngest one, the one who still flinched at sounds. "What's happening out there?"

The guard said nothing. Then, in a voice like a confession: "The harbor's gone. The wards went down an hour ago. They came out of the water, and they didn't—there wasn't any fighting, there was just *quiet*, and then the quiet walked inland."

Renn sat back against the cold stone. He thought of Odile, and made himself stop, because thinking of Odile in the past tense was a door he could not afford to open in a locked room with no window.

He thought of Sebastian instead. Of the night she'd first come up the river in his boat, soaked and silent, with a face like a woman carrying a debt she couldn't name. Of the way she'd looked at storms—not afraid, the way everyone was afraid, but *recognized*. He had known, even then. Not what she was. Just that she was more than the smallness she performed, the way a river is more than the culvert it's forced through.

And now the culvert was breaking, and the whole city was going to find out what had been living in it all along.

"Guard," he said. "Get me a message to Inquisitor Marrec. Tell him Renn of the lantern-house wants to make a trade." He smiled, and it hurt his split lip, and he smiled wider. "Tell him I know the way through the flooded quarter to the seawall. And tell him—" he paused, choosing the words the way he chose cargo, by weight and by what they'd fetch— "tell him the storm-caller's standing on the wall right now, and if he doesn't get there fast, she's going to do something none of us can take back."

The guard stared at him. "How would you know that?"

Renn leaned his head back against the stone and looked up through the grate at the smoke-stained sky, at the storm-front rolling in off the sea like a held breath finally let out.

"Because I know her," he said. "And it's starting to rain."

* * *

It was not raining yet. That was the terrible thing. The storm stood offshore like a congregation waiting for a cue, ten miles of black cloud and green-lit heart, and it did not move, because it was *hers*—had always been hers, was hers now whether she claimed it or not—and it was waiting for her to decide what it was for.

Sebastian stood on the seawall with the wind pulling at her hair and her coat and the ten years of smallness she had worn like a second skin, and she listened.

She had gotten very good at listening. It was the first thing the suppression tattoos had taught her: how to hear the storm through the silence they pressed over her, the way you learn to hear the sea through a wall. For ten years the storm had been a rumor at the edge of her senses. Now it was a voice, and it was saying her name—not *Sebastian*, not the name Odile had given her and Renn had teased her with and the market had shouted over, but the older one, the one that lived underneath, the one that tasted like ozone and iron.

*Vessel,* said the storm. *Come home. Let me in. Let me carry you.*

*No,* she said. *That's the old bargain. I've seen where it ends.*

Below her, the harbor wards were dying one by one. She could see them going out—pale lines of old light guttering along the water, each one a note in a song that had been sung for three hundred years to keep the deep things deep. And in the water, where the light failed, the Hollow Knights were coming ashore. They did not splash. They did not struggle. They walked up out of the sea the way a memory walks up out of sleep, and where they passed, the night went quieter, as though the world were holding its breath around them.

Behind her, Corvane. The market where she had sold luck to people who deserved the real thing. The lantern-house on the river, dark now, its windows empty, its keeper—

She made herself not finish the thought. There would be time for grief later, or there would be nothing at all, and either way it would not help the people behind her now: the fishwives and the charm-sellers and the children in the flooded quarter, all of them asleep or pretending to sleep, all of them trusting in walls and wards and a city's stubborn ordinary luck.

*They will burn,* said the storm, gently, the way you tell someone a diagnosis. *They will burn, and you will watch, and you will hate yourself for every prayer you refused to answer. Let me in. I am so tired of being outside.*

"I know," Sebastian said aloud, and her voice was small against the wind, and she did not care. "I know you're tired. I know what they did to you—sealed you out, sealed me in, made us both into exiles and called it peace." Her hands were shaking. She looked at them—her hands, the charm-maker's hands, the hands that had healed a child in a market stall because she could not bear not to—and saw the suppression tattoos beginning to fade, the ink lifting from her skin like ash off a page, as though ten years of smallness had simply decided, tonight, at the end of all things, to stop pretending.

*Then open,* said the storm. *Open, and be done with being afraid.*

"That's just it." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I'm not afraid of you anymore. I'm afraid of *me*. Of what I'd let you do if I stopped steering. I watched what rage does to a storm, Veyra. I stood in it. I nearly—" The seawall. The Hollow Knights. The white fire she had called up and Renn had talked her down from, *you don't get to avenge us by erasing us.* "I nearly became the thing that burned the world the first time. I won't do it again. Not even for them."

*Then what will you do?*

Sebastian turned and looked at the city one last time—at the towers and the tar-smell and the crooked streets, at the customs house where a certain tired inquisitor was surely writing something true for the first time in his life, at the dark cell where a smuggler was trading secrets to save her, at the whole improbable, stubborn, leaking, arguing, alive sprawl of the only home she had ever chosen.

Then she looked at the sea.

The storm-front had come closer. She could see the lightning inside it now, moving in patterns, almost like writing. Almost like a hand reaching out.

"I'm going to stop running from you," she said. "And I'm going to stop fighting you. And I'm going to walk out to meet you on my own feet, with my own name, and we are going to make a new bargain—you and I, and it will be *mine*, do you understand? Not the priesthood's. Not the Concord's. Not the vessel-in-the-storm. *Mine.*"

The wind went still. The whole coast went still—the sea, the wards, the walking quiet of the Hollow Knights frozen mid-stride on the shingle, everything waiting, the entire world holding its breath around one small woman on a wall.

And far out on the water, at the heart of the storm-front, the clouds began to open—a long seam of green-gold light unzipping from sea to sky, and through it, a voice that was not a voice, vast and old and achingly glad:

*Then come and carry me, little storm. I have waited three hundred years to be held.*

Sebastian Villalobos stepped up onto the seawall's edge, and the first rain of the new storm fell on her upturned face like an answer.
