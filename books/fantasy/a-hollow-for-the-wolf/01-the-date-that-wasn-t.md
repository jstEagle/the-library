## 1. The Date That Wasn't

The moonsteel sang when the wind caught it, which was why Hazel Blackwood had wrapped it in three layers of wool and one of sheep's bladder, and was now regretting none of it less than usual.

"You're a chatty cargo," he told the pack on his back. The wind answered, tearing down the switchbacks of the Thornfell pass with the enthusiasm of something that had been doing this job for ten thousand years and still found it funny. "Yes, thank you. Very helpful."

He'd been over Thornfell in every month the mountain allowed and two it didn't. He knew its moods the way other men knew their mothers': the sullen silences, the sudden tempers, the long gray stretches where nothing happened at all and you learned to be grateful for that. Tonight the pass was in one of its thinking moods — snow falling fine and dry as flour, the sky the color of an old coin, visibility exactly as far as his next bootfall.

Which suited him. Hazel had built his whole life on the principle that being seen was the first step toward being remembered, and being remembered was the first step toward being dead. He had no name on any ledger from here to the coast except variations — H. Black, Hal Wood, once memorably *Hazel Blackwood, tinker, deaf* — and he liked it that way. Anonymity was a craft, like bladesmithing or lying, and after twenty years he considered himself a master of all three.

Moonsteel changed hands for enough silver to buy a small orchard and the loyalty of everyone in it, which is why it traveled wrapped in wool instead of riding openly on some lord's cart. It also sang under moonlight, faintly, a high thin note like a wet finger on a glass rim, and there were things in these mountains that followed song. So Hazel moved by day, slept by night, and kept his campfires to the size of a cupped palm.

By the time the light went he had made the high shelf below Cragwatch — the ruined waystation where the old road-builders had once watered their mules, before anyone remembered who the road-builders had been. It was a broken half-donut of stone facing south, sheltered enough to keep the worst of the wind off, and blessedly empty of everything except a drift of last week's snow and what looked, when he scraped his boot through it, like a corner of flagstone that shouldn't have been there.

Hazel looked at the flagstone the way he looked at all anomalies: with deep professional suspicion and a complete absence of surprise. In his trade, anomalies were either money or trouble, and usually both.

"Hello," he said to it, conversationally, because talking to yourself on a mountain is only madness if nobody answers.

The flagstone said nothing, but the wind shifted, and the snow around its edges melted in a neat ring, as if the stone had been warm recently. As if it had been *kept* warm.

Well. That was new.

He cleared the drift properly. Underneath: a slab fitted so well into the floor of the ruin that unless you knew stone the way Hazel did — the way you know a face — you'd never see the seam. There was no ring, no hinge, no handle. There was a word cut shallow into the surface, in the old oath-script they taught to no one anymore, worn nearly smooth.

Hazel knew maybe forty words of oath-script, most of them smuggled into his head by his mentor along with table manners and a lifelong distrust of people who smiled too readily. He read this one without thinking about it, the way you read a sign on a door.

*Speak, and be let in.*

Now, any sensible man would have put his boot back on, built a fire the size of a cupped palm, eaten cold sausage, and spent the night developing a rich fantasy about the orchard he was going to buy. Hazel stood there for a long moment with the wind worrying the hem of his coat, feeling the brand on his right palm itch — the oath-brand, the old scar shaped like a closed mouth, the one he'd had since he was nine years old and Vess had pressed her thumb against it and said *this is yours now, don't ask me why.*

"Don't be stupid," he advised himself.

Then he said, quietly, in the flat tone he used for haggling, "*Open.*"

The stone sank an inch and slid aside with a sound like a book closing in reverse. Below it: steps. And below the steps, impossibly, warmth — real warmth, rising up out of the mountain like breath, smelling faintly of dust and candle-tallow and something older underneath, something mineral and patient.

Every instinct Hazel owned told him not to go down. He went down anyway, because the other thing his instincts told him, quieter, underneath, in a voice that sounded annoyingly like Vess, was: *you've been walking toward this your whole life, boy, and you know it.*

* * *

The room at the bottom was round, dry, and lit the moment his foot touched the floor — not by torches but by lines in the walls waking up, veins of pale gold light threading through the stone like frost forming in fast motion. A scholar's cell. A hidden library the size of a generous pantry. Shelves cut into the rock, mostly bare. One desk. One chair. And on the desk, centered with the kind of care people give to things that matter, a book.

It was bound in gray leather gone soft with age, and its cover bore a stamped design Hazel recognized from maybe six places in his entire life: a sword crossed with a spoken word, rendered as a spiral. The mark of the swordspeakers. The order that had ended a century ago, that Vess had claimed was extinct, that she had sworn — looking him directly in the eye, which she almost never did — had died out entirely with the fall of the Oathkings.

Hazel picked up the book. It was heavier than it should have been, the way secrets are. The title page read:

*A True and Faithful Account of Things Yet to Come, Being the Prophecies of the Mountain Roads, Set Down in the Year 1103 by the Hand of Sereth Marlowe, Last Chronicler of the Speaking Order.*

One hundred and twelve years ago.

He turned pages. Most of it was the usual prophecy-fare — the kind of thing sold at fairs to farmers with more coins than sense. Kingdoms divided. A winter of iron. A crown that weeps. All vague enough to come true eventually, like weather.

Then, near the middle, a page whose margins were crowded with later annotations in at least three different hands, and the text itself — clean, precise, unhedged:

*In the hundred-and-twelfth year, on the night of the First Snow, a bearer shall cross the high shelf below Cragwatch bearing moonsteel wrapped in wool. His name is Hazel Blackwood. He will shelter where the road-builders drank, and he will find this book, and by finding it he begins.*

Hazel read it twice.

Then a third time, slowly, moving his lips, because somewhere in the second reading his careful, orderly, anonymous life had developed a crack straight down the middle and things were starting to fall out of it.

His name. Not a description, not a riddle, not *a wanderer with a crooked staff* or *one born under the third moon*. His actual, full, government-bothering, ledger-ruining name. *Hazel Blackwood*, set down in ink a hundred and twelve years before his mother ever thought of him, on a page dated — he checked the almanac in his head, checked it again, felt the vertigo arrive like a trapdoor opening — tonight.

The night of the First Snow. High shelf below Cragwatch. Moonsteel wrapped in wool. He looked down at his own pack, at the corner of wool showing where the strap had worked loose, and felt a laugh start somewhere low in his chest and die before it got anywhere near his mouth.

"No," he said, to the room, to the book, to the general arrangement of the universe. "No, I don't think so."

Because here was the thing. Here was the whole thing, laid out plain. Hazel Blackwood had spent twenty years constructing a man who could not be predicted. No fixed address. No true name given to anyone living — not even Vess, though she'd known it anyway, because she'd named him. Every alias rotated on a schedule. Every route varied by dice he carried for exactly that purpose. He had once detoured four days around a flooded valley purely because a fortune-teller in Lowmark had looked at him too long. He was, by any reasonable measure, unpredictable. He was weather. He was smoke.

And a dead chronicler had written him down like a delivery date.

He sat down in the chair, because his knees had opinions, and set the book on the desk with the gentleness you use on something that might bite. The gold lines in the walls pulsed faintly, patiently, like a heartbeat waiting for him to catch up.

"Let's say it's coincidence," he told the room, in his haggling voice. "Let's say some chronicler a century back wrote down every name she could think of and one of them happened to stick. Let's say the date is approximate. Let's say—"

The brand on his palm itched. Hard. And when he looked at it, the old closed-mouth scar had changed: the lines around it had darkened, fresh and sharp, as if re-inked minutes ago. As if the book had signed him, and his own hand had countersigned.

Hazel closed his fist and breathed through his nose until the vertigo shrank from a canyon back to a well. This was the discipline. This was what Vess had actually taught him, under the swordwork and the scripts and the smuggling: *when the world tilts, hold still and count what hasn't changed.* He counted. One: he was alive. Two: he had moonsteel to deliver. Three: whoever had kept this place warm — and someone had, recently, the melted ring upstairs proved it — might come back.

That third one arrived with a cold little click, and Hazel was on his feet with the book under his arm before he finished deciding to move. Because there was one annotation in the margins he hadn't examined closely. He'd taken it for another hand, another reader, faded brown ink scrawled beside the passage naming him:

*He reads this on time. Good. Get him off the mountain before they come. They have always been coming. — V.*

Vess. Her handwriting. He'd have known it blindfolded, drunk, dead — the impatient downstrokes, the way she crossed her letters like she was cutting them.

Except Vess had been dead for six years. He'd burned her body himself, on a headland, in the rain, because she'd asked him to and because she'd earned it. And the ink beside a hundred-year-old prophecy was barely dry.

Above him, faintly, drifting down through the open seam in the floor: the sound of boots on stone. Many boots. Descending the pass in perfect, unhurried unison, the way men walk when they are not searching for something because they already know exactly where it is.

Hazel snuffed the gold light in the walls with a word he hadn't known he knew, took the book, and went up the stairs toward the snow and the dark and whatever had been sent, a hundred years early, to collect him.
