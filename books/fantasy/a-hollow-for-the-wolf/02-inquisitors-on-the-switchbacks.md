## 2. Inquisitors on the Switchbacks

The trouble with a prophecy naming you for tonight, Hazel decided somewhere around the fourth switchback, was that it made tonight feel like an appointment. He had spent thirty-one years cultivating the fine art of being nobody — the kind of man innkeepers forgot by morning, whose face dissolved in memory like sugar in rain. And now some century-old book had gone and put his name on a date, and suddenly every rock and raven on this mountain seemed to know it too.

He'd left the waystation before dawn, moving fast, the moonsteel ingots wrapped in oilcloth and slung against his spine where they rode like a cold, patient passenger. The pass descended through a series of switchbacks carved into the mountain's western face — eighteen hairpin turns, each one narrower than the last, dropping toward the pine shadow below. Smugglers called it the Ladder. Honest folk didn't use it at all.

Hazel knew the Ladder the way he knew his own hands. He knew which turns held ice until noon, which ones crumbled at the outer edge, which boulder offered cover from watchers above. He had run this route forty times without so much as startling a marmot.

Today, the marmots were gone.

That should have been his first warning. Marmots were the mountain's gossip — they whistled at everything, hawks, hikers, weather. A silent switchback was a switchback holding its breath.

His second warning came as a smell: cold iron and lamp oil, threaded with something sweeter, something that reminded him of the waystation's book. Old paper and old power.

Hazel crouched behind the third-turn boulder and listened. Below him, faint but unmistakable, came voices. Not smuggler voices — smugglers argued about tolls and grumbled about their knees. These voices were precise. Rehearsed. The kind of voices men used when they'd been told exactly what to say and were saying it in unison because they'd been promised something for obedience.

*"...the Swordspeaker comes down the Ladder..."*

He heard it plainly, drifting up through the thin mountain air, and felt the words land in his chest like a dropped stone.

They weren't looking for a smuggler. They weren't even looking for *him*, not really — not Hazel Blackwood, carrier of moonsteel, keeper of three false names and one true one. They were looking for a title. *The Swordspeaker.* As if they'd rehearsed for a role, cast an actor, waited out a hundred-year performance, and now the marquee had finally lit up with his name on it.

"Come down," one of them said, softly, almost gently, to no one visible. "We've been waiting."

Hazel pressed his back to the cold stone and did the arithmetic of fear. Six of them, by the sound — six sets of boots on gravel, six lanterns he could smell but not yet see. Crown inquisitors wore grey and carried instruments instead of swords: pincers, vials, little brass wheels for measuring oaths. He'd seen them twice in his life, both times from a great distance, both times running.

He was not at a great distance now. He was directly above them, on a path eighteen inches wide, with a drop on one side and a wall on the other, carrying sixty pounds of metal that would kill him if he fell and kill him faster if they caught him.

There was no third option except down, through them, and that was the worst option of all.

Except.

Except that his palm had been itching since the waystation. Not the brand itself — the oath-brand under his skin, the pale spiral scar he'd carried since Vess pressed her thumb and her will into him at fourteen — but around it, beneath it, a warmth like a coal waking. All night it had pulsed when he touched the prophecy book. Now, hearing his title spoken aloud by strangers, it burned.

He flexed his hand. The spiral glowed faintly, the way iron glows just before it remembers being fire.

"Oh no," Hazel said quietly, to himself, to the mountain, to whatever was listening. "No, no. We don't do that. We are a quiet profession. Quiet is the whole business model."

The warmth ignored him entirely.

Below, the voices stopped. Boots shifted on gravel. One of them said, clearly enough to carry: "He's here. Turn nine. Above us."

Turn nine. Which was, Hazel noted with the detached interest of a man watching someone else's disaster, exactly where he was crouched.

How did they — he hadn't made a sound. He'd been silent as frost. Unless the brand had been shouting the whole time, glowing through stone like a lantern under a blanket, announcing him to anyone with the instruments to hear it. Which they had. Of course they had. That was rather the point of inquisitors.

Six lanterns swung into view below, climbing. Grey coats. Brass collars. And at their head, a tall figure with a face like a closed ledger — Inquisitor Corvane, though Hazel didn't know the name yet, wouldn't learn it until later, and would spend a long time wishing he never had.

Corvane looked up at the empty path above him and smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man who has read a prophecy and found it *convenient*.

"Swordspeaker," Corvane said, upward, into the air, confident the air would answer. "A hundred years we have kept your seat. A hundred years your chair has stood empty in the Hall of Oaths, dusted weekly, waiting. Come down. Be weighed. Be *known*."

Every instinct Hazel owned screamed *run*. Up was impossible — the Ladder only went down from here, and the cliff face above was sheer. Sideways was a two-hundred-foot fall onto rocks that would apologize to no one. Down was them.

So Hazel Blackwood, professional coward, master of the discreet exit, drew his sword.

Not because he wanted to. His hand simply went to the hilt the way a drowning man's hand goes to a rope, and the blade came free of its sheath with a sound like a held breath released, and the oath-brand on his palm flared white-hot, and —

— and the words came.

Not his words. He hadn't chosen any. But his mouth opened and his voice arrived from somewhere deeper than decision, older than training, and what came out was:

*"Steel remembers what flesh forgets."*

The sword sang.

There is no honest way to describe what happened next except that the world briefly rearranged itself around a sentence. The blade in Hazel's hand rang like a struck bell, and the ring wasn't sound exactly — it was *weight*, a pressure that rolled down the switchback ahead of him, and the six inquisitors below staggered as if a wave had hit them, lanterns swinging wild, two of them dropping to one knee on the narrow path.

Hazel stared at his own arm, which was extended in a guard position he had never learned, angled in a way that made no sense for fencing and perfect sense for something else. Something older. Something that lived in the steel and had apparently been waiting for permission for seventeen years.

"What," he whispered, "was that."

The warmth in his palm answered with another pulse, and another word rose in his throat like water finding a crack:

*"Stand."*

He hadn't meant to say it. He was fairly sure he'd meant to say something more along the lines of *retreat* or possibly *scream*. But the word left him, and it landed, and the mountain landed with it — the gravel under his boots locked solid, the loose scree that had been trickling off the path edge all morning went still mid-fall, hanging in the air like a photograph of itself. For three full seconds, nothing on turn nine moved. Not dust. Not wind. Not Hazel's own heartbeat, which paused out of what he could only assume was politeness.

Then everything resumed, and Hazel understood, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with heights, that he had just commanded a mountainside. And the mountainside had *obeyed*.

"That's new," he told the boulder beside him. The boulder declined to comment.

Corvane recovered first. Inquisitors always did; doubt was not in their job description. He climbed the last few feet to turn nine with his brass instruments chiming, and got his first clear look at Hazel — a dusty man in a worn brown coat, sword drawn, glowing faintly in the palm, wearing the expression of someone who has just knocked over a shelf in a shop and is hoping nobody noticed.

"You're not what I expected," Corvane said. There was disappointment in it, genuine and almost human. "The texts describe a giant. A storm-caller. A man whose voice splits stone."

"The texts," Hazel said, "sound exhausting."

Something flickered across the inquisitor's ledger-face — not amusement, inquisitors weren't built for it, but the ghost of a place amusement might once have lived. Then he raised his right hand, and the brass wheel at his wrist began to spin, and Hazel felt the spinning in his teeth, in the brand, in the marrow of him.

"Weighing-oath," Corvane announced, formally, the way a herald announces a coronation. "Swordspeaker, by authority of the crown and the Hall of Oaths, I call your bond to account. Speak your binding word or be bound anew — to *us*."

And there it was. The trap inside the trap. Hazel knew, in the way he knew smuggling routes and bad weather and which innkeepers watered the ale, that this was the real ambush. Not six men on a path. A question with no safe answer. Refuse the weighing and be taken by force. Accept it and speak his binding word aloud — the word Vess had sealed into him, the word that tied his gift to his silence — and the moment it left his lips in front of a Hall witness, it would belong to the crown. They'd own his oath. They'd own *him*. A hundred years of dusted chairs and weekly dusting, all of it waiting for exactly this transaction.

Clever. Genuinely clever. Hazel felt a brief, involuntary respect, the kind one thief feels for another's craftsmanship.

Then his palm burned again, and the words came before he chose them, and they were not the words Corvane wanted.

*"My oath was given to the mountain and the moon. You hold neither."*

It came out of him like a river breaking a dam — smooth, ringing, absolute — and Hazel heard his own voice say things he had never learned, in rhythms he had never studied, and watched the brass wheel at Corvane's wrist shudder, stutter, and crack clean in half. The pieces fell onto the path and lay there shining, dead.

The other five inquisitors went very still. Stillness, Hazel was learning, was contagious among people whose entire profession rested on instruments working.

Corvane looked down at his broken wheel. Then up at Hazel. Then down again, longer, as if hoping the wheel would reconsider.

"This is not possible," he said finally, with the calm conviction of a man reporting a malfunction he intends to have fixed. "The weighing-oath has not failed in ninety years. No unregistered speaker can refuse it. The *texts*—"

"I'm beginning to think," Hazel said, edging sideways along the path, keeping the boulder between them, "that whoever wrote those texts met a different Swordspeaker. Or wrote them before something changed. Or—" and here the thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome, glowing, "—or wrote them a hundred years ago about *tonight*, and everyone involved has been reading the wrong end of the story."

He hadn't meant to say that either. It had simply come out, plain and conversational, and Corvane flinched as if Hazel had read it off a page in front of him. Which, in a sense, he supposed he had. The brand knew things. The brand had opinions. The brand, Hazel was rapidly concluding, was the single worst business partner he had ever acquired, and he had once partnered with a man who paid tolls in buttons.

"Take him," Corvane said quietly. "Alive. The crown does not waste what it has waited a century for."

Two inquisitors moved to flank. Their instruments woke — small brass rods that hummed, designed to pin an oath-bearer's speech in his throat, to make words impossible. Hazel felt the hum reach him and felt, simultaneously, the brand rise to meet it like a dog hearing a whistle.

And then the strangest moment of the whole strange morning occurred, and Hazel would remember it for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

One of the inquisitors — young, freckled, nervous, the kind who still polished his collar — hesitated. Just for a breath. Just long enough to glance at Hazel's face instead of his instruments. And in that glance Hazel saw something he had not expected to see on this mountain: recognition. Not of his face — nobody knew his face — but of the *brand*. The spiral. This boy had one too. Faint, half-healed, on the inside of his wrist, hidden under the brass cuff. An oath-taker, conscripted, collared, pointed at strangers with a gift he probably didn't understand either.

Their eyes met for half a second.

Hazel didn't speak. He didn't need to. The brand spoke for him, a pulse, a warmth, a single wordless push that crossed the space between them like heat off a stove — and the young inquisitor's humming rod dipped, just slightly, just for a moment, aimed at gravel instead of throat.

In that moment, Hazel cut past them.

He didn't fight. Fighting six trained inquisitors on an eighteen-inch ledge was a plan with a very short life expectancy. Instead he did the thing smugglers do best: he moved like smoke. Down the inner edge, where the wall met the path, through the gap the hesitant boy had opened without quite meaning to, past lanterns and grey coats and one broken brass wheel, close enough to smell the lamp oil on their cloaks — and gone, onto turn ten, then eleven, running, the moonsteel bouncing against his spine like a cold heart.

Behind him, shouts. Behind him, Corvane's voice rising over the gravel, furious now, no longer rehearsed: *"The Swordspeaker escapes! Seal the Ladder! Seal the pass!"*

Ahead of him, the path dropped through pine shadow toward the valley road, and Hazel ran it with his lungs burning and his mind doing something it had never done before: not planning, not scheming, not calculating tolls and timings, but *listening*. Listening to the brand. Listening to the mountain. Listening to the strange, humming, ancient thing that had woken in his hand and was now, apparently, driving.

Because here was the thought that had lodged in him on turn nine and would not come loose, the thought that curdled his fear slowly, grain by grain, into something else — something hotter and stranger and much more dangerous:

The words hadn't come from nowhere.

Vess had taught him oathspeech the way you teach a child table manners — a phrase at a time, carefully, with rules. *Never speak in anger. Never speak in haste. Never let the steel choose for you.* She'd drilled him until the discipline was bone-deep, and then she'd died, and he'd kept the discipline like a keepsake, mostly unused, mostly unnecessary for a life of hauling moonsteel past bored toll-keepers.

But these words today — *steel remembers*, *stand*, *my oath was given to the mountain and the moon* — these were not Vess's phrases. He knew her whole lexicon the way he knew his own pockets. These were different. Older. Deeper. Words with the mountain's accent on them, words that fit the Ladder like a key fits a lock, words that sounded — and this was the part that made his scalp tighten — like they belonged to the same family as the rite that had crowned a king. Like they'd all come from one original speech, one first binding, one source, and Vess had only ever taught him the shallow end of it.

Which meant the deep end existed. Which meant his brand had always known the way down to it. Which meant the prophecy book in his pack, dated to tonight, naming him by name, and the inquisitors on the switchbacks, waiting a hundred years, and the burning in his palm, were not three separate disasters.

They were one disaster. Coordinated. Scheduled. *Expected.*

By whom?

Hazel crested the tree line's edge and paused in the shadow of a leaning pine, chest heaving, and looked back up the Ladder. Far above, tiny with distance, lantern-light crawled down the switchbacks like slow amber ants. They were sealing the pass. They would seal the valley road after it. Every road down from Thornfell would have grey coats on it by dusk.

Which left, as any smuggler worth his salt could tell you, exactly one route.

Nobody used the deep roads. Nobody legal, anyway. The old mining tunnels ran under the mountain's roots like veins under skin — unmapped, unstable, humming in places with a resonance no miner had ever explained and all miners refused to discuss. Smugglers used the upper galleries sometimes, the shallow ones, quick dark shortcuts. But the deep roads, the true ones, the ones that went down and down into the dark where the air tasted of stone and time — those were legend. Those were stories smugglers told each other over bad ale, about the tunnels that sang, about the heat that came from nowhere, about the old days when something vast had lived under Thornfell and the mountain had been warm.

Hazel stood in the pine shadow, catching his breath, and became aware that his palm was burning again. Steadily now. Purposefully. Like a compass needle that had finally found north and intended to keep pointing there.

Down. The warmth pulled *down*. Toward the deep roads. Toward the singing tunnels. Toward the heat with no source.

"No," Hazel said aloud, out of habit, because saying *no* to impulses was most of his personality. "Absolutely not. The deep roads are a myth. The deep roads are how smugglers die alone in the dark and become cautionary tales. I am a cautionary tale *avoider*. It's my core competency."

The brand pulsed. Patiently. The way a parent waits out a child insisting there are no vegetables in the soup.

And then, faintly, from far below — from under the mountain, under the roots, under the stone — Hazel heard it. Not with his ears. With the brand. With the burning spiral that had spent all morning answering questions he hadn't asked.

A hum. Deep, slow, vast, patient beyond all human patience. A note held so long it had become part of the mountain, or the mountain had become part of it; there was no telling which anymore.

Dragon-song. Or the memory of it. Or the hollow where it used to live.

Hazel Blackwood, last oath-bound swordspeaker, carrier of moonsteel, keeper of careful anonymity, stood in the pine shadow with six inquisitors above him, a prophecy in his pack, a king-shaped catastrophe ahead of him, and a dying song underneath him — and realized, with the particular clarity that arrives only when every exit has already closed, that he was not being hunted so much as *herded*.

Everyone on this mountain — the inquisitors, the prophecy, the brand, the mountain itself — wanted him in the same place. And it was straight down.

"Well," he muttered, shouldering his pack, stepping off the honest road onto the smugglers' trail that wound toward the old mine mouths. "When everyone agrees, it's usually a trap. But I've escaped traps before."

The brand pulsed once, warmly, almost fondly.

"But never one this big," he added honestly, and started walking down.

* * *

An hour later, standing at the rusted gate of the Delve-mouth mine with the sun going copper behind the peaks, Hazel allowed himself one last look at the sky. Somewhere above, sealed passes. Somewhere ahead, singing dark. Somewhere in his pack, a book that knew his name better than he did.

He touched the brand once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was friendly.

"If you get me killed," he told it, "I want it noted that I complained in advance. Extensively. In writing, ideally."

The spiral glowed, and for the first time all day, the warmth felt less like an order and more like an answer — and the answer, translated out of whatever language coals speak, was roughly this:

*You were always going down here. The book only named the date.*

Hazel pushed open the mine gate, and the mountain swallowed him, and the song rose up to meet him like an old friend who'd been waiting a very long time — which, he was beginning to suspect, it had.
