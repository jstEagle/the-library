## 3. What the Oath Remembers

Hazel woke at dawn in a smuggler's cache he'd have sworn on his mother's grave didn't exist until yesterday.

That was the trouble with the last two days generally. Things kept existing that hadn't existed before — prophecies with his name in them, inquisitors who knew titles he'd never worn, and now, apparently, a hollow behind a waterfall on the north face of Raven's Tooth that he had used a dozen times and never once remembered being this *comfortable*. There was a bedroll. There was a tin of tea. There was, absurdly, a small clay stove, dry-stacked from river stones, with a flue that drew perfectly.

He hadn't built any of it. He was almost sure.

"You're showing off," he told the brand on his palm.

The spiral — the oath-brand, burned into him at seventeen by a woman who'd apologized the whole time she did it — pulsed once, warm and unbothered, like a cat being accused of something it had absolutely done.

Hazel sat up, winced at everything, and took stock. Two days ago he'd been Hazel Blackwood, smuggler of moonsteel and other people's secrets, a man whose entire professional identity rested on being forgettable. Then a book written a hundred years before his birth had named him for a specific Tuesday, and crown inquisitors had ambushed him on the switchbacks calling him *the Swordspeaker* like they'd been rehearsing the introduction since before his grandfather learned to walk.

And when they'd come at him with steel and binding-chains, the words had come out of his mouth before he'd chosen them — old words, heavy words, words that had bent three swords into question marks and sent an inquisitor's oath-shackles falling open like a gift box.

He'd run. Running was the part he was good at. But you could only run so far before your own hand started asking questions.

He held his palm up to the grey light leaking through the waterfall curtain.

The brand was a spiral, coiled tight as a sleeping adder, no wider than a coin. Every oath-taker had one somewhere — a knuckle, a collarbone, the back of the neck. Most brands were shallow things. A smuggler's brand let you swear a cargo honest. A soldier's brand let you hold a shield-wall line past the point where legs stopped agreeing to it. Small oaths. Practical oaths. The kind of magic that kept the wheels greased and nobody wrote songs about.

Hazel's had never been small. He'd just spent eleven years pretending it was.

*Say the word,* Vess had told him, the night she branded him. *Only the word. Never ask what else comes with it.*

He turned his hand slowly in the light. And for the first time, instead of looking at the brand, he looked *into* it — the way you'd finally stop avoiding eye contact with someone you'd been sharing a house with for a decade.

The spiral wasn't one line. It was many. Dozens of fine threads wound together, hair-thin, each one etched with script too small and too old to read — except that he *could* read it, suddenly, the way you understand a language in dreams. Not with his eyes. With the oath itself, which had apparently been sitting inside him all these years like a tenant who'd never unpacked, waiting for him to notice the walls were covered in writing.

And the writing was familiar.

Not familiar the way a face is familiar. Familiar the way a *room* is familiar — the specific dread of recognizing a place you've only seen described, in a story you were told as a child, right before the bad thing happened in it.

Because Hazel knew this script. Everyone in Thornfell knew it, the way everyone knows the shape of a coin without ever reading the mint-mark. It was coronation script. Binding script. The formal hand of the Rite of Crowning, the one every schoolchild copied out on festival days, the one that had put the iron-and-gold circlet of Aldric the Unifier onto Aldric the Unifier's head two hundred years ago —

No. Not two hundred. The book had said a century. The book had been wrong, or the book had been *edited*, and either way —

Hazel made himself look closer.

The threads of his brand weren't copies of the coronation rite. They were *fragments* of it. Phrases torn out of the whole, the way you might tear pages from a songbook — and not the pretty verses, either. These were the load-bearing lines. The clauses that did the work. He recognized the cadence of them now, the terrible grammar of a bargain: *that which is bound shall bind; that which holds shall be held; the fire given shall be fire owed.*

Fire given shall be fire owed.

His mouth had gone dry. He poured tea anyway, because Vess had taught him that panic and dehydration were a bad combination, and drank it scalding, because he deserved small punishments.

"Vess," he said aloud, to the empty cache, in the tone of a man addressing a ghost who had some explaining to do. "What exactly did you burn into me?"

* * *

She'd been drunk, the night of the branding. That was the thing he'd never let himself think about clearly — Vess, who could drink any smuggler in Thornfell under the table and still count change to the copper, had been *drunk*, properly, tragically drunk, the night she'd taken his hand in the back room of the Drowned Lantern and heated the iron in the hearth until it glowed the color of an apology.

"You can still say no," she'd said. Fourth time she'd said it. Her hands had been steady on the iron, which was how he should have known something was wrong. Vess's hands were never steady unless she was making them be.

"I said yes four times already."

"And I'm giving you a fifth, because I like round numbers." She'd laughed, but it had come out cracked down the middle. "Hazel. Listen to me. What I'm putting in you isn't a smuggler's brand. It isn't anything with a name the guilds would recognize. If anyone ever asks — and someone will, someday, I've seen it — you tell them it's a hedge-witch's ward against fever. Boring. Forgettable. You're good at forgettable."

"You say the most romantic things."

"Hazel." And her voice had changed then, gone quiet in a way that had reached through even his nineteen-year-old invulnerability, and he'd looked up from the iron into her face and seen something he'd never seen there before and never saw again after: fear. Real fear, worn openly, like a scar she'd stopped bothering to hide. "This is going to hurt more than it should. And someday it's going to matter more than either of us wants. When that day comes, don't fight what the words want to be. Fight *for* them. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I'm too drunk to explain it better than that."

Then she'd pressed the iron to his palm, and he'd screamed, and she'd held his wrist through all of it and wept without making a single sound, which he'd assumed at the time was grief for hurting him.

Sitting in the cache twenty years later, watching steam curl off his tea, Hazel understood that she hadn't been crying for his hand.

She'd been crying for what she knew was coming down the road toward him. Possibly for what she herself had done to put it there.

* * *

He laid the pieces out on the bedroll like contraband at a checkpoint, because arranging facts in rows was the closest thing he had to prayer.

Piece one: the prophecy book, a century old, naming him for tonight — written in a hand he'd have known anywhere, because he'd forged documents *from* that hand for years. Vess's hand.

Piece two: the inquisitors, who hadn't hunted him like prey. They'd *received* him, like guests expected at a wedding a hundred years in the planning. *The Swordspeaker.* A title with dust on it. A title from the founding wars, from the age when kings were crowned with spoken oaths and dragons still answered letters.

Piece three: the brand. Fragments of the Rite of Crowning, living in his palm like splinters of a broken mirror, each shard still reflecting something.

Piece four — and this was the one he'd been circling all morning, the way your tongue circles a cracked tooth — the coronation rite itself. Every child in the Nine Valleys learned its history. Aldric the Unifier had ended the Dragon Wars not with a sword but with a *bargain*: the great wyrms had given their fire to the new crown, freely given, sworn in binding script, and in exchange the kingdoms would keep the peace for a thousand years. The fire lived in the crown. The crown lived on the king. Peace, forever, amortized.

Everyone knew the story. Nobody asked what happened to a bargain when one side stopped paying attention.

But Hazel had spent two days reading a prophecy book that read him back, and he'd felt, in the tunnels under Raven's Tooth, a song in the stone that sounded less like music and more like *grief* — long, slow, patient grief, the kind that takes centuries to finish a verse. And he'd watched inquisitors move with a smoothness that was almost right and not quite, like puppets whose puppeteer had studied human beings from a distance, through glass.

*That which is bound shall bind. That which holds shall be held. The fire given shall be fire owed.*

"Oh," Hazel said quietly, to the tea, to the waterfall, to the spiral glowing steadily in his palm like a coal that had finally decided to introduce itself. "Oh, that's not good."

Because here was the shape of it, the ugly geometry underneath: his gift and the king's crown came from the same root. Same rite. Same script. Same ancient bargain, split like a loaf — half baked into the circlet of Aldric's line, half torn out and hidden in the palm of a smuggler's brat from Thornfell, branded in by a drunken woman who wept while she did it.

Which meant the words that answered in his mouth weren't his. They were *leftovers*. Echoes of the oldest oath in the kingdom, still doing their work, still holding up their half of a deal struck before his grandmother's grandmother was born.

And if the crown's half of the rite had gone wrong — if whatever wore Aldric's face in the capital was the result of a binding curdling into a haunting — then his half was not a gift at all.

It was a *counterweight*. Or a key. Or a fuse.

"That's three options," he told the brand, "and I hate all of them."

The spiral warmed, gently, in a way he was beginning to learn meant *yes, and also there's a fourth option and you won't like it either.*

"Fantastic."

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing the only sensible thing, which was packing to run somewhere very far away. Salt pork. The good rope. The moonsteel he'd abandoned on the pass — gone, mourned, a month's profit feeding crows — and in its place, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his pack, the prophecy book, because apparently he was the kind of idiot who carried the cursed evidence *with* him rather than dropping it in a river like a man with survival instincts.

He was cinching the last buckle when he heard the voices.

Not inquisitors. Smugglers — two of them, upstream along the falls, moving fast and low, the particular fast-low of people carrying something illegal and frightened of it. Hazel melted into the cache's shadow out of pure habit and would have stayed there, invisible, forgotten, professionally forgettable, except that one of them spoke, and the words drifted through the roar of the water and caught in his ear like a fishhook:

"—swear it, the whole court's gone strange. My cousin's girl serves in the chroniclers' hall, and she says the king signed forty writs in one sitting and his hand never once — never once — paused for ink. And Sera Vane's been pulled from the archives, *arrested*, or near enough, for asking why the king's shadow—"

The rest was lost under the falls.

Hazel stood very still in the dark of a hiding place he hadn't built, in a body carrying fragments of a king-making rite, and thought about a chronicler named Sera Vane who had apparently gotten close enough to the truth to be dangerous, and was now alone in the teeth of the thing itself.

The brand pulped warm against his palm. Not an order. An answer. The same answer it had been giving him since the mine gate, patient as geology:

*You were always going.*

"Fine," Hazel said, to the spiral, to Vess's ghost, to the century-old book in his pack. "Fine. But I want it noted — extensively, in writing — that I complained in advance."

Somewhere far below, through miles of stone, the dragon-song rose a half-tone, and it sounded, unmistakably, like laughter.

He shouldered his pack and started down.
