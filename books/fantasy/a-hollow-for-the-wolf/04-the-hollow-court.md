## 4. The Hollow Court

Sera Vane had been a chronicler long enough to know that history did not live in battles or coronations. It lived in the margins — in the ink a clerk ran out of mid-sentence, in the wine stain shaped like a duck on the treaty of Marrow's Ford, in the way a king's hand trembled on the day he signed away a province and didn't tremble at all.

She had come to the capital eleven years ago with one trunk, two letters of recommendation, and a firm belief that the truth was simply a matter of writing things down before anyone could make them prettier. She still believed that. It was getting harder to practice.

The morning of the ninth of Embermonth began, as most mornings did, with her arguing with the Master of Records about candle allowances.

"I'm not asking for a chandelier," Sera said, holding up the requisition form like a warrant. "I'm asking for candles. Three of them. The archive is so dark I've started transcribing 'the' as 'the,' which sounds fine until you realize one of them is supposed to be 'then.'"

"Vane." Master Corvel did not look up from his ledger. He was a man who had achieved perfect harmony with his ledger; they would likely be buried together. "Candles are allocated by rank."

"Then allocate me some rank."

"You have exactly as much rank as your handwriting deserves, and I've seen your handwriting."

"It's *legible*, which is more than can be said for the treasury's accounts, and I'd wager my three candles that's deliberate—"

"*Vane.*"

She went. She always went, eventually, but she had learned that the going mattered less than the fact that she made him say her name twice. A small rebellion. The kind that kept a person human.

It was on her way back from this defeat, crossing the Long Gallery with her arms full of folios she had no business carrying herself, that she saw the king sign the orders.

* * *

The Gallery of Kings ran the length of the palace's east wing, all pale stone and tall windows, hung with portraits of every monarch since the Founding. Courtiers drifted through it the way fish drift through water — present, moving, thinking nothing. At its far end stood the signing table, where His Majesty took petitions three mornings a week, because Aldric the Second believed a king should be seen doing the work of the crown even when the work was only ink.

Sera had watched him sign perhaps two hundred documents over the years. She knew his signature the way she knew her own: the confident downstroke of the *A*, the little flourish at the end of the *c* that he'd once told her, laughing, was "the only vanity I permit myself."

Today there was no flourish.

She stopped. Not dramatically — she was too well-trained for drama — but she stopped, and shifted the folios into one arm, and watched.

The king sat at the table with a secretary hovering at his shoulder and a stack of orders before him. He was signing them. That was all. Signing them the way a mill wheel turns, the way rain falls, without pause, without thought, each signature identical to the last down to the width of the strokes. No flourish. No hesitation. No small human stumbles — no moment where the pen skipped and he pressed harder to recover, no moment where he paused to blow on wet ink because he was left-handed and smudged everything if he didn't.

Aldric the Second had been left-handed for thirty years. He had complained about it cheerfully and often. Today the hand that moved across the parchment was smooth, unhurried, and — she squinted — *right-handed.*

She told herself she was seeing it wrong. Distance, light, the angle. She walked closer, pretending great interest in the portrait of Aldric the First, who gazed down at her with the painted disapproval of a man who had conquered three duchies and never once smiled about it.

From ten feet, it was unmistakable. Right hand. Perfect script. And the signatures were not merely similar. They were *identical.* Every one. As though the same instant of writing had been pressed onto page after page after page, a seal rather than a signature.

"Chronicler Vane."

She startled badly enough that a folio slid from her arm. The secretary had crossed to her — a thin, gray man named Pell whose face suggested he had been assembled from leftover secretaries. He picked up the folio and handed it back with a bow that was correct in every particular.

"You're staring," Pell said pleasantly.

"I'm admiring the portrait. Aldric the First. There's something in the brushwork around the eyes—" She was good at this. Eleven years of being ignored had made her excellent at sounding boring. "Is His Majesty well? He seems... efficient today."

"The King is in excellent health." Pell said it the way a bell says its note — instantly, completely, with nothing behind it. "Thank you for your concern."

"I wasn't concerned. Chroniclers don't get to be concerned. We just write things down."

Something flickered across Pell's face then — not expression, exactly. More like the memory of where an expression used to go. "Then write down that the King is well," he said, and returned to the table.

Sera stood among the dead kings and watched the living one finish his stack. Forty-one orders. She counted, because counting was what she did instead of screaming. Forty-one identical signatures, and not once — not once — did the king look up, or shift his weight, or do any of the hundred small restless things that bodies do when they've been sitting too long.

When the last order was signed, he rose. Smoothly. Too smoothly, like a puppeteer showing off. And as he turned toward the door, the morning sun came through the tall windows and threw his shadow long across the floor —

— and the shadow arrived late.

Half a second. Perhaps less. Perhaps she blinked. But Sera would swear on every archive she loved that the king turned first and his shadow followed after, catching up to him like a servant hurrying to keep pace, and just before it settled into place she saw — or thought she saw, and would spend many sleepless nights renegotiating that *thought* — that the shadow's shape was wrong. Broader at the shoulders. Crowned differently. Wearing, for half a heartbeat, the silhouette of something that was not a man.

Then it was only a shadow again, obedient and ordinary, and the king was gone through the far doors, and the courtiers flowed on, and somewhere a page laughed at a joke, and the world insisted on being normal.

* * *

That afternoon she did what she always did with things that frightened her: she looked them up.

The Royal Archive occupied three floors beneath the west wing, a cathedral of shelves that smelled of dust, leather, and the particular melancholy of things written by people now dead. Sera had her own corner down there, a desk inherited from a chronicler named Ollis who had died mid-sentence in 812 and whose last unfinished word — "*wherefore*" — she had framed, because it seemed rude to let it go to waste.

She pulled the signature ledgers. Every royal decree was copied into the permanent record, and Sera had spent enough years among them to read the kings like weather.

Aldric the First: bold, slashing script, deteriorating beautifully into drunken loops in his final decade. Regent Maren: cramped and apologetic, as though apologizing to the paper for existing. Aldric the Second, year one: the confident hand, the flourish on the *c*, the leftward slant of a left-handed man.

Year by year, reign by reign, she traced it forward. And here was the thing — the thing that made her sit very still in the dark with her hands flat on the desk like a woman steadying a table in an earthquake:

The slant changed.

Slowly. So slowly no one could ever have noticed in a single year, or five, or ten. But the leftward lean of a left-handed man had been straightening, degree by degree, like a ship quietly coming about, until eight years ago the script went perfectly vertical, and six years ago it began leaning right, and the flourish on the *c* — his one permitted vanity — shrank and simplified and vanished entirely sometime around the winter of 819.

And the last four years of entries were identical. Not similar. *Identical.* She laid two pages from different years side by side under her magnifying glass and could not find a single hair's difference in the pressure, the spacing, the angle of entry and exit of every stroke. Ink varied. Paper varied. The hand did not vary at all.

People changed. Even kings. Especially kings. Hands aged, moods wandered, bad nights showed up in the ink whether you wanted them to or not. A signature that never changed across four years was not discipline.

It was a mask that had stopped bothering to be a face.

"Well," Sera said aloud, to the empty archive, because saying it aloud made it slightly less enormous. "Well, that's fine. That's absolutely fine."

Her voice echoed somewhere between the shelves and came back sounding smaller than she'd sent it.

* * *

She tried, over the following week, to be reasonable about it.

Reasonable explanation one: she was tired. Chroniclers were tired the way wells are wet. She had been reading old handwriting until her eyes saw forgery in everything; she had once accused a sandwich of being haunted.

Reasonable explanation two: the king had recovered from an illness and retrained his hand. Illnesses happened. People retrained their hands. Except that no illness appeared in the court physicians' records — she checked, twice — and no one retrained their hand into *perfection*, because perfection was not a thing hands did. Hands were honest. That was the whole value of hands.

Reasonable explanation three, which arrived at three in the morning and sat on the edge of her bed like a guest who did not remove his boots: the shadow. The right hand. The forty-one signatures. The way Pell had said "the King is in excellent health" with the cadence of a man reciting something he'd been given, whole, from elsewhere.

She got up. She lit her one allotted candle — plus two she had liberated from a supply closet, because principles were for people with better lighting — and she sat at her desk and opened the book she had been avoiding opening, the one every chronicler avoided opening, the *Annals of the Binding*, the official account of how King Aldric the First had ended the Dragon Wars.

Every child knew the story. The great dragon lords had burned the old kingdoms, and Aldric the First had forged the pact that bound their fire into the crown, ending the wars and founding the dynasty. Fire for peace. The dragons dwindled; the kingdom flourished. Simple. Clean. The kind of story that made a good tapestry.

But Sera had read the original sources, the ones predating the official account, and the original sources were strange. They spoke of a binding not upon the dragons but *through* them — of fire drawn off "lest the vessel crack," of a king who gave something of himself to hold the pact shut, of a price paid "in perpetuity, unto the last ember." One fragment, author unknown, contained a single line that had bothered her for years and now, at three in the morning with her candle guttering, bothered her in an entirely new register:

*What is crowned must be hollow, else the crown will fill it.*

She wrote the line out in her own hand, on a fresh page, and beneath it she wrote the facts, in order, because facts in order were the only prayer she knew:

*The king signs with the wrong hand.*
*The signature has not changed in four years.*
*His shadow moves late.*
*I am the only person in this palace who appears to have noticed.*

She looked at that last line for a long time.

"That's not true," she said finally. "Someone else has noticed. Someone always notices. They're just — quieter about it than me. Or smarter. Or they noticed and decided that being alive was a hobby they enjoyed."

Because here was the arithmetic she kept arriving at and kept refusing to accept: if she was wrong, she was a fool. If she was right, she was a witness. And witnesses, in her considerable professional experience of reading about them, had historically short bibliographies.

The candle guttered. Outside, the palace slept its careful sleep, and somewhere above her the king — or the thing that signed for the king — sat in the dark doing whatever hollow things did between performances.

Sera blew out the flame, and in the sudden black she made the decision the way she made all her decisions: quickly, irreversibly, and with immediate regret followed by stubbornness.

She was going to prove it. Properly. With documentation. If the crown had been stolen, she would find the theft in the records, because everything ended up in records eventually — every crime, every lie, every empire. Ink remembered what men forgot. It was the entire reason she had a job.

She just needed access to the restricted vaults. The ones under the Signet Office. The ones that required the king's own seal to enter.

Which meant she needed to get close to the thing wearing the crown, and ask it, politely, for permission to look for the proof of what it was.

"Oh, I've made a terrible decision," Sera informed the darkness, with feeling.

The darkness, characteristically, declined to argue.

* * *

Three days later, the summons came.

Not for her — that was the strange part, the part that turned her blood to well-water. The summons came for the archive itself: a royal writ ordering a full inventory of the restricted collections, "in preparation for the King's historical commission," to begin within the week. Master Corvel received it with the delight of a man handed a promotion he hadn't earned, and immediately delegated the actual work downward, as was tradition.

To Sera. Naturally to Sera. She was, after all, the one with the legible handwriting.

She read the writ twice, standing in Corvel's office while he preened. The seal at its foot was flawless — the crowned dragon of House Aldric, every scale crisp, every line exact. She had catalogued four hundred impressions of that seal over her career. This one was perfect.

No seal was perfect. Wax cooled unevenly. Presses struck at angles. Four hundred impressions, and every single one carried some small flaw, some honest imperfection of the physical world — except this one, which was identical, she would have sworn, to the impression on the writ issued two years ago, and the one before that, and the one before that.

Perfect, and unchanging, and utterly without a hand behind it.

"Vane? Are you listening?"

"Yes, Master Corvel. The inventory. Restricted vaults. Within the week." She smiled the smile of a woman whose entire interior life had just quietly packed its bags. "I'll need candles."

"You'll need what?"

"Candles," she said sweetly. "For the vaults. Unless you'd prefer I transcribe 'the' as 'the' in the King's historical commission. I'm sure no one will ever notice."

He waved her off with the weary air of a man conceding to a natural disaster, and Sera walked back down through the archive with the writ in her hand and her heart hammering a rhythm she refused to name.

They were letting her in. Or they were letting someone in, and she was the convenient pair of hands, and either way she would be standing inside the sealed heart of the crown's memory within days, alone, with a lamp and a list and whatever the truth actually was.

She should have felt afraid. She did feel afraid — properly, coldly, comprehensively afraid. But underneath the fear, in the place where eleven years of being overlooked had hardened into something useful, another feeling was taking shape, warm and obstinate as a banked coal.

Whatever wore the king's face had made one mistake. It had ordered an inventory. It had put the records in front of the one person in the palace who read records the way other people breathed.

"Ink remembers," Sera murmured to the writ, to the flawless seal, to the hollow thing it came from. "You should have asked around."

Above her, faint through the archive's high vents, she heard the palace bells mark the hour — and thought, absurdly, of mountains. Of roads running south and west, deep under Thornfell, toward fires older than crowns. She didn't know why. She was a chronicler, not a prophet.

But she caught herself wondering, for the first time, what the dragons had been paid — and who, in all these centuries, had ever written down their side of the story.
