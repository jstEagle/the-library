## 5. Down the Deep Roads

The smugglers' gate was a slab of granite disguised as a dead end, and it only opened for people who already knew it would.

Hazel had learned that from Vess, back when he was fourteen and convinced the whole world ran on rules. *Nothing in Thornfell opens for a knock,* she'd said, tapping his knuckles away from the stone. *It opens for a habit. Walk up like you've done it a thousand times, and the mountain will believe you.*

So he walked up like he'd done it a thousand times, because he had, and the mountain believed him. The slab ground aside with a groan of old counterweights and older patience, exhaling air that smelled of cold iron and something faintly sweet — like honey left too long near a forge.

"Down there?" Sera Vane stood at his shoulder, arms crossed against the wind, looking at the darkness with the expression of a woman who had spent her life indoors and resented every minute of it. "That's not a road. That's a throat."

"That's exactly what it is," Hazel said. "And we're going to let it swallow us."

"I want it noted," she said, "that I am a chronicler of the royal court. I have a desk. I have a chair with cushions. I have a window."

"Noted."

"And I'm about to walk into a hole in a mountain with a smuggler."

"Alleged smuggler."

"You were carrying forty pounds of moonsteel wrapped in sheep fleece when the inquisitors found you."

"Allegedly carrying," Hazel said, and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The first hour was the worst, because the first hour was all stairs — worn hollow in their centers by generations of boots, spiraling down through rock that sweated cold water. Sera counted them out loud at first, then stopped counting, then stopped talking altogether, which Hazel decided was probably a good sign rather than a bad one. People who stopped complaining had either given up or committed. He preferred committed.

His lantern threw their shadows huge against the walls, and the walls answered. That was the thing about the deep roads that no honest map ever captured: they weren't quiet. Old oaths soaked into stone the way rain soaked into chalk, and after enough centuries the mountain itself began to murmur. Not words. Not yet. More like the memory of words — a hum under the hearing, felt in the teeth before it reached the ears.

Sera felt it too. He heard her breath catch somewhere behind him.

"What is that?"

"The road singing," Hazel said. "Or the mountain. Or something older than both. Smugglers call it the drone. You get used to it."

"I don't think I will."

"You will. It's like a bad knee. Eventually it's just part of you."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed so strictly in her life. "You're very calm for a man being hunted by the crown."

"I'm extremely calm for a man whose name showed up in a hundred-year-old book dated to tonight, yes." He kept his voice level, but his hand drifted to the satchel where the prophecy book rode, wrapped in oilcloth. "Calm is just fear that's found a job."

Ahead, the stairs ended and the tunnel opened into a gallery wide enough to ride four abreast, its ceiling lost in blackness, its floor ribbed with ancient cart-ruts filled with standing water. Someone had built this. Not smugglers — smugglers borrowed; they didn't carve. This had been a kingdom's work, a road meant for kings or dragons, abandoned so long ago that even the forgetting had forgotten it.

Hazel held up a hand and Sera stopped. He listened.

The drone was louder here. And underneath it — he tilted his head, half-closing his eyes — underneath it, something almost melodic. A phrase of sound that rose and fell and rose again, patient as tide.

"It changes," Sera whispered. She'd come up beside him without him noticing, which unsettled him more than he admitted. "Earlier it was just... hum. Now it's almost like—"

"Song," Hazel said. "Dragon-song. Or what's left of it."

"How do you know what dragon-song sounds like?"

He didn't have an answer that wasn't a confession, so he said nothing, and she didn't press, and the silence between them settled into something almost companionable. Two strangers sharing a secret neither fully understood: that was a kind of trust, wasn't it? The reluctant kind. The kind that grew sideways, like ivy, without anyone deciding to plant it.

They walked on.

* * *

At the second waystation — a vaulted chamber where an underground stream ran through a channel cut with impossible precision — Hazel called a halt. His legs ached from the descent and his palm ached worse. The oath-brand had been restless since the ambush, since the moment the swordspeech had come out of him unbidden, answering inquisitors' steel with words he'd never learned. Now it throbbed in time with the drone, as if the two were keeping count together.

He unwrapped his bandages by the stream and looked at the brand while Sera pretended not to watch and watched anyway.

"It's changed," she said finally, abandoning pretense. "Since the pass. There are new lines."

There were. Fine silver threads branching off the old scar, delicate as frost ferns, reaching toward his fingers. He flexed his hand and the threads caught the lantern light and seemed to move — or seemed to want to move.

"When did you take the oath?" Sera asked. She'd settled onto a dry ledge, chronicle-precise even now, her notebook open on her knees though she wrote nothing in it. "The oath-branding. When?"

"I was nine."

"Nine." Her voice did something complicated. "Who brands a nine-year-old?"

"A dying woman who didn't have anyone else." He rewrapped the palm, quick and practiced. "Her name was Vess. She found me in a border town after the fever took everyone else in it. She said I had a mouth that wanted to make promises and a spine that might survive them. So she gave me the only promise worth making."

"Which was?"

"'I will speak, and what I speak will hold.'" He recited it flatly, the way you recite something you've said ten thousand times until it wears smooth as river stone. "That's the whole oath. Nine words. Everything else is commentary."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "In the archives — the restricted ones, the ones I needed three signatures to enter — there are records of the coronation rite. King Aldric's. The binding that made him..." She paused, choosing the word with a scholar's care. "...whatever he is now. The core formula of that rite is nine words. The same nine."

The drone filled the pause between them, rising and falling, patient as tide.

"I know," Hazel said. "I worked it out on the switchbacks, somewhere around the time I was running for my life. My gift and his curse. Same root. Same well." He laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bright. "Vess always said the oath was older than the crown. I thought she meant it as comfort."

"Maybe it was."

"Maybe it was a warning I wasn't ready to hear."

Sera closed her notebook. In the lantern light her face was tired and sharp and honest, and Hazel realized he was glad she was here, which was inconvenient, because gladness was a debt and debts got people killed.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the records say the original rite required a speaker. Someone had to say those nine words over Aldric. A swordspeaker." She met his eyes. "A century ago. The last one."

The drone rose, and fell.

"And now there's me," Hazel said quietly. "Right on schedule. Like the mountain kept my seat warm."

* * *

They slept in shifts, and Hazel dreamed of Vess.

Not the good dreams. The teaching dreams, where she stood at the edge of some gray nowhere with her arms folded, watching him with that look she reserved for his worst decisions — the look that said *you know better, so do better.* In the dream she didn't say anything. She never did anymore. She just looked, and the looking was the lecture.

When he woke, Sera had the fire banked low and was feeding strips of dried mutton to absolutely no one, chewing hers with the grim efficiency of a woman eating because eating was a task on a list.

"Question," she said, before he was fully awake. "Back in the capital. Before I ran. I pulled the treasury ledgers — fuel allocations, foundry shipments, the whole chain. Dragon-fire doesn't just appear in the crown's vaults. It has to be taken from somewhere. From someone." She turned the strip of meat in her fingers. "From the last dragon."

"Emberveil," Hazel said.

"You know her name."

"Everyone in Thornfell knows her name. We just don't say it above ground." He sat up, joints cracking. "The deep roads run toward her hollow. They've always run toward her. The smugglers' maps — the real ones, the ones Vess burned into my memory — they all bend the same direction, like water finding a low place. I used to think it was geology."

"And now?"

"Now I think the roads remember her. And she remembers them." He looked down the tunnel ahead, where the drone waited. "There's a reason the crown never sealed these ways, Sera. A reason the inquisitors hunt the passes but leave the deep roads alone. They're afraid of what's down here. Or they're protecting it. Or—" he flexed his branded hand, watching the silver threads catch the light, "—or the roads are part of the same machine. The same root."

Sera considered this. "You say 'root' like it's a living thing."

"Everything down here is a living thing. Listen." He raised a finger.

She listened. At first: nothing. Then the drone resolved, the way a chord resolves when your ear finally finds the lowest note, and beneath the hum there it was again — the phrase, rising and falling, rising and falling. Patient. Ancient. Grieving, maybe. It was hard to tell grief from patience when the singer had a hundred years of practice at both.

"She's dimming," Hazel said. "Can't you hear it? Every year there's less. The old-timers used to say you could feel Emberveil breathing through the whole mountain range, like a heartbeat under the floor of the world. Now it's—" he searched for the word, "—guttering. Like a candle in a draft."

Sera was silent for a long moment. Then she opened her notebook and wrote something, quickly, the way she must have written a thousand entries in the court archives — except this time her hand shook slightly, and Hazel understood that whatever she was writing, it was the first true thing she'd recorded in years.

"What does the evidence say happens," he asked, "when the last light goes out?"

She didn't look up. "The ledgers show the withdrawals increasing. Doubling, then doubling again. Whatever the crown is feeding with her fire, it's getting hungrier." She snapped the notebook shut. "And the projections end in a column of question marks. I filed them as clerical error. Twice."

"And the third time?"

"The third time I stole the originals and ran into a hole in a mountain with a smuggler." She almost smiled. "Career trajectory's gone strange."

* * *

On the third day the tunnel began to change.

Hazel noticed it first in his feet — the cart-ruts fading, the floor smoothing, as if the road were remembering what it had been before carts. Then in the walls: veins of something luminous threading the rock, dim gold, pulsing faintly in time with the drone. Not ore. Not any mineral he could name. It looked, he thought with a chill that was somehow also a warmth, like light trying to become stone, or stone trying to remember light.

Sera touched one vein and snatched her fingers back. "It's warm."

"Don't touch anything," Hazel said automatically, and then, because honesty was becoming a habit with her and habits were dangerous, added: "Actually, I don't know if that's true. I don't know the rules down here. Nobody does. The last people who mapped these roads were doing it a thousand years before the crown existed."

"Wonderful. An unmapped road under a mountain, toward a dying dragon, pursued by inquisitors who've been waiting a century for me to exist." He could hear the grin in her voice before he saw it. "You know, most people's midlife crises involve boats."

"I'm not having a crisis. I'm having a calling. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Crisis means you want to go back. Calling means you can't."

She walked beside him in silence for a while after that, and the silence was the good kind, the kind that had weight and warmth instead of edges. Somewhere in it, without either of them announcing it, they had stopped being smuggler and defector, stranger and stranger. They were just two people walking the same dark road, holding the same unbearable fact between them like a coal passed hand to hand.

The road bent. The gold veins thickened. And the song — Hazel could no longer honestly call it a drone — swelled until it filled his chest, until his brand blazed silver through the bandage, until Sera gasped and gripped his arm and pointed.

Ahead, where the tunnel should have continued straight, the wall had opened. Not carved open — *opened*, the stone drawn aside like a curtain, revealing a passage that glowed faintly from within, breathing warm air that smelled of honey and hot iron and something older than both.

"That wasn't there yesterday," Sera said. "We passed this spot. I'm certain. There was a fall of rubble, a dead end."

"There was." Hazel stared at the opening, at the road that had unblocked itself, and felt the truth settle over him with the weight of a hand on his shoulder. "The mountain moved for us."

"Why?"

He flexed his burning palm. The silver threads had spread overnight, fine as spider silk, reaching all the way to his fingertips now.

"Because it knows the oath," he said softly. "Because everything down here — the roads, the stone, the song — it's all the same root. And I think..." He swallowed. "I think the mountain's been waiting for a speaker too."

Beyond the opening, far below and deeper than any map had ever dared to guess, something vast exhaled in the dark — a long, slow, guttering breath, like a candle deciding whether to go out.

And the road, patient as tide, sloped gently downward to meet it.
