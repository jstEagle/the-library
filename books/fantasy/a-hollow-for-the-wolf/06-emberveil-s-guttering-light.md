## 6. Emberveil's Guttering Light

The road down was older than the road up.

Hazel had smuggled enough of the deep roads to know their moods — which passages sulked, which ones sang, which ones had been walked so long by men carrying things they shouldn't that the stone itself seemed to have learned discretion. But this stretch was different. The walls here were not cut. They were *worn*, and worn in a way no pick or boot could account for: great sweeping grooves, as if something enormous had passed this way a thousand times, a thousand years ago, and the mountain had simply agreed to remember it.

Sera Vane kept close to his shoulder, her chronicler's satchel clutched to her chest like a child. She had stopped asking questions somewhere around the third hour. Not because she'd run out — Hazel suspected she never would — but because she'd begun writing them down instead, scratching notes by lantern-light with the grim diligence of a woman cataloguing her own disbelief.

"That groove," she said finally, "is wider than my house."

"Your house is small."

"My house is *adequate*." She ran her fingers along the stone, frowning. "What made this?"

Hazel didn't answer. He didn't know how to say it yet without sounding like a madman, and he'd only just gotten used to the idea that Sera believed him about the king. Mostly. In the way a scholar believes in a hypothesis she intends to dissect.

The song was louder here. That was the thing he couldn't explain to her, because he couldn't fully explain it to himself. The tunnels beneath Thornfell hummed — he'd noticed it for years, dismissed it as wind through fissures, water under stone. But since the waystation, since the book, since his palm had begun drawing silver threads like frost across a windowpane, the humming had resolved into something with shape. With *words* he almost knew.

It sounded, he thought, like someone singing a lullaby they'd forgotten the ending to.

The air changed first. Warmth, where there should have been cold at this depth — a dry, animal warmth, like standing near a hearth in a very large room. Then the smell: hot stone and old smoke and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like resin burned in a temple.

Then the light.

It came around a bend ahead of them, weak and amber and trembling, pooling on the tunnel floor in slow pulses. Not torchlight. Torchlight doesn't breathe.

Hazel stopped. Sera bumped into him, peered past his arm, and went very quiet in the particular way of people who have spent their lives among documents and have just encountered one written in fire.

"Oh," she said. Just that. A whole career of words, reduced to a single syllable.

The passage opened.

The hollow was vast beyond sense — a cavern the size of the capital's cathedral district, ceiling lost in darkness above, floor sloping gently toward a central depression like a bowl worn smooth by centuries of settling. And in the bowl lay the dragon.

She was smaller than the stories said. That was Hazel's first thought, absurd and immediate: *she's smaller than the stories.* The second thought arrived a heartbeat later and corrected him: no. She wasn't smaller. She was *diminished*. What lay before them was a creature built on the scale of mountains, folded now into herself like a letter someone had given up trying to read. Her scales, which must once have thrown back light like hammered copper, had gone dull and grey-brown, cracked in places, showing pale hide beneath. Her wings lay flat along her flanks, membrane thin as old parchment, veined with fading threads of ember-glow that pulsed in time with her breathing.

In. Out. In. Out. Each breath a little weaker than the last, if you watched long enough. Hazel watched long enough.

"She's dying," Sera whispered.

"She's being *drained*," Hazel said, and even as he said it he saw it — really saw it, the way you see a trick only once someone points at the mechanism. Channels. Grooves in the cavern floor radiating outward from the dragon's body like spokes from a wheel, dozens of them, running off into the dark in every direction. They glowed faintly, a sickly gold, and the glow *moved* — a slow, steady crawl of light away from her, always away, flowing out through the mountain toward... 

He didn't need a map. He knew what lay east of Thornfell. Everyone did.

"The capital," Sera breathed, following his gaze. She crouched beside one of the channels, held her palm over it, and snatched it back. "It's warm. It's — this is heat, actual heat, moving through the stone like water through an aqueduct. Do you understand what this means? This isn't legend. This is *engineering*. Someone built this."

"Not someone," Hazel said quietly. "Something. Something wearing a crown."

They descended into the bowl together, boots crunching on scale-dust and old ash. Up close the dragon was worse and better at once — worse because he could see the cracks in her clearly now, see how the pale hide beneath showed through like bone through a wound; better because her eye was open.

One eye, vast as a cartwheel, tracking them across the floor. Amber, shot through with veins of gold, and within the gold a flickering that reminded Hazel unbearably of the waystation lamp — a flame deciding whether to go out.

He should have been afraid. He understood that distantly, the way you understand a rule from a country you've left. A dragon. The last dragon. Any sensible man would be running.

Instead his palm ached, and the silver threads beneath his skin had begun to glow, faint and silver-gold, in time with her breathing. In. Out. His brand answering hers like two instruments finding the same key.

The dragon's head moved. Slowly, with the terrible economy of the very old and very tired, she lifted it from her forepaws and regarded them. When she spoke, her voice was not a roar. It was a rasp, deep and dry, like pages turning in a closed library — like the sound the tunnels made, Hazel realized, when they hummed. He'd been hearing her voice for years and calling it wind.

"*Little word-bearer.*"

The words settled over the hollow like snowfall. Sera made a small sound and sat down abruptly on a piece of rubble, whether from awe or weak knees Hazel couldn't tell.

"You know me," he managed. His voice sounded very small in all that space.

"I know the *oath*." The great eye narrowed, and something moved behind it — amusement, maybe, or grief wearing amusement's clothes. "You wear the binding on your hand. Old rite. Old, old rite. I felt it come down the mountain like a splinter working toward the heart." A plume of breath escaped her nostrils, grey and thin, smelling of cold ash. "I had wondered if any speaker remained. The world has been very quiet."

"My name is Hazel Blackwood."

"No," she said, with terrible gentleness, "it isn't. Not anymore. Not entirely. Names are doors, little word-bearer, and yours has been opening for some time now. Ask your palm, if you doubt me."

His palm, naturally, said nothing. But the threads pulsed, and he found he couldn't argue.

Sera had recovered enough to scramble forward, satchel open, charcoal already in hand. "Emberveil," she said. "You're Emberveil. The chronicles list you as dead. Twice. Once in the reign of Aldric's grandfather, once during the—"

"Chronicles are written by the living," Emberveil said, "about whatever the living can bear to look at." The eye shifted to Sera, and Hazel watched the chronicler freeze like a rabbit under it — and then, remarkably, straighten her spine. He was beginning to like this woman. "And you are ink-hands. You carry paper the way soldiers carry steel. Good. Paper lasts. We will want paper, before the end."

"Before the end of what?" Hazel asked.

Emberveil did not answer immediately. Instead she turned her head, slowly, toward the nearest channel — the golden thread of stolen light crawling away through the stone — and watched it go with an expression Hazel had no word for. He would spend a long time afterward trying to find one. It was the look of a mother watching her children walk out of a burning house into worse hands, knowing she cannot follow.

"Do you know what I was, little word-bearer?" she said at last. "Do any of you? Your songs make us hoards and hunger. Fire and greed. Your knights made careers of us." A sound rattled in her chest — a laugh, he realized, dry as tumbling gravel. "We were *keepers*. That is what dragons were. Every flame needs a hollow to live in, or it spreads and eats the world. We were the hollows. We held the fire so the world could stay cool enough for soft things like you."

"And now?"

"Now I am the last hollow, and I am leaking." She drew a slow breath, and the ember-veins along her wings brightened briefly, desperately, then dimmed again. "A century ago, a king came to me. A good king — that is the cruelest part, little word-bearer. Not a tyrant. A frightened, decent man who had looked into a dark too large for him and needed fire to hold it back. So we made a pact, he and I. My fire, poured into a vessel, to keep the dark contained. And I thought — I *thought* — a vessel holds. A vessel can be set down. A vessel can be emptied and refilled."

Her gaze returned to Hazel, and there was something in it that made his chest ache: pity, aimed at him, from a dying god.

"But a crown is not a vessel. A crown is a *thirst*. It drinks and drinks and calls the drinking duty. For a hundred years it has drunk from me through these channels, and the man who made the pact—" her voice roughened, guttered, "—the man is still in there. Somewhere. Underneath. Watching his own hands sign orders he does not mean. That is the worst prison ever built, ink-hands: a cell with your own face on the outside of it."

Sera had stopped writing. Her charcoal hovered, motionless, and tears stood in her eyes with the offended surprise of a woman who had not authorized them.

"Aldric," she whispered. "The histories say he changed. After his coronation. They blamed grief. His queen died that winter."

"His queen died that *century*," Emberveil said softly. "Grief is a fine door for such things. It stands open, and no one questions who walks through."

Hazel looked down at his palm. At the silver threads reaching his fingertips, glowing faintly in the dragon's amber light, pulsing with her breath. The oath-brand. The same root, Vess had said — no, Vess hadn't said it, he'd realized it himself in the dark of the tunnels, and now the realization stood up and demanded to be looked at.

"The binding," he said. "The rite that crowned him. It's the same rite that's on my hand."

"It is the first rite," Emberveil said. "There is only one. All the oaths your kind has sworn since — swordspeech, oathcraft, the little bindings of trade and border and marriage — they are children's copies of it. And you carry the original, little word-bearer, pressed into your skin by a woman who knew exactly what she was doing."

"Vess," Hazel said. The name came out rough.

"Vess," the dragon agreed, and something in her tone suggested she had known that name far longer than Hazel had been alive. "She visited me, near the end of her life. Sat where the ink-hands is sitting now. She asked me strange questions about endings. About what a story costs." The great eye half-closed. "I did not understand her then. I am beginning to."

The silence that followed was filled only by the pulse of the channels — that steady, greedy crawl of gold light away into the dark, away east, away toward a throne where something hollow wore a good man's face.

Hazel became aware, gradually, that he was angry. Not the hot flash of the switchbacks, when the oathspeech had come out of him unbidden and terrifying. This was colder. Older. It sat in his chest like a coal and refused to be discussed.

"How much longer?" he asked. "For you. Without the fire."

Emberveil considered him for a long moment. "A season," she said. "Perhaps two. I am old, little word-bearer. I was old when your mountains were young. I could end gently, if the drinking stopped — a hollow may close, when its keeper is done. But drained as I am..." She exhaled, and the lamp-flame in her eye shuddered. "I will gutter. And when the last hollow fails, the fire goes nowhere, little word-bearer. It simply *spills*. Everything I have held for ten thousand years, all at once, into a world that has forgotten how to stand near it."

Sera wrote that down. Her hand shook, but she wrote it down, and Hazel loved her a little for it.

"So we stop the channels," he said.

"You cannot stop the channels. They are oath-work, bound deeper than stone."

"Then we unbind them."

"With what? You have one word in you that matters, little word-bearer, and I promise you do not yet know what it costs." Her voice gentled further, which somehow made it worse. "Go home. Forget the book. Forget the prophecy. Live your small careful life. That is what I would tell any soft thing that wandered in here."

But she was looking at his palm as she said it. At the threads, glowing steady now, no longer pulsing with her breath but with his own heartbeat — as if the oath had heard everything and made its decision without consulting him.

"You don't believe that either," Hazel said.

The dragon was silent a long moment. When she answered, her voice was so quiet it barely stirred the ash.

"I am ten thousand years old, and I have watched a thousand brave fools walk toward fires they could not carry. I have learned to recognize the ones who will reach the fire anyway." The lamp-flame in her eye steadied — just barely, just for a moment — into something that might, in a younger face, have been called hope. "No. I do not believe it. More's the pity."

She lowered her great head back onto her forepaws, exhausted by the effort of speech, and the amber light in the hollow dimmed with her.

"Rest," she murmured. "Both of you. The mountain will not let anything find you here — it remembers whose hollow this is. Tomorrow, little word-bearer, I will show you what the channels truly are. And you will tell me about the book. The one with your name in it." A pause, and the ghost of that gravel laugh again. "Yes, I know about the book. Vess told me a story once, about a swordspeaker and a date that hadn't happened yet. I have been waiting a hundred years to learn how it ends."

Hazel settled against a ridge of warm scale-dust, Sera already scribbling beside him, and stared up into the vast dark where the ceiling should have been.

Somewhere overhead, a kingdom slept under a hollow king. Somewhere east, a crown drank and drank. And here in the last hollow of the world, a dying dragon watched him with one guttering eye — waiting, patient as tide, for him to tell her how the story ended.

He opened his mouth to say he didn't know.

What came out instead, soft and unbidden, in a language he had never been taught:

"*So do I.*"

In the old tongue. In the first tongue.

Emberveil's eye flew wide open.
