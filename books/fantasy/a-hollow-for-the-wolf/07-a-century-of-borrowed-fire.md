## 7. A Century of Borrowed Fire

Dragons do not sleep the way small creatures sleep. We sink. We go down through layers of ourselves like divers going down through cold water, and what waits at the bottom is memory — not remembered, exactly, but *inhabited*, the way a river inhabits its bed.

Emberveil was sinking now. She had been sinking for a hundred years, in truth; the sleeping was only the last courtesy her body paid her mind. Her fire guttered in the channels carved into her hollow's walls, drawn away drop by drop toward a city she had never loved and a crown she had once blessed with her own breath. The little word-bearer and his sharp-eyed chronicler sat somewhere above her, warming their hands at what remained of her warmth, and she let them. It was the first honest use her fire had found in decades.

She sank past the layer where her name lived. She sank past the layer where her grief lived, which was larger, and older, and better furnished.

And at the bottom, in the dark that was not dark because she had filled it with fire once, she dreamed the century backward.

* * *

It began, as most terrible bargains begin, on a beautiful evening.

The year was 1123 by the reckoning of men who counted such things, and Emberveil had come down from the high crags to answer a summons she should have ignored. Kings summoned dragons the way children summoned thunderstorms: with great ceremony, total sincerity, and no understanding whatsoever of what they were asking for.

Aldric was young then. That was the thing everyone forgot afterward, when they told the story of the hollow king — that he had been *young*, younger than his crown, younger than his kingdom, a broad-shouldered man with ink still sometimes on his fingers because he insisted on drafting his own letters rather than letting scribes make him sound wiser than he was. He had stood in the ruined amphitheater at Thornfell's foot with no army behind him and no guards beside him, just himself and a lantern and an audacity so pure it was almost holy.

"You came," he said, and sounded genuinely surprised.

"You rang the old bell," Emberveil said. "Nobody has rung the old bell in four hundred years. I assumed either the world was ending or a child had climbed the tower, and I could smell from here that you were not a child."

"I'm twenty-six."

"To a dragon, that is a child with excellent posture."

He laughed. She remembered the laugh. That was the cruelty of it — she would remember the laugh long after she had forgotten the shape of his face, the way one remembers sunlight from a room one can never enter again. He had laughed like a man who had not yet learned what his office would cost, and then he had stopped laughing, and shown her why he had rung the bell.

He showed her the wound in the world.

It lay under the capital — under the palace itself, under the throne room where later she would watch him sign his own unraveling. A tear, thin as a knife-cut in silk, seeping something older than the gods' quarrels. The scholars of that age had called it the Underdark Breach and written eleven treatises about it and understood perhaps a tenth of it between them. What Emberveil understood, looking at it with eyes made for seeing heat and hunger, was simple: it was a mouth. Not a demon, not a god, not any enemy with a name you could shout at. Just a mouth, patient beyond all patience, waiting for the world to forget it was there.

"Forgetting is how it feeds," Aldric said. "Every generation that doesn't know about it makes it stronger. My grandfather sealed it with wards. My father renewed them. I've renewed them twice, and each time they take less and cost more, and I am thirty years from being able to afford the next renewal even if I live to make it." He looked up at her, and there it was — the thing that would ruin everything, shining in his face like a coin at the bottom of a well. Hope. "The old texts say dragon-fire burns true. Burns *real*. Not like our fires, which burn wood and lie about it. Yours burns what actually is."

"Flattery," Emberveil said, "from a king, is usually a bill."

"It is," he agreed. "Here is mine."

And he told her the plan, and she listened, and she thought — this is the shame she carried down through every layer of herself — she thought it was *clever*. That was the trap of cleverness. It dressed the monstrous up in architecture and made it look like engineering.

Aldric proposed to bind a hollow thing into his own body.

Not the mouth beneath the palace — nothing so grand. A lesser fragment, a scrap of the same hungry dark, caught and caged inside a living frame. Himself. Because a hollow thing needed a vessel that could hold it, and vessels of stone cracked, and vessels of iron rusted, but a king who renewed his own wards every season would be a vessel that *maintained itself*. His body would be the lock. His will would be the bolt. And to keep the fragment fed — because a starving prisoner gnaws at its chains, and a fed one sleeps — the lock would be oiled with dragon-fire, the only substance in creation that burned true enough to satisfy a piece of the unmaking without feeding it.

"You are proposing," Emberveil said slowly, "to swallow a splinter of the end of the world, and pay me to keep it quiet."

"When you say it that way—"

"That is the only way to say it. All other ways are furniture arranged around this way."

He had the grace to flinch. Then he knelt — a king, kneeling in the dust of an amphitheater older than his dynasty — and said, "I have a wife who is expecting our first child in the spring. I have a kingdom of two million people who did not choose me and cannot choose otherwise. If the breach opens, everything downstream of the mountains drowns in whatever comes out of that mouth. I will pay any price that is *mine* to pay. I only ask that the fire not be taken. I ask that it be *given*, freely, so that the giving binds us both."

And there it was. The word that undid her. Not *take*. *Give.*

She was nine hundred years old and vain in the way of very old things, which is to say she was vain about her judgment. And her judgment told her: this young fool means it. This young fool will hold. Look at him. He has already decided to die badly and he is *grateful* for the opportunity.

So Emberveil did the worst thing a wise creature can do. She believed someone.

"One condition," she said. "When your line ends — when the last Aldric-blood goes into the ground — the binding ends with it. Whatever is left of the fragment passes to me, and I burn it out of the world over a thousand years if I must. Swear it on your house."

"On my house," Aldric said, "and on my daughter's name, which is Rosamund, and which she will hate, because every Rosamund in history has hated it."

She gave him fire that night. Not much — a dragon learns early that fire given is fire gone — but true fire, the deep kind, the kind that remembers being a star. It went into the wards and through the wards into him, and she watched a young king shudder as something vast and patient settled into the rooms of his body like an unwelcome tenant signing a very long lease.

"Does it hurt?" she asked.

"Like swallowing winter," he said, and grinned at her, the idiot, the brave idiot. "But I've had worse hangovers."

* * *

For thirty years, it worked.

This was the second cruelty, and in some ways the greater one. If the pact had failed immediately — if the fragment had devoured him in a season — the story would have been clean. A warning. A tragedy with edges. Instead it *worked*, magnificently, boringly, decade after decade. The wards sang. The breach slept. Aldric governed well, fathered two more children, developed a regrettable fondness for tax reform, and sent Emberveil a gift every midwinter: a barrel of the mountain honey she pretended not to enjoy, delivered with a note in his own hand, ink on the fingers of the messenger's memory if not the messenger.

*Still holding,* the notes said. *Thank you. R.*

She flew down once a year to renew the fire. They would sit in the amphitheater where they'd first bargained, two creatures who had nothing in common except the size of what they carried, and he would tell her court gossip as though she cared, and she would pretend to care as though he couldn't tell she was pretending, and it was — she could admit it now, sunk below all her defenses — it was the closest thing to friendship her long life had held since her clutchmates had gone into the ground.

"The fragment spoke to me today," he told her once, in his fifties. "First time in years. Just one word. It said *hungry*, and then it said *soon*, and then it was quiet again."

"What did you do?"

"Told it we'd discussed this. Told it the lease runs another forty years minimum." He rubbed his chest, where the binding sat like a swallowed coal. "It's patient, Emberveil. That's the horror of it. It isn't fighting me. It's *waiting* for me. Every year I get older and it stays exactly as hungry as it was, and eventually arithmetic does its work."

"Then we renegotiate before the arithmetic wins," she said. "There are other ways to feed it. Lesser fires. Bound saints. There are monks in the eastern ranges who have been trying to become fire for three generations—"

"You want to feed it *people*?"

"I want to feed it anything that isn't *you*."

"And that," he said, with the gentle stubbornness that was his whole character distilled to a single flaw, "is where we differ. I made this bargain on my life. Nobody else's. When my life runs out, the bargain runs out. That was the deal."

"The deal was your *line*, Aldric. Your blood carries the binding forward—"

"My blood carries it forward," he agreed, "and my granddaughter's blood after her, and somewhere down the centuries some poor descendant too weak or too weary or too *human* to hold what we're asking them to hold. I've read the histories, old friend. Dynasties don't produce heroes forever. They produce heirs. Sooner or later the crown lands on a head that can't bear the weight, and then the mouth opens under the palace anyway, except by then it will have been fed for eight hundred years and grown fat." He looked at his hands. "I won't build a bomb and call it a legacy."

"So what is the legacy instead?"

"Honestly?" And he smiled, tired, fifty-eight years old, a coal burning in his chest. "I don't know yet. I have maybe ten years to figure it out. Ask me again next midwinter."

He had seven.

* * *

What killed him was not the fragment. Understand that. The histories got it wrong, or the histories were edited, and Emberveil had spent ninety years wondering which and had finally, in the bottom-most layer of her dreaming, arrived at the answer: both. The truth was smaller and worse than any conspiracy.

He simply grew old. The binding took strength the way a mortgage takes wages — invisibly, relentlessly, always affordable right up until it wasn't. In his sixty-fifth year the wards flickered during a storm, and the fragment pressed against its cage, and Aldric held — of course he held, he was Aldric — but holding cost him a month in bed, and the physicians wrote *frailty*, and the court began, quietly, to prepare for a succession.

And the fragment felt the preparation. Felt the kingdom's attention slide off its jailer and toward his heirs. And it did the one thing a patient thing can do when patience stops paying: it *spoke*. Not to Aldric. To the court. To the fears. To the whisper-mills. It had been marinating in a king's mind for forty years; it had learned how power thinks.

Within a season, half the council believed the king was failing because he was cursed, and the other half believed he was failing because he was weak, and both halves were correct, and neither half knew what the failure would actually release.

Aldric, dying by inches in a palace full of people planning around him, did the last thing he ever did alone. He sent for Emberveil.

"You promised to renegotiate," she said, landing hard enough to crack the amphitheater stones, because she had flown angry and frightened and had stopped pretending otherwise.

"I'm past renegotiation." He was thin. She had not let herself see how thin. "But I'm not past *planning*. Listen. The binding needs a holder. My blood can't hold it — I've seen my heir's heart, and it's good, but it isn't *this*. So we change the terms. We stop pretending the cage can be handed down. Instead—" and here his voice did the thing it did when he was about to propose something monstrous in a reasonable tone, "—instead we make the cage permanent. No more vessel. A *structure*. Something that doesn't age, doesn't weaken, doesn't inherit. Something that holds the fragment and renews the wards and requires nothing from anyone's bloodstream."

"A structure," Emberveil said. "Made of what?"

"Of office. Of continuity. Of—" he hesitated, and she watched a man hear his own idea clearly for the first time, and continue anyway, "—of the crown itself. Fed by fire. Bound by oath. It wouldn't be me. It would be the *kingship*, the part that never dies, wearing my face until the realm adjusts. And when the realm is ready — a generation, two — we find a way to end it properly. Together. You'll help me. You promised to help me."

"I promised to burn the fragment when your line ended. I did not promise to help you invent an immortal king."

"No," he said softly. "You promised to give fire freely, so the giving bound us both. Read your own contract, old friend. You're in this whether you like it or not."

She should have refused. Nine hundred years of judgment, and she wants, in this deepest room of her dreaming, to reach back through the century and seize her own foolish noble neck and *shake* it. But he was dying, and he was afraid — truly afraid, the first time she had ever seen it — and underneath the fear was the same awful hope that had rung the bell forty years before: *there must be a way, and I will pay for it, and the payment will be mine.*

"Terms," she said, because refusal had died somewhere on the flight down. "Written. Witnessed. With an ending — a real ending, a date, a rite, something. No open doors."

"With an ending," he swore. "On Rosamund's name."

They built it together, in his last month. The rites were hers; the architecture was his; the fire was hers, given freely, freely, freely, until the word lost its meaning. And when Aldric the man finally stopped, on a gray morning in his sixty-sixth year, something wearing his face stood up from the bed and thanked the physicians calmly and asked about the grain tariffs, and the kingdom exhaled with relief, and Emberveil flew home to Thornfell and did not sing for a year.

* * *

That was the design. Here is what the design could not account for, the flaw no cleverness survives:

The fragment was patient, and the structure was not alive, and a living jailer — even a dying one, even a flawed one — *wants* the imprisonment to end. Wants it the way a lung wants air. Aldric had carried, all those years, alongside the hunger and the fear, a small stubborn pilot light of intent: *this ends. I decide when.* The fragment had feared that light. Had negotiated around it. Had waited for it to gutter.

The structure had no such light. The structure wanted only to continue. And so, year upon year, the fragment stopped being a prisoner and became a partner, and then stopped being a partner and became the tenant, and then stopped being a tenant and became the house. The face stayed. The face was load-bearing. But behind the face, decision by decision, signature by signature, the kingship hollowed — not into evil, which would at least have been legible, but into *continuation*, pure and mindless, a mouth that had learned to wear a crown and sign tax reforms.

And the fire — the fire kept flowing. That was the final joke, the one Emberveil could not stop tasting even now, even drowned. The wards still needed oiling. The binding still needed feeding. Every year the siphon-channels drew a little deeper, and every year she gave a little more, because the alternative was to let the thing under the palace starve its way loose, and she had seen what it looked like when it was hungry.

She had given fire to save a young king. She had given fire to bury a dead one honorably. And now she gave fire to keep a hollow thing comfortable in a stolen chair, and the giving had stopped binding anyone to anything, and the word *freely* had curdled into *forever*.

Ninety years. Ninety barrels of honey, unclaimed. Ninety midwinters, unanswered.

* * *

Now, in the bottom of the dream, in the present tense of a body too dim to dream properly anymore, Emberveil surfaced just far enough to feel the little word-bearer's warmth across the hollow, and the chronicler's scratch of a pen, and she thought:

*Aldric, you magnificent idiot. You built a lock with no key and called it mercy.*

And then, because the dream was not finished with her, because dreams at the bottom of a dragon are never finished, she saw it — the page, the prophecy book, the marginalia in a hand she almost recognized, and a line that made the last of her fire flare against her ribs like a struck match:

*The swordspeaker speaks the first word, and the king is unbound — or bound forever.*

Two endings. Someone had been editing toward the crueler one.

Emberveil opened one ancient eye in the dark of her hollow, and fixed it on the book lying open beside the sleeping smuggler, and thought, with the first true heat she had felt in ninety years:

*Little word-bearer. Before you trust that page — ask who wrote in its margins.*
