## 8. The Prophecy's Second Reader

Sera Vane had spent eleven years in the Royal Archive learning one lesson above all others: the truth rarely announced itself. It waited, patient as dust, in the margins of things.

She had smuggled the prophecy book out of Hazel Blackwood's pack while he slept — not stolen, she told herself firmly, *borrowed*, with every intention of returning it once she understood what he was carrying and why half the crown's inquisitorial apparatus seemed willing to burn down a mountain to take it from him. The book sat now under a single candle in her cramped quarters above the archive, and it looked, frankly, unremarkable. Calfskin covers gone soft at the corners. A spine repaired sometime in the last century with thread that had once been red and was now the color of old blood. The kind of book that a hundred cataloguers would have passed over without a second glance.

Which, of course, was precisely what made it dangerous. Sera had learned that too.

She turned to the first page and felt the familiar tightening behind her sternum — the archivist's equivalent of a gambler's thrill, which was ridiculous, because nothing about this was a game. A king who might not be a king. A swordspeaker who flinched when you looked directly at him and lied about his own name with the ease of long practice. An ambush on the switchbacks that she'd only survived because she'd been there to see it, and even then she'd spent three days afterward telling herself she'd imagined the way his blade had moved before his hand did.

The book did not care what she believed about it.

*The wolf shall come to the hollow,* read the opening line, in a hand that was formal and slightly cramped, the hand of someone trained to copy scripture. *And the hollow shall be full.*

"Ominous," Sera said to the empty room. Her cat, Bristle — a scraggly gray creature she had formally adopted out of pity and who had informally adopted her out of contempt — opened one eye from the foot of the bed, judged her tone insufficiently apologetic, and closed it again.

She worked the way she always worked: slowly, cross-referencing, copying each passage onto loose sheets so she could rearrange them like pieces of a puzzle. The prophecy ran forty-one pages. It was dated — and here was the thing that had kept her awake two nights running — *one hundred and six years ago*. The ink was right for it. The paper was right for it. She had held enough forged documents in her career to know forgery when she touched it, and this was not forgery. This was a genuine century-old text that named, by name, a man who by every reasonable accounting should not exist yet.

*Hazel Blackwood.* She wrote the name on a slip of paper, looked at it, and wrote beneath it: *smuggler? liar? or exactly what the page says he is?*

Beneath that, after a moment's thought, she added: *all three, probably. It's always all three.*

* * *

It took her until well past midnight to notice the marginalia.

Not because it was hidden — the opposite, really. The margins were *full* of it. But the earlier notes were so dry, so deliberately dull, that they read like the work of some monkish scholar annotating for annotation's sake. *Cf. the Rite of Crowning, folio 12.* *Word order disputed; see Aldrican recension.* *This passage is corrupt in the Thornfell copies.* Sera had skimmed them the way one skims any dead scholar's homework, filing them mentally under *thorough but uninspired.*

Then, on page nineteen, the handwriting changed.

It was still neat — neater, if anything, tighter and more certain than the original scribe's — but the personality of it was different. Where the main text marched, the margin-notes *leaned*. They crowded up against the prophecy's lines like a reader arguing with the author across a hundred years of silence. And the ink, Sera realized, tilting the page toward the candle, was a different shade. Not older. Newer. Perhaps sixty years old. Perhaps fifty.

Beside the line *the crown shall drink the fire and the fire shall forget its source*, the newer hand had written:

*No. Read it again. The crown doesn't drink. It is fed. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

Sera sat very still.

She turned pages faster now, hunting. On twenty-two: *The binding shall hold while the will holds* — and beside it, in that leaning hand: *Whose will? The rite never says. That omission is not an accident. I have checked four recensions and the omission is in all of them, which means it was cut deliberately before any of them were copied.*

On twenty-six, next to *and the sword shall speak the word that ends it*: *Two words fit here. Two endings. The text permits both and pretends it permits one.*

And then, on page thirty-one, wedged into the bottom margin in handwriting gone small and hurried, as though the writer feared being interrupted:

*I know now that someone else has read this before me and made choices. Passages trimmed. A leaf removed between thirty-four and thirty-five — the stitching holes are wrong, count them yourself. Whoever it was wanted the crueler ending and has spent years — decades — bending every copy toward it. If you are reading this, you are the second reader I have found evidence of, and I am sorry, because the first reader's work does not stop at the page.*

*— V.*

Sera read the note three times. Then she got up, fetched her magnifying lens and her good lamp, and did not sleep again that night.

* * *

By dawn she had counted the stitching holes, twice. There were five where there should be six. Someone had removed a leaf from this exact book, carefully, skillfully, and rebound it so cleanly that only a chronicler who had spent eleven years arguing with binders' guilds about proper technique would ever have caught it.

Someone had edited the prophecy. Not a century ago — within living memory. Within *recent* memory. And they had done it to this copy specifically, which meant they had known where it would be, or where it would go, or whose hands it would pass through.

Her quill hovered over her notes. She wrote: *V. Who is V.?* and then stared at the letter, because the shape of it nagged at her. Not the letter itself — the *hand*. She had seen that hand before. She was almost sure of it. Somewhere in the archive's reference collections, in a treatise or a dedication or—

It came to her over cold breakfast tea, the way these things did, arriving fully formed like a guest who had let themselves in.

The *Codex of Oath-Rites*, folio 190, donor's inscription. She had catalogued it herself six years ago and remembered thinking the donor's script was the most beautiful hand she'd seen outside illuminated gospel-books. She went down two flights of stairs, past the night clerk — who nodded at her without comment, because everyone knew Madam Vane kept strange hours and stranger company these days — and pulled the codex from its shelf with hands that were not quite steady.

*To the Archive, in hope that someone reads better than we wrote. — Vess Arden, Swordspeaker (retired), Thornfell.*

*Swordspeaker.* The word landed in her chest like a dropped coin.

Vess Arden. She knew the name the way every archivist knew it: from the histories, from the purge records, from the quiet footnote at the end of the chapter on the Oath Wars. The last great swordspeaker of the old tradition, teacher of students whose names had also mostly been struck from the rolls, dead — officially of fever, unofficially of something no record dared specify — some fifty years ago. Fifty years. The age of the marginalia's ink.

Hazel Blackwood had called himself a smuggler. He carried oathspeech in his bones like it embarrassed him. And his book — his impossible, century-accurate, personally-named book — bore the annotations of the most famous forbidden teacher of the last two centuries.

"You knew him," Sera murmured to the margin. "You taught him. You left him a map with half the roads scratched out."

Because that was what it felt like, reading the notes end to end in the gray light of morning. Vess Arden had not simply annotated. She had *warned*. Page after page, her lean, urgent hand pressed against the prophecy's smooth certainty: *this clause was altered. This verse is younger than it claims. Here the crueler ending has been stitched in place of the kinder one and the seam is good but not perfect.* She had documented the tampering like a forensic surgeon documenting a crime, and then — Sera flipped to the final annotated page, thirty-nine, and felt her stomach drop — she had stopped.

Page thirty-nine's note read: *I have learned who the first reader is. I cannot write it here. If you have gotten this far, do not trust the court, do not trust the archive, and above all do not trust anyone who asks you what the book says rather than whether you have it. The prophecy offers two endings. One costs a name. The other costs everything else. Someone very powerful has decided which one we get, and they have had a very long time to arrange it.*

Below that, smaller, almost hidden against the gutter of the page:

*The kind ending requires a choice made freely. That is why it must be prevented. Free choices cannot be arranged. Remember this, second reader. The cruelty is not the goal. The cruelty is the lock.*

Sera sat back. Bristle, sensing disturbance, flowed off the bed and wound around her ankles, which was the cat's version of either comfort or dinner negotiation, and Sera honestly could not tell which and didn't care.

*The cruelty is the lock.*

She thought of King Aldric signing orders in that perfect, lifeless hand. Of his shadow lagging half a heartbeat behind him, like a servant who had missed his cue. Of the way the courtiers praised the king's "renewed vigor" and "clarity of purpose" in tones of such careful flatness that Sera had begun keeping a list of who spoke warmly of His Majesty and when, because warmth toward the hollow thing on the throne was starting to look less like loyalty and more like instruction.

She thought of the inquisitors on the switchbacks, calling a scruffy smuggler *the Swordspeaker* as if they'd rehearsed it. As if they'd been waiting a hundred years. Or fifty. As if somewhere, a first reader had finally sent out orders for the second one.

And underneath all of it, quiet and cold, the question Vess Arden had died — probably died — trying to answer: *who benefits from the crueler ending?*

Who benefits from a prophecy fulfilled in blood and binding, from a swordspeaker bound forever into the crown's service, from a king who stays a king — hollow, borrowed, sustained — rather than a king who ends?

The answer assembled itself in her mind with the terrible simplicity of a correctly filed document.

*The crown does. The crown benefits. Whatever wears the crown benefits.*

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she did best: being ignored.

Archivists develop a genius for it. She moved through the Reference Hall and the Records Annex and the Restricted Cases with her cart of returns, and no one saw her, because no one ever saw the person pushing the cart. She checked the accession ledgers for the prophecy book and found its entry disturbingly recent — acquired fourteen months ago, donated through an intermediary, provenance listed as "private collection, Thornfell region." Fourteen months. The same season, she noted with a chill, that the king's new vigor had begun. The same season the inquisitors' budget had tripled. The same season her own department had received its first-ever direct request from the palace: *all documents relating to oath-speech, swordspeech, and the Rite of Crowning, to be transferred to royal custody.*

Transferred. She had helped pack those crates herself. She had assumed scholarly housekeeping.

Now she stood in the Restricted Cases aisle and looked at the empty shelf space where the Codex of Oath-Rites used to sit, and the empty slot where the Aldrican Recension of the crowning liturgy used to sit, and the empty slot, and the empty slot, seven of them in total, each with a transfer slip in a clerk's hand and a palace seal stamped over it like a wax wound.

They weren't hiding the prophecy anymore. They were collecting it. Every version. Every recension. Every copy that might contain a kinder ending — because a kinder ending, freely chosen, was the one thing the thing on the throne could not survive.

Except one copy had slipped through. One copy had ended up in the pack of a smuggler who could actually *read* it, in the old sense, with his blood instead of his eyes.

"Hazel Blackwood," Sera said softly, and the name felt different in her mouth now. Less like an accusation. More like a key she'd been carrying around without knowing the lock.

Bristle appeared at the end of the aisle, tail high, insistent. Dinner negotiation, then. But the cat stopped halfway, ears swiveling toward the hall beyond, and Sera heard it a moment later: boots. Inquisitor boots, the ones with the steel heel-plates, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the building they were walking through.

Three sets. Heading for her office.

She had perhaps a minute. She took the prophecy book off her desk chair, wrapped it in her spare shift, and lowered it into the laundry hamper with the calm of a woman who had hidden contraband chronicles from three separate administrations and considered it practically a core competency. Then she sat down, pulled her current legitimate project — the grain tariffs of the eastern provinces, God help her — across the blotter, and picked up her pen just as the knock came.

"Madam Vane." The inquisitor in the doorway was young, polite, and had eyes like a ledger: everything recorded, nothing felt. "Forgive the hour. Their Majesties' Office of Inquiry has some questions regarding your recent research interests."

"Of course," said Sera, with the gentle smile of a woman with nothing in her hamper but laundry and nothing in her heart but tariffs. "Do come in. Would you like tea?"

What she thought, as the young man stepped inside and his companions took up positions by the door — what she thought, with Vess Arden's warning burning in her memory like a coal —

*The cruelty is the lock. And I am holding the key. Which means, little inquisitor, that you are standing in the wrong room.*

But she poured the tea anyway. She was, after all, a professional.

And when they asked her — gently, terribly politely — whether she had recently come into possession of a certain book, Sera Vane smiled her archivist's smile and began to lie with the meticulousness of a woman transcribing scripture, and in the hamper beside her chair, wrapped in linen, the prophecy waited with both its endings folded inside it like wings.
