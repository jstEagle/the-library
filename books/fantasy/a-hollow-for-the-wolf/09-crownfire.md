## 9. Crownfire

The siphon-channel sang.

That was the only word for it. Hazel had spent three days following it down through the deep roads — a vein of heat running through the stone like a wire through wax, faintly audible if you pressed your ear to the rock. Not a hum, exactly. A song with the melody scraped out of it, leaving only the ache of one. Emberveil's fire, drawn thin as thread and pulled southward, mile after mile, toward the capital and the thing that wore Aldric's crown.

Sera walked beside him with a lantern in one hand and the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, and every hour or so she would say something like, "In two hundred years of court chronicle, no one thought to mention that mountains *sing*," in a tone that suggested she was filing a formal complaint against reality itself.

"They don't," Hazel said. "Not anymore. This is what's left when you take everything out of them."

"Wonderful. I've defected from my entire life to follow a man who hears grief in geology."

"You defected because you saw the king's shadow move late."

"I defected," Sera said, "because I am a chronicler, and I refuse to spend my career writing down lies for a thing that signs its own name wrong." She paused at a bend where the channel-glow brightened along the left-hand wall, then added, quieter: "It signs his name perfectly, actually. That's worse. Aldric's hand trembled. He was old, and he was tired, and he held the pen like a man who'd earned the shaking. This thing writes like a copyist. Every letter identical. I have four hundred pages of his signature and they are all the same signature."

Hazel didn't answer. There wasn't much to say to that which wasn't a scream, and he'd learned on the switchbacks that oathspeech answered screams whether he wanted it to or not.

The tunnel opened without warning.

One moment they were walking single-file through a crack in the world; the next, the crack yawned into a cavern so large his lantern-light simply gave up, swallowed by a red glow rising from below like a fever coming off skin. Heat rolled up in slow breaths. The air tasted of iron and cinders and something older underneath — the same smell as the oath-brand on his palm, he realized, the same smell as the moment Vess had pressed her thumb to it and spoken him into her keeping.

"Down there," Sera whispered. "Hazel. Look *down* there."

He looked.

The foundry filled the cavern floor like a heart grown where a heart shouldn't be. Channels of molten light converged from a dozen directions — the veins they'd been following, and others, dozens of others, threading in from the dark of the mountain like roots feeding a tree. They poured into a central basin where the fire pooled, thick and slow and *wrong*, stripped of its color. Emberveil's flame had been gold going to white at the core. This was orange drained toward gray, fire the way a corpse is a person.

And from the basin rose the mold.

It was crown-shaped. Of course it was. A great ringed form of black iron, taller than a house, and into its inner surface were set hundreds of small sockets, and into the sockets — Hazel's stomach turned over slowly, the way ice turns over on a thawing lake — were set *names*. Slivers of metal, each etched fine as hair with words in the old swordspeech. Oaths. Stolen oaths, pried out of dead or vanished oath-takers and hammered into the crown's bones like nails holding a coffin shut.

"That's why they vanish," Sera breathed. "The exiled oath-takers. The ones who swear service to the crown and are never seen again. It's not a purge. It's a *harvest*."

"It's a furnace," Hazel said, "and we're standing in its chimney."

The heat pulsed. Below them, catwalks of black iron crossed the basin, and figures moved along them — not men, or not anymore. They moved with the same half-second lag Sera had described in the king's shadow, bodies answering their intentions a beat behind, like puppets whose strings had stretched. Foundry-hands. Hollow ones. They fed the channels, adjusted valves of rune-carved brass, and never once looked up.

"We should go around," Sera said. "There has to be another route. Hazel. There *has* to be."

"There isn't. I've been listening to this channel for three days. All the veins run through here. It's the heart." He crouched at the cavern's lip, watching the fire pool and drain, pool and drain, and felt the brand on his palm itch in time with it. "And hearts can be closed."

"You want to close a *channel*." Sera's voice climbed. "Of dragon-fire. With what, exactly? Your charming personality?"

"With the words."

She went quiet. In the red light her face looked carved, careful, a chronicler deciding how much truth to write down while it was still happening. "Vess's marginalia said the gift answers before you choose it. That it's the same root as the binding rite. If you speak power here, in the place where the binding is being *maintained*—"

"Then I'll find out what I am," Hazel said. "I've been finding out since the waystation. Might as well do it somewhere with good acoustics."

"That is the worst joke anyone has ever made at me."

"I know. I'm sorry. Nerves." He meant it, mostly. But underneath the nerves — and he examined this feeling the way he'd examine a lock before picking it, honestly, professionally — underneath the fear was something else. Something that had been growing since the ambush on the switchbacks, since the word that leapt from his mouth unbidden and cut a rope of inquisitor-magic like it was wet string.

It felt like coming home to a house he'd never lived in.

* * *

They descended by way of a maintenance stair cut into the cavern wall, narrow and rusted, built — Sera noted grimly — by hands that must have belonged to someone, once. Halfway down, the heat became a physical weight, pressing on his shoulders like a second cloak. The brand on his palm woke fully.

It didn't hurt. That was the strange part. It *sang*, the way the channel sang, except the brand still had its melody. And the melody was the same one. His oath and the stolen fire were harmonics of a single note, and he understood, standing on the third catwalk with his boots ringing on black iron, exactly what Vess had tried to keep him from understanding:

The same rite that bound an oath into a swordspeaker's palm had bound the hollow thing into Aldric's body. One craft. One grammar. The crown was just a sentence written very large, in fire instead of ink, and someone had been editing it for a hundred years.

Below, the nearest channel ran through a brass collar no bigger than a wagon wheel — a junction where two veins merged before feeding the basin. If he closed that, the flow wouldn't stop. But it would stutter. Back up. Buy Emberveil time, maybe a season, maybe a year.

A season for a dying dragon was a coin tossed down a well. He did it anyway.

"Hazel." Sera caught his sleeve. Her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Whatever you're about to do — the book says the first true word costs the speaker. Not blood. Not years. Something else. Vess underlined it twice."

"I read the underline."

"And?"

"And I'm a smuggler, Sera. I've spent my whole life paying tolls to people I despise. At least this toll goes to me." He smiled at her, and was surprised to find the smile mostly real. "Stay behind the rail. If I start speaking and don't stop, hit me. Hard. Somewhere bony."

"I'm a court chronicler. I've ended three careers with a well-placed memorandum. I can manage a punch."

He knelt at the rail above the junction. The heat came up through the iron grating and licked at his knees, and the brand on his palm burned now, truly burned, and the song below rose to meet it — and for one suspended moment Hazel Blackwood, last of the oath-bound, knelt inside the machine that was killing the last dragon, and listened.

The channel had a seam. Everything did. Smugglers knew this the way priests knew scripture: every wall has a door, every chain a link, every law a loophole, because the world is made by hands and hands leave gaps. The channel was a sentence too. A long, grinding, century-old sentence of taking, and every sentence had a word that could end it.

He didn't decide to speak. That was the terrifying, wonderful truth of it. He opened — the way you open a fist, the way you open a window — and the word came through him.

It was not a word in any language he knew, and he knew it completely. It tasted of winter rivers and the first fire ever struck and Vess's voice saying *again, boy, again* in a yard behind a waystation twenty years gone. It was one syllable long and it contained an argument with the universe, and the universe, hearing it, conceded the point.

The junction closed.

Not melted. Not broken. *Closed*, the way a hand closes, the way a book closes, final and deliberate, and the two veins of stolen fire slammed against the seal and backed up and went dark for fifty yards in either direction, cooling from molten gold to sullen ember-red to nothing.

The sound it made was enormous and utterly silent — a pressure change, a note held and released — and every hollow foundry-hand on every catwalk stopped at once and turned their faces up toward him, all together, like a field of flowers tracking a sun that had just told them *no*.

"Yes!" Sera shouted, gripping the rail. "Yes, that's — oh, that's *magnificent*, write itself down, I'll remember every—"

Then the backlash hit.

It came back up the word like a whip cracking backward through the hand that cracked it. Hazel had braced for pain. Pain would have been fine. Pain was familiar; he had a professional relationship with pain going back to his first frozen pass at fourteen. This wasn't pain. This was *subtraction*.

Something left him. He felt it go — felt it the way you feel a tooth after the pulling, that ghost-weight, that absence with a shape. Warmth, maybe. Or the memory of warmth. Or the name of warmth. He gripped the rail and rode it out, teeth locked, and the world went white at the edges and very loud at the center, and somewhere far away Sera was shouting his name in the tone people use for people falling.

When it passed, he was on his knees with his forehead against the grating, breathing like a bellows, and the brand on his palm had changed.

It had always been a small thing — a spiral knot, Vess's thumbprint in scar, the mark of a kept oath. Now silver lines ran out from it, fine as the etchings in the crown's name-nails, branching up across his wrist and forearm in patterns that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts, the way truth hurts. They caught the dying light of the sealed channel and held it. When he flexed his fingers, the lines moved. Not glowed. *Moved.*

"Oh," Sera said softly. She had knelt beside him without him noticing, which frightened him more than the foundry-hands had. "Oh, Hazel."

"Don't write that part down."

"I'm absolutely writing that part down." But her voice shook, and her hand hovered over his arm without touching, the way you hover over a burn. "What did it take? What did you lose?"

He searched himself, honestly, the way he'd inventory a pack after a hard crossing. His mother's face — there. Vess's voice — there. The smell of the Thornfell pines in autumn, the weight of a good blade, the taste of bad wine drunk on a good night. All there. All present.

But there was a hole. Small, round, clean-edged, somewhere in the middle of him, and when he reached for it, it reached back with nothing.

"I don't know yet," he said. "That's the honest answer. Ask me tomorrow. Ask me in a year. Apparently this is a subscription, not a purchase."

Above them, distantly, a bell began to ring — a foundry-bell, a shift-change bell, except the shift hadn't changed and the hollow hands were no longer pretending to work. They were converging. Slowly, with that terrible half-second lag, dozens of them, flowing up the catwalks like mercury finding its level.

"Sera," Hazel said, standing, silver lines crawling past his elbow, the exhilaration of the sealed channel still singing in his chest even as the cost sat cold in the middle of him — "how attached are you to the way we came in?"

"Deeply. Why?"

"Because we're leaving a different way."

* * *

They went *down*.

It was against every instinct Hazel owned — smugglers flee upward, toward passes, toward sky, toward the lie of escape — but the lower galleries were older than the foundry, cut before the crown had ever thought to harvest fire, and they were full of doors. Not real doors. Seams. Places where the mountain remembered being open and could be persuaded to be open again, if you asked in the right language.

He asked in the right language. Three times. Each word cost a little — he could feel the tolls accruing, small debits against some account he couldn't see — and each time the rock ground apart just wide enough, and each time Sera muttered something about how in a just world this talent would be taxed and licensed, and each time he laughed, breathless, terrified, alive in a way he had never been in thirty years of careful anonymity.

The pursuit fell behind. Hollow things, he realized, are poor climbers — they follow orders, and the orders said *catwalks*, and the mountain was done providing catwalks.

They stopped at last in a dry gallery where water had once run, and Sera built a tiny fire from smuggler's twist and lit it with hands that only shook a little, and for a while neither of them spoke. The silver marks on his arm dimmed to a faint lacework, visible when the firelight crossed it, invisible when it didn't. Like frost on a window. Like something growing.

"The channel's closed," Sera said finally. "One of dozens. We bought her a season, maybe." She turned the prophecy book over in her lap, not opening it. "Was it worth it?"

Hazel looked at his arm. He thought about Emberveil in her guttering hollow, ancient and patient, calling him *little word-bearer* in a voice like coals settling. He thought about the clean round hole inside him and whatever used to live there.

"Yeah," he said. "Ask me tomorrow."

And then the firelight caught something he'd missed — because the mountain, having chosen its side, apparently intended to keep choosing. Scratched into the gallery wall, old enough to be worn soft, in a hand he knew better than his own: *Vess was here.* And beneath it, smaller, hurried, in the same script:

*If you seal the channels, it will know your name. It collects names. Come home before it learns to say yours.*

Sera read it upside down, the way chroniclers read everything, and went pale. "When was your mentor here?"

"Vess died eight years ago."

"Then who taught the foundry where to put its sockets?" Sera whispered. "Hazel — the name-nails. The etchings. I've seen that handwriting before. Tonight. On the king's orders."

Hazel looked at the wall, at his mentor's soft-worn letters, at the silver lacework crawling up his own arm in handwriting that suddenly seemed less like frost and more like practice.

Somewhere far to the north, in a palace that signed its name perfectly, something had just learned a new word.

His.
