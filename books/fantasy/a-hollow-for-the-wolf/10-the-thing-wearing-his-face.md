## 10. The Thing Wearing His Face

The summons came at dawn, folded in vellum so fine it felt like holding a moth.

Sera Vane read it three times by candlelight, though the words did not change: *Sera Vane, Chronicler of the Third Chair, is commanded to attend His Majesty's private audience at the ninth bell. Come alone. Come prepared to speak of Thornfell.*

*Come alone.* In eleven years at court, Sera had never once been summoned alone. Audiences were crowds; crowds were armor. A private audience with the king was either an honor or a grave, and lately the two had grown difficult to tell apart.

She dressed carefully, which was to say she dressed like a woman who had nothing to hide and then hid everything anyway. The prophecy pages went into the false binding of a tax ledger from the reign of Aldric's grandfather — dull enough that no thief would touch it, boring enough that even the censors slept. Her notes on the king's shadow went into her left boot, because no one searches a chronicler's boots. Chroniclers were furniture. That was the whole point of them, and for once she was grateful for it.

In the mirror, she practiced a face. Not innocence — innocence invited scrutiny. She practiced *mild competence*, the expression of a woman whose greatest worry that morning was ink smudges. She had worn it through two wars, one plague scare, and the entire tenure of Master Corvin, who had once made her re-copy four hundred pages because she'd used the wrong shade of brown for a border. Mild competence could survive anything.

Her reflection did not quite believe her. She decided that was fine. Neither did she.

* * *

The palace at the ninth bell smelled wrong.

Sera noticed it on the Long Stair, that marble spine running up the heart of the citadel. Courtiers passed her in their silks, bowing to nothing, chattering about a harvest festival that had been cancelled twice and rescheduled anyway, and beneath all of it hung a scent like a snuffed candle — not smoke exactly, but the memory of smoke, the ghost a flame leaves behind when someone has pinched it out rather than let it die honestly.

Wax, she thought. Tallow. Something burning down to its socket.

The guards at the royal doors did not check her ledger. They did not check anything. They stood too still, the way furniture stands, and when they opened the doors they did so in perfect unison, without a word between them, as if they were one guard wearing two bodies.

Which, she was beginning to suspect, was more or less how the kingdom was being run.

"Chronicler Vane."

The king's study was smaller than she expected. Kings performed their majesty in vast rooms; this was a working chamber, warm-lit, lined with books she itched to catalog. And there at the desk sat King Aldric the Second of his name, quill moving across parchment in that flawless hand she'd watched for months — every letter identical, every line spaced to the hair's breadth, writing that looked less composed than *printed*.

He did not look up. "You are punctual. Good. Punctuality is a form of honesty."

"Your Majesty honors me." She bowed to the correct depth for a private audience and waited for him to set down the quill.

He didn't. He kept writing while he spoke, and that was the first horror of the morning: kings stopped writing when people entered. Kings looked up. This man wrote on, steady as a loom, his voice coming out level and pleasant while his hand produced another perfect sentence about grain tariffs.

"You have served the crown eleven years," he said. "You have never missed a deadline. You have never petitioned for leave. You have never married, though you were courted twice — once by a knight who bored you and once by a poet who frightened you, and you chose neither because choosing would have meant being known, and being known is a risk you have never learned to afford."

The quill scratched. Sera's mouth had gone dry somewhere around *the knight*.

"I — Your Majesty is well informed."

"I am the crown," he said mildly. "Being informed is the least of what I am." Now he set down the pen, aligned it parallel to the page's edge with a small precise click, and looked at her.

His eyes were the problem. Everyone at court said the king had kind eyes, tired eyes, a good man worn honest by rule. These eyes were kind the way a portrait is kind — accurate, static, finished. They held warmth the way a hearth holds last night's fire: the shape of it, none of the heat.

And behind him, cast by three candles and no window, his shadow lay on the wall — and lay *wrong*, angled slightly off from where physics wanted it, like a guest seated in someone else's chair.

"You've been reading old things," the king said. "Prophetic things. From Thornfell."

Here it was, then. Sera arranged her mild competence over her hammering heart like a tablecloth over a stain. "I catalog what I'm given, Majesty. The archive receives materials from every province—"

"Sera." He said her name softly, and something in the softness was worse than shouting. "Please don't do the voice. The little bureaucratic voice. It's very good — truly, it's excellent work — but we both know what you found, and pretending otherwise wastes the hour, and my hours are..." He paused, head tilting, as if consulting something. "...fewer than they appear."

Silence stretched. Somewhere below, a bell rang the quarter-hour, and Sera realized she was counting bells the way sailors count waves — for comfort, for proof the world still moved on schedule.

"The book names a swordspeaker," she said finally. "It names a date. The date is soon."

"Yes."

"And you want me to find him."

The king rose. It was a smooth motion, too smooth, joints moving like well-oiled hinges rather than joints, and he crossed to the window — a real window here, overlooking the inner garden — and stood with his hands clasped behind him in the posture of every pensive monarch ever painted.

"I want you to *deliver* him," he said. "There is a difference, and the difference matters enormously to both of us."

*Both of us.* She turned the phrase over and found the crack in it. There was only one of them in the room. Unless there wasn't.

"Majesty, I'm a chronicler. If the crown wants a man found, the crown has soldiers, inquisitors, spies—"

"The inquisitors are mine, yes." He said it the way a man says *my dogs*. "But they break what they catch. I need this one brought gently. Unspoiled. And you—" he turned, and the smile arrived on his face like a document being stamped, correct and complete and utterly without origin, "—you find things. You found the book. You found the marginalia. Don't look surprised; I know about the marginalia. You are, quietly, the best finder in my kingdom, and you have spent eleven years making sure no one noticed. I noticed. I notice everything now. It is one of the few gifts of my condition."

"Your condition," Sera repeated, before she could stop herself.

The smile stayed exactly where it was. Nothing else did. For a moment — half a breath, half a heartbeat — the thing wearing the king's face seemed to recede behind its own eyes, like a lamp carried back down a corridor, and what looked out at her was old and vast and terribly patient, and then it was gone and Aldric was Aldric again, tired and reasonable, a king asking a favor.

"A long illness," he said. "Managed. You will find references to it in the chronicles of my coronation year, if you look closely — the week the court was closed, the foreign physicians. I am not well, Sera. I am sustained. And what sustains me requires... maintenance. Cooperation. A swordspeaker's cooperation, specifically, or everything I hold together comes apart, and I promise you—" and here his voice dropped, and for one instant it sounded genuinely afraid, which frightened her more than anything else, because fear implied stakes and stakes implied truth, "—you do not want to see what comes apart. The dark under the mountain is not a story. I have seen it. I am, in a sense, the wall built against it, and walls do not apologize for their masonry."

"The book offers two endings," Sera said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Unbinding or binding. Which do you want?"

"Binding, naturally." Promptly. Too promptly. "The swordspeaker bound to the crown, oath and soul, a new pillar for the old wall. It is the merciful ending. Ask yourself why your dead mentor feared it."

*I have,* she thought. *Every night since the foundry.*

"And if I refuse?"

The king considered her with those portrait eyes. Then he did something worse than threaten her. He walked to his desk, drew out a shallow drawer, and lifted out a small painting — a miniature, the kind wealthy families commissioned, and Sera's breath stopped because she knew the faces in it. Her mother. Her father. The little house outside Marrowdale with the crooked chimney, painted the summer before the fever took them both, when Sera was nine and already hiding in books.

"You sold this," she whispered. "When the debts came. I sold it myself. To a broker in Lowmarket. I was fifteen."

"You did. And I bought it. Or rather, the crown's collection acquired it, decades ago, through channels, for reasons that seemed sensible at the time." He set it on the desk between them, facing her, and stepped back as if presenting a gift. "I am not threatening you, Sera. I want to be very clear about that. Threats create rebels. I am *offering* you. Your family's history restored to you. Your position elevated — First Chronicler, access to the sealed archives, the ones with answers to questions you've been asking alone in the dark since you were a girl reading by stolen candlelight. And above all: safety. Deliver the swordspeaker, and you will be protected from everything that is coming. Because it *is* coming, whatever ending the book promises. Storms come regardless. The only question is whether you're inside the wall or outside it."

Sera looked at the miniature. Her mother's smile. Her father's crooked collar, painted crooked because he'd refused to sit still. Real things. Imperfect things. Everything the handwriting on the king's orders was not.

And underneath the ache of it, cold and clear, the chronicler's mind did what it always did: it *read*. It read the room. It read the drawer that had held the painting, positioned too conveniently near the desk, as if the piece had been staged. It read the offer itself — generous, detailed, tailored — and noted that lies are rarely tailored, because lies are mass-produced, and only truth gets fitted to the individual.

That was the trap inside the trap. The offer might be sincere. The hollow thing might genuinely want the binding ending, might genuinely believe itself the wall against the dark. Villains lied, but wounds told the truth, and this creature was, if Hazel's theory held, a wound wearing a crown.

Which meant the most dangerous possibility of all: that delivering Hazel Blackwood really would save the kingdom, and cost one smuggler his freedom, and cost Sera nothing but a lie she'd have to live inside for the rest of her life.

"Majesty," she heard herself say, "I found the book. I can find the man. But I'll need time, and authority, and — I'll need to go back through Thornfell. The trail runs through the deep roads. Smugglers' territory. Soldiers can't follow. I can."

Something flickered behind the king's eyes. Satisfaction? Suspicion? With this creature she could no longer tell a smile from a snarl; both arrived stamped and correct.

"Time, yes. Authority — you shall have a writ under my seal." He returned to the desk, took up the quill, and wrote — of course — flawlessly, the seal appearing as if it had always been there. "Go to Thornfell. Find the swordspeaker. And Sera—" the king looked up, and for just a moment the lamplight caught his face at an angle where the likeness failed entirely, where the skin seemed lit from some other direction than the candles, "—do not mistake gentleness for weakness. I have been gentle with this kingdom for a hundred years. I can stop."

*A hundred years.* The words rang in her skull all the way down the Long Stair, past the too-still guards, past the courtiers chattering about the festival. Aldric the Second had reigned twenty-three years. She had counted them herself, in her own hand, in the official rolls.

She walked out of the palace with the writ warm in her pocket and her family's faces burning behind her eyes, and she did not stop walking until she reached the archive, and locked the door, and pulled the tax ledger from her satchel with shaking hands.

Because there was one thing the king had asked for that she hadn't given him. One question she'd swallowed.

*Ask yourself why your dead mentor feared it.*

Vess had feared the binding. Vess had edited toward the crueler ending — toward unbinding — to spare Hazel. And Vess's handwriting was on the foundry walls, teaching sockets where to sit, which meant Vess had known about the hollow king years ago and had spent her last years building toward its end.

Sera opened the ledger, took out her pen, and began, in her own imperfect human hand, a letter she did not yet know how she would send:

*Hazel — it knows about Vess. It offered me my mother's smile. I said yes to its face. Meet me where the smugglers drink, the third door down in Oathbreakers' Row, before the ninth bell tomorrow. Bring the dragon.*

She stopped. Read it back. Crossed out *bring the dragon* and wrote instead:

*Bring everything you have. We are going to need a bigger lie than mine.*

Below the palace, in the dark under the city, something that had watched her through the king's eyes all morning turned its attention, slow and satisfied, toward the mountains — and began, in a flawless hand, to write her name on a list.
