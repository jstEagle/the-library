## 11. Oathbreakers' Market

The way into the Oathbreakers' Market was through a dead man's chimney.

That was the joke, anyway — the smugglers' joke, told in every tavern from Thornfell to the coast: *you go down the chimney like Father Winter, and what you bring out is nobody's gift.* The chimney belonged to a shepherd's cottage that had burned a hundred years ago and never been rebuilt, standing alone on a shelf of grey hillside where no sheep had grazed since. Hazel had used it twice before, back when his cargo was moonsteel and his worries were ordinary.

He stood at its lip now with Sera Vane beside him, looking down into a throat of soot and old stone, and felt the weight of everything he'd learned since his last visit settle on him like wet wool.

"You're certain this is the place," Sera said. She said it the way she said most things — flat, precise, a chronicler's voice that made even doubt sound like an entry in a ledger.

"I'm certain it's a chimney."

"That is not reassuring."

"It's not meant to be. Reassurance costs extra down there." He swung his legs over the edge. "Everything does."

The descent was forty feet of handholds worn smooth by generations of palms that would rather have been anywhere else. At the bottom, a door that looked like a wall until you knocked on it in the rhythm of a song nobody sang anymore — three slow, two fast, one slow, then wait, because patience was the real password — swung inward onto torchlight, woodsmoke, and the smell of too many people keeping secrets in one room.

The market had grown since Hazel last came down. It lived in the bones of an old mine, galleries cut into the mountain's ribs, and tonight every gallery was crowded. He'd expected perhaps thirty exiles huddled over a cookfire. What he found was closer to two hundred: oath-takers who had walked away from crown service, hedge-speakers whose words had gone strange, brand-bearers like himself, and a scattering of folk who were simply running and had heard the market took anyone who could pay in coin, gossip, or a genuinely good recipe.

Above them all, strung between the gallery beams on copper wire, hung dozens of small bells. They never rang. That was the point. Each bell was bound to a speaker's oath, and when an oath-taker broke their word inside the market, the bell tolled once, and everyone stopped, and everyone looked, and the breaker was escorted to the door by whatever large persons happened to be nearest. There were no second offenses. The bells saw to that.

Sera stared up at them. "They're silent."

"Silence means honest," Hazel said. "Around here, silence is the whole religion."

A woman with a ledger chained to her wrist met them at the foot of the entry ramp. She had the look of someone who had once been a soldier and had since discovered she liked counting things better than killing them. Her eyes went to Hazel's gloved right hand first, as everyone's did eventually, though most had the manners to start with his face.

"Name?" she said.

"No."

"Everyone has a name."

"Then I'm Everyone." He set a moonsteel bar on her ledger. "Two entries. The lady and me. We're buying, not selling."

She weighed the bar in her palm, bit it out of tradition rather than necessity, and something flickered behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, or the memory of it. "Word-bearers buy trouble," she said. "Trouble's the one thing we don't sell."

"Good. I brought my own."

* * *

They moved through the market slowly, because Hazel wanted Sera to see it, and because he wanted to hear what the market would say when it thought he wasn't listening.

What it said was quieter than he'd hoped and worse than he'd feared.

"—gone since midsummer, whole company of them, escorting a fire-cart east—"

"—the Warden of Grayharrow, remember him? Big man, oath of the gate? His wife came asking at the winter fair. Nobody's seen him since the king's birthday. Nobody remembers his face right, either. She swears she does, but she cries when she tries—"

"—my cousin swore the road-oath for the crown three years back. Writes letters. Well. Wrote. The last one was just his name, over and over, eleven times, like he was practicing not forgetting it—"

Hazel kept his hood up and his pace even. Beside him, Sera had gone very still, the stillness of a woman taking dictation from ghosts.

"They're being harvested," she murmured. "Names, oaths, faces. It's not just dragon-fire that feeds the hollow thing. It's *identity*. A body made of stolen selves needs a supply."

"I know."

"You knew?"

"I suspected. Now I know I suspected correctly, which is different, and worse." He nodded toward a gallery arch hung with small wooden plaques, each carved with a name, each name struck through. Dozens of plaques. More than dozens. "The market keeps a wall for the vanished. It used to be a corner. Look at it now."

Sera looked. Hazel watched her look — watched the chronicler in her do arithmetic she didn't want to finish. When she turned back, her jaw was set in a way he was beginning to recognize as the beginning of courage.

"We need more than allies," she said. "We need witnesses. If the kingdom forgets these people—"

"The kingdom forgot a king. It'll manage a warden." He said it gently, which somehow made it crueler, and hated himself a little for the skill. "Come on. There's someone here I need to insult professionally."

* * *

Dain Ferrow could be found, as always, near the best light in any room, because Dain believed that luck was a currency and presentation was its mint. He sat at a card table under the largest lantern in the eastern gallery, dealing stones-and-shadows to three merchants who were losing with the particular grace of men who did not yet know they were losing. He hadn't changed much in four years: still lean as a fence rail, still wearing a coat one season ahead of his finances, still smiling like the smile had been fitted by a good tailor.

His eyes found Hazel across the room before Hazel had decided how to approach. Of course they did. Dain had always had a knack for noticing the thing you least wanted noticed.

"Well," Dain said loudly, sweeping in the last hand before his victims could object, "either the mountain's finally fallen on Blackwood, or the mountain's finally spat him out. Gentlemen, excuse me — I have an appointment with history."

"You have an appointment with a debt," Hazel said, arriving at the table. "I'm just early."

"And here I'd heard you'd gone respectable. Smuggling with *purpose*, they say. Smuggling with *conviction*." Dain poured two cups of wine without asking, which was either hospitality or a trap; with Dain it was usually both. "Conviction doesn't pay tolls, friend."

"Neither do you."

"Ha!" Dain raised his cup to the room at large, as if Hazel had paid him a compliment worth sharing. Then his gaze slid past Hazel's shoulder and sharpened. "And who's your shadow? No — wait. Let me guess. Court ink on her fingers, court fear in her eyes, and she's carrying something under that cloak that weighs more than the cloak. Am I close?"

"She's not for sale," Hazel said.

"Everything's for sale. That's the first law of markets." Dain leaned back, and for half a heartbeat the tailor-made smile slipped, and underneath it was something older and wearier — the face of a man who had taken the same oath Hazel had, years ago, and walked away from it for reasons he'd never once explained sober. "Second law: everything has a price, and the price is always higher than you think."

Hazel sat. "Then let's talk prices."

* * *

They talked in the back gallery, where the mine's old ventilation shaft breathed cold air down through a crack in the rock, and the bells overhead hung silent as sleeping birds. Hazel laid it out plainly, because Dain respected plainness the way cats respect closed doors — as a challenge.

The hollow king. The siphon-channels running fire from Emberveil's hollow to the capital. The prophecy book with Hazel's name in it, dated to a night that was coming fast. The vanished oath-takers. All of it, except the parts about Vess, which Hazel kept behind his teeth like a stone he wasn't ready to spit out.

Dain listened without interrupting, which was so unlike him that Hazel checked twice for fever.

When it was done, Dain was quiet for a long moment, turning his cup in rings on the tabletop.

"How many," he said finally. "On your vanished wall. How many names?"

"Forty-one plaques. Some struck through twice."

"Forty-one." He laughed, short and airless. "There were sixty-two of us in my oath-cohort. Sixty-two children swearing on a sword-hilt bigger than we were, promised glory and pension both. You know how many are left that I can account for?" He held up a hand. Fingers spread. Folded three down. "Seven. And two of those are me, depending on the day."

"Dain—"

"Don't." The word came out with an edge Hazel had never heard him use. "Don't do the warm voice. Don't do the *old rival, old friend* voice. I know what you're going to ask. You want bodies for a fight against the crown itself, and you want them cheap, because that's what causes cost — nothing, to the man holding them." He drained his wine. Set the cup down with terrible gentleness. "So. Name your price. Mine first: the book."

Hazel went still. "No."

"The prophecy book. The one with your name in it. That's my price."

"Why in all the hells would you want it?"

"Because I can read, Blackwood, despite the coat." Dain's smile came back, but thin, stretched over something grim. "A century-old book that names a living man for a specific night isn't a curiosity. It's a *ledger*. Someone wrote that. Someone with access, and intent, and a hundred years' head start. I've spent ten years learning who sells to the crown and who sells to the thing wearing it, and every trail goes cold in the same decade — the decade that book was written. I want the handwriting. I want the marginalia. I want to know who edited our lives before we lived them."

The word *edited* landed in Hazel's chest like a dropped anchor. Vess's careful script. Vess's corrections. He kept his face still through long practice and long grief.

"The book stays with me," he said. "But you can read it. Any time you want. Under my eye."

"Under your eye." Dain snorted. "You guard that book like a mother wolf."

"Maybe I am one."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the cold air breathed down from the shaft, and somewhere above them a bell gave the faintest copper hum — not ringing, only remembering how — and fell silent again.

"Three conditions," Dain said at last, ticking them off on ringed fingers. "One: I read the book whenever I ask, and you don't hover. Two: when this is done — however it ends — the book comes to me, or to whoever's left to hold it. Three—" he paused, and the tailor's smile fell away entirely, and what was underneath was almost naked, "—you tell me why a man who spent ten years building himself a fine anonymous little life threw it all away the moment his name showed up in an old book. Because that's not fear, Hazel. That's *faith*. And I want to know what you believe in badly enough to die scheduled."

Hazel looked at him for a long time.

"I don't believe in anything," he said. "That's the point. The book says I'm either the king's unbinding or its final binding, and I've read enough of it to know that whichever I choose, somebody chose it for me a century ago. I'm not fighting for faith, Dain. I'm fighting for the right to be wrong on purpose."

Dain stared. Then he laughed — really laughed, loud enough that heads turned in the gallery, loud enough that the bells trembled and did not ring.

"Oh, you're *worse* than I remembered." He stuck out his hand. "Done. Bodies, blades, and my famously mediocre tactical genius. In exchange for reading rights and one posthumous bequest." His grip was dry and strong and did not let go quite immediately. "One more thing, free of charge, because I'm feeling historical. You'll want to know who else has been asking after that book."

Hazel's stomach cooled. "Who?"

"A buyer came through three weeks ago. Paid in new-minted crown silver — which, incidentally, hasn't been minted since the king stopped signing his own orders properly, if you follow me. Asking after a swordspeaker traveling with a woman. Asking after a *book*." Dain released his hand and reached into his coat, drawing out a folded scrap of paper, soft with handling. "Left this behind. My professional opinion? Deliberately. Bait wants to be found."

Hazel unfolded it. The paper was fine, capital-fine, and the hand that had written on it was perfect — every letter identical, every line evenly spaced, the writing of something that had learned penmanship from a rulebook rather than a wrist.

Four words:

**WE KNOW ABOUT VESS.**

The cold from the ventilation shaft seemed suddenly to fill the whole gallery. Beside him, Sera read it upside-down and made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"Dain," Hazel said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, "how fast can you get us out of this market?"

"Depends," said Dain Ferrow, already reaching for his coat, "on how attached you are to using doors."
