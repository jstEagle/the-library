## 12. Vess's Last Letter

The book had been trying to tell me for three days.

I should have noticed sooner. Vess taught me better than that — *a book is a door, Hazel, and doors are made by people who want something on the other side* — but grief makes a man stupid, and I'd been grieving her twice over: once when she died, and once again when I read her name in the margin of a prophecy written before she was born.

We were camped in the third hollow of the deep roads, the one the smugglers called the Kettle because the air down there was always warm and faintly tasted of copper. Emberveil's fire-siphon ran somewhere beneath us; I could feel it in my teeth, a low thrum like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Dain was on watch, which meant he was asleep sitting up with his knife in his hand, which was as close to trust as Dain ever came. Sera sat across the fire from me with the prophecy book open on her knees, and she kept doing the thing she did when a text was lying to her: tilting her head, like a dog hearing a whistle no one else could hear.

"It's wrong," she said, for the fourth time since dusk.

"The whole book is wrong," I said. "That's rather its defining feature."

"No." She turned a page with the exaggerated care of someone who had spent her life handling documents older than kingdoms. "The *margins* are wrong. Look." She held it up to the firelight. The marginalia — Vess's handwriting, cramped and slanted, the same hand that had filled every notebook I'd ever seen her carry — marched down the page in annotations a century old. *Check the third clause against the Thornfell recension.* *This ending assumes the bearer consents.* *Crueler reading preferred — see note.*

"See note," I repeated. "What note? Where's the note?"

"That's what I've been asking." Sera's finger traced the spine of the book, down along the binding, and stopped. Her nail caught on something. A seam. A ridge where there shouldn't have been one. "Hazel," she said slowly, "how attached are you to this binding?"

"It belonged to Vess."

"How *attached*."

I took the book from her. The spine was leather over boards, worn soft at the corners where a hundred years of hands — or one hand, a hundred years' worth of nights — had gripped it. And now that I looked, really looked, the leather along the back was a hair too thick. A hair too new. Someone had re-bound this book, carefully, lovingly, and hidden something in the space between the boards.

Vess used to say that about me, too. That I was thicker than I looked, and newer than I pretended.

"Give me your knife," I said.

"You have your own knife."

"I'm not cutting it with an oath-blade, Dain will sulk."

Dain, without opening his eyes or moving any part of himself except his arm, flipped his knife through the firelight so it landed point-down in the dirt between my boots. "Sulk later," he said. "Cut the book now."

I worked the blade under the spine-leather the way you skin a hare — slow, shallow, respectful of what's underneath. The glue crackled. The leather peeled back in a long curl, and there, sewn into the hollow between the boards with thread gone amber with age, was a packet of oilcloth no bigger than my palm.

Nobody said anything. The fire popped. Somewhere far off in the dark of the deep roads, water dripped onto stone with the patience of a thing that had been dripping since before the mountain had a name.

I unwrapped it with hands that were not entirely steady.

Three pages. Folded small, written dense, front and back, in Vess's hand — but younger. This was her writing from decades ago, before the arthritis bent her letters, before the cough. Before me, maybe. Or right at the beginning of me.

*Hazel,* it began, and my heart did something complicated and painful, because of course it began with me. Everything, apparently, began with me. I just hadn't been told.

I read it aloud, because Sera needed to hear it and because I didn't trust myself to read it silently. Some things you have to say out loud or they stay trapped inside you, growing teeth.

---

*Hazel, if you're reading this, then the book found you, which means I failed to stop it, which means you're reading this in the worst year of your life, and I am sorry. I have been sorry since before you could walk.*

*You deserve the truth plainly, so here it is: I edited the prophecy. Not copied it. Edited it. The original is older than the kingdom and crueler than anything I've written down, and I had a choice, the night I sat with it in the archive under Thornfell, about which version would survive. I chose the one that hurts you.*

*Let me explain before you hate me, though you'll hate me anyway, and you should, a little. It keeps a person honest.*

*The prophecy offers two endings. They share a beginning — a swordspeaker, an oath-brand, a hollow king — but they diverge at the last word. In one ending, the swordspeaker speaks the first word of swordspeech and lives. The king is unbound, the dragon-fire returns, and the speaker walks out of the smoke with his name still in his mouth. That is the ending everyone who has ever read this book has wanted. It is also, I believe after thirty years of study, a lie. A beautiful lie. The kind the prophecy tells on purpose, because prophecies are traps set by people who loved the future more than they trusted it.*

*I tested it. Don't ask how. Ask instead why there are no other swordspeakers, Hazel. Why the order died out. Why every record of the last three bearers ends mid-sentence, their names scraped from memorials, their students unable to recall their faces. The kind ending doesn't spare them. It just makes the forgetting painless for everyone except the one being forgotten.*

*In the other ending — the true one, I think — the first word costs everything. The name goes out of the world entirely. No grave, no song, no shape in the smoke. And the world goes on, which is the cruelest mercy I know.*

*So I chose the crueler ending and wrote it into the margins, and I bound the truth in the spine, and I spent forty years making sure the book would find you late. After I was dead. Because I knew you, little wolf. If you'd learned at fifteen what you were for, you'd have run straight at it, and I couldn't —*

*Here the ink blots. She'd been crying, or the archive roof had leaked, and knowing Vess it was both.*

*— I couldn't watch you do that. So I gave you a quiet life instead. Smuggling. Back roads. Anonymity like armor. I told myself the prophecy might simply never come due, that dates in old books are suggestions, that a man can outrun a sentence written a century before his birth.*

*I was wrong. I'm sorry. I was so tired, and I loved you, and those two things made a coward of me.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you be angry in peace. The hollow king is not only a monster. Remember that, whatever else happens. Remember that someone chose the binding once, believing it was mercy, the way I chose your ending believing it was kindness. We are all of us editors, Hazel. We all cut the story to spare someone. The question is only ever: who pays for our scissors.*

*If there's time — and there won't be — go to Emberveil. She remembers the original. She was there. Let her teach you what I was too afraid to.*

*Burn this letter after. Or don't. You never listened to me anyway.*

*— V., who was wrong about almost everything except you.*

---

The fire had burned down to coals while I read. My voice gave out somewhere around *coward*, and Sera took the last lines silently from my hand, her lips moving, her eyes wet in a way she'd deny later.

Dain, who had absolutely been awake the entire time, said quietly: "She hid it for a hundred years."

"She hid it for *me*," I said. "For a me that hadn't happened yet. She sat in an archive before my grandmother was born and decided how my story would go, and then she spent her whole life steering me away from it, and then—" My laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And then she trained me anyway. Taught me every form, every oath, every word she swore she'd never let me use. She built the sword and spent forty years hiding the whetstone."

"That's not cowardice," Sera said.

"It feels exactly like cowardice from in here."

"It feels like cowardice because you're the one holding the bill." She closed the letter with the reverence she gave all dead things on paper. "But think about what she actually did, Hazel. She read a prophecy that said *this boy dies forgotten*, and she said *no*. Loudly. In ink. For a century. She fought the future with a pen and bad glue and she nearly won." Sera's chin came up, that chronicler's stubbornness I was learning to rely on. "I've spent my life among people who write history after it happens so they don't have to answer for it. Your Vess answered for it in advance. There's a word for that and it isn't coward."

I wanted to argue. I found I didn't have the material. Grief and gratitude had gotten tangled somewhere in my chest like smugglers' rope, and every time I pulled one end the knot tightened somewhere worse.

"She lied to me," I said finally, which was smaller and truer than everything else I wanted to say.

"For forty years," Dain agreed comfortably. "Best kind of lie. The kind that keeps you alive long enough to be angry about it." He poked the coals with his boot. "My mother lied to me about my father for twenty years. When I found out, I was furious for a season. Then I found out *why*, and I've spent the twenty years since wishing I'd hugged her instead." He shrugged, elaborately indifferent, which from Dain was a confession. "Point is, angry's fine. Angry's honest. Just don't let it eat the part of you that knows why she did it."

"I know why she did it," I said. "That's the worst part. I'd have done the same."

Sera looked at me sharply. "Would you?"

Would I? I thought of the hollow king, wearing Aldric's face, sustained by a choice somebody had once called mercy. I thought of Emberveil, guttering in her hollow, paying a debt she'd never agreed to. I thought of Sera, defecting with a satchel of evidence that would get her hanged, and Dain, selling his help for a book he probably intended to sell again, and even that felt like a kind of editing — everyone in this story trimming the edges off the truth to make it survivable.

"Yes," I said. "God help me, yes. That's what frightens me."

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I sat with the book open on my knees and read Vess's margins properly, all of them, start to finish, the way you reread a dead person's letters looking for the parts you missed the first time because you were young and stupid and certain she'd always be there to explain.

And she *was* explaining. She'd been explaining for a hundred years, in a code I finally understood because I'd spent my childhood learning it: her habit of writing important things backwards, her trick of marking true statements with a dash and false ones with a dot, her private shorthand for *the dragon* — a little hooked glyph like a flame bending against the wind.

The margins weren't commentary. They were a map.

*The third clause governs,* she'd written, early on, in the backward script. *Clause three is the price. All the rest is scenery.*

I found clause three near the middle of the page, buried in the usual prophetic fog — *and the word that was first shall be spoken last, and the speaker shall be as he was before the naming of things* — and beneath it, in fresh-seeming ink, a single line I was quite sure hadn't been there three days ago:

*Ask her what was before the naming.*

The fire had gone out. The Kettle hummed. And from far below us, up through the bones of the mountain, through the siphon-channels and the old dragon-song and the dark that had been dark longer than the kingdom had been a kingdom, I felt — distinctly, unmistakably, the way you feel someone say your name across a crowded room —

something listening.

Sera woke at my sharp intake of breath. "What? What is it?"

I looked down at the page, at Vess's handwriting, at the line that had not existed an hour ago, in ink that was not yet dry.

"She's still editing," I said.
