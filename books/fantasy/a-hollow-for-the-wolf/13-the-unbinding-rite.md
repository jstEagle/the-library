## 13. The Unbinding Rite

I have lived long enough to know that humans do their worst thinking in the dark, and their most dangerous thinking by firelight.

Hazel Blackwood sat cross-legged on the warm stone before me, his coat still smelling of foundry smoke and burnt oath-steel, and he asked me the question I had been dreading for a hundred years. He did not ask it bravely. He did not ask it foolishly. He asked it the way he seemed to do everything — quietly, sideways, as though he were checking whether the road ahead was load-bearing before he put his weight on it.

"So," he said, turning the coal of my fire over in his scarred palm like a man examining a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "The first word."

"Yes." I let the syllable sit between us. Dragons are patient; it is one of our few virtues, and I hoard it like everything else.

"And if someone speaks it — truly speaks it, not the way I burned the channel at Crownfire, but *speaks* it —"

"Then every memory of the speaker unravels. From every mind that ever held one. From every book, every brand, every stone carved with a name." I watched him turn this over. "The word is older than memory, little word-bearer. It was the first thing spoken into steel, and steel has never forgiven it. To say it is to become the word: present everywhere, remembered nowhere."

Hazel nodded slowly, and I saw the terrible thing happen that happens to humans when they understand a price and find it, against all sense, acceptable. His shoulders settled. Not slumped — *settled*, the way a house settles into its foundations.

"Show me how it's done," he said.

"No," I said.

* * *

You must understand something about dragons, since your chroniclers will get it wrong anyway. We are not wise because we are old. We are old because we are stubborn, and wisdom is merely the sediment that accumulates around stubbornness given enough centuries. I have watched empires rise on the backs of oaths and collapse under their weight. I watched Aldric — young Aldric, the Aldric who laughed with food in his beard and once apologized to me for a week because he'd stepped on my tail — make his choice in this very hollow, kneeling where Hazel now sat, and I said *no* to him too.

He did it anyway. They always do it anyway. It is the single most predictable thing about your species, more reliable than your need to name things and your inability to leave well enough alone.

So when Hazel said *show me*, and I said *no*, neither of us believed for a moment that the conversation was over. We had simply begun the oldest dance in the world: the one where the old creature stalls and the young creature waits, and both pretend they aren't counting who blinks first.

"Emberveil." He used my whole name, which was either respect or manipulation, and with Hazel it was always both. "The king is still in there. You told me yourself. A flicker. A man begging to be finished."

"I told you what I dreamed," I corrected. "There is a difference between testimony and hope, and you are carrying both in the same pocket and calling them coins."

Sera Vane, who had been pretending to catalog her notes by the far wall for the better part of an hour, gave up the pretense. "She's right," she said. "I've read enough chronicles to know the difference. Hope is what gets written down. Testimony is what gets crossed out."

"Thank you, chronicler," I rumbled. "Your support is noted and unhelpful."

But she came and sat beside him anyway, on the warm stone, in the small circle of my guttering light, and I understood then that I was not arguing with one human. I was arguing with the two of them together, which is three times worse, because humans in pairs develop courage the way stagnant water develops life: spontaneously, and in forms you don't want touching you.

"Teach me the shape of it," Hazel said. "Not the word itself. Just — what it costs. What it does. Let me decide with my eyes open."

And there it was. The trap he'd built without building anything, the same trap Aldric sprang on me a century ago. Because I could refuse to teach him. I could coil around the knowledge like a dragon around gold, which is to say possessively and to no lasting effect. But if I refused, he would go to the capital half-blind, and he would die, or worse, he would fail, and the hollow thing wearing Aldric's face would keep drinking the world down through its stolen channels until there was nothing left worth remembering anyone *for*.

A refusal would protect his name and lose everything else. And Hazel Blackwood, smuggler, liar, keeper of other people's secrets, had never once in his life protected his own name at the expense of anything he loved.

That, I thought, is the trouble with teaching humans to carry words. Sooner or later they want to *be* one.

* * *

"Sit properly," I said at last, because stalling had failed and dignity required I at least make him earn it. "Back straight. Palm open. And if you interrupt me, I will sit on you, and Sera can write down how you died: *crushed during a lecture*."

"That's going in the chronicle regardless," Sera murmured, already writing.

"The first word of swordspeech is not a sound," I began. "You have spoken oaths all your life — binding words, cutting words, the little speeches that open roads and close wounds. All of those are *sentences*. Sentences have subjects. A sentence says: *I do this, to that, for this reason.* The first word has no subject. It does not say *I unmake*. It simply *unmakes*, and whatever stands nearest the speaking becomes the *I* — briefly, entirely, and then never again."

Hazel frowned. "Briefly and never again don't fit in the same breath."

"They are the whole point of the breath." I drew my head down closer, so my shadow fell over him like a roof. "When the word is spoken, the speaker pours everything they are into it — every memory, every name others held for them, every place they were loved. The word spends it all. That is why it works. That is why it has worked exactly twice in the history of the world, and both times the world afterward contained one fewer person and no one noticed but me."

Sera's pen stopped. "Twice?"

"Once before the mountains had their present names, and once—" I paused, because the second time was not mine to tell, and the one who could tell it had paid the fee. "Once. Long ago. Ask the mountain sometime; it keeps better records than your archives."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone of a man testing a bridge: "Does it hurt?"

"What a question." I laughed, low, and the hollow filled with the sound the way a cup fills with water. "Everything true hurts, little word-bearer. Birth hurts. Oaths hurt. Being remembered hurts, eventually, when everyone who remembers you is gone. The word is only honest about it. It takes the hurt and the self together, in one payment, no installments." I looked at him, really looked, the way dragons look — through the skin, through the cleverness, down to the small stubborn furnace humans keep behind the ribs and call a soul. "No. It does not hurt. It is worse than hurting. It is *completing*. You will feel, for one instant, entirely finished. And then you will feel nothing at all, including the nothing."

Sera had stopped writing altogether. Good. Some things should not be written down until they must be.

* * *

He practiced the shape of it for three days.

Not the word — I kept that coiled in the deepest vault of myself, beneath the memories of Aldric's laughter and the smell of the world before men put roads through it. But the posture of it, the breathing, the terrible openness. I taught him as my own teacher taught me, in the old way: with stories that were also instructions, so that he would not notice how much I was giving him until it was already carried.

"On the third day of the Sundering War," I told him, "a swordspeaker named Corren Hale stood at the pass of Vael and spoke a sentence so large it broke him. Do you know what his last recorded words were? Not a battle cry. He turned to the soldier beside him and said, *mind the left flank.* That was all. Mind the left flank. He spent his entire self on the word and his last ordinary breath on someone else's safety."

Hazel smiled crookedly. "Sounds like someone I'd have liked."

"You would have cheated him at cards and he would have let you," I said. "Now breathe. Slower. You breathe like a man being chased even when no one is chasing him, which tells me everything about your childhood and nothing useful about your lungs."

He laughed at that — actually laughed, from the belly, the first true laugh I'd heard from him since the waystation, since the page with his name on it, since the world decided to stop letting him be anonymous. And I confess something to you, whoever reads this in whatever age finds these words: in that laugh, I heard Aldric. Not the hollow thing. The man. The one who apologized to my tail. Grief does that sometimes; it wears a stranger's voice to visit you.

On the fourth evening, Sera brought out the prophecy book, and we read the passage together by my light, all three of us, because some sentences should be witnessed.

*When the crown drinks deep and the king is hollow, a word-bearer shall come up out of Thornfell, and he shall speak the First Word, and the hollow shall be unbound — and the speaker shall be as a wind through an open door, known to none, mourned by none, save one.*

"*Save one,*" Sera repeated, and looked at me.

I looked at the floor. Dragons do not cry. This is another of our famous virtues, and like our patience, it is mostly stubbornness wearing a respectable coat.

* * *

That night I begged him.

I want it recorded plainly, without dragon-dignity, without the sediment of centuries making it sound noble. I begged. I, Emberveil, last of the fire-drakes, oldest living thing between the mountains and the sea, pressed my brow to the cold stone of my own hollow and asked a smuggler in a patched coat not to do the thing he was going to do.

"There are other ways," I said. "Slow ways. Bad ways, but ways. We could starve the channels one at a time. We could find what remains of Aldric and speak to it, wake it, let it fight from inside. We could—"

"Emberveil." He crouched by my head, this small warm creature, and put his hand flat against my jaw the way you'd steady a frightened horse. "How much fire do you have left? Honestly. Not the answer you give to make me feel better."

I told him the truth, because he had earned truth the way few humans earn anything: completely, and by accident, just by being himself in front of me for long enough. "Three days of burning. Four if I sleep between. After Crownfire, after lending you the coal, after a century of the siphons — I am a hearth with one log left, and the winter is not nearly over."

"Then we don't have time for slow ways." His thumb moved, once, along the old scar tissue where the first siphon-channel had been cut into me. Gentle. Humans are always gentlest when they're about to break something. "You've spent a hundred years being drunk from. I'm not going to spend your last four days looking for a cleverer door when the right one is right here."

"The right door erases you."

"The right door frees a king who knelt in this exact spot and made the same bargain I'm making, except he did it to buy time and I'd be doing it to end the debt." He shrugged, that infuriating smuggler's shrug, the one that treats enormous things as freight to be declared. "Seems fair. One name against a kingdom. I've moved cargo with worse exchange rates."

"It is not *fair*," I said, and my voice cracked the air of the hollow like ice on a pond. "Fair would be the world paying its own debts. Fair would be Aldric paying for his own bargain instead of leaving the bill to a boy who never agreed to any of it. There is nothing fair anywhere in this, and you standing there calling it *cargo* doesn't make it lighter, it just makes me want to bite something."

"Bite me, then," he said mildly. "Might change my mind."

"Do not tempt me, Hazel Blackwood."

But he was smiling, tired and crooked, and behind him Sera had gone very still over her notes, and I understood that I had lost — had perhaps lost the moment I taught him the shape of the breathing, had maybe lost the night I laughed at his joke instead of eating him, back in the beginning, when he first stumbled into my hollow smelling of fear and moonsteel and offered me half his bread because he didn't know what else to bring a dragon.

Half his bread. To a starving god of the old world. That is the kind of creature he is. That is the kind of creature the word will spend.

* * *

Later — deep in the dark hours, when Sera slept curled against my flank like she'd been doing it all her life and Hazel kept watch by habit, because a smuggler sleeps with one eye open even in a dragon's hollow — he came and sat near my head.

"I heard you," he said quietly. "In there. When you begged. Underneath it, I mean."

"Then you know I meant it."

"I know you meant it." He fed a stick into the small fire they'd built, careful, always careful, even of flames smaller than his own hand. "And I'll tell you something true, since we're trading. I spent thirty years making sure nobody knew my name. Not because I was hiding from anything. Because names are handles, and handles are how people get carried — dragged into wars, hauled before courts, lifted onto shoulders whether they want it or not. I figured if I stayed handle-less, I'd stay free." He looked into the fire. "Turns out the prophecy had a handle on me the whole time. Been carrying me toward this since before I was born. So." A small, helpless gesture. "Maybe the only free choice I ever get to make is what I weigh when they set me down."

I said nothing for a long time. The mountain hummed around us, its old dragon-song, the song of roads and oaths and the slow patience of stone.

"When you speak it," I said finally, "I will be the one. The *save one*. Whatever the word leaves of you — the shape in the smoke, the warmth in the stone, whatever fragment escapes the spending — it comes to me. I will hold it. I am good at holding things; it is the family business."

"I know." He reached out and touched the tip of my snout, bold as you please, the way no one had touched me since Aldric. "Keep the good parts. Skip the card games. I cheat."

"Noted," I said, "and disregarded. I intend to remember all of it. Especially the cheating. Especially the bread."

He smiled, and lay back against my foreleg, and slept — actually slept, deeply, the sleep of a man who has finished deciding. And I lay awake in the dark with my one log of fire and my hundred years of stubbornness, and I did the arithmetic dragons are cursed to do: love on one side, loss on the other, and no column for the middle where sane creatures keep their answers.

It was near dawn when I smelled it.

Smoke, faint and wrong — not woodsmoke, not my fire, but torch-smoke and cold iron and the particular oily reek of inquisitor's lantern-oil, drifting down through the upper galleries of the hollow. And beneath it, fainter, a scent I knew from a lifetime ago and had hoped never to catch again: the smell of a man who had sold something he couldn't afford to lose.

Dain. And behind Dain, coming down the deep roads two by two, the measured tread of the crown's boots.

Hazel slept on, one hand still resting against my scales, dreaming whatever small dreams were left to him. I drew my breath slow and deep, gathering the last of my fire the way a mother gathers children, and prepared to wake them all very loudly indeed.
