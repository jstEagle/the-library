## 14. Betrayal at the Hollow Gate

The morning the world came down on us, Emberveil was teaching me to breathe.

Not ordinary breathing — I'd managed that for thirty-odd years without instruction — but dragon-breathing, which apparently was a whole discipline involving the ribs, the spine, and a certain philosophical surrender I was failing at spectacularly.

"You are holding the word in your throat," she rumbled. Her voice filled the hollow the way water fills a cup, patient and total. "A word is not a stone you carry. It is a bird you release."

"It's a bird that's been trying to peck my eyes out since Crownfire," I said.

"Then you have been gripping it too tightly. Little word-bearer, if you crush every bird—"

The mountain flinched.

I felt it before I understood it — a shudder through the stone under my boots, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Emberveil went still in a way that made the air itself seem to hold its breath. Her head turned toward the deep road we'd come by, the one that wound down through the dark toward the Oathbreakers' Market and everything else we'd rather forget.

"What was that?" Sera asked from her corner of the hollow, where she'd spread out the prophecy book and three days of notes written in a hand so cramped it looked like it was fleeing across the page.

Emberveil didn't answer her. She answered me, and only me, and what she said made my stomach drop through the floor of the world.

*Someone has spoken your name to the wrong ears.*

* * *

Dain.

I should have known. Some part of me, some stubborn smuggler's instinct buried under all this prophecy nonsense, *had* known — had watched him haggle over the book with eyes like a man pricing something he'd already sold. But he'd fought beside me at the market when the crown's men came. He'd bled. You don't easily believe a man who bled beside you would sell your grave to the people who dug it.

"He took the book," Sera said. She was already moving, sweeping her notes together with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had spent years watching courtiers reach for knives. "Last night. I thought — I assumed you'd lent it to him. He said he wanted to check Vess's marginalia against his own copy of the rite."

"I don't have a copy of the rite."

"Yes," Sera said quietly. "I've realized that now."

Emberveil rose. Even guttering, even dim as a hearth banked for the night, she was vast — wings folding and unfolding like storm fronts deciding where to land. "How long?" I asked her. "How long do we have?"

"The mountain will tell me before they arrive. The old roads remember footsteps." Her great eye fixed on me, gold gone amber gone dull copper, and there was something in it I hadn't seen before. Fear. A dragon, afraid. "But they will not come up the roads, little word-bearer. They will come the way the fire goes out. Through the channels."

The siphons. The veins of stolen flame running from her hollow beneath the mountains all the way to the capital, to the foundry, to the crown. We'd burned one closed. The others were still open — and anything open enough to let fire flow one way could let soldiers flow the other.

"They can't fit through those," I said. "Can they?"

"A man can fit through almost anything," Emberveil said, "if he is willing to be smaller inside."

* * *

We had perhaps two hours. Maybe less. Emberveil wanted me to run — take the high pass, vanish into Thornfell, let the mountain hide me the way it always had. Sera argued we should fortify the gate. I argued, mostly, with myself, which is where all my best arguments happened.

"You are the last oath-bound swordspeaker alive," Emberveil said, and the word *last* sat on my chest like a boot. "If they take you here, the prophecy closes its crueler page and nothing anyone does afterward matters. Not Sera's chronicles. Not my fire. Not Aldric's poor remnant flickering in that hollow thing's chest. Run."

"And leave you?"

"I have been alone for a hundred years. I am very good at it." A plume of smoke curled from her nostrils, thin as incense. "Besides, I intend to be extremely rude to our guests."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, the laugh of a man trying to keep a lid on something boiling. Because leaving her felt less like strategy and more like the first betrayal of many, and because some traitor part of me — the part that had spoken oathspeech without choosing to, the part the brand on my palm kept answering for — did not want to run at all.

Sera settled it, the way she settled most things: with paperwork.

"We need the book back," she said. "Dain has it, Dain sold us, and Dain will be standing out there with the inquisitors when they come, because men like him like to watch their purchases arrive. Which means—" she held up one finger, chronicler-fashion, "—we know exactly where the book will be. And I want it back before they read Vess's letter."

"You told them about the letter?"

"No. But I wrote it down, and then I remembered I write things down for a living, and I burned the note, and now I'm telling you because keeping secrets from allies is how courts rot from the middle." She said it lightly, but her hands weren't light. They were shaking, just slightly, and she'd tucked them into her sleeves so I wouldn't see. I saw anyway.

So we stayed. Not out of courage — I want that on the record, carved somewhere, in case anyone ever writes this down who isn't Sera — but because a dead woman's handwriting was sitting in a traitor's saddlebag, and I had already lost one mentor to secrets. I wasn't losing her last words too.

We prepared the gate the smuggler's way: badly, quickly, and with whatever was at hand. Old pitons. A coil of tarred rope. Three barrels of the moonsteel spirits I'd never delivered, which Sera eyed with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic about explosions.

"Is that wise?"

"No," I said. "But it's available."

"That," she said, "should be carved over every door in the kingdom."

* * *

They came an hour early, because they always do, because dread keeps its own schedule and it is never yours.

It started as a sound like rain falling upward — gravel ticking against stone, boots on scree, the whisper of mail under wool. Then the first lantern-light bloomed in the mouth of the deep road, and I counted torches and stopped counting at twenty because after twenty the number stops being information and starts being weather.

And at the front, on a grey mare, holding the prophecy book open on his knee like a hymnal, sat Dain Corvasser.

He looked older than he had at the market. Or maybe just lit differently — torchlight is honest that way; it shows you the seams. He wore no crown livery, but his horse wore crown shoes, and there was a new sword at his hip with a hilt too fine for his taste. Bought men always dress one purchase behind their price.

"Hazel!" he called up the slope, cheerful as a market-day greeting. "You still owe me for the ferry crossing at Marlow's Ford! Eleven coppers!"

"You're charging me interest on eleven coppers with an army?"

"Inflation," he said, and shrugged, and the shrug was the worst part. Not the betrayal — betrayals are common as rain — but the smallness of it. He hadn't sold me for hate or fear or even ambition. He'd sold me the way you sell a lame mule, with a little apology in the shoulders and a fair price in mind.

Behind him, the inquisitors came up the switchbacks in silence. Grey cloaks, grey masks, and between them, carried on a litter like a relic, a brazier of white fire that hurt to look at — dragon-fire, stolen and caged, burning cold and hungry. My palm-brand woke at the sight of it, itching like a healing scar, and words pressed up behind my teeth that I had never learned.

*Not yet,* I told them. *Not yet.*

"Hazel Blackwood," the lead inquisitor said. Her voice was flat and precise, a blade being measured. "By seal of the crown, surrender the Swordspeaker's tongue and the vessel that carries it, and the rest of you may walk away."

"The vessel," Sera muttered beside me. "They make you sound like a soup pot."

"I contain multitudes," I said. "Mostly panic."

She almost smiled. Almost. There would be time for full smiles later, I told myself. Later was a country I was beginning to suspect I'd never get visas for.

"Last chance," the inquisitor called.

"Dain!" I shouted down. "The book! Give her back the book and I'll forgive the rest!"

He looked genuinely puzzled. "Why would I do that? It's the valuable part."

There it was. The whole man, in one sentence, laid out neat as inventory.

I spoke the word.

I didn't choose it — that's the truth I keep circling and never landing on. My mouth opened and the oath answered, the way it always had since the waystation, since the switchbacks, since the day my careful anonymity died on a century-old page. The tarred rope I'd strung across the gate path caught fire from nothing, from the *word itself*, and ran blazing down the slope like a fuse with opinions. The barrels went up in a bloom of moonsteel-white flame that turned midnight briefly into noon, and the front rank of inquisitors scattered like startled crows, and Dain's grey mare decided, sensibly, that she had not signed any contract, and left.

And through the smoke and the ringing in my ears, I heard Emberveil sing.

Not roar — *sing*, low and vast, a sound that came up through the rock instead of the air, and every oath-brand within a mile must have felt it, because the inquisitors stumbled as if the ground had tilted. She came out of the hollow above us like a storm given a skeleton, dim and dying and still more terrible than anything the crown had ever bought, and her fire — what little remained of it, hoarded like a miser's last coin — washed the switchbacks clean of torchlight.

What happened next happened fast, and I remember it in pieces, the way you remember any night the world ends.

I remember cutting the brazier-litter free and the white fire screaming when it spilled — actually *screaming*, a sound like a voice stretched over a rack, and knowing, sickly, that it had once been a voice, that all stolen fire remembers being someone.

I remember Sera at my shoulder with a smuggler's knife she held like a pen — wrong grip, absolute conviction — putting herself between me and an inquisitor's blade and taking a cut along her arm rather than step aside.

I remember Dain, on foot now, scrambling in the ash for the fallen book, and me reaching him at the same moment, and neither of us drawing steel, because some debts are settled with hands. I got the book. He got my elbow in his teeth, which felt proportionate.

And I remember Sera.

Because that was when the second force arrived — quieter, later, coming up the old siphon-road from below exactly as Emberveil had said, men who had made themselves smaller inside — and at their head, under a banner bearing the king's own seal, rode a woman in court grey with a herald's sash and a face I knew.

Sera Vane. Standing ten feet from Sera Vane.

Except it wasn't. It couldn't be. The woman beside me was pressing her bleeding arm and swearing in three languages. The woman under the king's seal looked at her — looked at *herself* — and did not blink, which no living person can do, and said, in Sera's own voice pitched half a tone wrong, like a bell recast:

"Surrender the Swordspeaker. The crown knows everything you know. The crown is *becoming* everything you know."

The hollow thing had sent her face ahead of her, the way it wore Aldric's. It had reached into the capital's records, into her chronicles, into whatever it stole when it stole names, and it had put her on a horse as a message: *nothing you are belongs to you.*

My Sera — ours, mine, the one bleeding on my sleeve — went very still. I felt her decide. I watched it happen, the way you watch ice decide to become water: not a moment but a process, quick and irreversible.

"You got my nose wrong," she said to the thing wearing her, conversationally, terribly calm. "In the chronicles I'll write about tonight — and I will write about tonight, and they will be *accurate* — you will be recorded as having gotten my nose wrong."

Then she drew the smuggler's knife properly, finally, and threw it — badly, gloriously, with the aim of a woman who had never thrown anything sharper than an insult — and hit the false herald's riderless horse instead, and the horse bolted, and the banner fell into the snow, and the king's seal landed face-down in the mud, which felt right.

The false Sera smiled. That was the horror of it — the smile arriving a half-second late, the same lag as the king's shadow, the machinery underneath showing through the gap. "Noted," it said. "The original will be collected regardless."

Overhead, Emberveil wheeled, and her fire caught the false herald and the escort column and turned the siphon-road's mouth into a wall of falling rock, sealing it — buying minutes, not safety, spending coins she could not mint again. When she landed, the light in her had dimmed another shade, copper going to rust, and I understood with a clarity that felt like a wound that every breath she spent defending us was a page torn out of her remaining life.

"Go," she said. "Both of you. Now. The unbinding cannot wait for a better night. There are no better nights. There is only this one, and it is leaving."

* * *

We ran — up through the hollow's throat, along a ledge I'd have sworn was goat-country, into the high dark where Thornfell's old oaths hummed in the stone like struck bells fading. Behind us the battle noise sank to embers. Beside me Sera breathed hard and bled steadily and refused, with a chronicler's stubbornness, to fall behind.

At the first bend I stopped and tore a strip from my shirt for her arm, and she let me, and while I tied it she said, quietly, not looking at me:

"When it spoke in my voice. Did you hesitate? Even for a heartbeat — wondering which of us was real?"

"No," I said. It was the easiest lie I'd ever told, and the worst.

"Liar," she said comfortably. "Good. Hesitate for me sometime when it doesn't matter. It's flattering."

Below us, faint through miles of stone, I heard Dain calling something up the mountain — the price of the book, maybe, or my name, or just the sound a man makes when he has finally become exactly as small as he was always willing to be. Emberveil's answer rolled over us like distant thunder, and I chose to believe it was rude.

Then the humming in the rock changed key, and Sera felt it too, and grabbed my sleeve.

"Hazel. The stone. Listen—"

The mountain was speaking. And it was using my name.
