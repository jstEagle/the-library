## 15. Chains of Borrowed Names

They did not chain Sera Vane on the road to the capital, which she found more insulting than chains would have been.

Chains, at least, were honest. Chains said: we know what you are and what you've done, and we have taken precautions. What the inquisitors gave her instead was a fine wool cloak against the mountain wind, a cup of hot spiced wine at the midday halt, and the unblinking courtesy of men who had been told she was valuable. It was like being carried in the mouth of a very polite dog.

"You're not a prisoner," Inquisitor Corren had explained, for the third time since the hollow gate, as their horses picked along the switchbacks. "You're a witness."

"I watched a dragon burn half your company," Sera said. "I watched Hazel Blackwood walk into fire. I watched Dain sell all of us for—" she'd paused, doing the arithmetic of betrayal, "—actually, what *did* he get? He seemed the sort who'd do it for less than he asked."

Corren's jaw had tightened in a way that suggested Dain's price was a sore subject even among professionals. "A witness," he repeated, with the patience of a man reciting scripture to a stone.

Now, three days later, standing in the antechamber of the throne room while a page announced her with a title she had never held — *the Chronicler Sera Vane, returned to the crown's grace* — Sera decided that if she survived the evening, she was going to write a treatise on the grammar of captivity. There were sentences that imprisoned you, and there were sentences that merely waited for you to imprison yourself, and the second kind were far more dangerous.

The doors opened.

* * *

King Aldric sat on his throne beneath the great glass lanterns, and everything about him was correct.

That was the horror of it, Sera thought. Not wrongness — correctness. The king wore his crown at precisely the angle the court portraits showed. His hands rested on the armrests exactly as they had in the statue in Coronation Square. When he rose to greet her, he rose the way a king rises in an illuminated manuscript: gracefully, completely, without a single wasted motion or human hesitation.

She remembered the real Aldric from her first year at court, when she'd been a junior scribe copying correspondence. The old king had fidgeted. He'd cleared his throat before difficult pronouncements. Once, receiving ambassadors from the Salt Coast, he had gotten his own left and right confused and pointed them toward the wrong door, and laughed so hard at himself that the ambassadors had fallen in love with him on the spot. Kings who could laugh at themselves were rare. Kings who could laugh at all were rarer than dragons.

This one did not blink enough.

"Chronicler Vane." His voice was Aldric's voice, aged and gravelled, but it arrived in her ears a half-beat after his lips moved, like thunder lagging lightning. "You have been in the mountains. You have seen things."

"I have kept records, Your Majesty." She bowed the exact depth required of her station — no more, no less. Somewhere behind her ribs, her heart was performing a drum solo, but twenty years at court taught you to keep your face like a closed ledger. "It is my office."

"Your office." The hollow thing wearing the king's face considered this. "Yes. You keep records. You have always kept records. Even as a girl, copying letters in the scriptorium, you kept a private record of the mistakes your betters made." A pause. "The ambassador who pointed at the wrong door. You wrote it down. You thought it was charming."

Cold walked up Sera's spine on small feet. That memory existed nowhere but in her own head and in a journal locked in her chambers three floors above them.

"Kings should be able to laugh at themselves," she said evenly. "It's in the chronicles."

"It is in *you*," the king said. "Everything is in you now, Chronicler. Everything is in everyone. Do you know how much of a person can be carried in a name?" He descended one step from the dais, and the lantern-light slid across his face like water across glass. "I have been collecting names for a hundred years. I am quite the archivist myself."

* * *

He led her — led, not summoned; there was something almost eager in it, which frightened her more than any command — down through the palace's older bones, past the state apartments into corridors Sera had never known existed despite a decade of service. The stones changed underfoot. Newer masonry gave way to black granite veined with something that glimmered faintly red, like embers caught in ice.

"This wing does not appear on any plan of the palace," she said.

"No," the king agreed pleasantly. "It appears on mine."

The corridor ended at a door of grey iron, unmarked except by a single word engraved above the lintel in a script Sera recognized from exactly one place: the prophecy book. The old tongue. The language of oaths.

The doors swung open on darkness that breathed.

* * *

The chamber beyond was round, and cold, and full of light that came from nowhere — a pale, sourceless glow, like moonlight filtered through milk. And hanging in that light, suspended by nothing Sera could see, were names.

Not metaphorically. Sera's chronicler's mind reached for metaphor and found, for once, that reality had outrun it. Threads of pale script hung in the air of the chamber by the hundreds, thousands, drifting slow as snowfall, each one a name written in glowing letters that unwound and rewound themselves like living things. They turned lazily in currents of air she couldn't feel. Some were short. Some went on for lines. One near the ceiling was long enough to be a genealogy.

And around the walls, in alcoves carved into the black granite, stood bodies.

That was the only honest word for them. Figures of grey, translucent substance — not flesh, not smoke, something between — each one shaped like a person, each one flickering faintly, like a candle flame seen through frosted glass. An old woman with a scholar's stoop. A soldier missing half his silhouette. A child, which made Sera's stomach turn over and stay turned. They stood motionless in their alcoves, dozens of them, and as she watched, one of the drifting names drifted close to a figure and *sank into it*, and the figure brightened, gained definition, became briefly, horribly vivid — a woman laughing at some joke Sera couldn't hear — before dimming again to grey.

"What are they?" she whispered.

"Guests," said the king. "Or hosts. The distinction has become academic." He walked among the alcoves with the air of a gardener inspecting roses. "When I was bound into this body, Chronicler — and yes, you've guessed; you're clever, cleverness is in your name too — I was given one shape and one name, and neither was truly mine. A vessel wears out. A borrowed face cracks at the seams. But a *self*—" he touched one of the drifting names, and it recoiled from his finger like a fish, "—a self can be pieced together. From here, a fondness for honeyed wine. From there, a way of standing. From somewhere else, a mother's lullaby. I am building a new body out of everyone the kingdom has ever forgotten, and it is going to be magnificent."

Sera looked at the grey figures and understood, with a sick lurch, what she was seeing. Not prisoners. *Ingredients.* People whose identities had been skimmed away — the vanished oath-takers, the disappeared petitioners, everyone who had gone into the crown's custody and never come out. Their selves, harvested thread by thread.

Her eyes found the alcove nearest the door, and stopped.

The figure there was taller than most. Lean. It stood with its weight slightly forward, the stance of a man used to narrow mountain paths, one hand curled loosely as if around a sword-hilt that wasn't there. And across its chest, faintly glowing through the grey, was the ghost-shape of a brand — a palm-print sigil that Sera had last seen seared into Hazel Blackwood's skin.

"No," she said aloud, before she could stop herself.

The king turned. Something moved behind his eyes — hunger, maybe, or satisfaction, and with him the two were indistinguishable.

"Yes," he said softly. "You recognize him. Of course you do. You traveled with him. You *fought* beside him." He drifted closer to the alcove, and the Hazel-shaped figure brightened at his approach, its edges sharpening, its borrowed stance growing surer. "Do you know how difficult it has been to gather him? The swordspeaker keeps himself folded away like a letter no one is meant to read. Every road he takes forgets him. Every face he passes lets him go. I have had to reach for him sideways — through the people who remember him. Through his mentor's papers. Through—"

He paused. Looked at her.

"Through you," the king said, and smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a mask fits a face: perfectly, and therefore falsely. "You are the finest archive he ever kept, Chronicler. Every conversation. Every joke. The way he hums when he thinks no one can hear — off-key, incidentally, quite badly. All of it written in you. I need only read."

* * *

Later — she would never be sure how much later; time moved strangely in the name-chamber, pooling and sliding — Sera sat in a comfortable chair in a comfortable room with a tray of food she didn't touch, and made herself think.

That was the trick of it, she decided. Horror wanted her frozen. Horror wanted her standing in that round grey room forever, watching names drift like snow. So she would not give it that. She was a chronicler. Her entire life was the refusal to let terrible things simply happen unrecorded and unopposed. She took out the little notebook she'd been permitted to keep — they'd given it back to her, another courtesy-as-insult, because what harm could paper do? — and began, in her smallest, fastest hand, to make notes.

Item the first: the figures dimmed when the names withdrew. Which meant the names were not yet fully merged. Which meant the process was incomplete, and incomplete processes could be interrupted.

Item the second: the king had shown her the chamber. He had *wanted* to show her the chamber. Which meant either arrogance — possible; hollow things, she suspected, had hollow vanity — or a test, or a hook. He believed she could be turned. He had mentioned her family. Her sister Maren, up in Thornfell, who ran the ferry at Miller's Bend and had never once answered a letter promptly. *We could bring her to court,* the king had said, warm as a hearth. *Family should be together.*

Item the third, and the one that mattered: the Hazel-figure in the alcove was built from memories. From other people's recollections of him. And every memory in that chamber had come from someone who remembered Hazel *wrong*, or partially, or secondhand — except hers.

Hers were the freshest. Hers were the deepest. Three weeks on the deep roads: his terrible humming, his stubborn cheer, the way he'd named every rock formation they passed as though the mountains needed introducing, his voice going gentle when he talked about Vess. If the hollow king wanted to assemble Hazel Blackwood out of stolen pieces, then the piece of Hazel that lived in Sera Vane was the keystone. The load-bearing wall.

And she was the only one who knew it.

So the plan assembled itself in her notebook, block by block, the way she'd learned to reconstruct damaged manuscripts: work with what survives, infer the rest, and never let the forgers see you checking their seams.

She could not fight the king. She could not run — the palace was a lockbox and she was inside it. But she could *curate*. She could feed him an archive. Every day, in her "recovered testimony," in her dutiful chronicle entries, in her careful answers to his questions, she could salt the record. Give him a Hazel who was braver where he was actually cautious, louder where he was quiet, sentimental in ways he wasn't, attached to places he'd never seen. Build, thread by patient thread, a false swordspeaker in the king's collection — and let the true one, the real man somewhere out there in the dark with a coal of dragon-fire in his pocket, arrive at the gates as a stranger even to his own stolen reflection.

Let the hollow thing wear a lie to the final confrontation. Let it reach for Hazel's name and find her handwriting all over it.

It was, she reflected, dipping her pen, essentially forgery. Twenty years of detecting forged charters, and it had all been training for this.

There was a knock — soft, formal — and a steward entered to say that His Majesty requested the honor of the Chronicler's company at supper.

"Of course," Sera said, closing the notebook. "I'm famished."

She wasn't. But liars, she had learned from the best forgers in the kingdom, always ate heartily. It was in the records.

* * *

At supper, the hollow king ate nothing — the plates before him arranged beautifully, untouched, a still life of mortality he no longer possessed — and asked her questions in a voice that arrived late, like an echo that had gotten ambitious.

He asked about the deep roads. He asked about Emberveil's strength. He asked, idly, terribly, whether Hazel had spoken any words of power in her hearing, and Sera served him a Hazel who guarded his words like a miser, who feared his own gift, who had wept at the border-stones of Thornfell because he missed a village that did not exist.

The king listened. The king collected. And Sera Vane, chronicler of the realm, sat in the candlelight writing the first draft of a history that had not happened yet — knowing, with a certainty that felt like a blade laid flat against her palm, that somewhere below the mountain, the true draft was already climbing toward her.

She raised her wine glass to him across the table.

"To archives," she said.

The hollow king's smile arrived a half-second late, as everything of his did.

"To archives," he agreed.
