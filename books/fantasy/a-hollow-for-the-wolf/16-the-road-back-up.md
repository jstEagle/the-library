## 16. The Road Back Up

The coal sat in the hollow of his palm like a sleeping bird.

That was how Emberveil had described it, anyway — *a coal of true fire, little word-bearer, and mind it doesn't wake* — before she'd pressed it into his bandaged hand with all the tenderness of a mother tucking a knife into her child's coat pocket. It was warm. It was heavier than it had any right to be, a lump of stone no bigger than a walnut that weighed as much as a guilty conscience. And every few minutes, without warning, it pulsed.

Hazel had named it *the Heartburn* within the first hour, because if he didn't laugh at it he was going to scream at it, and screaming echoed in tunnels, and echoing things attracted inquisitors.

"You're not funny," he told it, somewhere on the third day of climbing. The coal pulsed. "See, that's exactly what I mean."

The tunnel answered with dripping water and nothing else. That was the trouble with arguing with a rock: it always won on points.

He was climbing out of Thornfell's deep roads toward the capital, which meant climbing, which meant that everything hurt in a new and inventive way each morning. The wound across his ribs from the battle at the hollow gate had closed into an angry red seam that pulled whenever he reached for a handhold above his shoulder, which was constantly, because mountains are made mostly of things above your shoulder. His left hand — the oath-brand hand, the one Dain's treachery had nearly cost him entirely — worked again, mostly, if you didn't ask it to grip anything heavier than a rope or hold anything hotter than a lie.

And he was doubting.

Not the way heroes doubt in ballads, all noble anguish and meaningful stares into middle distance. His doubt was smaller and meaner than that. It came at him in the quiet stretches, between one lantern-glow of dragon-song and the next, and it said things like: *You burned one channel closed and it marked you from wrist to elbow. There are nine more channels. There is a hollow thing at the end of them wearing a king's face, and it has been eating names for a hundred years, and your plan is to walk up to it and say a word.*

*One word,* the doubt said, while he chewed hardtack that tasted like the inside of a flour sack. *Your entire contribution to history is going to be one syllable. Vess edited prophecies for decades and she couldn't save herself. What makes you think—*

"Shut up," Hazel told the doubt, and ate another bite of flour-sack, and kept climbing.

It was, he reflected, not much of a strategy. But then, neither had surviving the last three weeks been much of a strategy, and here he was, upright-ish, ambulatory-ish, and in possession of a map drawn by a traitor who had at least had the decency to draw it well.

Dain's map. He'd gone through it eleven times by now, partly for navigation and partly because studying Dain's careful, smug little cartography was cheaper than thinking about what Dain had done. The man had sold their location to the crown with one hand and left Hazel an escape route with the other — or so he claimed, in the note tucked against the map's spine. *For old times. Don't die stupidly. It reflects poorly on me.*

"He could have just said sorry," Hazel muttered. "Sorry costs nothing. A whole letter of excuses costs nothing."

The coal pulsed, and this time it felt less like mockery and more like agreement, which was somehow worse.

* * *

On the fifth night he found one of the smugglers' caches — Dain's map marked them with small triangles, and the triangle nearest the capital's approach held oilcloth bundles lashed behind a false wall of rubble: dried meat, lamp oil, a coil of good rope, and, wrapped separately with almost ceremonial care, a flask of plum brandy and a note in a different hand entirely.

*To whoever's using my caches,* it read. *If you're reading this, you're either Hazel Blackwood or someone I'd like to meet. Leave the brandy. Take the rest. — Maret.*

Hazel took the brandy too. He figured Maret would understand. Maret, whoever Maret was, sounded like someone who understood about bad weeks.

He allowed himself two fingers of it, sitting with his back against the cache wall and his boots off for the first time in a day and a half, letting his feet complain loudly to him about the treatment they'd suffered. The coal sat in his lap, pulsing its slow patient pulse, and the dark of the tunnel pressed close and warm, and far below — deeper than the deep roads went, down where even smugglers didn't go — he could feel it again. The hum. The old dragon-song, threaded through the stone like sap through wood.

Emberveil's song. Or what was left of it.

She was dying back there in her hollow. He'd known it when he left; she'd told him plainly enough, in the way of very old creatures who have run out of patience for euphemism. *I am a guttering candle, little word-bearer. Do not waste grief on me. Waste it instead on being careful.* But knowing a thing and hearing it were different, and now, five days away, with her fire sitting in his hand keeping him warm through mountain nights, the knowledge had teeth.

Every pulse of the coal was her, spending what she had left on him. Every mile he climbed was bought with light she would never get back.

"That's a terrible bargain," he said aloud, to the dark, to her, to himself. "You know that's a terrible bargain."

The dark didn't answer. The dark never did. But the song hummed a half-tone louder for a moment, the way a room gets warmer when someone you love walks into it, and Hazel decided — stubbornly, unreasonably, with no evidence whatsoever — that it meant *yes, and I signed anyway.*

Old dragons didn't make bargains they regretted. They just made bargains that cost more than anyone guessed.

He drank the second finger of brandy slower than the first.

* * *

The doubt changed tactics around day six.

It stopped whispering about the odds — Hazel had gotten good at not listening to arithmetic, arithmetic being a coward's language — and started whispering about Sera instead. Sera Vane, court chronicler, the sharpest and most stubborn person he'd met since Vess died, currently in the capital, currently captured, currently sitting in a cell or worse while the hollow thing wearing Aldric's face assembled itself out of stolen pieces of everyone Hazel had ever—

No. Not everyone. Out of stolen pieces of *him*. Fragments of Hazel's name, Hazel's voice, Hazel's memories, stitched into a body that wanted to be complete. Somewhere in the gilded rooms of the palace, a thing made of other people's leavings was wearing his smile.

He tried not to picture it. He pictured it constantly, usually at three in the morning, usually while wedged into a crack in the tunnel wide enough for a man and his regrets and nothing else.

*She'll have given you up by now,* the doubt said. *Everyone gives everyone up eventually. Dain did. You'd do it too, if they asked nicely enough and threatened correctly. You're already planning what you'd trade.*

"I'm really not," Hazel said, to the ceiling of the crack, six inches from his nose.

*You're thinking about it right now.*

"I'm thinking about how I'd refuse. It's called preparation." He shifted his shoulder off a ridge of stone that had been digging into it for an hour. "There's a difference."

*Is there?*

"Yes," said Hazel, with the full confidence of a man who had not examined the question closely and did not intend to start. "The difference is that I'm extremely charming under pressure and would talk my way out of any bargain before it finished forming. Ask anyone. Ask—" and here the sentence ran aground on the reef he hit every time now, the reef with no bottom to it: *ask anyone*, except that soon there would be no anyone. The first word would take his name out of every mouth but one. Ask Emberveil. Ask the only witness who would remain, a dying dragon in a cold hollow, keeper of a name whose owner would be a stranger to his own reflection.

He lay in the crack and let that thought sit on his chest for a while, heavy as the coal.

Then he got up, because lying down is how doubts win, and doubts only win if you give them the whole night, and Hazel Blackwood had decided somewhere around day four that he was not giving them the whole night ever again. He'd give them the evenings. Evenings were fair. Evenings were for grief and brandy and talking to rocks. But mornings belonged to walking.

Stubbornness, he had come to understand, was not a virtue. It was a load-bearing wall. You didn't admire it. You leaned on it.

* * *

Day eight brought the first sign that the world above still existed: a thread of daylight, thin as wire, coming down through a ventilation shaft crusted with a century of bird droppings and moss. Hazel stood under it for a long moment, letting the grey light fall on his face, and something in his chest unknotting by a single degree.

Above the shaft, muffled by stone, he heard wheels. Cart wheels, many of them, in the steady rhythm of a supply road. The capital's appetite, feeding itself.

He was close. Two days, maybe three, if the map held and his ribs held and the coal held and his nerve held, and those were four separate gambles stacked like plates, and he had never in his life been good at carrying plates.

That evening he did something he hadn't done since the hollow gate. He built a fire.

A real one, small, shielded in a niche where the smoke could climb the ventilation shaft, fed with scraps of broken pallet-wood from another cache. He sat cross-legged before it with the coal in his hands and let the true fire look at the ordinary fire, and for a while the two of them kept company, the little mortal flame dancing and the old immortal one pulsing, and Hazel sat between them like a man at a table where both his grandmothers were visiting and disapproved of each other.

"You should rest," he told the coal. "You don't have to keep me warm. I've got a perfectly good fire here."

The coal pulsed, twice, deliberately, the way Emberveil used to blink at him when he said something foolish — which was often, and which she had treated as a personal entertainment.

"Don't you dare interpret that as me being foolish."

Pulse. Pulse-pulse.

"She taught you manners," Hazel said, "and none of them are mine," and he laughed, actually laughed, alone in a tunnel eight days from anywhere, and the laugh hurt his ribs and he laughed anyway, because some things are worth the cost, and warmth delivered by a dying dragon's stubbornness was one of them.

Later, banked and blanketed and trying to sleep, he had the dream again. The one where Vess sat across from him at the waystation table, the prophecy book open between them, and she was explaining something patiently, the way she explained everything, and he couldn't hear a word of it because the pages were blank, all of them, every page, and she kept pointing at the blankness saying *it's here, Hazel, look closer, it's all here,* and he kept looking and looking and there was nothing, and he woke with his heart hammering and the taste of ash in his mouth.

He lay in the dark and made himself say it plainly, once, the way you lance a wound:

She chose the cruel ending to spare me. She thought I'd die otherwise. She was wrong about what I'd choose, and she was wrong that sparing me was hers to decide, and I am angry at her, and I miss her, and both of those are true, and neither one changes what I'm going to do tomorrow.

Tomorrow he was going to walk. That was the whole plan. Tomorrow he was going to put one foot in front of the other foot, uphill, toward the worst place in the kingdom, carrying a dying star in his pocket and a dead woman's betrayal in his chest and a living woman's fate in his hopes, and he was going to do it on stubbornness alone because courage had checked out around the hollow gate and hadn't left a forwarding address.

Fine. Stubbornness had gotten him this far. Stubbornness and a traitor's map and a dragon's spare change.

It would have to be enough. It was, he suspected, what everyone else in history had used.

* * *

The tenth day, the tunnel ended.

Not badly — not in a collapse, not in a nest of something with too many eyes — but simply, the way roads end when they arrive. One moment he was stooping through the last low stretch of Dain's map, and the next the passage opened into a hillside shrine so old it had forgotten what it was a shrine *to*: a ring of standing stones furred with moss, a fallen lintel, and beyond it, spread out below like a spilled jewel-box, the capital.

Lanternlight in the tens of thousands. The river a black ribbon stitched with bridge-lamps. The palace on its hill, crowned — he could see it from here, even at this distance, even at dusk — with a glow that was not lamplight. It was the wrong color. Lamplight is amber, honest, flickering. This was a steady, hungry gold, the color of the coal in his pocket, the color of a fire being spent by someone who wasn't paying for it.

Nine channels, Emberveil had said. He'd closed one. Eight veins of her life, running night and day into that crown, into that thing.

Hazel stood at the shrine and looked at it for a long time, and the looking hurt more than the climbing had, because from up here he could finally see the whole shape of what he was walking into, and the whole shape was enormous, and he was one tired man with a bad hand.

But the stones were humming.

Old oaths, saturated into the rock over centuries — oath-takers' words, smugglers' promises, lovers' vows shouted from hilltops and regretted by morning, all of it soaked down into the stone like rain. He'd learned in Thornfell that the land could listen. Here, at the door of the capital, the land was practically chattering.

He put his branded palm flat against the nearest standing stone, and the hum rose to meet it, and a word rose with it — not a word he chose, never a word he chose, just a word that arrived, the way weather arrives — and the stone warmed under his hand like a dog leaning into a scratch.

"Well," Hazel said. "All right, then."

Below, the gates would be watched. Above, the walls were high. But the mountain had opened roads where none were once already, and it was humming at him now like an old friend clearing its throat, and somewhere down in that glittering sprawl a chronicler with ink-stained fingers was either alive or not, and either way there was a hollow thing on a throne that needed unmaking, and Hazel Blackwood — last of the swordspeakers, keeper of one word, owner of one very opinionative rock — intended to be about it.

He shouldered his pack. The coal pulsed, eager now, pulling toward its own stolen light like a compass needle finding north.

"One more road," he told it, and started down.
