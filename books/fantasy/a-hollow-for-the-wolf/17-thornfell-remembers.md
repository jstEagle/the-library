## 17. Thornfell Remembers

The mountain had opinions.

Hazel had known this for years, the way a sailor knows the sea is wet — abstractly, professionally, without ever expecting it to matter to him personally. Smugglers learned early that Thornfell was less a place than a temperament. It shrugged off roads every winter. It swallowed trails whole and spat out new ones in spring, arranged in configurations no mapmaker survived long enough to confirm. Old-timers swore the passes moved out of spite. Hazel had always assumed it was just erosion, weather, and the universal human talent for getting lost.

He had been wrong about a great many things lately. Why stop now?

The coal in his chest pocket pulsed — a slow, warm heartbeat that wasn't his own — and the rock beneath his boots answered it. Not loudly. Not dramatically. But as he picked his way down from the high shelf where he'd slept badly, he noticed that the scree slope ahead of him, which by all rights should have sent him sliding two hundred feet into a ravine, had arranged itself into something suspiciously like stairs.

"Coincidence," he told the coal.

The coal pulsed, smugly.

"I mean it. Rock shifts. That's what rock does. Gravity, frost-heave, the general decay of all things." He tested one of the stone steps with his boot. It held like it had been mortared there by a very patient mason with strong feelings about his safety. "This proves nothing."

The third step hummed faintly under his weight, a note so low he felt it in his teeth rather than heard it. The same note the deep roads had sung, down below Emberveil's hollow. Dragon-song, soaked so deep into the stone over centuries that the mountain itself had learned the melody — and now, apparently, the words.

Because that was the thing he couldn't keep pretending away, could he? Words were his trade now, whether he liked it or not. And last night, half-asleep against a boulder with his ribs screaming where the inquisitor's chain had caught him, he had muttered something — nothing, really, just *easier this way* — and the boulder had shifted six inches to cradle his shoulder.

Rocks did not do that. Rocks were famously bad at listening.

"Fine," he said, to the coal, to the mountain, to whatever was left of his dignity. "You're doing it on purpose."

The wind came down the pass and smelled, absurdly, of woodsmoke and bread. There was no village within a day's walk. He chose not to think about what that meant.

* * *

Dain's map was, as Dain himself would have put it, mostly honest. It showed the smugglers' ways through Thornfell's eastern flank: the Hollow Stair, the Weep, the Long Gallery where you walked silent because sound carried like gossip. What it did not show — what no map showed — was how to get from the dragon's hollow to the capital's back door without crossing the three fortified gates the crown had sealed since the inquisitors started hunting him in earnest.

Hazel had spent the morning staring at the map and the mountain and finding them mutually uncooperative. Then, somewhere around midday, hungry and limping and out of patience, he had said — aloud, bitterly, to nobody — *"There should be a way through here."*

And the mountainside had opened.

Not with thunder. With a sort of sigh, the way an old door gives when the hinges finally surrender. A seam in the cliff face widened into a crack, and the crack into a passage, and the passage exhaled air that smelled of deep stone and old fires, and Hazel had stood at its mouth for a full minute feeling like a man who has just discovered his house has rooms he never knew about.

That had been four hours ago. He was still walking in it.

The passage was smooth-walled but not worked — no chisel marks, no mortar. It was as if the stone had simply decided, some time ago, to be a corridor, and had been waiting patiently ever since for someone to ask. Old oath-marks spiraled along the walls at intervals, worn nearly to nothing, the kind of binding-sigils Vess used to trace in the dust when she was teaching him letters. He ran his thumb over one and felt it wake, briefly, like a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep.

"You knew her," he murmured. "Didn't you. All of you. You knew the swordspeakers."

The walls hummed. Low, warm, unmistakably affirmative.

Hazel kept walking, because if he stopped walking he would have to feel things, and he had a schedule.

* * *

He made camp in a widening of the passage where a natural chimney let in a thread of grey daylight and, more importantly, let out smoke. He built a small fire — small, because the coal in his pocket disapproved of large ones, radiating a distinct sense of *we are not doing that* whenever flame got ambitious — and ate the last of the waybread, which had achieved a texture best described as architectural.

Then he took out the map, weighted its corners with stones, and tried to plan the last leg. The capital sat in the valley mouth beyond Thornfell's southern spur. Two days' walk if the roads were kind. They would not be kind. Every gate would have inquisitors on it, and every inquisitor would be looking for a man whose face they'd seen in a book written before their grandmothers were born.

His hand drifted, without permission, to the oath-brand on his palm. It had changed since Crownfire. The old spiral was still there, but threaded through it now ran a bright seam, like a vein of gold in dark quartz, where his first true word had burned its way in. It didn't hurt anymore. It *hummed*. All the time now. A quiet chord, day and night, like the mountain's song played on a smaller instrument.

Like the mountain's song, he realized. Played on a smaller instrument.

"Oh," Hazel said.

He looked at his palm. He looked at the walls. The walls, helpfully, glowed — very slightly, very politely, the way a host lights candles when a guest finally notices the decor.

"You're not humming near me," he said slowly. "I'm humming near *you*. Or — no. We're both humming the same thing." He laughed, and it came out cracked and strange in the empty passage. "Vess, you absolute menace. This is what she meant. *The oath doesn't live on your skin, boy. Your skin lives on the oath.* I thought she was being poetic. She was being *literal*. She was always being literal, and I thought she was being poetic, and now I'm talking to a mountain."

The mountain, to its credit, did not gloat.

But the warmth spread anyway — up through his boots, along his spine, settling behind his sternum next to the coal like two old friends sharing a bench. And with it came something worse than warmth. Something he'd been outrunning since the waystation, since the prophecy, since the first inquisitor called him *Swordspeaker* in a voice that sounded like it had been waiting a hundred years to say it.

Belonging.

He had built his whole life on not belonging anywhere. It was the family trade, anonymity — Blackwoods didn't appear in ledgers, didn't sign guestbooks, paid in coin and left before dawn. His father had taught him that a man's name was the handle other people used to pick him up, and the fewer handles you offered, the harder you were to carry off. Hazel had taken the lesson to heart so thoroughly that he'd once introduced himself to a border clerk as "the man ahead of me in line." It had worked for eleven years.

And now a mountain knew him. Knew his name, probably — the true one, the one Emberveil carried, the one that lived in the oath older than any ledger. Knew his word. Was, at this moment, rearranging its own insides to make room for him, the way you'd shift furniture for family coming home after a long absence.

"It won't work," he told the fire. His voice was steadier than he felt. "Whatever you're planning. I'm going down there, I'm going to do the thing, and then everyone forgets me, and that's — that's the price, that's fine, I agreed to the price—" His throat closed. He stared at the flames until they blurred. "But you'll forget too, won't you. Stone remembers everything, they say. Except me."

The passage went very quiet. Even the wind in the chimney seemed to hold its breath.

Then, from deep below — far below, below the smugglers' roads, below the deep roads, below anything on any map — the mountain sang.

One note. Long and low and absolutely certain, rising through the stone like water through roots, and it was not a note Hazel had heard before, and it was a note he knew better than his own heartbeat, because it was built from the same chord that lived in his palm, the same chord that lived in the oath, the same chord that had crowned a king and cursed him and waited a century for someone to come finish the sentence.

It sounded, unmistakably, like a promise.

Hazel sat very still for a long time.

"All right," he said finally, and his voice broke on it, and he let it. "All right. I hear you. I'm not fighting it anymore." He pressed his branded palm flat against the floor, and the stone was warm as a living thing, warm as a hand. "I don't know what I am yet. But I'm done pretending I'm only a smuggler who got unlucky."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, snow slid from a ledge with a soft whisper, like the mountain settling comfortably.

"And if you remember me afterward," he added, because humor was load-bearing and his ribs needed all the help they could get, "you're contractually obligated to say nice things."

The hum, he could have sworn, chuckled.

* * *

He slept without dreams for the first time in weeks, which should have been restful and instead felt like missing an appointment. When he woke, grey light was leaking down the chimney, and the passage had changed overnight.

The wall opposite his camp had opened onto a new corridor — one that bent southward, sloping gently down, exactly the direction he needed to go. No map showed it. No smuggler had cut it. The stone had simply, sometime in the night, decided to be helpful.

"You know," Hazel said, packing up, "most people wait to be asked before they renovate."

He stepped into the new road. It was smoother than any tunnel had a right to be, and it descended in long easy switchbacks, and every hundred paces or so an old oath-mark woke under his fingertips as he passed, flickering gold, like lamps being lit ahead of a guest.

By midmorning he understood what he was walking through. These weren't new roads at all. They were *old* ones — ancient ones, pre-smuggler, maybe pre-kingdom — sealed shut for centuries and now opening one by one ahead of his boots like doors down a great hall. The swordspeakers had walked here once. Many of them, over many generations, speaking oaths into the stone until the stone learned the language. Thornfell hadn't forgotten them. Thornfell had been *waiting*, all this time, for the last one to come home.

And it had chosen a side. That was the part that made his chest ache. The crown had bought its gates and garrisons and inquisitors, but the crown had never owned the mountain. The mountain belonged to the oaths. And the oaths, it turned out, remembered exactly whose side they were on.

Around noon, the corridor ended at a curtain of solid rock, and Hazel stood before it knowing — the way you know a friend's knock — that this was the last door. Beyond it, by his reckoning, lay the southern spur, the valley, the capital's sprawling smoke-stained skyline. And somewhere in that glittering sprawl, Sera Vane was either alive or not, and a hollow thing wearing a dead king's face was either waiting or not, and the coal in his pocket burned steady and hot, pulling toward its stolen light.

He raised his branded palm.

"I don't suppose," he said, "you could open this one quietly?"

The rock considered. Then it parted — silently, smoothly, without so much as a grain of dust falling — revealing a narrow cleft that climbed to a hairline of daylight, and beyond the daylight, impossibly far below, the roofs and spires and foundry-smoke of the capital, laid out like a map of everything he'd come to unmake.

Hazel stood in the doorway of the mountain and looked at the city for a long moment.

"Thank you," he said. And then, because the oath deserved honesty even if the world wouldn't get it: "Whatever happens down there — it was good, coming home."

The mountain hummed its low warm chord, one last time, and held it — like a name being memorized.

He stepped out into the light, and began to climb down toward the throne of a hollow king.
