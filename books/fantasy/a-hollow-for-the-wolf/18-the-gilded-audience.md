## 18. The Gilded Audience

Sera Vane had spent eleven years as court chronicler, and in that time she had learned one iron law of the palace: the truth was almost never refused entry, because no one recognized it. It walked in wearing the plain robes of a record-keeper, carrying a satchel of dates and precedents, and it was waved through every door by guards who were looking for smugglers and assassins.

So it was that Sera walked into the throne room of the hollow king carrying a book that could unmake him, and no one stopped her.

The throne room had been gilded twice in Aldric's reign. She remembered the second gilding — she had recorded it, in fact, a ceremony of some three hundred words in the annals, most of them *gleaming* and *restored* and *the king's pleasure*. Now the gold seemed less like decoration and more like a scab. It covered everything. The pillars, the arches, the great windows that had once been left open to the mountain air because Aldric had said, on the record, that a king who feared a draft feared his own people. The windows were shuttered now. The light came from a hundred braziers, and it was the wrong color — too orange, too steady, like fire that had been told to behave.

And on the throne, the thing wearing Aldric's face sat with its hands folded, and its shadow on the floor moved a half-second behind it, the way it always did, and Sera's stomach did the same cold turn it had done the first time she'd noticed, four months ago, and told no one.

*You are here to buy time,* she reminded herself. *Not to be brave. Bravery is for people with swords. You have a satchel and a talent for pedantry. Use the pedantry.*

She bowed. Not too deep — a chronicler of the third rank bowed to the second mark, and getting it wrong would be noticed, and being noticed was the one thing she could not afford.

"Your Majesty. Sera Vane, court chronicler, with the annual reconciliation of the regnal ledgers, as required by the Ordinance of Records, subsection four."

The hollow king looked at her.

She had prepared herself for many things in that look. Hunger. Suspicion. That terrible smileless attention it gave people, like a clerk counting coins. What she had not prepared for, because it still caught her every time, was how *patient* it was. Aldric had been a good king in the way rivers are good — forceful, occasionally flooding, fundamentally moving somewhere. This thing did not move. It received.

"Chronicler Vane." The voice was Aldric's voice, kept in very good condition. "You have been away from court."

"Conducting research in the provinces, Majesty. The annals do not write themselves, though I have on occasion wished they would."

Something flickered behind the eyes. Not amusement. The hollow thing had learned the *shape* of amusement the way a bird learns the shape of a scarecrow — it could perch on it, but it got nothing from it.

"Research," it said. "Into what?"

"Succession law, Majesty." Sera opened her satchel with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had absolutely nothing to hide, which required acting, because her heart was attempting to escape through her ribs. "Specifically, the question of what occurs if a regent's authority is found to derive from an instrument that was never properly countersigned. It is a fascinating tangle. I have brought the relevant chronicles."

She had, in fact, brought them. Four volumes of the most punishingly boring legal history the royal archives possessed. She had chosen them the way a soldier chooses weapons: for weight, and for the certainty that no one would ever want to read them.

The hollow king gestured — a half-second late, the shadow following like a dog that had not quite learned the trick — and she approached the foot of the dais and laid the volumes out on the table provided for petitioners, opening the first to a ribbon she had placed that morning.

"If Your Majesty will indulge me. The matter concerns the Founding Ordinance of the Regency. As Your Majesty is aware, all regnal authority flows from the coronation rite of Aldric the First—"

"I am aware," the king said.

"Of course, Majesty. Forgive me. It is simply that I have found, in preparing the reconciliation, a discrepancy in the countersignatures." She turned the page with a fingertip, savoring this, actually savoring it — the one pure pleasure left to her, which was being *technically correct* in front of power. "The rite required three seals: the crown, the temple, and — this is the part I find genuinely interesting, Majesty, I hope you'll forgive the enthusiasm — the *land's own assent*, which was given in that era by the binding of a fire-oath, witnessed by a creature of the old pacts."

The throne room went very quiet. The braziers did not even crackle. Sera had the sudden, vertiginous sense that the entire room was listening — not the courtiers, not the guards, but the room itself, the gilding, the shutters, the air.

"The old pacts are ended," the king said.

"Ended, Majesty, yes. But — and here is my dilemma — *when*?" She turned another page. "The annals record the closing of the fire-pact in the eleventh year of the reign. But the eleventh year of the reign, Majesty, is precisely the year for which we have *no annals*. The chronicle breaks off in the spring and resumes in the autumn, and the intervening pages were removed by order of — well. That is the difficulty. The removal order itself is unsigned."

She looked up, and met the hollow king's gaze, and did the bravest thing she had ever done, which was to raise one eyebrow, mildly, in the manner of a clerk confronting a missing receipt.

"So you see my problem, Majesty. I cannot reconcile the ledgers. And an unreconciled ledger is, by the Ordinance of Records — subsection four, which I mentioned, I do apologize for my one-note personality — *not a ledger at all*. Which means that everything derived from it — appointments, judgments, the authority of the inquisitors, and I say this with the greatest respect — the regency itself, rests on a book with pages torn out."

Somewhere in the gallery, a courtier shifted. Sera heard a whisper start and die.

The hollow king regarded her for a long moment. She counted her own heartbeats to keep from counting the seconds. Eleven. Twelve.

"You are telling me," it said at last, in Aldric's patient voice, "that the kingdom's authority is in question."

"I am telling Your Majesty that the *paperwork* is in question," Sera said. "The kingdom, I'm sure, is fine. Kingdoms usually are. It's the paperwork that suffers." She gestured at the second volume. "Now. There is a procedure for this. It's tedious, it takes days, and it requires that no instrument of regnal authority be exercised until the record is made whole — because, Majesty, if the record is made whole *after* the fact, then every act performed in the interval is void, and we would have to do all of them again. The executions. The seizures. The inquisitorial warrants." She let that sit. "The clerks would riot. Well. Clerks don't riot. We would compose a very strongly worded memorandum."

*Days,* she had promised Hazel, when they had parted at the mouth of the mountain road three nights ago, her hands shaking around the satchel that held the prophecy book. *I can give you days. No one in that palace can out-stubborn a chronicler with a procedural objection. It's the one thing we're bred for.*

She had not been sure it was true. She was beginning to suspect it was worse than true — she suspected the hollow king *needed* the paperwork to be whole, the way a man needs a spine. It wore a crown sustained by stolen fire and stolen names, and names, she had learned from the marginalia, from Vess's frantic century-old handwriting, were *records*. The thing could not simply ignore the annals. It lived in them. Every entry Sera had ever written about King Aldric was a thread in the thing's body, and she had just stood in front of it and announced that the threads were fraying.

"Chronicler Vane." The king rose from the throne.

Its shadow stayed seated a moment, then hurried to catch up.

"You have served this court eleven years," it said, descending one gilded step. "Your records are meticulous. Your discretion is noted." Another step. It was very close now, and this near, she could smell it — or rather, smell the *absence* of smell, the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue. Aldric had smelled of cedar oil and horses. This wore his face over nothing at all. "Tell me. What do you know of the Swordspeaker?"

*Here it is,* Sera thought, with a strange, floating calm. *The moment. Hold the line. Pedantry is a shield, and you have four volumes of it.*

"The Swordspeaker, Majesty?" She frowned — a genuine frown, because the question genuinely alarmed her, and she had long ago learned that her real expressions were her best disguises. "The oath-bound order was dissolved a century ago. There's a chapter in the third volume. They were disbanded after the fire-pact closed, their brands struck from the rolls, their — I'm sorry, Majesty, are you telling me there is *one*?"

The hollow king studied her. She had the horrible sensation of being read — not her face, but the ledger of her, the entries of her, as though it could find the page where she had made her choices and check them for discrepancies.

"You were in the provinces," it said.

"I was in the provinces, Majesty."

"You met no one on the roads."

"I met a smuggler, Majesty, who sold me a cheese of frankly criminal quality, and a mule with opinions. Neither of them spoke with a sword." She paused. "Unless that's a thing they do. I confess my research didn't extend to the practicalities."

For one wild, giddy moment, she thought she saw something almost like frustration cross the perfect face — and then the doors at the far end of the throne room opened, and an inquisitor entered, gray-robed, moving fast, and the moment broke like ice.

The inquisitor did not bow. It — she — walked straight up the dais steps and leaned close to the hollow king's ear, and Sera, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of appearing not to notice things, noticed everything: the way the inquisitor's lips moved, the way the king's hand closed slowly around the arm of the throne, the way the gilding on the armrest — she was almost sure, she would check the records later, she would *swear* to it later — darkened under those fingers, like metal remembering it had once been ore.

The king straightened.

"Chronicler Vane," it said. "Your reconciliation is granted. You will have your days. You will have access to the sealed archives, the eleventh year included." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "We have nothing to hide from our own records."

*That,* Sera thought, *is the single most frightening sentence ever spoken in this room, and I once transcribed a confession involving the ambassador, two goats, and the state seal.*

"Your Majesty is the soul of transparency," she said, and bowed to the second mark, and gathered her four volumes of weaponized boredom, and walked out of the throne room at a chronicler's pace, which is to say: not one step faster than dignity required, while every instinct screamed.

* * *

The sealed archives were in the undercroft beneath the east wing, and Sera reached them by late afternoon, escorted by two guards who had the vacant courtesy of men who had been given an order and no context. The eleventh-year archive was a single iron-banded chest, and the seal on it was intact, and her hands were steady until she broke it.

Inside: the annals for the eleventh year. The missing pages.

Or rather — inside was the *absence* of them. The chest held the year's ordinary records, tax rolls, grain tallies, a petition about bridge repairs, and then, where the chronicle of the spring should have been, a gap so clean it looked surgical. Pages removed by a careful hand. And in the gap, resting on the empty spine where the pages had been, someone had left a single sheet of new paper, unsigned, unsealed, in a hand she did not know.

She read it twice. Then she sat down on the cold flagstones and read it a third time, because her legs had decided the matter for her.

*Chronicler. If you are reading this, you kept your word, and the king let you in, which means it believes it has already won. It will show you the eleventh year. It will let you "discover" whatever it wants discovered. Do not trust anything in this room that wants to be found. The real pages were never here. I took them out the night it happened, and I gave them to a friend of mine on the mountain, and if you need them, look to the one who carries the brand. — V.*

Sera read the name twice. *V.* Vess. Hazel's Vess. Dead a decade, and still, from beyond it, handing her instructions through a hole in the record like the most insufferable, magnificent librarian who had ever lived.

*It believes it has already won,* the note said. And Sera thought of the inquisitor's whisper, the darkened gold, the king's granted permission — *you will have your days* — and understood, with a lurch, that she had not been stalling the hollow king at all.

She had been kept busy.

The thing could not think — Hazel had told her that, Emberveil had told her that, the prophecy's marginalia had said it in a hundred different ways: *it does not reason, it remembers; it does not plan, it replays.* But it could *use* people who thought, the way a fire uses a chimney. It had let her in. It had let her "discover" the gap. It had given her Vess's note — or left it where she would find it — because somewhere in its century of stolen memories, it had learned that a chronicler who finds a mystery will follow it anywhere, and a chronicler following a mystery is a chronicler not standing where she's dangerous.

*It wanted me in the undercroft,* Sera thought. *Away from the throne room. Away from the court. Away from—*

The realization arrived all at once, the way the worst ones do.

Away from the *annals*. The living ones. The ones being written *right now*, in the scriptorium above her head, where a dozen clerks were recording this afternoon's proceedings — including, she knew, because she had trained them herself, the standard closing entry for every audience: *the petition was heard, and the king's pleasure was made known.*

The king's pleasure. Made known. In ink. In the record. In the thing's own body.

She was up the stairs before she had finished the thought, satchel banging, guards calling after her, taking the servants' passage two steps at a time in robes that were absolutely not built for it, and she burst into the scriptorium breathless and gray-faced, and the youngest clerk, Tam, looked up from his pen with the expression of a boy caught doing nothing.

"Tam," she gasped. "This afternoon's entry. The throne room. Read it back to me. *Now.*"

He blinked. "It's not finished, mistress Vane, I was just—"

"*Read it.*"

He read it. *The chronicler Sera Vane presented the annual reconciliation. The king heard her petition and granted her request. The king's pleasure was made known.*

Made known. Past tense. Sealed in the record by a clerk who didn't know what he was writing, an hour before she had even reached the undercroft. The audience had been *concluded* before it began. Her days, her access, her careful, brilliant, four-volume stall — all of it had been entered into the annals as already granted, already finished, a play performed once and filed.

And that meant — her mind raced ahead of her fear, and for once she let it — that meant everything she had said in that room, every procedural trap she had laid, had been theater. The hollow king had never been deciding. It had been *remembering forward*. Somewhere in its century of stolen pages, it had already seen an audience with a chronicler named Vane, and it had simply... performed the memory, and let the clerks write it down, and the record — its body, its spine, its strength — had closed around her like a book.

*It cannot think,* she thought. *But it has read everything I have ever written. It knows me the way I know Aldric. From the outside. From the pages.*

The room seemed to tilt. She gripped the desk.

Then Tam said, uncertainly, "Mistress? There's — there's one more line. It came in with the courier from the mountain gate, not ten minutes past. I haven't entered it yet, it's not my place, the gate log goes to the senior clerk, but—"

"Tam." Her voice came out very quiet. "What line."

"The mountain gate, mistress. The old deep-roads gate under Thornfell, the one that's been sealed for a hundred years." He held up the courier's slip with a puzzled frown. "It says the seal just broke on its own. And that the gate is open. And that something is *climbing the stairs*."

Sera looked at the slip. She looked at the unfinished entry in Tam's careful hand. And she felt — for the first time since she had watched a king's shadow move a half-second late — something in her chest that was not fear.

It was the feeling, familiar to every chronicler, of a record about to be broken open, and a blank page waiting, and history standing at the door with its hand on the handle.

"Tam," she said, "go and wake the senior clerk. Tell him the annals for tonight will need a second scribe." She was already moving, satchel over her shoulder, toward the window that faced the mountain — toward the thin thread of light, far up the black flank of Thornfell, that might have been a torch, or might have been a gate opening, or might have been a man she had promised *days* to, arriving early, because smugglers, in her experience, never once respected a deadline.

"Tell him," she said, "that the record is about to be made whole."
