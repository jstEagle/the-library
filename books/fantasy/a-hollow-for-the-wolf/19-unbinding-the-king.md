## 19. Unbinding the King

The mountain's hidden ways spat Hazel out behind the throne room like a secret being kept too long.

He came up through a smuggler's crawl that hadn't existed three days ago — a seam in the palace foundations that Thornfell itself had opened for him, stone remembering old oaths and choosing, at last, whose side it stood on. He emerged in a wine cellar that smelled of dust and spilled port, beneath the very heart of the Gilded Court, and for one absurd moment he crouched there in the dark thinking: *I have broken into palaces before. This is fine. This is practically Tuesday.*

It was not fine. It was not Tuesday. It was the last night of something, though he didn't yet know which thing.

Above him, muffled through floors of marble and ambition, the court was singing.

* * *

He had left Sera below, at the mouth of the deep road, holding the coal of Emberveil's fire wrapped in wool and iron like the world's most dangerous hand-warmer. She had argued, of course. Sera Vane argued the way rivers ran downhill — constantly, inevitably, and with a great deal of noise about the eventual destination.

"You can't stall him forever," she'd said. "I'll give you an hour. Maybe two if I invent a dispute over heraldic precedence. Kings *hate* heraldic disputes. They can't resist them."

"An hour," Hazel said.

"And you—" She had stopped, and looked at him the way she looked at documents she suspected of lying. "You're going to do the word. The first word. You've already decided."

Hazel had smiled at her, which he knew from long experience made people trust him exactly as much as they should, which is to say not at all. "Sera. If I say yes, you'll tie me to a stalagmite."

"If you say no, I'll know you're lying, and I'll tie you to a stalagmite anyway."

So he'd said nothing, which was its own kind of answer, and she'd grabbed his collar and pressed her forehead briefly against his — a chronicler's blessing, or maybe just a friend's — and said, "Then I'm writing you down *before* you do it. Every word I remember. So that somewhere, in ink, you were real."

And now here he was, alone in a wine cellar under a singing court, with a sword that whispered and a palm that burned and a name he was carrying like a full cup through a crowded room.

* * *

The oath-brand on his hand had been quiet since Thornfell opened for him. Quiet wasn't the right word. It had been *attentive*. Like a hound that has finally caught the scent after years of casting about, sitting now at the edge of the treeline, trembling with restraint.

He climbed.

The palace above the wine cellar was all gilt and gloom, corridors hung with portraits of Aldric's line — a dynasty of stern chins and hunting dogs. Hazel moved through them the way he'd moved through a hundred guarded places: soft-footed, breath shallow, mind empty of everything except the next ten feet. Smuggling taught you that. You didn't think about the whole journey. You thought about the next ten feet, and then the ten after that, and eventually you looked up and you were somewhere impossible.

Somewhere impossible turned out to be a gallery overlooking the throne room, behind a screen carved with stags mid-leap.

Below, the hollow king held court.

Hazel had seen Aldric once, years ago, from a crowd — a big man, weathered, with a laugh that carried. What sat on the throne now wore that face the way a glove wears a hand. Everything was correct. The jaw, the grey at the temples, the scar through the left eyebrow. But it was *arranged*, not lived-in. And when the king turned his head to acknowledge some petitioning lord, his shadow came along half a heartbeat late, dragging across the marble like something wading through deep water.

The court sang — some anthem, old and swelling — and the hollow king's mouth moved with it, perfectly timed, perfectly empty. A puppet that had studied the song so well it no longer needed strings.

*There,* whispered the sword at Hazel's hip. Not in words, exactly. Swordspeech didn't use words; it used meanings, and his mind translated the best it could. *There is where the fire goes in.*

Hazel followed the sword's attention down, past the throne, past the king, to the floor itself — and saw what he'd been unable to see until Thornfell had taught him how to look. Channels. Veins of dull orange light running through the marble like cracks in cooling glass, converging on the throne from every direction, and rising, all of them, into the crown.

The crown was drinking.

Not gold at all, or not only gold — it was a foundry cast small, a furnace shaped like a circlet, and through it poured a century of stolen dragon-fire, siphoned from a dying dragon in a mountain hollow, spent to keep a dead man's shape upright on a chair.

Hazel's stomach turned. Not from horror — he'd had his fill of horror, thank you, it kept quite well — but from recognition. Because the channels glowed with the same script that curled around his oath-brand. The same rite. The same root. Whatever bound the crown bound him, and whatever freed the crown would cost him everything he was.

*Little word-bearer.* Emberveil's voice, remembered, warm as banked coals. *You may still turn back. I will not think less of you. I will think of you always, which is more than anyone else will be able to say.*

"Not helping," Hazel murmured.

*I was not trying to help,* said the memory of her, dry as old paper. *I was trying to love you. It is a different discipline entirely.*

* * *

Getting down to the throne room floor was insultingly easy, because the mountain had not finished helping. A service door that should have been locked yielded at his touch; a guard who should have been watching was watching instead a pair of courtiers having a whisper-fight near the wine. Even the singing covered him, a hundred throats drowning the sound of boots on marble.

Hazel had time to reflect, crossing that vast room in the anthem's shadow, that he had spent his whole life being careful, and that care had bought him exactly one century-late mention in a prophecy book and a death sentence. Whereas walking straight up the center aisle toward a hollow god-king with a sword that hummed — this felt, horribly, like the first honest work he'd done in years.

Ten feet. Ten feet. Ten feet.

The throne dais was three steps high. The channels converged at its base in a knot of light, and standing on the lowest step, close enough to see the stitching on the king's sleeve, Hazel stopped.

The anthem ended.

Silence dropped like a blade.

And the hollow king looked at him.

It did not startle. That was the worst of it. A man would startle — a guard would shout, a courtier would gasp. The thing wearing Aldric's face simply rotated its attention onto Hazel with the smooth inevitability of a lighthouse beam, and the whole court turned to follow it, hundreds of heads moving together, and Hazel understood that none of them were seeing anything at all. The audience was a painting. The king was the only living thing in the room, and it wasn't alive either.

"Swordspeaker," it said.

Aldric's voice. Or nearly. There was a graininess underneath, like a recording played through ash.

"You took your time," it said. "We wrote you a hundred years ago. We expected punctuality."

"Funny," Hazel said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "I read the book. The margins were better than the text."

Something flickered in the king's face — not expression, but the *memory* of expression, a muscle twitching toward a smile it no longer owned. "You are afraid," it observed.

"Terrified." Hazel drew the sword. It came out singing, a thin bright note that made several courtiers flinch without knowing why. "But I've been terrified since chapter two, and look how far it's gotten me."

"We do not understand your humor." The hollow king rose. It unfolded upward and upward, taller than Aldric had ever been, joints moving a fraction too far, and beneath the skin of its face Hazel could see — faintly, terribly — other faces. Pressed close. Crowding. A century of the taken, layered like leaves at the bottom of a pond. "We do not need to. We only need you to stop."

"I get that a lot," Hazel said, and spoke the second word of swordspeech.

The world went white at the edges. The dais cracked. Two inquisitors who had been waiting by the pillars — of course they'd been waiting, they were always waiting — went down like cut wheat, their own oaths turning traitor in their mouths. Hazel felt the power run through him and out, felt it *cost*, felt the brand on his palm sear deeper, and he thought with distant clarity: *that was the cheap word. The easy one. And it just took a piece of me I don't have a name for.*

The hollow king did not fall. It stood in the crater of its own dais, robes smoking, and tilted its head.

"Yes," it said. "That one. We remember that one. We helped write it."

And it came for him.

* * *

What followed was not, strictly speaking, a fight. Fights have rhythm, exchange, the courtesy of two wills contesting. This was a storm trying to swallow a man with a word in his teeth. The hollow king moved like falling architecture, and Hazel survived it the only way he knew how: by not deciding. His oathspeech answered before he chose it — a parry spoken by the brand, a sidestep authored by the sword, his body a pen in someone else's handwriting. He gave ground, gave ground, gave ground, and bled from the nose, and laughed — actually laughed, high and unhinged, because some ancient smuggler instinct in him had realized that the thing was *afraid of the words*, and fear in an enemy was the one currency Hazel had always known how to spend.

He backed it onto the throne. He didn't mean to. But the throne was where the channels met, where the crown drank, and when the hollow king's shoulders hit the backrest the whole web of veins flared orange, and the thing *shrieked* — a sound made of a hundred voices, none of them its own —

— and one of them was human.

One of them was *tired*.

"Aldric," Hazel gasped, pressing forward, sword flat against the king's chest, pinning it. "Aldric, I know you're in there. Vess told me. Emberveil told me. You bound this thing into yourself to hold back something worse. You paid in fire. You paid for a *century*."

The faces beneath the skin churned. And then, in the middle of the hollow king's cheek, like a face surfacing in dark water, another face appeared — weathered, lined, honest. The face from the crowd, years ago. The laugh that carried.

"*Boy,*" it rasped, and the voice was a ruin, a candle-end, a man worn down to his final syllable. "*Boy, listen. It holds the door. Below the crown — there's a door — and things press on it — I have held it — gods, I am so tired —*"

"Hold on," Hazel said stupidly. "Hold on, I'm here to—"

"*Finish it.*" The remnant's eyes found his, and they were clear, clearer than anything else in the room, clear as the last inch of water in a dying well. "*Not save. Finish. Do you understand? Unbind us both. The thing and the man. It cannot leave while I remain, and I cannot remain while it feeds. One word. Your word. The first one.*"

"That word costs my name," Hazel said. "Everyone forgets. Everyone, Aldric. There's one witness. One."

"*Yes.*" And the ruined face did something no hollow thing could do. It wept. One tear, tracking down through a century of borrowed skin. "*I know what it costs. I have watched it eat names for a hundred years. Mine went first.*" The eyes burned. "*Do it, swordspeaker. Be forgotten by everyone. Be remembered by the dragon. It is more than I got. It is more than I deserve.*"

The hollow thing surged against the sword, screaming with a hundred mouths. The crown blazed. Somewhere far below, Hazel felt rather than heard the foundry waking, the channels reversing, the whole stolen engine of the kingdom gathering itself for one final pull.

And Hazel Blackwood — smuggler, liar, last of the swordspeakers, a man whose entire life had been the art of remaining unnamed — stood at the point of no return and found, to his mild astonishment, that he was not afraid anymore.

He was *clear*. Terribly, wonderfully clear. Every road he'd ever walked, every name he'd ever shed, every careful anonymity had been leading here, to this step, to this word. You couldn't smuggle yourself out of this one. There was no border to cross, no pass unwatched enough. The only way through was to give up the one thing he'd never once risked.

His name.

He opened his mouth. The first word rose in him like dawn — older than the rite, older than the crown, the word all other words were cut from —

—and the doors of the throne room exploded inward, and the smoke walked in wearing Dain's smile.

"*He's here!*" Dain shouted to the dark beyond the doors, and the dark came pouring in after him, inquisitors in ranks, and above it all, from the direction of the mountain, a sound Hazel felt in his teeth and his brand and his breaking heart:

Emberveil, burning her last reserves, coming to die beside him.

The first word trembled on Hazel's tongue, unfinished.

The hollow king laughed with a hundred faces, and raised the crown in both hands like a weapon.
