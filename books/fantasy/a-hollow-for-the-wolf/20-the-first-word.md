## 20. The First Word

The foundry was dying as I climbed it.

That should have been a comfort — a building coming apart around the thing that had built it — but collapsing architecture has no loyalties. The stairwell behind me had already gone, swallowed by a groan of iron and falling brick, and the catwalk ahead sang under my boots like a plucked wire. Below, the siphon-channels I had burned shut in Chapter Nine's reckoning — no, forgive me, in another life, three days ago — still smoked, and the smoke rose in columns that bent wrong, curling toward the hollow king instead of away from him, as if even heat had learned to flatter power.

He stood at the crown-forge at the heart of the platform, and he was wearing more faces than the last time I'd seen him.

That was the horror of it, if you want to know. Not the faces themselves — I'd seen stolen things before, smuggled relics, forged seals, a man once wearing his dead brother's signet like it fit. It was how *well* they sat on him. A century of names, layered like lacquer, and every one of them polished smooth by use. He turned toward me and his face was Aldric's, then a woman's, then a soldier's, then Aldric's again, cycling so fast it made my teeth ache, and beneath all of them there was nothing. A hollow with excellent tailoring.

"Little word-bearer," he said, and my skin crawled, because those were Emberveil's words, her voice, lifted whole from some memory he had drunk dry. "You've come far for someone about to be forgotten."

"You'd know," I said. "You've been forgetting people for a hundred years."

He laughed. It was the worst sound yet — a laugh assembled from forty borrowed laughs, none of them quite reaching the others, like a choir reading from different hymnals. "I could offer you a place in me," he said. "Your oaths. Your fire. Your name would live forever, spoken by my mouth."

"That's not living," I said. "That's taxidermy."

And then the roof came off the world.

* * *

She came through it sideways, which is not something dragons are supposed to be able to do, but Emberveil had never much cared what things were supposed to do. One moment the night above the broken foundry was open sky and cold stars; the next it was wings — vast, tattered, luminous at the edges like paper held too close to a candle — and heat rolled down over us both, the true kind, the kind that remembers being alive.

She was burning her last reserves. I knew it the way you know a friend is spending money they don't have: by the brightness. Emberveil had been guttering for years, a coal wrapped in scales, and now she blazed like the dawn she remembered, and every second of that blaze was a second of herself she would not get back.

She did not land. Landing takes strength. She *hovered*, which costs tenfold, wings hammering the air into submission, and her head swung low over the forge-platform, and one ancient eye — gold going grey at the rim, like a coin worn past its face — fixed on the thing wearing the king's crown.

"You," she said, in a voice that cracked stone, "have been drinking from my well for a hundred years."

"I have been *preserving* it," said the hollow king, mild as milk.

"You have been *sipping* it." Her tail came around in an arc that took out the eastern wall and half the western sky's reflection in the river below. "There is a difference, little thief, and it is measured in centuries."

Then the inquisitors arrived, because of course they did. They came up through the stairwell I'd thought destroyed — they'd rebuilt it as they climbed, oath-work knitting iron behind their boots — a dozen of them in grey and silver, blades drawn, and their captain pointed at me and said, in the flat reciting voice of someone reading from a very old script:

"The Swordspeaker will stand down. This is the will of the crown."

"The crown," I said, "is currently making faces at me. I'm disinclined to take its will under advisement."

They charged. And here is the part where I confess something that still sits strangely in my chest, like a pebble in a boot: I didn't choose what happened next. My palm burned, the oath-brand flared white, and words came out of me that I had never learned, in a cadence older than the kingdom, and the lead inquisitor's blade turned to frost mid-swing and shattered, and two more stumbled on air that had briefly decided to be stone. Oathspeech answering before I chose it. Vess had warned me about this, near the end. *"It isn't your weapon, Hazel,"* she'd said. *"You're its. Try to remember which of you is holding which."*

But I'll tell you what I held anyway. Because Sera was somewhere below in that chaos — she'd gotten the regency council's witnesses out through the tunnels, I'd seen her go, dark coat and quicker wits — and because Dain was nowhere, which meant Dain was everywhere, selling our position to whoever paid fastest, and because Emberveil was spending her death to buy me minutes.

Minutes were the whole currency of the night. I intended to spend them well.

* * *

The fight, if you can call it that, went like this: the hollow king did not fight. He *edited*. He reached into the space between moments and rearranged them — a blow I'd thrown arriving before I'd thrown it, a wall standing where a gap had been, my own shadow briefly detached from my heels and running off across the platform without me, which I want noted as deeply rude. He was not strong the way a dragon is strong or a swordsman is skilled. He was strong the way a ledger is strong: everything accounted, nothing alive.

Emberveil fought the way fire fights, which is to say completely and without any interest in tomorrow. She swept the inquisitors from the platform in a single pass — not killed, most of them; thrown, tumbling, disarmed of both steel and certainty — and then she poured her flame over the crown-forge itself, melting the channels, boiling the stolen fire back into raw light, and the hollow king screamed with forty mouths and none of them his own.

"Hazel," she said, and her voice was smaller now. Dimming. The blaze was banked already, the last reserves spent, and she settled onto the ruined platform like a ship grounding. "Come here, little word-bearer. We have very little time, and I have even less."

I went. Of course I went. When a dragon asks, you go; when *this* dragon asks, having carried your true name in her keeping since before you understood you had one, you run.

"Aldric," she said, and nodded toward the hollow king, who had gone quiet, crouched over the cooling forge like a gargoyle come to life and disappointed about it. "Look closely. Underneath."

I looked. And beneath the cycling faces — beneath the lacquer, beneath the ledger — there was a flicker. Faint as a candle in a cathedral. A man's eyes, human and exhausted and *terrified*, looking out through the mask of himself, and the mouth beneath the mask shaping words that the hollow thing would not let through.

*Finish it,* the flicker said. Or I dreamed it said. *Please. Finish it.*

"He bound the hollow into himself a century ago," Emberveil said softly. "To hold back a greater dark — you know this story now, you've read my memories, slept in them like a kitten in straw. He thought he could control it. He thought he could pay and stop paying. No one can stop paying. And now the remnant of him — the true him, the man who loved his kingdom enough to break himself open for it — that remnant is one candle-flame in a storm, asking to be let out."

"So I unbind him," I said. "I speak the first word."

"Yes."

"And I am erased."

"Yes." Her eye closed, opened again. Grey-gold, tired beyond the geography of tired. "Every memory but mine. Every record but the one I keep. Your mother's face — gone from you, though you keep yours, cruelly, and hers will simply be a stranger's kindness you can't source. Vess. Gone. The roads you walked, walked by no one, by a shape. You will be a rumor with no name attached, Hazel Blackwood, and then not even a rumor. Are you listening to me? I am begging you. There is no version of this where I do not beg you. Let me burn him instead. Let me spend what remains and die glad. You are young. You are—"

"You're dying either way," I said. "Tonight or tomorrow. And he—" I looked at the flicker in the hollow king's face, the man inside the mask, and something in my chest went quiet and certain, the way it does on the far side of a hard decision, when the fear hasn't left but it's stopped voting. "He asked. Did you hear him? He *asked*. A hundred years trapped in his own body, wearing his own face like a punishment, and when help finally comes, he doesn't ask to be saved. He asks to be finished. Gratefully. That's not a monster, Emberveil. That's a wound. And I am — apparently, according to a book written before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath — I am the bandage or the knife. So."

I knelt, and set my branded palm against the cooling iron of the forge, and felt the old binding rise up through the metal to meet me, the same rite that crowned Aldric, the same root that fed my gift. It recognized me. Of course it did. We were family, the binding and I. Two branches off one poisoned tree.

"Don't," Emberveil said, one last time, quietly, the way you say *don't* when you already know.

"I know," I said. "I love you too."

* * *

Here is what I remember of the first word, and I remember all of it, which is the joke, isn't it — the price is that no one else will.

It was not a word in any language. It was the word underneath language, the one that was spoken before there were ears, the one all oaths are fragments of, the way all rivers are fragments of rain. When I spoke it, my mouth made a shape and the world made an answer, and the answer was silence — a specific silence, surgical, the silence of a page being cleanly torn out.

The hollow thing came apart the way a bad argument comes apart when someone finally says the obvious: all at once, and with a great deal of noise, and then not at all. A century of stolen faces peeled off it like leaves in a gale — the woman, the soldier, the chancellor, the child, dozens, hundreds, each one flashing free and *going home*, dissolving into the air with small sounds like sighs, like debts forgiven. The crown itself cracked down the middle with a report like a mountain shrugging, and the stolen fire inside it, all that sipped and hoarded and hoarded-again light, burst upward and outward and scattered on the wind over Thornfell like seeds, like sparks, like everything it had always wanted to be instead of fuel.

And Aldric came out of it.

Not the king. The man. He collapsed onto the platform an old, old young man — a body aged by captivity rather than years, hands shaking, eyes wet and human and *his own* — and the last thing the remnant of King Aldric ever did was look at me, really look, with the full attention of someone seeing clearly for the first time in a century, and smile.

"Thank you," he said. His voice was rusted nearly through. "Whoever — whatever — you are. Thank you."

He didn't know my name. Even then, in the moment of the speaking, the erasure was already working backward through time, tidying as it went. He thanked a shape in the smoke. It was enough for him. It was, I decided, watching the light go out of him gently, like a lamp turned down at the end of a long evening, going to be enough for me too.

Aldric died human. That was the whole victory. Everything else was cost.

* * *

Emberveil found me afterward, kneeling beside the dead king in the ruins of the forge, and she put her great head down over me the way a hen covers eggs, and the warmth of her breath moved through my hair, and I felt the change already settling into me like snowfall — soft, total, irreversible.

"It's done," I said. My voice sounded strange. Lighter. Unburdened, and missing the burden the way you miss a limp after the leg heals — oddly free, oddly bereft. "Say it now. Before it finishes. Say my name, so it survives somewhere."

She was quiet a long moment. Dragons are quiet the way mountains are quiet: not absence, but mass.

"Hazel Blackwood," she said.

And the words went through me like bells through fog — I heard them, I *felt* them, and I understood that they were the only copy left in all the world, that outside her keeping I was already becoming a shape, a someone-shaped hole, a pair of boots on a road belonging to no story. Somewhere below us, I knew, Sera was climbing toward the ruins with witnesses in tow, and she would find a dead king, a scorched hollow, a guttered dragon, and no swordspeaker at all. Just smoke. Just a silhouette the chroniclers would argue about for a generation and then stop arguing about.

Good, I thought. Let them argue. Arguments keep a shape warm.

"Hazel Blackwood," Emberveil said again, softer, sealing it, a flame cupped in bare hands against the wind. "Once, so it cannot be lost."

"Once is enough," I said. "It only has to be kept. Not known."

Her eye was wet. I want that recorded somewhere, anywhere, even here, even in a book no one will read correctly: dragons weep, and this one wept for me, and it was the finest thing anyone ever did.

"Now go," she said. "Walk your roads, nameless wolf. And when you pass a hollow in the mountains — any hollow, every hollow — listen. Something in it will be saying your name."

* * *

I stood at the edge of the ruined foundry as the dawn came up over Thornfell, and the light hit me and slid off, uninterested, the way light slides off things that have stopped insisting on themselves. Below, faintly, I heard voices — soldiers, witnesses, a chronicler's sharp bright voice cutting through the confusion, demanding accounts, taking notes, beginning the long work of writing a history that would have a hole in it exactly my size.

Somewhere in that history, I knew, there would be a question no one could answer: *who?* Who burned the channels? Who broke the crown? Who knelt by the king? The shape in the smoke. The figure on the platform. A man-shaped absence that everyone saw and no one could name.

I smiled at it. At the hole. It was, when you got right down to it, the largest thing I'd ever built.

Then I picked up my pack — lighter now; I'd left the moonsteel on the platform, payment of a debt I only half understood — and I turned down the mountain road, and the road, saturated with a century of smugglers' oaths, opened ahead of me like a hand uncurling, and I walked into the new age carrying nothing at all except, somewhere far behind me in a mountain hollow, a single flame that knew my name.

Sera Vane would write it all down. She was already writing. And when she reached the empty space where the swordspeaker should have been — when she stared at the shape in the smoke and felt, without knowing why, that she had *missed someone* — I hoped she'd understand that the ache she felt was the whole memorial. Grief with no object. Mourning with no name.

It was, all things considered, a fair trade.

The road went down. The sun came up. And in a hollow deep in Thornfell, a dragon whispered *Hazel Blackwood* to the dark, once more, gently, the way you speak a name to keep it warm — and somewhere below, a faceless wanderer smiled at nothing, and kept walking.
