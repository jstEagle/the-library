## 21. Everyone Forgets Hazel Blackwood

The ink was still wet on the regency proclamation when Sera Vane realized she had a problem.

Not the regency itself — that had gone smoothly, in the way that things go smoothly when half the court is too frightened to object and the other half is too busy counting who's left standing. Lord Chancellor Merrick had been sworn in as Regent before Aldric's body was three days cold, and he'd done it with the same mild competence he brought to everything, like a man chairing a meeting about grain tariffs while the building burned around him. He was, Sera thought, exactly what the kingdom needed: someone profoundly unremarkable, in an age that had just learned what remarkable cost.

No, her problem was smaller than that. Her problem was a blank space.

She sat in the scriptorium that had been hers for eleven years — they hadn't taken it from her, which she suspected was Merrick's quiet way of saying *thank you* without having to hear the whole story — and she stared at the page in front of her, at the line where a name should have been.

*And the swordspeaker, whose name was—*

Nothing. Not a gap in her memory, exactly. Gaps you could feel the edges of. This was worse. This was a sentence that simply refused to continue, the way a road refuses to continue past a landslide. She knew there had been a man. She could feel the shape of him the way you feel the shape of a missing tooth — everything leaning slightly toward the absence. She remembered his hands on the oath-brand. She remembered him laughing once, badly, in the dark of the deep roads, at something she'd said about royal genealogy. She remembered the exact weight of the moment when he'd looked at Emberveil and made his decision, though she couldn't have told anyone what his face looked like while he did it.

She remembered everything about him except the part of him that was *him*.

"Damn it," she said, to the empty scriptorium, and dipped her pen again.

* * *

The trouble with writing true history, Sera discovered over the following weeks, was that everyone else was so eager to help.

Merrick wanted a version with himself braver in it. The surviving inquisitors — those who hadn't been in the foundry when it came down, and those who had been and somehow walked out with no memory of why their boots were scorched — wanted a version where the crown had been deceived by foreign sorcery, cleanly and from outside, so that no one would have to ask uncomfortable questions about how long the thing wearing Aldric's face had been signing orders. The guilds wanted a version where the smugglers' roads had never existed. The priests wanted a version with more light in it, and fewer dragons, and absolutely no mention of what the dragon had done.

Sera gave them all the same answer, which was a polite smile and a closed door, and then she went back to writing the truth, or as much of the truth as language would hold.

It held less than she'd hoped.

She wrote the hollow king first, because that part came easily — horror always does. She wrote the foundry beneath the capital, the channels of stolen fire running through the mountain's bones like veins in a corpse that wouldn't stop twitching. She wrote the battle atop the collapsing works, pieced together from forty contradictory accounts, and in every single one of them there was the same figure: a shape in the smoke, sword alight with fire that witnesses described variously as white, gold, blue, and — in one memorable case from a guardsman who had clearly been drinking — "the color of my mother's singing." She wrote Emberveil's arrival, the last reserves of a dying age spent in a single burning hour, and how the sky over Thornfell had glowed for two days after, and how the shepherds in the low valleys had come out of their houses and stood in the fields looking up, not afraid, just watching, the way you watch something leave.

And then she reached the swordspeaker, and the page went blank again.

She tried everything. She tried writing around it — *the oath-bound one*, *he who spoke the first word*, constructions that circled the absence like a tongue probing a socket. She tried asking others. That was how she learned it wasn't only her.

"You mean the one in the smoke," said Dain, when she cornered him outside the Oathbreakers' Market, which was no longer breaking any oaths since its clientele had mostly been pardoned, and had therefore rebranded, with the shameless pragmatism of all markets, as the Freeholders' Exchange.

"The swordspeaker," Sera said. "You traveled with him."

"I traveled with—" Dain frowned. He was a big man built for doors he didn't fit through, and watching him frown was like watching a mountain consider a problem. "There was someone. Wasn't there. On the climb up. I keep thinking there was someone who owed me money."

"He didn't owe you money."

"No," Dain agreed slowly. "I think I owed *him*. That doesn't seem right either." He shook his head like a dog shedding water. "Look, chronicler, I'll tell you what I tell everyone. There was a shape in the smoke. It did something impossible. Then it wasn't there anymore. Whatever name you want to put on that shape, put it on. The mountain won't correct you."

But the mountain might, Sera thought. That was the thing nobody else seemed to remember. The mountain had chosen a side. She'd seen the deep roads open where no roads were; she'd felt Thornfell hum under her feet like a struck bell. If anything in this kingdom still held the name, it wasn't a person. It was stone.

* * *

In the third week, Merrick summoned her to the regent's office, which had been Aldric's office, which had been — though almost no records survived to say so — the office of every king for a hundred years, each of them sitting in the same chair above the same channels of borrowed fire, and none of them knowing.

"You're writing it down," Merrick said. It wasn't a question. Merrick didn't ask questions; he stated things and waited to see if you'd contradict him, which was, Sera had decided, the entire secret of his success.

"All of it," she said. "The channels. The foundry. What was done to Aldric, and how long it went on, and who let it go on. Especially that last part."

Merrick winced, which she appreciated. A man willing to wince in front of a chronicler was a man who understood what chronicles were *for*.

"And the swordspeaker?" he asked. "What will you call him?"

And there it was. The blank space, sitting between them in the warm afternoon light like a guest neither had invited.

"I don't know his name," Sera said. It was the first time she'd said it aloud, and hearing it made her chest hurt in a way she couldn't account for, grief arriving without an address, like a letter forwarded too many times. "That's the truth of it, Regent. I was there. I fought beside him. I watched him unmake the thing that wore our king, and I cannot tell you his name, because there isn't one anymore. Not anywhere. Not in anyone."

Merrick was quiet for a long moment. Outside the window, the city went about its business — carts, bells, a fishmonger arguing with heaven about the price of haddock — the ordinary noise of a kingdom that had been given back to itself.

"There are stories already," he said finally. "You know that. Down in the Warrens they're leaving candles out. For the shape in the smoke. They don't know who it was. They just... leave the candles."

"I know."

"My grandmother used to say that a name is just a handle," Merrick went on, in the tone of a man embarrassed to be caught being wise. "You grab a person by it. But some people can't be grabbed. Maybe that's what he bought. Maybe he bought being un-grabbable." He cleared his throat, mortified by his own eloquence. "Write whatever's true, Vane. You're the only one who knows what that is."

*Whatever's true.* Sera walked back to the scriptorium turning the phrase over, and it was somewhere on the second staircase that she stopped, because she'd remembered something.

She remembered Vess.

Not well — Vess had been dead a decade, a court archivist of the old school, sharp-tongued and kind in the specific way of people who expected nothing from anyone. But Sera had been young and hungry then, and Vess had taught her the first rule of the craft: *a record isn't what happened. A record is what survives happening. Choose your survivors carefully.*

And Vess, Sera now recalled with a chill that had nothing to do with the drafty stairwell, had kept a private collection. Marginalia. Annotations in books that officially contained no annotations. Sera had seen it once, years ago — a shelf of volumes in Vess's quarters, each one marked in a small precise hand — and Vess had caught her looking and said, lightly, "Everyone edits, dear. The honest ones just sign their work."

Sera went back to the scriptorium and wrote, in the margin of her own manuscript, beside the blank space where the swordspeaker's name should have been:

*This absence is not damage. This absence is the price, paid in full, by consent, by the only person with the right to pay it. Whoever reads this: the emptiness you feel here is not a failure of the record. It is the record.*

Then she sat back, read it over, and added:

*He laughed badly, once, in the dark, at a joke about royal genealogy. I choose to believe that counts as surviving.*

* * *

Spring came to the capital the way it always did — badly, then all at once. The scaffolding came down off the palace. The channels under the mountain were sealed with stone and oath both, the masons working alongside the few remaining oath-takers, who had discovered that being suddenly legal suited them and had formed, of all things, a guild, with dues and a newsletter. The dragon-fire that remained unburned in the foundry vaults was carried north in escorted wagons, and Sera rode with the convoy herself, because there was one more piece of the history she needed, and it wasn't in any book.

Emberveil's hollow was warm.

That was the first thing. After everything — the guttering, the siphoning, the last great burning over the capital — Sera had expected cold, ash, an empty cave with a story attached. Instead the deep chamber breathed heat like a banked hearth, and at its center, coiled around a single flame no larger than a candle, lay the dragon.

She was smaller. That was the second thing. Dragons, Sera supposed, must burn mass as fuel like anything else, and Emberveil had spent nearly everything. What remained was still vast — a hillside of bronze and ember-dark scale, breathing slow as tide — but vast the way a ruin is vast, grandeur measured in what was missing.

"You're the writer," Emberveil said, without opening her eyes. Her voice filled the hollow the way water fills a bowl. "The little one with the ink-stained fingers. Come to ask me the name."

"If you'll give it."

One eye opened. It was the color of the foundry fire, the color of the last hour, and looking into it Sera felt — briefly, unbearably — the shape in the smoke again, the missing tooth, the ache with no address.

"No," said Emberveil, gently.

Sera had prepared arguments. She'd prepared them the whole ride north — duty, history, the rights of the living, the cruelty of a memorial only one creature could visit. She opened her mouth to begin the first of them, and the dragon's breath moved through the chamber, warm and slow, and every argument died of natural causes.

"Why not?" was all she managed.

"Because a name spoken is a name spent," Emberveil said. "Spoken once, to the dark, kept warm — that is keeping. Spoken into your book, it becomes a handle. And he did not spend everything he was to become grabbable." The eye closed again. "You loved him. I know. You don't know that you know, but I know. Grief with no object — I have carried that longer than your kingdom has existed. It does not need a name to be real. Neither does he."

Sera stood in the warmth for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice came out steadier than she'd expected.

"What do I write, then?"

"Write the truth you have," said the dragon. "A shape in the smoke. A debt unpaid. A candle in the Warrens. And at the end —" the great head shifted, scales whispering against stone, and for a moment the whole hollow seemed to lean toward Sera, confidential, conspiratorial, almost amused — "write that somewhere on the high roads, travelers sometimes meet a faceless wanderer who smiles at nothing, and that those who share their bread with him report unusually good weather afterward. Let them wonder. Wondering is a kind of remembering."

* * *

She finished the manuscript in autumn. She titled it *A True Account of the Hollow King, with Marginalia*, and she signed her work, because she'd promised a dead woman she'd be honest about editing.

On the last page, below the account of the regency, the sealed channels, the guild of reformed oath-takers and their inexplicably popular newsletter, she wrote the ending the way the dragon had taught her:

*The kingdom remembers a shape in the smoke. The mountain remembers more, but mountains keep their own counsel. And in a hollow deep in Thornfell, something is kept warm that the world agreed to lose.*

*If you have read this far and feel you've missed someone — if there is an ache in you shaped like a person you never met — do not grieve that you cannot name it. Name it anyway. Call it whatever your heart hands you. I am told the weather improves.*

She set down her pen. Outside the scriptorium window, the first snow was starting, fat and unhurried, and down in the Warrens someone had already lit the first candle of the season — a small stubborn flame in a doorway, left out for no one, left out for everyone.

Sera watched it a while. Then she blew out her lamp, and in the dark she said, quietly, to the blank space on the last page, the words she'd chosen to be his name — the ones her own heart handed her, on a night much like this one, in a tunnel under a mountain, when a man had laughed badly at a joke about royal genealogy:

"Goodnight, Hazel Blackwood."

And far away, deep in Thornfell, a dragon lifted her head as if she'd heard — and smiled, in the way of dragons, which is mostly teeth and entirely sincere.
