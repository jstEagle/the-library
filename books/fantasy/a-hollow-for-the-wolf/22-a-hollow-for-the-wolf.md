## 22. A Hollow for the Wolf

The hollow was quieter now.

Emberveil had expected that. Hollows are always quieter after the ending of things — she had outlived enough endings to know the acoustics of aftermath. But she had not expected the silence to feel like this: not the silence of a tomb, nor the silence of an empty den after cubs have grown and gone, but something closer to the silence inside a held breath. The whole mountain seemed to be waiting to exhale.

She lay with her chin on her forelegs and watched the coal.

It was a small thing, as her fires went. A bead of true flame no larger than a robin's egg, cupped in the shallow basin of stone where her heart-fire had once pooled in rivers. Once — a century ago, two centuries, she had stopped counting honestly somewhere around the reign of Aldric's grandfather — this hollow had glowed so brightly that travelers on the far peaks swore Thornfell wore a second moon at night. Now there was only the coal, and the dark, and her.

But oh, what a coal it was.

*Hazel Blackwood.*

She said the name inwardly, the way one turns a smooth stone over in the palm, and felt it settle into place again, warm and complete. That was the trick of it — the terrible, beautiful trick he had never fully understood until the moment he chose it. The first word of swordspeech did not merely unmake. It *remembered*. It took everything a thing was and folded it down into a single syllable of fire, and then it erased every other copy in the world. Every memory, every record, every half-remembered anecdote told over ale — gone. Sera Vane had written his name in her chronicle three times before the ink finished drying, and by the time she looked up from the page, the page said only *a swordspeaker, whose name is lost*, and Sera had wept onto the parchment without knowing why.

Emberveil was the exception. Dragons are exceptions; it is one of our few redeeming qualities, along with excellent hearing and a well-developed sense of grievance. The first word could not erase what had spoken it into being, and Emberveil had been present — in body if not in strength — when Hazel learned it from her. She was woven into the word's making. So the name lived here, in her, in this coal, and nowhere else in all the wide world.

One witness. One flame. One name.

It would have to be enough.

* * *

In the weeks that followed the fall of the foundry, visitors came to the hollow. Not many. The mountain chose them carefully — she could feel Thornfell doing it, the old oaths in the stone sifting through the traffic of boots and hooves like a housekeeper sorting laundry, admitting some and turning others away with landslides and wrong turnings and a general air of *nothing to see here.*

Sera Vane came first, of course. She climbed the last stretch of the smugglers' road on foot, having discovered that mules and the deep roads had reached a mutual understanding that excluded her, and she arrived at the hollow's mouth dusty, furious, and clutching her chronicle to her chest like a shield.

"You," she said, before she had even properly seen Emberveil in the dimness. "You knew him. You know his name. I wrote three hundred pages about that man and I cannot remember his *face*, I remember his hands and his terrible jokes and the way he said *well* when things were going badly, and I cannot—"

"Sit down, chronicler," Emberveil said. Her voice filled the hollow the way water fills a cup. "You are shouting at a dragon who is older than your kingdom. There are traditions about this."

Sera sat down. To her credit, she also apologized, which put her ahead of most humans who shouted at dragons.

"I can't give you the name back," Emberveil told her, when the young woman had caught her breath. "Not truly. If I spoke it aloud to you, the word would pass through you like light through glass. You'd lose it before you reached the bottom of the mountain. That is the shape of the price he paid. It does not bend."

"Then what good is remembering?" Sera demanded, and her voice cracked on it, and Emberveil saw that beneath the fury was grief, and beneath the grief was guilt, because Sera had stood in the throne room and stalled a hollow king with nothing but footnotes and procedural objections while a man she could no longer name walked into the fire for all of them.

"The good," Emberveil said gently, "is that you remember *him*. The name is just the handle on the door, little scribe. You still have the room."

They talked for a long time after that. Sera read passages from her chronicle aloud — the ones that had survived the erasure, the shape of him without the substance — and Emberveil corrected her on points of dragon etiquette ("No, he did not *bow* to me, he did that awkward half-thing humans do when they can't decide, and I found it charming, write it down exactly that way") and on points of history (the pact, the binding, the century of borrowed fire, all of it, because someone had to be the witness and Sera was clearly determined to be the other one).

Before she left, Sera paused at the hollow's mouth.

"He smiled at me once," she said. "Right before the gate. When Dain's treachery came out and everything was falling apart. He smiled like — like this was all an inconvenience that had happened to *other people* and he was just waiting politely for it to finish." She shook her head. "I don't know why I'm telling you this. You were there."

"I was there," Emberveil agreed. "Tell me anyway. That is what witnesses are for. We hold things so others don't have to carry them alone."

Sera wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, very briskly, the way people do when they have decided crying is inefficient. Then she went down the mountain to write the true history of the regency, and to spend the rest of her life, though she didn't know it yet, being slightly haunted by a face she couldn't picture.

* * *

Dain came in autumn.

He did not come up the road. He came limping up the road, which was different, and Emberveil heard him from three switchbacks away — the uneven tread, the hitch of breath, the particular rhythm of a man carrying guilt like a sack of wet sand.

He stopped at the threshold of the hollow and did not enter. Wise of him. The mountain had opinions about Dain, and the opinions were currently expressed as a thin film of loose scree that would, if he stepped wrong, deposit him back at the bottom with educational force.

"I know you're awake," he called. "Dragons breathe. You've been breathing at me since the second bend."

"State your business, oathbreaker."

"Ex-oathbreaker. Ex-everything, really. Career's in ruins. Reputation too, such as it was." He shifted his weight. "I sold him out. You know that. Everyone knows that, or they'd know it if anyone remembered him, which nobody does, which means I betrayed a man whose name I can't even—" His voice snagged. He cleared his throat roughly. "I got what I asked the crown for. The book. The prophecy book. And then I read the last letter in the spine, the one from his teacher, and I understood what I'd actually done, and I've been climbing toward this mountain ever since because I don't know where else to put it."

"Put it down," Emberveil said. "The guilt. Set it here. This hollow has held worse."

Dain laughed — a short, broken sound — and then, to her mild surprise, he did exactly that. He knelt at the threshold, in the cold, on the stones, and he talked. About the market at Oathbreakers' Cross. About the crown's silver and how easy it had looked. About the battle at the hollow gate, and the look on Hazel's face when the inquisitors crested the ridge — not anger, worse than anger, *disappointment*, the specific disappointment of a man who had genuinely hoped better of you. About the letter. About Vess, and the cruel ending, and the choice made to spare one life by dooming everyone else's peace.

"He'd have forgiven me," Dain said finally. "That's the worst part. I watched him forgive people his whole — well. However long I knew him. He forgave like it cost him nothing. And I can't even ask him to, because I can't find him, because there's no *him* to find, except—" He gestured vaguely upward, at the peaks, at the roads, at the world. "Except people say there's a wanderer. On the high roads. Faceless, near enough. Helps folk with broken cart axles and lost children and arguments about property lines. Smiles at nothing. Is that—"

"Yes," Emberveil said.

Dain let out a breath that seemed to empty him entirely. "Is he happy?"

Emberveil considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. She thought of the coal, and the name folded inside it, and the strange freedom of a man with no name to live up to, no prophecy to fulfill, no past to owe anything to. She thought of the smile Sera described, and the smile Dain described, and the smile she herself had glimpsed — through the mountain's old oaths, through Thornfell's thousand listening stones — on a road above the clouds, worn by a man helping a shepherd find a lost lamb in the fog.

"No," she said at last. "And yes. He is something better than happy. He is *unburdened*. Happy will come back to him, I think, in pieces, the way spring comes back to a burned hillside. Slowly, and from unexpected directions, and looking nothing like what was there before."

Dain nodded slowly. He left a bundle at the threshold — dried meat, a flask of something strong, a new whetstone, habits of a lifetime — and he went back down the mountain, and Emberveil noticed that the scree let him pass without incident.

Thornfell, it seemed, had its own opinions about forgiveness. They were more generous than hers. She resolved to work on that.

* * *

Winter came, and with it the regency's first hard questions, and with those, a delegation.

Three riders, under a white banner with no sigil — the banner of the interregnum, of a kingdom between stories. At their head rode a girl of perhaps sixteen, wearing the chain of an apprentice chronicler, and she dismounted at the base of the final climb with the expression of someone who has rehearsed a speech the entire way up and intends to deliver it if it kills her.

"Great Emberveil," she called up the path, in the formal cadences of the old court style, getting roughly half the honorifics right. "I bring greetings from Lady Sera Vane, Chronicler of the Regency, and a request, and a gift, and — and a question, if the first three don't get me eaten."

"They won't," Emberveil rumbled, amused. "Come up. Mind the fourth step. It bites."

The girl minded the fourth step. She laid the gift at the hollow's mouth: a book, bound in blue leather, hand-copied. Emberveil knew it at once — Sera's chronicle, the true one, the one that named the hollow king and the stolen fire and the swordspeaker whose name was lost. The first copies were going out into the world now, Sera had told her. Into libraries and schoolrooms and the hands of anyone who would read.

"And the question?" Emberveil asked.

The girl took a breath. "Lady Vane says you keep his name. She says it isn't hers to ask for, and she'd never ask, but she says — she says that someday, maybe, if the world gets kinder, names shouldn't have to stay lost forever. And I wanted to know if that's true. If it could be true. Because I've read her book four times and I cry at the same part every time, the part where nobody remembers, and I think — I think a kingdom ought to know who saved it. Even if he doesn't want us to. Even if it costs something. I think somebody should at least *ask*."

Emberveil was quiet for a long moment.

"You may tell Lady Vane," she said finally, "that the answer is: not yet, and perhaps, and ask me again in fifty years. Names kept in dragon-fire do not fade. They wait. And waiting is a thing dragons are famously good at." She lowered her great head until one ancient eye, gold-ringed and warm, was level with the girl. "And you may tell her something else. Tell her that he knows. What she did. What she carries. The word remembers everything that was folded into it, and she was folded into it — her stalling, her footnotes, her standing between him and the king's gaze with nothing but a ledger. He knows, and he is not sorry, and if he could speak to her he would say something infuriating and kind, probably beginning with *well*."

The girl blinked back tears, bowed approximately correctly, and went down the mountain with her book and her message, and Emberveil settled back into the dark, feeling — there was no other word for it — *lighter*.

* * *

Spring found her doing something she had not done in a century: planning a future.

The hollow was healing. Without the siphon-channels drinking her dry, her fire crept back by patient degrees — not the ocean of flame she had once been, perhaps never that again, but a lake where there had been a puddle, a river where there had been a trickle. Enough. Enough to warm eggs, someday, if any eggs remained anywhere in the world. Enough to fly the length of Thornfell without resting. Enough, on clear nights, to make the far peaks glow faintly again, so that a new generation of travelers might invent new superstitions about the mountain's second moon.

And the coal burned on, steady as a heartbeat, holding its single treasure.

*Hazel Blackwood.*

Some nights she spoke it aloud, just to hear it in the hollow where he had slept, where he had argued with her about prophecy, where he had eaten an entire wheel of smugglers' cheese and then blamed the bat. Some nights she only turned it over silently, the way one keeps a promise warm.

And sometimes — this was her favorite, though she'd have denied it under torture — the mountain showed her things. Thornfell's old oaths ran through every tunnel and switchback, and they carried images the way rivers carry leaves. A figure on the high road, cloak pinned against the wind, pausing to help a merchant whose axle had cracked. The same figure sitting at a crossroads fire with a family of travelers, making their children laugh with a story about a dragon who was afraid of mice. The same figure, alone at dawn on a ridge above the clouds, looking east at the waking light — and smiling at nothing, at no one, at the simple outrageous fact of another morning.

Once — only once — the figure stopped, mid-stride, on an empty road with snow on either side, and turned its face directly toward the mountain, toward her, as though it had felt the coal turn over in its keeping.

And it smiled. Not at nothing, that time.

At her.

"Well," the wind seemed to say, in a voice only she could hear, in a tone she knew better than her own fire. *Well.*

Emberveil laughed — a great rolling sound that sent echoes tumbling down the deep roads and startled, she later learned, an entire caravan of mules — and she curled herself around the coal, around the name, around the long patient years ahead.

"Go on, then, little word-bearer," she murmured to the empty road, to the smiling wanderer, to the morning. "Walk your roads. Be no one. Be everyone. Spring comes back slowly, and from unexpected directions."

The coal flickered, warm and content, like a heartbeat.

Like a promise kept.

And high above the hollow, on a road with no name, a faceless wanderer walked on into the new age — unhurried, unburdened, and humming, very softly, a song the mountain had taught him, in a language only one living creature in all the world still understood.

She understood it perfectly.

She always would.
