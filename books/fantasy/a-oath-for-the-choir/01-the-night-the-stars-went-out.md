## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

There are three rules to being nobody in Vaelmoor, and I had followed all of them for ten years without a single slip.

One: never sell a charm that actually works. Two: never look a soldier in the eye. Three: never, under any circumstances, listen to the animals where anyone can see you listening.

I was breaking rule one at my stall in Tallowmarket when the evening went wrong.

"Guaranteed against bad luck," I told the fishmonger's wife, holding up a knotted cord with a river-glass bead at its center. "Bad luck hates knots. Can't get a grip on them. Slides right off."

It was a lie, of course. The cord was decorative. The bead was glass. But Marta Hobb had lost two husbands to the river and one to a card debt, and she wanted very badly to believe that the universe could be bribed with string, so she paid me her copper and walked away clutching it like a talisman, which—honestly—was worth more than any real charm I could have made her. Belief is the only magic most people ever need, and I sold it by the yard.

I was good at this. That was the whole trick of my life: be mediocre on purpose. A charm-seller with a crooked stall and a crooked smile and charms that were ninety percent theater. Nobody looks twice at a fraud. Fraudulence is a kind of invisibility cloak, if you wear it long enough.

The animals started going quiet just as the lamps were lit.

I didn't notice it at first, the way you don't notice a headache arriving—it's only there once it's fully settled. Vaelmoor at dusk is never silent. The gulls argue over the fish-docks until full dark. The cart-horses grumble their way down Copperlane. Somewhere behind the tanneries there is always a dog with opinions about everything, and the pigeons in the eaves of the chandlers' row mutter like old men complaining about bread prices.

I'd spent ten years not listening to them. Ten years of holding that door shut in my head, wedging it closed with boredom and drink and the deliberate cultivation of a mind like a shut shop. It hadn't been easy. The gift doesn't care about your plans; it sits in you like a second heartbeat, and every animal voice in earshot presses against that door like wind against a shutter. You learn to let it become weather. You learn to hear nothing so hard that hearing nothing becomes who you are.

But that evening, without meaning to, without deciding to, I noticed the silence.

Not quiet. Silence. There's a difference, and the difference has teeth. Quiet is the absence of sound. This was the absence of *voice*—the gulls still wheeling over the docks with their beaks open, making no sound at all. The horses plodding with their ears flat and their mouths shut. And under it all, faint as a smell of smoke from a fire streets away, something wrong in the air itself, a taste like a struck bell that won't stop ringing somewhere below hearing.

I stood there with a knot-cord in my hand and felt the hair rise on my arms.

Because I knew what silence meant. Everyone born before the Reforms knew, though we weren't supposed to say it aloud anymore. The border wards—the great unseen walls that had stood between the free cities and whatever waited beyond the fells for four hundred years—were said to run on more than stone and sigil. My grandmother's generation had whispered about it. *The wards have voices,* the old women said, and then the Reforms came, and the usurper's men came through the cities asking questions, and everyone learned to whisper even quieter or not at all.

I hadn't thought about the wards in years. I hadn't allowed myself to think about much of anything in years. That was the arrangement: I kept my head down, my gift locked, my past buried under a false name and a fraud's smile, and in exchange the world left me alone.

The world was breaking the arrangement.

* * *

The stars went out between one breath and the next.

I want to be precise about this, because later—when they ask you, and they will ask—you'll want to have been precise. It wasn't clouds. Clouds come in from the sea; you feel them, you smell them, the gulls go inland ahead of them. There were no clouds. The sky over Vaelmoor was the hard clear black of late autumn, salted with stars the way a baker salts bread, and then—

They didn't flicker. They didn't fade. They went out the way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick. All of them. At once. As if a hand had passed over the whole bowl of heaven and snuffed it.

Tallowmarket did not scream. That surprised me, afterward. What it did was worse: it stopped. Every conversation died mid-word. A hundred faces tilted upward at the same moment, the way a field of flowers turns toward the sun, except toward nothing. Somewhere a child began to cry and was hushed—not comforted, hushed—as if noise might draw the dark down closer.

An old man near the fountain dropped to his knees and started praying to the Star-Mother, loud and ragged, and nobody laughed at him, which told me everything. In Vaelmoor, people laugh at piety the way they laugh at bad poetry. Nobody was laughing.

I looked up with the rest of them, and I did not pray. I listened.

Ten years of discipline, and in that moment it cracked like river ice in spring. I couldn't help it. The silence was too enormous; it pressed against the shut door in my head like floodwater against a dam, and I felt—for the first time in a decade—the shape of everything on the other side. Not voices. Not yet. Just the vast, aching *absence* of them, the way an empty house still holds the shape of the family that left it. The animals of Vaelmoor weren't gone. They were *muted*. Held. Something had put its hand over the mouth of the world.

And beneath that, deeper, in the bedrock of things, I felt the wards—or rather, I felt the wound where the wards had been. It's hard to describe without sounding mad. Imagine standing in a room where a wall used to be and feeling the draft. The border wards hadn't broken. Broken things leave rubble. This was a clean absence, surgical, deliberate. Something had been *taken out* of them.

Stolen. The word arrived in my head uninvited, wearing someone else's voice—a woman's voice, low and certain—and I flinched from it like from a hot stove, because I knew that voice, had spent ten years not knowing it, and it had no business speaking inside me now.

*Stolen,* it said again, gently, and then it was gone.

Around me, the market was coming apart into motion at last—people gathering children, merchants slamming shutters, a watchman blowing his whistle in short panicked bursts as if sound could hold back the dark. I packed my stall with hands that almost worked. Knot-cords, luck-beads, love-charms carved from soapstone, all of it sham, all of it safe, all of it suddenly, absurdly beside the point.

That was when the soldiers came into Tallowmarket.

* * *

They moved in pairs, lanterns held high, and they weren't looking up at the vanished sky. That was the thing that turned my dread from a cold stone into a living animal in my chest. Everyone else in the city was staring at the stars that weren't there. The soldiers were staring at *us*.

"Hear the proclamation!" the nearest one bawled, in the flat reciting voice of a man who has said it forty times tonight already. "By order of Lord-Protector Cassian Vale: the failure of the wards is an act of sabotage. The saboteurs are the gifted. All persons harboring knowledge of forbidden arts will present themselves for questioning. All citizens are bound by law to report—" he stumbled over the next word, and I watched him force it out— "*neighbors.*"

Report your neighbors. I'd known it would come to that eventually. It always does.

They started at the fountain end of the market, going stall to stall, and they had a list. An actual list, written in a clerk's careful hand, and I heard a name read out—Bren of Copperlane, hedge-charmer—and saw a potter's apprentice go gray and let himself be taken without a word. Bren. I'd bought soapstone from him. His charms had probably worked, a little. Mine never did. Mine were safe.

Mine were safe, I told myself again, and my hands shook as I folded the awning, because I could hear the soldiers' horses at the far end of the lane, and the horses were screaming. Not neighing—screaming, wordless, but I could feel the shape of what they meant pressing against the shut door: *wrong, wrong, the dark is wrong, the master smells of iron and silence, run, run, run.*

Ten years. I clamped down harder than I ever had, hard enough to taste blood in the back of my throat, and the horses' terror dimmed to a muffled knocking, and I became Kestrel the charm-seller again, small and dull and harmless, a fraud among frauds in a city losing its mind.

A lantern swung toward my stall.

"Papers," said the soldier. Young. Nervous. His partner hung back, watching the crowd with the flat patience of a man who enjoyed his work.

I gave him my papers. False name, false trade license, forged well enough to pass a bored clerk, and they had passed a hundred times before. He held the lantern close, squinting, and I arranged my face into the mild vacant patience of a man with nothing in his head but cord and beads.

"You sell charms," he said.

"Charms against bad luck, mostly." I smiled my crooked smile. "Can't promise they work. Can't promise they don't. That's charm-selling."

Something flickered in his face—almost a grin, quickly killed. His partner stepped forward, though, and took the lantern, and raised it slowly past my face, past my stall, up to the level of my eyes, and held it there.

"The Lord Protector says," the partner said quietly, "that the gifted can't help listening. To beasts. To birds. Says it's in the eyes. Says a man who talks to animals looks at them like they might answer." He smiled. It was not a friendly smile. "You've got a pigeon roosting under your awning, charmer. Been sitting there since dusk. Funny thing—every bird in this city's gone dumb and sullen tonight. But that one's been watching *you*. Like it's waiting for you to say something."

I did not look at the pigeon. Looking at the pigeon was the single most dangerous thing I could do, and so of course every nerve in my body screamed to do it.

"I sell knots," I said. "Birds aren't customers."

The young soldier laughed, uncertain. His partner didn't. He lowered the lantern, slow, keeping his eyes on mine the whole way down, the way a man keeps his eyes on a dog he suspects of having teeth.

"Curfew's in an hour," he said. "Go home, charmer. We'll be walking these streets all night. Door to door." He tapped the clerk's list against his gauntlet. "Lots of names still to check."

I packed the rest of my stall faster than I'd ever done anything in my life, and I did not run—I walked, briskly, a tired tradesman heading home—but inside, under the fraud's smile, something old and cold had woken up and was saying, in a dead woman's voice:

*They're coming for you, little bird. And this time, hiding won't be enough.*

Behind me, in the starless dark, the pigeon under my abandoned awning finally opened its beak—and made no sound at all.
