## 2. What the Rats Knew

I did not sleep. That was the first mistake, or the first honesty—I've never been able to tell the difference.

The stars stayed gone all night. I sat in my rented room above the charm shop with the shutters open like an idiot, staring up at a sky that had been picked clean, and I waited for someone to explain it. Vaelmoor is a city of explanations. We have three universities, forty temples, and at least one man on every corner willing to sell you a theory for a copper. But nobody explained the stars. Nobody even tried. By midnight the bells had stopped ringing, which frightened me more than anything else, because Vaelmoor's bells never stop ringing—not for plague, not for funerals, not even when the old king died. The bell-ringers' guild has a contract with death itself, or so they claim.

Silence, it turns out, is contagious.

The animals knew first. They always know first; that's the whole curse of my life. At dusk yesterday the market dogs had gone quiet mid-bark, like singers cut off by a conductor's fist. The gulls over the fish stalls had landed and stood in rows, facing east, saying nothing. And now, in the grey hour before dawn, I lay in bed listening to the absence of sparrows under the eaves and felt something old and buried in my chest turn over in its sleep.

*Don't,* I told it. *We agreed. Ten years ago we shook hands and signed papers. You stay down there.*

The thing in my chest—the gift, the curse, whatever you want to call the part of me that hears what animals say—did not agree. It never had. That was rather the problem.

* * *

By full light the soldiers came, as I'd known they would. Not because I'm a prophet—beast-speakers don't get prophecy, we get fleas and opinions—but because when the impossible happens, the powerful always look for someone to blame, and "the gifted" is such a convenient word. It fits neatly in a proclamation. It rhymes with *traitor*.

I watched from the charm shop's back window as a squad of them worked down Copperlane with a list and a hammer. The list, presumably, held names. The hammer was for doors that didn't open fast enough. Old Mistress Ferrow the hedge-witch went out between two soldiers with her hands tied with red cord—red cord, the official color of magical contamination, chosen by some bureaucrat who thought fear should be color-coded—and her charms stall was overturned into the gutter, where her little woven blessings soaked in filth like drowned birds.

I had sold charms beside her for six years. She used to slip me honeycakes and call me "the quiet one," which I had always taken as a compliment to my tradecraft.

My own stock sat under the counter: luck-knots, fever-wards, mouse-charms. Nothing that would survive ten minutes of honest questioning. Nothing that would survive five minutes of dishonest questioning either, and inquisitors are famously dishonest questioners.

"You're going to have to run," said Bram, the shop's owner, without looking up from his ledger. He was a fat, kind, entirely ordinary man who had hired me despite my silences and paid me fairly despite my prices, and I loved him the way you love a wall that has kept the wind off you for years.

"I haven't done anything."

"No." He wrote a number. "That's never once saved anybody."

He pressed a purse into my hand through the ledger's shadow, and I took it, and I hated myself a little for taking it, and I took it anyway. That's most of courage, in my experience: doing the sensible cowardly thing while hating it loudly inside your own head.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of leaving Vaelmoor, which any reasonable person would have done: I went to find the rats.

You need to understand what this cost me. Ten years ago I made a decision, and the decision was this: I would never listen again. Not a word, not a whisper, not a stray thought from a pigeon's small gray mind. Because listening is a door, and doors open from both sides, and there are people in this world who can smell a beast-speaker the way sharks smell blood—except worse, because at least sharks are honest about wanting to eat you.

So I had built my silence brick by brick. Ear-wax and willpower. I walked past dogs begging with their eyes and didn't answer. I stepped over dying birds and hummed loudly. Once, early on, a cat had looked at me across a market square and said, quite clearly, *you're one of ours*, and I had thrown a boot at it. The cat forgave me faster than I forgave myself, cats being generous that way.

But last night the animals had gone silent all at once, all together, all across the city, and silence like that isn't natural. Silence like that is a lid. And under a lid, there is always something breathing.

The rats were easy to find. Rats are always easy to find; they're the one constant of civilization, more reliable than coin. What was hard was standing in the reeking dark behind the tannery, closing my eyes, and letting the door swing open after ten locked years.

It hurt. Nobody tells you that. It hurt like straightening a leg that's been crooked since childhood—all the cramped, atrophied truth of it flooding back at once, pins and needles of the soul. And then the world *spoke*.

Not the rats first. First came everything else: the tannery horses' low chronic misery, the flies' idiot joy, a dog three streets off dreaming of a rabbit in colors I don't have words for. A decade of suppressed signal crashing in like a dam breaking, and I put my hand against the wall and breathed through it, and hated how good it felt. That's the shameful part. It felt like coming home to a house I'd burned down myself.

Then I narrowed the channel, the way my— the way I'd been taught long ago, by someone whose face I keep deliberately blurred, and found the rats.

Rat-mind is not like dog-mind or bird-mind. Dogs think in pictures and loyalty. Birds think in weather and grievance. Rats think in *maps*. Their minds are corridors and larders and escape routes, a great living blueprint of the city, and when I touched the nearest one—a big scarred female gnawing on something I chose not to identify—her thoughts unfolded around me like a plan of Vaelmoor drawn in hunger.

*I need to ask you something,* I said, in the way that isn't sound.

She went still. Then she turned and looked at me with the frank appraisal of a creature who has survived every trap ever set, and I felt her recognition ripple through the corridor of her mind: *Speaker. Haven't smelled one in years. Thought you were all dead or leashed.*

"Both, roughly," I said aloud, because talking helps me think, and because no one questions a woman muttering behind a tannery. "Last night. The animals went silent. All of you went silent. Why?"

And here is the thing about asking rats questions: they answer honestly, but their honesty is shaped like a rat. What poured into me wasn't words so much as *knowing*—a sensation of the city's bones, of lines running under everything, lines of power that had hummed along the borders of the realm since before anyone alive could remember. The border wards. Every child learns about them in songs: the wards that keep out the wild dark, the wards that sing.

Except the rats had always been able to hear them singing. Faintly, constantly, the way you hear your own heartbeat. And last night—

The image she gave me made my stomach drop. Not a snapped harp-string. Not a collapsed wall. A *surgery*. Something reaching into the song and lifting out its heart, clean, deliberate, the way you'd lift the yolk from an egg. The wards hadn't broken.

They'd been *robbed*.

*Stolen,* the rat confirmed, and her mind flickered with a memory-not-hers, something passed mouth to mouth down generations of vermin: vast shapes in cages, voices screaming into silence, a tower like a needle. *Taken away. The singing things. Taken to the tall place.*

"What tall place? Where?"

But already her attention was sliding sideways, because rats do not linger on terrible knowledge—it attracts cats—and instead she offered me something else, something urgent and close: *There is one who didn't go silent. One of the loud ones, the ward-beasts. It is hurt. It is hiding. It has been calling for a speaker for two days and none came, and now it is too weak to call properly, and it smells wrong, and you—you smell almost right.*

"A ward-hound." My mouth had gone dry. Ward-hounds were legend. The old order's beasts, the ones woven into the wards themselves, the ones that were supposed to have died out with the order a generation ago. "Where?"

*Follow,* she said, with the smugness of a creature holding the only map. *And bring food. Hurt things are bad company without food.*

* * *

She led me through Vaelmoor's underside—the storm-gutters and coal-chutes and forgotten alleys that rats hold in trust for the rest of us—out past the tannery walls, past the last straggle of houses, to the ruined aqueduct on the city's north edge. The soldiers hadn't come this far yet. The silence here was total, absolute, a held breath, and I realized the rat had brought me to the one place in the city quieter than everywhere else, and understood why: whatever hid here wanted to be hidden from everything, including ears like mine.

She stopped at a seam in the aqueduct's foundation, a crack barely wide enough for her, and looked back at me with an expression I can only describe as *well?*

I got down on my knees in the mud, which is a sentence that describes both my body and my life, and peered into the dark.

Two eyes looked back. Not rat-eyes. Amber, deep-set, intelligent, and full of a pain so old and so large that it made my chest ache just to meet them.

*Hello,* said the ward-hound.

I should tell you what that word did to me. Ten years of humming over dying birds, ten years of throwing boots at cats, ten years of building my silence—and the first creature to speak to me in a decade says *hello* in a voice like a struck bell wrapped in wool, and every wall I ever built comes down at once, and I am kneeling in the mud crying like a child while a wounded legend watches me with patience.

*You came,* it said. Wonderingly. *Someone came.*

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it more than I've ever meant anything. "I'm sorry it took so long. I didn't— I stopped listening. On purpose."

*Yes.* No judgment in it. Only exhaustion. *So did everyone. That is why it worked.*

"What worked?" I crawled closer. In the gloom I could see it now: a shape like a wolf if wolves were designed by someone who also designed cathedrals, silver-flanked, ribs showing, and along its spine a line of sigils that should have glowed softly, forever, and were dark. Dark like the sky. Dark like the bells.

*They reached into the song,* the ward-hound said, echoing the rat, but with the authority of one who had been *inside* the song, who had perhaps *been* the song. *They took the voices. My pack-sisters. The singers in the deep places. Took them north, to the Spire, and the wards fell silent, and the stars—" it lifted its muzzle toward the crack of daylight, toward the empty sky, "*—were only ever their reflection. Did you not know that? The stars are the wards, seen from below. When the wards die, the heavens go dark. Your astronomers will be very embarrassed.*

Despite everything, despite the mud and the tears and the terror, I laughed. A wet, broken laugh, but a laugh.

"You're hurt."

*The silence-spells,* it said. *They sweep the country now, hunting anything that still speaks. I ran ahead of them for two days. I am the last of the north pack, and I am tired, speaker, and I have been calling and calling, and you are the first ear in ten years.*

It laid its great head on its paws, and its amber eyes held mine, and it asked me nothing. That was the worst of it. It asked for nothing. It simply let me see what it was: the last voice of a murdered choir, waiting in the dark to find out whether anyone in the world still listened.

And somewhere in the city behind me, boots were going door to door with a list and a hammer, hunting exactly what I was. And somewhere north, in a tower like a needle, my mother's— *someone's* stolen legacy sang silently in cages.

The rat sat by my knee and washed her face, waiting, in the manner of her kind, to see what the strange human would do next.

"Can you walk?" I asked the hound. "Can you hide in the city? There are people—maybe people—who help things like you. Maybe things like me."

The ward-hound raised its head. And though no soldier could have heard it, though the whole silenced world couldn't have heard it, I swear the ground itself leaned in to listen as it spoke:

*They are coming for me even now, little speaker. I can smell the silence walking. So decide quickly: are you the last of the listeners—or the first of something new?*

Behind us, far off, a dog began to bark—then choked, mid-note, into nothing.

The silence was moving this way.
