## 3. The Choir in the Cistern

I have been many things in my life: court musician, smuggler of sheet music, a woman who once made a duke cry with a single held note. But I never expected to become a rescuer of stray beast-speakers, and I especially never expected to do it by yanking a stranger through a drain.

Let me explain.

We keep three exits from the cistern, and I was checking the eastern one—the one that opens behind the tanner's yard, which smells exactly as you'd imagine—when I heard the running. Not the running of a cutpurse, which is quick and light and full of self-interest. This was the running of someone who had learned, over many years, to move like a rumor: present everywhere, provable nowhere. I knew that kind of running. I'd done it myself for two years after the palace fell.

And behind it, the other kind. Boots. Measured. Patient. Inquisitors don't chase; they *arrive*, on the assumption that the world will eventually arrange itself in front of them.

I looked through the grate. A figure in a hooded coat was pressed into the shadow of the tanner's wall, chest heaving, and twenty paces down the lane walked a man in gray with a tuning fork at his belt the way other men carry a knife. I knew that fork. I had heard what it did to a singer's magic in Grayford two winters past. I had heard what it did to the singer, too.

The figure in the shadows glanced around, desperate, and I did something impulsive—which my choir will tell you is my defining flaw.

I opened the grate.

"Down here," I whispered. "Unless you'd prefer to discuss your evening with the gentleman in gray."

They looked at me. In the dark I could see very little—sharp eyes, a face trying very hard to be forgettable and failing at it, the way honest faces do. There was a long moment in which they weighed me, the drain, and the inquisitor, and I had the distinct pleasure of watching them realize all three options were terrible.

The boots turned the corner.

They dropped into the dark, and I shut the grate behind them.

* * *

The cistern under Vaelmoor is older than the city above it. The old magical order built it, back when magic and masonry were on speaking terms, and the stone still remembers how to listen. That's why we chose it. Sound behaves strangely down here—it doesn't echo so much as *agree*, every note folding into the next until the whole chamber hums like a struck bell that never quite stops. Our harmonies vanish into that hum. Our magic hides inside it, the way a smuggled letter hides inside a hymnal.

Which, incidentally, is exactly how we smuggle things. But I digress.

I took the stranger down the maintenance stair with a hand on their sleeve, and I'll admit I was assessing them the whole way. Not their face—I'd forgotten it already, which was promising. Their *walk*. Ten years of court training taught me to read bodies like scores, and this one was written in a hand I recognized: someone who had spent a decade teaching themselves not to react. Shoulders drawn in to seem smaller. Ears doing the work of eyes. And beneath all that careful nothing, a tension in the jaw that said *I am listening to something you cannot hear, and it is telling me you are dangerous.*

"You're the one the rats are talking about," I said.

They stumbled on the stair. "The rats don't talk."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "They don't. To anyone else."

* * *

I brought them to the rehearsal gallery first, because I wanted witnesses and because I wanted to see how they'd react to the choir. That's the real test, you understand. Everyone can perform tolerance. But you put a person in a room with eleven outcast magicians mid-harmony and watch their face, and you learn everything.

The choir was working on a masking round—the one we call the Gray Hymn, because it turns our working into something so bland the wards slide right over it. Old Pell was on the drone, which he insists is the most important part, which is true, and which he mentions hourly. The twins, Orla and Brann, had the counter-melody. Tam sat at the center on his stool, bare feet on the stone, reading the vibration through the floor the way other people read a page.

The stranger stopped at the edge of the gallery and went very still.

I watched their face do something complicated. It started as fear—expected. Then it moved through longing, which was more interesting, and landed somewhere in the neighborhood of grief, which told me more than any interrogation could have. You don't grieve a thing you never had. This was someone looking at a table set for a meal they'd been starving outside of for years.

"They can't hear us," I said, when the round ended. "The harmonies. That's rather the point."

"I know," the stranger said. Their voice was rough, unloved, like a door opened for the first time in a season. "I mean—I know what masking harmonies are. I've *heard* them. From the outside. The wards used to—" They stopped. Closed their mouth like a trap snapping shut.

"Used to sing," I finished. "Yes. Everyone with ears knows the wards used to sing. And then one night the stars went out and the singing stopped, and the whole city has been pretending not to notice the silence ever since. You'd know something about that, I think."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course not." I gestured them to a bench. "Sit. Would you like tea? It's terrible. Pell makes it from something he insists is mint. We suspect it is not."

That, at last, got a reaction—a flicker at the corner of their mouth, quickly suppressed. Interesting. Humor as a survival reflex. I've known that reflex intimately; it lives in the same house as denial, and they share a very thin wall.

They sat. They did not take the tea. Wise.

* * *

Sera, you'll say, why not simply ask them? Why the theater?

Because I have watched what happens when this choir asks a stranger what they are. The last time, three years ago, a hedge-witch named Corla lied to us for a month before we learned she'd been sent by the provost's office to map our tunnels. She was nineteen and terrified and she still nearly got us all hanged. So now we have a rule: we watch first, and we ask nothing, and we let the room do the asking.

The room did its work. Pell, bless his suspicious heart, planted himself across from the stranger and stared at them with the expression of a man who has never once been wrong, which is remarkable given his track record. Orla drifted past and "accidentally" let a thread of her charm-work slip loose—a little warmth-wisp, harmless, the magical equivalent of a cough in a quiet room.

Most people flinch. Even the ungifted flinch; they feel it like a change in air pressure.

The stranger didn't flinch. The stranger *answered*—a tiny, unconscious thing, a half-second of attention turned toward the wisp like a plant toward light. And I would have sworn, would have staked my tuning fork on it, that the wisp *bent toward them in return*.

Orla looked at me across the gallery. I gave her the smallest nod. She banked the wisp and drifted on, and if her heart was hammering, she hid it better than most.

"So," I said, settling beside our guest with my terrible tea. "You've been in Vaelmoor how long?"

"Six years."

"Charm-seller. The market wardens have a file on you—thin, but it exists. You pay your taxes on time. You report nothing. You have no lovers, no drinking companions, no debts, and no history before six years ago." I sipped. "You are, by every measure the city keeps, a perfectly boring person. And last night the stars went out, and tonight an inquisitor with a Dravenmoor fork walked you through the tanner's district like a man following a scent. Forgive me if 'perfectly boring' has stopped convincing me."

At the name *Dravenmoor*, the stranger's teacup-steady composure cracked. Just a hairline. Just enough.

"I don't know what that is," they said.

"Then it's a funny coincidence," I said, "that you flinched at it."

* * *

Tam ended the argument before it became an argument, which is his gift and his tyranny.

The boy crossed the gallery with that unhurried certainty of his, planted himself in front of our guest, and held out his hand—palm up, the way he asks. He can't hear a word we say, and he has never once let that make him polite. He reads the floor, the air, the bones of the room. He reads *you*, if you stand still long enough, and God help you, because he is eleven and merciless.

The stranger looked at me. I shrugged: *your funeral.*

They took Tam's hand.

I watched the boy's fingers spread against their wrist—not their palm, their *wrist*, over the pulse—and his head tilt, the way it does when he's found something in the vibration that the rest of us need instruments to detect. His brow furrowed. Then his whole face changed, the way it does when he reads a chord he's never heard before, and he let go, stepped back, and signed something rapid and furious.

"Slowly," Orla said, translating. "What do you mean, *the book*?"

Tam signed again, emphatic, stamping his foot for emphasis so the floor carried it.

"He says," Orla said slowly, "that they sound like the book. The old hymnal. The one with the name in it." She looked at me, and then at our guest, and her voice went careful. "He says their voice is in the book. Under the name Dravenmoor."

The silence that followed was the loudest we'd had in the cistern in a year.

The stranger stood up. "I don't know what he felt," they said, and their rough door-of-a-voice had gone thin, "but my name isn't Dravenmoor, and I don't have a voice in anyone's book, and I would very much like to leave now."

"Through the tanner's yard?" I asked mildly. "Where your gray friend is no doubt still walking his patient rounds? Sit down, stranger. Nobody here is going to make you be anyone. We've all spent enough years not being someone to respect the craft." I set down the tea. "But I'll tell you what I told the others when they came down that stair, and what I'll tell you: we don't want what you are. We want what you *know*. There's a difference, and it matters, and you're welcome to figure out which side of it you're on at your own pace."

They didn't sit. But they didn't leave, either. They stood there in the hum of the old stone with Tam watching them like a hawk watching a field mouse that might, against all odds, turn out to be another hawk, and I could see the war behind their face: the decade of invisibility on one side, and on the other, the thing they'd felt in Orla's little wisp, the thing the rats had apparently been chattering about all over Vaelmoor's undercity.

"The ward-hound," they said at last, quietly. "You've heard about the ward-hound."

The choir went still. Even Pell stopped glaring.

"Found it in the Warrens," our guest went on, as if talking to the floor rather than to us. "Wounded. It spoke to me. First thing that's spoken to me in ten years." A breath. "It said the wards didn't break. They were *emptied*. Something was taken out of them. Taken *north*, to the Spire, and it's still—"

They stopped. Looked up. And for the first time since I'd hauled them out of the rain-dark, they looked directly at me, and there was nothing careful or forgettable in their face at all.

"You have a choir," they said. "You have harmonies that can hide magic, and a tunnel under a city that's about to start burning the gifted out of their beds. And you've been hiding." Their jaw tightened. "How long are you planning to keep singing under the floor?"

I looked around my gallery—at Pell, at Orla and Brann, at little Tam with his fists still half-clenched, at the stone that remembered the old order's music better than any of us did.

And I thought: *there it is.* The thing I'd been watching for. Not a stray. A key, walking around in a coat, asking to be turned.

"Come with me," I said, and led them toward the deep gallery, where the old hymnal sits chained to its lectern like the dangerous thing it is. "There's a name in a book I want you to look at. And then, stranger, you and I are going to have a much longer conversation about the Spire."
