## 4. A Name in an Old Hymnal

I am the only person in the cistern who has never heard the choir sing.

I want that written down somewhere, because everyone down here seems to forget it. They hum at me. They mouth things and expect me to catch them on the rebound of their breath. Sera Quill, who can conduct forty voices through a collapsing tunnel with nothing but her eyebrows, still talks to me like I'm a jar she's trying to open — lots of exaggerated lips, no actual information.

But I feel everything. That's the part they forget. Sound is just the world shivering, and I have my hands pressed against its bones all day long. The drip of the cistern is a slow heartbeat. The choir practicing two galleries over comes up through the stone floor into my bare feet like weather. When Kestrel Dravenmoor arrived last night, soaked and silent and flinching from every lantern, I felt them before anyone saw them — a wrongness in the floor, a tremor where no tremor should be, like a string someone had plucked and then tried to hold still with both hands.

That's how I knew, before Sera ever opened the hymnal, that the book was about them.

* * *

The hymnal lives in an iron box under Sera's cot, which is itself in a niche behind a curtain of hanging chains, because Sera Quill trusts no one, including me, including herself, especially the dark. She brought it out this morning after the others had gone to the singing-gallery, and she set it on the crate between us like it was something that might bite.

It was old. Older than old. The cover was leather gone the color of dried blood, and tooled into it was a tree with roots that went down and down until they became letters, and the letters spelled DRAVENMOOR.

Sera didn't say anything. She just turned the book toward me and watched my face, which is her one reliable method of talking to me: she watches what I look at, and she waits.

Kestrel looked at the name for maybe three seconds. Then they looked away, hard, the way you look away from a wound you've decided not to have.

"That's not mine," they said. "I don't— there's no Dravenmoor. There's no family. There's just me, and I'm nobody, and I'd like to keep being nobody."

I felt that sentence come out of them. Everyone thinks I miss sound. What I actually miss is almost nothing, because most of what people call sound is just noise wearing a costume. But I can feel a lie. A lie has a different weight than truth. Truth rolls off a person like water off a duck; it doesn't cost them anything to say it. A lie costs them. You can feel them paying for it — a tightening, a bracing, a small cold contraction somewhere in the chest that travels down the arms and out through the fingertips if they're touching anything, which Kestrel was, because they were touching the table, and the table told me everything.

They were lying. They were lying so hard the wood grain shivered.

"They're lying," I said, because I am apparently the only honest person in this entire underground kingdom.

Sera looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *finally*. Then she looked at Kestrel, who looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *how dare you*, which, honestly, fair. But I've been called worse by better.

"I'm not—" Kestrel started, and stopped, and started again. "My mother died when I was small. I don't remember her. I remember a house that wasn't ours, and a man who wasn't my father, and after that I remember ten years of making myself small enough that no one would ever ask questions."

"You made yourself small," Sera said, "by burying a gift strong enough to make ward-hounds weep."

"By burying *myself*."

And here is the thing about Kestrel Dravenmoor that I understood immediately, sitting there with my hands flat on the crate while the two of them circled each other like cats deciding whether to fight or mate: they are not hiding from the inquisitors. The inquisitors are a problem, sure. Vess is out there with his tuning fork, and the fork is real, and I felt it once through a messenger's boot when he walked past the market district, and it is the ugliest vibration I have ever felt in my life — a dead note, a hole shaped like sound, a thing that eats rhythm. I hate it more than I hate almost anything.

But Kestrel isn't hiding from Vess. Kestrel is hiding from the book.

* * *

Sera opened it anyway. Of course she did. She's Sera.

The pages were vellum, soft as moth wing, and the ink had gone brown at the edges but the notation was still legible if you knew what you were looking at, and Sera knew, because Sera knows everything about music except how to say goodbye to the court that exiled her. She read aloud, slowly, translating as she went, and I followed along the way I always do — not the words, but the shape of her voice in her throat, the rhythm of her breathing between phrases, the little accelerandos of excitement she can't control when she finds something that matters.

This mattered.

"Dravenmoor," she read, "*generals of the Choir Militant, keepers of the Border Song, whose voices held the wards of the north for nine generations.*"

Nine generations. I put my palm flat on the crate and let the number sink through the wood into my bones. Nine generations of people whose job was to be loud on purpose, loud in the way that holds up the sky, and Kestrel had spent ten years teaching themselves to be quiet on purpose, quiet in the way that lets the sky fall.

There were names in the margins. Little annotations, hand after hand after hand, corrections and harmonies and warnings. One margin note, near the front, in handwriting much fancier than the rest, said: *The gift passes true. Do not let them unlearn it. Unlearning is the first death.*

Kestrel read that line and went very still, and I felt the stillness — felt it the way you feel a door close. Something in them locked.

"My mother," they said finally, and the word came out of them like a splinter coming out sideways. "You think my mother wrote that."

"I think your mother *was* that," Sera said. "General Alarice Dravenmoor. Last of the line. She held the northern wards for eleven years, and then the man who served under her — the usurper, the one whose soldiers are searching door to door right now — took the wards apart from inside and sold every voice in them." She closed the book gently, the way you close a book you intend to reopen. "Your mother's voice was the first one he stole."

I felt Kestrel's hands on the table go from tight to trembling. Not grief exactly. Grief I know; half this choir runs on grief; grief is our cheapest fuel. This was something else. This was the specific horror of finding out that the thing you buried alive had a name, a history, a uniform, a war — that it wasn't a deformity you'd been hiding, it was a *post* you'd deserted.

Desertion. That word vibrated off them so strongly I could read it through the floor.

"I never asked to be any of this," Kestrel said.

"No," Sera said. "Nobody asks. That's what makes it a calling instead of a hobby."

* * *

Afterward, when Sera had put the book back in its iron coffin and gone to argue with the cook about lentils, Kestrel sat alone in the side gallery for a long time. I sat near them, not close — they weren't ready for close — but near, the way you sit near a fire you're afraid of.

They talked to themselves. Not out loud, mostly. Beast-speakers do a thing where they talk in the space between words, a kind of pre-verbal muttering, and I can feel it in their jaw and their throat and the floor under their boots. It sounds — feels — like animal speech. Like the ward-hound, probably. Like the rats. A language made of pressure and intent instead of grammar.

And here is what I noticed, the thing I said later that cracked everything open, the thing that made Kestrel look at me like I'd grown teeth:

Their voice sounded like the book.

I don't mean metaphorically. I mean mechanically. The hymnal's notation isn't normal notation — I've felt Sera trace the staves, and they're not five lines, they're seven, with a third axis marked in a script nobody uses anymore. Sera says it records *weight*, not pitch. How much force a voice puts into the world. The old generals didn't sing melodies; they sang masses. Their songs were measured in pounds.

When Kestrel mutters beast-speech, even the tiny buried domestic version of it, the version they've spent a decade starving — the weight signature matches the book. Sera confirmed it later, humming fragments against the notation, and I felt her confirmation travel through the bench we shared: match, match, match. Same mass. Same distribution. Same strange habit of putting force *downward*, into the ground, root-first, like the tree on the cover.

So I said it. In my terrible spoken voice, the one that comes out flat and over-loud and slightly wrong-shaped, the one the hearing people always wince at, the one I use anyway because sometimes the truth needs to be ugly:

"Your voice sounds like the book."

Kestrel flinched. Then they stared at me. Then they did the thing I will remember for the rest of my life, which is: they listened. Actually listened. To me. The deaf boy. They leaned in and watched my mouth and read my face and did not once treat my voice like a malfunction, and I think — I think that mattered to them as much as what I said. Maybe more. Because here was another person whose voice came out wrong-shaped, and here was Kestrel, whose whole tragedy is a voice that comes out wrong-shaped for the world it was born into, and for one moment we were the same kind of broken, standing on the same crack in the same floor.

"It's not possible," they said. "I never trained. I never learned any of this."

"The book says the gift passes true," I said. "Unlearning is the first death. Which means learning happened before you were born. Somebody sang into you before you could walk." I tapped the floor twice, so they'd feel it. "Somebody left it in you the way parents leave things in attics. You didn't desert anything, Kestrel. You inherited it. Deserting implies you had a choice about enlisting."

They were quiet for a long time after that. I felt the quiet change quality three times — denial, then grief, then something I don't have a word for yet, something that felt like a load-bearing wall being taken out of a house and the house, impossibly, standing.

Then, near evening, they started humming.

Just a fragment. Four notes. The opening phrase of the Border Song, the one printed on the hymnal's first page, the one Sera had shown them hours ago. They claimed they couldn't remember it, couldn't have reproduced it, that they'd only glanced. But I felt those four notes come up through the gallery floor, and I felt their weight, and I compared it against the memory of Sera tracing the notation, and it matched. Root-first. Downward. Pounds, not pitch.

They remembered it because it had always been in them. Because it was theirs before it was anyone's.

I tapped the rhythm back at them through the bench — four notes, answered, four notes returned — and Kestrel looked at me like I'd handed them something they'd been digging for in the wrong yard for ten years.

* * *

That night, the choir gathered in the main gallery for the evening harmony, the ritual that masks our magic and keeps us hidden. Forty voices. Sera conducting. And Kestrel stood at the edge of it, outside the circle, watching, and I stood next to them, and together we felt the song come up through the stone.

Halfway through, without planning it, without telling anyone, Kestrel added a fifth note. A low one. A root-note. It dropped through the floor like a foundation stone sliding into place, and I felt the whole choir's harmony tighten around it, the way a roof tightens when you finally build the wall it was waiting for. Nobody saw them do it. But everybody *felt* it, and afterward Sera turned around mid-conduct and looked straight at them, and her eyebrows said what her mouth wouldn't: *you cannot hide in a choir, Kestrel Dravenmoor. That is the entire point of choirs.*

And then, an hour past midnight, when the cistern was asleep and the drips had slowed to a funeral pace, I felt something else through the floor. Footsteps above us. Many. Heavy boots, moving in the pattern the scouts call the search-cadence — door to door, street to street, closing like a laced corset. And underneath the boots, faint but unmistakable, the dead-hole vibration of Vess's tuning fork, humming to itself in its case like a wasp in a jar.

It was heading toward the well-shaft. Toward us.

I woke Sera the only way I wake anyone: I stomped. Twice, hard, the alarm-rhythm, the one every singer in this cistern knows in their spine if not their ears. Within a minute the whole choir was awake and moving, and Kestrel was standing in the middle of it holding the hymnal's iron box like it was a shield, and I heard — felt — them say something I don't think they'd have said yesterday:

"If the book is mine, then so is the debt in it."

Above us, the boots kept coming.
