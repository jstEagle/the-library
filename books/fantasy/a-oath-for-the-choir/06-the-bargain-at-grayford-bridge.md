## 6. The Bargain at Grayford Bridge

I have led this choir for eleven years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things about survival: never sing the same harmony twice in a row, never trust a man who whistles while he works, and never, under any circumstances, let the group vote on anything after midnight. Groups are reasonable at noon. At midnight they are a pack of frightened animals wearing robes.

It was well past midnight when we gathered beneath Grayford Bridge, in the old toll-house with its leaking roof and its one stubborn candle, to decide whether to hand Kestrel Dravenmoor to the inquisitors.

I want it on the record that I did not call the meeting. Merrin did. Merrin, our tenor, who cries at sad songs and once fainted during a recitative, had found his spine somewhere between Vaelmoor and here, and he was not going to waste it. He stood at the head of the table with his arms crossed like a man auditioning for the role of Brutus.

"The inquisitor's terms are clear," he said. "They give us back Odile and Brannoc. In exchange, we give them the beast-speaker."

"Alive," said Wren, our alto, who kept accounts for us the way other people keep grudges—meticulously, and with footnotes. "The terms specify alive. Which means they want to *use* her. Or him. Or whatever Kestrel is; I still don't know and I've stopped pretending it matters."

"It matters to the price," said Merrin. "A live Dravenmoor is worth two singers. A dead one is worth nothing."

"A dead one is worth nothing to *them*," I said, "and everything to us, because if they kill Kestrel we will spend the rest of our short lives knowing we paid for our freedom with someone else's throat."

Wren looked at me over her ledger. "You say that like it's a bad thing."

I liked Wren enormously, but there were moments—I confess this now, in what I intend to be an honest account—when I understood why the court had let me go and kept her. She had a gift for making cruelty sound like arithmetic. "Two of ours," she said, tapping the ledger, "for one of theirs. That's not cruelty. That's exchange rates."

"Theirs," Merrin pointed out, "is the only person in this room who can hear what the rats are saying. The only person who knew the wards were dying before the stars went out. The only person who can lead us anywhere worth going."

"And the only person," added Tam, in the way he communicated everything—through the floor, through the table, through the small drum he carried everywhere, tapping rhythms into wood so the rest of us could feel what he felt—"who has ever made me feel like my magic wasn't a defect." Tam was fourteen and deaf and could read vibration the way literate people read ink. When he tapped, we all listened, because whatever he felt through his palms was usually truer than anything said out loud. He tapped twice: slow, deliberate. It meant *you are all being cowards.* He had a limited vocabulary of taps but he used each one with surgical precision.

Merrin flushed. "That's not fair."

"No," agreed Tam, tapping again. "*It's accurate.*"

Kestrel, meanwhile, was not in the room.

This is the detail I keep returning to, the one I'll be turning over for years: Kestrel was not in the room. They had gone up the ladder an hour before, ostensibly to check the drainage grate, and had not come down. And every person at that table knew—or suspected, or refused to suspect—that the grate above the toll-house carried sound the way a river carries leaves. We were debating a person's life within earshot of that person, and nobody moved to stop.

I tell myself we didn't notice. I tell myself a great many things.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me, since apparently I'm the one telling this story: I was a court musician for nine years. I sang for the old king, back when magic was taxed instead of hunted, and I learned that power always prefers its music soft. A quiet song keeps the powerful comfortable. A loud one makes them reach for their swords. So when the usurper came—when the wards fell and the stars went out and Halvard Vess began his door-to-door catechism of terror—I did the thing I'd been trained to do. I went quiet. I gathered the quiet ones. We sang in cisterns where no one could hear us, harmonies layered so precisely that our magic dissolved into the noise of our own making, invisible as breath on glass.

For eleven years, invisibility worked. Then Kestrel fell into our laps—literally, out of a drain—and everything got loud again.

I should have resented it. Some part of me did. But mostly I recognized something: Kestrel hid the way I hid, buried the way I buried, and watching them was like watching myself from eleven years ago refuse, night after night, to sing the true note. You can live that way. People do it all the time. But it's not living; it's withholding, and eventually the withheld thing starts to rot.

So when the argument turned—as arguments in our choir always eventually turned—to *what do we actually owe each other*, I found I already knew my answer. I just hadn't said it out loud yet.

* * *

The debate wore on until the candle guttered. Wren laid out scenarios the way she laid out everything, in tidy columns. Surrender Kestrel: we get Odile and Brannoc back, we go dark, we survive. Refuse: Vess burns the safehouses one by one, starting, probably, with this one. There was a third column she wrote and then scratched out so hard the paper tore. I saw enough of it to read the first word. *Fight.*

"We're singers," Merrin said finally, wearily. "Not soldiers. What does a choir do against the Silent Spire?"

"Ask the walls of Jericho," said Wren, dry as chalk, and then immediately wished she hadn't, because it put ideas in the room, and ideas in a midnight room are dangerous livestock.

Tam tapped something long and complicated. I felt it through the bench: a rising figure, a struggle, a resolution. He was playing us the shape of the argument itself. Fourteen years old, and conducting our consciences with a stick.

And then the ladder creaked.

Everyone froze. Everyone had known, on some level, whose footsteps those were, and everyone pretended otherwise right up until Kestrel's boots touched the floor and there they were, standing at the edge of the candlelight with drain-water on their sleeves and an expression I can only describe as the look of a person who has heard themselves discussed as currency and has decided to stop being polite about it.

"I'm going to save everyone some time," Kestrel said. "Because you're all wrong, and the vote you're working up to is worse than useless—it's a lie you'll tell yourselves so you can sleep."

Merrin started to object. Kestrel talked over him, which was unlike them, which told me everything about how long they'd been standing up there composing.

"You want to trade me for Odile and Brannoc. Fine. Do the arithmetic honestly. Vess takes me to the Spire. He doesn't release your singers—he trades them for the *next* outcast choir, and the next, because I'm not a ransom, I'm a revenue stream. The moment the usurper learns a living Dravenmoor exists, every gifted person in every city becomes leverage against every other. You won't be buying two lives. You'll be selling the principle that outcasts don't sell each other. And that principle is the only reason any of us are still alive."

Silence. The good kind, the kind that means the truth has landed and everyone is too busy feeling it to speak.

Then Kestrel did the thing I will remember on my deathbed. They sat down at the table—the fugitive, the accused, the person we'd been pricing like a bolt of cloth—and asked, quietly, "What were the terms? Exactly?"

Wren read them out. Alive. Unharmed. Delivered to Vess personally, at Grayford Bridge, by midsummer's eve.

"Midsummer," Kestrel repeated. "That's nineteen days. The Spire is twelve days north if the silence-spells don't slow us." They looked around the table, and something in their face shifted—the careful, flattened neutrality of a decade cracking like ice in spring. "So here's my counteroffer. Don't deliver me to Vess. Deliver me to the Spire. I know things about that place. I know what's inside the vault, because I know what the wards were *made of*—voices, stolen voices, caged creatures singing against their will until the whole border went dark. Your singers aren't in a dungeon. They're in the same vault. Everything Vess has taken is in there. Every voice. Every creature. Everything the stars used to hear."

"That's suicide," said Merrin.

"So is staying," said Kestrel. "Vess knows my name. He carries a tuning fork forged from a Dravenmoor blade—he wanted me to know that, he made sure of it. There is no version of my life from tonight forward in which I am anonymous. I spent ten years buying invisibility and it lasted one evening past the stars going out. Hiding is over. The only question left is whether I get taken or whether I *go*." They paused, and their voice dropped into something rawer. "I'd rather walk in than be carried."

Tam raised his drumstick and tapped four quick beats. I translated for the room, because I'd learned his grammar by then: *I'm coming with you.*

Wren stared at her ledger as though it had betrayed her. "You understand we'd be marching twelve days into the teeth of the usurper's own fortress. With what army? Thirty singers and a boy with a drum?"

"With a choir," Kestrel said. "Which is not the same thing, and might be better. Walls fall to rams. Vaults full of voices fall to voices. If the ward was built on song, it can be unmade by song—that's not hope, that's engineering. And none of us can be silenced the way a soldier can be killed, because our weapon is the thing they're trying to steal. They can't take what we're actively using."

"They can kill us," said Wren.

"Yes," said Kestrel, simply, terribly. "They can. But they're going to kill us anyway, one safehouse at a time, one singer at a time, forever, unless someone breaks the machine. I'm asking you to break it with me. Not for me. For Odile and Brannoc, who are sitting in that vault listening to stolen voices scream. For every creature in a cage between here and the Spire. For the stars." Their voice caught—actually caught, audibly, the first crack in ten years of practiced flatness. "I heard the ward-hound ask for help. I walked away. I'm done walking away."

Nobody spoke. The candle spat. Somewhere above us, the river ran on under the bridge, indifferent, ancient, the only thing in Grayford that had never once been afraid.

And then Merrin—the man who cried at recitatives, the man who had called this very meeting to surrender Kestrel—stood up and said, "Then we don't hand you over. We march you north." His voice shook. "We were wrong. Not about the danger. About the arithmetic. Some things don't fit in columns."

Wren closed her ledger. Slowly. Like a woman shutting a door on a house she'd lived in too long. "I'll want this in writing," she said, "that I objected," and then, quieter, almost to herself: "and that I was outvoted by my own conscience."

Tam tapped the rising figure again—the struggle, the resolution—but this time he added a fifth beat at the end, a new one, one I hadn't felt before. Joy, maybe. Or fear shaped like joy. At fourteen, the difference is mostly academic.

* * *

We voted at dawn, which violated my third rule of survival and proved it wrong, or perhaps proved that rules are for choirs that plan to survive rather than choirs that plan to matter. Twenty-eight for the Spire. Two abstentions. None against. Even the abstainers gave us their rations.

I spent the morning writing out the route in cipher and burning my last court songs—one cannot march on the usurper carrying compositions written for his predecessor's dinner parties; it felt too much like bringing old love letters to a new war. Kestrel watched me feed the pages to the stove and said nothing, but I caught them glancing at the flames with an expression I recognized. It was the look of someone watching their own hiding burn.

By noon we were on the north road, thirty-one strong, walking straight toward the place that had eaten our friends and swallowed the sky. Farmers watched us pass and did not wave. The fields were silent—not quiet, *silent*, the silence-spells had seen to that, hedges and henhouses alike holding their tongues—and I thought, absurdly, of the cisterns. Of eleven years singing where nothing could hear us.

Now we would sing where everything could.

On the second day out, at dusk, Kestrel stopped on the road, went very still, and held up one hand. "Something's speaking," they said. "North of here. Something that remembers my mother."

And we marched faster.
