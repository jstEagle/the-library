## 7. Road of Silent Flocks

The first thing you learn leading a choir north is that singers complain more than soldiers ever did, and I once sold charms to a garrison.

We were nine days out of Vaelmoor when the silence caught up with us. Not the ordinary silence of empty fields—this was a *worked* silence, a silence with edges. You could feel it the way you feel a draft under a door. The hedgerows still stood. The rooks still sat in them, a whole parliament of them, black and patient. But when Sera hummed three notes of the walking song, not one bird so much as ruffled.

"They're not gone," I said, because someone had to say it and the others were all looking at me like I was the one holding the map, which, unfortunately, I was. "They're *muzzled*. There's a difference. A dead field is quiet like a finished conversation. This is quiet like a hand over a mouth."

"Cheerful," said Bren, who carried the big drum and the bigger opinions. "Remind me why we brought the rat-charmer again?"

"Because," said Sera, without turning around, "the rat-charmer is the only one of us who can tell the difference between a dead field and a muzzled one. Walk on."

I did not tell her that being needed felt like a coat that almost fit. I'd spent ten years making sure nothing fit me. But the road north didn't care about my preferences, and neither, apparently, did the choir.

* * *

We made camp that night in a stone barn whose roof had half given up, and I did the thing I had sworn off doing for a decade: I opened the gift.

Not all the way. A crack. A window with the latch thrown. I let the listening spill out of me like water from a tipped cup, and what came back made my teeth ache. The silence wasn't empty. It was *loud*, if that makes any sense—full of creatures straining against it. A vixen two fields over, pressing her nose against the spell like a child against glass. A horse in a distant stable dreaming hard enough that the dream leaked through in colors instead of words. And everywhere, underneath, the silence itself humming a single flat note, the way a struck bell keeps ringing after you've grabbed it.

The usurper's spells weren't walls. They were *held* notes. And held notes, I knew from a lifetime of eavesdropping on things that don't talk, always crack eventually.

"You're crying," said Tam.

I hadn't heard him sit down. I never did; the boy moved like spilled mercury. He was watching my face with his head tilted, reading me the way he read everything—through the soles of his feet and the bones of his skull, through vibration. Deaf since birth, Sera said, and the gift had answered by giving him the world in rhythm instead of sound. He'd told me once that my voice "sounded like the book." I still didn't know what to do with that.

"Something like that," I said.

He sat beside me and put his palm flat on the barn floor. I put mine next to it. And because he couldn't hear the silence, I tried to give it to him another way—I tapped it out. The flat held note of the spell, the strain of the vixen, the leak of the horse's dream. Tap. Tap-tap. A long press of my hand against stone.

Tam watched my fingers for a while. Then he did something I didn't expect. He tapped back—but wrong. Deliberately wrong. He shifted the rhythm a half-beat, made it a syncopation, an argument instead of an echo.

I stared at him.

He grinned, gap-toothed and ferocious, and tapped it again. And I understood, slowly, the way dawn comes—not all at once but all at once—a held note can be broken not by shouting against it, but by singing *off* it. Counter-rhythm. Dissonance. The thing every choir in the world fears and every good one learns to use.

"Teach me that," I said.

He tapped his chest: *you already know it.* Then he pointed at me, then at the others sleeping in their bedrolls, then made a walking motion with two fingers: *teach them.*

I looked at the choir—Sera with her silver-streaked hair loose, Bren drooling on his drum, the Vell twins curled together like commas—and felt something I hadn't felt since I was a child watching my mother's world burn from a hayloft: responsibility. It sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

"Fine," I told Tam. "But if they're terrible, that's on you."

* * *

They were terrible.

Beast-speech, I should explain, is not a language the way Vaelmoor trade-tongue is a language. It's closer to weather. You don't say *fox*; you say the shape of fox-ness, the quick low slink of it, the hunger folded into patience. Human throats are badly built for it. Ours is a bell made for bells, and beast-speech is wind through grass.

But the choir had one thing going for them: they already knew how to listen with their whole bodies. That's what harmonizing is, when you strip the romance off it—listening so hard you stop being separately yourself.

So we started with rhythm, because that was Tam's country. He'd stand at the center of the barnyard at dawn and stomp a pattern into the frozen mud, and the choir would answer it with their feet, their palms, their humming chests. Then I'd lay the beast-shape over the top: the vixen's lean, the rook's flat suspicion, the horse's dream-leak. And slowly—grindingly, with Bren threatening to mutiny on the third morning and the Vell twins laughing at him in unison, which was somehow worse than arguing—something started to happen.

On the sixth day, Marta Vell got the rook right.

She didn't get it *perfectly*. She got it the way a foreigner gets a greeting—accent thick, grammar mangled, but the meaning arriving whole. And out in the hedgerow, one rook—one—cocked its head, and its beak parted, and what came out wasn't a word. It was a *feeling*, a thin gray thread of attention that hooked into all of us at once: *heard. Heard. Who is speaking?*

Marta burst into tears. Bren sat down in the mud. Sera looked at me across the yard with an expression I'd learn later was the closest she came to weeping, which was a slight wet brightening of the eyes and a jaw set like a fortress gate.

"It heard us," she said. "Ten years of the silence and something *heard* us."

"Don't celebrate yet," I said, because I could feel the flat note of the spell tightening, the way a fist tightens before it strikes. "We just poked it. It's going to poke back."

I was right. I'm usually right about unpleasant things. It's my least favorite talent.

* * *

The poke came two nights later, and it came wearing a uniform.

We'd crossed into the hill country by then, following the old drover roads where the wards had been thickest and the silence, consequently, was thickest too—a silence you had to push through, like wading a river. We were singing low, Tam's counter-rhythm underneath us, keeping the muzzled creatures company more than anything, when Bren's drum hand went up. His whole body went up, really, the way he did when trouble was near; he was a large man with a small man's instincts.

Lanterns on the ridge. Six of them. And between them, moving with the terrible unhurried confidence of someone who has never once been told no, a figure I recognized from Sera's descriptions and my own nightmares: gray coat, gray gloves, and at his belt, hanging like a little executioner's ornament, a tuning fork.

Halvard Vess. Or his shadow, at this distance. Either way, my stomach dropped through the hill and kept going.

"Off the road," Sera breathed. "Down. Now."

We went down, into a dry culvert choked with bracken, and lay there like corpses while the lanterns swung closer. And here is what I learned about fear: it makes the gift leak. I couldn't have shut the listening if I'd tried, and what poured in was the patrol's horses—muzzled like everything else, but horses are horses, and beneath the spell they were *screaming*. They didn't want to walk toward us. Something in their bones knew what we were, the way dogs know storms.

And beneath the horses, faint and cold, the tuning fork. Even silent it had a voice. It hummed that same flat note the whole countryside hummed, only *concentrated*, the way a river is water and the sea is also water but you don't swim in both the same way.

Vess stopped at the top of the ridge. I felt him do it—the horses' reluctance pooling against an invisible hand, the fork's note sharpening. He stood there for the length of ten heartbeats, and I swear on my mother's unmarked grave that he was listening. Not with ears. With the fork. Casting it out like a fisherman testing water.

Tam's hand found my wrist. He pressed a rhythm into my pulse: *steady, steady, steady.* Matching my heartbeat, slowing it, claiming it. Around me the choir did the same thing without being told—nine bodies going still and low and *rhythmic*, breathing together, hearts finding each other until we weren't nine heartbeats but one, buried under the bracken, indistinguishable from the hill itself.

The lanterns turned away.

We didn't move for an hour. When we finally crawled out, Sera looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us said the obvious thing, which was: *he knows we're here. He's herding us.* Because the alternative to that thought was worse: *he's letting us run north, straight to the Spire, because the Spire is where he wants us.*

"He's not chasing us," Bren said anyway, because Bren has never met an obvious thing he wouldn't say. "He's *walking* us."

"Then we walk faster," said Sera, "and we walk louder."

* * *

We crossed the Graywater on the ninth day, on a bridge the old order had built back when magic was a public utility and not a death sentence, and on the far bank the land changed.

Not gradually. All at once, like stepping through a door. The silence on the near side had been a held note. The silence on the far side was a *vacuum*. No rooks. No straining vixens. Not even the hum of the spell—just nothing, a countryside with the sound scooped out of it, where our own footsteps arrived at our ears wrong, too small, apologetic.

And the flocks. That's what I'll never be able to explain properly. The flocks were still there. Sheep in the fields, standing in perfect, terrible order, facing the same direction. Geese overhead in formation, flying north, wings beating in absolute unison, silent as paper cutouts. Being *driven*. Everything on this side of the river was being driven toward the Spire like weather toward a mountain, and none of it could say so.

Tam stood at the bridge's end with mud to his knees and his face turned up to the geese, and his hands shook. I put my arm around him and felt him reading the ground, the air, the vibration of a world with nothing left in it to answer him.

Then he stamped. Once. Hard.

And far off—miles off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of everything—a stamp answered. One beat. Then another. Then, faint and ragged and impossibly brave, a rhythm: creatures we couldn't see, muzzled we couldn't measure, signaling from somewhere ahead.

*We hear you. We hear you. Come.*

Sera looked at me. The choir looked at me. And I realized, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that they weren't looking at me for permission. They were looking at me for *direction*. Somewhere between Vaelmoor and this bridge, between a crack in a barn wall and a rook saying *heard*, I had stopped being the rat-charmer they'd dragged along and become the thing at the front.

Leader. Me. The woman who had spent ten years practicing the art of being overlooked until she could do it in her sleep.

"The Spire's three days north," I heard myself say, and my voice came out steady, which felt like a lie told by someone else's mouth. "The silence is worse ahead, and it's going to get worse still. Vess is letting us come. Everything on this side of the river is being herded toward that tower, and I don't think we're the exception." I looked at their faces—Sera's fortress jaw, Bren's fear wearing anger as a coat, the Vell twins holding hands, Tam with his chin up and his eyes wet. "But something in there is calling to us. And I say we answer."

I put my hand flat on the bridge stone, the old order's stone, worn smooth by a century of feet. And underneath the vacuum, underneath the nothing, I felt it—the faintest thread of the vixen's strain, the horse's dream, the rook's *heard*. Not gone. *Held*. All of it held, somewhere ahead, in a vault at the top of a white tower, waiting for a key.

The choir formed up behind me. Nine voices. One rhythm.

We walked north into the silence, and the silence, for the first time, walked back.
