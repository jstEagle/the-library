## 8. The Usurper's Garden

The Silent Spire did not look like a prison. That was the first obscenity of it.

It rose out of the northern moor like a candle someone had forgotten to blow out—white stone, slender, almost delicate, its peak lost in a haze that might have been cloud and might have been something worse. Around its base sprawled the usurper's garden: terraces of silver-leafed trees, hedges clipped into obedience, fountains that played without sound because there was no sound left in this country to spare. It was beautiful the way a taxidermy fox is beautiful. Everything correct. Nothing alive.

We lay on our bellies in the heather above it, all ten of us, and I felt Tam's small hand find my sleeve. He couldn't hear the silence, of course. He'd never had to lose anything to know it was gone. He just squeezed once—the rhythm we'd agreed on—and pointed at the garden wall, where a procession of servants was carrying covered cages through a gate of black iron.

Cages. Covered, like you'd cover a songbird so it would sleep and not sing itself hoarse.

"They're moving them," Sera breathed beside me. "Tonight. Before we can—"

"Before anyone can," I said. "He's not hiding them anymore. He's arranging them."

Because that's what it looked like from up here. The garden wasn't decoration. It was a display case. And somewhere inside it, behind glass or gold or whatever the usurper used to keep a voice from flying home, were the vixen's strain and the horse's dream and every other stolen thing between the borders and the sky.

I should have been afraid. I had spent ten years being very good at afraid; it was practically a trade skill, right alongside charm-selling and lying to landlords. But what I felt lying in that heather wasn't fear. It was something older and stupider, with teeth.

It wanted the wall down. It wanted the gate open. It wanted, more than it had ever wanted anything, to be let off its leash.

* * *

Getting in was insultingly easy, which is how I knew it was a trap, and went anyway.

The choir made noise—a real performance, Sera conducting nine outcasts through one of the hymnal's masking harmonies at the edge of the terraces, loud enough and strange enough to pull the guards' attention east while I went over the wall on the west side where the ivy grew thick as rope. The silence-spells didn't touch their singing; that was the whole point of the choir, magic folded inside music until it read as nothing but a tune. From the wall I heard them for exactly four bars before the hedge swallowed the sound, and those four bars were the bravest thing I'd ever heard, because every one of them knew what happened to singers Vess caught.

Inside, the garden went on being obscene. Paths of crushed white shell. Statues of the old order's generals—I recognized three of them from the hymnal's frontispiece, and one of them I recognized from a face I shaved every morning, which is a particular kind of horror I don't recommend. Someone had chiseled her name off the plinth. The stone under the gouge still read, if you knew how to look: *DRAV*.

Mother. Of course. He kept her statue in his garden the way a thief keeps a stolen crown—not out of love, but because looking at it reminded him he'd won.

I followed the covered cages. Past the fountains, past a topiary cut into the shape of the Spire itself (the man had a sense of humor like a knife wound), to a long gallery of glass and gilt that opened onto the central terrace. And there I stopped breathing, because the gallery was full of cages, uncovered now, and the cages were full of voices.

Not creatures. Voices. That sounds mad until you've seen it, and then it doesn't sound mad, it sounds like the world's worst funeral. Each cage held a shape of light and pressure, bird-sized or beast-sized or sometimes nothing-sized at all, and each one pressed against its bars when I came near, and each one *knew me*. Not Kestrel-the-charmseller. They knew the blood. They pressed toward me like plants toward a window.

And one of them spoke.

It came as vibration first, through the floor, up my boots, into my bones—Tam's language, though I hadn't taught it to him yet, or maybe he'd always known it and the rest of us are the deaf ones. Then it resolved, and it was a voice like frost forming on a pond, patient and old:

*Little Dravenmoor. You have her walk.*

My knees nearly went. I put a hand on the cage—gilt, of course, everything here was gilt—and the voice-shape flared against the bars like a moth.

"You knew her," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded.

*I marched beside her for eleven years,* said the frost-voice. *I was her scout-hawk before I was anything else. Before he took my cry and hung it in a box.* A pause, and the light dimmed, guttered. *He was her second, little Dravenmoor. Her right hand. Corvane. She trusted him with the wards' key-song, because that is what trust is for, and he learned it, and he fed it to the men who came in the night.*

Corvane. The name landed in me like a coin dropped down a well. Everyone in the free cities knew Lord Corvane, the Usurper, the Quiet King, the man who'd ended the border wars by ending the border's magic. Nobody had told me—nobody could have told me—that he'd been nobody. A second-in-command. Hers.

"He betrayed her," I said. "And then he built a garden."

*He built a monument,* the hawk said bitterly. *To himself. To his victory over the only person who ever saw what he was. Ask the wolf, two cages down. She held your mother's last order.*

I moved down the gallery. Two cages down, a grey shape the size of a curled dog lifted its head, and its voice was low thunder rolling uphill.

*She ordered us to run,* the wolf said. *Last thing she ever said to any of us. Run, and carry the songs, and wait for her blood to come back for them.* The great head turned, and eyes like banked coals found mine. *Ten years you waited without knowing. We waited knowing. Do you understand the difference, little Dravenmoor?*

I understood. That was the problem. Because standing there in that gilded gallery, with my mother's soldiers pressing against their bars and my mother's name chiseled off a plinth twenty paces away, I understood exactly what I had come for, and it had stopped being rescue.

There was a knife in my boot. There was a corridor behind me, and beyond it, somewhere in this white tower, a man who had taken everything—her order, her soldiers, her name off a statue, my whole childhood of hiding—and he was here, he was *here*, and I had a knife, and I had a gift he'd spent a decade failing to catch, and the fury in my chest said one word, over and over, in a voice that sounded like mine but older: *upstairs, upstairs, upstairs.*

Rescue could wait an hour. Rescue had waited ten years.

That's what the fury said. And the terrible thing, the thing I'll carry even after everything else, is how reasonable it sounded.

* * *

What stopped me wasn't wisdom. Wisdom had nothing to do with it.

What stopped me was the vixen.

Her voice-cage sat at the gallery's end, apart from the others, smaller than the rest, and when I passed it she didn't press to the bars. She just sang—one thread of the strain I'd known since I was nine years old and too stupid to hide it, the melody she used to run along the hedgerows of my childhood, the one that meant *safe passage, little one, the night is yours*. She sang it quietly. She sang it the way you'd sing to something wounded.

And I realized she wasn't singing to comfort me. She was singing to remind me what I was.

Not an avenger. A speaker. My mother's whole order, every general and scout-hawk and ward-hound in it, had never been about power—it had been about *listening*. About standing between the speaking world and the silent one and carrying messages across. You don't avenge a bridge by burning it. You cross it.

"Help me get you out," I whispered to the vixen. "All of you. That's the order. That's the only order."

The wolf's coal-eyes watched me a long moment. Then, slowly, the great grey head dipped—the old order's salute, the one from the hymnal's margins, the one I'd seen a hundred times in ink and never once in life.

*Then go up, little Dravenmoor,* the wolf said. *But go up as her heir, not as his mirror. He keeps his vault at the top. And he keeps a guard there who has been asking about you by name.*

"I know," I said. "Vess."

*No,* said the frost-hawk, and its light flared hard, urgent, alarm-bright. *Vess is below. He is in the garden. He has been in the garden since sundown.*

The cold went through me all at once, clean as a blade. Below. Where the choir was singing. Where nine voices were holding a masking harmony that had one purpose—to sound like nothing—and one listener who carried a tuning fork forged from a Dravenmoor blade, whose whole profession was hearing the note underneath the note.

I was already running when the first scream came from the eastern terraces. It wasn't a singer's scream.

It was the sound of a harmony breaking.
