## 9. The Unmaking Song

*Kestrel*

I want it noted, for whatever history survives us, that I did not want to do this.

The choir had climbed the Spire's throat for six hours—up stairwells of black stone that drank our lantern light, past niches where the usurper's silence-spells hung like cobwebs made of nothing—and the whole way down and up again my body kept trying to make a case for turning back. My knees voted against me at every landing. My voice, that treacherous inheritance I'd spent ten years strangling in its sleep, hummed under my sternum like a wasp in a jar, and I told it firmly: *No. Not yet. Not until we have no other choice.*

Which is a lie you can only tell yourself for so long before even you stop believing it.

The vault chamber opened above us like the inside of a bell: a dome of pale stone scored with grooves, and hanging from its crown, a cage. Not iron. The cage was made of sound itself—a sphere of held silence so dense it looked solid, and inside it, pressed against its walls like moths at a window, were the voices. Hundreds of them. The ward-beasts. The griffins and river-singers and the low patient voices of things older than cities, all of them stolen the night the stars went out.

And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, something that made my legs go strange: a woman's voice, singing a lullaby I did not know and somehow knew by heart.

"Kestrel Dravenmoor." Vess stood on the far side of the chamber, and he did not draw his blade. He didn't need to. In his hand, catching what little light remained, was the tuning fork—the one forged from a Dravenmoor sword, the one that ate magic whole. "You came. Good. He said you would."

"Your master," I said. "Where is he?"

Vess smiled, and it was the saddest smile I've ever seen on a man holding a weapon. "Closer than you think."

* * *

*Tam*

The floor sang before anyone else heard anything.

I felt it through my boots, through my palms when I put them flat against the stone, because Sera taught me that everything true arrives as vibration first and sound second, and some truths never bother becoming sound at all. The floor said: *footsteps, many, wrong rhythm.* The walls said: *stone remembering being sung into shape.* And the cage overhead—

The cage overhead said *grief.*

Not mine. Theirs. Hundreds of griefs braided together, pressing down through the dome like rain. I signed to Sera, fast, and she read my hands the way she read music, and her face went white and then went calm, which is what her face does instead of screaming.

We took our places. That was all any of this ever was, in the end: knowing your place in the line. Merrin with his low drone. The Ashford twins, their harmonies wound tight as rope. Sera at the center, arms rising like a conductor who had waited twenty years for a hall worthy of her.

Kestrel stood apart, facing Vess, and even without hearing I could feel them arguing—not with words, with weight. Two people whose voices could unmake each other, circling.

Sera gave the downbeat.

And the Spire, which had been silent since the night the stars died, began—for the first time—to answer.

* * *

*Kestrel*

Here is what nobody tells you about the usurper: he wasn't in the room, and he also wasn't anywhere else.

He was *in the cage.*

I understood it the moment the choir's first chord struck the dome and the silence-sphere shuddered. The voices inside weren't just imprisoned—they were layered, stacked, and at the very bottom of the stack, oldest and deepest, was a voice I recognized from the hymnal, from the stories, from the lullaby bleeding through the walls of my own skull. General Maren Dravenmoor. My mother. And wrapped around her stolen voice like a hand around a throat, wearing it, speaking through it for eleven years, was the man who had been her second-in-command. Corvane. The Usurper. He hadn't merely caged the ward-voices—he'd built himself out of them, stitched a new self from the old order's song, and ruled the borderlands as a chorus of everyone he'd silenced.

"He isn't here," I said aloud, and my voice cracked the chamber's hush. "He's *them*. He's all of them."

"Yes," Vess said quietly. "Now you understand why the fork must stay intact." He raised it, and I felt my gift recoil like a snail into its shell. "He keeps me alive for the same reason he keeps them. A man made entirely of stolen voices needs someone who remembers what silence costs."

Behind him, the choir's song rose. It was not a battle-hymn; Sera had built it from a harvest round, something about bread, of all things, and the vault grooves caught it and multiplied it until the whole dome thrummed like a struck bell. Cracks spidered up the silence-sphere. Threads of light leaked through—starlight-colored, though there were no stars left to be starlight.

Corvane screamed through a hundred throats at once.

It should have been horrible. It was horrible. But underneath the horror, every stolen voice was doing something none of them had been permitted in eleven years:

They were singing *back.*

* * *

*Tam*

I have felt earthquakes. I have felt Sera's orchestra hit the passage in the Third Lament where the basses stop pretending to be gentle.

I have never felt anything like Kestrel's mother.

When her voice found the seam in the cage and pushed, the whole mountain rang like a cupped palm over my ears, except I could not take my ears away, could not step outside the sound, because the sound was not sound. It was *claiming.* Every vibration said the same thing in different tongues: *mine, mine, return, return.* The rats in Vaelmoor had spoken of her once, in the early days—*the Loud One, the Loud One who listened*—and now I finally knew what they meant. She wasn't loud. She just refused, at the level of the bones of the world, to be quiet.

But the cage needed more than her. The cracks spread and then slowed, healing themselves, because a prison built from stolen voices knows how to steal. It reached for our song and began drinking it. Merrin's drone thinned. The twins' harmony frayed. Sera bled into the music harder, and I saw her nose start to bleed too, and I understood we were losing—that the choir could hold the note but not break the vault, that the vault wanted exactly what we were giving and would simply eat it forever.

Everyone looked at Kestrel.

Kestrel looked at the cage.

And I watched them decide. I have spent my whole life reading faces because I cannot read voices, and I am telling you: there is no sign for what crossed theirs. Grief and choice arrived in the same instant and became the same thing.

They opened their mouth.

* * *

*Kestrel*

My mother's lullaby has no words I could write down. Beast-speech doesn't work that way—it's not language the way ink is language. But I had been carrying it for thirty years without knowing, the way you carry a scar without remembering the cut, and when I finally let it rise, it came up whole.

I poured it into the crack.

I felt it leave. That's the part I'll try to describe to Tam later, in signs, badly, and fail: it didn't feel like shouting or crying out. It felt like opening a door in my chest that I had bricked over at fifteen, and everything behind it—all those years of swallowed words, all the conversations I'd never had with horses and hawks and the small gray citizens of Vaelmoor's gutters, all of it, my entire hoarded inheritance—rushing toward the light at once, glad, *glad*, the way a released bird is not tragic but furious with joy.

The vault did not shatter. Better. It *unmade itself*, because a cage built of theft cannot survive its thieves' restitution. The silence-sphere came apart into threads, and the threads came apart into voices, and the voices came apart into *stars*—because that's what the wards had always been, I understood then, watching them pour up through the broken dome and out through stone as if stone were a suggestion: the stars were the voices. They'd been up there all along, singing the borders safe, until a man who couldn't bear to be only himself pulled them down.

One by one, then all at once, the sky reignited.

And in the center of the freeing storm, two shapes remained: a woman made of light who looked at me—looked at *me*, Kestrel Dravenmoor, charm-seller, coward, last of her line—and did not say goodbye, because she had already given me everything she had, including the thing I'd just spent.

She smiled, and went up with the rest.

Then there was only Vess, on his knees, the tuning fork ringing uselessly in his fist, his face wet, listening—I realized with a jolt that he could hear it, he alone could hear it—his master's stolen voice, screaming as it left him.

"No," Vess whispered. "No, please, don't take him, he's all I—" and then stopped, because there was nothing left to beg from. Whatever Corvane had been to him, whatever twisted devotion had forged the inquisitor out of the orphan, it departed with the voice. What knelt on the stones afterward was just a man, hollowed and ordinary, weeping at an empty sky full of new stars.

I tried to say something to him. Something wry, probably. Something about how the fork made a poor souvenir now.

Nothing came.

I touched my throat. I shaped the words. The words had gone where all the others went—into the sky, into the wards, into the borders of the world, holding the dark out.

Tam found my hand in the settling dust and squeezed it, and through his palm I felt him laughing and crying at once, and I thought: well. There are worse ways to lose your voice than spending it.

Below us, already, the city bells of the free quarters had begun to ring—and for the first time in a generation, the beasts of Vaelmoor were answering.
