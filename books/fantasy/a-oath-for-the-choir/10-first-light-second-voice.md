## 10. First Light, Second Voice

The first thing you should know about me is that I never heard a single word until the stars went out.

That's not a riddle. I was born deaf, which in Vaelmoor used to mean one of three things: a beggar, a burden, or a bad omen. I managed all three by my ninth birthday. But when the wards fell and the stars snuffed out like candles pinched by a spiteful god, something else went out with them—the silence-spells, the ones the usurper had woven into the very stones of the city to keep magic quiet and the gifted quieter. And when those spells unraveled, the world got *loud* in a way I could feel through my boots, my teeth, the bones of my chest. Magic, it turns out, isn't sound. It's vibration. It's rhythm. And I had spent my whole life learning to read rhythm the way other people read street signs.

So when Kestrel Dravenmoor walked into the cistern with a wounded ward-hound limping at their heel and a decade of buried grief written across their face, I felt them before I saw them. Their magic had a rhythm like a heart trying to forget how to beat. I told Sera it sounded like the old hymnal with their family name in it. Sera, being Sera, made a face like she'd swallowed a lemon and asked me to be sure.

I was sure. I'm still sure. This is the story of what happened after, and I'm telling it because Kestrel can't, and because somebody in this city ought to write down the truth before the council gets hold of it and sands off all the interesting parts.

* * *

Kestrel came back to Vaelmoor eleven days after the Spire fell, riding in the back of a grain cart because their legs had given out two miles from the gates and their pride hadn't. I rode with them. Someone had to hold the water skin, and someone had to listen to the silence where their voice used to be, and I had gotten very good at both.

They looked terrible. I want that on the record, because the ballads that are already being sung in the taverns—the ones where Kestrel stands radiant atop the shattered vault with light pouring from their outstretched hands—leave out that they were gray as old porridge, that their hands shook for three days, and that when the stars reignited they were face-down in the vault dust, weeping too hard to see them.

I saw the stars come back. I felt it. Every star that lit was a voice returning to the world, and each one landed in my chest like a struck bell. Two hundred and forty-one voices, if you're counting. I counted. It's what I do.

Kestrel couldn't speak anymore. That was the price of the Unmaking Song—pour your own voice into the release, permanently, and the vault shatters and everything inside goes free. They'd known the cost before they climbed the Spire. They climbed anyway. When I asked them afterward—asked with my hands, in the sign language we'd built on the road north, half borrowed from beast-speech and half pure invention—whether they regretted it, they looked at me for a long moment and then tapped the rhythm of the stars returning against my palm.

That was answer enough.

* * *

Vaelmoor had changed while we were gone.

You have to understand what the silence-spells did to this city. For a generation, magic was a thing you did in cellars and whispered about in alleys, and the gifted were a thing you reported to the inquisitors if you wanted the good bread ration. When the wards fell, everyone assumed the usurper's grip would tighten. Instead it snapped. A city that had spent twenty years pretending it had no magic woke up one morning with stars in the sky again and realized it had been holding its breath the whole time.

By the time we rolled through the gates, the inquisitors' tower was a smoking ruin and someone had hung the usurper's banners from the bridge railings to scare crows. The market was open. The animals were loud—gods, the *noise*, I felt it in my fillings, geese and dogs and the fishwives' donkeys all shouting at once, and nobody shushing them, because a loud animal meant a free city now.

And in the middle of the market square, someone had set up a platform. Not a gallows—the city had seen enough of those—but a speaking stand, with a crowd around it arguing at full volume about what to do with magic now that it was out of the cellar.

Arguing. Openly. In daylight. A tanner was shouting that all magic should be registered. A dyer was shouting that registration was how the usurper started. A woman who turned out to be the head of the fishmongers' guild was shouting that both of them were idiots and what the city needed was a council, and everyone shouted at her too.

Sera Quill stood at the edge of the crowd with her arms crossed and an expression I can only describe as a musician listening to an orchestra tune badly.

"They're getting the words wrong," she said, "but they're singing. That's more than we had last month."

Kestrel looked at the platform, then at Sera, then at me, and made the sign we'd invented on the road: *they need a choir.*

Sera laughed. It was not a kind laugh, exactly, but it was an honest one. "They need a *lot* of things. And they'll never ask us for any of them. Outcasts don't get asked. We have to volunteer."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being an outcast: you get very good at doors. Which ones stick, which ones have watchful hinges, which ones you can slip through. The choir had spent years learning the doors of Vaelmoor—the cistern gates, the safehouse cellars, the loose grate behind the chandler's. And when the city needed somewhere to hold its first open assembly on magic, somewhere the inquisitors' old networks couldn't reach, it turned out the choir knew more usable rooms than the guilds did.

The first council met in the cistern. I insisted on that, and Sera backed me, and the guild delegates grumbled about the smell but came anyway, because the alternative was meeting in the tower ruins and nobody wanted to sit in that ash.

Kestrel did not speak at the first council. Kestrel could not speak at any council, ever again, and I want to be very clear about what that meant, because I've heard people say it pityingly—*poor Kestrel, struck mute*—and it makes me want to throw something.

Kestrel was not silenced. Kestrel *gave their voice away*, the way you give away a coat to someone freezing in the street. Two hundred and forty-one creatures got to sing again because Kestrel went quiet. And here's the part the pitying people miss: Kestrel has never been louder.

I know because I'm their interpreter. It started as a practical arrangement—I read the vibration of their signs better than anyone, and I could shout them into speech for the room. But somewhere in the second week it became something else. When the tanner said magic should be chained "for safety," Kestrel signed back a whole argument—not just a rebuttal but a history, everything they'd learned from the rats and the ward-hound and the caged voices at the Spire, about what chaining magic actually does, how it starves, how it goes mad in its cage and takes the sky down with it. And I stood in front of the council and said it all in my own cracked, unlovely, hearing-world voice, and the room went quiet in the way rooms only go quiet when they're listening.

Afterward the fishmonger woman—her name was Odile, and she became the council's first chair—came up to Kestrel and said, "You should speak for us."

Kestrel shook their head and pointed at me.

"No," Odile said, patient as a woman explaining fish to a cat. "I mean *you*. Through them, around them, however you do it. You're the one who's seen what silence costs. This council needs that voice."

Kestrel looked at me. I looked at Kestrel. And I signed, because it was true and because someone had to: *You spent ten years hiding your gift so nobody would hear it. Now the whole city wants to hear it. That's not a trap, Kess. That's the door finally opening.*

Kestrel cried a little. I'm telling you this because the ballads won't. Then they signed, *Fine. But you're standing next to me.*

Always, I signed back. That was never in question.

* * *

The council of voices—yes, that's what they ended up calling it, and yes, Sera takes credit—has been sitting for two months now. It is a mess. The tanner still wants registration; the dyer still calls him a usurper-in-training; last week somebody proposed a citywide festival and somebody else proposed a tax to pay for it and the two motions got tangled together so badly that now Vaelmoor is having a taxed festival whether it likes it or not.

But the wards are being rebuilt. Not with caged voices—Odile made that the council's first law, before they even settled the festival question: *no voice shall be taken to power another's walls.* The new wards hum with volunteered song instead. Sera trains ward-choirs the way she once trained the outcasts, and the creatures who came back from the Spire—the ward-hound among them, whose name, it turns out, is Bramble, and who has appointed herself Kestrel's shadow—take turns lending their harmonies. Voluntarily. With contracts. Bramble negotiated for weekly marrow bones and I have never respected a negotiator more.

And Kestrel. Kestrel walks the market now with their head up. The people who once would have reported them to the inquisitors nod to them in the street. Children ask them to make the rats dance—Kestrel pretends to find this beneath their dignity, and then makes the rats dance. Their gift is no longer a secret and no longer a curse. It's just theirs. It turns out that's all they ever wanted, and it took the loss of a voice and the fall of a sky to get it.

Some nights, when the council adjourns and the cistern empties out, Kestrel and I sit on the edge of the reservoir with Bramble's head in Kestrel's lap, and Kestrel signs into the dark, and I listen with my hands and my chest and my teeth, and I answer. We've gotten fast at it. Sometimes we forget the hearing people in the room can't follow and Sera has to clear her throat pointedly.

Last night Kestrel signed something new. A sign I hadn't taught them and they hadn't taught me, one we made in the moment, the way living languages do.

It meant: *thank you for being my voice.*

I signed back the sign we'd invented on the road north, the one that means *always*, and then, because I'm Tam and honesty is my whole personality, I added the sign for *but you were always your own voice. I just turned up the volume.*

Kestrel laughed. It's a silent laugh, of course—I felt it more than saw it, a shaking in their shoulders, a rhythm like the one the stars made when they came back. Two hundred and forty-one bells. One of them is Kestrel's. It never stopped ringing. It just rings somewhere else now, in the wards, in the sky, in the space between my hands when they speak.

The city is loud. The stars are lit. The choir sings.

And Kestrel Dravenmoor, who spent a decade being no one, has never been more heard.
