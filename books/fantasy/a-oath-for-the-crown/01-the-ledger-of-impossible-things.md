## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Vellum Guild keeps its ledgers in a vault beneath the counting house, and the vault keeps its own weather. It is always cold enough to fog your breath, always dry enough that the ink never blots, and it smells the way all important places smell—of tallow and dust and the particular, patient silence of things worth more than lives.

I have spent eleven years in that silence. Long enough to stop hearing it. Long enough to mistake it for peace.

On the night this story properly begins—a Thursday, as it happens; even catastrophe respects a schedule—I was auditing Volume Nine of the Acquisitions Ledger, which covers the years of the old Guildmistress's reign, when the guild still bothered to write down where its merchandise came from. The pages were calfskin, soft as an ear, and the entries ran in three different hands across four decades of theft.

*Item: one (1) first kiss, stolen from a cooper's daughter in Marrow Lane, aged sixteen. Condition: excellent, barely remembered by seller. Notes: tastes of cider and terror.*

*Item: one (1) grief, bottled, source deceased. Wept from a widow over the course of nine days. Sold to a playwright who could not manage to feel anything himself. Condition: potent. Warning: do not uncork indoors.*

*Item: childhood of the river Vel, complete with tributaries. Taken by arrangement with the river itself, which had grown tired of remembering every stone it had ever worn smooth. Payment: the guild agreed to forget it owed the river a favor. The river has not forgotten. Rivers never do.*

I read these the way other men read scripture—with the particular attention of someone who has long ago stopped believing and started taking notes instead. My pen moved. My columns balanced. Somewhere above me, Vellum Street closed its shutters and lit its lamps and went about the business of forgetting its day, and none of it touched me, because I was Emrys Fairweather, keeper of accounts, and I was very good at my work.

Good at it in the way that matters least, perhaps. Anyone can add a column. What I could do—and what only six living people knew, if you counted Sarelle, and you should always count Sarelle twice—was hold what the ledgers held. Touch a memory long enough and it comes off the page like lint off a sleeve. Hold it in your palm and it warms, or chills, or aches, depending on what it was when it was alive in somebody's head.

That night I held the cooper's daughter's first kiss for exactly as long as it took to check the entry against the inventory seals. Cider and terror, just as advertised. I put it back. That was the rule, the first rule, the rule carved into the lintel of the guildhall in letters older than the city: *What passes through your hands is not yours.* Keepers who kept became thieves. Thieves who were caught became examples. There was a wall in the counting house with a list of names on it, and the list was short, and every name on it had been crossed out in red, and nobody talked about the wall, and everybody thought about the wall.

Eleven years, and I had never once been tempted past the line.

I want to be honest about why, since honesty costs me so little now. It wasn't virtue. Virtue is a story people tell about you afterward. It was that I had nothing to trade a stolen feeling against. A man who has tasted cider-and-terror wants more of it, and then wants the whole orchard, and then wants the girl. I'd watched it happen to Dannel Ostrow, third keeper before me, who lifted a single hour of his dead brother's laughter and within a season had hollowed himself into a reliquary of other people's joys, sitting in the dark smiling at nothing, until Sarelle had him crossed off the wall's list in the other direction—struck from the guild entirely, memory wiped down to the grammar, turned loose to be a stranger in his own city.

So I took nothing. For eleven years. I audited impossible things and went home to two rooms above a chandler's shop and ate bread and cheese and slept without dreaming, mostly, and told myself this was contentment because I had no other word for it that didn't embarrass us both.

But here is the thing about a man who handles treasure all day: eventually the hands get ideas of their own.

* * *

It happened near midnight, on the last folio of Volume Nine, in the margin of an otherwise unremarkable entry. Someone—a junior appraiser, by the hand, someone bored, someone like me—had noted, in pencil, almost apologetically:

*Item: one (1) summer afternoon, unclaimed. Found loose in the strongroom after the Harrow fire, provenance unknown. No buyer. Filed pending valuation. Valuation overdue 14 years.*

An afternoon with no owner. An orphaned memory, sitting in our inventory like a coin left face-up on a curb, fourteen years past the point where anyone could reasonably claim it. Fourteen years past the point where anyone would even remember looking for it.

I checked the seal. Faded but intact. I checked the cross-reference. Nothing. I checked, with a thoroughness I can only describe as premeditated wearing the costume of diligence, the disposition logs, the destruction manifests, the unclaimed-goods rolls. Nothing, nothing, nothing.

Legally speaking—and the guild is nothing if not legal, in the way a spider is nothing if not architectural—the item was mine by right of salvage. Unclaimed goods escheat to the finder after seven years. I know because I wrote the footnote in the guild's own codebook explaining this, during a slow winter, as a joke.

God has a sense of humor. It runs to irony and long game.

I sat there a long time. The lamp burned. The vault breathed its cold dry breath. Somewhere overhead a cart rattled past, and I heard the chandler's dog complain about it, and both sounds seemed to come from a country I had once visited.

Here is how a memory feels when you take it properly, palm flat, intention clear, no fumbling. It feels like picking up a coin that someone has just pulled from their own pocket—warm on one side, cool on the other. This one was warm on the side that mattered. I closed my fingers around it, felt it dissolve upward through my skin the way ice melts in a fist, and then it was in me, settled behind my sternum, humming faintly, a small sun waiting for permission.

Then I did something I hadn't done in eleven years.

I stopped working.

I put down the pen. I leaned back in the chair until it creaked its objection, and I opened the memory the way you open a window in a stuffy room, and I let the afternoon happen to me.

Grass. Real grass, knee-high, gone gold at the tips because the summer had been dry and kind at once. A sky doing that thing skies only do in memories, which is being bluer than physics allows. Someone's washing on a line—white sheets, snapping, smelling of lye and sun. And a sound underneath everything, rhythmic and patient, of wood being planed, because there was a carpenter somewhere out of sight, and he was good at his work, and he was in no hurry, and neither was the afternoon.

That was all. No people I could see. No event. Just an hour of ordinary sunlight, ownerless, going gently to waste, and me lying in it like a cat.

I don't know whose it was. I don't know if they sold it, lost it, died holding it, or set it down the way you set down a basket to rest your arms and forgot which tree you leaned it against. The guild's records end at the strongroom door; beyond that is the country of the actual living, where things happen without documentation.

But for one hour—which lasted, in the way of such things, either a moment or the whole night—I was a person lying in grass in a world where washing dried in the sun and someone planed wood without hurry, and I was not Emrys Fairweather, keeper of accounts, thirty-one years old, two rooms, no family, no history before the age of nineteen, no debts, no loves, no enemies, no stories. I was nobody at all, in the best possible way. I was a page before the writing.

When it ended I sat in the cold again with my eyes stinging, which I attributed to the lamp, the way you do.

Because here is the rest of the truth, the part I have only recently learned to say plainly: I have no childhood. Not suppressed, not hidden—absent. My earliest memory is a ferry crossing in the rain, aged nineteen by the guild's reckoning, and before that there is simply nothing, a clean edge like paper cut with a blade. Sarelle says I came to the guild half-drowned and half-mad with grief I couldn't source, carrying nothing but the clothes I wore and a name I couldn't explain knowing. Fairweather. Who names themselves Fairweather? Nobody, she says. Names are given by mothers or earned by reputations, and I had neither.

The guild filled the gap. They gave me the ledger-work because I was careful, and the solitude because I was quiet, and the small rooms and the steady wage and the wall with the crossed-out names, and they called it a life, and for eleven years I called it that too.

You can live a long time on an afternoon saved up like coins in a jar. But jars empty. Jars always empty.

I filed Volume Nine. I snuffed the lamp. I signed the audit roll—*E. Fairweather, all entries verified, no discrepancies*—and the lie of that sentence sat as comfortably on my tongue as my name does, which is to say, like something borrowed.

And then, because the night wasn't finished with me, and because the gods of clerks punish exactly the pride they've just rewarded, I made my last mistake of the evening: I paused at the return shelf, where the deep-storage volumes wait to go back down into the dark, and I noticed that Volume Forty-One—the prophecy acquisitions, the guild's oldest and most embarrassing shelf, full of the ravings we bought off desperate prophets in lean centuries purely so no one else could read them—was out of order.

Volume Forty-One does not leave the case. It hasn't been requested in sixty years. Its spine is uncracked. Its place on the shelf is marked by a brass plate engraved with its catalogue number, and the volume was sitting four inches to the left of that plate, as though someone had taken it down, looked at it, and put it back in too much of a hurry to care about precision.

Nobody works this floor after me. Nobody works this floor before me. I am the beginning and end of the accounting, the alpha and omega of the ledger, and I am very good at noticing what is four inches out of place.

I stood there with the orphaned afternoon still warm behind my sternum, feeling—for the first time in eleven years—like an item in someone else's inventory.

I took Volume Forty-One down.
