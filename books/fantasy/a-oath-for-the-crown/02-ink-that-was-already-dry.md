## 2. Ink That Was Already Dry

There is a particular kind of quiet that settles over the Vellum Guild's archive after the third bell, and I have come to think of it as the only honest thing in the building. Everything else in the place is somebody else's truth, decanted, corked, and priced. But the quiet at three in the morning belongs to whoever is willing to sit in it.

That night, the quiet belonged to me, and I was squandering it on cross-checking.

I want to be clear that cross-checking is not a glamorous activity. It is the accounting equivalent of scrubbing grout. When the Guild acquires something old—truly old, older than the current Guildmistress's grandmother and the grudges she inherited—protocol requires that the item be reconciled against every ledger it has ever touched. Provenance, they call it, in the tone of people who believe naming a thing makes it clean. I call it reading other people's mail across a hundred years, which is more accurate and considerably less flattering to everyone involved.

The acquisition in question had come in eleven days earlier: a folio of pressed vellum, water-stained, smelling faintly of cedar and older faintly of something I chose not to name, from the estate of a dead collector in Lowmark whose heirs had no idea what they were selling. The heirs thought they were selling a book of recipes. The Guild paid them in recipes' worth of coin and I was instructed to make the thing disappear into the catalogue under a boring enough heading that no one would ever ask about it again.

*Botanical records, misc., 19th c.* I had written. Boring is a craft. I like to think I'm good at it.

The trouble with old things is that they were made by people, and people are incapable of leaving a box empty. Somewhere around the fourth hour of reconciliation—matching the folio against acquisition entries in ledgers so old their ink had gone the brown of dried blood—I found a page that had been pasted over. Someone, a century ago, had glued a blank leaf on top of it, the archival equivalent of hanging a curtain over a window you don't like.

The glue had failed. Glue always fails. It's the one reliable prophecy in this city.

I eased the leaf back with a bone folder and a patience I usually reserve for defusing my own mistakes, and there beneath it was a page that had no business existing, written in a hand that slanted hard to the left, as if its author had been left-handed or in a hurry or both.

It was a ledger. That was the first wrong thing. Not a diary, not a letter, not a recipe—a ledger, with columns ruled in faded red, the way we rule ours. Someone had kept accounts. And the entries were not for coin.

I have spent eleven years in the Vellum Guild. I have catalogued a first kiss lifted from a dying man and sold twice. I have shelved a bottle of grief so dense it sweated. I once held, for the length of a sneeze, the childhood of a river—don't ask, the entry is sealed, and I regret reading the description more than I regret most of my sins. I am not easily unsettled by the inventory of the impossible.

But a century-old ledger written in our format, in our column-rules, by hands that had never heard of us, is a different order of wrong. It is the wrongness of finding your own handwriting on a wall you've never visited.

I did what any sensible archivist does with a document that frightens him. I made tea. Then I told myself the tea was for warmth and not for delay, which was a lie, and drank it anyway, and went back.

The entries were dated. That was the second wrong thing, and it took me a while to understand why it was wrong, because at first it just looked like ordinary bookkeeping. Dates in the old style, the pre-Reform calendar, which took me an embarrassing quarter hour to convert because I am an accountant, not a historian, and because my fingers had begun doing something I didn't authorize, which was trembling.

The entries ran across decades. *Received: the last words of Queen Maren, unspoken. Paid: one winter, entire.* *Received: a king's name, surrendered voluntarily, condition: he must never learn what he paid.* *Received: the memory of a coronation, from the crown itself—note: the crown remembers; this is a problem.*

And then, near the end, in a hand that had gone shakier, an entry with no received and no paid. Just a date, and beneath it a passage that was not bookkeeping at all.

I am going to set the date down here, because it matters, and because writing it out is the only way I could make myself believe I'd read it correctly.

It was tonight's date. Not the month, not the season—*tonight's*. The same night, the same year, the same three-in-the-morning hour in which I sat holding it, converted from a calendar that had been dead for a hundred years by a man whose hands were now shaking hard enough to rattle the folio.

Beneath the date, the shaky hand had written:

*The keeper of accounts shall open the crown. He will not know his own name when he does. Whether the crown is opened to be freed or to be fed, the ink does not say. The ink was dry before I was born. I have copied it faithfully. Forgive me. I know what it costs to be written.*

I sat in that quiet for a long time.

Here is what you have to understand about me, and I say this without pride because there isn't any to be had in it: I have built my entire life on the principle of being unwritten. Not unimportant—I know what I am, and it's competent, and competence is its own quiet comfort—but *unwritten*. No prophecies. No destinies. No entry in anyone's book. The Guild took me in off the streets of the Tallow Quarter when I was fourteen with no family, no history, and, thanks to the Guild's own practices, no memories older than that, and I made a philosophy out of the gap. Other people have pasts. Other people have burdens. I have ledgers. Ledgers balance. Pasts don't.

A man like me does not appear in prophecies. A man like me is the one who *catalogues* prophecies, files them under *Folklore, apocalyptic, misc.*, and goes home to his narrow bed feeling pleasantly superior to everyone whose life is governed by a story.

And here was a story, a century old, with my job title in it. Not my name—*the keeper of accounts*—which is worse, if you're wondering, not better. A name would have been a coincidence. A name could be argued with. But there is precisely one keeper of accounts in the Vellum Guild, and I was looking at him in a mirror of old glass, and the mirror said he was going to open the crown, and it said he would not know his own name when he did.

The last part I dismissed first, because it was the easiest. I know my name. Emrys Fairweather. It's not much of a name—Fairweather was assigned to me by a guild clerk with a sense of humor and a list of weather-related surnames, and Emrys I chose myself at sixteen because it sounded like someone who read books—but it's mine, and I know it, and I say it to myself sometimes the way you touch a tooth that aches, just to confirm the ache is still there. So the prophecy was wrong, or the prophecy was about someone else, or the prophecy was the fevered copywork of some long-dead clerk who, forgive me, I did not forgive.

That was the denial. I want to be honest about the order of operations, because the order matters. Denial came first, and it came easily, and it wore the sensible face I show Sarelle and the Guild and the world: *this is an artifact, artifacts are not oracles, correlation is a thing accountants are trained to be suspicious of.* I have audited enough books to know that a number can be made to say anything if you squint correctly.

But underneath the denial, doing what dread does, which is seep, was the other thought. The one I didn't invite. The one that pointed out, in a voice that sounded unpleasantly like my own, that the entry had no *received* and no *paid*. That it wasn't a transaction at all. That whoever wrote it hadn't been recording a trade—they'd been passing along a sentence they couldn't refuse to write, the way you can't refuse to sneeze. *The ink was dry before I was born.*

I know something about ink that was dry before I was born. I work in a building full of it. The entire Guild runs on the principle that memory can be moved from one vessel to another, that a mind is a ledger and entries can be transferred, and there is nothing in our charter—nothing in a hundred years of our practice—that says the transfer only goes one direction, or that the past cannot reach forward and write itself into a person who wasn't consulted.

I put the folio away. I put it away carefully, under *Botanical records, misc., 19th c.*, where it would be boring forever, and I sat back down at my desk, and I opened my own working ledger, the one with my own hand in it, and I looked at tonight's date at the top of the page, and I felt the two dates lie down next to each other in my head like animals that had always known each other.

I did not tell anyone.

I want that recorded, since apparently my whole life is being recorded somewhere without my consent: I did not tell anyone. Not the night archivist, who was asleep at his post as usual. Not Master Quillon, who signs off on acquisitions and would have taken the folio and buried it deeper than I ever could, which is its own kind of telling. And certainly not Sarelle. Sarelle, who notices everything, who once deduced from the way I'd reshelved a single bottle that I'd been reading the sealed entries, and who had smiled when she said so in a way I still think about at bad moments. Sarelle, whose kindness is the load-bearing wall of my entire existence, and who I have never once tested, because a man who lives in a house he can't afford to lose doesn't tap the walls.

The Guild's first law is that nothing acquired is kept. Everything that comes in goes out again, sold or traded or spent, because a memory hoarded is a memory that starts hoarding *you*. I have watched it happen. I have catalogued the results, and some of those catalogues are the reason I drink my tea with the window open.

So I should have logged the folio's contents. Protocol, provenance, the whole grout-scrubbing apparatus of my respectable little life, all of it said: report the page, file it, let the machinery of the Guild do what the machinery does.

Instead I copied it. By hand, in my own ledger, in a cipher of my own devising that I have never taught another living soul, and then I burned the scrap I'd copied from, and then I sat there in the honest quiet with ash on my fingers and the distinct sensation of having just done the first dishonest thing of my career, if you don't count the small thefts, which I don't, because those are vitamins.

Here is the arithmetic I performed, because arithmetic is where I go when I'm frightened, the way other men go to drink. One: the prophecy named my job, my night, and my condition of namelessness, which is either a coincidence of such magnitude that I should buy a lottery ticket for the first time in my life, or it is not a coincidence at all. Two: the entry concerned the crown. The crown, as in the one across the city, the one that sits on the head of a king I have seen exactly twice from a distance, the one the Guild has never traded with, which I know because I keep the client ledgers, and the crown's absence from them has always struck me as the loudest silence in the building. Three: I am a keeper of accounts who has never once been written into an account, and the human animal cannot encounter a blank space bearing its own description without wanting to fill it.

Four—and I wrote this one nowhere, because four was the thought that made me close the ledger and put my forehead down on the cool wood of the desk like a schoolboy with a headache—four: *he will not know his own name when he does.*

I know my name. I have told you I know my name. Emrys Fairweather, assigned and chosen, a name like a coat that fits well enough if you don't move too quickly in it.

But I have also, for eleven years, had a gap where my childhood should be. The Guild says the memories were lost before they found me—burned, sold, scattered, the usual sad story, the story they gave every foundling they took in. And I believed it, the way you believe the floor will hold, which is to say completely and without ever once looking underneath.

*The ink was dry before I was born.*

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, the archive breathed around me—thousands of bottled lives, shelved and sleeping, all of them somebody's, none of them mine—and for the first time in eleven years the quiet didn't feel honest. It felt like the quiet of a room where a page has just been turned somewhere you can't see.

I told no one. I went home. I slept badly, which is to say I lay in my narrow bed doing the tooth-touching thing with my own name until dawn.

And in the morning, when I came down into the archive with my tea and my sensible face, the folio was not where I had left it.

Someone had moved it. Someone had opened it. And the pasted-over leaf—the curtain I had eased back and, coward that I am, pasted down again with fresh glue, because some windows you look through exactly once—was loose at one corner, curling away from the page beneath it like something inhaling.

* * *

I stood there with my tea going cold and did the arithmetic again, faster this time. The folio sat in acquisition bay four, spine out, exactly as catalogued, exactly where a folio under *Botanical records, misc.* belongs. Nothing about it was wrong except the glue, which was my glue, and which I had applied with my own hands eleven hours ago, and which was now failing in a direction I had not chosen.

Glue always fails. I told you that. It's the one reliable prophecy in this city.

I had just failed to laugh at my own joke when the archive door opened behind me, and the quiet—the dishonest, page-turning quiet—said the only thing it had left to say, which was her name, in her voice, before she'd spoken a word:

"Emrys," said Guildmistress Sarelle, from the doorway, in a tone I had never heard her use on me before, which was gentle. "Come and have breakfast. I think we should talk about what you found last night."

I had told no one.

Someone had told her.
