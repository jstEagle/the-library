## 3. The Guild Takes Notice

The summons came the way bad news always comes to a thief: politely, and with paperwork.

A slip of grey vellum slid under my door sometime before dawn, sealed with the Guild's wax and bearing a single line in the clerk's immaculate hand: *Mistress Sarelle requests the pleasure of your company at the ninth bell. Regarding an irregularity in the archives.*

*Requests the pleasure.* The Vellum Guild did not request. The Vellum Guild invoiced. A summons dressed as courtesy was like a lockpick dressed as a hairpin—same function, prettier lie.

I sat on my narrow bed in my narrow room above the counting-house and read the note four times, as if repetition might change its meaning the way a memory changed hands. It didn't. Somewhere between the third reading and the fourth, my fingers began doing that thing they do when my mind has already decided something my pride hasn't admitted: they itched toward my coat pocket, where the small stolen warmth of a stranger's first snowfall lived in its little glass vial, and toward the other pocket—the one I'd told myself all night was empty—where the prophecy ledger was not.

Because here is what you should know about me, before this story gets any worse: Emrys Fairweather does not make mistakes. Emrys Fairweather audits. He reconciles. He balances columns so clean that Sarelle herself once said—and she says very few things worth keeping—that if honesty were a trade, he'd be its master thief. For eleven years I had kept my ledgers perfect and my head down and my name unspoken in rooms where names were currency, and I had told myself this was safety. Invisibility, freely chosen. The one acquisition no one could repossess.

And last night, in the archive's deep stacks, I had found a page written a hundred years ago with tonight's date on it and a description of my own face, and I had done the single most dangerous thing a keeper of accounts can do.

I had taken it home.

* * *

The ninth bell found me standing outside Sarelle's office, which is not really an office but a converted chapel where the guild's founders once stored their first acquisitions before anyone knew memories could be bottled at all. The stained glass had been replaced with plain panes—Sarelle disapproved of anything that made light interesting—but the ceiling still arched like a held breath.

She was at her desk, and she did not look up when I entered. That was deliberate. Sarelle understood power the way I understood inventory: it depreciates when displayed carelessly. So she let me stand there among the smell of old vellum and lamp oil while she finished writing whatever she was writing, and I used the time to take inventory of her, because that is what I do instead of praying.

Guildmistress Sarelle Vane, fifty-some years, though she wore them the way a fence wears stolen goods—well, and deniable. Grey shot through her black hair at the temples only, as if age had been audited and trimmed. Her hands were bare; she wore no rings, because rings are memories you can see coming. On the desk before her: an inkpot, a letter knife, two cups of tea, and—

My stomach performed a small, professional appraisal of the floor.

Two cups.

"You'll take yours black," she said, still writing. "You've taken it black for eleven years. People think that's a preference. It isn't. It's a habit you built the first week you arrived, because the milk was sour that morning and you were too proud of your new position to complain." She signed whatever it was, blotted it, and looked up. "That's the sort of thing I know about you, Emrys. Sit down."

I sat. I took the tea. My hands were steady, which I noted with the detached interest of a man watching someone else's hands.

"Now." She folded her hands atop the desk. "Tell me about the Ashcroft bequest."

"The Ashcroft bequest," I repeated, "was catalogued in the spring. Fourteen lots. Bottled griefs, mostly, from a widow who sold her mourning rather than feel it. All accounted for."

"Mm. And the ledger that came with it?"

There it was. No preamble worth the name, no fencing. Sarelle had never fenced in her life; she simply opened doors and watched whether you walked through or jumped out a window.

"It came boxed with the rest," I said. "Old stock. I logged it."

"You logged it," she agreed. "In the general catalogue. Under *miscellaneous antiquarian volumes*, lot fourteen-adjacent." She turned a page on her desk—I hadn't seen her move it, which meant she'd moved it while I was busy being terrified, and that frightened me considerably more than anything she'd said so far. "And yet when my archivist went to shelve it yesterday evening, the box was empty. And yet the catalogue entry in your own fair hand shows the volume checked out to the audit room for three hours two nights ago. And yet—" she lifted her eyes, mild as milk, "—the audit room's lamp-oil expenditure for that night runs eleven minutes long."

Eleven minutes. I had spent eleven minutes with that page open, reading my own description by lamplight, feeling the floor tilt under a century of careful nothing. Eleven minutes, itemized somewhere, in some ledger, against my name.

"Someone read it," Sarelle said quietly. "Before you could hide it. Or after. The order hardly matters now. What matters is that a book written a hundred years ago, naming a date that arrives in six days, describing a young man with ink-stained fingers and a gift for holding what isn't his—" she let the sentence hang there, unfinished, the way you leave a hook in water, "—has been off the shelf."

"I can explain—"

"No," she said, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow worse. "Don't. Explanations are for people whose stories I haven't already read. Yours I've been reading since you were seventeen years old and walked through my door with no history, no family, and no name worth stealing—which, incidentally, made you the safest hire this guild ever made. Do you know why I took you in, Emrys?"

I said nothing. The tea steamed between us like a small accusation.

"Because you were empty stock," she said. "Not empty the way people mean it—empty the way a vault is empty. Sealed. Nothing inside worth taking, nothing leaking out worth tracing. A boy with no past is the perfect keeper of other people's pasts. You can't be blackmailed by a childhood you don't have. You can't be bought with a mother you don't remember." She smiled then, and it was the smile she used when closing a sale, warm as a banked fire and exactly as safe to touch. "I have protected that emptiness for eleven years. Fed it. Housed it. Made you comfortable in it. And now a dead prophet goes and writes on your empty page."

"The ledger," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "You want it back."

"I want," she corrected gently, "to protect you. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. That page names you, child. Not *a* keeper of accounts—*you*. Do you understand what happens when a prophecy names a member of my guild? Every buyer from here to the Salt Coast starts pricing his memories. Every rival house starts planning how to acquire him. Every fanatic with a bottle and a prayer decides he's either the instrument of a king's salvation or the blade of his ruin, and both kinds come looking, and neither knocks." She pushed the second cup aside. "So. Give me the ledger, and I will make the problem go away the way I make problems go away. Quietly. Permanently. And you will remain what you have always been, which is nothing—which, believe me, is the most valuable thing you own."

And there it was. The offer, wrapped in kindness, tied with concern, delivered in the voice of the woman who had fed me and taught me and never once asked me where I came from—because, I understood now, sitting very still in that converted chapel with my tea going cold, she had never needed to ask. She had known. She had known I came from nowhere, or believed I did, and had valued precisely that, the way a merchant values a blank page over a written one. Blank pages are easier to fill. Blank pages don't argue back.

Every kind thing she had ever done rearranged itself in my head, the way entries do when you find the error on line forty and suddenly the whole column above it means something else. The room with no questions. The salary generous enough that I'd never need a second employer. The gentle steering away from field work, from clients, from anything that might have given me a life outside these walls. Eleven years of invisibility, and I had called it freedom.

It had been permission. Granted, rationed, revocable.

"You think I'm lying to you," Sarelle observed.

"I think," I said carefully, "that you're offering protection."

"And you don't trust protection from a woman who profits from theft." She sighed—not wounded, just tired, the tiredness of a merchant watching a customer hesitate over a fair price. "Emrys. I am going to tell you something I tell no one, as a show of good faith. The Regent—the crown, the throne, whatever the ballads call it—has been this guild's client longer than this guild has had a name. Longer than I've been alive. We keep the palace supplied. We keep the kingdom quiet. And we keep people like you alive, because chaos is terrible for business. Whatever that page thinks you're going to do in six days, the smartest thing you will ever do is let me burn it and continue being nobody." She rose, smoothed her coat, and came around the desk to stand over me—a small woman, and somehow the largest thing in the room. "Take tonight. Think about it. Return the ledger to my desk before the tenth bell tomorrow, no questions, no marks against your record. Or don't." She touched my shoulder, briefly, and her hand was light as a receipt. "But understand that if you don't, I will assume the guild's interests require more formal handling. And Emrys—" the smile again, the closing-a-sale smile, "—everyone is in someone's ledger. Even me. Especially me."

I walked out of the chapel with my spine straight and my mind screaming, down corridors that suddenly seemed full of eyes I had mistaken for lamps for eleven years. Past the counting-room, where clerks glanced up and glanced away too quickly. Past the archive stairs, where a junior archivist I'd known for a decade failed to meet my gaze. Past the door of the audit room itself, where someone had left the lamp burning—someone who wanted me to know they knew I'd notice.

By the time I reached my room, I had stopped shaking and started thinking, which for me is the same process running at different speeds.

Here is what I had, laid out on the bed like lots at auction: a prophecy naming me, six days out. A mistress who had just confessed—in the language of confession, which is to say denial—that the guild serves the crown's parasite, though she'd called it *client*, the way you call a leech a physician. An ultimatum with a deadline and no honest exit. And a growing, cold certainty that the empty vault of myself, the beautiful blankness I'd built my whole careful life upon, was not an absence at all.

It was a withdrawal. Something—or someone—had emptied me. And the guild had found me like that, a boy with no past, and had thought: *perfect.*

I took out the prophecy ledger from beneath a floorboard that had never actually hidden anything, because I had learned long ago that the best hiding place is the one your enemy believes you'd consider stupid. I took out the vial of borrowed snowfall, and hesitated, and put it back unopened. Borrowed warmth wasn't going to save me now. I needed my own cold.

Six days. One ledger. One guild that would hunt me the moment I chose wrong. And somewhere in my own skull, behind walls I'd never thought to audit, a locked room with my name on it—if indeed Emrys Fairweather had ever been the name, and not merely the label on an emptied jar.

I sat down at my desk, drew a fresh sheet of paper toward me, and did the only thing that has ever calmed me in my life.

I began a new ledger. One column headed *What I Know*. The other, longer by far, headed *What Has Been Taken From Me*.

Under the second column, my pen hovered a long time. Then I wrote the only entry that felt true:

*A memory that is not mine. Snow. A woman's voice, calling a name that is not Fairweather.*

And beneath it, in smaller script, because even now I could hear Sarelle's voice correcting my penmanship:

*I need to know whose it is. And I need to know before the tenth bell.*
