## 4. A Memory Not Mine

There is a particular hour—somewhere past the second bell of the night, when even the moths have given up on the lamps—when my hoard and I understand each other best.

I don't call it a vault. Vaults are for things you fear losing. This is more like a garden that grows in the dark, if gardens could be made of other people's yesterdays. I keep it behind three locks and a lie: the guild believes my private collection is modest, a hobbyist's shelf. In truth it runs deeper than Sarelle suspects, because a man who keeps accounts for thieves learns very quickly which numbers to leave off the page.

That night—the night after I'd read my own description in a century-old ledger and told no one—I went down to the hoard the way a man goes to a bottle.

* * *

Let me explain how memory-keeping works, since you're wondering, and since explaining is easier than thinking about what I found.

A memory, properly lifted, has weight. Not metaphorical weight—I mean it sits in the palm like a coin, warm at first from the living mind it came out of, cooling slowly over years until it's room-temperature and inert. First kisses are light. Griefs are heavy. The childhood of a river, which we held once, briefly, before selling it to a canal magnate who wanted to feel what he was paving over—that one took two men and a wheelbarrow.

My gift, if you want to call it that, is that I can hold them without opening them. Most handlers have to crack a memory to verify its contents, and every cracking costs a little of the shell; memories go leaky, then stale, then worthless. But I can read a memory's edge the way a moneylender bites a coin. I know what's inside without letting any of it inside me.

Mostly.

* * *

The hoard that night was arranged as always: by acquisition date, not sentiment. Sentiment is how collectors get robbed. Row upon row of small glass vials, each stoppered with wax stamped with the guild's seal or mine, each labeled in my own careful hand. *Baker's pride, 40 years proof. Widow's last summer, handle gently. A name, bottled, provenance unknown.*

I wasn't there for any of them. I was there because I couldn't sleep, and because when I can't sleep I do what lonely men do with their vices: I take a small one. Nothing grand. A stolen warmth, sipped neat.

That's the confession, since we're being honest in the dark where honesty is cheap. The keeper of the Vellum Guild's ledgers steals from his own inventory. Small things. A child's delight in a first snowfall. The smell of bread from a baker who sold us his Tuesday afternoons. Ten minutes of some stranger's contentment, sitting on a dock doing nothing. I open them just enough to feel the edges of them, and then I close them up again, and I go back to bed feeling—for an hour, sometimes two—like a person rather than a function.

It was while I was choosing that night's sip that I saw it.

* * *

At the back of the third row, in the section I think of as *miscellaneous and mildly embarrassing*, sat a vial I had no record of acquiring.

Understand: I have records. I have records of records. Every item in that hoard exists in triplicate across two ledgers and a cipher only I can read, because I am, whatever else I am, thorough. Thoroughness is the armor of invisible men. And this vial appeared in none of them.

It was smaller than most. Older—the wax had gone amber and brittle, and the glass had that faint grey tinge that memories get when they've been kept a very long time, longer than most memories survive outside a living head. My label was on it. That was the thing that stopped my breath for a moment: my handwriting, my numbering system, my wax. As if I had catalogued it myself and then, with the same hand, erased every trace of the cataloguing.

Except I hadn't. I would remember. Cataloguing is the closest thing I have to prayer.

I picked it up. It was cold. Colder than the room, colder than glass has any right to be, cold the way things are cold when they've been waiting.

And here is where I should tell you about the biting-the-coin trick, and how it failed me. Because I read its edge, the way I've read ten thousand edges, and for the first time in my career the memory read me back.

* * *

Snow.

Not the polite snow of the city, which falls already grey and apologetic. This was field snow, deep snow, snow that came up past the ankles of someone very small. I knew the height of the someone because I was seeing through their eyes, which should not have been possible, because I had not opened the vial. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left after that night: the vial was sealed, the wax unbroken, and I was *in* it anyway.

Small hands in mittens, one mitten missing so the bare hand burned pink with cold and didn't care. Snow falling so thick the world had been reduced to a white room with no walls. And breathless running, the particular staggering run of a child being chased playfully, or fleeing genuinely—it's hard to tell those apart at that age—and ahead, a shape resolving out of the white: a woman, kneeling, arms open, her hood back despite the cold, dark hair going white at the temples, and her face—

Her face I couldn't hold. Not blurred, not forgotten—*withheld*, the way a word sits on the tip of the tongue. Some part of me flinched away from it, and I had the sick certainty that the flinching was trained into me, installed like a lock on a door I didn't know I had.

And she was calling a name. Calling it over and over into the snow, laughing and crying at once, the way women in memories do when the thing they feared has come home:

"*Corvyn.* Corvyn! *Corvyn, slow down—*"

The name hit me like cold water and I was out of the memory, standing in my own hoard with the vial clenched so hard the glass bit crescents into my palm, my heart going like a debtor's knock.

Corvyn.

Not Fairweather. Not Emrys Fairweather, keeper of accounts, unwritten man, blank page, invisible. Corvyn. A name in the snow, called by a woman whose face I couldn't look at, in a memory I had never acquired, catalogued in my own hand, kept in my own vault, older—older, I realized as my professional instincts did their grim arithmetic from the wax and the greying of the glass—older than the earliest thing I remember.

I remember the guild. I remember being perhaps seven, maybe eight, arriving with no history and a head full of fog, and Sarelle saying I had a talent and the guild having use for talents. Everything before that is the smooth grey nothing I built my whole self on top of. *Unwritten*, I called it. I'd made it a philosophy. You can't steal from a man who owns nothing; you can't blackmail a man with no past. I had turned my emptiness into a fortress and lived in it for twenty years, smug as a miser.

And the whole time, apparently, there had been a door in the fortress wall. And something had been kept behind it. By me. From me.

* * *

I sat down on the floor among my stolen goods and did what I do best: I audited.

If the vial was in my hoard, someone put it there. If it bore my mark, either I marked it—which I hadn't—or someone forged my mark, which means someone with access, which means the guild, which means Sarelle, or someone above Sarelle, which is a sentence I don't like finishing. If the memory predates my arrival, then it isn't foreign at all, is it? It's mine. It's the oldest thing I own, and someone locked it away from me and let me believe I had nothing.

Or—and here's the thought I kept circling like a tongue probing a broken tooth—if the memory isn't mine, if it belongs to some other child named Corvyn, then it was *installed*. Placed in my head or my hoard deliberately, along with everything else I call my early years. And if one memory can be planted, then the whole grey nothing before age seven stops being an absence and becomes a construction. A wall, built by someone, for a reason.

You see the vertigo of it. A man finds one counterfeit coin in his life's savings and suddenly every coin is suspect. I have spent my career handling other people's pasts, weighing them, pricing them, never once asking whether my own had passed through someone else's hands first.

And underneath the vertigo, quieter and worse: the prophecy. *The keeper of accounts shall open the crown.* Written a hundred years ago. Describing me. And now a memory surfacing from before my memory, bearing a name I'd never heard and a woman reaching for me through falling snow.

Coincidences are entries that haven't been reconciled yet. That's the first rule of my trade. Somewhere there is a ledger where all of this balances. I just wasn't holding it.

* * *

I did three things before dawn.

First, I copied the vial's position, dimensions, and condition into my private cipher, in case it vanished—which, I suspected, it soon would.

Second, I tried to open it fully. Properly. Wax cracked, memory poured out, the whole truth of it. I got as far as pressing my thumbnail to the wax seal, and my hand stopped. Not hesitated—*stopped*, the way a limb goes absent when the nerve is cut. I stood there commanding my own fingers and they ignored me with the serene obedience of well-made tools. Whatever lock is on that memory, it isn't on the vial. The vial is just where someone stored the key.

Third, I wrapped the vial in oilcloth and moved it out of the hoard entirely, into a hiding place I won't describe, because I have learned in the last two days that hiding places are only as safe as the number of people who don't know about them.

Then I climbed back up into my narrow room above the counting-house, lay down on my cot, and stared at the ceiling beams while the city woke around me, cart-wheels and gulls and the milk-bells, all of it proceeding as though the world were still a place where Emrys Fairweather knew what he was.

Fairweather. I said the name over and over, testing it, and for the first time in twenty years it sounded like what it had always been: a label on a jar. Fair weather. Clear skies. Nothing written on the wind.

Somewhere out there was snow, and a woman, and a boy called Corvyn who ran toward her with one mitten off.

And somewhere closer—downstairs, behind a door I'd walked past ten thousand times—was whoever had decided I shouldn't remember any of it.

I was still staring at the ceiling when the knock came. Three raps, evenly spaced. Sarelle's knock.

She never comes upstairs.

"Emrys," her voice said through the door, pleasant as a knife laid flat on a table. "We need to talk about your family."
