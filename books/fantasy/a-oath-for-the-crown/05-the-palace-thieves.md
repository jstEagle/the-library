## 5. The Palace Thieves

There are three kinds of jobs the Vellum Guild offers you. The first kind pays well and costs little: a merchant's memory of a debt, a widow's grief decanted into glass so she can sleep at night, that sort of gentle larceny. The second kind pays poorly and costs much, and you take it anyway because Sarelle asks with that particular softness in her voice that is not kindness but arithmetic. The third kind is not offered at all. It is simply placed on your desk, already signed, already dated, and you understand that the word *offer* is being used in the way a knife *offers* itself to a throat.

The contract on my desk that morning was the third kind.

*Acquisition from the Royal Vault of Vhalen. One item. Keeper of accounts to perform extraction personally. Discretion absolute.*

No client named. No fee listed. No reason given for why it had to be me — though I could guess at the reason, because I had spent three nights failing to burn a certain prophecy ledger out of my own memory, and the guild had eyes everywhere, including, I suspected, inside my own skull. Someone had read that page before I could hide it. Someone had decided that the man described as *the keeper of accounts who shall open the crown* ought to be sent, as a joke or a test or a leash-tug, to stand inside the crown's actual house.

I told myself it was coincidence. I have always been good at telling myself things. It is, after all, most of what a ledger-keeper does: we write down what happened until the writing becomes more real than the happening, and then we believe the writing.

* * *

Sarelle received me in the map room, which is what she calls the long chamber on the guild's third floor where no maps hang. Instead there are shelves of bottled memories arranged by year of theft, each vial labeled in her own hand, each one a small crime she has never once lost sleep over. She stood among them like a woman standing in a garden she'd poisoned personally and was rather proud of.

"You'll want to review the approach documents," she said, without looking up from whatever she was writing. "The vault sits beneath the east wing. The wards are keyed to royal blood, which means you won't be picking any locks. You'll be going in through the servants' passage behind the laundry court, and you'll be going in during the vigil."

"The vigil," I repeated. "The night the king prays alone."

"The night the king is *alone*," Sarelle said, and something in the way she said it made the back of my neck prickle. "Which he insists upon. Every month, same date, since his coronation. No guards within three doors. The palace staff think it's piety." She finally looked up, and her eyes were the color of old varnish, warm and impenetrable. "You'll have four hours. Take only what's listed. Touch nothing else. And Emrys—" she smiled, and I felt the smile land on me like a hand on my shoulder, friendly and heavy, "—do try to come back. You're the only auditor I trust with the deep ledgers, and replacing you would be tedious."

It occurred to me, walking out, that this was the closest thing to affection the guild had ever shown me, and that it arrived attached to an order that might get me killed. That is how love works in the Vellum Guild. It is always stapled to something sharp.

* * *

I spent two days preparing, because preparation is the only prayer I know. I memorized the laundry court's rotation of guards. I learned that the east wing's third corridor smelled of beeswax on even days and lamp oil on odd ones, which meant the wardens walked it in pairs on evens and alone on odds, and alone was better. I bought a servant's coat from a tailor who asked no questions because I overpaid him in the one currency that never needs laundering: cash, and a small stolen memory of his first love returned to him unasked, which made him weep into his measuring tape and forget my face entirely.

That last part is the gift, if you want to call it that. Most thieves carry lockpicks. I carry other people's yesterdays. A memory, lifted gently, leaves a hole shaped exactly like itself, and people will walk around a hole in their evening rather than examine it. It is a clean way to move through the world. It is also, if you do it often enough, a lonely one, because you end up carrying everyone's best moments and keeping none of your own. But loneliness is a trade secret. You don't discuss it. You just file it under operating costs.

On the appointed night I went over the wall in the laundry cart, as planned, wearing the coat of a man who was at that moment happily reliving a girl named Marta and a summer that smelled of cut grass. The palace at night is a different animal than the palace by day. By day it performs itself — banners, guards, the whole theater of gold. By night it breathes. The stones sweat cold. The portraits watch you with the specific resentment of painted men who were flattered in life and abandoned in pigment. I moved through the breathing dark counting corridors, counting smells, counting my own heartbeats the way I count entries in a ledger, because numbers are a rope and I have never trusted a room I couldn't count my way out of.

The servants' passage behind the laundry court was exactly where the documents said, which unsettled me more than if it hadn't been. Somebody had done this before. Somebody had written these instructions with the confidence of repetition. I filed that thought away in the drawer where I keep thoughts I can't afford to open yet.

Three doors down from the vault stair, the corridor opened into a gallery, and the gallery was where I heard the voice.

* * *

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have ever had, and because what happened next has refused every attempt I've made to file it neatly.

The king's private chapel sat off the gallery, its doors half-open, candlelight spilling across the floor in a long gold tongue. I should have kept moving. Every professional instinct I possess said *keep moving*. But the voice stopped me — not because it was loud, but because it was wrong. It came from the chapel, and it had the cadence of conversation, the rise and fall of a man talking with someone, except that between each of the king's lines there was a pause, a listening pause, and nothing in the room answered. Nothing I could hear.

I did the stupid thing. I am writing this account, so clearly I survived it, but let the record show I did the stupid thing, which was to look.

King Aldric of Vhalen knelt at his own altar, and he was talking to empty air.

He was not an old man — forty, perhaps, with the brown hair and broad hands of a man who'd once been athletic and now merely stood still a great deal. He wore no crown. He wore a plain wool robe, the kind penitents wear, and he knelt on bare stone, and his face was wet, and he was speaking in a low, urgent, reasonable tone, the tone of a man negotiating with a creditor.

"—and I have given you thirty years," he was saying. "Thirty years, faithfully. You said if I kept the schedule, if I never missed a vigil, you would leave her alone. You gave your word. Whatever you are, you keep ledgers too — I know you do, I have *seen* them — and a ledger is a promise, isn't it? An entry is an entry."

The pause. The listening pause. And then the king laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound I have ever heard, because it wasn't his laugh. I don't know how I knew that. I had never heard King Aldric laugh before in my life; I'd barely seen him, a figure on a balcony at coronation parades, a name in acquisition manifests. But I knew, the way you know a forged signature when you've handled enough genuine ones. The laugh had another man's shape. It had *centuries'* worth of another man's shape, the way a coin worn smooth by ten thousand hands stops belonging to the face stamped on it.

"Then take tonight's portion and be satisfied," the king said, in that borrowed voice, and bowed his head, and closed his eyes, and went silent, and began, very slightly, to tremble.

I stood in the gallery with my hand pressed flat against cold stone and watched something feed on a king.

I cannot describe it better than that, and I have tried. There was no light, no smoke, no theatrical horror. There was only the trembling, and the way the candle flames leaned toward him — *toward* him, against the draft, all of them, like iron filings toward a magnet — and the way the shadows in the chapel gathered and thickened as though the darkness itself were drinking. And underneath it, faint as a second heartbeat, I felt it. My gift, my curse, the sense that lets me feel memories the way a jeweler feels the weight of stones through a cloth. Something in that chapel was *full*. Horribly, gluttonously full, stuffed with lifetimes, and it was still eating.

And then the king raised his head, and looked directly at the empty air beside the altar, and said, in a voice that was suddenly, perfectly calm, perfectly articulate, and absolutely not his own:

"He's here."

The candles went out. All of them, at once, like held breath released.

I ran. I am not ashamed of it; shame is a luxury for men whose legs work better than their judgment. I went down the vault stair on muscle memory and terror, took the acquisition — a slim black case, unlisted contents, sealed with the royal sigil, and I did not look inside it because looking inside things is how auditors die — and I went out the way I'd come in, through the laundry court, over the wall, into the city's indifferent smoky dark, and I did not stop running until I was three districts away and my lungs had filed a formal complaint.

* * *

I sat in my rooms above the counting-house until dawn, with the black case locked in my floor safe and my hands wrapped around a cup of tea I never drank, replaying it.

*He's here.*

Two possibilities. The first: coincidence. The king, mid-vigil, had sensed a thief in his gallery the way animals sense weather, and had announced it to whatever confessor or spirit he believed attended him. Possible. Uncomfortable, but possible.

The second possibility was worse, and it fit the facts like a key fits the lock it was cut for. The thing in that chapel — the thing the king negotiated with, scheduled with, fed on a monthly calendar like a debt with a due date — had known I was there. Had known precisely, had said *he's here* with the mild satisfaction of a clerk noting a delivery. Not *someone's here.* *He's* here. As if I were expected. As if I were an entry in someone's ledger, filed under a date I was beginning to suspect I'd read before, in ink that had been dry for a hundred years.

*The keeper of accounts shall open the crown.*

I had told myself the prophecy was a forgery, a guild trick, a test of my discretion. Sitting there in the grey light with the tea going cold, I found the story wouldn't hold anymore. Forgeries are meant to be found. Tests conclude. This felt less like a test and more like a summons — and I had just answered it, walked into the crown's own house, stood in its chapel, and been *counted*.

And there was a third thing, smaller and stranger, that I kept turning over like a coin of uncertain mint. When the creature spoke through the king's mouth, some animal cellar of my mind had recognized the timbre. Not the words — the timbre. The way it layered voices, centuries of them, kings stacked inside kings. It reminded me of something. Of the hoard. Of the private shelf in my own head where I keep the memories I've lifted over the years, and in particular of the one memory I have never been able to place — snow, and a woman's voice calling a name that isn't Fairweather, older than any recollection I own, sitting in my skull like a stone that predates the house built around it.

For one vertiginous moment in that chapel, listening to a dead-sounding voice pour out of a living king, I had almost heard — beneath the centuries, beneath the hunger — an echo of that woman's voice. Calling that name.

That's impossible. I know it's impossible. Things do not echo backward across a lifetime into a stolen scrap of snow. Memory flows one direction, like water, like debt.

But I am a keeper of accounts, and I know what it means when the numbers refuse to balance. It doesn't mean the arithmetic is broken. It means somewhere, in some column you haven't checked, there is an entry you don't know about.

By morning I had made two decisions. The first: I would find out what was in the black case, contract or no contract, seal or no seal.

The second: I would go to the forbidden archives, down in the guild's oldest levels, where the ledgers nobody audits are kept — and I would look up what, exactly, has been feeding on the kings of Vhalen for the last three hundred years.

Because I had seen the thing behind the king's eyes. And the terrible, ledger-balancing truth of it was this:

I think it had seen mine.
