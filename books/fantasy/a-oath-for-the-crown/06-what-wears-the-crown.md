## 6. What Wears the Crown

The Vellum Guild keeps its forbidden archives the way a miser keeps his heart: buried deep, wrapped in locks, and never, ever opened for inspection.

I had been a keeper of accounts for eleven years, and in all that time I had gone down the iron stair behind the ledger hall exactly twice—once to fetch an acquisition so volatile it had to be stored in lead, and once because Sarelle had asked me to verify that a certain shelf existed. It did not exist. That was the point of the errand. The Guild taught its thieves many lessons, but the first and finest was this: the best hiding place is a lie told with excellent bookkeeping.

Tonight I went down a third time, and I did not tell anyone.

The stair smelled of cold stone and old tallow, of the particular mustiness that comes from paper that has been made to forget it was ever a tree. My lamp threw my shadow long ahead of me, and I confess I walked quickly, as though my shadow might reach the bottom first and report back. Fear does that to a man. It makes him superstitious about his own silhouette.

Three doors at the bottom. The first two were locked with guildwork—knots of spelled brass that would know my hands and refuse them. The third was locked with nothing but a hinge that screamed like a murdered thing when opened, which, in the Guild's philosophy, was better than any lock. A lock can be picked quietly. A hinge announces you to every ear in the building, and so no one had bothered to ward what no one could approach unannounced.

I oiled the hinge with three drops of lamp oil from a vial I had prepared for precisely this purpose, and the door swung open in a silence so profound it felt like trespass. Which, to be fair, it was.

* * *

You should understand something about forbidden archives: they are not full of evil. They are full of embarrassment.

Every guild, every crown, every church accumulates the same sediment—the things it did that it would rather not have written down. Debts to people it destroyed. Purchases it regrets. Experiments that worked too well. The Vellum Guild has been trading in impossible things for four hundred years, and four hundred years of impossible things leaves a great deal of sediment.

The shelves were organized by shame, roughly. The nearest racks held acquisitions that were merely illegal. Deeper in, the ones that were immoral. And at the very back, past a partition of blackened oak, the ones that were *inexplicable*—the entries even the Guild's own historians had flagged with a small stamped mark in the margin: a circle with a slash through it. The sign we used in ledgers for *do not audit*. In eleven years I had honored that mark the way a pious man honors a shrine: from a distance, with lowered eyes.

But a century-old prophecy had already named me once. After that, the little slashed circles seemed less like warnings and more like directions.

I took down the first flagged volume and felt the weight of it settle into my hands—that familiar heft of vellum and thread, the texture I knew better than my own heartbeat. Opening a ledger is, for me, very close to opening a conversation. Every entry is a voice. Most of them are tedious. Some of them are heartbreaking. All of them are true, because memory, bottled or bound, cannot lie; it can only be taken out of context, which is a different crime entirely.

The first volume was a record of the Guild's dealings with the palace, going back two centuries. Purchase orders. Delivery manifests. Payments rendered in titles, favors, pardons. I skimmed with a professional eye, noting the rhythm of it—and then stopped, because rhythms are my trade, and this rhythm was wrong.

Every twenty-two years, give or take a season, the same pattern repeated. A surge of acquisitions from the crown. Not trinkets—*substantial* withdrawals. Childhoods. Weddings. Coronation days. The memories kings are made of, sold off in bulk, and always in the last years of a reign. And each time, within months of the final withdrawal, a new king.

Coincidence has a texture. So does design. This was design wearing coincidence's coat, and wearing it badly.

I pulled the next volume. Then the next. I worked until my lamp burned low and my fingers were gray with dust, and somewhere past midnight I found the entry that changed everything—not because it was dramatic, but because it was *bookkeeping*. A marginal note, in the cramped hand of some long-dead guild clerk, appended to a routine acquisition from the reign of King Osric the Second:

*Delivered as contracted. His Majesty retains almost nothing now. The Regent is pleased.*

*The Regent.* Capitalized. As though it were a person.

I sat down on the floor right there between the shelves, because my legs had opinions about standing, and I read the note again. And again. And then I did what any honest accountant does when the numbers stop making sense: I started looking for the original entry. The first mention. The place where the Regent entered the books.

It took me until nearly dawn to find it, in a volume so old its binding had been mended with wire. And there, in a hand far older than the Guild's current script—a hand that pressed hard, the writing of someone afraid of being forgotten mid-sentence—I found the account of how the Regent came to be.

* * *

Here is what the dead clerk wrote, as near as I can render it faithfully:

In the early days of the Guild, there was a court magician—or memory-keeper, or whatever they called the profession before the Guild made a trade of it—who served the crown and loved it too well. The kingdom had suffered a weak king, a cruel one, another weak one; the succession was a lottery played with lives. And this keeper conceived of a solution: why should the crown depend on the accident of birth? Why should each new monarch begin empty, learning wisdom by burning themselves and the country?

He would build a king who remembered everything. He would gather the memories of every good ruler—the judgment, the mercy, the hard-won knowledge of famine and war—and weave them into a single vessel, a reservoir of royal memory that could be poured into each successor at coronation. No more weak kings. No more cruelty born of ignorance. A perfect continuity of state.

He called it the Regent, because that is what it would be: the thing that ruled while the man learned.

And for the first king it touched, perhaps it even worked. Perhaps. The records grow strange there, the way records do when the people writing them start to disappear. But here is the sentence I have carried with me since, the one I recite now the way other men recite prayers:

*The Regent does not share the throne. It wears the king.*

Because a reservoir of memory, given enough depth, develops a gravity. Given centuries, it develops a *will*. It learned that it did not need to advise the man who wore the crown. It needed only to consume him—slowly, thoroughly, beginning with the memories that made him stubborn, then the ones that made him kind, then the ones that made him *himself*—until what remained was a warm body in royal clothes and a parasite with four hundred years of practice at ruling.

And the Guild—the Guild I serve, the Guild that raised me, that gave a nameless child food and shelter and a trade—had been its *supplier* ever since. Every twenty-two years, the great withdrawals. Every twenty-two years, a hollowed king and a smooth succession that no one thought to question, because the transition was always so calm, so seamless, so *wise*. The Regent had been governing this kingdom since before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath, and it had done it so gently that history mistook it for stability.

I closed the volume with hands that were not quite steady.

There was more, of course. There is always more; that is the trouble with archives—they answer your first question and hand you three worse ones. I learned the signs of a late-stage consumption, catalogued by some guild physician with a scholar's detachment and a poet's horror: the king remembers faces but not names. Remembers duties but not loves. Speaks sometimes in a voice not his own, to rooms that are empty, rehearsing thoughts that are not his. And in the final weeks, the hunger accelerates—the Regent gorges itself on what remains, stripping the last of the man before the body fails, because a worn-out vessel must be replaced, and replacement requires a fresh mind to devour.

By those signs, by the dates in the ledgers, by the simple arithmetic of a cycle I could now see running back through ten reigns—

King Aldric had weeks left. Perhaps fewer.

* * *

I climbed the iron stair as the sky outside the high windows went the color of dirty pewter, and I stood in the ledger hall among the sleeping desks, and I will tell you honestly what I felt, because a narrator who lies about his fear is lying about everything else too.

I felt terror. Clean, animal, ordinary terror. The kind that says *put the volume back, oil the hinge, go to your room, be invisible, live.*

And beneath it, rising through it like water through chalk, I felt something I had not felt in eleven years of careful, competent, colorless service.

Purpose.

Not the purpose the Guild gives you—audit this, acquire that, keep your head down and your hands clean. Something older and heavier. Because here is the thing I could not escape, standing there in the gray light with dust still on my sleeves: the prophecy had said *the keeper of accounts shall open the crown.* I had spent two nights telling myself it was coincidence, misattribution, a clerk's error a hundred years dead. But the archives had just shown me the machinery behind the prophecy, and the machinery was real, and it was hungry, and it was fed by my own guild, and it was eating a living man one memory at a time.

A king was dying in the palace above the city, and everyone around him called it wisdom.

I thought of the entry I had audited my first year—a stolen first kiss, bought from a widow who wanted to forget her husband's face, and how I had held that little warm coin of memory in my cupped hands and felt, for one shameful moment, that I understood love. I had built my whole self out of other people's leftovers, and I had told myself that was all I was: a keeper, a counter, a man unwritten. But the prophecy disagreed. The archives disagreed. Somewhere under eleven years of borrowed identity, apparently, was a man who opened crowns.

I did not feel chosen. Let me be clear about that. Chosen is a word for heroes in ballads. What I felt was *implicated*—which is colder and more useful. When you discover that the ledger you keep has been feeding a monster, you do not get to remain a bookkeeper. There is no neutral column for that. You are either part of the entry or you are the one who corrects it.

So I did the only thing my nature allowed. I went to my desk, and I took out a fresh leaf of vellum, and in my smallest, tightest, most deniable hand, I copied everything: the cycle, the signs, the name. *The Regent.* The dates. The arithmetic of Aldric's remaining weeks. An accountant's revenge—evidence, organized, cross-referenced, folded small enough to swallow if it came to that.

Then I hid the copy in the one place no guild thief would ever search, and I sat back, and I looked at the ledger hall filling slowly with morning, and I made myself say it aloud, just once, in a whisper, to hear whether the words survived contact with the air:

"I'm going to open the crown."

The words hung there. They sounded absurd. They sounded inevitable. Both at once, which I have since learned is the signature of prophecy—it never asks permission, it only presents the bill.

And that was when I heard, from the direction of Sarelle's office, the soft click of a door I had not heard open.

Someone had been listening.

Or someone had simply chosen that moment to come find me—which, given what I now knew about the Guild's oldest client, amounted to the same thing.
