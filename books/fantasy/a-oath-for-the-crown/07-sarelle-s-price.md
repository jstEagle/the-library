## 7. Sarelle's Price

She didn't knock. Sarelle never knocked; knocking, she once told me over a glass of wine neither of us drank, is an admission that the person on the other side of the door has a right to be surprised.

So she simply appeared in the doorway of the archive, and the lamplight behind her made a silhouette of her—tall, silver-streaked, wrapped in that grey coat she wore the way other people wore armor—and for one absurd heartbeat I considered shoving the forbidden folio back into its shelf and pretending I'd been doing inventory all along.

I didn't, because Sarelle would have known. Sarelle always knew. That was rather the point of her.

"Emrys," she said, in the tone she used for ledgers that wouldn't balance. "Walk with me."

"I was just—"

"You were just reading something you were not assigned to read." She turned before I could finish lying to her, which was a mercy, because I hadn't yet decided which lie to use. "Bring it. You'll want to throw it at me later, and I'd hate for you to be unarmed."

* * *

The Guildhouse at night is a different animal than the Guildhouse by day. By day it's clerks and couriers, apprentices haggling over vials, the smell of ink and beeswax and the faint copper tang of memories too fresh to bottle cleanly. By night it goes quiet in a way that isn't peace—it's digestion. The building settles around its secrets like a fat man around his dinner.

Sarelle walked me through it without hurry. Past the Vault of Small Sorrows, where we keep the griefs nobody wanted. Past the Long Gallery, where the bottled names hang in their velvet cradles, each one humming faintly if you press your ear close enough—a name, after all, is just a memory compressed until it sings. She walked like a woman giving a tour of her own funeral procession, and I followed like the good little keeper of accounts I had spent eleven years training myself to be.

She stopped at last in her office, poured two glasses of the wine we never drank, and set one in front of me.

"Sit down, Emrys."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would." She sat herself, unhurried, and folded her hands. "You found the Vellum Prophecy. Page forty-one of the Corvane acquisition, misfiled under agricultural contracts—which, incidentally, was not a mistake. I filed it there myself, thirty years ago, so that no one would ever find it."

The floor did something subtle and unpleasant beneath my chair.

"You knew about it," I said. "All this time."

"I knew about everything in this building. That is my function. The Guildmistress does not trade in memories, Emrys. She trades in knowing where they are." She took a sip of wine, watching me over the rim. "What I did not know—what I have spent three sleepless nights confirming—is that you would find it anyway. Which tells me something about you that I confess I had hoped never to learn."

"And what's that?"

"That you are your mother's son after all."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened inside me when she said that. Not the dramatic version—the real one, the one that actually occurred, which was much smaller and much worse.

There was no thunderclap. There was just a click, very quiet, somewhere behind my ribs. The same click as a ledger closing. Because I understood, in that instant, that Sarelle had known who I was since the day she pulled a half-starved boy off the streets and taught him to hold memories like coins. Eleven years of kindness, recalculated in a single sentence: it hadn't been kindness. It had been custody.

"You knew my mother," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"I knew of her. Court memory-keeper. A line of them, going back four hundred years—the Fairweathers were only ever a name we gave you, by the way. A placeholder. Something to write in the intake ledger." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. Gentle was how you talked to someone whose cage you were describing. "Your mother hid you from the crown, Emrys. From what wears the crown. And then she hid you from us, which took considerably more skill, and succeeded for considerably less time."

"The Regent." I said the word and watched her face. Nothing moved in it. Twenty years of practice, presumably. "You know what it is."

"I know what it *is*." She set down her glass. "Do you? Or do you know what the archives say it is? Because the archives say parasite, and monster, and devourer of kings, and every word of that is true the way a knife's description is true. It omits the function."

"And what's the function?"

"Stability." She leaned forward. "Emrys. Sit down properly. Listen to me as a bookkeeper, because this is a bookkeeping problem, and you above all people will understand it when I put it in your language."

I sat. Not because she asked. Because some treacherous clerk-part of my brain genuinely wanted the numbers.

"A reign is a memory," she said. "That's all a kingdom is—one long shared recollection, held together by a single head. And heads fail. Kings forget treaties. Queens misremember borders. Every succession is a wound, every coronation an amputation. Except—" she tapped the desk, once, "—when the succession doesn't happen. When the same king endures, decade after decade, consistent, stable, remembered exactly as he was yesterday. Do you know why Aldric has reigned thirty-one years without a single civil war?"

"Because he's been eaten alive from the inside out."

"Because the realm has been *cared for*," she said, with the first flash of genuine heat I'd ever seen in her. "The Regent is not a squatter, Emrys. It is the oldest continuous administration in the Nine Kingdoms. It remembers every treaty ever signed, every debt ever owed, every border ever drawn. It cannot be bribed, because it owns the bribes. It cannot die, so it cannot be inherited. In exchange, it takes payment. Royal memory. Only royal memory. It has never once taken a peasant's childhood or a merchant's wedding day. Its appetite is narrow, disciplined, and *contractual*. And do you know who brokered that contract?"

The answer arrived in my stomach before she said it.

"The Vellum Guild."

"The Vellum Guild," she confirmed. "Four hundred years ago. Our founding client. Our founding *debt*. Everything this Guild is—the vaults, the ledgers, the trade itself—exists because the first Guildmistress learned to bottle memory in order to feed a thing in the palace. We are not thieves who stumbled into a monster, Emrys. We are a larder that grew a front door."

I looked at my hands. Clerk's hands. Thief's hands. Larder's hands.

"So when you tell me," Sarelle went on, quieter now, gentler again, which was the worst register she owned, "that you intend to open the crown—to unbind the oldest contract this Guild holds, to collapse the only stable administration the Nine Kingdoms have ever known, and to hand the realm back to the chaos of actual human kings who forget things—you understand that I cannot permit it. Not won't. *Cannot.* There are signatures older than nations on that contract. Mine among them."

"Then let me ask you a bookkeeping question," I said. "When the Regent finishes eating Aldric—weeks, the archives say—what happens to the contract? Does it move to Ilsette? Does it wait for the next heir? Or does it start taking payment from people who aren't royal?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind with weight.

"You've read further than I thought," she said finally.

"I'm a keeper of accounts, Sarelle. I notice when the columns don't balance. Thirty-one years of feeding, and the entries are getting bigger. The withdrawals are accelerating. Whatever discipline it had—" I stood up, and my chair scraped loud in the quiet, "—it's losing. And when a debtor starts missing payments, the creditor comes for collateral. You're not protecting the realm. You're protecting a loan that's about to default on everyone."

Something crossed her face then. Fast. Gone before I could name it—but I've spent my life reading faces the way I read ledgers, and I'd swear on every forged entry in this building that it was fear.

"That," she said, "is precisely why you must stop."

* * *

Here is the thing about betrayal: it rarely arrives looking like betrayal. It arrives looking like arithmetic.

Sarelle rose too, and when she spoke again, the warmth was gone—not replaced by cruelty, but by something more frightening: procedure.

"If you go to the palace, the Guild will know within the hour. If you attempt the rite—I know you've been reading about the rite, Emrys, don't look surprised, I told you I know where everything is—if you attempt it, you will be committing theft against our senior client. And the Guild protects its clients."

"I've been the Guild for eleven years. Who protects me?"

"Nobody," she said, without a flicker. "Nobody protected you when I found you, either. I did. And I am telling you, as the person who has kept you alive for eleven years, that this is the one account you cannot open." She came around the desk and stood close, and for a moment—just a moment—the warden slipped and something older looked out of her eyes. Something tired. "You think I don't know what it costs, being unwritten. Being a blank page everyone else fills in. I chose you, Emrys. Out of every thief in this city, I chose the empty one, and I told myself it was mercy."

"And what was it?"

"A deposit," she said softly. "And now I'm asking you not to withdraw it."

Then she straightened, and the moment closed over like water.

"Go home. Sleep. Tomorrow the Corvane folio returns to page forty-one's shelf, misfiled, forgotten, and you return to your ledgers. If you do this, the Guild's response will not be personal. It will be procedural. Every thief in this city owes us, Emrys. Every one of them carries our marks and our debts. Defy the contract, and you will learn what it means to owe the Vellum Guild and not pay."

"And if I already know what that means?" I asked. "If I've been keeping those accounts for eleven years? If I know exactly what happens to people who end up as entries instead of names?"

For the first time all night, Sarelle looked genuinely sad.

"Then you'll forgive me," she said, "for having taught you so well."

* * *

I walked home through the rain, which in Vellamarch in autumn is less weather than atmosphere—a grey drizzle that gets into everything, including, apparently, eleven years of assumed loyalty.

I should have been afraid. Part of me was; I want to be honest about that. The part of me that survived the streets knows the exact weight of being hunted, and it woke up tonight and started counting exits.

But underneath the fear, something else was settling into place. Cold. Clear. Almost relieved.

Because here is what I realized somewhere between the Guildhouse and the bridge: Sarelle had never once lied to me. Not tonight. Every threat was real, every warning accurate, every number correct. She had simply spent eleven years being honest about everything except the one thing that mattered—that the woman who saved me was also the woman who shelved the prophecy where no one would find it, and filed me the same way.

Invisible things aren't safe, she'd told me once, my first year, when I asked why we stole. Invisible things are just things nobody's checked on lately.

I'd thought she meant the memories.

I reached my rooms, lit the lamp, and pulled the false bottom from my writing desk. My hoard sat there in its little rows—snow, a woman's voice, a name that wasn't Fairweather, and all the small bright stolen things I'd collected to convince myself I had a self worth protecting.

Tomorrow they would come for me. Or the day after. Sarelle was many things, but she was never arbitrary; she'd give me one night to obey, because she'd want to be wrong about me. She'd want the deposit to hold.

I took out the snow-memory and held it in my palm until it warmed.

One night, then. One night to find Wren, or the princess, or the rite, or God—or failing all of those, one night to decide exactly how much of myself I was willing to spend to unmake a thing that had been eating kings since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to write.

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as a creditor.

And somewhere across the sleeping city, I knew, a ledger was already open, and a fresh line was being ruled across a clean page, and a clerk with steady hands was writing the date—

—and then my name.
