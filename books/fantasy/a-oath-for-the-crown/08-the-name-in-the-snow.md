## 8. The Name in the Snow

The memory had snow in it. That was the first thing I trusted about it.

Not the woman's voice, which called a name that wasn't mine and made my chest ache every time I held it up to the light of my attention. Not the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool. Not the blue door with the chipped paint. Memories can lie about all of that—they're soft things, easily bent by whoever carries them. But snow is honest. Snow falls when it falls, and it fell in that memory in fat, unhurried flakes, the kind you only get in the high valleys north of the city where the air is too cold to be in a hurry about anything.

So I went north.

I told myself it was research. That's what a keeper of accounts tells himself when he does something reckless: *this is an audit*. I was auditing my own skull, thank you very much, and if the books didn't balance, well, that was hardly my fault. I'd been keeping Emrys Fairweather's ledger for twenty years and only now discovered there were entries in it written in someone else's hand.

The coach cost me three bottled memories from my hoard—a merchant's first taste of honey wine, a soldier's memory of his mother's lullaby, and one truly regrettable entry involving a duchess and a stableboy that I sold without examining too closely. The guild taught me that memories are currency, but it never taught me how it would feel to spend pieces of other people's lives on a stagecoach ticket. Answer: like picking your own pocket. You arrive richer in miles and poorer in everything else.

* * *

The valley was called Hallowmere, according to the driver, who pronounced it as though the name had personally wronged him. "Nothing down there but sheep and old grudges," he said. "You got family?"

"Something like that," I said, which was either the truest or falsest thing I'd said all year.

The village resolved out of the mist the way memories do—gradually, then all at once, then more real than you were prepared for. Stone cottages with slate roofs furred green. A stream running quick and loud through the middle of everything. And doors. Doors everywhere, painted every color grief and stubbornness could produce, because in Hallowmere, the driver told me, they painted their doors bright so the dead could find their way home and wouldn't take it personally if they couldn't.

I walked past forty of them before I saw the blue one.

Chipped paint. The chip shaped, if you squinted, like a bird mid-flight. My hands started shaking so badly I had to put them in my pockets like a man who simply found the cold bracing rather than a man whose entire architecture of self was developing cracks.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being raised by thieves: they give you rules instead of roots. *Never carry anything you can't drop. Never sleep twice in the same bed. Never let them see your face long enough to remember it.* I had followed those rules so faithfully that I had become a rumor wearing a coat. And now here was a door—a stupid, chipped, blue door—and it remembered me when I had spent two decades making sure nothing did.

I knocked. Then I stood there composing excuses. I'm a tinker. I'm a tax assessor. I've lost my goat. All lies with good bones.

The door opened on a small woman, ancient in the way mountains are ancient—not frail, just patient, weathered into her final and most honest shape. Her hair was white as the snow in my stolen memory. Her eyes went to my face, and I watched something move across her expression like weather: confusion, then disbelief, then a grief so old it had fossilized into tenderness.

"You have her chin," she said.

"I—" Every prepared excuse evaporated. "I'm sorry, madam, I think you have me confused with—"

"Emrys." She said it quietly, the way you say a word you've kept in a drawer for twenty years and are afraid will crumble if you handle it roughly. Not Fairweather. Just *Emrys*, plain, the way you'd say it to a child. "Your mother used to say it meant something in the old tongue. *Beloved of the last light.* She thought that was funny. She said names should be modest."

I did not remember walking inside. I have a gap in my recollection between the doorstep and a chair by her fire, a cup of something hot and bitter in my hands, and the old woman—Maren, she said her name was, Maren Ostwick—sitting across from me with her hands folded like a woman who has rehearsed this conversation for decades and is determined not to rush it now that it has finally arrived.

* * *

"Your mother was Lirien," Maren said. "Lirien Vael. Court memory-keeper, third rank, which sounds humble until you learn there were only ever three ranks and the first two were ceremonial." She smiled at some private joke between herself and the dead. "She came north every autumn. Officially, to catalog the village's dying traditions—the guild loved a tradition it could bottle and sell back to us. Unofficially, she came to breathe. The palace air, she said, was full of other people's yesterdays. You couldn't hear yourself think in it."

"And my father?"

"A court man too. An archivist. He stayed behind the winter you were born." Her face did something complicated. "The last king was failing, even then. Everyone felt it, though no one had the word for it yet. The king would look at his own daughter and ask who she was. Politely. Like a stranger at a funeral asking after the widow."

The fire popped. Outside, actual snow had begun to fall, matching the memory flake for flake, and I found that almost unbearable—the world confirming itself against the thing in my head, the thing in my head confirming itself against the world.

"She hid you," Maren said. "That's the short version, and she'd hate it, because Lirien never did anything short. She spent two years preparing. Two years of quiet work, moving memories around the palace like a woman rearranging furniture so no one notices the silver's gone. When the Regent—" she used the word plainly, and I flinched, and she noticed, and continued gently, "—when it began taking the king's memories in earnest, it grew careless. Greedy. It reached for anyone standing close to the throne. Your mother knew she was next. Keepers taste sweeter, she said. Whole lifetimes of other people's memories, curated. A feast."

"So she ran."

"So she sent *you*," Maren corrected. "On a night much like this one. Snow to cover tracks, a carter paid in coin he never counted, and you, eighteen months old, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of lavender because she'd read somewhere—I never learned where—that scent anchors memory better than anything else. She wanted you to keep something of hers even if you kept nothing else." Maren's eyes glistened. "Did you? Keep the lavender?"

I shook my head. "There's a memory. In my hoard. Snow, and a voice calling a name, and—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it fragile. "A blue door."

"That's mine," Maren said simply. "That door was blue when I was young and it'll be blue when I'm buried under the yew tree. She must have stood right where you're sitting and given you that day. A keeper's trick—she could press a memory into a child's mind like sealing wax. It takes years to surface, sometimes. Sometimes never." She leaned forward. "What name was I calling, in it?"

And here is where I confess something shameful: I had carried that memory for weeks, turned it over nightly like a coin I couldn't spend, and I had never once been able to hold onto the name. It slid off my attention every time, the way water slides off oiled leather. Some part of me, I think, had refused to know.

But sitting in her kitchen, with the door visible through the window and the snow falling in the same fat unhurried flakes, the seal cracked.

"Maren," I said. "You were calling *me* Maren. No—you were calling me by—" I pressed my palms against my eyes. "It wasn't a name. It was a diminutive. Something she called me. It sounded like—"

"*Rysie,*" Maren whispered.

The sound went through me like a hook through cloth. Twenty years of Emrys Fairweather, keeper of accounts, invisible man, and underneath all of it, at the bottom of the well, a woman's voice calling *Rysie, Rysie, come in before your fingers freeze.*

"Yes," I said, and my voice broke on it, and I let it break, because there was no one in that room to report the weakness to except a dead woman's oldest friend, and she had earned the sight of it.

* * *

We talked until the candles burned low. Or rather, Maren talked and I sat there receiving, like a man being handed the contents of a house he hadn't known he owned.

Lirien Vael had smuggled out more than a child. She'd smuggled out a *duty*. Maren didn't know its shape—Lirien had been careful even with her—but she knew its weight. "She told me, the last time I ever saw her, that if you ever came back grown, I was to tell you: *the blood remembers what the mind forgets. Don't go looking for it until you're ready to pay.*" Maren's mouth tightened. "I asked her what it cost. She said, 'Everything you can spare, and then a little more.' She laughed when she said it. She always laughed at the worst moments. It's how you knew she was frightened."

"Where did she go? After?"

Maren looked at the fire for a long moment. "Back to the palace. To the king." She said it without drama, and somehow that was worse than drama. "Everyone who could run, ran. The court emptied like a breached ship. And your mother walked back into it. Because someone had to keep the king's own memories safe from the thing eating him, and she was the best keeper alive, and she believed—" Maren stopped. Started again. "She believed there was a rite. An unbinding. Old blood-rite, older than the dynasty, kept by her family since before the palace had a name. She believed the Regent could be pulled out whole, if someone was willing to stand in front of it and hold its attention while the words were spoken."

"And did anyone—"

"No one spoke the words." Maren's voice was flat now, sanded smooth by twenty years of not saying this. "She died trying to reach the point where they could be spoken. Or she didn't die. The guild records say she was 'acquired.' Their word. They acquired her entire self, Emrys. Somewhere in a vault beneath your guildhall is a bottle with your mother's name on it, and inside that bottle is everything she was, filed and catalogued and priced."

I set down my cup very carefully, because if I hadn't, I'd have thrown it.

The Vellum Guild. My guild. My ledgers. I had audited acquisitions for eleven years, initialing entries in my neat clerk's hand, and somewhere in those volumes—maybe in a volume I had touched, maybe in a column I had totaled—was my mother, reduced to an inventory line. *One (1) memory-keeper, court-trained, contents intact. Provenance: palace acquisition. Condition: excellent.*

I had been keeping the books of my own family's destruction and calling it competence.

"Emrys." Maren took my hand. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Your mother left you a second thing. Sealed. Blood-locked—only a child of her body can open it safely. Anyone else who tries, it unmakes them. She said you'd know when you were ready, and you'd know because ready would feel exactly like terrified."

"It does," I said. "It's felt like that for weeks."

"Then you understand why she waited." Maren rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the dresser in the corner—the same dresser, I realized, that stood in the corner of my stolen memory, younger then, its wood pale instead of dark. From a drawer lined in oilcloth she took a small object and set it in my palm.

A key. Iron, old, warm from the drawer. Stamped on the bow with a sigil I recognized with a sick lurch: a stylized eye above an open book. The mark of the court memory-keepers. The mark that had been on documents in the guild's oldest archives, the ones Sarelle kept locked, the ones I'd told myself were none of my business.

"This was hidden under the third floorboard of my kitchen for twenty years," Maren said. "She told me you'd come for it eventually, or you wouldn't, and both answers were acceptable, because she loved you either way." Her voice caught on the last word and steadied itself. "But she hoped. Mothers hope. It's our worst habit."

I closed my fist around the key. It bit into my palm, and I was grateful for the pain, because pain is proof of presence, and I had spent so long being a rumor that I needed reminding I cast a shadow.

"What does it open?" I asked.

"The sealed memory," Maren said. "And something else. Something in the capital." She hesitated, and I saw her decide whether to spend the last of what Lirien had given her. "The crypt beneath the palace chapel. There's a door down there that hasn't opened in a hundred years. The keepers called it the Unbinding Chamber. Your mother's family built it, back when the first king still dreamed honestly." She met my eyes. "She left you the key to the room where the rite happens, Emrys. She planned this before you could walk. She knew she might not live to finish it, so she made sure the ending was already in your pocket, waiting."

Outside, the snow kept falling, fat and unhurried, covering the road south and the road north alike, indifferent to which direction I chose. Inside, I sat holding an iron key and the wreckage of a life story I'd never known I had.

I had gone looking for a source. For provenance. For the satisfying little notation in the margin of myself: *acquired thus, from hence, on such a date.*

I had found it. Son of Lirien Vael. Keeper's blood. Heir to a rite that cost everything you could spare and then a little more. Target of a parasite that ate kings. Named in a century-old prophecy I was increasingly certain had not been a prophecy at all but a *schedule*.

Relief and grief arrived together, entwined like lovers, and I couldn't tell anymore which was which. My identity had a source after all. It also had a price tag, and the currency was me.

Maren walked me to the blue door. The snow gathered on her white hair until she looked crowned.

"One more thing," she said, as I stepped past the threshold. "The carter who carried you south, all those years ago—he was paid by your mother, but he answered to someone else. Someone in the capital who arranged the route, the timing, the village. Your mother never learned who. But I did, later, from her letters." She paused, letting the cold sharpen the moment. "Whoever it was knew exactly where you'd be safe. Knew Hallowmere. Knew *me*." Her old eyes held mine, steady and troubled. "Someone inside the guild has been watching over you your whole life, Emrys. And I've never decided whether that should comfort you or frighten you half to death."

I walked out into the snow with a key in my fist and that question lodged in my chest like a splinter, and behind me the blue door closed, and ahead of me the road ran south toward the capital, toward the guild, toward Sarelle—who had summoned me just days ago with kindness in her voice and offered, so generously, to protect me.

*I wonder,* I thought, boots crunching, breath steaming, *who's been protecting whom.*
