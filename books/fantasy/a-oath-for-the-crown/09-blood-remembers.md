## 9. Blood Remembers

The old woman's name was Maren, and she fed me stew before she told me anything useful, which I have since learned is the traditional order of operations for grandmothers everywhere. You do not get prophecy on an empty stomach. You get barley, root vegetables, and a look that says *sit down, boy, you're too thin to be carrying whatever you're carrying.*

I sat. I ate. And when the bowl was empty, she took my hands in hers—her palms rough as bark, mine soft as a clerk's, because that is what I was—and she pressed her thumb into the center of my left palm, hard enough to hurt.

"There," she said. "You flinch like she did."

"Like who did?"

"Your mother." She said it plainly, the way you'd say *it's raining* or *the bread's gone stale*, and it went through me like a blade through wet paper. "Sera. Court memory-keeper under the old king. She had that same flinch. Quick little jump, like a bird deciding whether to fly."

I should say something here about how I'd spent my whole life believing I had no mother worth the word—that the guild had found me feral in some gutter, or bought me off a debt-wagon, or simply invented me out of spare parts and ledger paper. That was the story I'd been given at seven years old, delivered by a guild functionary with all the warmth of an invoice: *You are Emrys Fairweather. You belong to the Vellum Guild now.* No before. No family. The guild's whole philosophy was that a thief with no past steals better, and I had been their best proof of concept.

And now there was a woman named Sera with my flinch, sitting somewhere in this dead village's history, and a name—not Fairweather, something older—waiting in the snow of a memory I'd carried for twenty years without knowing why.

"Maren," I said carefully, "the memory I have—the snow, the voice calling—it isn't mine, is it? It's hers."

"It's yours." She fixed me with a look. "It's just not only yours. Memory-keepers don't work like your guild does, boy. They don't take. They *leave*. A keeper seals what matters most into her bloodline, so no thief, no parasite, no king can pry it loose. What you've been carrying all these years is a locked door. And you—" she tapped my sternum, "—are the key."

I want to tell you I understood immediately. I want to tell you I felt the weight of destiny settle over my shoulders like a warm cloak. What I actually felt was heartburn from the stew and a cold crawling sensation at the base of my skull, the kind you get when you realize the floor you've been standing on has been a trapdoor all along.

"What's behind the door?" I asked.

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Outside her window, the wind moved through a village of maybe forty souls, half of them related to her, all of them pretending not to watch the stranger through their shutters.

"The rite," she said. "The unbinding. The thing your family kept for six generations against exactly the day that's coming. But I can't open it for you, and neither can anyone else alive. Your mother sealed it to blood. You'll have to open it yourself."

"How?"

"She'll show you. Keepers always leave instructions." Maren smiled, and there was grief in it, old and worn smooth as river stone. "Mothers are like that. Even dead ones."

* * *

She gave me a room above her kitchen—a pallet, a blanket that smelled of lavender and sheep—and told me to sleep with the memory held in mind. Not examined. Not pried at. Just *held*, the way you hold a question you actually want answered, instead of the way I usually held questions, which was at arm's length with tongs.

I lay down. I did what I do best, which is handle a memory with care.

Here is what I know about my own gift, and I have never written this down anywhere, not even in the margins of my private ledgers: when I lift a memory, I feel its edges. Most people's memories are smooth, worn like coins passed hand to hand. But a sealed memory—one deliberately made, deliberately locked—has *seams*. Craftsmanship. My mother's memory had seams like a masterwork, and every one of them was stitched with a word I didn't recognize until I stopped trying to read it and simply let it be true:

*Emrys.*

Not Fairweather. Just Emrys. My name, spoken in her voice, was the lock.

I spoke it—in my head, in the dark, feeling ridiculous—and the snow-memory opened like a book whose pages had been waiting two decades for thumbs.

I will not pretend I remember all of it clearly. That is rather the point of what happened next. But I remember the shape of it. I remember falling *into* it, the way you fall into deep water, except the water was winter and the voice was my mother's and she was running—running through snow toward a wagon, toward a man in guild colors, holding a bundle that was me, three years old and screaming—and she pressed her hands to his temples and I felt her *pouring*, felt her give him everything, a whole life compressed into a single act of theft in reverse, buying my passage out of the palace with the only currency the guild respected—

And then she turned. In the memory, she turned and looked directly at me. At the three-year-old. At the thirty-year-old watching through him. Both of us at once. Sealed keepers are clever that way; they know exactly who will come looking, and when.

"If you're hearing this," she said, and the snow hushed around her voice, "then the crown is failing again, and you're the last of our line, and I am so sorry, little one. I'm sorry for all of it—for the leaving, for the hiding, for the name I buried in you where only you could dig. Listen now. There isn't much time, and the door only opens once."

And she taught me.

That's the only word for it. She taught me the rite of unbinding the way my mother teaches you to tie a knot or whistle through grass—hands over hands, voice inside your voice, six generations of women folded into a single lesson. I felt them all. I felt a line of keepers stretching back behind her like lanterns in fog, each one adding a stitch, each one whispering a correction, until the rite stood complete in my mind: the words, the order, the terrible arithmetic of what it would cost.

Because it costs. Of course it costs. Nothing in this world is free, least of all magic, and my family's rite was priced like everything the guild ever sold me, except honest about the bill.

To unbind a parasite woven through a king's memory, a blood heir must speak the words while *holding the creature's full attention*—and the only bait heavy enough to hold a thing that has eaten centuries is a life. Not the whole life. But a piece of it, torn out and offered, real enough to feed on while the words do their work. The rite takes what you give it. And if you give it nothing—

Maren had warned me, in her plain way, over the stew: "My grandmother saw the last attempt. The man who tried wasn't of the blood. He spoke the words anyway." She'd refilled my bowl before continuing. "There wasn't enough of him left afterward to bury. They scattered what remained across four parishes."

So yes. I understood the price going in. I'd like that noted in the record, whoever keeps records after I'm done: the thief knew the cost and opened the door anyway.

What I did *not* understand—what no warning could have prepared me for—was that opening the seal itself had a toll. The memory was built to open once. Once, and violently, and it would take its payment on the way through.

* * *

I woke on Maren's floor.

Not the pallet. The floor, face-down, cheek against cold flagstone, and the first thing I knew was that something was wrong with the light. Evening light came through the window—amber, low—but I had lain down at dawn. I was almost sure I had lain down at dawn.

"Ah," said Maren, from the doorway, holding a cup of something steaming. "You're back."

"Back?"

"You were gone a day." She said it the way she said everything, flat as a ledger line. "A day and a night. You breathed. You blinked. You ate a little when I made you. But nobody was home, boy. Whatever your mother left you, it collected its fee on the way out."

I sat up. My body worked, mostly. My hands shook, and there was a taste in my mouth like burnt paper, and when I tried to remember the previous morning—the walk up from the shrine, the frost on the fence posts—I found a hole. Not a fog. A *hole*. Clean-edged, deliberate, like a page razored out of a ledger. I reached for the memory of arriving in the village and found the moment before it, and the moment after, and between them nothing but smooth absence, stitched shut at both ends with craftsmanship I recognized.

My mother's craftsmanship. She'd built the toll into the seal herself. Open the door, lose a day. It was almost funny. It was exactly the kind of term a guild contract would bury in clause nine, except this one was written in love, which somehow made it worse.

"A day," I repeated. "Of what? What did I lose?"

"That's not how it works," Maren said. "You don't get to choose, and you don't get to audit it. It's gone. Be grateful it was only a day—the rite gets greedier the closer it's used to the crown. By the time it's truly needed..." She didn't finish. She handed me the cup instead. Willow bark and honey. She'd known I'd need it.

I drank. And as the warmth spread through me, I took inventory, the way I inventory everything, because inventory is the only prayer I know.

The rite: intact. All of it, every word, every step, sitting in my mind with the weight of something forged rather than remembered. I could recite it. I could feel the shape of the working the way a smith feels a blade he's made—every stage, every hinge, the moment where the blood-heir speaks and the moment where the offering is taken. I knew what I would have to give. I knew what it would do to me. Six generations of knowledge, installed in one night, paid for with one blank page.

The rest of me: present, minus the day. Minus, I suspected, other things I couldn't detect by their absence—you never can. That's the cruelty of stolen memory, whether the thief is a guild or a mother trying to save her son's life: you don't mourn what you can't see missing. Somewhere in me was a gap shaped like a morning I'd never know I'd lost, and I would walk past it every day for the rest of my life without once tripping on the edge.

I thought about that longer than I should have. Because here is the thing the rite had shown me, clear as the snow in that sealed memory: the final working would demand far more than a day. The Regent could only be baited with an anchor—a memory so central that the creature would turn from the king to seize it. And I knew, with the certainty of a man reading his own invoice, which memory in my hoard was heavy enough. The one I'd guarded longest. The one I'd never let myself examine too closely, because some part of me had always known it was load-bearing.

But that accounting could wait. I was learning, slowly, the family trade: every answer costs, and you pay in installments, and you try not to count what's left until you absolutely must.

* * *

Maren walked me to the edge of the village at first light, because of course she did; grandmothers are constitutionally incapable of letting you leave unfed and unwarned. She pressed a parcel of bread into my hands and then, unexpectedly, gripped my wrist.

"Sera had a saying," she said. "Keeper's saying. *We do not save the crown because we love the crown. We save it because of what hides underneath.*" Her eyes searched my face. "Do you understand what's under yours, boy? Truly?"

"I've seen it," I said. "In the vault. Wearing the king."

"And do you know what it wants from you?"

I did, actually. I'd had a whole sleepless night—well, a sleepless *hole where a night should be*, plus the hours I could account for—to think about it. The Regent had eaten kings for centuries, and my family had kept the counter-spell for just as long, and neither of those facts was coincidence. The parasite feared the rite. It had hunted my line to near-extinction. And now the last heir had walked into the open, announced himself by opening a seal that must have rung through whatever passes for the creature's senses like a bell through a cathedral.

It knew where I was. I would have bet the entire guild archive on it.

"It wants me," I said. "Or it wants me gone. Either way, it's already looking."

"Then don't be here when it finds you." Maren released my wrist and stepped back, and her face did something complicated—pride and fear and the particular grief of old women who bury everyone they guide. "Go to the city. Find whoever's still loyal to the crown and doesn't know it yet. And boy—" she caught me before I turned, one last time, "—when the rite asks for its price, and it will ask for more than days... remember that your mother paid first. You're not starting the account. You're finishing it."

I walked down through the frost with the bread under my arm and the rite coiled in my chest like a second heartbeat, and I did not look back, because looking back is how you end up staying, and staying is how you die in villages.

The road bent toward the city. Toward Sarelle, who would want to know where I'd been and would accept none of my answers. Toward the guild, which had surely marked me absent and begun, in its patient bureaucratic way, to write me out. Toward a king being eaten alive in a palace full of people who called it stability.

Toward the price.

I was a mile down the road before I noticed the rider behind me—distant, unhurried, keeping pace—and another mile before I admitted what the cut of his cloak meant. Guild colors. Not assassins yet. Auditors. The guild's gentler teeth, sent to escort home a ledger that had wandered off its shelf.

I put my head down and walked, and began, quietly, to plan which memories I could afford to lose.
