## 10. Hunted by the Ledger

The rider behind me did not close the distance for three miles, which told me everything I needed to know about him. Assassins hurry. Auditors stroll. Whatever he was, he wanted me to know he was there, the way a cat wants a mouse to know it's been seen—because the watching is most of the sport.

I stopped at a crossroads shrine to adjust my bootlace and used the pause to take his measure in the polished brass of the offering plate. Broad through the shoulders. Hood back despite the drizzle. A face like a closed ledger: no entries visible, but you could feel the weight of what was written inside.

I knew him. Of course I knew him. You don't keep the Vellum Guild's accounts for eleven years without memorizing its teeth.

Corvane. Senior retriever. The man they sent not for ledgers that wandered off shelves but for thieves who wandered off guilds. He had once brought back a defector from Saltmarsh in a barrel, and the defector had thanked him. That was the part everyone whispered about—the thanking. Whatever Corvane had done to the man's memory on the journey home, it had left him *grateful*.

"Master Fairweather." His voice carried across the wet road, pleasant as a clerk announcing closing time. "You've taken an unapproved holiday."

"I'm auditing," I called back. "Field work. Very rigorous."

"The Guildmistress would like her property returned."

"Which property? I've stolen so many things this week, I've lost count."

He smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes; it stopped at the border like goods awaiting inspection. "You know exactly which."

*The prophecy.* Or the bloodline. Or me—I suspected that by now the categories had collapsed into one another, and Emrys Fairweather, keeper of accounts, had become an entry himself: one line in Sarelle's great book, *asset, compromised, recover or erase.*

I straightened my boot and stood up slowly, hands loose at my sides, and let him see me do the arithmetic every thief does when cornered: exits, odds, prices.

There were no exits. There was a crossroads, a shrine to a god of travelers who had never once answered me, and two hundred yards of open road in either direction with a retriever standing in the middle of it.

But there were prices. There are always prices. That is the first law of my trade, and tonight I intended to honor it.

* * *

Let me explain how theft works when the goods are memories, because Corvane—and probably you, if you've never held someone else's past in your palm—thinks it works like picking a lock. Find the door, force it, take what's inside.

It doesn't. It works like conversation.

A mind is a market at dawn. Every memory has a stall, a vendor, a price scrawled in chalk, and a thousand neighbors leaning over to watch the bargaining. You can't just snatch a loaf. But you can distract the whole square—one shout, one fire, one beautiful lie—and while every head turns, you lift what you came for, and the vendor doesn't even notice the weight gone from his shelf until he reaches for it later and finds only the shape of where it used to be.

Corvane's courage lived in a prominent stall. Men like him build their whole selves around one load-bearing memory, and his was easy to find because he wore it openly, the way soldiers wear scars: a burning house, years ago, and young Corvane walking *into* it when everyone else ran. He had built a career on that moment. He reached for it every morning the way other men reach for prayer.

So I shouted fire.

Not literally. I opened my own hoard—a lifetime of lifted memories, first kisses and bottled griefs and one river's childhood, all jostling like smuggled goods—and I spent three of them at once. A mother's lullaby hurled wide. A wedding's joy, scattered across the crossroads like thrown coins. And underneath them, threaded through, the suggestion, soft as ink bleeding through paper: *you have forgotten something important. Look. Look now.*

Every mind flinches at that whisper. Even trained ones. Especially trained ones, because a retriever's worst fear is not death but loss—the day he reaches for his load-bearing wall and finds it gone.

Corvane's head turned inward for exactly one and a half seconds.

In this trade, one and a half seconds is a fortune.

I crossed the road at a dead sprint, caught his wrist as he surfaced, and went into his market square like a man who owned a stall there. No finesse. Finesse was for customers. I put my hand flat against the burning-house memory and *pulled*, the way you'd pull a keystone, and felt the whole arch of him groan.

Here is what I took: not the memory itself. I took his *relationship* to it. The pride. The certainty. The quiet knowledge, renewed each morning, that he was the kind of man who walks into fire. I left him the burning house intact—I am a thief, not a monster—but I left him standing outside it, with everyone else, watching.

Courage, it turns out, is not a thing you have. It's a thing you remember having. Remove the remembering, and you remove the man.

* * *

He sat down in the mud.

That was the terrible part—not the fight, which barely qualified as one, but the sitting. A big man in guild colors folding down onto the wet road like a puppet whose strings had been quietly cut, staring at his own hands.

"I walked in," he said. His voice had gone strange, thin, a stranger borrowing his throat. "They told me afterward. Everyone said. I walked into the house."

"You did," I said. "I believe you."

"But I don't—" He looked up at me, and his eyes were doing something I had never seen them do in eleven years of watching him across the counting floor. They were filling. "Why can't I remember why?"

Because I took the why, Corvane. Because the Guildmistress sent you to retrieve a piece of paper and a boy, and the boy unmade the one brick your whole self was built on, and now you're sitting in the rain discovering that you were never a building at all, just bricks stacked hopefully against nothing.

What I said was: "Go home. Tell her the trail went cold at the crossroads. Tell her I'm better than she remembers."

He nodded. He actually nodded. Then he began to cry—not loudly, not dramatically, just the steady silent weeping of a man whose grief has arrived before his understanding of it—and I turned and walked away from him as fast as dignity allowed, which was not very fast, because my legs had discovered what my hands had done and were registering their objection.

Stealing courage is not clean. People think the danger is in the taking. The danger is in the *carrying*. His fear came with me, all of it, the full weight of a life spent being brave suddenly unsupported, and it settled into my chest like swallowed lead. For half a mile I could not tell whether I was running from the guild or simply running, the way he must have wanted to run from that burning house, the way everyone runs, always, and bravery is just the story we tell about the ones who didn't.

I stole a stranger's calm from my own hoard to steady myself. Paid for it gladly. Walked on.

* * *

The village of Marrow's End had one inn, and the inn had one room with a window facing the road, and I paid triple for it and then did not sleep in the bed. I slept sitting against the door with my hoard open and my senses stretched, listening to the building settle and the rain tick against the glass and, once, near midnight, hoofbeats passing on the road below—travelers, ordinary, gone again—while my heart performed percussion.

This was the new arithmetic of my life, I reflected somewhere in the small hours. Eleven years of invisibility, purchased honestly with tedium and competence, and I had spent it all in a single season. First the prophecy. Then the flight to Marrow's End. Now Corvane weeping in the mud behind me and Sarelle's patience, which had never been patience at all but merely the leisurely pace of a woman setting a trap, wearing thin toward its end.

And she had proof now. That was the thought that kept the dark at arm's length and no farther. Somewhere between the archive and my flight, she had confirmed it—the bloodline, the memory-keeper's line, the old royal office that my mother's family had held since before the guild existed. I didn't know how she'd confirmed it. I didn't need to. Sarelle had run the Vellum Guild for thirty years; she didn't chase rumors, she *audited* them, and her audits were never wrong.

Which meant that every thief in the city was about to receive new instructions. Not *bring him back.* Nothing so gentle. The instructions would read more like a page from one of my own ledgers: *item description, last known location, condition on recovery negotiable, payment upon delivery of proof.*

I had spent my whole adult life keeping accounts for other people's sins. Now I was an entry in someone else's column, and the entry was marked *outstanding,* and outstanding entries get collected.

Toward dawn I gave up on rest and took stock instead, because stock-taking is the only prayer I've ever trusted. My assets: the rite of unbinding, bought at the cost of a day of my life and still humming behind my teeth like a struck bell. The foreign memory—snow, a woman's voice, a name that wasn't Fairweather—which had turned out to be a map, and had led me true. An old woman in a village who had touched my face and wept and called me by a name I was still learning to carry. A princess who believed her father could be saved. A disgraced chronicler rotting in a palace cell who knew more about the Regent than any living soul should.

My liabilities: the entire Vellum Guild. Its network of informants in every tavern, every temple, every customs house from here to the coast. Its retrievers, of whom Corvane had been merely the polite one. And its Guildmistress, who had fed me supper from my first night under her roof and had known, I now understood, exactly what she was raising—had kept me ignorant of my own blood the way you keep a blade oiled and sheathed, waiting for the night it would be useful.

Or dangerous. She hadn't decided yet. That was the worst of it. Somewhere in the guild's tall house, Sarelle was weighing me on her scales—*asset or liability, recover or erase*—and the pan had not yet tipped.

I meant to tip it for her.

* * *

I left Marrow's End before the baker lit his oven, going out through the stable and over a fence like the amateur I apparently still was, because no amount of ledger-keeping teaches you how to flee properly. The road south was empty. The rain had moved off eastward, dragging gray light behind it, and the fields on either side steamed like something freshly exhumed.

Halfway to the river I passed the shrine again—the crossroads one, the traveler's god, the one who had never answered me—and I stopped, because Corvane's borrowed grief was still sitting in my chest like an unpaid debt, and because there was a coin in my pocket I had lifted from a merchant two towns back and had been meaning to spend on something honest.

"For what it's worth," I told the shrine, "he really did walk into the house. I checked. The memory's real. I only took the part where he was proud of it."

The god, naturally, said nothing. Gods of travelers are famously tight-fisted with comfort; they deal in roads, not destinations.

But as I turned back to the road, I felt it—that faint wrongness at the edge of perception that every thief learns to read like weather. The sense of being *described.* Somewhere out in the wet morning, someone was holding a page with my particulars on it and comparing the world against it, line by line. Height. Coloring. Last seen heading south with a retriever's courage in his pocket and a dead man's name in his mouth.

They weren't behind me anymore.

They were ahead.

I stood at the crossroads with the rain starting again, doing the only arithmetic left to me, and understood with perfect clarity that the ledger had turned. I had spent eleven years writing other people down—every sin, every sale, every impossible thing the guild had ever bought or bottled—and now some clerk in Sarelle's back room was writing *me,* in a fair hand, in a book I would never be permitted to audit.

Fine, I thought. Let them enter me. Let them describe me down to the scar on my knuckle and the lie in my surname.

But every entry, however careful, leaves something out. And I knew one thing about myself that no guild scribe had yet recorded, one line that would not appear in their fair hand until it was far too late to matter:

*I am coming back for the king.*

I turned off the south road, toward the river, toward the city, toward the palace where a hollow man waited in a gilded room for someone to remember him—and behind me, faint on the wind, I heard horses.

Many horses.

Coming fast.
