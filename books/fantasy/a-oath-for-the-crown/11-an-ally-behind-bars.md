## 11. An Ally Behind Bars

There is a particular art to visiting a man the Crown has decided to forget.

Not execution—execution leaves paperwork, and paperwork leaves witnesses. No, the palace prefers its embarrassments *archived*. Sealed in a room with no name on the door, fed irregularly, remembered by no one whose memory hasn't been purchased or pruned. I know this because I have audited the entries. Somewhere in the Vellum Guild's deep ledgers there is a column of payments labeled only *maintenance, chronicler*, going back eleven years, and until three nights ago I had never once wondered what was being maintained.

Now I knew his name. Wren. And I knew where they kept him, because Ilsette had drawn me a map in charcoal on the back of a laundry list, which is the sort of document no guard thinks to confiscate because it looks like exactly what it pretends to be.

The map showed the undercrofts beneath the east wing—the administrative bowels of the palace, where clerks who will never be noble shuffle papers that will never matter, and beneath *them*, one floor further down, a corridor with six doors and five occupants. The sixth door had been empty for years, according to a bribed turnkey whose silence cost me two bottled memories from my hoard: a summer afternoon and the smell of my—of someone's—bread baking. I am learning to spend myself carefully. I am also learning that I spend myself at all times whether I choose to or not.

I went at the hour when even the rats take stock and find their accounts wanting. The turnkey's replacement—a younger man, cheaper, whose courage I did not need to steal because he had none to begin with—took one look at my guild seal and decided I was someone whose business was better left unexamined. This is the great gift of the Vellum Guild's reputation: we are the reason people lock their doors at night, and therefore the last thing anyone expects to come knocking politely.

The corridor smelled of damp stone and older despair. Five doors, five silences behind them. At the sixth, I pressed my palm flat against the iron and listened.

Scratching. Faint, rhythmic, patient. A pen nib on paper, or something serving as one.

"Chronicler," I said softly. "Wren."

The scratching stopped. A long pause followed, the kind of pause a man earns by surviving a decade of visitors who were not visitors. Then, muffled: "If you're here to add to my sentence, you should know I've already written it down. Everything they do gets written down. It's the only power I have left and I intend to die fluent in it."

"I'm not from the palace."

"No," he said, and I could hear the smile in it, dry as old vellum. "The palace doesn't knock."

* * *

Getting the door open took less bribery than skill; the lock was older than the corruption that put him behind it, and locks that old trust keys more than they should. I have a talent for persuading metal of things. Inside, by the light of a stub of tallow candle, I found a man shaped like a question mark—narrow, bent slightly forward, as if perpetually leaning toward something just out of reach. His hair had gone the color of ash that has thought about becoming snow. His eyes had not gone anywhere at all. They were quick, dark, and cataloguing me before I'd finished ducking under the lintel.

"You're a thief," he said.

"I'm an accountant."

"You're a thief with excellent posture and ink stains on your cuff in the pattern of ledger columns. You count stolen things." He tilted his head. "Vellum Guild?"

I didn't answer, which answered for me.

"Wonderful," Wren said, and sat back down on his cot with the air of a man settling in for the best evening he'd had in years. "Sit. Mind the bucket. And before you ask—I'm not telling you anything until I know what you are. Not whose. *What*. There's a difference, and the difference is currently the most important fact in this kingdom, though almost nobody knows it yet."

So I told him. Not everything—I am still, whatever else is true of me now, a man who keeps accounts—but enough. The prophecy ledger with tonight's date already inscribed a century past. The keeper of accounts who shall open the crown. The thing I had seen standing behind King Aldric's eyes like a lodger who had redecorated without asking. The word I'd pulled from forbidden archives, the one that made the archivist who spoke it go pale and then go missing: *Regent*.

Wren listened without interrupting, which after eleven years alone must have cost him physically. When I finished, he was quiet for a moment, and then he laughed—one short, sharp syllable, with no joy in it whatsoever.

"A century-old prophecy," he said, "and the guild kept it in inventory. Filed. Shelved. *Audited.*" He shook his head slowly. "Do you understand what that means? They've known. Someone has known for a hundred years that the crown would crack open on this date, and their institutional response was to price it."

"The guild trades in impossible things. Prophecy is just inventory that appreciates."

"Yes, and that's precisely what terrifies me." He leaned forward, and the candlelight moved across his face like water over stones. "Because if the Regent's cycle is what I believe it is—and I spent nine years in the royal archives confirming it before they sealed me down here—then prophecy isn't prediction. It's *scheduling*. Something wrote that date down because something wanted it written down. The Regent doesn't just eat kings, master Fairweather. It eats *time*. It arranges its meals."

I want to tell you I received this calmly. I did not. My hands, resting on my knees in the manner of a composed professional, had gone cold, and somewhere behind my ribs a small voice that sounded suspiciously like Sarelle observed that I had walked into a cell voluntarily, which was either very brave or very stupid, and that history rarely waited around to distinguish between the two.

"Tell me about the cycle," I said.

Wren got up. From beneath a loose flagstone—he moved it with the ease of long practice, revealing a cavity lined with scraps of cloth—he began lifting out papers. Dozens of them. Small, cramped, written on everything: cloth, bark paper, the backs of his own ration notices, once, horrifyingly, a page of scripture. Eleven years of a man refusing to be erased.

"Every monarch since the founding," he said, dealing them out on the cot like a card sharp showing a losing hand. "I reconstructed the reigns from tax records, harvest tallies, diplomatic correspondence—the boring bones of history, the things nobody bothers to falsify because nobody bothers to read them. And every reign follows the same shape. Listen." He held up one scrap. "King Maren the Second. First decade: brilliant. Reformed the courts, ended a famine, personally negotiated peace with the Thornehold clans. Second decade: competent but strange. Forgets appointments. Repeats himself. Third decade—" he let the scrap fall— "a figurehead. Smiling. Vacant. Signs what's put in front of him. Dies quietly. The next reign begins brilliant."

"And the histories say—"

"The histories say the later kings grew *sedate*. *Contemplative*. *Above earthly concerns*." His mouth twisted. "Court chroniclers are paid in proximity, master Fairweather. We learn very quickly which truths fit in a sentence and which ones end our careers. I was the first in three hundred years to write the unfittable truth, and you can see my office."

He gathered the scraps into a rough chronology and held up the last one, the oldest, the most worn. "Here. The founding. The first king, Aldous. And here's the part that made me certain: the founding records mention a *counselor*. Unnamed. Present at every coronation for the first four reigns, always described identically—*the same counselor, unaged*. Then the mentions stop. Not because the counselor left. Because the chroniclers learned to stop seeing him. Or because he learned to stop being seen." He set the scrap down gently, the way you handle something that might still be alive. "The Regent isn't a parasite that infected the crown. The Regent may well be the reason there *is* a crown. It built the throne. It has been farming it ever since."

I sat with that. It sat with me, heavier.

"Why kings?" I asked finally. "Why not just—take anyone? It clearly has the power."

"Because memory compounds," Wren said simply. "A king is a century of decisions wearing one face. Every treaty, every pardon, every grief witnessed publicly by an entire nation—that's not one life, it's a *library*. The Regent eats libraries. A fisherman would feed it for a day. A king feeds it for thirty years, and then it inherits the fisherman-king's son, raised in the same halls, carrying the same archive, plus everything the Regent learned from the father." He smiled thinly. "It's not gluttony. It's husbandry."

Outside the door, far off, a boot scraped stone. We both froze. It passed. In cells like this you learn to measure time in boots.

Then Wren said the thing I had come for, though I didn't know I'd come for it until he said it.

"Now. Your prophecy. May I see it?"

I had brought a copy—of course I had brought a copy; I copy everything, it's the one instinct stronger than self-preservation. I unfolded it and laid it on his cot, and he read it twice, lips moving, one finger tracing the lines like a man checking a sum.

"'The keeper of accounts shall open the crown,'" he murmured. "'And the crown shall be opened unto freedom, or the opener shall wear it.'" He looked up. "You noticed the second ending?"

"There is no second ending. That's the point. It names me and stops."

"It doesn't stop. It *withholds*." He tapped the page. "Prophecies of this vintage—the ones I've cross-referenced from the same scriptorium, same ink batch, same century—are conditional by design. Look at the grammar. *Shall open*, and then a disjunction. The scribe who wrote this wasn't predicting your future. He was describing a mechanism. Open the crown *unto*—that's a direction, a purpose clause. Freedom flows through you, or you become the vessel the opening creates." He leaned back against the wall. "The Regent enters through openings, master Fairweather. That's how it took the first king. That's how it takes all of them—not through violence, through *access*. A coronation is an opening. An oath is an opening. And a rite of unbinding—" his eyes came up to mine, and they were not unkind, which made it worse— "a rite of unbinding is the widest door this creature has ever been offered."

The cold in my hands traveled up my arms and settled somewhere behind my sternum, where it intended, apparently, to stay.

"You're saying if I do this—if I open the king the way the rite requires—the Regent might simply step out of him and into me."

"I'm saying the prophecy says so, and the prophecy was written by someone who understood the mechanism well enough to date it a century in advance." He folded my copy and handed it back with both hands, formally, like returning a knife. "Two endings. One where the keeper frees the king. One where the keeper becomes the next crown for the thing to wear—and think what *that* would mean. A vessel who can lift and hold memories like coins. A thief who already knows how to carry what isn't his. If the Regent could choose its next body from everyone in this kingdom, it would choose you. It may have chosen you a hundred years ago. It may have arranged the guild, the ledgers, the prophecy itself, the whole architecture of your life, so that tonight you would walk willingly through the one door it cannot open alone."

For a long moment the only sound was the candle eating its own wick.

I thought about the memory in my hoard that wasn't mine. Snow. A woman's voice calling a name that wasn't Fairweather. I thought about how it had gotten there, installed before my earliest recollection, and about Wren's phrase—*it helped take it*—which I hadn't heard yet, wouldn't hear until the Regent itself whispered it to me in a dream some nights from now. But standing in that cell, holding a folded copy of my own doom, I felt the shape of it already. The vertigo of suspecting that your whole self might be furniture arranged in someone else's room.

"So why help me?" I asked. "If I might be the ending where everything gets worse?"

Wren was quiet for a while. Then he reached into the cavity beneath the flagstone one more time and brought out a single scrap, different from the others—newer, cleaner, written in ink rather than berry-stain—and pressed it into my hand.

"Because there's a third possibility the prophecy doesn't mention," he said. "Prophecies describe mechanisms, not certainties. Mechanisms can be *gamed*. The Regent expects a keeper who opens the crown and either succeeds or is consumed. It does not expect a keeper who understands the mechanism and walks in anyway, knowing the price, having chosen which memories to lose—which pieces of himself to spend as bait." He tapped my chest, lightly, over the sternum, right where the cold had settled. "Whatever you're planning, plan it so that the thing it takes from you is a thing you've already decided to give. Terror is just resolve that hasn't signed its ledger yet. Sign it. Decide what you'll pay. Write it down. Then when the moment comes, it won't be terror. It'll be arithmetic."

I looked down at the scrap. On it, in his cramped, stubborn hand, was a single line:

*A man who chooses his losses cannot be robbed.*

Eleven years in a hole, and the man had emerged with better accounting than the Vellum Guild.

* * *

I stayed an hour longer than was wise. He told me about the rite—or what fragments survived in the archives he'd memorized before they took his access: that it required blood, that it required a keeper, that the words had been sealed away by the last court memory-keepers precisely because they were dangerous in careless hands. He watched my face when he said *memory-keepers*, and I watched him watching, and neither of us said the obvious thing out loud, because the obvious thing was still too raw, still sitting in my hoard wrapped in snow and a woman's voice, waiting for me to be ready to unwrap it.

At the door, he stopped me with one hand on my sleeve.

"They'll move me soon," he said. "You coming here was noticed. Boots notice everything; it's their whole profession. When the Regent realizes what you are, it will realize what I know, and this cell will get quieter and then it will get emptier." He smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked and entirely unbought. "So take the rest of it now. Everything I haven't said. Take it as a memory."

"I don't understand."

"You're a thief of memories. So steal mine." He tapped his own temple. "All of it. Every fragment of the Regent, the cycle, the counselor, the founding, the grammar of that prophecy—eleven years of the only true history in this kingdom, stored in the one place they haven't figured out how to search. Steal it, keep it safe, and give it back to me afterward, when there's an *afterward*. Consider it a deposit. Chroniclers don't donate, master Fairweather. We lend, at interest, and our interest is simple: to be read."

And so, in a cell with no name on the door, by the light of a dying candle, I committed the strangest theft of my career: I stole a man's entire mind with his blessing, lifted eleven years of forbidden truth out of him the way I'd lift a coin, and left him—this is the part that still catches in my throat—*lighter*. Relieved. He laughed when it was done, actually laughed, and said it was the first quiet his head had known since his arrest, and asked me, please, to be careful with it, the way you'd ask someone to be careful with your child.

I promised. It is the only promise I have ever made that I am afraid I cannot keep, because keeping it requires there to be a *me* left, at the end, to hand it back to.

The boots in the corridor were coming more often now. As I slipped out through the undercrofts, moving from shadow to shadow with another man's lifetime folded into my coat, I passed a messenger hurrying the other way, and I caught—because I catch everything, it's my curse and my trade—the flicker of his surface thoughts as our shoulders brushed.

They were addressed upward. They were urgent. They concerned the princess.

Someone had told the Regent about Ilsette.
