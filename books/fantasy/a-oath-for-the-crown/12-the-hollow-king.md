## 12. The Hollow King

Wren's old contacts turned out to be a laundress with a face like a closed ledger and a one-eyed falconer who communicated primarily in grunts and gestures toward things he wanted you to avoid.

"Names," I said, when they first led me down a servants' stair that smelled of lye and candle grease. "I should like to know your names."

The laundress—Marta, though she only told me after the third corridor, as if identity were something to be dispensed in small doses, like poison—didn't look back. "You don't want our names. Names are how they find you." A pause, wringing out a sheet that was already dry. "But Wren vouched for you, so: Marta. And that's Corvin. He doesn't talk because the last time he talked, someone listened."

Corvin grunted, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

We moved through the palace the way water moves through a wall—not quickly, not loudly, finding every crack. It struck me, somewhere around the fourth hidden passage, that these two had been doing this for years. That Wren's "old contacts" were not contacts at all but co-conspirators, people who had read his true histories and believed them, and had spent the years since his arrest quietly keeping faith in the dark. There is a particular kind of courage that never gets written into chronicles: the courage of continuing to be inconvenient long after everyone has stopped watching.

I would know. I had made an entire life of it.

"The king's apartments," Marta murmured, stopping before a door so unremarkable it had clearly been made remarkable on purpose—the kind of door that says *nothing to see here* so convincingly that only someone looking for exactly that would look twice. "He takes supper alone. He always takes supper alone now. The staff draw lots to avoid serving him."

"Why?"

She looked at me then, really looked, and her eyes were older than her face. "Because he asks them their names. Every night. The same ones, over and over. And then he weeps because he can't keep them." She pressed the door handle down. "Whatever you're going to do for him, keeper, do it soon. He asked me yesterday if I'd seen his daughter. She was standing right beside him."

* * *

The king's private dining room was small—which surprised me until I understood it. Kings who are being eaten alive apparently prefer small rooms. Large rooms echo, and echoes require a self to bounce off of.

Aldric sat at a table set for four, eating nothing, moving food from plate to plate with the absent precision of a man performing a memory of dining rather than dining itself. Candles burned low. A fire had been laid and forgotten. Through the window behind him, the city spread out in its winter dark, ten thousand lives flickering, none of them aware that the man at the center of all of them was hollowing out like a gourd left too long in the sun.

I had planned a grand entrance. Some subtle misdirection, a stolen servant's manner, the practiced anonymity of my trade. Instead I stood in the doorway like an idiot, because the first thing I felt was not professional caution.

It was grief.

You have to understand what I saw. I had expected the Regent's work to be dramatic—a vacant stare, a puppet's stiffness, some visible wrongness. But Aldric looked *ordinary*. Tired, gray at the temples, lines around his eyes that spoke of worry rather than decay. He could have been any father in any house in the city, sitting down late to a cold supper.

That was the horror of it. The Regent didn't consume you dramatically. It consumed you *domestically*. One name at a time. One evening at a time. Until the man remaining was just enough man to sit upright and sign documents and wear the crown to ceremonies, and no more.

"Hello?" Aldric said, without alarm. He'd noticed me the way you notice weather. "Are you... I'm sorry. Have we met? You seem—" His brow furrowed, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes, something quick and desperate, like a bird trapped in a room, throwing itself against the glass. "You seem familiar. Everyone seems familiar lately. It's the strangest thing. Like the whole world is a word on the tip of my tongue."

"I'm no one, Your Majesty," I said, and meant it in every sense.

"No, no, that's not right either." He rose, and there was a courtesy in the motion so deeply worn-in that even a gutted man performed it. "No one doesn't make a person feel *remembered*. You do that. You make me feel remembered." He smiled, and the smile cracked something in my chest, because it was a good smile, a warm one, the smile of a man who had once—before whatever got into him got into him—been genuinely kind. "Sit. Please. The chairs are lonely. Everything here is lonely; I've made certain of it."

Every instinct I owned screamed that this was a trap, that the Regent knew I was coming, that Sarelle's hunters were already climbing the stairs behind me. I sat down anyway. Some debts you pay in coin. This one I was apparently paying in stupidity.

"You take supper alone," I said carefully.

"I prefer it." He resumed his seat across from me, folding his hands. "When there are others, I say things. Wrong things. I called the Lord Chancellor by his predecessor's name last month—a man eleven years dead—and old Havelock went white as milk and wouldn't meet my eye for a week." He laughed, softly, without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "They think I'm eccentric. Kings are permitted eccentricity. It's one of the few honest perks of the office."

"What else do they think?"

"That I'm grieving." He glanced at the empty place settings. "My wife died six years ago. That part is true; that grief is mine, I'm sure of it. It's one of the few things I'm sure of anymore." His hands tightened on each other. "There was a daughter too. There *is* a daughter. I know her face—I could draw it, I think, if my hands still obeyed me—but her name..." He shook his head slowly. "It's gone. I lost it the way you lose a thing in a dream. You know you had it. You can feel the shape of where it was. But reach for it and your fingers close on nothing."

"Ilsette," I said.

And oh, the way his face changed. Not recognition—recognition would have been simple, would have passed. This was *relief*, vast and immediate, the relief of a drowning man shown a rope he cannot quite reach. "Ilsette. Yes. Yes, that's—it sounds right, it sounds like *her*, how did—" He stopped. Looked at me properly for the first time. And behind his eyes, that trapped bird threw itself against the glass again, harder, and I heard—felt, in the deep bone-place where I hold other people's memories—a voice that was not Aldric's say, very quietly:

*Who let you in?*

I kept my face pleasant. Inside, every thief-sense I possessed went taut as wire.

"A friend told me," I said. "Your daughter worries about you."

"She shouldn't. Or—no. She should. She *should* worry, that's what daughters are for, worrying about foolish old fathers who lose things." He was talking faster now, and I realized with a lurch that he was afraid—not of me, but of the losing itself, racing against it, trying to say everything before the next piece of himself went down the drain. "Listen. Whoever you are. There's something wrong with me. I've known for years, and I've told no one, because who does one tell? 'Forgive me, Chancellor, I believe something is eating my soul'? They'd bring physicians. They'd bring priests. They'd bring—" His voice dropped to almost nothing. "They'd bring whoever did this. Because someone did this. Didn't they. Someone did this, and I think—" 

The trapped bird went still.

"—and I think it works for the crown," Aldric finished, in a voice that was not entirely his own. Smooth. Patient. Amused. "Everything works for the crown, little keeper. That is rather the point of crowns."

The candles guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

Aldric blinked, and the gentleness flooded back into his face like water returning to a tide pool, and he smiled at me apologetically. "Forgive me. I drift. Where were we? You were telling me about my daughter. Is she well? Is she happy? I feel that I've failed her somehow, and I cannot for the life of me remember how, or when, or—" He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "I used to sing to her. I know that much. I used to sing to her, and she'd laugh at how badly I did it, and I would give anything—*anything*—to remember the song."

I thought of my hoard. Of the little velvet darkness inside me where I kept other people's treasures: a first kiss, a river's childhood, a dead woman's laughter. I had never once given one back. Taking was my gift; keeping was my sin.

But I reached in, and I found a lullaby—one of the guild's acquisitions, decades old, sold by a mother who needed bread more than she needed the memory of singing to her son. I had carried it for three years without listening. Now I opened it, and held it out across the table the way you'd hold out a coal in cupped hands, and I sang.

Badly. Emrys Fairweather cannot carry a tune in a bucket. My voice cracked on the second line and wandered off-key on the fourth, and none of that mattered, because Aldric's eyes filled, and his lips moved along with words he didn't know he knew, and when the song ended he sat there trembling like a man set down gently after a long fall.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's the song. How do you—that was *mine*. Wasn't it? I sang that. To her." He looked up, and his gaze was fierce and wet and, for one shining moment, entirely, unmistakably *his*. "Who are you? What are you?"

"I'm a thief, Your Majesty," I said. "One of the honest ones."

He laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and for a moment the room was warm, and the king was a man, and the mission was not a prophecy or a rite or a war between a parasite and a bloodline. It was this: a hollowed father, remembering a song.

Then the laugh died in his throat.

His head tilted. His eyes fixed on a point slightly to the left of my face, and the temperature of the room changed—not colder, exactly, but *thinner*, as if the air had been skimmed of something essential. When he spoke again, the voice was Aldric's shape but not his grain, the way a forged signature is the shape of a hand but not the hand.

"You sang to him," the Regent said through the king's mouth, wonderingly. "You gave him something back. Do you know how long it's been since anyone gave this body anything?" A slow blink. "They only ever take from kings. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement. Stability for memory. Continuity for self. The guild understands the arrangement better than anyone alive, little keeper. So why are you here, breaking it?"

"Because someone wrote me into a ledger a hundred years ago," I said, "and I've never liked leaving entries unresolved."

"Mm." The Regent considered me through Aldric's fading eyes, and I felt it the way you feel a hand hovering over your belongings—cataloguing, appraising, deciding whether to buy or burn. "Sarelle taught you that flippancy. I remember when she learned it herself. She was softer, once. They all are, at the start." The king's head bowed, briefly, and when it rose the gentleness was back—but stretched thin now, worn like cloth at the elbows. "Go," Aldric said, and his own voice broke through, ragged and urgent. "*Go*, boy, please—you shouldn't be here, it knows you now, it will use me, it will use *anything*—"

"It's already used you for thirty years."

"I know." And the terrible thing, the thing I still carry even now, even after everything, is that he said it kindly. As if comforting *me*. "I know what I am. Or what I'm not. I've had time to make peace with the arithmetic. But my daughter hasn't. Whatever you're planning—and you have the look of a planner, thief, I can see the ledgers turning behind your eyes—do it fast, and keep her out of it. Promise me."

Everyone keeps asking me for promises lately. It's becoming a theme.

"I'll try," I said, which is what liars say instead of yes, and what honest men say when yes would be a lie.

Aldric nodded slowly. Then, as I rose, he caught my wrist—his grip stronger than it had any right to be, the grip of a man holding onto the last solid thing in a flooding room.

"The song," he said. "Will I still know it tomorrow?"

I looked at this gentle, gutted man, this king reduced to a guest in his own body, and I could not lie to him. "I don't know," I said. "But I'll write it down. Somewhere it can't be eaten. And when this is over—if I'm still me, and you're still you—I'll give it back. Properly. Freely given, freely held. No ledger. No debt."

His eyes widened, and behind them, faint and far away, the trapped bird beat its wings once more.

"Freely given," he repeated, tasting the words like a foreign language. "What a strange idea. What a *wonderful* idea. Why has no one ever—" He stopped. Smiled. Let go of my wrist. "Hurry, thief. I don't know how many tomorrows I have left where I'm still the one answering."

I was three steps from the door when the candles went out—all of them, at once—and in the sudden dark, the Regent spoke one last time through the king's mouth, soft as dust settling:

"She's lovely, your princess. So brave, coming to see her father tomorrow. I do hope nothing frightens her."

* * *

Marta found me in the corridor, took one look at my face, and started walking fast.

"You heard," I said.

"Heard what?"

"Nothing. That's the problem. I need to get a message to Ilsette. Tonight. Before—"

"Before what, keeper?"

Before the thing wearing her father decides she's a loose thread. Before it uses his mouth, his guards, his seal—everything it owns—to tidy her away. Before I add another name to the list of people destroyed for standing near me.

"Before she makes a mistake," I said, "that I talked her into."

Marta studied me for a long moment, this laundress with the closed-ledger face, and then she did something I did not expect. She reached into her apron and pressed a folded scrap of paper into my hand—a note, written in a cramped, urgent hand I recognized from a prison cell: *Wren's hand.*

It said only: **She already knows. She's already gone.**

Gone where? I wanted to shout. Gone to do *what*?

But I already knew. Because I had spent one evening with Aldric, one hour, one song—and I would have torn the city apart for him. And Ilsette had spent years watching her father disappear by inches, memorizing his face while his name for her rotted away, singing to a man who couldn't remember the song.

She wasn't going to wait for my plan. She was going to make her own.

And somewhere above us, in the dark of the royal apartments, something ancient and patient and very, very hungry was smiling with her father's mouth—because it had just realized that the fastest way to catch a thief is to threaten the person he's promised to protect.

I ran.
