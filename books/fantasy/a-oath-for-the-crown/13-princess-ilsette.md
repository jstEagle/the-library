## 13. Princess Ilsette

I learned to count my father's disappearances the way other children learn constellations.

There was the night he forgot my mother's funeral was over and set a place for her at dinner, smiling at the empty chair as though she might still come down the corridor any moment, smelling of rosewater and court gossip. There was the afternoon he called me by my aunt's name for three hours straight and wept when I corrected him, because some part of him knew he'd been wrong and couldn't bear it. There were the smaller vanishings, too—the way his laugh had gone thin as old vellum, the way he sometimes paused mid-sentence as if listening to someone speak just behind his own teeth.

The physicians called it melancholy. The priests called it a trial of faith. My father's oldest friend, Lord Casserly, called it nothing at all, because calling it something would have required looking at it, and looking at things is not what courtiers do.

I called it theft. Because that is what it looked like to me—someone coming into a room while you sleep and carrying away pieces of you, one at a time, until what remains is furniture that occasionally says your name.

So when I heard footsteps hurrying out of my father's chambers at an hour when no honest person moves through the west wing, I did not send for guards.

I stepped out of the alcove where I'd been standing for the better part of two hours, holding a candle I had no intention of using, and blocked the corridor.

* * *

He was smaller than I expected. Thieves usually are; the trade selects for people who can fold themselves into shadows without complaint. He wore the grey of a servant who has been promoted exactly once and resents it, and he moved like water finding a drain—all economy, no wasted motion, right up until the moment he rounded the corner and found me standing there with my candle and my father's face arranged on my face in its most unyielding configuration.

He stopped so abruptly I heard his boots squeak against the marble.

"Princess," he said, and his voice did something complicated—part apology, part calculation, part the particular flatness of a man deciding how much lying the situation will bear.

"You're the second thief I've caught in this palace," I told him. "The first stole my mother's rings. The guards beat him half to death and recovered two of them." I let that sit between us for a moment. "You'll notice there are no guards here."

"I noticed," he said carefully, "that you're alone. Which seems unwise."

"It seems deliberate." I raised the candle slightly, more prop than light source; the wall sconces were doing the actual work. "Everything I do tonight is deliberate. You've been in my father's rooms. Don't bother denying it—I watched you leave them, and I have been watching those doors for six years. You are the first person besides physicians and liars to go through them at this hour, and I can tell the difference, because the physicians walk slowly and the liars walk quickly and you—" I tilted my head. "You walked like a man who had seen something."

His face did not move. It was, I will grant him, a very well-trained face. But his eyes flicked—just once, just briefly—back toward my father's door, and that flick told me everything the rest of him refused to say.

"What did you see?" I asked.

"Nothing," said the thief. "An audit. Routine. The guild keeps accounts on all its clients, and the crown is a client of long standing."

"Liar," I said pleasantly. "Try again."

"There was nothing worth taking."

"That's not a denial, that's a review. Try again."

He exhaled through his nose. For a moment I thought he would simply try to go around me—he was quick, I could see that—but something held him. Curiosity, maybe, or the same thing that had held me in that alcove for two hours: the suspicion that the person in front of you knows something you need.

"The king spoke to the empty room," he said finally, quietly, as though confessing to something shameful rather than reporting something true. "In a voice that wasn't his. And then he looked at me with eyes that weren't his either, and he knew my name, Princess. He knew my name before I ever gave it."

I felt that land somewhere beneath my ribs, in the place where I keep the things I am not allowed to say aloud at court.

"And your guild sent you to steal from a dying man's chambers," I said, "and instead you found whatever it is that's eating him."

Something shifted in his posture—a loosening, barely perceptible, like a knot worked almost free. "You know what it is."

"I know what it does." I had spent years assembling the shape of it from fragments: my father's missing afternoons, the physician's careful silences, the way certain members of the Privy Council had grown strangely comfortable with a king who could no longer contradict them. "I don't know its name. I don't know where it lives. But I know my father used to be in there, and I know he is being carried out of himself piece by piece, and I know that everyone whose job it is to notice has decided not to."

"Why?" The question came out of him raw, before he could sand it smooth. "If you know—if you're the princess, if you could raise armies—why haven't you?"

"Because the last person who tried was Wren," I said, and watched recognition cross his face like weather. Yes. He knew Wren. Of course he knew Wren; the disgraced chronicler who wrote true histories would be exactly the sort of man a memory-thief's guild kept files on. "Wren wrote down what he saw happening to my father. They arrested him within the week. He is in the Kettle now, and every copy of his history was burned, and my father—who loved that man once, who gave him his position—signed the order without reading it, because by then my father signed whatever was put in front of him."

The thief's jaw tightened. "Wren is in the Kettle."

"Wren is in the Kettle," I confirmed. "And I am a princess with no army, no allies, and a great many relatives who would find it convenient if I married abroad and stopped asking questions. So I stand in corridors at midnight and watch doors." I lowered the candle at last. "And tonight a door opened, and a man came out of it who looked like he'd seen the inside of my father's skull. So. Here we are."

* * *

We talked in the alcove, because I refused to be overheard and he refused to be seen, and the alcove served both purposes. It is a stupid little architectural accident—a recess behind a tapestry where a bored girl can hide during tedious receptions—and it has been the most useful room in the palace to me for six years.

He gave me his name eventually. Emrys Fairweather. He said it the way a man recites something he's memorized but doesn't quite believe: correctly, completely, and without warmth. I noticed that. I notice most things; it is the family curse, inherited from a mother who could read a room the way scholars read scripture.

He told me about the guild. Not everything—he told me the version of the guild that a stranger is permitted to hear, ledgers and acquisitions and clients of long standing—but enough. Enough that I understood the creature in my father was not merely a disease but a *customer*. That somewhere in the city there existed books recording, in tidy columns, the memory of my father's life being sold off or consumed or traded, entry by entry, year by year, while the court applauded the stability this produced.

"A stable king," Emrys said bitterly, "who agrees to everything. Do you understand what that's worth? Wars avoided. Rebellions quieted. A kingdom that runs smoothly because the man at the top of it has been emptied of every reason to object."

"I understand exactly what it's worth," I said. "I also understand what it costs. You've seen him. Tell me what you saw."

He was quiet a long time. When he answered, he didn't dress it up, and I was grateful for that; I have had enough of courtiers dressing things up to last several lifetimes.

"He remembers your face," Emrys said. "But not your name. He asked about you—I think he was asking about you—in words that weren't quite words. Like a man trying to describe a dream to someone who wasn't there. And underneath the voice that wasn't his, there was..." He searched for it. "Someone screaming. Very far down. Very faint. Like hearing shouting through the floor of a house you're visiting."

That was my father. That faint, distant, floor-below shouting. Six years I had felt it without being able to hear it, and here was a stranger confirming that it was real—that somewhere under the smooth, agreeable, hollowed thing that attended council meetings and signed orders against his friends, Aldric was still Aldric, still awake, still drowning in his own body.

I sat down on the alcove bench, because my legs informed me they were done pretending otherwise.

"He used to teach me chess," I said, mostly to myself. "Badly. He let me win and pretended he hadn't. He used to sing when he thought no one was listening—terribly, enthusiastically, songs his nurse taught him in a dialect nobody at court speaks anymore. He remembered all of them. Every verse." I looked up at the thief crouched across from me. "Do you know what I would give to hear him sing one? Just one? Off-key and wrong and entirely himself?"

Emrys didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice had changed—less the careful merchant, more the man underneath, whoever that was. "Yes," he said. "I think I do."

* * *

Here is what I had decided, standing in that alcove with my useless candle, before he ever came around the corner: that if anyone ever came out of my father's rooms knowing what was inside them, I would not turn them in. Whatever else they were—thief, spy, monster's courier—they would be the first living soul in six years to look at the truth of my father instead of politely looking away. You do not hand such a person to the guards. You hold onto them.

So I offered him what I had.

"I can get you back into these rooms whenever you need. I know the guards' rotations better than their captain does—I've bribed, flattered, and blackmailed my way through three separate shift changes, and I keep the current schedule embroidered into the lining of a fan, which no one has ever thought to search, because no one takes a princess's fan seriously." I let him absorb that. "I can get messages to Wren. I cannot get him out—not yet—but I can get him paper, and paper in the Kettle is worth more than gold. And I can do the one thing no one in this palace expects anyone to do."

"Which is?"

"Tell the truth, loudly, at the worst possible moment." I smiled, and it was my mother's smile, the one that made ambassadors request different seating arrangements. "My father cannot be saved quietly. Whatever you're planning—it involves rites, I'd wager, and witnesses, and moments when everything hangs on one person doing one impossible thing—will require the court to be looking somewhere specific at a specific time. I can make the entire court look anywhere I choose. I have been practicing invisibility in reverse my whole life."

He stared at me. "You want to help."

"I want my father back," I said. "Help is simply the instrument at hand. You came out of that room having seen him. You didn't run to your guildmistress—you ran toward the corridor where I happened to be standing, which means some part of you already wanted a co-conspirator more than you wanted safety. So yes. I want to help. And I want you to stop flinching every time I say something that surprises you, because we are going to be doing a great deal of mutually surprising work together, and flinching wastes time."

He laughed—short, startled, genuine. It was the first honest sound I'd heard from him all night, and I catalogued it the way I catalogue everything: proof of personhood. Thieves who laugh are thieves who can be trusted with something larger than theft.

"One condition," he said.

"Name it."

"When this is done—whatever it costs, however it ends—your father gets told. Everything. What was taken from him, who took it, and who stood beside him while it was given back." His voice went quiet and strange. "People should get to remember who fought for them. Even if... even if the remembering has to be done on their behalf."

I studied him for a long moment. There was something underneath that condition, some private grief wearing a principle's clothing, and I did not press it. Pressing is for later. Later is for pressing.

"Agreed," I said, and shook his hand, which scandalized approximately no one, since no one was watching, which is the only way I have ever been able to do anything properly.

* * *

He left through the servants' stair, moving like poured shadow, and I returned to my chambers and did not sleep. Instead I sat by the window with the fan in my lap, tracing the embroidered guard rotations with one finger, and thought about the sound he'd described: shouting through the floor.

All those years, I had told myself my father was going. Dying by degrees, fading, dissolving—the gentle vocabulary of loss, the vocabulary that asks nothing of you except endurance. It had never once occurred to me that he might be *held*. That the man who sang in forbidden dialects might be locked in a small dark room inside his own skull, listening to a parasite conduct his life badly, waiting—for what? For someone to come through the door.

A thief had come through the door. A thief with careful hands and a trained face and a laugh that escaped him exactly once, and eyes that flicked back toward my father's chamber the way a man's eyes flick toward a wound.

Toward dawn, I finally lay down, and dreamed—not of my father, but of a ledger. Great bound volumes stretching past sight, and somewhere among them a page with my name on it, written in ink that gleamed wetly, freshly, as though it had been written very recently by someone who knew me.

I woke uneasy and could not have said why.

Three nights later, Emrys came to me pale as candle tallow and asked, in a voice stripped of all his careful training, whether I had ever dreamed of a face I did not recognize.

I told him I had dreamed of a ledger.

He went very still, and I understood, watching him, that whatever hunted my father had begun hunting us both—and that it liked to introduce itself in dreams, wearing borrowed faces, making offers.

"We need to talk," he said. "Now. Before you sleep again."

I blew out my candle, and we talked.
