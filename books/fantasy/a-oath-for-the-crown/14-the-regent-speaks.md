## 14. The Regent Speaks

I had always assumed that if my past ever came for me, it would come with knives. Guild work teaches you to expect your debts to arrive armed. What I did not expect was for it to come politely, in a dream, wearing my own face and offering to buy me a drink.

The dream began, as my dreams had begun lately, in snow.

I want to be clear about something first: I am not a man who dreams much. Years of lifting memories out of other people's heads taught me that sleep is a door best kept barred. A thief who dreams loudly is a thief who leaves his hoard unattended. So I had trained myself into shallow, gray sleep — the kind where you surface every hour to check the locks. It is a lonely way to rest, but it is *mine*, which by then was more than I could say about most things in my head.

The snow changed that. The snow came in under the door like water, and it was warm.

I stood in a village I had never seen and knew intimately. That is a strange sentence to write and a stranger thing to live. Every detail arrived pre-loaded with feeling: the crooked bell-tower, the well with its rope gone green, the smell of woodsmoke and bread from a bakery that no longer existed anywhere on earth except inside me. Snow fell without cold. Somewhere behind me, a woman's voice said a name — *my* name, the real one, the one I'd paid a day of my life to almost-remember — and the sound of it went through me like a hand through water.

"You're doing it again," said a voice in front of me. "Standing in other people's weather."

The man in the snow looked like a portrait I might have commissioned of myself by someone who'd only heard me described. My height. My build. My habit of standing with one shoulder slightly lower, as if perpetually bracing against a ledger falling off a shelf. But the face was wrong in the way a memory is wrong when someone else has held it too long — smoothed at the edges, polished, *loved*. He wore fine clothes cut in a style two centuries out of fashion, and he smiled at me with an expression I recognized because I practiced it daily in guild mirrors: pleasant, attentive, entirely closed.

"Emrys Fairweather," he said, tasting the name like a coin he suspected was clipped. "Or should I use the other one? I know it, you understand. I've known it since before you did."

"The Regent," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is the single most useful trick I own.

"At your service." He bowed. Actually bowed, deep and courtly, snow gathering on his shoulders and refusing to melt. "Though I confess I prefer the older title. *Custodian.* It sounds less like something you'd find under a floorboard."

"You've found me in a dream," I said. "That's under quite a few floorboards."

He laughed, and the laugh was the worst part, because it was *my* laugh. I have a laugh I'm not proud of — too quick, a little sharp, the laugh of a man who learned early that being amusing keeps hands off you. Hearing it come out of another mouth, in my own voice, in a village that existed only in the sealed room of my skull, made my skin try to leave my body.

"I'll be brief," he said. "You've been rude enough to make this necessary. You broke into my house."

"Your house is a king."

"My house," he said gently, "is *memory*. Kings are merely where I keep my furniture." He began to walk, and the village walked with him, assembling itself ahead of his feet — a lane here, a shuttered window there, a child's sled half-buried and perfectly preserved. He was building my memory around us as we moved, brick by remembered brick, showing me he could. "You've been reading my correspondence. Wren's little histories. The archives under the Guildhall — Sarelle really ought to change those locks; she's had the same key since the reign of Aldric's grandfather's grandfather. And now you stand in the crypt with a princess and a rite you barely understand, and you think yourself a threat."

"Am I not?"

"Oh, you are." He stopped and turned, and for one unguarded instant the pleasant mask slipped and I saw what lived underneath: vastness. Not malice — malice would have been manageable. This was *accumulation*. Centuries of dead kings' birthdays stacked like cordwood. Ten thousand coronations. Every lullaby sung to every royal child, hoarded and digested and worn smooth as river stones. Looking into him was like looking into the ledger of a country. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't look like temptation. It looks like relief.

The Regent sat down on the green-rope well as though he'd been sitting on it for a hundred years, which, in a sense, he had — it was my memory, after all, and everything in my memory was now apparently common property. He gestured for me to sit on a mounting block nearby. I remained standing. He seemed to find this charming.

"Do you know how you got here?" he asked. "To this village, in your head?"

"A dead woman's memory. My mother's line. It came with the rite."

"Mm. Partially true. The rite opened the door." He tapped his temple. "But someone furnished the room. Who do you imagine kept the memory-keepers' records, all those centuries? Who archived the bloodlines, stored the rites, maintained the *inventory*? Your family were librarians, boy. I was the library." He spread his hands, and snowflakes hung suspended between them, each one holding a tiny glinting image — a face, a name, a moment. "When the last purge came, when the old king grew suspicious and started burning keepers, someone had to smuggle the inventory out. A woman with a baby. She came to me first. We made an arrangement."

My mouth had gone dry, which is impressive in a dream made of warm snow. "What arrangement?"

"She hid the rite where only her blood could reach it. And she left a deposit with me. Insurance." His smile widened by a degree, precise as a ledger entry. "A memory. Hers. Of you."

The world tilted. I don't mean dramatically; I mean the snowfall actually skewed sideways, because the dream was mine and my balance was failing. "You're lying."

"I never lie. Lying is for creatures with something to hide. I *am* the hiding place." He reached into his coat — my coat, two centuries old — and drew out nothing at all, just a gesture, but the gesture *held* something; I could feel it across the space between us like heat from a stove. "Would you like to hear it? Her voice. The weight of you in her arms. The name she gave you and the reason she gave it. I have kept it safe for twenty-five years. No mold, no rot, no decay. I am, whatever else I am, an excellent keeper."

"And the price," I said, because there is always a price, and a man who forgets that ends up as an entry in someone else's book.

"Walking away." He said it lightly, tossing it like a beggar's coin. "Leave the crypt. Leave the princess. Let the rite stay buried. In exchange: everything. Not just her memory — *all* of it. Every piece the Guild took when they found you half-frozen on their steps. Your childhood. Your mother's face. Your name, whole and unbroken, returned to its rightful owner. Plus interest." He leaned forward. "And I'll throw in the Guild's entire vault while I'm at it. Every stolen first kiss, every bottled grief, the river's childhood. You could give them back. All of them. Think of it, Emrys — the great restitution. The keeper of accounts balances the books at last. Doesn't that sing to you? I've read your heart's margins. It sings."

And here is the shameful part, the part I will deny under oath: it sang.

I stood in the warm snow and let myself want it for three full seconds, which in the economy of temptation is a fortune. My mother's voice. My name. Twenty-five years of being unwritten, undone, erased so thoroughly that even I had become a stranger to myself — and this creature offered to hand it all back in a single transaction, clean, painless, *final*. No more hoard of borrowed feelings. No more practicing smiles in mirrors. Just me, complete, walking out of the dream into a life that finally had a first page.

"You helped take it," I said quietly. "Didn't you. My memories. When the Guild bought me."

"They didn't buy you," the Regent said. "They *traded* you. To me. You were collateral on a debt — Sarelle's predecessor owed me for certain services rendered during a succession crisis, and a memory-keeper's orphaned son is worth a great deal to a creature in my line of work. I took the archive of you. Then I looked at you — a hollowed child, exactly the shape of the kings I wear — and I thought, *no. Not this one. This one I will leave empty on purpose.* An unwritten man makes a wonderful wild card. I've been waiting twenty-five years to see how you'd be played." He shrugged, elegant, unapologetic. "So yes. I helped take it. Which means I alone can give it back. Every theft needs a fence, Emrys. I am simply offering to reverse the flow."

"Why?" The question came out rawer than I intended. "Why offer at all? If I'm such a useful wild card, why fold the hand?"

"Because you're not the only card on the table anymore." For the first time, something flickered behind his face that wasn't confidence. Something faster than confidence. Fear wears many costumes, and on a creature made of memory, fear wears *haste*. "The girl. Ilsette. She has her grandmother's spine and her father's stubbornness and none of her father's holes. Blood rites are crude instruments, but she carries the oldest version, and old versions have teeth. And Wren—" he paused, and the pause had weight, "—Wren has been corresponding with people he shouldn't. People outside the city. The Guild's monopoly is cracking, and cracks are how I once got in. I do not intend to be evicted by a chronicler with a grudge and a princess with a birthright." He stood, brushing snow from his antique coat. "So. One last time, plainly, because I respect bookkeeping: walk away, and wake tomorrow with your whole life in your head. Refuse, and I will take from you the way I take from kings — slowly, from the inside, until some future thief finds you smiling at empty air."

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of honest accounting, exactly how close I came.

Not because I'm weak — or not only because I'm weak. Because he was *good*. He had studied me the way I study ledgers: line by line, margin note by margin note. He knew I wanted my mother's voice more than I wanted air. He knew I dreamed of returning the vault's stolen goods, that some nights the guilt of my profession sat on my chest like a sleeping cat. He knew loneliness was the load-bearing wall of my personality and he offered to demolish it with a handshake. That is not seduction. That is architecture.

And beneath the offer, coiled and patient, was the truth he never quite said aloud: he wanted me *empty*. A hollow vessel, grateful and full of someone else's memories, was exactly the shape he liked his houses. He'd worn forty kings. Why not a keeper? Why not the one man with the keys to both the Guild and the crown?

I thought of Aldric. Gentle, fading Aldric, who remembered his daughter's face and not her name, who had thanked me — *thanked* me — for visiting a stranger. I thought of the young king still screaming behind the walls of the thing that wore him. I thought of Ilsette saying, in the dark of the safehouse, *he used to hum when he worked. I'd give anything to hear it wrong again.*

Then I did the only thing I am truly gifted at. I counted.

"Here's my counter-entry," I said. "You get nothing. Not my walk-away, not my gratitude, not my vacancy. And you should know something about me, Custodian, since you've read my margins: I keep duplicates. Everything you claim to hold of mine — my mother's memory, my name, my history — I have spent months copying, hiding, seeding through places you can't reach, in heads you can't wear. Burn my hoard and the copies survive. Chase the copies and you'll find I've written them in blood, which only opens for my line. You cannot own what I've already given away." I met his polished, beloved, terrible face and smiled my quick sharp smile. "You taught the keepers archiving. Be proud. Your student out-archived you."

For a long moment the snow stopped falling. The whole dream held its breath — the bell-tower, the well, the suspended flakes with their tiny captive faces — and the Regent looked at me with an expression I had never seen on any face, mine or otherwise.

It was respect. Cold, furious, ledger-balanced respect.

"No," he said softly. "You haven't copied everything. There's one memory you couldn't copy, because you don't have it yet. The one where you lose." And the dream folded up around him like a ledger snapping shut, and the last thing I saw before waking was my own face, smiling, receding into white.

* * *

I woke in the safehouse with dawn coming gray through the shutters and my heart going like a debtor fleeing bailiffs. My hands were shaking. I sat up, checked the hoard — all present, all locked, the anchor memory still sealed in its deepest drawer — and laughed, once, too loud, at the absurdity of a man auditing his own skull before breakfast.

Ilsette was already awake. She stood in the doorway with two cups of something hot and an expression that meant she'd heard me laugh and hadn't liked the sound of it.

"He came to you," she said. Not a question.

"How—"

"You said my father's name in your sleep. And then you said *no* very firmly, twice, and then you apologized to someone." She handed me a cup and sat down across from me, and her eyes were her father's eyes, which is to say they saw far too much. "The Regent doesn't visit unless he's frightened. What did he offer you?"

"Everything I've ever lost," I said. "In exchange for everything I've recently found."

She nodded slowly, the way people nod at bad news they'd already priced in. "And you refused."

"For now."

Her cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "*For now?*"

"That's the trouble with temptation, Highness." I drank, because my hands needed something to betray besides words. "It doesn't expire. It compounds." 

Outside, somewhere below the safehouse window, a church bell began to ring — the wrong pattern, the alarm cadence, the one that meant fire. Smoke was already threading up past the shutters, and it was coming from the direction of the Guildhall, where a century-old prophecy ledger had waited in the dark for someone to burn it.

Sarelle had made her move.
