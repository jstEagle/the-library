## 15. Ledgers Burned

The bell rang three times, stopped, and started again — the fire cadence, the one every thief in Vellamarch knows in their bones the way priests know vespers. I was moving before Ilsette finished setting down her cup.

"Guildhall's burning," I said, which was a stupid thing to say, because she could see the smoke as well as I could, threading up past the shutters like ink poured into water.

"I can see that." She was already on her feet, pulling on a plain grey cloak that made her look like nobody's daughter at all. "The question is what's *in* it."

"The prophecy ledger," I said. "Sarelle doesn't burn things. She archives them. She once had a clerk flogged for discarding a receipt from 1811. If she's burning it—"

"Then she's not destroying evidence. She's destroying your claim to it." Ilsette pulled the hood up. "No ledger, no prophecy. No prophecy, no proof you're anything but a runaway clerk. Clever woman."

"She's not clever," I said, and heard how my voice cracked on it. "She's thorough. There's a difference, and the difference is that thorough people finish what they start."

We went out through the laundry stair, because the front door of a safehouse is a suggestion, not an exit. The streets were already filling — not with panic but with its opposite, that terrible orderly calm of people who have been told to stay indoors and are obeying. Guild criers moved along the corners with lanterns, calling the same words at each one:

*"Emrys Fairweather, keeper of accounts. Struck from the rolls. Dead to the guild. Any hand that feeds him feeds ash to his own hearth."*

They said it like a price list.

I stood in the shadow of a cooper's awning and listened to my own name being auctioned off street by street, and I want to tell you I felt afraid, and I did, but underneath the fear there was something worse: a kind of professional admiration. Sarelle hadn't just declared me dead. She'd declared me *unwritten* — struck from the rolls the way you'd strike a bad debt, so that no record anywhere would show I'd ever existed. For twenty years I had built my life on being invisible, and I had thought that was my own achievement. It took hearing it done to me by someone else to understand that invisibility was never mine. It was hers. She had permitted it, the way you permit a candle to burn in a room you own.

And now she'd snuffed it.

"We can't go back for anything," Ilsette said quietly. "Not the hoard."

My stomach dropped through the cobblestones. "The hoard is in the safehouse."

"The safehouse will be the second place they look. The first is wherever they think you'll run." She caught my arm. "Emrys. Listen to me. A memory hoard is glass and wax and paper. You are the only person alive who can open the rite. They can't take that out of your skull without killing you, and killing you ends their problem too neatly — Sarelle wants you *brought back*, or she wants the Regent to have you. Either way, you're worth more breathing."

"You're very comforting."

"I'm a princess. Comforting is beneath my station." But her grip on my arm didn't loosen, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not fear for herself. Fear for me. It looked strange on her, like a crown worn crooked.

We cut east through the tannery lanes, where the smell covered everything and even guild hounds lose a trail, and by the time the bells went quiet we were three districts away, standing in the doorway of a chandler's shop that Wren had told me about weeks ago — one of his old contacts, he'd said, a man who owed him for a history written true.

The chandler took one look at us and turned white.

"No," he said. "Whatever you're selling, no."

"We're not selling," Ilsette said. "We need passage to Wren."

"Wren." The man laughed, and it was a horrible sound, wet and short. "You haven't heard."

The floor tilted. I've spent my life reading ledgers; I know the moment before bad news the way I know the moment before a lie — the little stillness, the pen hovering. His hands were still. His eyes went everywhere but at us.

"Heard what?"

"They came at dawn. Not guild — palace guard, but wrong ones, wearing the king's colors like costumes. They had warrants with the royal seal, and the seal was *fresh*, Emrys Fairweather, and everyone knows the king hasn't signed anything himself in years." He wiped his mouth. "They took him. The chronicler. Carried him out through the front like a sack of meal, and his landlady's screaming still hasn't stopped."

Ilsette made a small sound. I didn't. I stood very still and did arithmetic, because arithmetic is what I do when everything else burns: fresh seal, wrong guards, dawn raid. The Regent had signed the warrant itself. The thing wearing the crown had reached into the city's law and pulled Wren out of it like a splinter.

"Why?" Ilsette asked, though I think she knew. "He's been in prison for six years. He's no threat."

"He's a witness," I said. "Wren wrote true histories. He knows the cycle — every king the Regent has eaten, going back four hundred years. And now there's a blood heir of the memory-keepers walking around with a plan." I looked at the chandler. "Where were they taken him?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. That's rather the point of taking someone, isn't it?"

* * *

We spent that night in the crypt of a deconsecrated chapel that Ilsette swore no one had entered since her grandfather's reign, and she was almost right. We sat among the dry bones of minor nobility, sharing bread that tasted of stone dust, and I did something I'm not proud of: I opened my hoard.

Not the whole thing. Just a sliver — the memory of my first night in the Guildhall, age nine, when Sarelle herself had found me hiding in the ledger room and instead of turning me out had sat down beside me and said, *Well. Someone has to keep the numbers honest.* Twenty years of loyalty, distilled into a single warm minute, and I held it up against everything that had happened this week and tried to make them fit together.

They wouldn't fit. That was the horror of it. Both things were true. She had saved me, and she had caged me, and she had done both with the same unhurried kindness, and now she was hunting me with the full network of the only family I'd ever had, and somewhere under all of it she still believed — I was almost sure she still believed — that she was protecting me from myself.

"You're doing the thing," Ilsette said. "Where you go somewhere behind your eyes and count."

"It's called thinking."

"It looks like grief wearing an accountant's face." She broke her bread in half and handed me the bigger piece, which is either kindness or strategy depending on the princess. "Tell me about her. Sarelle. Tell me why it hurts."

"Because she taught me everything," I said. "How to lift a memory clean, how to hold it without bleeding into it, how to read a person's past the way you read a ledger — looking for the entry that doesn't balance. She found me starving in the ledger room and she fed me and trained me and never once asked where I came from, which I thought was generosity." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "It was bookkeeping. An unclaimed asset is a liability. She never asked because she never wanted to know what she might owe."

"And now she knows what you are."

"Now she knows what I cost."

Silence, then, except for the small sounds a building makes when it's older than the country around it. Ilsette leaned her head back against a sarcophagus and closed her eyes, and I watched her and understood suddenly, completely, that she was running the same arithmetic I was — except her column was her father, and every day the Regent wore him was a day subtracted from whatever remained of Aldric, and she had known it for years and had done nothing because there had been nothing to do, until a clerk with stolen memories walked out of her father's chambers carrying a prophecy.

"We get him back," she said, to the dark. "Wren. Whatever it costs."

"Yes."

"You have a plan."

"I have a ledger," I said. "Every debt in this city runs through the guild eventually. Even the Regent's. Especially the Regent's. Sarelle said it herself once, drunk on festival wine: *the crown is our oldest client.* Oldest clients have oldest accounts. And oldest accounts—" I tapped my own temple, where the rite sat coiled behind my eyes like a debt waiting for collection, "—have terms."

"That's not a plan, that's a metaphor."

"In my experience, Highness, metaphors are plans that haven't been audited yet."

She smiled despite herself, and for one moment in that cold crypt, with my name being cried through the streets above and my oldest friend in a cell somewhere and my life's work burned to ash, I felt something that had no business existing: hope. Small, badly kept, misfiled — but there.

Then the chapel door opened.

* * *

There were three of them, and I knew two by reputation: Vess, who lifts faces the way I lift memories, and Corvane, the lead hunter, whose file I had personally maintained and whose courage, I knew from his own intake records, had been purchased from a dying soldier forty years ago and had never quite fit him. The third was young and nervous and held a lantern too high.

Corvane spoke first. "Fairweather. The Mistress says come home."

"The Mistress burned my home."

"The Mistress burned a *ledger*. Homes are different things." He stepped forward, boots careful on old flagstone. "She wants you unharmed. I'd prefer that too. Unharmed keeps the paperwork simple."

Ilsette had gone very still beside me, and I saw her hand drift toward the knife at her belt, and I put my own hand over hers and pressed it flat. *No,* I mouthed. Because here is what twenty years of keeping accounts teaches you: you never fight a collection agent on their terms. You change the terms.

"Corvane," I said pleasantly. "Intake year 1843. Courage, field-grade, acquired from a soldier named Halloway, dying at Redford Bridge. Do you know what the intake notes say? *Subject reports the courage fits poorly. Subject reports it sits like armor two sizes too large.*" I took a step toward him, and I watched the young one's lantern waver. "I filed those notes myself. I've read your whole account, Corvane. Every line. Shall I recite the rest?"

"Stop."

"There's a page where you describe dreaming of Redford Bridge. Of the moment before Halloway stopped being afraid. You dream it from *inside* him, because the memory remembers its original owner, and it's been trying to go home for forty years—"

"*Stop.*"

"—and the reason you drink before every job, and the reason your hands shake after, is that you are a man wearing another man's bravery, and some cold, honest part of you has always known it doesn't belong to you—"

He lunged.

Here is the trick of it, the one Sarelle taught me in a warm room when I was nine years old and she still loved me: a memory isn't held in the mind. It's held in the body. In the breath, in the stance, in the set of the shoulders. To steal it, you don't reach into a man's head. You match his rhythm, and you *lift*.

So when Corvane lunged, I breathed with him, stepped inside his reach the way you'd step into a dance, and lifted.

Courage leaves a body fast, like water from a tipped cup. One moment he was the most feared hunter in Vellamarch; the next he was a man standing in a crypt holding a sword he suddenly couldn't remember why he'd drawn, and his face — gods, his face. Forty years of borrowed bravery drained out of him and left the original stock underneath: a frightened boy from the river districts who had joined the guild because the alternative was worse, and who had never once, not for a single day, felt safe.

He sat down on the flagstones. He began to cry, quietly, terribly, the way men cry when they've forgotten how.

Vess stared at him, then at me, and did the smartest thing anyone did all week: she ran. The young one dropped the lantern and followed, and the light rolled and guttered across the bones of dead lords, and then it was just me and Ilsette and Corvane, weeping among the sarcophagi like a child at a funeral he was too old for.

I crouched beside him. I meant to say something merciful. What came out was bookkeeping.

"I'll return it," I said. "When this is finished. Every drop. You have my word as a struck-from-the-rolls dead man, which is apparently the only kind of word I have left."

He nodded without looking up.

Ilsette touched my shoulder. "Emrys. The lantern. Look."

The lantern had rolled against the base of the far wall, and its light had caught something scratched into the stone — old scratches, filled with centuries of dust, in a hand I recognized because I had spent a week learning it from a century-old ledger. Memory-keeper's script. My mother's script, or her mother's, worn into the wall of a crypt beneath a chapel in the capital, and beside it, half-obscured:

*a door below the altar keeps what the crown cannot*

I read it twice. Then a third time, while Corvane wept and Ilsette held the lantern high and the smoke from a burned ledger was still settling somewhere over the city.

A crypt beneath a crypt. A place the guild had never thought to look, because the guild had never been invited into the memory-keepers' secrets — but a princess's ancestors had built chapels, and princesses' ancestors kept their own accounts.

"Emrys," Ilsette said softly. "What is it?"

I stood, and brushed the dust from my knees, and felt the darkest hour of my life tilt — just slightly — toward something else.

"It's an entry," I said, "that somebody hid from the audit. Come on. Bring the light."

Above us, faint through the chapel's broken roof, the bells began again — not the alarm cadence this time, but the slow tolling of a funeral bell, and I remember thinking, with the clarity that comes in the worst hours of a life: *someone is ringing it for me.*

Let them. Dead men keep the best secrets.

And the door below the altar was already cold under my hands.
