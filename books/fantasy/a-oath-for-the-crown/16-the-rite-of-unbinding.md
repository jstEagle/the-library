## 16. The Rite of Unbinding

The crypt beneath the palace smelled of candlewax, cold stone, and the particular dust that accumulates only around dead royalty. I had audited ledgers in worse places, technically. There had been that flooded archive under the Tanners' Row, where the ledgers grew a fine coat of mold and the mold, in one memorable case, had grown opinions. But this was the first time I had ever prepared to do inventory in a room where the stock was me.

"You're making that face," Ilsette said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're doing arithmetic nobody asked for and you don't like the total."

She was kneeling at the far end of the crypt, chalking a circle onto the floor around the sarcophagus of some long-dead queen whose name had been worn off the lid by centuries of reverent touching. Ilsette chalked the way her father, I suspected, had once ruled — precisely, without flourish, and with the quiet fury of someone who had watched other people do the job badly for years.

"The total is fine," I lied.

"You're a terrible liar for a man whose entire profession is lying with numbers."

"Ledgers don't lie. They just wait for you to catch up to them." I set down the last of the candles and looked around the crypt. "Which is more than I can say for prophecy."

* * *

We had come down the night before the rite, because the rite had a schedule, and the schedule did not care about anyone's feelings. Wren's notes — smuggled out of his cell in a manner I still didn't fully understand and had decided not to ask about, because Ilsette's explanation had involved a guard, a chessboard, and the phrase "he owed me from before the disgrace" — laid it out in the chronicler's cramped, furious hand:

*The unbinding requires two. A blood-heir to speak the words of severance, for the Regent is bound to the royal line and only royal blood may cut it. And a keeper — a mind trained to hold and hoard — to stand at the center of the circle and hold the creature's attention. The Regent feeds on memory. A keeper who opens his hoard to it is, to the Regent, what a lit lamp is to a moth. It cannot look away. And while it looks away from the king, the words can be spoken.*

*The keeper must offer his deepest memory. Not a trifle. Not a decoy. The Regent has eaten a hundred lifetimes of memory and it knows the taste of the true thing. Offer it anything less and it will turn back to the king before the rite is finished, and both of you will be consumed.*

*The keeper will not survive unchanged. What the Regent takes, it keeps.*

Wren had underlined that last line three times. Wren, who had gone to prison for writing true things, had underlined the truest one.

I had read the passage four times on the way down and then, because I am who I am, a fifth time while pretending to arrange candles.

"You've gone quiet again," Ilsette said. She straightened, chalk dust on her fingers, and studied me with her father's eyes — no, that wasn't right. With her own eyes. The king's eyes were mostly gone now, hollowed into something the Regent wore like a borrowed coat. Ilsette's eyes were still entirely her own, and they were far too perceptive for my comfort. "Emrys. Say the arithmetic out loud. Whatever it is, I'd rather hear it ugly than watch you swallow it."

"It's nothing."

"Say it anyway."

I lit a candle with fingers that did not shake, which I considered a professional triumph. "The rite needs a keeper," I said. "Someone who can hold a memory up in front of that thing and keep it held, steady, for as long as it takes you to speak the words. The words take time. Wren's notes say the severance is seven sentences, and each one has to land true, and the Regent will be fighting you the whole way."

"Yes."

"So the keeper's memory has to be deep enough to hold it. Not just deep. *Deepest.* The anchor. The one memory the whole self hangs from." I turned the candle in my fingers. "That's all. I was just confirming the terms."

Ilsette set down the chalk. She crossed the crypt and stood in front of me, and for a moment she was not a princess or an ally or the only person in this city who had as much reason to hate the Regent as I did. She was just a tired woman in a cold room, looking at a tired man.

"You already know which memory it will be," she said. It was not a question.

I am, as established, a terrible liar in the presence of royalty.

"It doesn't matter," I said. "It's a tool. That's all a memory is, in the end. Inventory. You hold it up, you use it, you put it back on the shelf."

"That's what the guild taught you."

"The guild taught me a great many useful things."

"The guild also taught you that people are entries," she said, "and you spent a whole chapter of your life unlearning it, so don't you dare recite it back at me now, in a crypt, the night before you feed yourself to a monster." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse — it got quieter, the way it did when she talked about her father. "You don't have to tell me what it is. But don't tell me it doesn't matter. I've watched one man be hollowed out while everyone around him pretended it was nothing. I will not stand in this circle and do the pretending myself."

I looked at her. I thought about the memory — my memory, the one I had not told her about, the one I had not told anyone about, because some inventory you keep in a locked drawer and some inventory you keep in a locked drawer *inside* the locked drawer.

Snow. A woman's voice. A name that wasn't Fairweather.

The truth of who I was before the guild took me in.

"I know which one it has to be," I said finally. "And I'm not going to tell you, because if I say it out loud it stops being a weapon and starts being a wound, and I need it to be a weapon tomorrow." I managed something that was almost a smile. "Consider it professional discretion."

She held my gaze for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she nodded. "My father used to say something like that. About state secrets." Her mouth twisted. "Back when he was still in there to say it."

* * *

We worked through the small hours. The circle needed to be closed but not sealed — chalk, not blood, because blood invited, and the last thing anyone wanted was to invite the Regent anywhere before we were ready for it. Ilsette marked the seven stations of the severance, one for each sentence of the rite, and I checked her work the way I checked everything, twice and then once more out of spite.

At the third station she paused. "You're certain about the timing?"

"The Regent moves at coronations. Wren was certain. It's fed on every crowning for four hundred years — the whole court assembled, every eye on the throne, every mind in the room full of ceremony and hope. A banquet." I set another candle. "It'll be fat and slow and looking at the crown. That's our moment."

"And if it isn't slow? If it's already eaten so much of him that it's faster than we think?"

"Then we improvise," I said. "I'm told that's what people do when the ledger doesn't balance."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed, like something stolen. I recognized the gesture. It was mine.

We finished the circle near dawn. The candles burned evenly. The chalk lines held. The dead queen in her nameless sarcophagus kept her own counsel, as dead queens generally do, and I found I envied her a little. She had gotten out of her story with her name worn away by kindness rather than theft. There were worse fates. I had read most of them in the ledgers.

Ilsette went up to sleep — or to lie on a bed staring at the ceiling, which in my experience was the royal version of sleep — and I stayed.

I told her it was to check the circle a final time. That was even true, in the way that a ledger entry is true: accurate, and incomplete.

The real reason was the drawer inside the drawer.

* * *

I sat down in the center of the circle, cross-legged, like a child at lessons, and I opened my hoard.

You should understand what the hoard is, if you don't already. It isn't a place. It isn't a room in my head with shelves, though that's the image I use, because I am a keeper of accounts and I think in shelves. It's a discipline. A way of holding a memory so that it stays *yours* — not dissolved into the general fog of a life, not worn smooth by retelling, but kept bright and specific, the way a coin stays bright when it's never spent. The guild trained it into me. Or perhaps — and this was the thought that had been circling me for weeks now, patient as a debt — perhaps I had been born knowing how, and the guild had merely found the talent already installed, the way you find a lock already fitted to a door.

I walked my shelves.

The stolen first kiss, warm and ridiculous. The river's childhood, all green light and the smell of mud. The grief in the blue bottle, which I had never dared open all the way. Sarelle's face the day she told me I was dead to the guild. Wren's voice through the cell door, reciting the two endings of the prophecy. Ilsette's laugh just now, short and stolen.

And beneath them, at the bottom, behind the inner lock, the one I had carried since the village in the snow. Since the old woman had looked at my face and said *you have your mother's eyes* and the ground had shifted under a lifetime of assumed ground.

The memory of who I was before.

I won't describe it here. Not because I'm being dramatic — though I have been accused — but because I made a decision in that crypt, alone, at the hour when candles burn lowest, and the decision was this: it would be spent, not told. There is a difference. A told memory can be argued with. A spent one is gone, and what it bought is bought forever.

I held it up. I looked at it the way I had looked at every impossible thing in the guild's ledgers, and for the first time I let myself appraise it honestly, as an entry, with a value attached.

Here is what the appraisal said. It said: *this is the heaviest thing you own. It is the only thing you own that cannot be replaced. If you lose it, you will not know what you lost, which is the cruelest kind of loss, the kind with no mourning in it, because mourning requires a name.*

And it said: *the Regent has eaten a hundred kings. It has never eaten a man who chose the meal.*

That was the line I kept coming back to. Every memory the Regent had ever consumed had been *taken* — ripped out of screaming minds, devoured by force, the way the guild took things, the way I had once taken things. It had never been *given* something. It had never been handed a memory by someone who opened the drawer on purpose, who said *here, this is the truest thing I have, take all of it, and while you're swallowing, look away from the king.*

Gluttony has a weakness, and the weakness is the moment of the swallow. Ask any cutpurse who has ever worked a feast.

I put the memory back behind the inner lock. I did not seal the lock. That was the whole point. Tomorrow, when the Regent poured into me — and it would pour, Wren's notes were clear on that, it would pour into an opened hoard like water into a broken cistern — it would find that drawer waiting, unlatched, and it would know, in whatever way such a thing knows, that this was the deepest thing. The true thing. The taste it had waited four hundred years for.

And it would drink.

And while it drank, a princess with chalk on her hands would speak seven sentences, and each one would land true.

I sat in the circle until the candles guttered, and I did the last piece of arithmetic, the one I had been avoiding since the crypt door closed behind us. It was simple, as the worst sums are.

*Cost: everything I am. Return: one king, restored. Probability of recovery of principal: zero.*

I had spent my whole life keeping other people's accounts. It seemed fitting, in the end, that the only ledger I would ever truly balance was the one I would not be present to read.

* * *

The bells woke me. Not the dawn bells — I'd slept two hours on the crypt floor, which I do not recommend to anyone whose spine has opinions — but the great bells, the coronation bells, ringing hours early.

I was on my feet before I finished waking, which is a trick the guild teaches you and your body never forgets. Ilsette came down the crypt stairs two at a time, and I knew before she spoke, the way you know an entry is wrong before you find the figure.

"It's moving early," she said. "The court is assembling. They're saying the king commanded it — that he couldn't wait, that the crown must be set on his head *this morning*." Her voice cracked on *crown*. "Emrys, it's not waiting for the ceremony. The ceremony *is* the feeding. It's going to finish him in front of everyone and wear him forever, and there won't be a rite after that because there won't be a *him* after that."

I looked at the circle. Complete. The candles, relit. The seven stations, chalked and waiting. Everything ready except the hour, and the hour had just been stolen from us — which, I noted with the part of my mind that never stops keeping accounts, was exactly the kind of thing a creature made of stolen memories would do.

"Then we don't wait for our moment," I said. I took the memory out from behind its lock and held it, once, briefly — snow, a voice, a name — the way a man checks a coin before he spends it.

"We make our moment," I said, and started up the stairs toward the light.
