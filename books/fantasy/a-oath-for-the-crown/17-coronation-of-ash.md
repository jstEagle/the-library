## 17. Coronation of Ash

I had planned to be invisible at the coronation. That was the whole architecture of the plan: Ilsette in the light, Wren's old contacts holding the doors, me in the crypt below with my hoard open like a snare, waiting for the Regent to come down to us on its own schedule.

The Regent, it turns out, does not keep appointments. It keeps ledgers. And somewhere in its centuries of accounts, it had learned the first rule of my own trade: never let the mark choose the ground.

So when the trumpets sounded and Aldric—what was left of him—walked into the Great Hall beneath a canopy of white silk to be crowned a second time, crowned properly, publicly, before every lord and lady and guild factor in the capital, I understood that we had not set a trap.

We had accepted an invitation.

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because the hour matters.

Ilsette had dressed me as a palace under-steward, which is a real position that exists solely so that no one has to look at you. Grey coat, grey gloves, a slate of meaningless errands chalked against my hip. She'd adjusted my collar twice without meeting my eyes, which was how she said things she couldn't say aloud.

"The moment he's crowned," she murmured, "the rite begins. You hold the creature's attention. I speak the words. That's all you have to do. Hold."

"Hold," I said. "Like a ledger."

"Like anything," she said. "Just don't let go."

Wren stood by the servants' door in borrowed livery, hands shaking around a tray of wine cups he had no intention of serving. He'd been out of the guild cells for two days and still flinched at keys. "For what it's worth," he said, "the chronicles say the last keeper who faced it down there—" He stopped himself. "They don't say. There's no one left to write it down."

"Then you'll finally have something original," I said, and he laughed the way people laugh at funerals, and I loved him a little for it.

And then the trumpets, and the doors, and Aldric.

He looked better than he had in the crypt. That was the horror of it—the Regent knew how to dress its host. It gave him color, gave him steadiness, let him walk the length of the hall with his daughter's arm through his while the court bowed and whispered and saw only a king restored. Only I could see the seams: the half-second delay before each blink, the way his smile arrived slightly after his eyes decided on it, like a puppeteer remembering which strings to pull.

His gaze crossed mine once, mid-hall. And behind the king's grey eyes something *looked back*. Something patient. Something that had eaten eleven monarchs and found this one nearly done, and was already tasting the twelfth.

It winked. I swear on every ledger I've ever kept—it winked.

* * *

The coronation itself is a blur of ritual I only half absorbed. The High Cantor read the Lineage. The Sword of Vessels was presented and not drawn, because drawing it would be an omen, and omens were out of fashion. Ilsette knelt to receive her father's blessing, and her hand trembled against his ring, and the Regent wearing her father bent down and kissed her hair with perfect tenderness, because it had worn kings long enough to know exactly how a father loves, having consumed that love from the inside.

That's the thing about it that no archive captured properly. It isn't cruel. Cruelty requires wanting something from you. The Regent doesn't want. It *takes*, the way winter takes, the way rot takes—without malice, without appetite even, just process. A thing that happens to kingdoms.

Aldric took up the crown from its cushion of cloth-of-gold.

And stopped.

The hall noticed all at once, the way a room notices silence. The Cantor faltered mid-verse. Somewhere a noblewoman's fan went still. Aldric stood with the crown in both hands, arms beginning to shake—not with weakness. With *resistance*. For one impossible heartbeat I thought: he's fighting it. Eleven kings eaten and this one, gentle, fading Aldric, is fighting—

Then the king turned his head and looked directly at me across two hundred nobles, and smiled with someone else's mouth, and spoke in a voice that filled the hall without rising above a whisper, the way frost fills a window.

"*Keeper.*"

Every head turned along the line of his gaze. Every head arrived at me. Two hundred faces, and mine the only one with nowhere left to hide.

"You came," the Regent said through the king, warm as a banker greeting a valued client. "I did so hope you'd come. Shall we do this here? In front of everyone? I find I'm tired of cellars."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I want to record it accurately, because if Wren writes the true history—and he will, the stubborn inkstain—I want him to get the order right.

Guards moved first. Not toward me—toward the doors. Sealing them. The Regent had arranged the whole ceremony as a jar with a lid. Sarelle's thieves, I realized, some of them, standing in noble livery among the guests, moving with that particular economy the guild trains into your shoulders. She hadn't come herself. She never did. She sent accounts receivable.

Ilsette stepped between me and the throne. "Father—"

"Your father is *here*, princess." The Regent tilted Aldric's head, almost fondly. "Deeper than ever. Would you like to see?" And the king's face *changed*—cracked open into something young and terrified, Aldric's true self surfacing like a drowning man breaking water—one raw second of *help me* before the surface closed over. Ilsette made a sound I will hear until I die.

"That's enough," I said.

My voice didn't carry. It didn't need to. The Regent heard it the way I heard memories—in the teeth.

"There he is," it said. "The keeper of accounts. Do you know what I offered you? Everything back. Your name. Your mother's voice in the snow. One word, Fairweather-that-isn't, and you walk out of here whole, and I finish my meal in peace, and in fifty years nobody remembers either of us." The king's eyes glittered. "You're going to refuse. They always refuse. It's why I like this part."

"It's why you *need* this part," I said. "You can't take freely given memory. You've never been able to. Eleven kings, and you had to starve them into surrendering, piece by piece, because you can't open a lock—you can only wait for the owner to hand you the key." I started walking. Down the aisle. Into the light. "But I'm a thief, Your Majesty. Opening locks is the family trade."

"What are you doing?" Ilsette hissed.

"Changing the venue."

* * *

Here is what I had decided in the crypt, alone, at the hour when decisions are made, and here is what I had told no one:

The rite required a keeper to hold the Regent's attention with their deepest memory while the blood-heir spoke the unbinding. Attention. Bait. But bait dangles; bait can be refused; bait can be sniffed and passed over, as it had nearly been in my dreams. So I had refined the plan the way you refine any con: don't offer the mark a taste. Offer it the vault. Open the doors wide. Let it see everything.

My hoard. Every memory I'd stolen across ten years in the guild—the first kisses and bottled griefs, the river's childhood, the soldier's last sunrise—all of it held in a mind built precisely to hold. And beneath it, sealed, the anchor: my name, my mother, the snow. The one memory that made me *me*.

The Regent glutted itself on royal memory, century after century, and royal memory was always the same shape: duty, lineage, crown. It had never once fed on a hoard like mine. Ten years of other people's most precious moments, curated by the finest thief the guild ever produced.

I wasn't offering it bait.

I was offering it a banquet it couldn't refuse, and I was the table.

"Ilsette," I said, and I did not look at her, because if I looked at her I would stop walking. "When I open, you'll have maybe thirty heartbeats. The words. All of them. Don't wait for me to signal. Don't wait for anything. Say them."

"Emrys—"

"That's not my name," I said, and it was almost funny, saying it out loud at last, in front of two hundred strangers, "but it's the only one anyone here knows, so use it anyway. Use it loudly. Names matter today."

I reached the foot of the dais. The Regent watched me come with the king's body perfectly still, the crown still held in those shaking hands, and I understood suddenly that it was afraid—not of me, but of the *timing*. It had moved the ceremony early to catch us unprepared, and instead it had handed me exactly what I needed: a crowd. Witnesses. Because the rite of unbinding, per my mother's line, required one more thing the archives had buried in a footnote: it had to be spoken *before memory that mattered*. Before minds that would remember. A rite performed in secret unravels in secret. Performed before a kingdom, it becomes history, and history is the one thing even the Regent cannot eat.

Sarelle had burned the prophecy. Fine. Prophecies are just futures written in advance.

Today we'd write one in the past tense, where it sticks.

* * *

"Last chance," the Regent said, and for the first time its voice through the king's mouth sounded less like frost and more like hunger. "Walk away. Keep your little snow-memory. Grow old as nobody. Isn't that what you wanted? To be unwritten?"

"It is," I said honestly. "It was. And then I met a princess who fights for a hollow man, and a chronicler who flinches at keys and writes truth anyway, and a king who forgot his daughter's name and still reached for her hand." I climbed the first step. "Turns out being nobody was just being nobody *yet*."

I opened my hoard.

I don't have words for what it is to open yourself deliberately—to reach into the place where I keep ten years of stolen lives and unlatch every door at once. Imagine a vault where every shelf holds a person's most treasured moment, and imagine setting fire to the walls so the light pours out of everything. The first kiss. The river's childhood, green and cold. Grief in a bottle, uncorked. The soldier's sunrise. A hundred small eternities, all shining outward at once, and at the center, wrapped in seven seals of my own making, the anchor: *snow, a woman's voice, a name that is not Fairweather.*

The Regent made a sound. Not through the king—through the air itself, through the stone, a sound like a library inhaling.

"Oh," it said. "*Oh.* What *is*—"

"Dinner," I said. "Come and get it."

It poured into me.

Not through the eyes or the mouth. Through the opened places, the way cold pours through an unlocked door, and I will tell you truly: I had imagined it as invasion, and it was worse than that. It was *gratitude*. It came in loving what it found, caressing each stolen memory as it passed, cooing over the first kiss, savoring the grief, and every memory it touched it *took a little of*, and every little it took, I felt myself thinning, becoming more doorway than man, and I held the doors open anyway, because that was the job, that was always the job: keeper of accounts, holding the ledger steady while the audit runs.

Thirty heartbeats. I counted them. It's what I do.

On the fourth, the Regent found the seals.

On the ninth, it broke the first, and I tasted snow.

On the fifteenth, Ilsette began to speak—the blood-rite, the words of my mother's line, in a voice that cracked and steadied and cracked again, and the whole court heard it, two hundred minds remembering, and the words *landed*, I felt them land, felt them go down through me into whatever floor the Regent stood on.

On the twenty-second, the Regent stopped eating and started screaming.

On the thirtieth, it showed me my mother's face, and said, in her voice, in the voice it had stolen from the deepest locked room of me: "*Clever boy.*"

And the world went white, and the crown fell, and somewhere very far away a princess kept speaking.

I held the doors open.

I held them.

I held—

* * *

The last thing I remember clearly is the snow. Just the snow, and the voice calling the name that isn't Fairweather, and me reaching for it the way you reach for a coin rolling toward a drain.

Then nothing, which—for a keeper of accounts—is simply the blank page at the end of the book.

Whether it's the end depends entirely on who picks up the pen next.
