## 18. Inside the Crown

Here is what no ledger ever taught me: being eaten does not feel like being eaten. It feels like being *read*.

The Regent poured into me the way ink pours into blotting paper, and I opened for it—opened everything, the whole hoard, every stolen kiss and bottled grief, every coin of other people's lives I'd kept warm in my pockets like a miser pretending he was a shepherd. It came in greedy and vast, and for one vertiginous heartbeat I thought: *this is what it is to be an entry. This is what the ledgers feel.*

Then the world turned inside out, and I was inside the crown.

Not a crown of gold. I want to be clear about that, because I'd spent weeks imagining the thing behind the king's eyes as some coiled serpent of metal and malice, and the truth was worse. The crown was a *place*. A hall without walls, lit by nothing, floored with nothing, and stretching away from me in every direction were shelves.

Shelves. Of course it had shelves. I almost laughed. I had spent my whole life among shelves, and here I was, swallowed by the largest archive in the kingdom, and every volume on every shelf was a king.

I walked. I don't know how I walked, in a place without a floor, but the Regent let me, the way a spider lets a fly inspect the web. And because I am, at the end of everything, still a keeper of accounts, I read.

* * *

The first shelf held a man in armor the color of old blood. He stood in a field of wheat that wasn't wheat—it was memory shaped like wheat, golden and endless—and he was counting soldiers. I recognized the gesture before I recognized the man. The set of the shoulders. The way his lips moved over numbers like a prayer.

A counter. Like me.

"Hello," I said.

He didn't hear me. He counted his soldiers, and the wheat rippled, and somewhere behind his eyes a door closed that I could hear closing, a soft archival sound, a ledger shutting, and the man in the armor grew fainter, and fainter, and then he was just wheat.

The Regent's voice came from everywhere, from the shelves themselves, from the light that wasn't light. It sounded—this is the detail I will carry if I carry nothing else—it sounded like *me*. My voice, aged a few centuries, worn smooth as a river stone.

*You are reading my collection.*

"It's not a collection," I said. "Collections are curated. This is a larder."

*Semantics,* said the thing that wore my voice, *from a thief.*

"From an *accountant*," I said. "There's a difference. A thief takes. An accountant knows exactly what was taken, from whom, and what it cost. Shall I tell you what you cost, Regent? Because I've been reading your ledgers from the inside, and your margins are terrible."

The shelves shivered. Somewhere far off, a king screamed, briefly, and was shelved.

*You are inside me,* it said, with the patience of a creature that had eaten nineteen monarchs and expected to eat a twentieth. *You are a morsel conducting an audit.*

"I've been called worse by better."

But my hands were shaking, and it knew. Of course it knew. I was inside it; every tremor of me was a tremor of it. I was a fly conducting an audit inside the spider, and the spider had been alive for longer than the guild, longer than the city, longer than the word *king*.

I kept walking. Because that was the plan. The plan was always to keep walking.

* * *

Here is what the plan was, since I am apparently writing my own eulogy in my head: Ilsette would speak the rite. The rite required the Regent's attention—its whole, glutted, undivided attention—fixed on a keeper holding his deepest memory open like a lantern. While it fed, it could not hold the king. While it gorged, the walls of Aldric would go unguarded. And a blood heir, speaking words sealed in my mother's line, could unbind what centuries had bound.

It was, I will grant, a terrible plan. It required me to be bait, a meal, and a distraction all at once, and it required the Regent to be greedy enough to overeat.

Fortunately, I had met the guild. I knew how its oldest client thought. Everything in the Vellum Guild's ledger had a price, and everything with a price could be bought, and the Regent was the guild's oldest client, which meant it was, at its cold immortal heart, a *shopper*.

It wanted what I carried. It had offered me everything I'd lost to get it. And now I stood inside its hall of shelves, and I had brought the one thing it had never tasted.

The memory of who I was before the guild.

* * *

I walked centuries of kings. I want to tell you about them, because someone should remember them, and I was beginning to suspect that someone was going to be me, briefly, before the shelves took me too.

There was a queen—yes, a queen; the Regent did not discriminate, hunger is an egalitarian employer—who had ruled for forty years and whose shelf held her daughter's wedding, held it whole and glowing, and the queen stood inside the memory every day, watching from the back of the crowd, because the Regent had eaten her memory of *attending* and left her only the memory of the memory, a copy of a copy, and she knew, some part of her always knew, that she was looking at a forgery of her own joy.

There was a boy-king who had been crowned at nine and eaten by fourteen, and his shelf was the smallest, and it held a dog. Just a dog. A big ugly hound that had slept at the foot of his bed, and the boy-king stood beside the dog forever, and the dog was the last thing the Regent had let him keep, because even a parasite understands that a host needs one warm thing to stay alive long enough to be finished.

There was a king who had tried to fight. I could tell because his shelf was scorched. He had gone down inside himself burning his own memories like a retreating army burns its fields, and the Regent had eaten the ashes, and what remained of him was a silhouette of defiance with nothing inside it, a king-shaped absence still standing at attention after everything that made him a king had been consumed.

Nineteen of them. Nineteen monarchs, and the hall went back further than nineteen, further than the shelves admitted, back into darkness where even the Regent's memory grew hazy, because the Regent had been eating so long that it had begun to eat its own early meals, and the first king it ever consumed was a flavor it could no longer recall.

That, I thought, is the joke at the center of everything. The creature that devours memory cannot remember its own beginning. It is a ledger whose first pages have rotted. It has eaten a dynasty and kept nothing but the *eating*.

"You're hungry," I said to the shelves, to the light, to the thing wearing my voice. "That's all you are. You're the hunger that outlived the meal. Nineteen kings, and you're still hungry. Do you know why?"

*Do not.*

"Because you never eat anything that's *given*."

The hall went very quiet. In a place with no walls, the silence still managed to have edges.

*Everything is given,* said the Regent. *Eventually. Given up. Given in. Given over. I have never once been refused.*

"That's because you've never been offered a gift."

* * *

And then I found him.

I wasn't looking for him, or I was, or I had been looking for him since the moment I stepped into the light at the coronation and opened my hoard like a merchant's coat full of stolen watches. I had walked the outer shelves, the old kings, the far centuries. And there, near what I can only describe as the center—not a geometric center; a *weight* center, the way a library has a heart—I found a young man sitting on the floor.

Not standing at attention. Not counting soldiers. Not watching a forgery of his own joy.

Sitting on the floor of his own mind with his knees drawn up, and he was screaming.

He had been screaming for years. I understood that the way you understand things in dreams, totally and without evidence. Aldric—the real Aldric, the original, the gentle man who remembered his daughter's face but not her name—had been screaming behind the walls of himself since the Regent had moved in, and no one had heard him, because the walls were made of his own memories, eaten and stacked and mortared with his own eaten memories, and he had built his own prison out of his own life without ever lifting a stone.

"Ilsette," I said.

His head came up. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified and *alive*, and I have stood in front of a great many terrible things in my life—Sarelle's smile, the prophecy ledger, my own reflection on the night I learned what I was—and none of them looked at me the way this ruined young king looked at me.

"What did you say?" His voice was a ruin. Screaming for years will do that.

"Your daughter," I said. "Her name is Ilsette. You've forgotten it. You look at her face every day and you cannot find the name, and it's killing you slower than the thing eating you. It's Ilsette. She has your stubbornness and her mother's temper, and she caught me fleeing your chambers and instead of calling the guard she joined a conspiracy to save you, because that's who you raised, Aldric. That's what's left of you that even the Regent couldn't digest. It ate your memories and it couldn't eat your *teaching*."

He stood up. Slowly, like a man who has forgotten how his body works, which he had.

"Who are you?" he said.

And there it was. The question. The whole question, the one the prophecy had asked, the one Sarelle had asked, the one I had spent thirty years not asking myself, and here at the end, inside the monster, asked by the man I had come to save.

I could have lied. Old habits. I had a whole shelf of names in my head, Fairweather chief among them, a coat of borrowed identities, and every one of them was a forgery, and here was the one man in the world who would know exactly what a forged name was worth, because he was living inside one.

"My name," I said, "is Emrys."

It wasn't. Not the whole truth of it. But it was the name I had worn longest, the name I had chosen when I was a child with nothing, and a chosen name, I was beginning to understand, is worth more than a given one. A given name is a gift. A chosen name is an *oath*.

"Emrys," he repeated. And then, because he was a king even hollowed, even shelved, even screaming: "You shouldn't be here. It will eat you."

"It's going to try," I said. "That's rather the point. I need it looking at me. I need it so busy swallowing me that it forgets it's holding you. And when it lets go—" I put out my hand. "When it lets go, you run for the door. Ilsette will be there. She'll know what to do. She's been preparing her whole life for a father who needs saving. Give her the chance."

He took my hand. His grip was astonishing, for a ghost.

"The dog," he said, suddenly, urgently, like a man remembering a debt. "When I'm out—when I'm me—the dog. Do I still—"

"I don't know," I said. "I'll ask around."

He laughed. It was a terrible, cracked, beautiful sound, a laugh with years of screaming behind it, and it echoed down the hall of shelves, and every king on every shelf turned toward it like flowers toward a sun they'd forgotten existed.

And the Regent came for me.

* * *

It did not come as a monster. I want that recorded, in whatever ledger survives me. It came as a *bargain*. It came the way the guild comes, the way Sarelle came, all silk and reasonable terms, wearing my own forgotten face—the face from the snow, the face the woman in the foreign memory had called a name to—and it stood in front of me in the hall of shelves and it opened its hands.

*Everything,* it said. *You know what I'm holding. Every memory the guild ever took from you. The day they found you. The day they unmade you. Your mother's voice. Your true name. The snow. All of it, whole, restored, returned—I have sat at the guild's table for three hundred years, boy, I have *tasted* their inventory. I can give you back yourself. Every piece. You need only stop. Walk away. Let me finish my meal in peace, and wake tomorrow as a complete man.*

And here is the terrible thing, the thing I will confess because I am past the point where confession costs me anything:

I wanted it.

I stood inside the devourer of kings and I *wanted it*, the way the starving want bread, the way the drowning want air. My whole life I had been a man with a hole where his beginning should be, a ledger with the first pages torn out, and here was the creature offering me the torn pages, offering me the snow and the voice and the name, offering to make me *whole*, and all it cost was one old man's remaining years and a daughter's heartbreak and nineteen dead kings' worth of complicity.

"No," I said.

It tilted my face—my face, wearing my expression, wearing my doubt—at me, and it said, gently, the way you speak to a child, the way Sarelle had spoken to me in her office with its kind lamp:

*You will forget this moment. You will forget the refusal. You will spend eternity wondering what you traded away, and you will never know whether you were brave or simply a fool who never got the chance to find out.*

"I'm an accountant," I said. "I always know what things cost. And I know what you cost, Regent. You cost nineteen kings. You cost a queen her daughter's wedding. You cost a boy his dog. You are the most expensive thing in this kingdom, and you are *worthless*, because everything you've bought, you've eaten, and everything you've eaten, you've lost, because you cannot keep a memory. You can only keep the *hunger*."

I opened my hoard.

Not the stolen things. Not the coins of other people's lives. I reached past all of it, down to the bottom, to the one entry I had guarded my whole life without knowing I was guarding it, the anchor, the keel, the truth of who I was before the guild took me in—and I held it up like a lantern, like bait, like a gift.

The snow. The woman's voice. The name that wasn't Fairweather. My mother's face, which was my face, which was the face the Regent wore to tempt me. The smell of woodsmoke. The rite, humming in my blood like a struck bell. *All of it.* The whole torn first chapter of my ledger, and I held it out, and I let the lantern of it blaze, and I said the words every keeper of accounts says at the end of a reckoning:

"Take inventory."

* * *

It took inventory.

God forgive me, it took inventory. It fell on that memory the way I had once seen a starving man fall on bread, with no grace and no strategy and no thought for anything but the swallowing, and I fed it, and I fed it, and I fed it.

I fed it the snow, and the snow went. I fed it my mother's voice, and the voice went, and I felt the shape of the sound leave me like a tooth leaving a jaw. I fed it the name—the true one, the one in the snow, the one the old woman in the village had spoken with tears in her eyes—and I felt it go, and I did not reach for it, because reaching was the one thing I could not do. A gift, remember. It had never once been given anything. It did not know how to refuse a gift. It did not know that a gift can be a hook.

I fed it the woodsmoke, and the rite, and my mother's face, and the face was the last to go, and I watched it go, and it was like watching my own reflection walk out of the mirror and down a long road, getting smaller, getting colder, getting *gone*.

And the Regent glutted.

I felt it happen from the inside, which is a thing no one has ever felt and lived to describe, so consider this a posthumous report. The hall of shelves *swayed*. Nineteen kings felt their prison walls go soft. The boy and his dog looked up. The queen in the wheat looked up. And at the center, the young man with the ruined voice—the king who had been screaming for years—felt the walls around him loosen like a fist deciding, for the first time in its long cruel life, to open.

*What,* said the Regent, and its voice was slurred now, drunk on me, drunk on a memory so dense with love and blood and truth that it had never tasted its like in three hundred years of eating kings who were mostly duty and ceremony and carefully rationed joy. *What *was* that.*

"A gift," I said.

My voice sounded strange. Thin. I was reaching for my own name and finding a shelf where a book used to be. The snow was gone. The voice was gone. The mother was gone. I was a ledger with the first pages torn out, and this time I had torn them myself, and handed them over, and watched them swallowed.

*It's not enough,* the Regent said, and there was something new in its voice, something I had put there, something that in a smaller creature I would have called *doubt*. *It's not enough. There was more. There was a name—*

"Gone," I said, and smiled at the thing wearing my face, because it was wearing my face and I had nothing left for it to wear, and that, I thought, is the last joke I'll ever get to make: *you ate the mask and the face went with it.* "You ate it. All of it. That's what you do, isn't it? You eat, and you keep the hunger. Enjoy the meal. It's the last one that was ever *mine*."

The hall was tilting. The shelves were groaning. Somewhere at the center of everything, a wall of eaten memories was coming down, and behind it a king was standing up for the first time in years, and somewhere far above us both—far above the crown, above the crypt, above the coronation of ash—I knew, the way you know things in dreams, that a princess was opening her mouth to speak words sealed in my mother's blood.

Words I could no longer remember.

Words that no longer needed me to remember them.

"Run, Aldric," I said, to the young man with the ruined voice, as the hall came down around us in a rain of other people's lives. "Your daughter's waiting. And for the love of whatever's left of either of us—"

The light went out. The shelves went out. I went out.

"—ask her about the dog."

* * *

The last thing—the very last thing, the final entry in the ledger of Emrys, keeper of accounts, thief of nothing, giver of everything—was a sound.

Not the Regent screaming, though I think it screamed. Not the walls falling, though they fell.

A voice. A young woman's voice, speaking words old as my mother's blood, and underneath the words, underneath the rite, underneath the unraveling of a three-hundred-year hunger—

My name.

Not the one in the snow. That was gone, eaten, gone. *My* name. The one I'd chosen in the dark when I was nothing, the one I'd given the hollowed king inside the crown, the one that was mine because I had said it was, because a chosen name is an oath and oaths outlast memory—

Someone was saying it. Calling it. Calling *me*.

And I was too empty to answer.
