## 19. The Unbinding

I have stood in the crypt beneath the palace exactly twice in my life. The first time I was nine years old, and my father carried me down on his shoulders to show me where the kings of Vellamar slept, and I thought it was the most beautiful terrible place I had ever seen — all that marble patience, all those carved faces pretending they weren't listening.

The second time is now, and I am carrying someone myself.

Emrys weighs almost nothing. I don't mean that as poetry; I mean it as an indictment. Whatever the guild fed him, whatever life they permitted him, it was not enough to put meat on a man's bones. His head lolls against my shoulder as I lower him onto the stone slab at the center of the crypt — the same slab, I realize with a chill I refuse to examine, where the first king of Vellamar was laid out before his crowning. The old rites were always held here. The new ones apparently are too.

"Talk to me," I say, because his eyes are open and empty and that is the single most frightening thing I have ever looked at, and I have looked at my father forgetting my name. "Emrys. Blink twice if you're still in there."

He blinks once. Slowly. Like a man surfacing from very deep water who isn't sure the surface counts as real.

"Once," he says. His voice sounds scraped. "I'm keeping the second one in reserve."

Relief hits me so hard I nearly sit down on a dead king. "You absolute— you're making jokes *now*?"

"Now is when jokes are cheapest." He tries to prop himself up on one elbow and fails, which kills the humor in the room very efficiently. Above us, muffled through feet of stone and ceremony, the coronation continues. I can hear the choir. They're singing the Ascension Hymn, which means we have perhaps six minutes before the crown comes down onto my father's head and the thing inside him gets its centennial feast.

Six minutes. My whole life has been waiting for things — for Father to remember, for the court to notice, for someone, anyone, to look at what was happening to the king of Vellamar and say it out loud. And now everything arrives at once, in a crypt, with a thief who gave away his own face less than an hour ago.

"Tell me what you see," I say, kneeling beside the slab. "In there. With it."

His eyes focus somewhere past the ceiling, past the palace, past sense. "Kings," he says. "So many kings. It keeps them like — like pressed flowers. Like entries in a ledger." A shudder goes through him. "Your father's in here. The real one. He's been screaming for eleven years, Ilsette. Nobody heard him because nobody knew to listen."

"I'm listening now," I say. "Tell him."

"He can't—" Emrys stops. Swallows. When he speaks again his voice is smaller. "He says he knows your voice. He says you used to sing to him when the headaches came, and he never told anyone because he didn't want them to think the king was weak." His eyes fill, and he doesn't wipe them, and neither do I, because if either of us starts weeping right now the rite dies in the cradle. "He says he's sorry about your mother's funeral. He wasn't gone yet then. He was just — going."

"I know," I whisper. "I know he was. Tell him there's nothing to forgive. Tell him to hold on."

Above us, the hymn swells toward its final verse.

I take Emrys's hand. It's cold. Everything about this moment is cold except the words I've had memorized since the village, since the old woman pressed her thumb, ink-stained, against my palm and said *you are the last one who can speak this, child, so speak it well.* I have rehearsed the blood-rite in mirrors, in baths, in the dark behind my eyelids. I have whispered it until the syllables wore smooth as river stones.

What I did not rehearse was saying it while the man who taught me what courage looks like lies hollowed out on a tomb, having poured his entire self into a monster's mouth to buy me these six minutes.

"The words need your blood on his brow," Emrys murmurs. "And mine. That's how it anchors. One line royal, one line keeper. Two threads, one knot."

"You told me that part already."

"I'm telling you again because my hands won't work and yours will have to do both." He manages something that is almost a smile. "Story of my life lately."

I draw the little ritual knife across my left palm — the pain is bright and clean and honestly welcome, proof I'm still a person and not just a daughter watching — and press my bleeding hand to my father's forehead through the link of Emrys's opened hoard, which hangs above the slab like a wound in the air. That's the only word for it. When Emrys opened himself to the Regent, something tore loose in the world, and through the tear I can see light that doesn't belong in a crypt: gold and gray and moving, like weather made of memory.

Through that light, faintly, I can hear it breathing.

* * *

You should understand what the Regent sounds like, if you're going to judge what happens next.

It does not roar. Monsters in the ballads roar. This thing *purrs*. Eleven years of my father's laughter, worn smooth and reused. Centuries of royal voices layered under it like sediment. When it spoke through Father at court — when it praised a minister with Father's mouth while Father screamed underneath — it sounded warm. It sounded kind. That was always the worst part. It had eaten so much love that it could produce love the way a music box produces a tune: mechanically, beautifully, and with nothing behind it.

Now, through the tear in the air, it purrs at me.

*Little princess.* Not aloud. Directly, the way a headache is direct. *You brought me such a gift. Do you know what he gave me? A whole self. Untasted. I haven't had a fresh soul in three hundred years — they come pre-hollowed now, all of them, your whole rotten dynasty. But this one kept himself like a sealed letter.*

"Let him go," I say.

*I intend to keep him,* it says pleasantly. *Both of them, actually. Your father is nearly finished — a decade of slow dining — but the thief. Oh, the thief I will savor. He tastes of snow.*

Snow. I don't know why that word lands in my chest like a thrown knife. There's no time to ask. The hymn above us reaches the verse where the crown is lifted.

"Emrys." I grip his hand. "Whatever happens — whatever you hear it say — hold on to one thing. Just one. Pick it now."

He turns his empty eyes toward me, and for a moment something surfaces in them, something young and stubborn and terrified, a boy from a snowy village who was stolen and survived anyway.

"My name," he says. "I'm holding my name."

"Then hold it," I say, "and let go of everything else."

I begin the rite.

* * *

The blood-rite of unbinding is not long. That surprised me, when I learned it. I'd expected something operatic — hours of chanting, circles of salt, the machinery of epic. Instead it's eleven lines. Eleven lines, spoken by a blood heir over a keeper's opened hoard, while the parasite is busy drinking. That's all the armor our ancestors ever needed. The monster's own hunger is the lockpick; you just have to be brave enough to stand next to its mouth while it eats.

My blood is on Emrys's brow. His blood — he managed to drag two fingers across the knife before his arms quit — is on mine. Through the tear in the air, the Regent drinks and drinks, glutting itself on a complete human soul for the first time in centuries, and it does not notice what I am doing, because why would it? What threat has a princess ever been?

Line one. Line two. My voice doesn't shake, which astonishes me. Somewhere in the walls of the crypt, the old kings seem to lean in.

At line five, the Regent notices.

The purring stops. The light in the tear goes from gold to a color I have no name for — the color of a page after fire has passed near it. And every dead king's face on every sarcophagus around us seems to turn, all at once, toward me.

*You little—*

It reaches for me. I feel it the way you feel a hand close over your shoulder in the dark: pressure, cold, intent. It pours itself backward out of the feast, out of Emrys, out of the gluttony that was killing it with kindness, because I have spoken the fifth line and the fifth line means it can no longer stay where it is not wanted.

But here is the thing about a creature made entirely of stolen memory:

It needs somewhere to be.

It tears free of my father — I will describe that sound once, briefly, and never again: it is the sound of a boot pulled from deep mud, if the mud could scream — and it tears free of Emrys, and for one endless heartbeat it hangs in the crypt with nothing to wear, a king-shaped absence wearing three hundred years of other people's best days, and it looks at me with my father's eyes, and it says, in my father's voice, in the gentle tone he used when I fell off my pony at seven years old:

*Ilsette. Sweetheart. You'll catch your death down here.*

For one full second — I counted it later, in the dark, many times — I almost reached out. That is the trap at the bottom of the thing. It doesn't threaten you. It *loves* you, with borrowed love, perfectly, and some starving animal part of you wants to walk into it and be held forever.

Then I hear Emrys, hollowed and barely conscious, whisper from the slab: "Line eight, Ilsette. *Line eight.*"

I speak line eight.

The Regent unravels.

I want to tell you there was a battle. There wasn't. There was a sound like a book closing, and then the great shape of it came apart the way smoke comes apart when you open a window — not destroyed, exactly, but *unemployed*, with nothing left to possess and nowhere left to pour itself, thread by thread by thread, until the last of it was dust drifting down through the crypt light onto three hundred years of tombs.

Dust settles. Dust always settles. It's the one honest thing dust does.

And then two men fall at once.

My father collapses like a puppet whose strings were finally, mercifully cut — and I am moving toward him before I decide to, catching him before his skull meets stone, and oh, oh, his weight is *different*, I can't explain how I know, but a hollowed man is carried differently than a whole one, the way an empty jug is set down differently than a full one, and my father is *full*, he is heavy with himself, and when his eyes open they are my father's eyes, only my father's, confused and wet and present.

"Ilsette?" he says. His voice cracks on the second syllable. "Why are you crying? Where — how long — Ilsette, how *long?*"

"Eleven years," I sob, laughing, holding his face in my bloody hands. "Eleven years, Papa. You're back. You're all the way back."

He looks at me the way he used to look at me across the breakfast table, before the headaches, before the forgetting — like I am a fact he is grateful for — and I would live inside that look for the rest of my life if the world would allow it.

The world does not allow it.

Because behind me, softly, there is the sound of a body sliding off a stone slab.

* * *

I get to Emrys in four steps. Maybe three. Time goes strange.

He's on the crypt floor on his side, curled slightly, the way people curl when they've decided not to bother anyone. His eyes are open. That's the first thing I check — eyes open, good, eyes open means alive, everyone knows that — except his eyes are open the way windows are open in an abandoned house. Nothing looking out.

"Emrys." I take his shoulder. Shake it. "Emrys. It's done. It worked. You can stop holding on now — you can let go of everything, do you hear me? You *won.*"

Nothing.

I press my fingers to his throat. His pulse is there — steady, slow, infuriatingly calm, the pulse of a man sleeping off a long day — but when I put my mouth by his lips there is breath, and when I shout his name there is no flicker, no blink, no reserve-second spent. His face, which ten minutes ago was making jokes with the last of its strength, is smooth and vacant as fresh vellum.

"No," I say. Out loud, to the crypt, to the dust, to the dead kings. "No, no — this isn't — the rite doesn't *kill* anyone, the old woman said—"

*The rite doesn't kill,* agrees a memory in my head, in her cracked voice. *But a keeper who gives his anchor gives everything the anchor held. He'll breathe. He'll eat, if you feed him. Whether there's anyone home —*

Whether there's anyone home.

My father is sitting up now, blinking at the crypt like a man waking from a fever that lasted a decade, and Wren's contacts are pounding on the far door because the coronation above has dissolved into chaos — the king collapsed mid-ceremony, the crown rolling on the flagstones, half the court convinced it's assassination and the other half, correctly, convinced it's miracle. None of it matters. None of it can reach me down here, where I kneel between the two men the Regent wore, one returned and one taken, and I cannot make the arithmetic fair.

"Papa," I say, without turning around. "Come here. I need you to help me carry him."

"Who is he?" my father asks — and it breaks something in me, that question, because it is asked with love and total innocence, a man eleven years out of date asking about a stranger on the floor.

I look down at Emrys Fairweather, keeper of accounts, thief of impossible things, the boy from the snow who held his name until there was nothing else left to hold, and I answer my father with the only true thing I have:

"He's the reason you're asking."

We carry him up out of the dark together, into the noise and the light and the wreckage of a coronation, and behind us the dust of the Regent settles over the tombs of every king it ever ate, and ahead of us — gods help us — is the long work of teaching a blank page how to be a man.

He does not wake.

Not that day. Not the next.

But on the third morning, as I sit beside his bed reading aloud from Wren's confiscated chronicles because someone told me the voices of strangers sometimes reach the drowned — his finger moves.

Just once. Toward the sound of my voice.

Like a man, in a country with no roads, taking a first step anyway.
