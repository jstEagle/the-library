## 20. A Blank Page

The first thing my father did after the unbinding was ask for a chair.

Not a throne. A chair—plain, wooden, dragged from a servant's corridor by two guardsmen who wept while they carried it. He set it beside Emrys's bed in the infirmary wing and sat down in it like a man taking up a post he intended to hold until the walls fell down around him.

"He saved me," Father said, when the physicians tried to move him along. "The least I can do is sit."

So I let him sit. And I sat too, because someone had to keep an eye on both of them—the king who remembered everything now, every year of his slow devouring, and the thief who remembered nothing at all.

* * *

Emrys woke on the third day.

I was there when it happened, though I nearly missed it. I had stepped out to argue with a physician about leeches—I hold strong opinions about leeches, all of them hostile—and when I came back his eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling with the particular blankness of a man reading nothing.

"You're awake," I said, brilliantly. Years of court training, that. My father's daughter, quick as a drawn blade.

He turned his head toward me. His eyes were grey. I remember thinking that I hadn't known that before—that I had fled past him in a corridor once, demanded truth from him in a stairwell, planned a revolution of memory with him at my side, and I had never once registered the color of his eyes. Grief does strange bookkeeping.

"Who—" he began, and stopped. Tried again. "Where am I?"

"The palace. The infirmary wing." I pulled the chair closer. "You're safe."

"Am I?" He said it without fear, which frightened me more than screaming would have. It was the question of a man checking the weather. "I don't know why you'd say that. I don't know anything." A pause. "I don't know what I don't know. That's the worst part. There's no shape to the hole."

"There's no hole," I said. "There's a page. Blank. You get to write it."

"That's a very pretty thing to say to someone who can't remember his own name."

"It's the truest thing I've said all week, and I've been saying quite a lot of things to quite a lot of terrible people."

Something moved across his face—not recognition, not quite, but the ghost of a response to a certain kind of tone. "You're the princess," he said slowly. "Ilsette. You're Ilsette. How do I know that?"

"Because I told you. Just now."

"No." He frowned, pressing fingers to his temple as if the answer were lodged there like a splinter. "No, I knew it before you said it. Or—I knew the shape of knowing it. Like a word on the tip of the tongue, except the whole tongue is gone."

I sat down properly then, because my knees had opinions of their own. "What do you remember? Anything?"

He was quiet for a long time. Somewhere below us, the city was still celebrating; three days of bells and bonfires, and the noise drifted up through the windows like weather.

"I remember snow," he said finally. "But I don't know if it's mine. I remember a woman's voice, but I couldn't tell you if she was calling me or someone else. I remember—" his hand drifted to his chest, patted it absently, found nothing there, and dropped. "I keep reaching for something. A ledger, I think. A habit. My hands know things my head doesn't."

"What do your hands know?"

He looked at his own fingers as though meeting them for the first time. "Numbers," he said, wonderingly. "Columns. I could probably audit this entire kingdom's accounts right now and I have no idea where I learned to do it." A breath that might have been a laugh, if laughter knew how to live in him yet. "Is that normal? Is that how forgetting works?"

"I honestly don't know," I said. "But I know someone who remembers everything, and he's very stubborn, and he's been sitting in that chair for three days waiting for you to wake up. Would you like to meet him?"

* * *

My father came in like a man approaching a wild animal—which, given everything, was exactly right. He had spent twenty years being eaten from the inside out by a thing that wore his face, and now here was the boy who'd cut it out of him, sitting up in bed with no more self than a newborn, and my father, who remembered every moment of his own hollowing, had to look at the cost of his rescue wearing a bandage and a confused expression.

"Hello," Father said. "My name is Aldric."

"I know," Emrys said. Then, horrified: "I think I know. Do I know that, or did I just decide it?"

"You may decide it," Father said gravely. "Deciding is allowed. I've decided several things since Tuesday and it's gone very well."

And that—gods help us—is how the King of Vellamere won over the man who saved his life: with bad jokes delivered in the voice of a man who had learned, the hard way, that solemnity is just another kind of cage.

They talked for an hour. I mostly watched. Father told him about the rite—or what little of it he'd witnessed from inside, the Regent's attention wrenched away, the tearing free, the dust. He did not tell him what Emrys had fed the creature to buy that distraction. I saw him decide not to, saw him fold that knowledge away behind his eyes like a letter he wasn't ready to deliver.

"And I'm supposed to be somebody," Emrys said at the end of it. "In the story you're telling. The keeper of accounts who opened the crown."

"You are somebody," Father said. "That's rather the point."

"But I'm not anybody *to myself*. There's a difference." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "You look at me and see a hero. I look at me and see a stranger with excellent arithmetic."

"Then we'll introduce you," Father said simply. "Slowly. At whatever pace you like. Memory isn't only kept in one head, Emrys. It's kept between people. You gave me back mine. Let me return the favor—one piece at a time, freely given."

Emrys stared at him. And I watched something happen on his face that I would spend a long time afterward trying to name: not hope exactly, but the ground hope stands on, suddenly appearing beneath a man who'd been falling.

"Freely given," he repeated. "That phrase means something. I can feel it meaning something." He shook his head. "It's like carrying a key to a door I've never seen."

"We'll find the door," I said.

* * *

We established a routine, because routines are scaffolding and a blank page needs scaffolding worse than it needs ink.

Every morning, Father sat with him and testified. That was the word he used, and he used it deliberately—*testimony*, the word for what witnesses give, what courts record, what history is built from. He told Emrys about the guild contracts, the vault, the night a thief had stood in the shadows watching a king talk to empty air. He told him about Sarelle, about the prophecy ledger, about the choice Emrys had made in the crypt without telling anyone which memory he meant to spend.

"He didn't tell me either," I admitted, on the fourth morning. "I found out standing in front of the whole court, watching him open himself like a door. I could have strangled him. If he'd had a self left to strangle."

"Would it have changed what he did?" Emrys asked.

"No. But I'd have felt better."

He laughed then—a real one, short and startled out of him—and I counted it as the day's victory.

Father brought artifacts, too. Small ones. A brass token stamped with the Vellum Guild's seal, recovered from Emrys's confiscated effects. Emrys turned it over in his fingers and reported, apologetically, that it meant nothing—but his thumb kept finding its worn edge, tracing it, and he didn't seem to notice he was doing it. The body remembering what the mind had lost. We collected those moments like coins ourselves, Father and I. A flinch at the smell of hot wax. An instinctive step to the side, silent, when a guard walked past—thief's footwork living on in abandoned muscles. The way he read a room's exits within seconds of entering it, every time, and could not explain how he knew.

"Am I dangerous?" he asked me once, quietly, after such a moment. "Should people be worried?"

"You're the least dangerous person in this palace," I said. "You gave away the most dangerous thing about you to save a man you'd met twice."

"I did?" He looked down at his hands. "Why would I do that?"

"Because that's who you are," I said. "You just don't have the paperwork yet."

* * *

Not every day was gentle. On the sixth day, Wren came.

They brought her up from the cells herself—my order, countersigned by Father, and if the Lord Chancellor's eyebrows climbed any higher they'd need their own heraldic registration. She was thin and jail-pale and furious in the way only honest chroniclers can be, and she marched into that infirmary room, planted herself at the foot of the bed, and looked at Emrys like a scholar confronting a corrupted manuscript.

"Do you know who I am?" she demanded.

"No," Emrys said.

"Good." She pulled out a chair. "Then you'll believe what I tell you without any sentimental interference. I'm going to recite to you. Everything I witnessed. Every document, every conversation, every fact relevant to your existence prior to eleven days ago. I wrote true histories and it cost me four years in a cell, so you will sit there and receive them."

"Wren," I started.

"He needs a source," she snapped. "Your father gives him testimony filtered through gratitude and guilt. You give him testimony filtered through—what is it you filter everything through, princess?"

"Charm," I said.

"Through charm. I am giving him the primary documents." She opened a notebook. "Now. Item one. In the autumn before last, a guild archivist named Emrys Fairweather cross-referenced an acquisition against a century-old prophecy ledger, and—"

"Stop." Emrys's voice had gone strange. "Fairweather. Say that again."

"Emrys Fairweather."

He pressed his palm flat against his chest, over the heart, over the place where something had been taken out. "That's a borrowed name," he said slowly. "Isn't it. I can feel it—it fits like someone else's coat. There was a name under it. There was a name in the snow."

Wren and I exchanged a look over his head. Neither of us knew it. Father knew it—he had carried it out of the Regent's gutted interior like a coal cupped in bare hands—but he had made a vow to himself, I think, that Emrys would hear it last, when there was enough of a person rebuilt to hold its weight.

"Then that's what we write next," Wren said, and her voice lost its edge, went almost gentle, which from Wren was roughly equivalent to weeping. "Item one, revised. A man named Emrys Fairweather, whose real name is waiting for him. Shall I continue?"

"Yes," Emrys whispered. "Please. Keep going. All of it. Even the parts that hurt."

"Especially those," Wren said. "Those are usually the load-bearing ones."

* * *

On the ninth evening, I found him alone at the window, watching the city. The celebration fires had burned down to embers in the streets below, and the smoke rose straight up in the windless dark like the city exhaling.

"They offered me a room in the east wing," he said, without turning. "With my name on the door. Except I don't have a name, so the steward asked what he should write, and I panicked and said 'the guest,' and now somewhere in the palace records there is officially a room belonging to 'the guest.'" He glanced back at me. "That's funny. I know it's funny. I can recognize humor even when I can't produce it."

"It's a start," I said. I leaned on the windowsill beside him. Below, a lamplighter was working his way down the Processional Way, each flame a small stubborn argument against the dark. "Can I tell you something? Not testimony. Just... something."

"All right."

"When I caught you running from my father's chambers, I thought you were one more thief come to pick at what was left of him. I had a knife at your throat before you'd finished turning around. And you—you were terrified, I could see it—but you *told me the truth*. Straight out. No bargaining, no lies. 'Your father is being eaten alive and I'm trying to save him.' Who does that? A thief with a knife at his throat, and he leads with honesty?"

"A stupid one," Emrys said.

"A brave one," I said. "And here's the thing I've figured out, watching you these nine days. The memories are gone. The guild, the mother, the name—all of it, ash. But the *shape* of you is still here. The honesty. The hands that know numbers. The way you laugh like it surprises you. Whatever they took, they took the contents, not the vessel. And vessels can be filled."

"By whom?"

"By whoever loves you," I said, and then immediately wanted to bite my own tongue off, because nine days ago that sentence would have been armor-plated in irony and tonight it came out naked.

He looked at me for a long moment. Grey eyes, steady, searching my face the way he'd searched the ceiling on the first day—except now there was something reading behind them.

"That's the second truest thing you've said to me," he said.

"What's the first?"

"'There's a page. Blank. You get to write it.'" He turned back to the window. "Except I've been thinking about that. A blank page isn't empty, is it? Not really. It's got margins. Watermarks. The grain of the paper. Whoever made it left marks whether they meant to or not." His hand drifted up again to his chest, to the absence. "Someone made me. Someone loved me enough to smuggle me out of a dying court. That's a watermark. I can't read it yet. But it's there."

"Then we'll hold the page up to the light together," I said, "until you can."

Below us, the lamplighter reached the end of the street, and turned, and began working his way back the other way—lighting the same lamps a second time, because some flames gutter and need tending twice, and the work is not in the lighting but in the returning.

A knock came at the door. One of Father's guards, cap in hand, looking uncomfortable.

"Begging pardon, Highness. The king asks if the guest will receive him. He says—" the guard hesitated, clearly repeating words verbatim, "—he says he's ready to talk about the crypt. About what was given. And he says the guest should know it's good news, or as close to good news as that story gets."

Emrys went very still beside me. I felt it more than saw it—the thief's stillness, the held-breath quiet of a man deciding whether a door is a trap.

"The crypt," he repeated. "I don't remember the crypt."

"No," I said. "But it remembers you. And apparently my father has decided you've waited long enough." I pushed off from the sill and offered him my arm, absurdly formal, princess to pauper, and watched him decide to take it. "Come on, then, guest. Let's go collect the first entry in the new ledger."

He laughed—surprised by it again, always surprised—and we went down together toward whatever piece of himself my father had carried out of the dark and kept warm all this time, waiting for the page to be ready to receive the ink.
