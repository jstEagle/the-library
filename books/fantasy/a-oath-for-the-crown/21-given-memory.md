## 21. Given Memory

I woke, as I had woken every morning for the past month, to a room that did not know me and a face in the mirror that offered no testimony.

The mirror was a kindness, actually. Ilsette had chosen it—polished bronze, slightly warped at the edges so that everything reflected in it came back a little softer than it truly was. "You'll want honesty eventually," she'd said when she hung it. "But you're not ready for honest yet. Start with kind."

That was the princess all over. She had spent six years watching her father be eaten from the inside and had learned, somewhere in those years, that cruelty is just impatience wearing armor.

My name was Emrys. They told me this every morning, gently, like handing me a cup of water. Wren wrote it on a slate by my bed for the first two weeks until I stopped asking. The king's physician had opinions about memory that sounded like weather reports: *the fog lifts unevenly, some mornings better, expect rain.* What he meant was that I remembered how to read but not where I'd learned it. I could hold a pen but the hand holding it belonged to a stranger. I knew what grief felt like without knowing what I'd lost to earn it.

Which, if you think about it, is its own kind of theft. Someone had stolen from me the knowledge of being robbed.

* * *

Aldric came most afternoons.

He arrived without ceremony now—a habit he was still teaching himself, having spent years as furniture for a parasite. He wore plain wool. He carried his own tea. The first time he sat with me he'd brought a guard, out of caution or habit, and dismissed him within a minute because, as he put it, "he kept looking at you the way men look at executions, and I've had enough of that for one reign."

We would sit by the window of my room in the palace infirmary wing—the good light, the view of the inner gardens where nothing had ever happened to anyone—and he would tell me pieces of myself back.

"Your hands," he said one afternoon, watching me turn a cup in circles without drinking. "Even empty, you hold them like they're full. Like you're counting something."

"Do I?"

"You do. It's very annoying. You make everyone around you feel audited." He smiled. He had a good smile, unpracticed, the smile of a man who hadn't been permitted genuine amusement in half a decade and was relearning it like a language.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I don't know how to stop."

"Don't stop. It saved my life." He said things like that plainly, which somehow made them heavier. "Emrys. Do you remember anything of the crypt?"

"No."

"Of the rite? Of me?"

I shook my head. This was our ritual too: the daily inventory of my absence. He always asked. He never flinched when the answer was no, though I had learned enough about him by then to see the flinch arrive late, in his hands, in the way he smoothed his sleeve.

"Then let me tell you what I saw," he said. "Because I remember all of it. Every hour. That is the curse of being restored—you get the whole ledger back, including the pages you'd rather burn."

And so he told me, again, the story of my own sacrifice, in the voice of the only witness who'd been conscious for it.

How I had walked into the coronation hall with my hoard open like a man baring his throat. How the Regent had poured into me, greedy, expecting a hollow vessel and finding instead a keeper who knew exactly how to hold a thing that wanted to consume him. How I had fed it my anchor memory piece by piece—my name, my mother, the snow, the village, the whole architecture of who I had been before the guild—until it gorged itself on a life so small and stubborn that it forgot, briefly, the king sitting right there behind its teeth.

"And then Ilsette spoke the words," Aldric said. "And it came out of both of us like smoke off a doused fire, and it had nothing left to wear, and it unraveled." He looked out at the gardens. "It screamed. Did you know that? It made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life. And through all of it, you stood there with your eyes open and your hands held like they were full, and you did not scream at all."

"What did I look like?" I asked. I asked every time. I couldn't help it. It was like pressing a bruise to confirm the body was yours.

"Like a blank page," he said quietly. "Like someone had erased a man and left the shape standing."

* * *

On a grey afternoon in the second month, he came with a box.

Not a grand thing. Cedarwood, small enough to sit in his lap, old enough that the hinges had gone soft. He set it between us on the table and did not open it, which I understood, even in my fog, to be significant. Kings announce things. This he was presenting.

"Do you know," he said, "what a restored monarch can do? Once the parasite is gone and the crown sits on an actual head again?"

"Ilsette says you can't do magic anymore. She seems disappointed."

"She would be. She wanted lightning bolts." He turned the box over once, twice, feeling its weight. "No. There's only one rite left to a king whose memories are his own. The old court keepers called it the Giving. A freely given memory, passed hand to hand. No theft in it. No cost. The opposite of everything that was done to either of us."

"That doesn't sound real," I said. "Nothing is free. Everything costs a piece of yourself—I learned that even if I learned nothing else. It's written in my handwriting somewhere, apparently, in a margin, in three different ledgers."

"It does cost," Aldric agreed. "But not the giver. That's the miracle of it. The cost falls on no one, which is why no guild ever traded in it. You can't steal a gift. There's no leverage in grace." He smiled ruefully. "Believe me, Sarelle's predecessors looked."

The name landed in the room between us. Sarelle. I didn't remember her, but my body did; my hands went still on the table, and Aldric watched them go still and nodded as if I'd answered a question correctly.

"She's fled past the borders. Her network is ash or ours. We'll come back to that." He set the box down flat and rested his hands on it. "Emrys. I have thought about this for a long time. Since the crypt, honestly. Since I woke up whole and found you vacant on the floor, and Ilsette kneeling beside you, and Wren weeping into a manuscript like a child—which I mention only because he'll deny it."

"He denies everything. It's how I know we're friends."

"You are friends. He told you so twice last week while insisting he wasn't sentimental."

I laughed. The laugh surprised me—it came up from somewhere below the fog, from some stratum of myself that the forgetting hadn't reached. Aldric heard it too, and something eased in his face.

"Here is my problem," he said. "You gave me everything. Your name, your mother, your history—all of it, poured out to buy my freedom. And I am a man who remembers everything now, including every kindness anyone has ever shown me, weighted against every debt. And kings are terrible at debts. We pay them in land, in titles, in gold, and none of it fits a man who emptied himself out and asked for nothing."

"I didn't ask for nothing," I said. "Ilsette tells me I asked for a window. And a slate. And that Wren's tea is undrinkable and should be replaced."

"All true. All granted." He opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of faded blue velvet, lay a memory.

I recognized it the way you recognize weather—not the object but the pressure of it. It shimmered faintly, the way bottled things do, but there was no seal on it, no wax, no guild-mark. Just a soft glow, patient, like a lamp left burning in a window for someone expected home.

"When the Regent consumed my memories," Aldric said, "it took them whole. But a few weeks before you came—before the prophecy, before any of it—there was an evening. Small. Nothing. Ilsette was twelve—" he caught himself, corrected—"no. Forgive me. The evening I mean was recent. Two months ago. Before the coronation, before the rite. Ilsette had smuggled you into my chambers, and you were supposed to be stealing from me, and instead we talked. About her mother. About the garden. About whether a king can be a person. You stayed an hour. When you left, I thought: *that man is going to die for me, and he doesn't know it yet, and neither do I.*"

I stared at the little light in the box. My chest did something complicated.

"The Regent took that evening with the rest," Aldric said. "When I was restored, it came back to me. All of it. And here is the thing about the Giving, Emrys: a king may give away a copy of what he holds. Freely. The original stays with him. Nothing is lost. Nothing is paid. Except—" he hesitated, and I saw that this part had cost him more than the rite itself, whatever the rite had cost—"except pride. Which I am reliably informed is the easiest thing in the world to lose and the hardest to admit you're carrying."

"Aldric." My voice came out strange. "What is it?"

"It's the evening we met," he said. "Truly met. And I'm giving it to you, because you were there, and you have no record of it, and I cannot stand"—his voice cracked, and he mastered it, the way he must have mastered a hundred things in front of courts—"I cannot stand that the only man in my kingdom with no past should be missing the one evening that proves he chose to be in this story. You weren't conscripted. You weren't prophesied into it. On that night, you sat with a hollow stranger and treated him like a human being, knowing it might cost you. That's not fate. That's character. And character shouldn't live in other people's testimony."

He lifted the memory from the velvet. It weighed nothing. It glowed in his palm like a coal that had decided to be gentle.

"This is how it works," he said. "You take it. You hold it. You let it in. It will feel like remembering something that was always yours, because it was. I'm not writing your history, Emrys. I'm returning a page."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I shall sit here every afternoon for the rest of both our lives telling you stories, and you will die of boredom, and it will be on my conscience, and I already have quite enough on my conscience." He held it out. "Take it."

* * *

I took it.

There is no honest way to describe what happened next, but I'm a keeper of accounts by instinct if not by memory, so let me try the ledger form:

One: warmth, starting in the palms, spreading up the arms like sunlight through shutters.

Two: a smell—candle smoke and old stone and, absurdly, oranges. Ilsette had brought oranges that night. I would learn that later and laugh until I cried.

Three: the chamber. His chambers, high in the palace, the same ones I apparently now lived near. Ilsette closing the door behind us with the particular care of a girl who had been lying to guards since she was nine. The king sitting by the fire, hollow-eyed, speaking in a voice not entirely his own, and me—me, younger by months and certain I was walking into my death—sitting down across from him anyway.

Four: the conversation. All of it, arriving whole. Him asking me what it felt like to hold other people's memories. Me answering: *like coins that spend nowhere.* Him laughing—a broken, rusty sound—and saying that was the first true thing anyone had said in that room in six years. Him telling me about his daughter's face, which he could see and not name. Me promising nothing, because I was a thief and thieves promise nothing, and meaning everything anyway.

Five: leaving. Looking back from the doorway at a man lit by firelight, thinking, with the strange clarity of the condemned: *someone should save him.*

Six: the light going out as the memory settled into place, and the room coming back, and Aldric's face in front of mine, wet-eyed and trying very hard to look like a monarch dispensing justice rather than a friend dispensing grace.

"Well?" he said.

"I remember," I said. And then, because the fog had parted just far enough to show me the shape of the man I must have been: "You realize what you've done."

"I've returned property."

"You've made me *witness* to my own sacrifice. From the outside. Do you know what that means?" I was standing now; I didn't remember standing. "It means I know what I chose. Before all of this—" I gestured at my own head, at the beautiful blank disaster of it— "I chose you. Sitting in that chair. An hour of conversation, and I decided a stranger was worth dying for. And now I have to live up to a decision I can't remember making."

"Yes," Aldric said. "That's generally what memory is."

I laughed again—that same deep laugh from under the fog—and then, to my horror and nobody's surprise, I cried a little, which he pretended not to notice with the skill of a man who had spent years pretending not to notice a parasite eating his soul.

"There's more," he said, when I had mastered myself. "There's a reason I chose that evening, specifically, out of all the evenings I could have copied."

"Because it proves I chose this."

"No. Well—yes. But also." He reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, worn at the creases, clearly handled many times. "Wren transcribed this from the prophecy before Sarelle burned the original. The ending. Both endings. Listen."

He read aloud: *"The keeper of accounts shall open the crown. One road: he pours himself out and stands empty, and the kingdom remembers for him. Other road: he keeps himself and wears the crown's hunger, and becomes the thing he came to kill."*

"I know," I said. "Wren told me. In prison. Very cheerful man about it."

"But listen to what comes after. The last lines. They were smudged—Sarelle tried to burn them first, the fire took the ink partially. Wren reconstructed them from context." Aldric looked at me. "*And on the day the empty keeper is filled again, not by theft but by gift, the ledger opens to a new page, and what is written there shall be written by his own hand, and the crown that was his burden shall be his inheritance.*"

The room went very quiet.

"Inheritance," I repeated.

"The rite of unbinding was your mother's line," Aldric said. "Blood remembers. And blood, in the old law—the law your family kept, the law the guild buried—is not just a chain. It's a claim. The keepers of the royal memory were not servants of the crown, Emrys. They were its stewards. The crown answers to them as much as they answer to it. Your family didn't serve kings." He leaned forward. "They *vouched* for them. Every coronation for four hundred years required a keeper's oath. The last one sworn was your grandfather's. The office fell empty when your family was scattered, and an empty office is a door, and a door is how the Regent got in."

I sat down slowly. The chair seemed further away than it had been.

"You're saying the throne has been waiting for me."

"I'm saying the throne has been *unguarded*, and I have been living in a house with no locks, and I would like very much to hire the locksmith." He smiled, but his eyes were serious as winter. "There will be a ceremony. Not yet—when you're ready, and not before. Ilsette insists. Wren is already drafting the oaths, badly, with too many adjectives. And when it happens, you will swear the keeping not to me but to the crown itself, which belongs to the kingdom, which belongs to everyone, which is exactly the kind of accounting your mother's line was born to do."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no, and I spend the rest of my reign sleeping badly, and you spend it tending Ilsette's new guild, and we both remain friends, and the door stays unlocked for as long as you like." He shrugged, kingly and boyish at once. "I'm not Sarelle. I don't snare people with kindness. I just... offer it, and try to survive the embarrassment when they cry."

"I'm not crying."

"Noted. The records will reflect it."

* * *

That evening I sat by my window with the good light and did what I had apparently always done: took account.

The inventory went like this. One name, borrowed and kept: Emrys. One evening, returned, proving I had chosen. One mother, known only through other people's grief for her, which was somehow both unbearable and sufficient. One sister-figure who threatened guards on my behalf and called it administration. One chronicler who denied our friendship weekly and showed up daily. One king who had given me back a page of myself and asked for nothing, which I now understood was the entire point, the thesis statement, the moral of the whole impossible ledger: *some things cannot be stolen because they were never withheld.*

And one future, unwritten, sitting on the desk in front of me like a fresh entry.

Because that was the hook of it, wasn't it. The prophecy's last line. *Written by his own hand.* Wren had reconstructed the smudged words from context, yes—but context, as any auditor knows, can be arranged. And there was another thing, a small thing, a thing I had noticed and told no one: among the papers Aldric had given me, the reconstructed prophecy bore a watermark in its vellum. A guild watermark. Vellum from the Guild's own stores—the same stock as the century-old prophecy ledger, the same stock as Sarelle's private archives.

Which meant someone in the guild had written—or rewritten—that final line long after the prophecy was made. Someone had planned, a hundred years ago or last year, for an empty keeper to be filled and crowned. Someone had built a door into the future and left it ajar.

And tomorrow, Ilsette was sending me to interview the one prisoner who might know why: a guild archivist they'd pulled from the river below the palace walls, half-drowned, clutching a waterproof satchel containing a single sheet of hundred-year-old vellum with today's date already inscribed upon it.

Some doors, it seemed, were still opening.

I dipped my pen, and on the blank first page of the notebook Ilsette had given me, beneath my borrowed name, I wrote the only entry that mattered:

*Received, freely given: one evening, one choice, one self. Balance owed: everything.*

*Payment begins at dawn.*
