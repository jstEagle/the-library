## 22. A New Entry

The rebuilt guildhall smelled of fresh-cut timber and old ink, which is, I have decided, the correct smell for a place where impossible things are traded honestly.

I stood in the doorway of what had once been Sarelle's counting room—now a reading room, open to any citizen who wished to consult the ledgers—and let myself feel the strangeness of it one more time before I got on with my life. Sunlight came through windows that were actually windows and not arrow-slits. The floorboards didn't creak in warning when someone approached; they creaked because floors do that, and no one had paid a carpenter to make them silent. There was a kettle on a stove downstairs, and anyone could use it, including me, including now, without logging the transaction.

Freedom, it turns out, smells like cedar shavings and someone else's tea.

"Emrys." Ilsette's voice from behind me, and I still hadn't trained myself out of flinching at my own name. Old habits. Dead habits, technically—I'd been given this name four months ago—but grief for a habit is a real thing, ask any reformed thief about his lockpicks. "You're standing in the doorway again."

"I'm admiring the architecture."

"You're stalling."

"I'm admiring the architecture *while* stalling. They're not mutually exclusive. I was a professional."

She came to stand beside me, arms crossed in that way she had that made dukes feel audited. She wore her hair up now, plainly, and dressed like a clerk, which she said was deliberate: a princess who looked like a clerk could walk into any room in the city, and a clerk who turned out to be a princess could stop the room entirely. She had used this trick twice already on the Council of Guilds, to devastating effect.

"The first entry's yours," she said. "If you still want it."

"I want it."

"You've wanted it for three weeks. You've visited the ledger eleven times. Wren counted."

"Wren counts everything. It's his whole personality."

"It's why we pardoned him," she said, "so yes. Eleven times. Today you write or today you admit you're afraid of your own handwriting, and honestly, either answer would be very on-brand for you."

I loved her, in the way you love people who have seen you vacant-eyed on a crypt floor and decided to keep you anyway. I did not say this. I said, "Give me an hour," and she gave me two, which was her way of saying it back.

* * *

The ledger itself sat on a lectern in the center of the reading room, under glass that lifted, because Ilsette had insisted that a ledger of returned memories should never be locked away from the people whose memories filled it. It was bound in grey calfskin, unadorned, because Wren had argued—correctly—that ornamentation implied ownership, and nothing in this book belonged to the guild anymore.

Its title page read, in Wren's careful chronicler's hand:

**THE LEDGER OF RESTITUTION**
*Being a true account of all things taken and given back.*
*Nothing herein shall be taken again.*

Below that, blank space, waiting.

Eleven visits. I'll confess to them all. The first time, I couldn't get past the title page. The second time, I wrote *Emrys Fairweather* and then sat there looking at it until a junior archivist asked if I needed help, and I fled. The third through tenth times are various flavors of the same cowardice, and I won't bore you with the inventory. A man who kept accounts for fifteen years knows exactly how many ways there are to fail to write a single line.

Because here was the problem, the one I hadn't told Ilsette, though she'd guessed: a name isn't just letters. A name is an entry, and every entry implies a ledger, and every ledger implies a keeper, and I had spent my whole remembered life being the keeper precisely so that no one would ever enter *me*. Invisibility wasn't something that happened to me. I had built it, plank by plank, entry by entry, and Sarelle had only ever maintained the structure I handed her. The guild didn't make me unwritten. I had volunteered.

And now I was being asked, politely, by royalty, to pick up the pen and author myself, in front of witnesses, permanently, in ink.

You can see why it took eleven visits.

* * *

On the twelfth visit, I brought company.

Aldric came down from the palace incognito, which for him meant a plain cloak and a guard detail he'd argued down from twelve men to four, which Ilsette had then argued down to two, and which he had then lost entirely somewhere near the fish market through a maneuver he refused to explain and I chose not to investigate. He arrived at the guildhall looking pleased with himself, holding two meat pies like scepters.

"They say the pie cart on Coriander Street is the best in the city," he announced.

"They say that about every pie cart within a mile of the palace, because the palace guards eat at all of them."

"A king must remain humble," he said, and handed me a pie.

He was better these days. Not whole—Wren's histories suggested no king had been whole since the Regent first coiled into a crown three hundred years ago, and Aldric had been its longest meal in generations—but *better*, filling in around the edges like frost forming on glass, each day a little more pattern. He remembered his daughter's name now. He remembered his wife, gone eight years, and grieved her properly instead of grieving the shape of a grief he couldn't source. He still lost things. Last week he'd forgotten the name of his Master of Coin mid-audience and covered by promoting the man, which everyone agreed was the smoothest fiscal policy the crown had produced in a decade.

But he remembered *me*. That was the strange grace of it. The Regent had eaten so much of him, and yet the months since the crypt had given him back the shape of what we'd done together, and he held onto it fiercely, the way you hold a coal in winter.

We ate our pies on the steps of the reading room while clerks and citizens streamed past us into the building where their stolen childhoods waited in labeled vellum cases. An old woman emerged clutching a small sealed folio to her chest—the river's childhood, actually, the very entry I'd audited on my last ordinary night, returned to the river wardens who'd petitioned for it. She was crying. Nobody stopped to stare. That was the other thing about the new guild: miracles happened in it daily, and the city had already begun to take them for granted, which is, I suppose, what victory looks like from the inside.

"Do you know what I remember most," Aldric said, "from before?"

"The Regent?"

"No. Before *that*. Young. My coronation." He wiped his hands carefully, a man who respected a good pie. "Everyone remembers the crown going on. What I remember is my father's chamberlain, a dry old stick named Halloway, leaning over as they carried the crown toward me on its cushion, and whispering, *'Sire, whatever happens in there, remember that you were a person first.'*" He smiled at the middle distance. "I thought it was advice about humility. It took me thirty years and a parasite to understand it was a survival instruction."

"And now you give it to me."

"I give it to everyone. I've put it in the coronation oath. Ilsette will hate reciting it." He looked at me then, directly, with those faded eyes that had learned to be sharp again. "You were a person first, Emrys. Before the guild. Before the prophecy. Whatever you write in that book today—it doesn't have to be the final word. Ledgers can be amended. That's rather the point of the new one."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me in months."

"I've had practice. I spent four months being read my own life back by strangers." He stood, brushed crumbs from his cloak, and became, in the space of a breath, a king again—you could watch it happen, the posture assembling itself, though the twinkle stayed. "Write your name. Sign it boldly. And if the hand shakes—" he patted my shoulder, "—let it. Shaking hands mean the person is still in there."

Then he went off to lose his guards again, and I went inside to commit identity fraud against myself.

* * *

The reading room was quiet at the hour before closing. Wren sat at his desk by the window, ostensibly cataloguing returns, actually watching me over the top of his spectacles with the patience of a man who had spent two years in prison composing arguments and now deployed them only when certain of victory.

"Eleven visits," he said.

"Twelve."

"Twelve. Shall I tell you what I've observed across the sample?"

"Wren—"

"Incarceration taught me that a man's relationship to a blank page is a complete diagnosis of his soul." He set down his pen. "Sarelle feared blank pages, so she filled them with other people's lives. You *are* a blank page, or believe you are, so you fill other pages and call it work." He gestured at the lectern. "That page has been waiting four months. It will wait forever if you let it. Blankness is patient. It's the only thing in this building that doesn't need anything from you—which is, I suspect, exactly why you keep visiting."

I hated him a little. I loved him more. This was, I'd gathered, the standard experience of knowing Wren.

"What if I choose wrong?" I said. "What if I write a name and it's—small. Wrong-shaped. What if the old woman in the snow village knew what I was called before, and I pick something else, and I spend fifty years wondering if I stole even my own name?"

"Did you go back?" he asked. "To the village. After the funeral."

"Twice."

"And?"

"And the second time, Marta—she says the snow-name was a nickname anyway. Her sister called me something else. Nobody remembers what. The record burned with the house." I laughed, and it came out rougher than intended. "My entire provenance is a nickname and a gap in the archives. Very fitting. The guild trained me well after all."

"Or," Wren said, "you are the freest man in this kingdom. Every noble in Vell is shackled to a name some dead ancestor chose for land-grabbing reasons. You get to choose yours the way authors choose characters: deliberately, knowing what story it needs to carry." He picked his pen back up. "Also, you've been signing documents *Emrys* for four months. Arrests, petitions, the testimony at Sarelle's trial. Legally speaking, the theft already occurred. You're merely here to file the paperwork."

"*That's* the argument that does it? Bureaucracy?"

"Emrys. I am a chronicler. Of course bureaucracy is the argument that does it."

* * *

I opened the glass case. I took up the pen—a plain one, guild-issue, the same make I'd used for fifteen years of other people's accounts, because Ilsette had insisted on that too: *the tools aren't guilty, Emrys, only the uses.*

The page waited. Blankness, patient as promised.

Here is what I knew, standing there. I knew that memory is not a possession but a practice—that who I am is not buried somewhere under the ash of what the Regent took, waiting to be excavated, but built, daily, out of what I do and who sees me doing it. I knew that the guild had taught me the most dangerous lesson in the world: that a self is a ledger entry, fixed, auditable, closeable. And I knew that the new ledger existed to teach the opposite: that a self is written and rewritten and amended in the margins, and the crime was never keeping accounts—it was keeping them closed.

I knew one more thing. Small, private, mine. On the night everything began, I stole a stranger's memory from the shelves just to feel alive—a young man's first kiss, stolen under a paper lantern at the Lanternmarket, tasting of cinnamon and terror. I'd held it like a coin for years, one warm thing in a cold inventory. When the guild fell, that vellum case had come back to us in the general restitution, unlabeled, orphaned. We never found its owner. It sits in the returns archive to this day, and once a month, when no one's watching, I take it out and live in it for a minute, and I am twenty years old and brave and kissed.

Ilsette thinks I should return it to the Lanternmarket and let chance find him. Wren thinks I should log it as unclaimed property. I think some entries belong in the margins, unresolvable, and that a man is allowed one warm coin he refuses to spend. The new guild can accommodate a margin. That's what makes it new.

I dipped the pen. My hand shook, exactly as predicted, exactly as permitted.

And I wrote:

*Entry the First. Returned freely, this day, to the undersigned: one name, long withheld, provenance uncertain, condition much improved. Received in full gratitude by—*

And then the name. Not Fairweather—that had been the guild's surname, issued with my first apron, a label on a jar. Not the snow-name either; Marta's nickname belonged to a boy who died of safety, and I honor him, but I am not filing him as myself.

I wrote the name I'd been carrying for four months without letting it touch paper. The name I'd chosen the way Wren said—deliberately, knowing the story it needed to carry. It means, in the old river-tongue of the wardens' district, *one who keeps the light on.* I found that out in week two and pretended for ten more weeks that it wasn't the reason.

I signed it. Boldly. With the shake.

Wren rose from his desk, crossed the room, examined the entry with the gravity of a coronation, and said, "Legible. Slightly sentimental. Historically sound." Then he shook my hand, which from Wren was a eulogy and a christening both.

From the doorway, Ilsette watched, arms crossed, and did not pretend she hadn't been there the whole time. "Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"How does it feel? Authorship."

I considered the question properly, because she deserved a proper answer, and because—for the first time in my life—I had a self worth consulting.

"Terrifying," I said. "Like standing in a doorway."

She laughed, and came to look at the page over my shoulder, and read my name aloud once, quietly, trying it the way you try a key in a lock. It turned. It had been turning for months. Now it was on record.

* * *

We closed the guildhall together that evening—I locked the front door myself, with a key, openly, which remains one of my favorite crimes—and I walked home through streets that knew my face. The lamplighter nodded to me. A child selling chestnuts called me by name, having heard it from her mother, who had gotten her brother's memories back in March. The city had entered me into its ledgers everywhere, in a hundred casual hands, and none of the entries were secrets, and none of them were cages.

At my door, I paused. Habit. The old instinct to check the shadows, count the exits, confirm that no one had followed.

No one had. No one was hunting me. The only ledger I appeared in anymore was one I'd opened myself.

But old instincts, as I say, die hard, and as I stood there indulging mine, I noticed the envelope.

It lay on my doorstep, plain grey vellum, unsealed, unsigned. Inside, a single sheet, and on it, in a hand I did not know—neat, patient, practiced—the following:

*A correction to your records, Keeper. Restitution was incomplete. One item taken from you was never catalogued in our inventory, and therefore never returned. We hold it still. If you wish to know what the Regent kept for itself—the piece of you it valued above all others, the reason the prophecy named a keeper of accounts—inquire at the address below.*

*Some ledgers, Mr. ——— , are never truly closed.*

It used my new name. Written correctly. By someone who had read today's entry, hours old, under glass, in a locked hall.

I stood a long time on my doorstep, the lamp above me buzzing, the street empty and warm and safe around me, holding a page that smelled faintly—not of cedar, not of tea, but of dust, and deep places, and crowns.

Well. I had always been good at finding things that weren't supposed to exist.

I went inside, took out a fresh sheet of my own, and began a new file.
