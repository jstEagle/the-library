## 6. Korrath Wakes

Vael had knelt at a hundred shrines in her life, and every one of them had been stone.

That was the thing about Korrath, she had learned long ago, in the years before she understood what she was learning: he did not live in stone. He did not live in altars or reliquaries or the gilded cages the church built to hold what it feared. He lived in people. He lived in the set of a soldier's jaw before a hopeless charge, in the fury of a mother defending her child, in the terrible bright patience of a smith hammering a sword through a winter's night. The old texts called him the God of the Red Road, the Lord of the Broken Shield, He Who Answers When the Answer Is Blood. The church called him the First Heresy. His faithful called him, when they dared call him anything at all, *the General*.

Tonight, in the hollow beneath the Hanging Stair where forty of the faithful had gathered out of the dark like sparks rolling uphill, Vael stood at the edge of the firelight and felt him coming the way you feel a storm coming — not in the sky, but in the teeth.

* * *

She had brought Juniper down from the high passes over two days of hard climbing, and he had come the way a drowning man comes to a rope: without trust, without choice, gripping so hard his knuckles went white. He had not slept. She had watched him not sleeping through the small hours, sitting with his back against the cave wall, one hand pressed flat against his sternum as if holding a door shut from the inside. Once, near dawn, she had heard him whisper to it — to the thing inside him — and the whisper had not been a prayer. It had been a negotiation.

*Hold on,* he had said, the night before, to the dark. She had heard it. She had pretended she hadn't.

Now they stood in the hollow, and the faithful were arriving, and Vael's stomach was a knot of old rope. Forty of them. Fifty. More still coming down the goat-tracks with torches hooded and voices hushed, smiths and shepherds, two deserters from the Ashen Wardens' auxiliary with their iron badges buried in the scree, a one-armed veteran of the Border Wars who had walked eleven days on a leg that should have killed him. They came because the dreams had come. That was how Korrath called his people now, in the season of the Ember's waking — not with trumpets but with dreams, the same dream, night after night: a hearth burning in a place with no house around it, and a voice saying *come*.

Vael had had the dream too. She had had it for twenty years.

"Is it true?" whispered the veteran, Bren, standing beside her, his remaining hand trembling on his spear-haft. "What you said in the message. That the vessel walks. That it's *here*."

"He's here," Vael said.

"Does he know what he is?"

"He's learning." She looked across the firelight to where Juniper stood apart from everyone, as he had stood apart from everyone his whole life, though he didn't know she knew that. "He's learning the way you learn a wound is yours. Slowly. And all at once."

Bren grunted. Around them the faithful were arranging themselves in the old pattern — not the church's pattern, not rows and ranks, but a ring, faces inward, the way soldiers had stood before battle in the years before the Binding, when the gods still walked and the war-god's people had needed no church to tell them how to gather. Vael had read about that pattern in books the church had burned. She had never seen it. Now she stood inside it, and the shape of it fit her the way her mother's voice had once fit her, and that frightened her more than the Wardens ever had.

* * *

It began, as it always began, with the drum.

There was no drum. That was the truth of it, the truth she had never been able to explain to anyone who hadn't felt it: there was no drum, and there was a drum, and both were true at once. The heartbeat came up out of the ground, out of the forty throats, out of the stone of the Hanging Stair itself, slow at first, then quicker, and the faithful began to sway, and their breathing found the rhythm, and Vael felt it take her too, the way it always took her, a hand closing around her spine from the inside.

She looked at Juniper.

He had gone rigid. His hand was at his chest again, and through his fingers — she saw it, she was the only one close enough to see it — a light was leaking. Not firelight. Not torchlight. Something older and more patient, the color of coals at three in the morning, the color of a hearth that has been kept alive all night for someone who hasn't come home yet.

The Ember knew its kin were gathering.

"No," Juniper whispered. She read the shape of the word more than heard it. "No, no — I didn't call anyone. I didn't *ask* for—"

"You don't have to ask," Vael said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to. "That's what none of us ever understood. You don't have to ask. He's been waiting for you since before you were born."

The light through his fingers brightened. The drumbeat quickened. And the fire in the center of the ring — a modest thing, cookfire-small, built from wet mountain scrub — stood up.

Vael had seen Korrath manifest twice before in her life, and both times she had failed to describe it afterward, and both times the failure had felt like a kind of blasphemy. Language was built for the world the church had made, a world of walls and ledgers and sealed doors, and what stood up out of the fire did not fit in that world. He was tall the way a cliff is tall. He was armored, but the armor was not iron — it was every shield that had ever broken in defense of someone else, fused and layered and scarred, and it moved like living muscle. His face was the worst of it and the best of it: a face like every face in the ring, all of them at once, the veteran's and the smith's and the shepherd's, and beneath it all, faint as a watermark, a face Vael recognized because she had seen it in her own reflection on her worst days — the face of someone who has lost everything and refuses, *refuses*, to stop.

The hollow went to its knees. All of it. Forty people and fifty and the scree and the goats on the ledges above, everything that had knees.

Juniper did not kneel.

He stood in the ring with his hand pressed to his burning chest and his face gray with exhaustion, and Vael saw him sway, saw his knees buckle a half-inch, saw him lock them. Stubborn. Gods, he was stubborn. She had known him four days and she already knew it was the truest thing about him.

Korrath looked at him.

The whole mountain seemed to lean in.

* * *

*Vessel.* The voice did not come through the ears. It came through the floor, through the bones, through the fillings of the teeth. It was not loud. That was the trick of it, the horror and the beauty: it did not need to be loud. It was the voice you heard in the last moment before sleep, the voice that had been talking the whole time, the voice you had mistaken for your own.

*Little brother. Little hearth. You walked far to reach us.*

"I didn't walk to reach you," Juniper said. His voice cracked in the middle and he kept going anyway, and Vael felt something in her chest turn over — admiration, she realized, clean and sharp. "I walked to get *away* from everyone who wants something from me. You'll have to form a line."

A sound went through the ring that might have been laughter, or might have been the mountain settling, or might have been both. Even Korrath's armored shoulders moved with something like amusement. Vael did not laugh. She had seen that amusement before, in officers reviewing troops, and she knew what it cost the things it was amused at.

*You are angry,* Korrath said. *Good. Anger is honest. The church taught the world to kneel and call it peace. I taught mine to stand and call it war, and they burned us for the difference.* The great head tilted, and the firelight ran along the broken shields of the armor. *They burned us, little hearth. Do you know what the Binding was? Not a victory. A theft. They took the gods' hearts and buried them in iron and told the world the world was safer quiet. Three hundred years of quiet. Three hundred years of prayers going into a hole.*

"And now they're being answered again," Juniper said. "Because of me."

*Because of what sleeps in you.* Korrath took a step, and the step made no sound and shook the ground anyway. *The last free piece of the divine heart. The church will come for it. Not to free it — to cut it out. I have seen their ledgers, vessel. I know what they do to the god-touched. Iron and fire and a hole in the world where a person used to be.* The voice dropped, and the drop was the cruelest thing yet, because it made the god sound almost gentle. *But you. You they will never touch. Not while you stand under my banner.*

Juniper's breath went ragged. Vael watched it happen — watched the offer land, watched it find the place in him it was aimed at. She knew that place. She had one too. It was the place where the loneliness lived, the place every orphan and outcast and stray in this ring carried, the place Korrath had been finding in people for three thousand years, because he was not a god of war the way the church said, not only that. He was a god of the besieged. He was the god of everyone the walls had been built against. And he offered what every besieged thing wants: *Come inside. Be one of us. No one will ever make you stand alone again.*

She watched Juniper's eyes go bright and wet in the coal-light, and she hated Korrath in that moment with a hatred that had no floor to it, because she knew the offer was real. That was the thing no one understood about the war-god, the thing she had spent twenty years refusing to say aloud even to herself. The offer was always real. The protection was real. The brotherhood was real. The belonging was real.

It was only the price that was never spoken.

*You have been alone your whole life,* Korrath said, softer now, the voice coming from everywhere, from inside Juniper's own ribs where the Ember burned. *Carried like a package. Passed like a coin. Even the ones who hid you in your mother's arms — did they ask you? Did anyone, in all your years, ask what *you* wanted?* A pause, and in the pause the whole ring heard Juniper's breath break. *I am asking. Kneel, and be my brother, and no one will ever use you again. Kneel, and be feared, and be free. Kneel, and I will burn the church's iron out of Thornfell mountain by mountain, and when the last god is free we will build a world where no foundling is ever again a vessel for anything but his own life.*

The silence after was total. Even the drumbeat stopped. Even the wind on the Hanging Stair.

And Juniper — gray-faced, burning, four days hunted, a lifetime alone — took one step forward.

Vael's heart stopped.

* * *

She had loved Korrath for twenty years. She needed him to know that, needed it said somewhere, even if only inside her own skull where no god could hear it: she had loved him, and the love had not been stupid. He had found her at fourteen, half-dead in the aftermath of a village burning, and he had given her the only thing anyone had ever given her, which was a reason to keep standing up. Everything she was, every scar she was proud of, every person she had pulled out of the Wardens' reach — all of it grew from that root. She was not going to stand here in the firelight and pretend the root was rotten.

But she had watched him for twenty years, too. And she had noticed the thing that the faithful were not supposed to notice, the thing she had buried so deep even her dreams had stopped digging for it: that Korrath's wars never ended. That freedom, in his mouth, was always one more campaign away. That the brotherhood he offered was real, and the price of it was that you never, ever stopped being a soldier, because a soldier was the only shape in which he could love you.

She watched Juniper take the second step.

His hand came away from his chest. The light beneath his shirt was bright enough now to throw his shadow up the cave wall — a tall shadow, a burning shadow, a shadow that looked nothing like the tired smuggler who cast it. The Ember was answering Korrath. Of course it was. It was a piece of the divine heart, and the divine heart had been alone in the dark for three hundred years, sealed in iron, and here at last was a voice from home.

*Kneel,* said Korrath, and every soul in the ring leaned toward the word like grass toward sun. *Kneel, little brother, and be home.*

Juniper's knee went down.

It went down an inch. Two inches. Vael saw the whole future balanced on that knee — she saw the war, the burning villages, the iron legions marching and the faithful marching to meet them and the mountains running red, she saw Korrath with the Ember's power poured into him like wine into a cup, she saw the world the church had made burning and a new world built on the same old bones — and she opened her mouth to say *no, don't, please*—

And Juniper stopped.

He stopped with his knee a hand's width from the stone, and he stayed there, trembling, and when he spoke his voice was very quiet and it carried to every corner of the hollow the way only true things carry.

"You said no one would use me again," he said. "But you didn't ask me anything, either. You told me what I've suffered. You told me what you'd burn. You told me who I'd be — *your brother*, *your vessel's vessel*, yours, yours, yours." He lifted his head, and the coal-light and the hearth-light met in his eyes, and Vael saw something there that made the god's fire flicker. "You made a beautiful speech and there wasn't one word in it about what I want. Everyone who's ever wanted something from me at least pretended to ask."

He stood back up.

He stood back up, and the Ember flared once — not in anger, Vael thought. In *relief* — and Korrath's face, the face made of every face, went through a change that took Vael's breath away, because she had never seen it before in twenty years of loving him.

He looked hungry.

For one heartbeat — one drumbeat that no one drummed — the mask of the benevolent general slipped, and what looked out of the fire was not a protector. It was a thing that had been starving for three hundred years, looking at a meal. It was the church's hunger in a different armor. It was every cage that had ever called itself a home.

Then it was gone, smoothed over, and Korrath was magnificent and sorrowful and kind again, and he said, *Think on it, little brother. My banner does not lower. My door does not close,* and the fire sank, and the god went out of the ring like a tide going out, leaving the faithful kneeling in the ashes of an ordinary cookfire, weeping, not knowing why.

Juniper stood in the middle of it with his hand pressed to his chest, breathing hard, and looked directly at Vael across the dead fire.

"You knew," he said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — *checking*. "At the end. You saw it too. What was that? What did I just see?"

Vael looked at the ashes. At the forty faces. At the dark mouths of the passes above, where, sooner or later, the Wardens would come, and Korrath's faithful with them, and every road out of Thornfell would be a road through someone's army.

"I saw my god," she said, "and I saw what he's hungry for. And I don't think they're the same thing anymore."

She held out her hand to Juniper Voss, vessel of the Ember, the most hunted man in the mountains.

"Come on," she said. "We need to be gone before he decides to stop asking."

Above them, far off across the valley, the church's signal-fires began to bloom on the peaks — one, then three, then a dozen — answering each other across the dark like a chain of small cold suns.
