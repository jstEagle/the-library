## 7. The Iron Tithe

The smoke reached Halvard before the village did.

She stood at the head of the column, her white cloak gone grey with road dust, and watched the plume rise from the valley of Marrow's Bend — a fat black column, too wide for a chimney fire, too deliberate for an accident. Beside her, the tithe-collectors who had been rechristened inquisitors three days ago in a ceremony lasting less than an hour adjusted their new sashes and tried not to look at the sky.

"You smell that, Sister?" asked Brother Anselm, a soft-bellied man who had spent twenty years weighing grain against prayer-debts and had never, until this week, carried anything sharper than an abacus.

"I smell it," Halvard said.

"Is it true, what they're saying? That the villages are contaminated?"

Halvard looked at him. Anselm had a wife once, before he took the ash-grey; she kept a bakery two valleys over, and every year on the feast of the Sealed Hearth he still bought a loaf from her and left it, untouched, on the shrine step. Halvard knew this because she had watched him do it. She had never told him she knew.

"The Doctrine of Contamination is very clear," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat as slate. "Where the old gods stir, corruption follows. The church burns what it cannot bless."

Anselm nodded, satisfied, or wanting to be. That was the thing about faith, Halvard had learned across nineteen years as a Warden: most people did not want to believe. They wanted to have believed. They wanted the warmth of it afterward, the way a man wants the memory of a fire without the labor of the wood.

The column moved down into the valley, toward the smoke.

* * *

Marrow's Bend had been a tithe-village for as long as the church had kept ledgers. Halvard knew it from the rolls: three hundred souls, a shrine to the Sealed Hearth rebuilt four times, a tithe paid faithfully even through the famine years — grain, wool, and once, memorably, a brass hearth-hook that the village elder had wept to surrender because it had been in his family since before the Binding.

The village was not burning. That was the first wrongness. The smoke came from the shrine.

They had piled the shrine's own timbers against it and set them alight, and the Wardens who had done it stood around the fire in a loose ring, feeding it hymnals, and the village stood further out in a wider ring, silent, watching their shrine become a pyre. A woman held a child whose face was pressed into her neck. An old man still held the brass hearth-hook, and Halvard saw that his knuckles had gone white around it, and that no one had yet taken it from him.

Warden-Captain Dree turned when Halvard approached. He was a broad man with a burn-scarred jaw and the untroubled eyes of someone who had never once lain awake at night. He saluted with two fingers to the ash-sigil on his breast.

"Sister Halvard. You're late for the purification."

"I wasn't informed of it."

"You are now." He gestured at the fire with something like pride. "Three answered prayers in this village in the past month. A fever broken. A well that sweetened. A child born with eyes like banked coals." He said it the way another man might list vermin. "The old god's breath is in the stones here. The Synod has ruled it contaminated ground."

"The fever was broken," Halvard said. "The well was sweetened. The child was born alive."

"By means the church did not license."

"By means the church has not *seen* in three hundred years." She kept her voice level. Around them the villagers were listening, and she was aware of that, and she was aware that Dree was aware of it, and that this conversation had stopped being a conversation and become a performance with an audience of the damned. "Answered prayers are a contamination now, Captain? Then the Doctrine of the Sealed Hearth has changed since I last read it. I was taught the gods were bound because their *wrath* was dangerous. I was never taught their mercy was."

Dree smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which made it worse. "Sister. You're a fine inquisitor. The best ledger-reader the Ashen Order has. But you're asking the wrong question. The question is not whether the miracle was pleasant." He leaned in, and lowered his voice, and the smoke moved between them like a curtain. "The question is who is *answering*. And what they want in return. Nothing old and buried gives gifts without wanting the grave opened. The Synod understands this. I'd advise you to."

Behind him, the old man with the hearth-hook had begun, very quietly, to sing. It was an old song, a tithe-village song, the kind sung over bread and banked fires — Halvard had heard fragments of it in a dozen valleys, always the same melody, always the same refrain: *keep the coal, keep the coal, the dark is long but the coal is whole.* The Wardens shifted. One of them reached for the man's shoulder.

"Let him sing," Halvard said.

Dree looked at her. The firelight made his untroubled eyes briefly troubled. "Sister."

"Let him sing, Captain. He is watching his god's house burn. If the church cannot spare him a song, then we have already lost whatever it is we think we're protecting." She heard the words leave her and knew, even as they went, that they were a hairline crack in something load-bearing. She said them anyway. "I'll write it in my report. A purification conducted in silence would have looked like fear."

Dree held her gaze a moment longer. Then he stepped back, and let the old man sing, and the village of Marrow's Bend sang with him, all three hundred, low and cracked and steady, while the shrine came down in embers, and Halvard stood in the middle of it with her ledger under her arm and understood that she was standing inside the exact thing her order had been founded to prevent.

She just could not have said, yet, which side of it she was on.

* * *

The Synod's orders had come down from the High Ashen Seat a week before, and Halvard had read them eleven times, because the eleventh reading was the one where the horror finished arriving.

*By decree of the Synod of the Sealed Flame: the tithe-collectors of the nine valleys are hereby invested with the authority of inquisition. All settlements reporting unlicensed warmth, unlicensed healing, unlicensed vision, or unlicensed prayer are to be assessed for contamination. Contamination is to be purified. Records of all god-touched persons are to be forwarded to the Seat. The Warden Halvard is commended for her diligence in the matter of the pilgrim and the cartographer, and is assigned to trace the contamination's source.*

*The source is to be taken alive.*

*Alive*, in the Synod's cipher, had a specific meaning. Halvard had processed enough of the taken-alive to know what waited for them beneath the High Ashen Seat: the Quiet Rooms, where inquisitors gentler than Dree and colder than she was asked questions of the flesh until the flesh confessed. She had always told herself the work was necessary. She had always told herself the things beneath the Seat were not people anymore, or had never been, or had earned it.

She was finding it harder, this season, to finish the sentences.

Because of the cartographer. Because of Juniper Voss, who had sat across from her in a smuggler's waystation with his ink-stained fingers and his careful, deflecting smile, and who had lied to her badly and then well and then, at the end, not at all — who had looked at her when she asked about the dying pilgrim with an expression she had never been trained to classify, because it was not guilt, and it was not fear, and it was not defiance. It had been *grief*. The grief of a man being asked about something he had only just learned he had lost.

She had let him go. Her report said she had released him for insufficient evidence. The truth was she had released him because when he stood up to leave, some animal part of her had wanted to say *run*, and she had not trusted herself to open her mouth and have anything else come out.

Now the Synod wanted him taken alive, and Halvard was assigned to trace the contamination's source, and she was beginning to understand that everyone above her already knew what the source was.

* * *

The archive beneath the High Ashen Seat was called the Cold Repository, and it was the church's second-best-kept secret. The first was what the church had done to the gods.

Halvard had spent nineteen years climbing toward access to it. Inquisitors of the fourth rank could request ledgers; of the fifth, correspondence; of the sixth — her rank, earned in the autumn, in a ceremony she could no longer remember feeling proud of — the sealed collections. She went down into it three days after Marrow's Bend, with a lantern and a legitimate pretext and a heart beating like a fist on a door.

The Repository was a cavern of shelved stone, older than the church that claimed it, and the oldest shelves were the reason she had come. The church had been founded on the Binding — that was doctrine, that was the first hymn every child learned: *the gods grew proud, the gods grew cruel, and iron was the merciful tool.* But doctrine was written by the winners, and the Cold Repository was where the losers' paper had ended up, catalogued by archivists who, across the centuries, had apparently possessed consciences of wildly inconsistent size.

She found the collection she wanted under the heading *Pre-Binding Correspondence, Hearth Cults, Misc.*

The archivist's name was Brother Emmerich Vael-Sorren — no relation, she would later learn, to the woman she had never heard of yet — and he had been Keeper of the Third Shelf in the last years before the Binding, four centuries gone. His letters were filed in a box that had been mislabelled with what Halvard slowly recognized was deliberate incompetence: *Hymnals, Damaged, Do Not Repair.* No one had opened it in a very long time. The dust on it had its own smaller dust.

The letters were written in a quick, careful hand, and they were addressed, over and over, across eleven years, to a woman named Ilsa, who had apparently been his friend and his conscience in equal measure. Halvard read them by lantern-light while the Repository's cold worked into her bones, and she read them twice, and by the second reading her hands were not steady.

*They tell us the Binding is mercy, Ilsa. Perhaps it is. But I have stood in the hearing-rooms when they brought in the god-touched, and I have watched what they do to a child who can light a candle by wanting it, and I will tell you what I have concluded: we did not bind the gods to protect the flock. We bound them because a god who answers prayers makes the church unnecessary. Fear keeps the tithe paid. Hope, unlicensed, is competition.*

*The Synod has ordered the Ember destroyed. You know what the Ember is — the heart of the hearth-god, the last piece that stayed warm when the rest of him was ironed flat. The Synod fears it. They are right to. A heart like that, loose in the world, would undo in a generation everything the Seat has built. But I cannot do it, Ilsa. I have held it. It is not a weapon. It is the warm side of the door. I will not be the man who put out the last fire.*

*I have a plan. You will call it madness and you will be half right. There is a foundling coming up from the lowlands, a babe with no name and no kin, the kind the Seat grinds up without noticing. I am going to put the Ember into him. Not beside him — the Synod searches bodies, they would find it in a heartbeat — into him. It will sleep. It will keep him alive, because that is what hearths do. And the Synod will never look for their stolen fire inside a smuggler's orphan, because the Synod has never once in its history looked at a poor man and seen anything worth stealing.*

*If you are reading this and I am dead, then know: I did not hide the Ember to save the church, or to damn it. I hid it the way you hide a coal in ash — so that someday, someone cold could find it again. Let him live his life first. Let it be HIS life, Ilsa, as much as it can be. And if they ever come for him — and they will, they always come — then God of hearths forgive me, but I have left him one gift beyond the fire itself: I have left the proof. The proof of what he is, and of why, and of what the Seat knew and when. A man who knows the truth of himself cannot be quietly disappeared. That is the whole of my strategy. Truth, and a good hiding place, and prayer.*

*Pray for him, Ilsa. Not to the bound ones. To whatever is left listening.*

The last letter was dated nine days before the Binding was sealed. There was no record anywhere in the Repository of what happened to Brother Emmerich. There was a record of what happened to the foundling: a single line in an intake ledger, *male infant, no name, transferred to the monastery at Greyfall for raising,* and beside it, in the margin, in a different hand — a Synod hand, a hand that had known — the notation:

*Vessel confirmed. Monitor. Do not retrieve until the fire spreads. Retrieval before maturation risks loss of the fragment.*

*Monitor*, Halvard thought. Nineteen years she had served the Seat. Nineteen years of purifications and Quiet Rooms and the doctrine of merciful iron, and all of it built on a lie she could now hold in her two hands: the church had not lost the Ember. The church had *watched* it. Had known for four centuries what Juniper Voss was, had let him be born and raised and mapped and hunted, had waited for the fire to spread so they could take it at its ripest — and had sent her, Halvard, to find him, because she was the best ledger-reader they had, and because she had not yet been trusted to know.

The lantern guttered. Somewhere far above, the Synod was singing evening office, and the hymn came down through the stone in fragments: *the gods grew proud, the gods grew cruel, and iron was the merciful tool.*

Halvard sat in the cold with a dead archivist's letters and understood that she had just read the account book of her entire life, and the sums did not balance.

* * *

She copied everything. It took her until the small hours — her hand moving in the trained, unlovely shorthand of the Ashen Order, the script they taught for reports that might need to be destroyed. When she finished, she sat looking at the pages, and she thought about the old man in Marrow's Bend and his brass hearth-hook, and about Brother Anselm's loaf on the shrine step, and about a smuggler-cartographer with grief on his face, and about the word *monitor* sitting in a margin like a spider in a web.

There was a version of her that burned the copies. That version had been comfortable. That version had believed the iron was mercy, or had at least been able to finish the sentence that said so.

She put the copies inside her cloak, against her ribs, where the warmth of her own body would keep the ink from cracking in the cold. Then she climbed the long stairs out of the Cold Repository, past the shelves of the losers' paper, up into the light and the singing, and she went to her cell, and she took out a fresh map of the nine valleys — the church's map, the official one, all roads and tithe-routes and shrines marked with the ash-sigil — and she spread it on her desk and looked at it for a long time.

Then she did the thing she had never done in nineteen years of service.

She began to mark it with what she knew that the church did not. The villages already purified. The villages next. The passes where a man who knew the mountains better than any Warden alive might run. The route, half-guessed and wholly certain, that the contamination's source had taken into the deep Thornfell.

The Synod believed she was hunting him. Let them. She was hunting too — but she had finally understood, sitting in the cold with a dead man's letters, what it was she had been hunting all along, and it had never been a smuggler with a stolen god in his chest.

It was the truth. And she intended to reach it before the iron did.

She rolled the map, sealed her cell, and went down to the stables in the dark, and by the time the Synod's bells rang for matins, Sister Halvard of the Ashen Wardens was three miles into the Thornfell passes, riding toward a man the whole machinery of her church was coming to cut open — carrying against her ribs the only weapon that had ever frightened the Seat.

A letter. And a truth, and a good hiding place, and prayer.
