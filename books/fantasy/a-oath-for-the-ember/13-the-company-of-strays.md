## 13. The Company of Strays

The valley had no name on any map, which was precisely why Vael had chosen it.

She had found it years ago, in another life, when she still carried Korrath's sigil burned into her shoulder and called her scars a uniform. A fold in the Thornfells where the peaks leaned together like conspirators, hiding a bowl of green from every road and ridge-line. The old maps called it nothing. The shepherds who grazed its upper meadows called it *the Hollow* and asked no questions of anyone who came down into it, because shepherds who asked questions did not stay shepherds long.

Now it held forty-one souls, a herd of scrawny goats, three cookfires that smoked only at dusk, and the first thing Vael had ever built that was not a weapon.

She stood at the mouth of the Hollow at first light, as she did every morning, watching the mist burn off the terraces. Below her, the fugitives were waking. She had learned all their names by heart — names were the first thing she offered anyone who came down the goat-track with hollow eyes and burned papers behind them — but she still thought of them, privately, as what they were: strays.

There was Brother Anselm, defrocked for asking aloud why the Ashen Wardens needed iron if their faith was true, who now kept bees and wept when the hives swarmed, saying it reminded him of processions. There were the twins, Pell and Sorrel, nine years old, god-touched — their eyes went silver when they slept, and they woke speaking words in a language the church had spent three centuries drowning. There was Dagny, a deserter from the tithe-legions who still flinched at the sound of a hammer on an anvil, and Marta of Lowbridge, whose shrine-tending hands had healed a dying child two winters past and earned her a Warden's brand instead of a blessing.

And there was Juniper Voss, asleep under a lean-to near the eastern wall, with his ruined maps spread around him like shed skin.

Vael watched him a long moment. He slept badly. He always slept badly now; she could see it in the way he'd arranged himself, curled around his own chest as though guarding something, one hand pressed flat below his sternum even in dreams. The Ember never let him rest entirely. It warmed him from inside like a banked fire, and some part of him, she thought, had learned to be afraid of warmth.

*He belongs here,* she told herself. *For the first time, he belongs somewhere without being owned.*

She tested the sentence the way she tested a rope before trusting it to hold her weight. It mostly bore.

* * *

The morning passed in the small labors that made up their fragile commonwealth. Vael split wood because splitting wood was the only prayer she trusted anymore — the clean crack of the axe, the smell of sap, the stack growing straight and honest against the wall. Anselm moved among the beehives humming a hymn with the dangerous verses changed to nonsense syllables. The twins chased goats and pretended not to listen when the adults spoke of roads and Warden patrols, and failed utterly at pretending, because god-touched children heard everything twice: once with their ears and once with whatever else lived behind their silver eyes.

Juniper emerged mid-morning, hair wild, looking like a man who had been arguing in his sleep and lost.

"You drew again," Vael said. It wasn't an accusation. It was just the weather report of their days.

He looked down at his ink-stained fingers as if they belonged to someone else. "I didn't mean to."

"I know."

In the night, without waking, he had redrawn the western approach to the Hollow — not on paper but in the world itself, or so it seemed. Dagny had come back from checking the snares swearing blind that the second ravine had shifted, that a shelf of rock now stood where open slope had been, that the path down twisted in a way no path should. Juniper's maps, spread out on the flat stone beside the lean-to, showed the change already inked in a hand steadier than his waking hand had ever been.

"The Ember's building walls around you," Vael said quietly. "Around us. It knows you're safe here and it's—"

"It's *editing*," Juniper said. There was fury in it, but the fury was worn thin, stretched over something sadder. "The mountains are not a draft, Vael. You can't just— move a ravine because a smuggler is frightened." He pressed the heel of his hand against his chest, that unconscious gesture she had catalogued long ago. "Every day I'm less sure which parts of my life I chose."

"You chose this valley," she said. "I led you here, but you chose to stay."

"Did I?" He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, brief and crooked. "Or did the fire decide it wanted a hearth?"

Before she could answer, Sorrel came pelting across the terrace, nine years old and breathless, silver-eyed even awake for once, which meant the twins had seen something.

"There's a woman on the high track," the girl gasped. "Walking wrong. Bleeding. Pell's watching her. She's got—" Sorrel swallowed. "She's got church boots on."

* * *

They brought Halvard in through the goat-track, because the front approaches were watched now — Vael had seen to that after Whitefall Pass, after the blizzard, after the maps that lied. The Warden inquisitor walked between Vael and Dagny with her hands visible and empty, and Vael noted everything with a soldier's cold inventory: the wound in her side, crudely bound; the frost-nip on her cheeks; the absence of a sword; the way she favored her left leg but refused to limp more than the terrain strictly demanded.

Pride, Vael thought. Even bleeding, even hunted by her own church, the woman had pride like a spine of iron. Vael knew that spine. She'd had one herself, once, before she learned what it cost to stand straight for the wrong god.

"She comes armed with paper," Dagny muttered. "Nothing else."

"That's worse," said Marta of Lowbridge, and spat.

They laid Halvard by the central fire, and Anselm — defrocked, branded, exiled, and still unable to stop being a priest — cleaned her wound with honey-spirit and linen while she sat perfectly still and let him, which told Vael more about her condition than any groan would have.

"Why are you here?" Vael asked, when the wound was closed. Around them the strays had gathered in a loose ring, drawn by the gravity of a Warden in their midst the way iron filings answer a lodestone. Fear moved through the crowd like wind through barley. "You tracked us through three passes. Your legion burned the shrine at Coldmere. Why walk into our hands now?"

Halvard looked up. Her eyes were gray and very tired and very clear.

"Because I read a letter," she said. "Because I spent twenty years hunting god-fragments for men who knew exactly what Juniper Voss was, and hid him anyway, and burned villages to keep the secret warm." She reached into her coat — slowly, telegraphing every motion — and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, stained dark along one edge with her own blood. "And because the church has stopped collecting tithes and started collecting kindling. They're coming here. Not a patrol. The Fourth Legion, with fire-priests. They mean to burn the Hollow and everyone in it, and cut the Ember out of him alive on the altar ash."

The ring of strays went silent in the particular way silence falls on people who have already run once and cannot bear to run again.

Juniper stood at the edge of the firelight. Vael saw his face do several things at once — fear, yes, and a bleak confirmation of something he'd already suspected — and then settle into a stillness she had learned, in the weeks since Whitefall, to read as decision forming.

"How long?" he asked.

"Twelve days," Halvard said. "Perhaps ten, if the passes clear early."

* * *

That evening the company met in the long barn, by lamplight, because secrets kept in daylight had a way of sprouting wings. Vael stood at the door and counted them in: forty-one, plus a Warden who sat apart with her wound pulled tight and her hands folded like a woman at her own trial.

Anselm spoke first, voice shaking. "We should scatter. Small groups, different roads. It worked before—"

"It worked before because there were twelve of us," Dagny said flatly. "There are forty-one now, and half can't walk far. And the roads are watched. The Wardens have learned how we move."

"Then we surrender the Ember-bearer," said a voice from the back — Corvin, a gaunt former tithe-collector whom Vael tolerated because turning away a penitent was a door she refused to open. "Give them what they want and they leave the rest of us. It's arithmetic."

"No," said Marta of Lowbridge, simply.

"It's *arithmetic*, Marta—"

"He healed my daughter's fever by sitting with her," Marta said. "He didn't know he was doing it. He just sat with her because she was frightened, and the fever broke. You want to hand that man to the fire-priests so your arithmetic balances?" She looked around the barn. "Then count me out of your sums."

Vael felt it then, moving through the lamplight — not agreement, not yet, but the shape of it, the way a crowd stops being a crowd and starts being a company. She had stood in war-councils where that same shape formed, and she knew how rare it was and how easily broken.

Juniper rose.

He looked smaller than he had at Whitefall Pass, thinner, the Ember burning visibly now beneath his skin on bad nights, a faint gold threading his wrists like veins of ore. But when he spoke, the barn went quiet the way it goes quiet in temples.

"I've been running since the day the pilgrim died in my arms," he said. "I ran from the Wardens, and from Korrath, and from my own maps, and from the truth in my own chest. And everywhere I ran, I left people behind me to pay the toll." His voice caught, steadied. "I charged tolls in secrets my whole life. I think it's time I paid one of my own. Here is mine: I am going to the First Temple. I am going to give the Ember back. And I would rather walk that road with you than without you — but I will not ask any of you to die for a mapmaker's penance."

Silence. Then Pell, the twin, said in his strange doubled voice — the one that always sounded like two children and something older underneath:

"We'll carry your stories if you drop them."

And Sorrel took her brother's hand, and the silver faded slowly from their eyes, and Vael understood that the vote had been taken, though no hands had been raised.

* * *

It was deep in the night, when the fires had burned to coals and the sentries walked the rim, that Vael heard the voice.

She was alone at the eastern wall, doing what she did every night: standing where the mountain shadow ended, listening. Listening for iron on stone, for dogs, for the small betrayals of an approaching army. Instead she heard it inside her skull, familiar as an old scar aching before rain.

***Daughter.***

Her whole body went rigid. Twenty years of discipline kept her from answering aloud, but Korrath had never needed her lips.

***You crouch in a shepherd's hole with cowards and broken priests, and you call it a company. I offer you legions. I offer you a throne of enemy banners. I offer you HIM — protected, honored, brother to a god — and you choose goats.***

"We choose each other," Vael breathed. "That's the difference between us and you. That's the whole difference."

***The other gods sleep, and the church grows fat on their silence. Only war wakes them. Only blood unbinds. You know this in your bones, daughter — you were forged for it. Come home. Bring me the vessel, and I will never ask anything of you again.***

*Liar,* she thought, with a clarity that hurt like cold water. *You will ask everything. You will ask until there is nothing left to take.* She had given Korrath her body, her scars, her twenties, her capacity for mercy, and he had called it devotion right up until the day she'd seen him spend a village of faithful like ammunition and call their deaths a hymn.

"I'm not your daughter," she said aloud, to the dark, to the god, to the girl she had been. "I never was. I was your *weapon*. Weapons get put down."

The presence withdrew the way a storm withdraws — not gone, only gathering elsewhere. But as it faded, Vael felt it turn, briefly, toward the lean-to where Juniper slept, and what she felt in its attention was not love, not need, not even hunger.

It was appetite.

She stood a long time in the cold, and then she went and woke Juniper, because some things should not be carried alone.

"He spoke to you too," he said, when she told him. It wasn't a question. He was sitting up already, hand pressed to his sternum, and in the darkness Vael could see the faint gold pulse beneath his fingers, quickening like a hearth stirred. "He's been whispering for days. At the edges. Saying the same thing." Juniper looked up at her, and his eyes in the dark were steady and terribly sad. "Vael — he isn't trying to recruit me anymore. He's counting the days until the church does his work for him. If they cut the Ember out on their altar, in their city, surrounded by their faithful—"

"—he takes it through their pain," Vael finished. "Their fear. Their blood. A war-god doesn't need to win the Ember. He needs someone else to spill enough blood around it."

The wind moved through the Hollow, and somewhere down the terraces a dog began to bark, and did not stop.

Vael was moving before the sound fully registered, knife out, calling the sentries — and above the eastern rim, against the stars, she saw them: not the iron legion, not yet, not the slow army Halvard had promised.

Faster. Closer. Torches, dozens of them, descending the high track with the terrible disciplined joy of the converted, and borne ahead of them on a pole, a banner she knew better than her own name: the red circle of Korrath, open like a mouth.

His faithful had found the Hollow. The storm had arrived four days early — and it was not the church's storm at all.
