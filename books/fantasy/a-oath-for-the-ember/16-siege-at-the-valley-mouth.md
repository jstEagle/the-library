## 16. Siege at the Valley Mouth

The valley mouth opened before them like a wound in the world — a narrow throat of grey stone where the river of fugitives would have to pass between two walls of iron. Sister Halvard saw it first from the ridge above, and what she saw made her blood run cold in a way that no heresy ever had.

Banners. Hundreds of them, black and ash-grey, snapping in the wind that funneled down from Whitefall Pass. The Fourth Iron Legion had come at last, drawn up in ranks across the only road south, their shields overlapping like scales on some enormous serpent, their pikes angled skyward against a threat that had not yet shown itself. Behind them, supply wagons. Behind those, the pyre-crews with their barrels of oil and their long-handled brands, waiting for orders that everyone knew were already written.

Halvard stood at the ridge's edge with the archivist's letter folded against her chest, pressed there by a strap of oiled leather she had sewn herself in the days after she left the Abbey of Saint Corvane. She had walked eleven days to reach this place. She had crossed frozen rivers, slept in shrines whose gods she no longer feared to name, eaten snow when bread ran out. And now, looking down at the legion she had once been proud to serve, she understood that every step of that journey had been leading here — to this hour, this valley, this choice.

Below, strung along the last mile of open ground, came the company of strays. Defrocked priests walking beside god-touched children. Deserters from three different garrisons. Old Vael at the front with her scarred face and her spear held low, trying to look like a woman who commanded rather than a woman who fled. And somewhere among them, unremarkable except to those who knew, Juniper Voss — the cartographer, the vessel, the man who carried a god's heart in his chest without ever having asked for it.

Halvard counted the legion's strength and then counted the company's, and the arithmetic was obscene. Four hundred souls against four thousand. Pikes against pilgrims' staffs. It would not be a battle. It would be a harvest.

She thought about turning back. About finding another road, another life, another way to carry the letter that would never be quite enough. But the letter was not hers to keep. It belonged to a dead man named Aldric Vane, Archivist of the Third Rank, who had spent his final years hiding god-fragments in foundlings out of mercy while his superiors built an empire on their imprisonment. He had written the truth in his own hand, sealed it in wax, and trusted it to time. Time had given it to her. And time, she understood now, was running out for everyone standing in that valley.

* * *

She went down alone.

Not because she was brave — though the others would tell it that way afterward, and perhaps she let them. She went down alone because she could think of no other way. An armed company approaching the legion's line would be met with steel. A single unarmed sister in full Warden's habit might, just might, be met with hesitation. And hesitation was all she needed. Hesitation was a door, and doors could be widened.

Vael argued with her for ten full minutes at the valley's edge. The war-god's faithful did not believe in surrendering anything, least of all initiative. "You'll die," Vael said flatly, gripping Halvard's shoulder. "They'll burn you where you stand and call it cleansing."

"Probably," Halvard said. "But they'll listen first. They'll have to. A Warden reading aloud is not a thing a soldier can ignore — he'll want to know what I'm saying before he decides whether I'm a traitor or a madwoman, and in that wanting, there's a crack." She touched the letter. "And cracks are where light gets in. I've seen it happen. I *was* it happening."

Juniper said nothing during the argument. He stood apart, hands clasped around a walking staff, watching the legion's banners with the particular stillness of a man who has already decided how he will spend himself if spending is required. When Halvard finally turned to go, he was the one who walked her the first hundred yards, saying nothing, matching her pace, until the ground sloped away toward the enemy lines and he stopped and simply looked at her — a look she would remember for the rest of her life, however much or little of that life remained.

"You don't have to do this," he said quietly.

"I know," she answered. "That's why I can."

* * *

The morning fog still clung to the valley floor when she stepped into the space between the lines. It parted around her like water around a prow, and suddenly she was visible to both armies at once: a lone figure in ash-grey wool, silver Warden's insignia polished bright, carrying nothing but a folded letter and a voice.

The legion's front rank bristled. Pikes dropped from vertical to ready. Somewhere behind the third rank, an officer's voice barked a challenge — *identify yourself* — and Halvard lifted her chin and called back in the formal cadence every soldier of the church learned before he learned to march:

"Sister Halvard of the Order of Ashen Wardens, formerly Inquisitor Second Rank, currently under sentence of excommunication. I come unarmed. I come to read."

The word *read* traveled down the ranks like a spark down a fuse. Men craned their necks. Officers murmured. A reading was a rite; it demanded witnesses; witnesses demanded judgment; and judgment, in the church's own law, required that the words be heard entire before any hand moved against the reader. Halvard had spent fifteen years exploiting the letter of canon law against heretics. Now she stood inside its shelter herself, and it held.

She walked the length of the no-man's-land slowly, deliberately, letting them see her, letting them wonder. At the center point — equidistant from the legion's shields and the strays' ragged line — she stopped, unwrapped the leather strap, broke the ancient seal of grey wax, and unfolded pages that had yellowed past cream into the color of old bone.

Then she began to read.

* * *

"To whoever finds this, and has the courage to finish it:

"My name is Aldric Vane. I served the Church of the Sealed Flame for forty-one years, and I write this knowing it will cost me whatever remains of my soul's comfort, because silence has already cost me more.

"You have been told, all of you, that the old gods were bound for your protection. That their power was too wild, too dangerous, too hungry to be permitted among mortals. You have been told this by men in high offices, and you believed them because believing was easier than doubting, and because the men seemed sincere, and sincerity is a terrible argument.

"I was there when the binding was drafted. I read the original deliberations, the ones that never left the archive vaults. And I am telling you now, in words that will survive my death: the gods were not bound for humanity's safety. They were bound for the church's power. Every miracle the old gods performed freely — healing, hearth-fire, safe passage, answered prayer — became, after the binding, a commodity. Something to be sold. Something to be withheld. Something to be granted only through the church's intercession, at the church's price. We did not cage wolves to protect the sheep. We caged the shepherds so that we alone could sell wool."

Halvard paused there, as the letter instructed its reader to pause, and looked up.

What she saw made her throat tighten. The front rank had not moved. But faces had changed. She could see it even at forty yards — the small muscular shifts of men listening harder than they wanted to, eyes flicking sideways toward comrades, jaw muscles working around thoughts that had no permission to exist. These were soldiers of the Fourth Legion. Many of them had served twenty years. Some had burned villages. All of them had knelt.

And lately — she knew this, the whole valley knew it, the whole country knew it — lately, some of their prayers had been answered.

A fevered child waking cool. A lost patrol finding its road. A dying mother lasting one more day, one more day, one more day, long enough to say goodbye. Small things. Impossible things. Things the church's own doctrine insisted could no longer happen, because the gods were sealed and the sealing was absolute. And yet they happened, more each season, tracing a line across the mountains like heat rising from a hidden fire — and every soldier in that legion had heard the stories, and half of them had whispered a prayer of their own in the dark watches of the night, and some of those whispers had been answered too.

They had been told the Ember was a contamination. A plague of false grace. They had been ordered to burn it out of the world.

Now a sister of their own order was telling them, in a dead archivist's voice, that the fire was real, and holy, and theirs by right older than any throne.

* * *

She read on.

"The hiding of the fragments was my work, and I will answer for it gladly. When the order came down to destroy the last of the god-hearts — to burn them, grind them, scatter them beyond recovery — I could not do it. Not because I doubted the church's doctrine; doubt came later, and slowly, and painfully. I could not do it because I had seen what the fragments were. Not weapons. Not contagions. *Hearths.* Each one a small surviving piece of the god who taught mortals to keep fire alive for one another, back before there were churches, back when keeping a fire lit for a stranger was the whole of religion.

"So I hid them. In vessels that could carry them unnoticed. In foundlings, mostly — children with no families to ask questions, no inheritances to complicate, no one to mourn them if the church came hunting. I told myself it was strategy. It was not. It was mercy, dressed in strategy's clothes, because I could not bear to be the man who put out the last fires in the world.

"One of those children was an infant left at the monastery gate at Thornfell, wrapped in a blanket stitched with mountain-flowers. I placed a fragment within him — not implanted, not imprisoned, *entrusted* — and I gave the monks a lie to raise him on, and I prayed, in defiance of my own office, that he would live long and never learn what he carried, unless the day came when learning it was the only thing left that could save us all.

"If you are reading this, that day has come. And if you are reading this aloud, in front of armed men, then you are braver than I ever was, and I ask you to tell them the rest."

Halvard's voice cracked on the last line. She steadied it. She kept reading.

* * *

By the fourth page, the legion's discipline had begun to fray in ways no officer could stitch shut.

It started small. A sergeant in the second rank shifted his weight and did not shift it back. A young pikeman lowered his weapon a hand's width, then another, as though the wood had grown heavy in his grip. Two soldiers near the left flank turned to each other and spoke — actually spoke, mouths moving, in violation of every silence order — and whatever they said made a third man turn away from the line entirely, facing the mountains, shoulders shaking in a way that might have been cold and was not.

An officer strode down the rank shouting for order. The order came. Slowly. Patchily. Like frost retreating from sun.

And Halvard, reading, felt something she had not felt since childhood: the sensation of words doing physical work. Of language as a lever long enough to move a thing that steel could not. She had spent her career believing that faith was a wall and doubt a chisel, chip by patient chip. She had been wrong. Faith, in these men, was not a wall. It was a rope bridge, and it had been bearing weight it was never built for — the weight of burned villages, of unanswered prayers, of orders that smelled of oil and ash — and all her words had to do was show them the frayed strands they had already half-seen.

On the fifth page, the letter named names.

"The signatories of the binding decree included His Eminence Cardinal Osseric, who took for himself the tithe-rights over every hearth-blessing in the southern provinces, and whose personal accounts I have seen. The church did not seal the gods and grieve. The church sealed the gods and *profited*, and the profit bought the palaces you have marched past, and the legions you serve in, and the very pikes in your hands. You are not protecting the faith. You are guarding a lock that guards a treasury, and the treasury was filled with stolen fire."

A murmur ran the length of the legion like wind through wheat. Not loud. Not yet mutiny. But unmistakable — the sound of four thousand men discovering, all at once, that they had been lied to by the institution they had given their lives to, and that the proof was being read to them by one of their own.

The legion commander — Halvard could see him now, a broad man on a grey horse behind the third rank, Legate Corvus Dain, whom she had dined with twice in another life — sent a rider forward. The rider halted ten paces from her and delivered the message formally: cease reading, return to your lines, or be taken.

Halvard looked at the rider. Looked past him at the legate. And then, in a clear voice pitched to carry, she said:

"I will finish the letter. Under the church's own law, a testimony interrupted is a testimony void, and I will not make void the last words of a better man than any of us. If you take me, take me after the final page. Let the record show that the Fourth Legion heard the truth entire and chose what it chose."

The rider hesitated. Turned back. And the legate — she watched this closely, hungrily — the legate did not give the order to advance.

He gave the order to *wait.*

* * *

Waiting was the crack widening.

For twenty minutes, while she read the sixth and seventh pages — the account of the burned shrine at Marrow's Ford, the testimony of the monk who had watched Wardens torch a nursery of god-touched children, the archivist's final plea — the legion stood in the cold and did nothing, and the nothing itself became a kind of decision. Men talk when they wait. They talked. She could see the conversations blooming rank to rank, heads bent together, hands gesturing, and she could imagine the content of every one of them, because she had had those conversations with herself for months: *Is it true? Did we burn them for a lie? What did I kneel to? What do I kneel to now?*

Some men hardened. That was true too, and she did not pretend otherwise. There were true believers in the Fourth Legion, officers whose whole identity was the Seal and the Sword, men who would burn the letter and her with it if their legate gave the word. She counted them as she read — maybe a third of the force, concentrated in the veteran cohorts, clustered around the standard. They would fight. Perhaps today, perhaps later. Mercy did not disarm everyone; sometimes it only clarified who had never wanted peace.

But the rest —

The rest were farmers' sons and miners' sons and one shepherd's daughter she spotted in the fifth rank, helmet pushed back, face streaked with dirt and tears she wasn't bothering to hide. The rest had mothers. Had hearths. Had, in the last two years, at least one prayer whispered toward a fire that had somehow, impossibly, been heard.

When Halvard reached the final page, her voice had gone raw, and she read the closing lines almost in a whisper, and the whisper forced four thousand men to lean in to hear, and there is no posture more vulnerable, or more honest, than leaning in.

"I do not ask you to rebel. Rebellion is just another oath, and oaths made in anger break like green wood. I ask only this: when the order comes — and it will come — to burn the vessel, to cut the fire out of the living man who carries it, to make a wasteland of a valley full of people who have done nothing but pray — I ask you to remember that you have a choice. You have always had a choice. The church's greatest fear was never the old gods. It was the moment you remembered that.

"Keep a fire lit tonight. For someone. For anyone. That is the whole of the old faith, and it requires no permission, and it cannot be taxed, and it cannot be sealed.

"— Aldric Vane, Archivist of the Third Rank, who kept his fire lit to the end."

She folded the letter. Held it up so both lines could see it. And then she did the thing she had planned in the eleven days of walking, the thing she had rehearsed against every tree and every stone between the abbey ruins and this valley: she set it down on a flat rock at the center of the no-man's-land, weighted it with a stone, and stepped away from it.

"Read it yourselves," she called. "Pass it hand to hand. Truth that must be guarded by one person dies with that person. Truth set loose belongs to everyone."

* * *

What happened next took less time than the reading and changed more.

A pikeman from the second rank — Halvard would learn his name later, Tomas Rehl, nineteen years old, a baker's son from Lowmark — stepped out of formation. Walked to the rock. Took the letter. Read the first page again, slowly, lips moving. Then passed it backward over his shoulder to the man behind him, who read faster, and passed it on, and so it went, hand to hand down the length of the legion like a coal passed down a bucket-line, each pair of hands warming on it briefly before passing the warmth along.

And as it traveled, the legion began to dissolve. Not in panic — in *decision*. Whole sections shouldered arms and simply stood aside, opening lanes in their own formation, declaring with their bodies what they would not say aloud: *not us, not this, not for a lie.* The left flank went first, then the supply-guard companies, then nearly half the fresh recruits, who had the least to lose and the newest memories of answered prayers. By the time the letter completed its circuit and returned, worn soft at the folds, to the rock at the center, the Fourth Iron Legion was no longer a wall. It was two walls with a street between them, and the street faced the valley, and the strays were already walking up it.

Legate Dain made one attempt to halt it — a curt order to close ranks, backed by his veteran cohort leveling pikes at their own comrades. It nearly sparked the fratricide everyone dreaded. But a centurion named Ossa, twenty-two years of service, lowered her blade with deliberate ceremony and said, loudly enough for three ranks to hear: "I took an oath to guard the faith. I am looking at it right now, and it is not behind me." And the moment crystallized, the way moments do, and half the veterans followed her, and the legate, seeing his command dissolve into arithmetic he could no longer deny, sounded the withdrawal for those who would follow him east, and did not pursue.

Half the legion stood down. Half walked away. And the half that remained — some two thousand soldiers, disarmed of certainty if not of weapons — looked toward the ragged company of strays coming up the valley road, and toward the lone sister standing in the mud between the lines, and waited to see what would be asked of them.

Nothing was asked. That was Juniper's doing, Halvard learned afterward. He had come forward with Vael when the lanes opened, and he had looked at the two thousand men who stayed, and he had said only: "Go home if you can. Keep fires lit. Tell people what you heard." No banners. No recruiting. No new oath to replace the broken one. Just the archivist's own request, handed onward like the letter itself.

* * *

Halvard stood in the emptying no-man's-land as the afternoon light went long and gold, and she felt the strange grief that comes after a victory won without blood — the grief of realizing how close it had been, how easily the legate's nerve might have held, how many valleys like this one would get no letter, no reader, no reprieve. She picked up the worn pages. They were warm. Not metaphorically — she could feel it, faint and impossible, the same warmth the dying pilgrim had felt years ago at a hearth that should have been cold, the warmth that had started everything. The letter had passed through two thousand hands in an hour, and somewhere in that passage it had caught something, or given something, and now it held heat like a stone from a fire.

Behind her, up the valley, the company of strays was preparing to move again. There was no celebrating. Everyone understood that the legion's dissolution was a delay, not a deliverance — that the church would send more, angrier, led by men whose faith had no hairline fractures. And everyone understood, too, that the road ahead had narrowed to a single direction. South, through the unmapped country, to the First Temple. To the hearth where the gods had been bound. To the place where Juniper Voss meant to give back what burned.

Halvard rejoined them at dusk. Vael found her first, and for once the scarred woman did not argue or strategize — she simply put a hand on Halvard's shoulder and left it there, and the touch said everything the war-god's faithful rarely said in words.

Juniper was last. He stood at the edge of the campfire — an actual fire, ordinary and mortal, built from wet scrub by soldiers and fugitives together — and he looked at Halvard with the expression of a man trying to thank someone for something larger than thanks.

"You gave them the choice," he said.

"Aldric gave them the choice," she answered. "I only read it aloud."

"No." He shook his head, and the firelight caught the exhaustion in his face, and beneath the exhaustion something else — the calm of a man who has stopped measuring the distance to his own ending. "Letters sit in archives for centuries. Someone has to walk them into the teeth of the thing. You did that. Whatever happens at the temple — whatever happens to me — the church will never be able to seal this again. Too many hands have held it. Too many fires are lit."

He was right, and she knew it, and it was the first time in her life that being right had felt like standing on a precipice. Because if the truth could not be resealed, then the church's next move would not be persuasion. It would be annihilation. Burn the valley. Burn the company. Burn the vessel, cut the Ember out, salt the earth behind them. Somewhere south of here, in the capital, orders were already being drafted by men who had heard about the valley mouth and drawn the only conclusion their power could permit.

And there was worse news coming down the mountain with the night wind — news that made even the day's miracle feel like a held breath rather than a victory. From the west, from the ruined approaches to the First Temple, scouts were reporting fires that burned without fuel, and a war-horn sounding at intervals no human lungs could sustain, and a host gathering under a banner no living army had flown in three hundred years.

Korrath had not waited for an oath. Denied Juniper's fealty, the war-god had begun taking what he wanted directly — pouring himself into his faithful, igniting the old hunger, marching on the temple not to free the gods but to seize the heart that had come home to them.

Two hosts now converged on the First Temple. One carried iron and a lie. The other carried fire and a hunger. And between them, moving south with all the speed their broken bodies could manage, walked a company of strays escorting a cartographer who was losing a memory with every mile, toward a hearth where he intended to give away the last of himself.

Halvard folded the archivist's letter one final time and tucked it back against her chest, where it had lived through rivers and ridgelines and the unraveling of everything she had believed.

"We move at first light," Vael announced to the camp, and no one argued, and no one slept well, and the fires they kept — every single one, all down the valley, fugitive and deserter and defrocked priest alike — they kept lit until dawn, for someone, for anyone, the whole of the old faith practiced in the dark by people who finally understood what it had always been for.
