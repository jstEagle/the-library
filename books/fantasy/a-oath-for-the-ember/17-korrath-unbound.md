## 17. Korrath Unbound

Vael had served the war-god for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once heard him laugh.

She heard it now.

It came up out of the ground itself, out of the standing stones where his faithful had gathered, out of the iron pendants at every throat and the scar-tissue of every old vow — a sound like a blade being drawn very slowly from a very long sheath, and beneath it, threaded through it, something almost like joy. It rolled down the hidden valley at dawn while the company was breaking camp, while Halvard was cinching her pack, while Juniper stood at the valley's mouth looking east toward the First Temple with an expression Vael had learned to read the way sailors read weather.

Juniper went white. His hand came up to his chest, flat against the sternum, as if holding a door shut.

"He's awake," the cartographer said. "He's *awake*."

"No," Vael said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Awake is what he was last night. This is something else."

She knew what it was. She had known since the whispering started three nights ago, when Korrath's voice had begun reaching into the dreams of everyone who had ever sworn to him, soft as ashfall: *Bring me the vessel. Bring me my brother's heart. Do this and I will never ask anything of you again.* She had told herself it was desperation. A god on the edge of irrelevance, grasping. She had not let herself consider the other possibility — that this was not grasping at all, but patience finally paying its dividend.

The laugh rolled again, closer now, and with it came the smell.

Every soldier knows the smell of a battlefield before there is a battlefield. It is a smell of the future: hot metal, churned earth, the copper ghost of blood not yet spilled. It came down the valley on a wind that should not have existed, because the morning was still, because the peaks held the air like cupped hands, and yet the wind moved, and it carried that smell, and Vael's body understood before her mind did. Her knees wanted to bend. Her hands wanted a weapon. Nineteen years of devotion lived in her muscles, and those muscles said: *your god is coming, make ready.*

"Form up," she heard herself say. "Shields if you have them, blades if you don't, and anyone who can run — be ready to run."

"We're supposed to reach the temple by tomorrow night," Halvard said. She had her letter out, half-unfolded, as though the archivist's words might be a shield too. "If we scatter—"

"We are not scattering." Vael looked down the valley, to where the light was wrong. That was the only way she could describe it, even to herself. The dawn light came from the east, as it always did, but a second light was rising in the west, behind them, deep red, the color of a forge seen through a keyhole, and it was getting brighter. "He's coming to us."

* * *

They came out of the western pass an hour after sunrise, and they were not an army, and that was the worst part.

An army could be counted. An army could be flanked, starved, bought. What came over the ridge was a congregation. Farmers with scythes. Miners with picks still crusted from the shafts. Old men and young women and children — children, Vael saw, children with iron suns nailed to little wooden boards, marching behind their mothers with the solemn faces of acolytes. There were perhaps four hundred of them, and they walked in no formation whatsoever, and they were singing.

Vael knew the song. She had sung it herself, kneeling in the war-god's hilltop shrine at fourteen, her hand pressed to a blade's edge until the blood made the oath real. *Korrath who breaks the chains, Korrath who wakes the sleepers, Korrath whose hammer falls on the doors of tyrants.* It was a good song. It had been written, so the priests said, three hundred years ago, when the church sealed the gods away and Korrath alone refused to go quietly into his prison of stone. He had raged against the binding. He had promised his faithful he would return. Every verse ended with the same line: *and on the day of waking, all debts are paid.*

The congregation sang it as they descended, and their voices were ordinary voices, cracked and off-key and human, and that was what made Vael's stomach turn. These were not fanatics flown in from some distant zealot stronghold. These were the people of the valleys. People who had prayed lately and been answered — small answers, kind ones, a fever broken, a lost child found, a debt forgiven by a creditor who woke weeping and couldn't say why. The Ember's warmth leaking out through Juniper had touched them all. And Korrath had gathered that warmth, that gratitude, that sudden hungry hope, and he had poured himself into it like water into cracks.

He wasn't commanding them. He didn't need to. They had come *willingly*, and they would stand between him and anything he wanted, and most of them would die smiling.

"They're not soldiers," Halvard said beside her. The ex-Warden's face was gray. "Half of them can't even hold a blade."

"They don't need to." Vael's mouth tasted of iron. "They're not here to fight. They're here to be fought *near*. You think the Wardens will spare a village that raised these people? You think the legion will see farmers and put up their swords?" She shook her head slowly. "This is how he's always fought. Not battles. *Atrocities.* He makes the other side slaughter innocents, and then he stands in the smoke and says: look what they do to you. Look why you need me."

Down in the valley, the song reached its final verse, and four hundred throats sang *all debts are paid*, and the red light behind the western ridge pulsed once, like a heartbeat.

* * *

It happened at midday, at the valley mouth, where the road narrowed between two shoulders of stone and the company had hoped to slip past the congregation and run for the temple road.

Korrath did not walk out of the west. He *rose*.

Later, Vael would try to describe it to herself and fail, because language kept insisting on shapes and what she saw had none. He came up through his faithful the way fire comes up through a house — first a glow under a door, then a licking at the eaves, then everywhere at once. He used them. That was the thing she could never forgive, later, when she finally had a *later* to stand in: he used his own worshippers as fuel. She watched a farmer in a patched coat stiffen, arch, and shine, his eyes filling with molten gold, his mouth opening to speak words in a voice four centuries dead — and then another took his place, and another, the god passing through his congregation like a wave through water, each one left gasping and hollowed and *grateful*, gods help them, grateful to have been ridden.

And then he had enough of them, and he stood.

He stood forty feet tall at the head of the valley, a figure of black iron and ember-light, wearing the shape the old carvings gave him: the broad shoulders, the broken crown, the hammer across his back that was less a weapon than a horizon. But the carvings had shown a god defying chains, and this — this was different. The chains were gone. In their place, around his wrists and throat, were rings of gold light, and Vael understood with a lurch that they were not restraints.

They were *harvested oaths*. Every vow ever sworn to him, worn as jewelry.

"*CHILDREN OF THE HAMMER.*" His voice did not come from him so much as arrive, fully formed, inside every skull in the valley. "*I HAVE WAITED THREE HUNDRED YEARS IN THE DARK, AND I FIND YOU STILL FAITHFUL. STILL MINE. TODAY THE CHURCH THAT CHAINED US BURNS, AND MY BROTHERS AND SISTERS WAKE, AND THE WORLD REMEMBERS WHAT IT WAS TO BE LOVED BY ITS GODS.*"

A cheer went up from the congregation. It was a real cheer. That was the horror of it.

Beside Vael, Juniper made a small sound. She turned and saw him swaying, one hand fisted in his own shirt over his heart, sweat standing on his forehead despite the cold. The Ember was answering. Of course it was. The heart of the hearth-god recognized its kin, even kin like this.

"It's pulling at me," Juniper whispered. "Like it wants — I don't know. Like a dog hearing a whistle."

"Don't answer it."

"I'm not trying to." He laughed, high and thin. "Vael, I've never *tried* to do anything it does. That's rather the whole problem."

"*AND YOU,*" Korrath said, and the great iron head turned, and the ember-eyes found Juniper across half a mile of stony ground as easily as a man finds a candle in a dark room. The god's voice gentled, and somehow that was worse than the thunder. "*LITTLE VESSEL. CARRIER OF MY BROTHER'S HEART. I DO NOT COME FOR YOU IN ANGER. COME DOWN FROM THAT RIDGE. GIVE ME WHAT SLEEPS IN YOU, AND I WILL BUILD YOU A SHRINE AT THE CENTER OF THE NEW WORLD. NO ONE WILL EVER HUNT YOU AGAIN. NO ONE WILL EVER USE YOU AGAIN. YOU WILL BE FREE, AND HONORED, AND NEVER ALONE.*"

*Never alone.*

Vael saw it land. She saw it strike Juniper exactly where Korrath had aimed, in the soft place below the ribs where every lonely year lived, and she saw him take one step forward before he caught himself, one step, his boot scraping stone, his face full of a longing so naked it hurt to witness.

She grabbed his arm.

"Listen to me." She pulled him close, her mouth near his ear, her voice low and rough. "Nineteen years I served him. Nineteen years, and I want you to understand what I'm about to tell you, because it cost me everything I had to learn it." Her throat closed. She forced the words through. "He has never once, in all the songs and all the scriptures, in all the years I knelt to him — he has never once asked what *we* wanted. Only what we could give. Freedom is just the word he uses for a world where everyone owes him."

Juniper looked at her. Behind him, Halvard had stepped forward to the ridgeline's edge, the archivist's letter held high in both hands, and was shouting something about mercy, about evidence, about the truth — and the congregation wavered, some of them turning to listen, because Halvard had a voice built for barracks squares and it carried —

And Korrath, seeing his moment fraying, stopped pretending.

* * *

What he did next, Vael would carry to her grave.

He reached into his faithful — not the gentle possession of before, not the wave-through-water, but a *taking* — and he pulled. Vael felt it even at a distance, felt it in the old scar on her palm where her oath-blood had dried, a hook set behind her navel hauling toward the god. And the congregation — the farmers, the miners, the mothers and the children with their little wooden icons — began to burn with borrowed light. Their prayers, their devotion, three hundred years of accumulated faith, all of it siphoned upward into that towering shape, and Korrath grew, and brightened, and laughed his blade-drawn laugh, and raised the hammer.

Not at the company. At the eastern sky. At the far mountains, where the First Temple slept, where the other bound gods lay in their prisons of stone.

"*BROTHER HEARTH,*" he roared, and now there was nothing gentle anywhere in him, nothing of the shepherd, only appetite wearing a crown, "*YOU HID YOURSELF IN A MORTAL AND LET THEM FIGHT OVER YOU LIKE THIEVES OVER A PURSE. SO I WILL TAKE YOU THE WAY STORMS TAKE VILLAGES. I WILL CRACK THIS VALLEY OPEN. I WILL DRAG YOUR SPARK OUT OF YOUR LITTLE VESSEL WHATEVER IT COSTS THESE FOOLS WHO LOVE ME — THEY ARE MINE, AND I HAVE ALWAYS SPENT WHAT IS MINE —*"

And Vael, watching the hammer rise, watching the light gather in the congregation like a tide going out of a hundred living bodies, watching a small girl at the front of the crowd begin to shine from the inside the way the farmer had shone —

Vael understood.

It arrived whole, the way the worst truths do, without stages or mercies. *He never wanted freedom.* All the songs, all the chained-god iconography, all the righteous rage against the church — she had read them as a story about liberation, because she had needed it to be. But freedom was only the door. Dominion was the room behind it. He had spent three centuries in the dark not grieving his lost worship but *counting it*, hoarding every oath, nursing every grievance into a weapon, and now that the door stood open he was not walking through it into a free world. He was burning the doorway wider so that nothing in the world could ever stand outside his shadow again. The gods who would wake because of him would wake into a world already conquered. The mortals who loved him were fuel. She had been fuel. Nineteen years of fuel, and she had called it devotion, and the girl at the front of the crowd was shining brighter now, and screaming, and her mother was screaming too, and Korrath's hammer began to fall.

Vael did not decide. Later she would search her memory for the decision and find none, only motion, only the absolute certainty of a body doing the one thing it had been made for — except that the thing it had been made for, the thing she had trained for and bled for and prayed for, had been to serve him. And instead:

Instead she drew her blade, turned it flat-side out, and struck the old oath-scar on her palm as hard as she could, and shouted the unbinding — the words every sworn sword learned first and hoped never to use. "*KORRATH, I WITHDRAW. WHAT I GAVE WAS GIVEN; WHAT I KEEP IS KEPT. MY OATH DIES HERE.*"

The scar tore open. Blood ran. The hook behind her navel ripped loose, and the pain dropped her to one knee, and she screamed, and it was the best pain of her life.

And around the valley, one by one, others heard her. Others remembered the words. A deserter. Two miners. A defrocked priest whose voice cracked on the ancient syllables. The unbinding spread through the congregation like the possession had, like fire in reverse, and where it passed, the borrowed light guttered and the people sagged free, and the little girl at the front dimmed from furnace-coal to frightened child, alive, alive, her mother clutching her.

Korrath's hammer fell anyway — but weakened, starved, the blow meant to crack mountains landing as a blow that merely shattered the valley's western wall and sent a river of stone dust boiling down over the congregation. Through the dust, the god's ember-eyes swept the crowd, searching, furious, *betrayed* — and found her.

For one moment, across the ruin and the drifting rock-fume, Vael and her god looked at each other.

She had imagined this moment her whole life — the day of waking, the day of meeting, the day she would kneel before magnificence. She had imagined love. What she saw in the ember-eyes was inventory. He was weighing her, the way a man weighs a tool that has failed, deciding whether it was worth the trouble of repair.

"*VAEL,*" he said, softly, only to her, the way he had spoken to her in dreams for nineteen years. "*I MADE YOU. EVERY STRONG THING IN YOU IS MINE. WHERE WILL YOU GO, LITTLE BLADE, WHEN I AM THE ONLY FIRE LEFT IN THE WORLD?*"

"You won't be," she said. Blood dripped from her opened hand onto the stones. "You never were. You were just the loudest."

His roar shook snow from peaks ten miles distant. And through the settling dust, up the eastern road, past the shattered wall, past the weeping and the cheering and the dying embers of a faith coming apart — the road to the First Temple lay open, and empty, and waiting.

"Go," Vael rasped, dragging herself upright, pointing Juniper east with her bleeding hand. "Run. Don't stop for us. Don't stop for *anything*."

"And you?" Juniper's face was streaked with dust and tears.

"I have nineteen years of sins," Vael said, and turned to face her god, and lifted her blade. "I mean to start paying them back."
