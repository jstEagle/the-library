## 1. The Weight of Small Mercies

There were forty-one ways to record a grief in the Guild of Small Mercies, and Jonah Zhou had catalogued every one of them by his nineteenth winter.

The oldest method—Entry One in the founding ledger, written in a hand like frost on a window—was simply to name it. *Grief, one measure, belonging to Maren of the Salt Quarter.* But grief was slippery stock. It evaporated if you named it too plainly, so the guild had learned over four centuries to write around it: grief as a color, grief as a weight, grief as the sound a door makes when it closes for the last time. Each formulation held the thing differently. Each one leaked a little.

Jonah's job—the job he had given himself at fourteen, when the third-to-last apprentice left and no one stopped him—was to make sure nothing leaked past the point of accounting.

He sat now in the counting room above the guild hall, where the morning light came through windows stained the green of old bottle glass, and worked through the overnight returns. The ledgers were arranged not by date but by weight of the transaction, lightest first, because Master Sefa said that was how a day should be carried: you lifted the feathers before you lifted the stones.

*One borrowed year,* he wrote, *returned slightly worn. Debtor: the widow Ansel. Creditor: the guild, general fund.* A borrowed year always came back worn, the way a coat comes back from lending—still serviceable, but you could feel where another body had lived in it. He noted the wear in the margin, in the small precise script that was the only beautiful thing about him, according to Sefa, who said it the way she said most things: as an accusation.

Below that: *Half a name, returned intact. The bearer found it did not fit after all.* Names were the strangest stock they kept. People came to the guild when a name had grown wrong on them—a name given in grief, or in ambition, or by parents who had imagined a different child—and the guild would hold it in trust until either the person grew into it or the name could be quietly retired. Retired names went into the cellar, into long drawers lined with cedar, where they murmured to themselves in the dark. Jonah checked on them monthly. They sounded, mostly, like people talking in their sleep.

And below that, the entry that made him sit back and press the heels of his hands against his eyes:

*Bottled grief, one measure, vintage uncertain. Shelf seven. Seal weakening.*

Shelf seven again. That was the third seal this season.

* * *

The cellar of the Guild of Small Mercies was older than the guild itself, which was the sort of sentence that made new members laugh and old members go quiet. The guild had not built the cellar; it had found it, the way you find a shell on a beach, already shaped for something living. Its walls were stone that sweated in summer and rang faintly, if you knocked, with a note too low to hear properly—you felt it in your teeth instead.

Jonah took the stairs slowly, lamp in hand, out of respect for what waited below.

The inventory of impossibles occupied eleven rooms carved into the hill beneath Copperlane Street. Room one held time: borrowed years in stoppered jars, each labeled with a borrower's mark and a due date, the liquid inside moving against the direction of the light. Room two held names, cedar-drawered and murmuring. Rooms three through six held the emotional stocks—grief, joy, courage, regret, homesickness—each bottled at the moment of its surrender, each a different color if you knew how to look. Grief was blue-black, like deep water at dusk. Homesickness was the exact gold of lamplight seen through a stranger's window.

Room seven was the room Jonah liked least. It held things the guild had accepted without fully understanding: a key that fit every door except the one it was cut for; a mirror that showed the viewer at age ten; a letter that arrived before it was written, and had done so, patiently, for two hundred years. These were shelved under *Pending Classification*, which was ledger-speak for *we do not know what we have agreed to keep.*

Tonight he went straight past all of them to shelf seven, in the grief room, where the seals were failing.

The bottle sat where the ledger said it sat, and even before he lifted the lamp to it Jonah could see the trouble: the wax seal, once the deep red of arterial blood, had gone pale and cracked, and around the neck of the bottle the air shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers over a road in high summer. Something inside was trying to get out. Or perhaps—it occurred to him, as it often did, in this room especially—something outside was trying to get in.

He set the lamp down and did what he had been trained to do, which was nothing dramatic. He opened the day-ledger to the current page, dipped his pen, and wrote:

*Inspection, shelf seven. Seal integrity failing. Re-sealing required. Assigned: J. Zhou.*

The words settled onto the page with the small finality of all well-made entries, and the shimmer around the bottle dimmed—not gone, but acknowledged. That was the whole secret of the guild, if it could be called a secret, though Sefa would sooner bite her tongue off than call it one: the impossible became merely strange the moment someone wrote it down honestly. Chaos was only order that hadn't been introduced properly yet.

Jonah resealed the bottle with fresh wax and a thread of his own hair twisted through it—personal guarantee, per regulation nine—and stood a moment longer in the blue-black dark of the grief room, listening to the bottles breathe.

Four hundred years of mercies, and the shelves were more than half empty. Not sold. Returned. The world had stopped coming to them.

* * *

Upstairs, the guild hall smelled of tea and lamp oil and the particular dust of books no one had opened in a decade. Three people sat at the long table, and Jonah counted them the way a man counts coins he knows are running out.

Master Sefa occupied the head of the table without appearing to claim it. She was past seventy and built like a dock crane, all angles and rust-colored stubbornness, and she was reading the tariff schedules aloud to no one, because reading them aloud was how she thought. Her voice filled the hall the way weather fills a valley.

At her right hand sat Old Tam, who had been the guild's keeper of acquisitions for fifty years and now, in the guild's diminished state, was also its cook, its gardener, and its sole field agent. He was mending a net that had not been used since before Jonah's birth. Tam mended things the way other men prayed. The net might never catch anything again. That was not the point.

And at the table's far end, wrapped in three shawls despite the fire, sat Wren.

Wren had joined the guild sixty years ago as its youngest member and had simply never aged out of the title, though her hair had gone white as salt and her hands shook unless she pressed them flat. She was the last member who remembered the guild full—forty strong, halls ringing, clients queuing down Copperlane Street in the rain. She remembered the law of invisibility being spoken over her at her oath, the way it was spoken over every member: *Be unseen, be unrecorded, be nowhere when they look for you. What the world does not see, the world cannot burn.*

She was dozing now, her chin on her chest, her flat-pressed hands finally still.

"—and the assessment on stored years rises again," Sefa was saying, "which is the crown's polite way of telling us they know we exist and have decided not to mind, for now." She looked up as Jonah came in, and her eyes did their usual arithmetic: the boy, the lamp, the smell of the grief room on his sleeves. "Seven?"

"Seal failed. I've re-waxed it and logged the guarantee."

"Third this season."

"Third this season," Jonah agreed, and hung his coat on the peg that had held five coats once, then two, and now held his alone.

Sefa studied him a moment longer than the report required. She had been doing that lately—studying him, weighing him, the way you weigh a rope before trusting it with a heavy load. He pretended not to notice. He was good at pretending not to notice; it was practically a guild skill.

"Sit," she said. "Tea's stewed. Drink it anyway."

He sat. The tea was terrible. He drank it anyway.

For a while there was only the sound of Tam's needle through netting, and the fire, and Wren breathing in her sleep with the slow tide-pull rhythm of the very old. Jonah opened the day-ledger across his knees and began entering the evening's inspections, and the familiar work settled over him like a coat that fit.

This was the thing no one understood about a dying institution, he thought. It did not die the way a man dies, all at once, dramatically, with witnesses. It died the way a fire dies: smaller and smaller circles of warmth, everyone huddling closer, tending what remained, until the tenders themselves were most of what was left. And the strange truth—the truth he would not have said aloud, not to any of them—was that some part of him loved it exactly as it was. Four people and a cellar full of the world's surrendered sorrows, and every night a ledger to close, and every line in his own hand. If the guild was going to end, he wanted to be the one who ended it *accurately.*

"Wren had a dream," Sefa said suddenly, quietly enough that it did not wake the woman herself.

Tam's needle paused.

"She dreams every night," Jonah said. "She told me. Mostly she dreams about the harbor."

"Not tonight." Sefa turned her teacup in its saucer, once, the way she turned things when she was deciding how much truth a moment could carry. "Tonight she dreamed of frost. First frost, early, out of season. And she dreamed a knock at our door—" Sefa's eyes came up and fixed on Jonah, and there was something in them he had never seen there before, and it took him a moment to recognize it because it had no business on Sefa's face.

It was fear.

"—and the knock spoke," she said. "In the old tongue. The one from before kingdoms. The one we don't keep in any ledger, because we swore four hundred years ago never to write it down."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the cellar rang its note too low to hear.

"That's a dream, Master Sefa," Jonah said. His pen had stopped moving. He noticed he was holding it too tightly. "Dreams aren't inventory."

"No," Sefa agreed. She looked toward the tall front door of the guild hall, with its iron knocker shaped like a closed hand, and Jonah watched her look at it and saw that she was not really seeing it at all—she was listening. "But the frost is real. Feel the draft under the door. First frost, Jonah. Tonight."

Outside, the wind had changed. It came down off the hills carrying the smell of cold iron and something older underneath it, something like snow that had fallen on a country that no longer existed.

And far down Copperlane Street, so distant it might have been imagination, came the sound of small feet on frost-hardened cobbles, and a voice speaking words no ledger contained—

—and among them, unmistakably, Jonah's true name.
