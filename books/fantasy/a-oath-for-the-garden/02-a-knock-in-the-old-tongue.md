## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The first frost came on a Thursday, which Jonah Zhou considered an insult. Frost belonged to the deep of winter, to the long ledger-months when the guild's business slowed and he could finally rebalance accounts that had gone crooked over autumn. It did not belong to October, and it certainly did not belong arriving unannounced, like everything else that had ever knocked on their door.

He was in the counting room when it happened, as he was most nights, hunched over the great ledger with a stub of candle and a pot of ink gone thick with cold. The guildhouse breathed around him—old timber settling, the cellar breathing its peculiar cellar-breath, the three clocks upstairs ticking at three different opinions of what time actually was. Master Ovid was asleep in his chair by the parlor fire, as always, his snoring entered in the margins of Jonah's private notebook under *fixed costs*. Sefa was somewhere above, doing whatever guildmasters did at night: worrying, mostly.

Jonah dipped his pen and wrote:

*Received, one grief, bottled, medium-blue, returned by client Mrs. Anselm of Rope Walk. Client states she has "finished with it." Grief appears lighter than at intake. Note: griefs do not lighten. Investigate.*

It was good work. It was the only kind of work he had ever been good at, if he was honest, which he tried not to be, honesty being expensive. Numbers behaved. Entries stayed where you put them. A debt written down could be forgiven; a debt unwritten simply festered until it ate the debtor from the inside out. That was the whole philosophy of the Guild of Small Mercies, though Ovid would have phrased it more poetically and Sefa would have phrased it more bitterly.

The knock came at half past eleven.

Three strikes, spaced too evenly, too deliberate—no drunk's stagger, no creditor's fury. And there was something else about it, something that made Jonah's pen stop mid-word and leave a small black bloom on the page. The sound didn't echo. In a house built of hollow spaces and old wood, every sound echoed. This one landed and *stopped*, as if the house itself had decided to listen.

"Ovid," Jonah said. The old man snored on. "Master Ovid."

Nothing. Jonah set down his pen, took up the poker—he was nineteen and slight and had never won a fight in his life, but a poker improved the odds—and went to the door.

Through the peephole, carved into the oak at the height of a kneeling child, he saw frost. Not frost *on* the step. Frost *as* the visitor—a small figure rimed white, silvered at the shoulders, breath hanging in the air like smoke from a doused fire. Then the figure moved, and the frost fell away, and it was only a child after all. Six years old, perhaps seven. Barefoot. Bareheaded in the first cold night of the year. Eyes the color of deep water under ice.

Jonah opened the door because there was nothing else a keeper of mercies could do.

The child looked up at him and spoke.

Jonah did not understand a single word. He understood, somehow, everything. The language came out of the child's mouth like water over stones—rounded, ancient, unhurried—and it bypassed his ears entirely and settled somewhere behind his sternum, where meaning lived before words were invented for it. He heard: *I have been walking a long time. I was told this is where they keep the things that don't fit anywhere else.*

"We—" His voice cracked. He tried again. "We're closed."

The child tilted its head, considering this lie with the gravity of a magistrate, and said something else in that rolling old tongue. Jonah felt it land like a hand on his shoulder: *No, you aren't. You are never closed. That is what your kind forgot to be ashamed of.*

"Right," said Jonah faintly. "Come in, then."

* * *

Sefa was already on the stairs when Jonah led the child into the parlor, drawn down by some guildmaster's instinct or perhaps by the strange silence the child carried with it, the way a bell carries its own hush before ringing. She was sixty-eight years old, small and iron-colored, and in forty years of keeping impossible things accountable she had seen bottled plagues and purchased sunsets and one memorable Tuesday involving a man who had sold his shadow twice. Jonah had never once seen her go pale.

She went pale.

She stopped on the third stair from the bottom, one hand on the rail, and stared at the child standing dripping meltwater onto Ovid's good rug, and all the color drained out of her face as if someone had opened a valve.

"Sefa?" Jonah said. "You know the language?"

"I know it." Her voice came out flat and careful, the way she spoke when entering figures she did not trust. "Every guildmaster learns to recognize it. We are taught one word of it, and the word is *run*."

"That's not encouraging."

"It isn't meant to be." But she came down the stairs anyway, slowly, the way one approaches a sleeping animal or an open ledger with a suspicious entry. She knelt—not easily, at her age, and Jonah saw her wince and ignore it—and brought herself level with the child's eyes.

"What is your name?" she asked, in Trade-Tongue, then in High Court speech, then in two older dialects Jonah recognized from the archive shelves.

The child answered in the old language each time, patient, almost gentle, and each time Jonah felt the meaning arrive behind his breastbone like a letter delivered by a bird that had flown through his chest: *I don't have one yet. I lost it. Or it lost me. I was told someone here keeps things.*

Sefa closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, which frightened Jonah more than the paleness had.

"She says," Sefa translated, her voice very quiet, "that she has no name. That she lost it, or it lost her. And that she was told—" Sefa stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "That she was told someone here keeps things."

"Do we?"

"We keep accounts." Sefa rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Jonah, fetch the intake ledger. The blank one. If we're taking this child in, even for one night, we do it properly."

* * *

The intake ledger was a slim volume, rarely used—the guild had taken in perhaps four clients under the age of ten in its entire history, and none of them barefoot in October. Jonah laid it open on the counting room table while the child sat in Ovid's chair with its feet tucked under it, watching the candle flame with an expression of profound familiarity, as though it and fire were old acquaintances catching up.

"Name of client," Jonah read aloud, pen ready. "Unknown. Age of client—" He looked at the child. The child held up six fingers, then thought about it and added a seventh, then shook its head and put them all away, as if numbers too were a country it had been exiled from. "Approximately six. Nature of goods or services requested—"

The child spoke.

And this time the word was not water over stones. This time it was a single syllable, soft and round, and it struck Jonah between the shoulder blades like a thrown snowball, and every hair on his body stood up, and the candle flame leaned toward the child as if bowing.

Because Jonah knew that word. Not its translation. Not its meaning, exactly. He knew it the way you know your own heartbeat—wordlessly, totally, from the inside.

It was his name.

Not *Jonah*. Jonah was the name the guild had given him at eight years old, found on no document, attached to no family, entered in the ledgers under *acquired, circumstances unclear*. Jonah was a useful name, a coat he wore. But beneath it, in the place where he kept the things he never said aloud, there had always been another name—a true one, wordless, a shape rather than a sound, the thing he had been before anyone needed to call him anything. He had never spoken it. He had never written it. No ledger contained it, because he had made certain of that himself, in his first year, with his own careful hand: *the keeper's true name shall not be entered, for what is written can be stolen.*

The child had just said it. Perfectly. Effortlessly. As if picking it up off the floor where Jonah had dropped it without knowing.

The pen slipped from his fingers and left a long ink scar across the intake page.

"Jonah." Sefa was beside him now, gripping his arm hard enough to bruise. "What did she say? What did she say to you?"

"You heard it."

"I heard the old tongue. I heard—" Sefa's breath caught. "I heard a word I have not heard in fifty years. A word that appears in exactly one place in this building, Jonah, and that place is sealed, and locked, and *supposed to be empty*."

Above them, floorboards creaked. Ovid stood at the top of the stairs in his nightshirt, awake for the first time in living memory before noon, staring down at the child with an expression Jonah had no ledger category for.

The child looked from face to face, unbothered, unsurprised, its ice-water eyes reflecting the candle flame. Then it reached up with one small frost-pink hand and touched the table, the open ledger, the spilled ink—and where its fingers passed, the ink rose from the paper and hung in the air, a thin black thread, turning slowly, writing nothing and everything.

It spoke again, and this time Jonah needed no translation at all, because the word was the same one, his own true name, offered back to him like a key.

*I was told,* the meaning arrived, gentle as snowfall, *that the keeper would know what I am. Do you?*

"No," Jonah said honestly, because honesty was expensive but some debts had to be paid. "But I think I know who does."

He looked at Sefa. Sefa was looking at the far wall—at the shelf, at the founding ledger behind its glass case, at the sealed page whose lock had not been opened in three hundred years.

The seal was glowing.
