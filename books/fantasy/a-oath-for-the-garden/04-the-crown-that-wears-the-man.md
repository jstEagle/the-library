## 4. The Crown That Wears the Man

Jonah had learned to read the guild's ledgers the way other people read weather: not for what was written, but for what the writing was trying to survive. So when he opened the founding ledger on the third morning after the child arrived and found the sealed page still sealed—wax unbroken, but sweating, as if the heat of something beneath it were working through—he did not panic. He made tea first. He sharpened his pen. He sat with the book in his lap like a sleeping animal that might wake angry.

The child slept upstairs in the room above the counting floor, in Sefa's old bed, under three blankets and a charm stitched by Maren out of ledger-thread. The charm was meant to keep dreams from leaking. It was not meant to keep them *in*. But when Jonah climbed the stairs at dawn to check, he found the boy sitting upright, eyes open, mouth moving in that old language—the one older than kingdoms, the one Sefa had gone pale at hearing—and frost was blooming on the inside of the window in shapes too deliberate to be accident.

"What are you saying?" Jonah asked softly.

The boy looked at him. His eyes were wrong this morning—not cold, exactly, but *distant*, as though they belonged to someone standing far behind them.

"He's dreaming," the boy said, in trade-tongue now, his voice layered strangely, two speakers in one throat. "He always dreams it. The garden. He can't remember the gardener's face."

"Who? Who is dreaming?"

But the boy only lay back down, and the frost on the window melted into ordinary water, and Jonah stood there holding a candle against a morning that felt suddenly much older than it was.

Downstairs, Sefa was already at the long table, the founding ledger open before her, her thumb resting on the sealed page without pressing.

"It sweated overnight," she said, before Jonah could speak. "I know. I've been watching it since before you woke."

"What's under the wax?"

Sefa was quiet a long moment. Outside, the harbor bells rang flat and slow; the first frost had come early this year, earlier than any year in the ledgers' margins, where past members had recorded such things the way farmers record rain.

"A bargain," she said finally. "Struck before the guild had a name. Before the law of invisibility, even. Our founder kept accounts for a king who wanted to hold something back." Her thumb moved, very slightly, across the wax seal. "I think we are looking at what he held back."

"And the child?"

"The child," Sefa said, "is the part that didn't fit in the crown."

* * *

Aldric Vey dreamed in a language he did not know, and had done so for eleven years.

In the dream there was always a garden. It was not any garden he had ever walked—it was smaller than memory, warmer than hope, walled in old stone with moss in the mortar seams. Someone knelt in it, planting something. He could never see the face. He knew the hands, though. He knew them the way you know your own reflection in dark glass: by the ache of recognition, not the sight.

*"You're late,"* the dreamer said, in that tongue that predated crowns, and Aldric understood it perfectly, the way you understand things in dreams, and answered in it fluently, and woke each morning having lost every word.

He woke, as he always woke, with wet eyes and no reason for them.

The chamber was gray with early light. Frost ferned the windows despite the season being wrong for it—autumn had curdled into winter weeks ahead of itself, and the court whispered about witch-work, about the guild by the harbor, about omens. Aldric let them whisper. Whispering was easier than asking him questions, because he was increasingly unsure he would know the answers.

There was a girl standing at the foot of his bed.

She came most mornings. She had his coloring—dark hair, the Vey jaw—and she watched him with an expression he could not parse, patience stretched over grief like canvas over a frame. He knew she was important. Everyone bowed to her slightly more deeply than protocol required, which told him she was family. Which told him—

"Good morning, Father," she said.

Father. Yes. Of course. He was her father. He reached for her name the way a man reaches for a stair in the dark and finds the step missing.

"You look well today," he said, because it was true, and because it filled the space where her name should have been.

Something behind her eyes went out. Not all at once—a lamp turned low, not snuffed. "You dreamed again," she said. Not a question. "In the old language."

"I don't—" He pressed his palms to his temples. "I dream of a garden."

"I know." She came closer, hesitated, then took his hand. Her grip was fierce. "Papa. Say my name."

*Papa.* The word landed somewhere deep and rang like a struck bell, and for one vertiginous second the garden flared behind his eyes—the moss, the kneeling figure, the small green shoots—and the figure turned its head, and he almost, almost saw—

"Elira," he said.

Her whole body changed. She laughed, or sobbed, or both, and pressed her forehead to his hand. "Yes. Elira. You remembered."

And then, like water finding a crack, it drained away. He felt it go. He felt himself forget her name even as he held her, the knowledge sliding out through some wound in him he could not locate, and the crown on the stand across the room caught the gray light and gleamed, patient as a spider.

"Elira," he repeated desperately, holding on. "Elira. Elira."

"Yes," she whispered. "Hold it. Hold it as long as you can."

He could hold it until noon, some days. Once, memorably, until dusk. The crown took it back every night while he slept, and he woke lighter and emptier, a cup poured out to make room.

He never wondered, anymore, what the room was being emptied to make space *for*.

That was the cruelest arithmetic of it: the crown did not merely take. It took *and replaced*. Something else was being stored in King Aldric Vey, shelf by shelf, year by year, in a warehouse built of a living man. Some mornings he found thoughts in his head that were not his—cold, orderly, vast thoughts, thoughts in the old language, thoughts that regarded people the way an accountant regards figures. He had stopped mentioning them to Corvane. Corvane always smiled so kindly when he did, and adjusted the crown's fit, and said *the burden is nearly balanced, Majesty, soon, soon,* and Aldric would feel a door close inside himself that he could not find again.

*Soon.* Eleven years of soon.

He dressed. He ate. He held audience and signed what Corvane set before him, and the ink felt cold going down onto the page, colder than ink should be, and once—only once—he looked at a decree ordering the harbor watch doubled against "guild sedition" and thought, in a voice that was not quite his own: *good, good, we are closing the net.*

Whose net?

He raised his hand to his temple. Under his fingers, faint as a pulse, the crown hummed.

* * *

"We have to see him," Jonah said.

It was evening. The counting floor smelled of tallow and cold iron; Maren had lit the good lamps, the ones reserved for hard conversations. The child sat on the stairs, wrapped in a blanket, listening with the terrible attentiveness children have when they understand more than they're told.

Sefa set down her pen. "That is not a sentence I ever expected to hear from you, Jonah Zhou. We are invisible. That is not a preference. It is the load-bearing wall of everything we are."

"I know what it is." Jonah laid his hand flat on the founding ledger. Beneath the wax, the sealed page had begun, faintly, to glow—or perhaps to warm; he couldn't tell anymore where the ledger ended and his own dread began. "But the king is forgetting his own daughter. Piece by piece. And whatever is being put in the empty places—" He looked at the child. "Is coming *from* him. Or to him. The bargain goes both ways, doesn't it? A crown made to hold something back. Half in the man, half out of him. And the out-half walked to our door on the first frost and spoke my true name, which isn't written in any book we own."

Maren said quietly, "How does a child know a name our ledgers refuse?"

No one answered. The lamps guttered in a draft that had no source.

"The sorcerer forged the crown," Jonah went on. "Corvane. Every account we hold that touches the palace routes through his office. If anyone knows the terms of the original bargain, it's him. And if we go to the king directly, we walk into Corvane's house wearing our invisibility like a coat in a windstorm." He met Sefa's eyes. "We need to know what we're walking into. I can get into the archives. Tax assessors go everywhere; nobody looks twice at a man with a ledger."

"That's the problem," Sefa said. "Everyone will be looking at a man with a ledger. Ours especially. Corvane has spent thirty years trying to find us, Jonah. You'd be carrying the one thing he wants through his own front door."

"Then I'll carry it carefully."

Sefa looked at him for a long time. Behind her, the sealed page pulsed once, soft as a heartbeat, and the frost on the window upstairs cracked with a sound like a small bell.

"The child dreamed of him again tonight," Maren said. "The king. The boy says the garden is getting darker. He says the gardener is starting to forget the garden too."

Jonah closed the ledger. The wax seal, when he touched it, was warm as skin.

"Then we're out of seasons," he said. "I leave for the palace tomorrow."

* * *

That night, Aldric Vey stood at his window and watched frost crawl across the city in patterns he almost recognized.

Below, in the dark, a light burned in a building near the harbor that appeared on no map he had ever approved. He frowned at it. Somewhere very deep, beneath eleven years of taken things, a voice that was still entirely his own whispered: *that light matters. Remember that light.*

He reached for the thought.

The crown hummed, and took it.

In the garden of his dream, the kneeling figure looked up at last—and Aldric woke with a name on his lips that was not his daughter's, a name in the old language that meant *keeper*, and no memory of why he was weeping.
