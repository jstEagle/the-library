## 5. Terms of Invisibility

Sefa had buried two guildmasters and outlived three kings, and in all her hundred and six years she had learned one lesson above the rest: the guild survived because it was never seen. Not hidden—hidden things were sought. *Invisible.* There was a difference, and the difference was written on the first page of the founding ledger in ink that still smelled faintly of iron:

*We do not exist. We do not interfere. We keep what is given to us, and we are forgotten by morning.*

Three sentences. Four centuries of life bought with them.

She stood now at the head of the long table in the meeting room above the cellar, watching candlelight gutter in the draft from the door Jonah had left open behind him, and she thought: *this is how it ends. Not with fire, not with the King's men at our throats. With an open door and a child's wet boots on our floor.*

The child sat at the far end of the table, wrapped in one of Jonah's spare coats, hands curled around a cup of barley tea gone cold an hour ago. It hadn't spoken since it arrived. It only watched them, the way a deer watches a lantern—not afraid exactly, but measuring the distance to the trees.

Jonah stood beside the child's chair like a fence post planted in a storm. Twenty-three years old, youngest member the guild had taken in ninety years, and already Sefa could see the shape of what he would become: someone who stood in front of things.

Old Merrick sat to her right, sharpening a knife he had no intention of using, because sharpening was how Merrick prayed. Old Wren sat to her left, half-asleep as always, though Sefa knew better than anyone that Wren's sleep was a kind of listening.

"Say it again," Sefa said quietly. "What it said to you. At the door."

Jonah's jaw tightened. "It said my name."

"It said your *true* name." Sefa let the words sit in the room like a stone dropped in still water. "A name no ledger contains. I have kept this guild's books for sixty years, Jonah Zhou. I have recorded every name ever given to us, sold to us, stolen by us. Yours is not among them. Do you understand what that means?"

"That I'm not in the inventory," Jonah said.

"That you are not *ownable*." She leaned forward. "Nothing in this world speaks a true name unless it has been present when that name was made. Names are not words, boy. They are wounds. And that child carries a wound old enough to know yours."

The child looked up. Its eyes were the color of winter sky just before snow—pale, patient, wrong for a face so young. When it spoke, its voice came out doubled, a child's treble laid over something vast and slow beneath, like a bell rung under water.

"*Sefa of the Third Ledger.*"

Merrick's knife stopped mid-stroke.

"You know me," Sefa said.

"I know your debt." The child tilted its head. "You borrowed forty years from the well under the harbor. You have spent thirty-nine. You are spending the last one now, tonight, deciding."

The silence that followed had weight. Wren opened one eye.

"I did not tell you that," Sefa said.

"No," the child agreed. "I remember it. I remember everything that was ever set down anywhere. That is why they put me away." A pause, and then, softer, in a voice that was suddenly, terribly just a child's voice: "That is why they cut me out of him and put me in the dark. Because I remember too much."

Sefa felt the floor tilt under her certainties. She had prepared arguments on the walk upstairs—send it away, bind its memory, deliver it to some distant abbey where impossible children went to be someone else's problem. She had rehearsed them all the way up the stairs.

None of them survived those eyes.

She straightened anyway. Duty was duty, and duty did not blink first.

"This guild exists," she said, "because for four hundred years we have done exactly nothing that anyone could see. We bottle grief for widows who cannot carry it. We lend years to dying farmers so they can finish their harvests. We hold names that no longer fit until someone grows into them. And we do it *invisibly*. No client remembers our faces by morning. No king knows we exist. No sorcerer can find our door. That law is not cruelty, child. It is the only reason we are still here to be cruel or kind at all."

"The crown found us anyway," Jonah said.

Sefa turned on him. "The crown did not find us. A *child* walked through our door."

"A child the crown has been looking for." Jonah's voice stayed level, but his knuckles were white around the back of the chair. "You heard what it said downstairs. It's the unbound half of Aldric Vey. Corvane forged that crown to hold something back—took whatever the king was, split it, and buried half of it in a body small enough to forget. And now the binding is failing, and the half that remembers everything walked out of the dark and came *here*, to the one place in the city that keeps accounts of impossible things. You think that's accident? Things that remember everything don't have accidents."

"Then it is a trap," Sefa said.

"Or a rescue."

"Or both." She pushed back her chair and stood, because sitting made her feel old and standing made her feel angry, and angry was more useful. "Do you know what happens if we shelter it, Jonah? Corvane will come. Not with soldiers—with *paperwork*. He'll arrive with warrants older than our charter, claims on debts we forgot we owed, and every seal we've ever signed will turn in his hand like a key. We are three old people and a bookkeeper. Our entire arsenal is that we are nobody. Shelter that child and we become *somebody* by sunrise."

"Good," Jonah said.

Sefa stared at him. "Good?"

"Somebody has to be." He came around the table, slowly, and stopped across from her, and for a moment she saw not her junior clerk but the man the ledgers had been quietly making for five years—the one who reconciled impossible columns without flinching, who wrote *paid in full* beside entries that should have broken him. "Master Sefa. I have read every page in that cellar. Every bargain, every mercy, every debt we've carried so no one else had to. And in four hundred years, do you know how many times this guild has chosen itself over a client?" He didn't wait for her answer. "Once. One entry. 1187, the year of the ash plague—we refused a family shelter because sheltering them risked the door. Their names are in the ledger. We wrote them down so we'd remember we did it. That's the whole reason the record exists. So we can't pretend we didn't."

"We survived," Sefa said. "Because of that choice, everyone after lived."

"Yes. And the family died." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. "I've read that page maybe a hundred times, and every time I think: next time we'll be braver. Next time the ledger will say something else. Well." He gestured at the child, at the frost creeping up the inside of the windows despite the banked fire. "It's next time."

The child had begun, very quietly, to cry. It made no sound doing it—clearly it had learned, somewhere in the dark it came from, that crying silently was safer—and somehow that was worse than wailing. Merrick set down his knife. Wren rose, crossed the room without hurry, and settled into the chair beside the child, and took its small cold hand in her spotted ancient one, and said nothing at all, which from Wren was a sermon.

Sefa looked at her guild. Her found, stubborn, doomed family.

"Merrick," she said. "Wren. I want your voices, not your silence. If we vote, we vote truly."

Merrick grunted. "Been voting with the boy since he carried it in. Somebody has to stand in front of things. Always liked that about him." He nodded at Jonah. "Hated it too. Same thing, mostly."

Wren spoke without turning her head, her voice dry as autumn paper. "I have held dying hands for sixty years, Sefa. I know the difference between a death that comes for you and one you invite by being decent. Only one of them lets you sleep afterward." She patted the child's hand. "I'm voting for sleep."

Three against one. Sefa closed her eyes.

She thought of the founding ledger's first page. *We do not exist.* She had recited it ten thousand times, taught it to apprentices, built her whole long borrowed life on it. And underneath the recitation, all along, had been the thing she'd never let herself read closely: that invisibility was never meant to be a shield for the guild. It was meant to be a *cost*. They hid themselves so that the mercies could be pure—given without thanks, without witness, without price returned. Somewhere in four centuries, the shield had become the point. They had stopped being invisible *for* the work and started being invisible *instead of* it.

The child's tears fell into the cold tea, one by one, each drop ringing faintly, impossibly, like a coin dropped into a well.

"If we do this," Sefa said at last, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, "we do it as the guild has always done everything. On the record. Every step entered, every cost counted. If Corvane comes for us, he will find no confusion in our books—only intent. Do you hear me, Jonah? If we break the law of invisibility, we break it *cleanly*. I will not have our last act look like cowardice in the margins."

Jonah exhaled. Something in his shoulders loosened—a debt acknowledged, an entry balanced. "First line's already written," he said. "*Received, this night of first frost: one child, unbound, remembering. Terms pending.*"

"Terms," Sefa repeated, and laughed, short and bitter. "Yes. Terms. Against a court sorcerer who forges crowns and collects centuries." She looked down the table at the child, and the child looked back, and between them passed something that was not quite forgiveness and not quite permission—only recognition, keeper to kept.

"Then hear my terms," she said. "We do not send it away. But we do not sit here waiting to be found either. Tomorrow, Jonah opens the ledgers—all of them, even the sealed pages—and we learn exactly what bargain made that crown, because you cannot dissolve a contract you haven't read. And I—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and continued, "—I will write to the palace. Anonymously. A warning, nothing more. If there is a way to end this without breaking us, I want it tried first. That is my price for treason."

"Accepted," Jonah said softly.

The child lifted its head. The doubled voice filled the room, quiet as falling snow.

"*Thank you, keeper.*"

And outside the window, unnoticed by any of them, the first frost thickened into something whiter, deeper, and older than the season—creeping down the street from the harbor, patient as an unpaid debt, while in the palace above the city a king slept and dreamed in a language no one at court could speak, and woke, as he had every morning that month, unable to remember his daughter's face.

* * *

Later, past midnight, Sefa descended alone to the cellar with a single candle.

She did not go to the founding ledger. She went to the bottom drawer of the iron cabinet, the one locked with a key she wore against her heart, and drew out a folder of correspondence so old the string tying it had grown into the paper. Letters from the guilds that had come before—inland chapters, sister houses, the network that once spanned nine kingdoms.

Every letter was a final report. Every house had ended the same way: found, exposed, burned or absorbed, the moment it chose a side. The last one, from the chapter at Vell, ended mid-sentence, the rest of the page scorched away.

Sefa read them all again by candlelight, the way she did every few years, the way one rereads a diagnosis. Then she tied the string, returned the folder, and locked the drawer.

Above her, faintly, she could hear Jonah moving through the meeting room, putting the child to sleep on the good bench by the fire, blowing out candles one by one. Keeping house. Keeping accounts. Keeping faith with something Sefa had spent a century teaching him to fear.

"He'll be the death of us," she told the empty cellar, and her voice did not believe it, and neither did the cellar.

She climbed the stairs. Behind her, in the dark among the shelves, the sealed page of the founding ledger lay waiting for morning—and beneath the wax that sealed it, pressed flat for four hundred years, a single pressed flower that had been white once, and was not anymore.
