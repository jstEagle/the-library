## 6. The Sorcerer's Account

The tax assessor's coat itched. That was the first thing Jonah noticed, and the last honest thought he would have for hours.

It was a good disguise—Sefa's work, and Sefa did nothing badly. Grey wool, brass buttons worn to the color of old teeth, a ledger-case slung at his hip that contained, instead of the guild's true books, three years of perfectly boring harbor dues copied out in his own careful hand. A forged writ of inspection from the Office of Revenues, sealed with wax that smelled faintly of tallow rather than authority. And pinned inside the collar, where no one would look, a single thread pulled from the guild's threshold—a mercy against being seen too closely.

*Be dull*, Sefa had told him at the door, pressing the case into his hands. *Duller than you have ever been in your life. Assessors are furniture with opinions about barrels.*

Jonah had never been furniture in his life. He was twenty-three years old and he had spent all of them making the impossible legible, which meant he noticed things: the way the palace guards stood too far apart, as if the space between them were a wound; the way the frost on the courtyard flagstones made shapes that were not frost-shapes, spirals in the old tongue, small as coins; the way everyone he passed looked tired in a manner that had nothing to do with sleep.

He walked through the palace like a man reading a ledger he already suspected was cooked.

* * *

The court archives occupied the oldest wing, below ground, where the windows were arrow-slits and the light came down in blades. A clerk with ink-stained cuffs led him down stairs worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet, chattering about reorganization, about the new cataloguing system, about Master Corvane's insistence that every record be *accounted for*.

"Accounted for," Jonah repeated.

"Oh, yes." The clerk beamed. "Master Corvane says an archive is only a promise the past makes to the future. If you can't find the promise, there is no promise." He said it with the reverence of a man quoting scripture, and Jonah felt something cold walk down his spine, because he had said almost exactly those words once—to Sefa, over the founding ledger, before they knew what was sealed inside it.

The archive itself was beautiful. Jonah hated how much he loved it. Rows of cedar shelving going back further than lamplight reached, folios bound in leather gone soft as skin, chains of office hanging from hooks like sleeping serpents. The air smelled of dust and cold iron and, beneath it, something sweeter—the smell of a spell long steeped, like tea left too long in the pot.

And there, in the deepest row, behind a gate of black iron, a shelf of ledgers bound in white.

Jonah stopped walking.

He knew those bindings. Every guild member knew them. Vellum from the same tannery, corner-guards of the same pale metal, the same blind-stamped sigil on the cover that the guild used on its own books—or had used, four hundred years ago, before the split, before the hiding, before the law of invisibility. The Guild of Small Mercies kept its ledgers. But somewhere, someone else had kept *accounts*.

"Ah." The voice came from behind him, mild as milk. "You've found my favorite shelf."

* * *

Master Corvane did not look like a villain. This was the first trap, and Jonah fell into it with his eyes open.

He was a spare man, silvering at the temples, dressed in dark blue so deep it read as black until the light moved. His hands were clean. His eyes were the brown of strong tea, warm, attentive, the eyes of someone who listened for a living. When he smiled—and he smiled often, easily—it reached all the way up, and that was the second trap, because Jonah had spent his life around liars and knew that the best ones never needed to fake anything. They simply believed their own reasons.

"You're the assessor from the Revenues Office," Corvane said. "Come about the harbor tariffs."

"The winter tariffs," Jonah said, and heard his own voice come out flat and bored, furniture-voiced. "There are discrepancies."

"There are always discrepancies." Corvane gestured him toward a reading table, poured wine from a decanter without asking whether he wanted any, and sat across from him with the ease of a man who had done this ten thousand times. "But you're not here about tariffs."

Jonah kept his face still. "Master Corvane—"

"I know why you're here." The sorcerer turned his wine glass slowly by its stem. "You came to see if we hold the account of Aldric Vey. The bargain. The crown. What was given and what was taken." He smiled again, gently. "You want to read the original terms."

The cold in Jonah's spine spread outward, into his fingers, his jaw. *Be dull.* He was failing at dull. "I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you do." Corvane set the glass down. "You are Jonah Zhou. Keeper of ledgers for the Guild of Small Mercies. Youngest member. Last real one, I'm told—I keep accounts too, young man, and mine say your guild has three members left, one of whom is spending her years like a drunkard spends coin, and a child in your cellar who speaks a language older than kingdoms." He leaned forward slightly. "You see? Everything is accounted for. Even you."

For a moment Jonah could not breathe. Not fear, exactly—something worse, the vertigo of realizing the walls you hid behind had been mapped long ago by the thing hunting you.

"How?" he managed.

"Because I built the crown." Corvane said it plainly, without pride, the way a mason might admit to a wall. "Not me personally—my predecessor's predecessor, the first Corvane, who signed the bargain with the first king. But the craft passes down. The office passes down. The *debt* passes down." He rose, walked to the black iron gate, and unlocked it with a key that appeared from nowhere. "You want to see. Come."

* * *

The white-bound ledger lay open on a lectern under its own small lamp, and Jonah read it the way he read everything—fast, hungry, line by line—and what it said made his stomach turn over.

It was the mirror of the guild's sealed page. Where the guild's founding ledger recorded the *mercy*—a crown made to hold something back, to carry what a king could not—the court's account recorded the *price*. Itemized. In the old hand, in the old tongue, but legible, because accounting is legible everywhere:

*One half of a soul, unbound, to be held in trust.*
*Interest accrued annually: memory, tenderness, the names of those loved.*
*Term: until the crown is paid in full or the bearer is empty, whichever comes first.*

Below it, in fresher ink, additions in Corvane's own writing. Annual entries. Fourteen years of them. Each one a withdrawal: *the queen's face, remitted. The princess's first word, remitted. The taste of rain, remitted.*

"It's very tidy," Corvane said, watching him read. "I'll give the first Corvane that. Tidy work. No ambiguity, no loopholes. Your guild could learn from it."

"This is a child," Jonah said. His voice cracked on the word and he hated himself for it. "The other half. It's a child."

"It is an asset," Corvane corrected, softly. "An unclaimed asset. Which brings us to the reason I let you find this shelf, Jonah Zhou, and the reason your little disguise amused me enough to allow it this far." He sat on the edge of the table, close, and his warmth did not waver. "I'd like to make you an offer."

"I'm listening." He wasn't. He wanted to run. He stayed, because running would tell Corvane everything.

"The child cannot stay hidden. You know this. The power in her grows—your guildmaster is burning her own years to contain it, and she will run out, probably before spring. The city is already whispering about witch-work. The harbor froze in harvest-time. Sooner or later, a mob or a magistrate comes to your door, and mobs are not tidy. Mobs do not itemize." He paused. "Or—you bring me the ledgers. All of them. The guild's true books, the sealed page, the whole hidden history of four centuries of impossible mercies. In exchange, I take custody of the child. She will be fed, clothed, educated, protected. Bound properly, this time, under terms I will write myself—with interest paid *to* her, not extracted from her. And the king—" he shrugged elegantly— "the king continues. The kingdom continues. Everyone eats."

"And the ledgers?" Jonah asked. "What happens to them?"

"They become part of the archive." Corvane touched the white binding almost tenderly. "Accounted for. Catalogued. Sealed under my key. Nothing destroyed—Corvane does not destroy records, records are sacred—but nothing *available*, either. No more bottled grief sold in back rooms. No more borrowed years. No more names that no longer fit. The age of small mercies ends, quietly, in a basement, and no one will ever miss it, because no one will ever know it existed."

"That's not mercy," Jonah said. "That's a mausoleum."

"It's *order*." For the first time, something moved under the warmth—a flicker, a hairline crack, and through it Jonah glimpsed the thing underneath: not cruelty, but certainty, vast and cold and patient as the ice on the harbor. "You think chaos is kindness because it wears a soft voice. I have read four hundred years of your guild's accounts, Jonah. Do you know what I found in them? Debts never collected. Mercies that curdled. Grief sold twice to the same widow. Your guild has been laundering the world's pain through a cellar and calling it grace." He straightened. "I am offering to end the ambiguity. That is not corruption. That is housekeeping."

And the terrible thing—the thing Jonah would lie awake turning over for nights after—was that it sounded reasonable. It sounded like a ledger entry. Balanced columns. Costs on one side, benefits on the other, and the child alive on the benefit side, written in Corvane's neat hand.

Sefa would call it seduction. Sefa would be right. But standing there, in the blade-light of the archives, with the white book open between them, Jonah understood for the first time how kingdoms fell—not to monsters, but to men who offered to do the arithmetic for you.

"I need time," he said.

"Everyone needs time." Corvane smiled and refilled Jonah's untouched wine, though Jonah hadn't lifted the glass. "Take three days. On the fourth, I come to you—no, better: I will send for you, publicly, as a tax matter. No one will look twice. Bring your answer and, if you're wise, bring the books." He walked Jonah to the stairs himself, hand resting briefly on his shoulder, a touch that lingered a half-second too long. "For what it's worth—and I am told worth matters to you—I think you're the only one in your guild who understands what these books actually cost. Think about whose side of the ledger you'd rather stand on."

* * *

The frost-spirals on the courtyard stones had grown while he was underground. They climbed the fountain now, the benches, the lower courses of the eastern wall, written in the old tongue, and Jonah knelt and brushed snow from one and read it, because he could not stop himself from reading things.

It said: *come home.*

The child's handwriting. The child's reach, reaching past Sefa's holding-spell, past the cellar wards, past the whole city's forgetting, to find the one person who had spoken kindly to her in a language older than kingdoms.

Three days, Corvane had said. The child was asking for less than that.

Jonah stood in the falling snow with a forged writ under his arm and a sorcerer's offer in his pocket and the beginning of an answer forming in him that was not a choice between the ledgers and the child, because Corvane had framed it wrong, deliberately, the way all reasonable men frame things wrong. The real question was not *which side of the ledger*.

It was whether some things should ever be entered in one at all.

He started walking, faster than an assessor should walk, and behind him the frost-spirals bloomed open like flowers made of knives, spelling his name across the palace yard in letters of ice that would melt by morning—but not before every guard in the courtyard had seen them, and not before one of them, a captain with a familiar face, began to wonder why a tax man made the cold sit up and listen.
