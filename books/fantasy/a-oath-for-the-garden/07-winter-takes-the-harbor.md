## 7. Winter Takes the Harbor

The frost came three weeks early, and it came wrong.

Jonah noticed it first in the ledgers. Ink froze in the well overnight—a thing that had never happened in the guild hall's two hundred years—and when he cracked the shutters at dawn, the glass was furred white on the *inside*. The harbor beyond lay silvered to the horizon, and it was not yet even autumn's end. Gulls stood on the water as though it were cobblestone, heads tucked, patient as statues.

He wrote the date in the margin of the day-book and underlined it twice. *Frost out of season. Frost that does not melt.* By noon the sun had climbed high and pale and done nothing at all against it. The ice held. The fishmongers along the quay swore and stamped and hacked at it with boathooks, and the harbor simply absorbed their fury, thickening by the hour like a wound deciding to scar over everything at once.

By evening, the city had found someone to blame.

It did not take long. It never did. A chalk mark appeared on the guild door—rough, crooked, the kind of sign a frightened person draws when they want a witch and don't know the proper sigils. Then another on the step. Then a stone through the window of the chandlery next door, because the chandler had once bought a candle from them, and guilt travels sideways when it cannot travel up.

"They're saying it in the taverns," Pell said, coming in with his coat still smoking cold air. He was the oldest of them now, after Wren passed in spring, and his hands shook more than they used to, but his ears were still sharp. "Witch-work. Guild-work. *That* work." He tapped the floor meaningfully, as if the cellar full of bottled grief might seep upward through the boards and freeze a harbor out of spite.

"The harbor isn't ours," Jonah said.

"No," Pell agreed. "But the child is."

And there it was. The word nobody said aloud anymore, sitting in the room like an unopened ledger: *the child*.

* * *

The child—Aldric, though no one called him that yet; they called him Little Wren, after the member they'd lost, because Sefa said grief needed somewhere to go—had been with them six weeks now. Six weeks of a language older than kingdoms spoken into the rafters while he slept. Six weeks of Jonah transcribing what he could, letter by letter, into a ledger he'd had to bind himself because no paper in the city would hold the ink without weeping.

The night the frost came wrong, the child did not sleep.

Jonah heard him before he saw him—standing at the top of the cellar stairs in his borrowed nightshirt, small feet bare on cold wood, speaking to the dark in that old tongue. Not crying. *Addressing* it. The way a king addresses a court. And the dark was answering, or something was, because the candle flames all through the hall bent toward the stairs like grass toward wind.

"Little Wren," Jonah said gently, and took him up, and carried him back to bed, and the flames straightened, and the frost outside crept one street closer to the guild door.

In the morning, Sefa looked at the boy for a long time and said nothing, which from Sefa was worse than anything she could have said.

* * *

**From the palace, King Aldric dreamed.**

He dreamed in the language he did not know he knew. In the dream there was a garden, and in the garden a small figure with his own face, younger, unafraid, holding out both hands. *Come back*, the figure said, in letters Aldric's waking mind could not read but his sleeping blood understood perfectly. *You left half of yourself in me and I have been carrying it up a long hill.*

He woke with tears frozen on his cheeks. That was new. The chamber was warm—the sorcerer Corvane kept it warm, kept everything warm, kept everything *arranged*—and yet the salt tracks on his face had gone hard and white as frostwork.

"Your Majesty," said the steward, "your daughter is asking for you."

"What daughter?" Aldric said, and meant it, and the look on the steward's face told him how much he should be frightened of having meant it. There was a girl. He knew there was a girl the way one knows there is a moon. Princess Maren, fourteen years old, who used to sit on his knee and demand stories about the sea. He could remember the fact of her the way a man remembers the fact of a country he has never visited.

He went down to breakfast and sat across from her and felt nothing at all, and she watched him feel nothing, and neither of them said a word about it, because the court sorcerer had taught them both, in different lessons, that some silences are safer than speech.

Corvane appeared at the window that afternoon, unannounced, robes the color of deep water, smiling his reasonable smile.

"A hard frost, sire," he said. "Unseasonable. The people are frightened."

"People are always frightened."

"These people are frightened of *you*, Majesty. Or rather—" the smile tilted, precise as a surgeon's blade, "—of what you carry. I can contain it, of course. I have contained it for years. But containment grows expensive, and the crown grows heavy, and you grow..." He paused, delicately. "...lighter."

"I feel heavier," Aldric said.

"Yes," said Corvane. "That too."

* * *

On the ninth day of the false winter, the harbor froze solid from shore to shipping lanes, and the child's power broke its banks.

It began at dusk. Jonah was in the cellar doing inventory—bottled grief, shelf forty-one, three jars lost their seals in the cold and had to be re-waxed, grief going thin and sharp as vinegar in the frost—when he felt it come up through the floor. Not cold exactly. *Certainty.* The old language, spoken at volume, spoken by something that was not only a child anymore.

He took the stairs fast.

Little Wren stood in the middle of the great hall with his arms out and his eyes open and white as skimmed milk, and the frost was coming off him in banners, and every window in the guild hall had gone opaque with ice, and through the ice—glowing faintly, like a lamp behind cloth—came the light of the harbor freezing. All of it. At once. The fishing fleet locked in place, masts groaning. The tide stopped mid-pull, a wave standing frozen off the breakwater like a sculpture of itself. Somewhere out on the ice, faintly, a bell buoy rang once and then could not ring again.

"Sefa!" Jonah shouted. "SEFA!"

She was already coming down the stairs, and she was already old—older than she'd been that morning, visibly, terribly, the way a fire is older after it catches. She had made a decision in the seconds between the first shout and the stairs, Jonah realized later. She had opened the ledger of her own remaining years—that private ledger every guild member keeps, the one with the borrowed time written in it—and she had begun spending.

"Hold him," she said, and knelt, and put her hands on either side of the child's face, and spoke back to him.

Not in the old tongue. She didn't have it whole; almost none of them did, only fragments preserved like pressed flowers in the founding ledger. But she had enough. Enough of the words for *enough*, for *rest*, for *not yet*, for *I am here and I will hold this with you so you do not have to hold it alone*. Her voice came out layered, doubled, an old woman's voice with something vast underneath it, and where it touched the child's power the frost bent, wavered, drew back from the walls like a tide meeting a seawall.

The hall shuddered. The ice on the windows thinned to lace. Out in the city, Jonah would learn afterward, the standing wave collapsed back into the sea with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and the harbor ice cracked into floes, and the fishing fleet floated free, and no one aboard ever learned how close they had come to becoming part of a monument.

Little Wren's eyes cleared. He looked at Sefa's hands on his face, and then at her face, and said something in the old language, soft, questioning.

"I know," Sefa said, in ordinary Common, and her voice was wrecked and gentle. "I know. You didn't mean to. Nothing that large ever means to."

Then she stood up, and Jonah saw what it had cost, and had to put his hand against the wall.

Her hair had gone from iron-grey to white. Her hands, steady for seventy years, shook now like Pell's. She had spent—I counted the ledgers later, because counting is what I do when I cannot bear a thing—she had spent eleven years. Eleven years she did not have to give, given anyway, poured into a stranger's child on a night the whole city was learning to hate us.

"Why?" Jonah asked her, when the boy was sleeping and the fires were rebuilt and the frost had withdrawn to a respectful distance. "Sefa. They'll say it was us anyway. They're saying it already. Why spend years protecting a city that's chalking witches' marks on our door?"

Sefa sat down across from him, slowly, the way old things sit down, and looked at him with eyes that had seen two hundred years of the guild's mercies and remembered most of them.

"Because that is the entire trade, Jonah," she said. "That is the *only* trade. We do not do mercy because it is returned. We do mercy because it is owed—to everyone, especially to those who owe us nothing but fear. If we stop, if we let that harbor freeze and those boats die and call it the price of our safety, then we are not a guild of small mercies anymore. We are just hoarders with better shelves." She folded her shaking hands. "I would rather be poor in years and rich in that than the reverse. And I am running out of chances to choose."

Jonah could not speak for a while. When he could, he said, "Eleven years."

"Nine, probably, the way I live." She smiled, dry as paper. "I've had a good run. Longer than the guild deserved from me, honestly." Then her smile failed, and she said the thing she had clearly been carrying since the night the child arrived: "But listen to me, Jonah Zhou. Listen, because I am old and tired and I will not say it twice. What happened tonight was a warning shot. That power is growing faster than any of us can hold, and I will not always be able to buy it back with years I don't have. Next time, there may be no Sefa. Next time there may be nothing between that child and that city but ice and fear and a mob with torches."

"So what do we do?"

"We stop hiding," she said, and the words fell into the room like stones into a well. "Or we bury that child so deep in wards he suffocates. Those are the choices. There are no others. I have checked the ledgers. I have checked them *twice*."

* * *

The knock came at midnight, three days later.

Jonah answered it himself, because Pell was asleep and Sefa was resting and the door had been chalked and stoned and spat upon for a week and no one else wanted to touch it. He expected a mob. He expected the magistrate. He expected fire.

It was a single figure, hooded, alone, snow settling on shoulders that did not seem to notice it. The figure pushed back the hood, and Jonah's breath went out of him.

Princess Maren. Fourteen years old, alone, at midnight, in a false blizzard, at the door of the guild her father's court blamed for the death of the harvest-frosts.

"You're the ones who keep accounts," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "My father is forgetting me. Every day a little more. Last week he called me by my mother's name. Yesterday he looked at me and asked the steward whose child I was." Her chin trembled once and steadied. "The court sorcerer says nothing can be done. The court sorcerer smiles when he says it. And I have heard—I hear things, children hear everything—that *you* keep accounts of impossible things."

She held out one gloved hand. In it, wrapped in silk, was a single frozen tear—one of her father's, she said, caught on the morning it froze on his cheek. Proof. Evidence. An exhibit.

"So keep an account of this," said the princess of Veymar, standing in the snow like a supplicant at a shrine. "Tell me what was taken from my father, and who took it, and what it will cost to get it back. Because if you won't help him, no one will. And if no one helps him, then by spring there will be no father left to save—only the crown, wearing whatever is left, and a sorcerer standing behind it, smiling."

Jonah looked at the frozen tear in her palm. He thought of Sefa's white hair. Of nine years. Of the law of invisibility, older than the guild's own founding, carved into the first page of the first ledger: *Be unseen, be unknown, be nothing, and live.*

He thought: *We are already dead if we stay hidden. We are only choosing the manner of it.*

He opened the door wide, and brought the princess in from the cold, and reached for the founding ledger—and on the table beside it, where no one had placed it, he found a note in Sefa's hand, written hours ago, waiting for him:

*If she comes, we are past choosing. Wake me.*
