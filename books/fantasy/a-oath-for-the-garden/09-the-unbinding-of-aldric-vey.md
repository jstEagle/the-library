## 9. The Unbinding of Aldric Vey

The throne room had become a cathedral of ice.

Jonah stood at the great doors with the founding ledger open across both arms, its weight no longer paper but something older—oath-weight, gravity-weight, the weight of every small mercy ever written down and made accountable. Frost climbed the pillars in ferns and feathers. The banners hung stiff as grave-clothes. And in the center of it all, on a throne that had frozen black, sat King Aldric Vey, crowned, his breath rising in slow silver ghosts.

Between them, on the ice-slick floor, Master Corvane waited like a man waiting for a coach he knew would be late.

"You came," Corvane said. He sounded almost pleased. "I confess I gave you even odds of running."

"The guild doesn't run," Jonah said. "It keeps accounts."

Behind him, the child's small hand found his sleeve. Ashen—no, not Ashen anymore; Jonah had stopped pretending he didn't know the other name—stood wrapped in Sefa's old gray cloak, frost blooming and dying on their skin with each heartbeat, as though winter itself couldn't decide whose side to take.

"Then let us settle accounts," Corvane said, and raised one hand, and every candle in the room went out at once.

* * *

Aldric dreamed the throne room before he saw it.

He dreamed it in the language without kingdoms—the child's language, which was also his, which had been his all along, worn down under the crown like a coin rubbed smooth by a thousand thumbs. In the dream there was a garden. There was a girl with mud on her knees laughing at something he'd said, though he could not afterward remember what, or her name, or whether she still lived. There was a door with a knock in it, and a voice reading numbers aloud, and numbers had never sounded so much like singing.

*You are half,* said the dream, in the old tongue. *You have always been half. Come and be whole.*

He woke into cold. His hands were someone else's hands, older, cracked at the knuckles. The crown sat on his brow like a hand pressing down, patient, permanent. Somewhere far away—or very near—a voice was speaking, and every word of it landed in his chest like a key finding its lock.

"—entered into this bargain," the voice said, "on the first day of the first frost, between the Crown of Vey and the Guild of Small Mercies, witnessed and written: that the king should give up half of himself entire—"

"That will do," Corvane said pleasantly, and the dark leaned in.

* * *

The dark did not lean gently. It came off Corvane in sheets, and where it passed, the frost turned black, and the years turned visible—Jonah saw them, hanging in the sorcerer's wake like smoke: decades that were not Corvane's own, harvested from courtiers and servants and, once, horribly, from a girl with mud on her knees who had grown old in a single season and died thanking him for his care.

Stolen years. Corvane wore them the way the king wore the crown.

"You cannot read that page here," Corvane said. "The bargain is sealed. Sealed things stay sealed—that is guild law, keeper. You taught me that law yourself, in a manner of speaking. I read your ledgers for thirty years before you were born."

"Then you know the other law too." Jonah's voice held. He was surprised by how steady it came out, how much it sounded like Sefa's. "The one older than invisibility. *What is written may be read aloud.* All of it. To anyone. Anywhere."

Corvane smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on three generations of frightened people. "And who will hear it? Your guildmaster burned herself hollow holding back your child's winter. Your two remaining elders are guarding a door in a cellar. It's only you, boy. One ledger-keeper with ink on his fingers, against everything I have taken and kept."

"Not only me."

Jonah opened the ledger wider.

* * *

Aldric heard the pages turn, and the sound went through him like a bell through ice.

He knew that book. He had signed its first page—he understood this suddenly, completely, the way the drowning understand water—in another life, under another name, when he had been afraid of something so large he could not look at it and so he had asked clever men to cut it out of him and keep it safe. Half of himself entire. They had told him it would feel like nothing. They had lied, or he had wanted them to lie, and either way the crown had grown into the wound ever since, filling the empty place with ceremony and forgetting, wearing him down year by year the way water wears a stair.

And the half they had cut away had walked out of the guild one frost-night and knocked on a door in a dead language, and some ledger-keeper had opened it.

The voice was reading now. Reading *him*. Every clause of the bargain, every price, every escape clause the clever men had thought to hide—and the child's language rose underneath the common words like a river under ice, and Aldric found he understood both at once, and wept without knowing it, tears freezing on cheeks that felt borrowed.

"—and should the halves reach for one another freely, in witness and in light, the bargain dissolves, and the crown falls, and the throne with it—"

"There is no light here," Corvane said softly. "I made sure of that."

But the ledger began to glow anyway.

* * *

It started as a seam of gold along the spine, the way dawn starts along a mountain. Then the letters themselves caught—not burning, *waking*, each word lifting off the page in threads of light, and Jonah read faster, because he understood suddenly that the words were leaving the page to do their work and he must speak them while they still existed to be spoken.

Corvane screamed something in a language Jonah refused to learn, and threw everything.

The stolen years came at him like weather. Jonah felt them hit the shield of the open ledger—felt decades break against it, lives spent like coins, a whole harvest of other people's time crashing over him in waves of heat and hunger. He staggered. His knees found the ice. He kept reading. His lips split on the cold. He kept reading. Somewhere behind him the child was chanting in the old tongue, high and clear, matching him word for word, and the two voices braided together, ledger-language and kingdom-language, and the ice beneath them began to sing.

"You are *killing yourself*," Corvane snarled, and for the first time there was nothing reasonable in his voice at all. "Every clause costs you. I can see it taking you. Stop, and I'll let the child live. That has always been the offer. It remains the offer."

Jonah looked up at him through the light.

"You've offered me that trade four times now," he said. "And every time, you wrote it down somewhere. You can't help it. Men who live by accounts—" he turned the page— "always forget that accounts go both ways."

* * *

Aldric stood up.

The crown fought him. Of course it did—it had been fighting him for forty years, gently, then less gently, and now with the full desperate strength of a thing about to lose its host. It pressed him down toward the frozen throne with the weight of every ceremony, every decree signed in a fog, every morning he had woken unable to name his own daughter. He felt it grinding at the roots of his thoughts, whispering in Corvane's language: *sit, sit, sit.*

In the middle of the blazing room, a child turned and looked at him.

Not at the king. At *him*. At the half that had been carried off in swaddling and cleverness, standing now in a gray cloak with frost in their hair and Jonah Zhou's steadiness at their back, and the child lifted one small hand across all that distance and all those years, and reached.

Aldric reached back.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than war, harder than grief, harder than the coronation, because the crown poured its whole weight down his arm and turned his bones to lead and told him in his own voice that he would die if he took another step. Perhaps he would. He took the step. The ice cracked under his boot like a struck bell. He took another.

*Freely,* said the ledger-light, in both languages at once. *In witness. In light.*

*Freely,* Aldric answered, aloud, in the language older than kingdoms, and the crown shrieked.

* * *

Jonah read the final clause with blood on his teeth and tears freezing in his lashes, and the last line of the founding bargain left the page entirely and hung in the air above the throne room, written in fire and frost at once:

*Let what was divided be made whole, and let the account close.*

Corvane made one last throw of it—all the stolen years at once, a lifetime of theft hurled like a spear—and the child stepped forward, out from behind Jonah, into its path, and simply *opened their arms*, and the years broke around them like surf around a lighthouse, and the light took them back. Gave them back. Jonah watched decades fly out of the sorcerer like birds released from a snare, streaming toward windows, toward doors, toward the city, going home to whoever they had been taken from, or at least going free.

Corvane fell to his knees among his own black frost, suddenly old, suddenly only as old as he truly was, which was not old enough for what he had done but far too old to outrun it.

"No," he whispered. "The guild is invisible. We are *invisible*—"

"We were," Jonah said, and closed the ledger. "We're done being."

The crown came off.

It did not fall—it *left*, rising from Aldric's brow like a bird from a branch, unraveling as it rose, its gold becoming script, its script becoming light, until there was nothing left of it in the world except a warmth on everyone's face, briefly, like sun through a window. And King Aldric Vey crossed the last ten feet of ice and knelt down—knelt, uncrowned, in front of a child—and the child ran the remaining distance, and they met in the middle, arms around each other, and the frost in the child's hair melted all at once, everywhere in the room at once, a whole winter letting go its breath.

The light did not fade. It settled. Into the walls, into the floor, into Jonah's open hands, into the ledger—which was blank now, every page, blank as snow, blank as a beginning.

Aldric looked up at him over the child's shoulder. His eyes were wet and clear and entirely his own.

"What am I, now?" the king asked. "Without it?"

Jonah, who had spent his whole life writing down what things were, found he had only one honest answer.

"Whole," he said. "And unemployed."

The child laughed—a bright, unbroken sound, both voices in one throat—and outside the frozen windows, far below, the harbor began, at long last, to crack and move and run silver-blue toward the sea.

And in the doorway behind them, breathing hard, leaning on the frame with frost still in her white hair, stood Sefa—who should have been miles away and half-spent, and was instead smiling like a woman who had just won an argument with death itself.

"Ledger-keeper," she said. "You'd better come see what the city is doing at our door."
