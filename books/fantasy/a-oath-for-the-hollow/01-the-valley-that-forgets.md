## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The garden had outlived its gardener's strength, and so Kestrel knelt in the dark loam where Old Wren could not, pulling weeds with fingers that trembled more from grief than cold.

"Left of the well," Wren said from his chair on the porch, his voice a papery rustle. "The feverfew is crowding the rue again."

"They've always hated each other."

"Everything that grows hates its neighbor. That's how you know it's alive." A cough took him then, long and wet, and Kestrel kept his eyes on the soil until it passed. He had stopped looking up during the coughs in the spring. Looking up meant seeing how thin the old man's wrists had become, how his skin had gone the color of candle tallow, and Kestrel could not bear it yet. There would be time enough for bearing it. That was the lie he told himself each morning, and each morning it grew thinner, like Wren himself.

The valley of Nethermere held itself close around the cottage — a bowl of green with one road in and none out but the same road, folded between shoulders of hills so steep the sun arrived late and left early. No one came here. That was the whole of its virtue. When Kestrel had been brought down the switchbacks as a boy, fevered and orphaned and carrying something inside him that burned, Wren had told him the truth of the place in his flat, unhurried way: *Magic cannot follow you here, lad. The valley forgets it. That's why I came, and that's why you'll stay.*

For eleven years, Kestrel had believed him. He had learned to graft fruit trees, to keep bees, to read the weather in the behavior of swallows. The gift he'd carried out of the burning lowlands had gone quiet as a banked coal, and he had come to think of it as a childhood illness, survived.

He was reaching for the last of the feverfew when it happened.

The weed came free of the earth, and with it came a thread of green light — fine as spider silk, rising from the torn root, curling once around his wrist like a question before it dissolved.

Kestrel went very still. A bee droned past his ear. Somewhere down the slope, the chapel bell did not ring, because nothing in Nethermere ever happened.

"Left of the well," Wren said again, mildly, and Kestrel understood that the old man had seen. Had maybe been seeing for days.

He pulled the next weed with his bare hands and felt nothing. Pulled another. On the fourth, the light came again — brighter, a vein of it, and this time it did not fade so quickly. It hung in the air between his fingers like something waiting to be named.

"Wren." His voice came out cracked. "The valley's forgetting how to forget."

The old man was quiet a long while. When Kestrel finally looked up, Wren was watching the hills with an expression Kestrel had never seen on him — not fear, exactly. Recognition.

"Nothing lasts," Wren said. "Not even forgetting. Come inside, lad. There are things I should have told you years ago, and now I'll only have time for half of them."

* * *

Inside, the cottage smelled of dried herbs and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a sickroom that its occupant refuses to acknowledge. Wren directed him to the loose stone beneath the hearth, and Kestrel pried it up with a kitchen knife, expecting coins or letters and finding instead a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: a map, so old its vellum had gone soft as cloth, and a ring of iron, plain and cold, no larger than a wedding band.

The map showed the kingdom — every kingdom, in fact, a dozen of them in faded inks — but one region had been drawn and redrawn so many times the vellum wore thin there. A country whose name had been scraped away and rewritten and scraped again, until the parchment itself seemed tired of holding it. At its western edge, a mark like a keyhole had been inked and reinked, the paper worn to translucence beneath it.

"The Hollow Door," Wren said, when Kestrel asked. "If the stories are true. I never went. I told myself the valley was enough." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Kestrel had ever seen him do. "It was, for a long time. But valleys forget, and kings remember. The writ will come, lad. It's already coming. I can feel it the way you feel weather in an old scar."

"What writ? Wren, what are you talking about?"

"The king has a new god, or the god has a new king; it hardly matters. What matters is that the crown has decided the gifted are a rot in the fruit, and it's begun cutting. They burn the lowlands first, then the coasts. They'll work inward. They always work inward." Wren's hands shook as he poured himself a cup of small beer, and Kestrel moved to help, and was waved off with the fierce pride of the dying. "When it comes, you run. You don't bury me, you don't wait a respectful interval, you don't stand in the yard and grieve like a fool. You take the map and the ring and you go west, and you find the Door, and you do not look back."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are." Wren said it without cruelty, the way he said *the feverfew needs thinning*. "You're leaving me, and you'll hate yourself for it, and then one day, years from now, in some country with a name, you'll plant a garden, and you'll understand that I made you the only promise I could keep: that you'd outlive me by more than a season."

Kestrel wanted to argue. He opened his mouth and found that everything in him had gone to water, and what came out instead was: "The magic came back. In the garden. Why now?"

"Because the valley is dying too," Wren said simply. "Everything forgets, and everything is forgotten. Even this place. Even me." He closed his hand around Kestrel's, around the iron ring, and pressed them both down against the table. "It doesn't follow you here, I told you. I lied. It was always in you, lad. The valley just muffled it, the way snow muffles a bell. And snow melts."

* * *

The writ came three days later, nailed to the chapel door with four iron nails, and Kestrel found it because he was the one sent for lamp oil and had to pass the green.

He knew before he touched it. The whole village knew; he could feel it, the way a flock knows the hawk. A handful of neighbors stood in a loose half-circle before the chapel, not reading, not speaking, just looking at the paper as if it might go off. Widow Marsh had her hand pressed flat over her mouth. The cooper's boy, who could not have been more than ten, was reading it aloud in a stumbling whisper, because no adult would.

*BY ORDER OF THE CROWN, let it be known throughout the shires: that the practice of the gift, in any form, by any person, is hereby declared treason against God and realm. That those harboring the gifted share their crime. That a commission of inquisition is established, with power of search, seizure, and burning. That the names of the guilty will be struck from the rolls of the living.*

At the bottom, beneath the seal — a sun with too many rays, the new god's mark — a line in different ink, added by a different hand:

*The commission rides under Inquisitor Dain.*

Kestrel read the name twice. Three times. The world tilted slightly, the way it does when a stair isn't where your foot expects it.

Dain. Tam Dain, who had shared his bread on the road down into the valley eleven years ago, a boy of twelve with a chipped tooth and a laugh like a door swinging wide, the son of the soldier who had escorted Kestrel and Wren to the border of safety. They had spent one summer together before the soldier moved on and took Tam with him — one summer of fishing the Nether stream, of Tam daring Kestrel to climb the mill roof, of two boys who did not yet know what they carried or what they'd become. Kestrel had thought of him perhaps twice a year since, the way one thinks of a scar: without pain, without much of anything.

*The commission rides under Inquisitor Dain.*

The cooper's boy had stopped reading. The neighbors were looking at each other, and then — one by one, with the terrible instinct of the small and the frightened — they were looking at Kestrel. Eleven years he had lived among them. Eleven years of grafting and beekeeping and being unremarkable. And now, because a name on a writ had turned his face toward them, they saw it. Whatever had always been faintly strange about him. The way his garden grew too well. The way he'd never taken a wife, never gone to chapel more than duty required, never quite belonged.

Widow Marsh lowered her hand from her mouth. "Kestrel," she said, and her voice was gentle, and that gentleness was the worst thing he had ever heard, because gentleness meant pity and pity meant they had already decided. "You should go home now."

* * *

He packed by candlelight while Wren slept, or seemed to. Bread, cheese, the good knife, a flint. The map and the ring went inside his shirt, against his skin. He took almost nothing else. It astonished him, standing in the middle of the room that had been his whole life, how little of a life could be carried in two hands.

"You'll want to leave before the light," Wren said from his bed, without opening his eyes. "The mist comes up the valley at first bell. It'll hold you till the high pasture."

"You'll be alone."

"I've been alone since before you were born, lad. I just had company for a while." Wren opened his eyes then, and they were clear, terribly clear, the eyes of a man who had finished his accounts. "Come here."

Kestrel knelt by the bed, and the old man's hand found his hair, the way it had when Kestrel was nine and fevered and afraid of the dark he'd carried inside him.

"I lied to you once more than I told you," Wren said. "I told you magic couldn't follow you here. I told you the valley would keep you. Both lies, and both the kindest I knew. But here's a truth, free of charge, and you can carry it west: the gift isn't the rot, Kestrel. The gift is the root. Everything else — the crown, the god, the burning — that's the rot. You hold on to the root."

"I don't know how to use it. I never learned."

"No. And that's a grief, but it's also a mercy. They can't make you name what you never learned to name." His hand tightened, sudden strength in it, the last of the banked fire. "Now swear it. Swear you'll go and not look back."

"I swear."

"Liar. You'll look back. Everyone looks back." Wren smiled, and coughed, and waved him away. "Go on, then. Go on. The mist won't wait, and neither will I, and neither, lad — neither will Dain."

Kestrel stood in the doorway a moment longer than he meant to. The cottage looked the same as it had every night of eleven years: the lamp turned low, the herbs hanging from the rafters, the old man a shape beneath the blanket, already turning his face to the wall. It looked the same, and it would never be the same, and he understood that he was looking at the last room in the world where he had ever been safe.

He went out into the dark.

* * *

The mist rose to meet him at first bell, exactly as Wren had promised, pouring up from the valley floor like something breathed out by the earth itself. Kestrel climbed through it with the map against his chest and the iron ring in his fist, and when he reached the high pasture he broke his oath, because Wren had known he would, and looked back.

Nethermere lay below him in the gray, roofed in mist, already half-unreal. The chapel, the mill, the cottage with its thread of chimney smoke. The garden he would not live to see harvested. Somewhere down there, Wren was dying, and Widow Marsh was deciding whether to tell the inquisitors she had ever known him, and the valley that had forgotten his magic was learning, too late, that it could no longer forget anything at all.

He stood until the cold drove him onward, and then he turned west, toward the worn place on the map, toward the mark like a keyhole, and did not look back again.

The road was empty in both directions.

He had been walking an hour when he smelled the smoke.
