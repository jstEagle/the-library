## 2. Ash on the Toll Road

The valley let him go the way a sleeping man lets go of a held breath — slowly, and without knowing it had happened.

Kestrel climbed the sheep track above the orchard terraces until the last chimney of home sank behind the ridge, and then he did not look back, because he had promised himself he would not, because looking back was the first step of every story that ended badly, and Maren — no, that was Aldous, his mentor, dead three days now in the garden he could no longer tend — Aldous had always said the road behind a man grew heavier the longer he stared at it.

The toll road ran east along the spine of the hills, a pale scar of packed clay and old gravel, and for the first hour it was empty in the way roads are empty at dawn: honestly, benignly, with only the mist for company. Kestrel walked with the writ's words nailed into his memory the way they had been nailed into the chapel door.

*By order of the Crown: all persons harboring, concealing, or bearing the unsanctioned gift are to be surrendered to the King's Inquisitors. Mercy is extended to those who confess. Mercy is not extended twice.*

Aldous had read it aloud in his dry, unhurried voice, the way he read seed catalogs, and then he had coughed blood into his handkerchief and said, *Well. It seems the world has finally caught up to what I've been telling you for twenty years, boy. Run east. Magic cannot follow you out of the valley — but men can. Men follow anywhere.*

Kestrel had argued. Of course he had argued. He had argued while packing, argued while standing at the deathbed, argued while the old man's breath went shallow as a bird's, and Aldous had smiled with the half of his mouth that still worked and said the thing Kestrel could not stop hearing now, in the rhythm of his own footsteps:

*The gift was never yours to hide, Kestrel. It was only ever yours to carry. There's a difference, and the difference is a road.*

So: the road.

By mid-morning the mist had burned off and the emptiness of the road had stopped being benign. He passed a shrine at the crossroads where the painted saint's eyes had been scratched out, and he did not like the scratch marks. He passed a milestone whose inscription had been chiseled away, leaving only a smooth blank stone, and he liked that even less. He was beginning to understand that the hunt did not only take people. It took the *evidence* of people — their names on stones, their faces on saints, their histories on milestones — and a country that erased itself as it went was a country that had already decided its people were guilty.

He smelled the waystation before he saw it.

Smoke, but not fresh smoke — the sour, settled smell of a fire two or three days dead, soaked in overnight rain and re-released by the morning sun. The waystation sat where the toll road bent around a hill shoulder: a long, low building of timber and thatch that had once offered beds to drovers and ale to pilgrims and, if the sign was anything to go by, *The Kindly Hearth*, a warmth its charred skeleton now mocked. The roof had fallen inward. The walls stood black and open to the sky like the ribs of something that had been eaten.

Kestrel should have walked past. Every instinct Aldous had ever instilled in him said *walk past, boy, burned things draw the eye and the eye draws the crown*. But there was a cart in the yard, half-burned, and beside the cart — he told himself he was checking for danger, and perhaps he even believed it — there was a boot. A boot with a foot in it. And beside the boot, a hand, and the hand was moving.

He crossed the yard at a half-run.

The survivor was a woman, or mostly a woman; it was hard to tell under the ash. She had dragged herself out of the wreckage and then, apparently, given up on the last ten feet to the tree line, and she lay in the trampled mud with one arm flung over her face. When Kestrel knelt beside her she did not flinch, which frightened him more than screaming would have. Screaming was for people who still believed someone might come to help.

Her eyes opened. They were gray as slate and twice as hard.

"Robbers?" she said. Her voice was rough, but steady.

"No."

"Soldiers?"

"No."

"Then you're either a fool or a crow, and crows don't kneel." She tried to sit up, failed, and let him help her — a concession that seemed to cost her more than the burns did. There was a raw weal across her forearm where a beam had caught her, and her hair, black shot with premature silver, was gray with ash to the roots. "Waystation keeper's daughter did the burning. Or the soldiers did, and told her to say she did. Hard to say which is worse for her."

"Where is she?"

"Gone to her aunt's in Corrow, with a story ready. I taught it to her on the way out." The woman looked at him properly then, and Kestrel felt the look like a hand turning him over to check his underside. "You're from the west. Valley country, by the accent. And you're walking east with a pack and no weapon and the face of a man who's never been lied to by a checkpoint before. Which means you're either very brave or very new."

"New," Kestrel said, because it seemed safer than brave.

"Good. Honest, at least. That'll get you killed slower." She accepted his waterskin and drank like someone who had decided rationing was for the optimistic. "Sable," she said, and then, with a flicker of something that might have been a warning or an apology, "that's the name I'm using today. It changes."

"Kestrel."

"Does yours change?"

"I don't know yet."

Something moved behind her eyes — amusement, or its close cousin — and was gone. "Well, Kestrel-who-doesn't-know. First lesson of the toll road: you don't stop at burned places, and you especially don't stop at burned places to pull strangers out of the mud, because the crown's sweep riders use burned places the way fishermen use chum. They'll be along by midday to see what the fire attracted." She handed back the waterskin. "You've just become the chum. Congratulations."

She got to her feet in stages, swearing with a fluency that suggested a life spent among drovers, and Kestrel saw that she was older than he'd first thought — forty, maybe, with the weathered competence of a woman who had survived a great many things by refusing to be surprised by them.

"You're hurt," he said. "You should rest."

"I should *move*. There's a difference, and the difference is staying alive." She looked at the sky, reading it the way Aldous had read his almanac. "Sweep riders come up from the toll bridge at Vetch. Twelve men, maybe, with a truth-reader on a gray horse and a writ box chained to his saddle. If they find you on the open road, they'll ask you your name, your business, and your mother's maiden name, and the truth-reader will taste your answers like wine. Can you lie?"

"I — I've never had to."

"Then you're dead by sundown, and it was nice knowing you for a quarter of an hour." She was already limping toward the tree line, then stopped, turned, and looked at him with an expression of genuine irritation, as if his impending death were a personal inconvenience. "Well? Come on, then. I'm not going to let them have you just because you're stupid. It sets a precedent."

He followed her. Later, he would not be able to say why — only that in a country where every stone had its name chiseled off, this ash-covered stranger was the first thing in two days that had looked at him and seen something worth the trouble of saving.

* * *

They went to ground in a fold of the hills half a mile north of the road, in a hollow where a stream had undercut a shelf of rock and left a dry pocket curtained by hazel. Sable moved through the undergrowth like a rumor, and Kestrel, valley-born and hill-ignorant, crashed along behind her apologizing to every branch.

"Quieter," she hissed, without turning. "You walk like a man who's never been followed."

"I haven't."

"Everyone's been followed. Most people just never find out." She settled into the hollow, probed her burned arm with two fingers, winced, and produced from some inner pocket of her ruined coat a small clay flask. "Hedge-salve. Don't ask where I got it. Roll your sleeve up — no, not yours, mine, I'm not a *healer*, I just know what hurts."

She dressed the burn herself, teeth gritted, and Kestrel watched the sweep riders arrive.

They came up the road from Vetch in a column of eight, and the sight of them did something to Kestrel's stomach that he had not anticipated: not fear, exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. These were the same kind of men who had bought apples at the valley market, who had danced at the harvest fires. The crown had not sent monsters. It had sent *neighbors*, and that was the whole horror of it, that the machinery of the hunt was made of ordinary hands.

At their head rode a man in gray who was not a soldier. He was lean and quiet in the saddle, and he did not look at the burning the way the soldiers did — with appetite or boredom — but with the weary attention of a man reading a letter from home. When he dismounted at the waystation, he stood in the yard for a long moment with his head slightly bowed, and Kestrel, watching through the hazel, thought: *he is praying*, and then thought: *or apologizing*, and could not decide which was worse.

"That's a truth-reader," Sable breathed. "See the gray horse, the plain harness, the writ box? They taste lies on the air. You don't lie to one with words. You lie with a truth so dull they can't be bothered to check it."

The riders worked the yard for the better part of an hour. They turned over the burned cart. They examined the boot. One of them found the trampled mud where Sable had lain, and Kestrel's heart went up into his throat and stayed there, beating against the back of his teeth.

Then the truth-reader walked to the edge of the tree line — ten yards from the hazel curtain, close enough that Kestrel could see the ink stains on his fingers — and stopped, and stood, and listened to the wood the way Aldous had listened to a sick field.

Sable's hand found Kestrel's wrist and gripped it hard. Her lips moved, shaping words with no sound: *when he looks at you, think about turnips*.

The truth-reader's gaze lifted and swept the undergrowth, unhurried, thorough, and passed over Kestrel like a lantern passing over a wall — and in that instant Kestrel did not think about turnips. He thought about the garden. He thought about Aldous's bean rows and the way the old man had hummed off-key on watering days, and the grief that rose in him was so enormous and so *ordinary* that the truth-reader found nothing in it but a farm boy mourning, which was, after all, the truth.

The gray man turned away. He said something to his sergeant, quiet and tired, and the column remounted, and within a quarter hour the road was empty again except for dust and the smell of horses.

Sable did not relax until the dust had settled. Then she sat back against the rock and laughed — a low, disbelieving, slightly unhinged sound, muffled against her good hand.

"Turnips," she said. "I told you to think about turnips and you thought about *whatever that was*, and it worked anyway. What was that? What did you think about?"

"My mentor's garden," Kestrel said. "He died three days ago. The hunt — the writ — it came the day after."

Sable went still. When she spoke again, the laughter was gone and something more careful had taken its place. "Aldous," she said. "Aldous Wren. The valley hedge-man."

Kestrel stared at her. "You knew him?"

"Everyone in the trade knew Aldous Wren. He forged boundary-blessings for smugglers for thirty years — quiet ones, gentle ones, wards that made a checkpoint officer's eyes slide off a cart. Half the unmarked goods between the mountains and the sea moved under his hedges." She was looking at him differently now, and Kestrel understood, with a lurch, what she was seeing: not a farm boy. An inheritance. "You're his boy. The one he was teaching. Which means you're not just some valley innocent with grief on his face. You're *cargo*, Kestrel. High-value cargo. And I need to know one thing before I decide whether I'm saving you or selling you."

The words hung in the hazel shade between them, and Kestrel found he was not afraid of them, only tired — tired in the marrow, the way he had been tired since the chapel door.

"Ask," he said.

"The sweep that just passed. The truth-reader. Did you look at his face?"

"Yes."

"Did you know it?"

And Kestrel, who had been holding this knowledge at arm's length since the moment the gray man had bowed his head in the burned yard, let it finally arrive.

"Dain," he said. The name came out of him like a splinter coming out of flesh. "His name is Dain. We grew up together. We caught fish in the same river. He carried me on his back when I broke my ankle on the fell, eight miles, and never once put me down." He heard his own voice go flat and strange. "He's my friend."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had lost its edge and gained something almost gentle, which was somehow more frightening.

"Not anymore," she said. "Inquisitors don't have friends. They have *cases*. And you, Aldous Wren's boy, are the case of his life — the crown doesn't send a truth-reader to a border sweep for no reason. Dain asked for this road. Dain *requested* it. Which means somewhere in that gray head there's a file with your name in it, and he's been carrying it for years, waiting." She leaned forward. "So here's what I know, and you can decide if I'm worth trusting: there's a place east of here that the crown can't follow you to. Not won't. *Can't.* A Door, the old stories call it, and I've spent three years and most of my blood chasing the road to it, and I am one pair of working hands and one working lie short of what I need to get there."

She put her ash-gray hand out into the green shade between them.

"You know the valley wards your mentor made. You know how a boundary thinks. I know the roads and the lies and where the crown's eyes are blind. Neither of us survives the other's absence. That's not friendship. That's just arithmetic."

Kestrel looked at the hand. Behind them, far off on the toll road, a hawk turned in the empty sky over the burned waystation, and he thought of Dain at eight years old, solemn and kind, holding a broken boy on his back through the rain, saying *I've got you, I've got you, I won't let go* — and he thought of the same man bowing his head over ashes, and could not make the two of them fit into one face.

He took her hand. Her grip was iron and mud.

"East, then," Kestrel said. "Show me the road to the Door."

Sable smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a woman who had just made a bargain she wasn't sure she could keep.

"First," she said, "we need to find the others. And Kestrel — the others are worse than me."
