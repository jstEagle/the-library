## 3. What the Maps No Longer Hold

Sable had stolen a great many things in her life — silks from Vellish merchants, letters from dead men's coats, a bishop's ring off his finger while he slept in the confessional — but she had never before stolen hope out of thin air and handed it to a stranger. That was what she was doing now, walking east along the old toll road with the boy from the valley at her side, telling him about a door that probably did not exist.

She told herself it wasn't kindness. Kindness got you burned. It was arithmetic: a boy who could do what she'd seen him do at the checkpoint was worth more than any cargo she'd ever run. And yet when she spoke of the Hollow Door, she heard something in her own voice she didn't like — a warmth, a wanting, as if some part of her had been carrying the rumor for years just waiting for someone to tell.

"The crown isn't hunting us because we're dangerous," she said. The road unspooled ahead of them, pale as an old scar, hedged with hawthorn gone feral. "Anyone can be dangerous. A knife is dangerous. No — they're hunting us because we're *evidence*. Every gifted person alive is proof the country used to be something else. Proof it had magic in its bones. And you can't erase a thing while its witnesses are still breathing."

Kestrel walked with his hands jammed in his pockets, shoulders drawn up around his ears like a boy expecting a blow. He hadn't slept. She could see it in the bruised skin under his eyes, in the way he flinched at every crow-call from the hedges.

"So they burn the witnesses," he said.

"They burn everything." She kept her voice flat, cartographer-flat, the way she'd learned to deliver bad news so it sounded like weather. "Villages. Records. Names. There are towns I traded through as a girl that I cannot find anymore. Not ruined — *gone*. I stood on the road where Millhaven should have been and there was nothing there but grass, and no one in three parishes had ever heard the name. Not the innkeepers. Not the priests. The maps have holes in them now, Kestrel. Real holes. You put your finger on the parchment where a town should be and your finger goes cold."

He was quiet for a long while. A kestrel — she noted the irony and said nothing of it — hung trembling in the wind above the hedge, then folded away north.

"And the Door?" he said finally. "You said there's a Door."

"I said there's a *rumor*." She held up a hand before hope could bloom too far across his face. She'd seen hope kill people faster than inquisitors; hope made them walk into open ground. "A rumor with good bones. Smugglers' talk, at first — the kind of story that gets told in cellars after the third bottle. But then people started vanishing over the border and coming back *wrong*, or not coming back at all, and the ones who came back wrong all said the same word. The Hollow Door. An exit, Kestrel. Out of the country entirely. Not across the border — the borders are watched, walled, warded. I mean *out*. Out of the reach of the erasure. Somewhere the crown's writ doesn't run because the country it's written for has ended."

"Where?"

"That's the beauty and the bastardy of it." She smiled thinly. "Nobody knows. It moves. Or rather — it isn't anywhere until it decides to be. The stories say it shows itself along the old boundaries. The first borders, the ones from before the kingdom, before the maps. Standing stones, old barrow-lines, the places where the land itself once drew a line and said *here ends one thing and begins another*. The crown is eating the country from the edges inward, and the Door lives in what's being eaten last." She glanced sideways at him. "The stories also say it doesn't open for everyone. It opens for the found. Someone has to be able to feel where the old boundaries still breathe."

"And you can't."

"I can find anything that's been lost," Sable said, "except the things that want to stay lost. I've tried. I stood on a boundary stone two winters back, outside Corve, and felt nothing. Stone. Cold stone. Whatever used to live in those things, the crown's already killed it there." She let the silence sit a moment, then said the rest, because he deserved it plain: "But you touched that ward-stone at the checkpoint, boy. You didn't even touch it — you looked at it hard, and it sang loud enough to light every witch-sense within a mile. If anything left in this country remembers where the old lines were drawn, it'll remember you."

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a tithe-barn, its roof burned to ribs, its walls still standing like the ribcage of something enormous. Sable built no fire. She sat in the dark doorway with her knees drawn up, watching the road both ways, and listened to Kestrel sleep badly behind her.

It was near midnight when she heard him sit up.

"Sable." His voice was strange. Strained. "There's — I think there's something—"

She was beside him before he finished. In the dark beyond the barn's broken wall, maybe forty paces off, half-swallowed by bramble, stood a stone. She had walked this stretch of toll road a dozen times in her smuggling years and never marked it — just another field-boundary rock, lichen-eaten, cow-ignored. But now, in the moonless dark, it was *glowing*. Faintly. A green-gold shimmer under the lichen, like embers breathing under ash, pulsing slow as a sleeper's heart.

And Kestrel was glowing with it. She could see it — anyone with smuggler's eyes could see it — a faint answering light beneath his skin, at his throat, his wrists, the hollow of his collarbone, flickering in time with the stone. Pulse and counter-pulse. Call and answer.

"Stay here," she breathed, though she was already moving, and he followed her anyway, because of course he did.

Up close the stone hummed. Not sound exactly — a feeling in the teeth, in the breastbone, the way thunder feels before it arrives. When Kestrel laid his palm against it, the glow flared up his arm like ivy growing in fast-forward, and Sable heard him make a small broken sound that was half wonder and half grief.

"It knows me," he whispered. "Sable, it *knows* me. It's like — like my mother's voice, from a house I've never been in. It's saying—" His brow furrowed. "East. It's saying east. And *down*, toward water. And it's afraid. Why is a stone afraid?"

"Because it's the last of its kind," Sable said quietly, "and it can smell the axe coming." She crouched, pressed her own hand flat to the cold granite, and felt — for the first time in her life, on a boundary stone — the faintest tremor. Not for her. Through him. Like holding a tuning fork against a bell. "This is real," she heard herself say, and hated how much her voice shook. "Gods below, Kestrel, it's actually real. You can find it. You can find the Door."

He turned to her in the stone-light, and his face — gods, his face. She had spent her whole life reading faces across card tables and customs desks, and she knew every expression greed ever wore, and this wasn't greed. This was a drowning man shown a rope. Hope, naked and enormous, and it made her chest ache, because she knew — she had always known — what happened to people who became useful.

That was the thought that arrived, cold and punctual, even as she smiled at him: *the moment they learn what you're worth is the moment you stop being a person.* She had watched it happen to others. The gifted girl the river-clan had used as a living compass until her gift burned out and they left her on a sandbar. The fire-touched man the rebels had marched ahead of their columns like a torch. Useful things get spent. That was the whole of the world's oldest law.

And she was doing it too. Right now. With her smile.

"We'll need the others," she said, standing, brushing off her knees, putting the ledger back into her voice. "I told you — there are others. People who know things, who can do things. If we're going east toward water, we'll need Maren's craft, and we'll need eyes that see endings. Meet me at the flooded quarry off the Corve road, four days—"

She stopped.

Down the valley behind them, south and west, the night had changed color. Not dawn — dawn was hours off. This was orange, flickering, rooted to one place. A village burning. Even at this distance she could hear it: a low rolling roar, and under it, thin as thread, voices.

Kestrel saw it too. The stone-light died in his face.

"Is that—"

"Brackenlow," Sable said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The village we passed through this morning. The one with the well and the fat dog." She had counted chimneys there, the way she always counted chimneys — habit, trade-craft, the smuggler's catechism. Eleven chimneys. A chapel. A mill.

Even as they watched, a line of riders broke away from the glow, black shapes against the fire, wheeling north along the road they themselves would have to take. Banners snapped in the wind, and the wind carried up to them a scrap of sound — a horn, three notes, repeated. Sable knew that call. Every runner in the country knew that call. *Quarry swept. Move or die.*

"They're burning it because of us," Kestrel said. It wasn't a question. His voice had gone very quiet, the way things go quiet just before they break. "We slept a mile from there. We bought bread there. That woman gave me—"

"Yes." Sable took his shoulder and turned him away from the fire, firmly, the way you turn a horse from a cliff-edge. "Look at me. Yes. That is what being useful costs, and that is what being hunted means — it means everyone who touches you burns. So you don't get to stand here and carry all eleven chimneys on your back, because if you do, you'll freeze, and freezing gets us both caught, and then it's twelve chimneys." She hated the words even as she said them, hated how true they were. "Move. East. Now. We go cross-country, off the road, and we don't stop till the quarry."

He moved. But as they dropped down from the barn into the wet dark of the fields, running with the firelight stuttering behind them like a heartbeat, Sable caught his face in a flash of distant flame — and saw that the hope was still there. Burned down to coals now, banked, harder. Alive.

And behind the hope, riding north along the burning road, she glimpsed the banner at the head of the riders catch the firelight: a white circle on black, the sigil of the Crown Inquisition, and beneath it a figure who did not ride like the others — upright, unhurried, turning his helmeted head slowly from side to side as he rode, the way a man looks when he is searching for one particular face in a crowd.

Sable had never seen Inquisitor Dain. But she had seen that searching look before, on creditors and jilted lovers and mothers at funerals, and she understood it at once, and her stomach turned over.

They weren't chasing fugitives.

They were looking for someone.
