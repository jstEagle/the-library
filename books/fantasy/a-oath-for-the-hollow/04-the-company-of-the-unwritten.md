## 4. The Company of the Unwritten

The quarry opened beneath them like a wound the land had tried to close and failed.

Sable went first down the switchback path, her boots finding the old cart-ruts worn into the limestone by men two generations dead. Rain had filled the pit to a depth of forty feet, and the water lay green and still as glass in a frame of grey stone. At the bottom, half-drowned, stood the ruins of the works — a crane arm like the rib of some enormous beast, a shed with its roof caved in, and on the shelf of rock above the waterline, a fire.

Around that fire, people.

Sable had learned long ago to read a camp the way other women read letters. She counted them without seeming to: an old woman sitting straight-backed on an upturned crate, hands folded as if in church; a big man in a soldier's coat with the insignia torn away, mending a boot with the patient hands of someone who had done nothing else for years; and two girls, sixteen perhaps, identical down to the soot on their cheeks, sitting so close together their shoulders touched, staring into the flames as if the flames were a book.

"They're here," Kestrel said behind her. His voice had gone strange since the boundary stones had sung to him on the road — a hush in it, like a man walking into a temple.

"They're here," Sable agreed. "Which is not the same as safe."

She had told him almost nothing in three days of walking. That was her way and her trade: information spent like coin, never given where it could be taken. But the stones had hummed under his palm, had *answered* him, and she had watched his face when they did — the awe, the fear, the terrible openness — and she had thought, *he doesn't know what he is worth yet. Good. Neither did I, once, and it nearly killed me.*

"Kestrel Iversen." The old woman rose from her crate. She was small and brown and wrinkled as a winter apple, and her eyes were the pale grey of weather. "Maren of the Hedgerow. I taught Aldous Vane everything he knew, and he taught you everything he knew, which means you are the third hand on a very old tool."

Kestrel stopped as if struck. "You knew my mentor."

"I knew him when he was a boy who cried at the slaughtering of pigs and lied about it after." Her mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "He wrote to me, the last three years. Said his valley had a boy in it with a flicker in him. Said the valley kept it down, kept it quiet, kept it *safe*." Her gaze sharpened. "Said that. Past tense."

"The writs came," Kestrel said. "The hunt. The valley — the valley can't protect me anymore. Nothing can, I think. Except—"

"The Door," said one of the twins.

"The Door," said the other.

They spoke in unison without looking up from the fire, and the sound of it went through Sable like a fishbone caught sideways. She had heard of the twins. Everyone in the smuggling routes had heard of the twins, though no two accounts agreed. They saw endings. Only endings. Ask them about tomorrow morning and they would stare at you blank as stones; ask them about the last day of anything — a life, a war, a country — and they would recite it to you like scripture.

"You're the ones who started the rumor," Sable said, stepping down onto the shelf of rock. "The Hollow Door. I've heard it in four provinces. I've heard it from a one-eyed ferryman and a bishop's cook. I assumed it was a story people told to keep walking."

"It is a story people tell to keep walking," said the left twin.

"It is also a door," said the right.

The soldier looked up from his boot. His face was broad and plain and scarred along the jaw, and when he spoke his voice was the flat, unaccented nothing of a man who had sanded the region out of his own speech. "It's a story that gets people killed, is what it is. I've buried eleven of them. Believers. They walked into crown lines asking after a door like pilgrims asking after a saint's toe, and the inquisitors gave them a different kind of door." He tied off his stitching and bit the thread. "No offense meant to the company."

"None taken," said Sable, "since you're sitting at its fire."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Brann," he said. Then, after a pause, with the air of a man handing over the last of his purse: "That's not my name. I sold my name. It was the price of walking out of the Third Regiment with my skin, and I paid it gladly, and I've been Brann ever since because a man has to be called something."

"You *sold* it?" Kestrel asked. He had gone very still. "To the crown? They can — take a name?"

"They can take whatever we let them take," Maren said quietly. "That is the whole of the war, boy. The rest is marching."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned down to coals and the green water below turned black, and what they talked about was the country.

It was Brann who laid it out, because he had seen it from the inside, from the map-rooms and the registry offices where the unmaking was done with ink instead of fire. "It starts at the edges," he said, drawing in the ash with one scarred finger. "A village here. A river bend there. First they strike the names — burn the registers, rename the roads. Then they strike the records. Then, and this is the part the crown denies and every soldier who's served on the borders knows is true, then they strike the *thing*. You stand where the village was and there's grass there, ordinary grass, and you cannot for your life remember what stood on it. You ask the next village over and they look at you like you've asked them to name the clouds."

"The stars have moved," said the left twin.

"Over the western marches," said the right. "We saw it. Three nights. Constellations with no names, in places where names used to be."

"And the Door?" Kestrel asked. He was hugging his knees like a child, and Sable saw that he did not care who saw it. There was something in that nakedness that both irritated her and, against her will, moved her. "The Hollow Door. What is it, truly?"

Maren answered, and her voice had changed — it was the voice she must have used over sickbeds and birthing-cots for sixty years, low and certain and without ornament. "Every country is a story, and every story has a door out of it. This country's door was never used because no one ever needed it. Now the crown is unmaking the story from the edges in, and the door is the one thing their unmaking cannot reach, because it stands outside the story. It is the last place where this land remembers itself." She looked at Kestrel, and the look was heavy as a laying-on of hands. "Aldous wrote me that the boundary stones sang to you."

"They hummed. I put my hand on one and it — it knew me. It knew the valley."

"The stones are the country's memory," Maren said. "And the crown has been killing them one by one. But a stone will sing for a keeper, boy. There's always been a keeper. There's always been one person the land holds to itself, one pair of hands it trusts with its remembering." She said nothing more. She did not need to. The fire cracked and settled, and everyone at it looked at Kestrel, and Kestrel looked down at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger.

Sable felt the old cold move through her, the smuggler's cold, the cold that said *this one is worth more than you, and worth is danger*. She had pulled him off a checkpoint road three days ago because he was useful, and now he was useful in a way that would get them all hanged, and she found — to her considerable annoyance — that she did not want to walk away from him. She wanted, of all the foolish things, to keep him.

"The Door demands a price," said the right twin suddenly.

"Everything does," said Sable.

"A greater price," said the left. "Than coin."

They would say no more. They never said more; Sable had watched them all evening, and the endings came out of them like water from a crack in a dam, in whatever quantity the crack allowed. But Maren's face had gone carefully still, and Brann's stitching hand had stopped, and Sable understood that the old woman and the soldier had heard this before and had chosen to walk toward it anyway.

* * *

Dawn came up grey over the quarry rim, and the water turned from black to green to silver, and it was Maren who called them together.

She stood at the water's edge with the light behind her, an old woman in a patched shawl, and she looked smaller than she had in the firelight and somehow more formidable, the way a single stone looks small until you understand it is the last one holding up a wall.

"I have buried a husband, three children, and a country's worth of neighbors," she said, without preamble. "I have watched the crown eat the map the way moths eat a coat — from the inside out, patiently, leaving the shape of the thing while hollowing it. I am seventy-one years old. I do not intend to die in a country that has forgotten my mother's grave. So I am going to the Hollow Door. I am telling you this not as an invitation. Invitations can be declined. I am telling it as an oath, and I am asking for yours."

"An oath," Brann said. "I broke the last one I swore. To the crown." He said it flatly, but his hands were not steady. "Why would mine be worth having?"

"Because you know exactly what oaths cost," Maren said, "and you're still standing here. That's the only kind of man worth swearing with."

The twins rose together, one motion in two bodies, and spoke together: "We have seen the ending of the road. We do not see what lies past the Door. We want to see it. That is our oath — to reach the place our sight cannot go."

Sable laughed — a short, hard sound with no humor in it, surprise dressed as contempt. "You understand what you're asking," she said to Maren. "You're asking a smuggler to swear. I've spent my whole life making sure nothing held me to anything. Contracts, borders, oaths — they're all just nets. I cut nets."

"Yes," said Maren placidly. "And yet here you are."

Sable opened her mouth to answer and found nothing in it. Here she was. Three days with a boy who touched stones and they sang, and she had not once considered leaving him behind, and the not-considering had been the whole answer, hadn't it, the thing done before the decision was made. She looked at the water, green and patient in its bowl of stone, holding the sky upside down.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. To the Door. But I'm not dying for any of you, and if anyone slows me past the point of sense, I'm cutting the net. That's my oath, and it's the honest one."

"The honest ones are the ones that hold," said Maren.

Brann stood last. He stood for a long time, a big man making himself bigger by not moving, and then he said, "To the Door," in his flat, nameless voice, and it was somehow the heaviest oath of all.

Kestrel spoke last, because Maren turned to him last, and the whole quarry seemed to lean in.

"To the Door," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word and he did not hide it, and Sable thought: *that's the keeper's voice, the one that doesn't know how to lie.* "To the Door, and to each other on the way to it. My mentor taught me an old words — *the land remembers those who remember it*. I don't know what I am to this land. But I know I'm not going to let it be erased while I can still walk."

They clasped hands over the water, all six of them, an ugly chain of the hunted: a smuggler, a nameless soldier, two girls who saw only endings, a hedge-witch older than the road, and a boy with a flicker in him. And it was in that moment — Sable would remember it precisely, later, when memory was all any of them had left — that the twins went rigid, both at once, like puppets whose strings had been plucked.

The left one said: "Riders."

The right one said: "Behind us, on the toll road. Many."

The left one turned her head, and her eyes found Kestrel, and there was something in her face that was worse than fear, and she said:

"They're not riding for all of us. They're riding for *him*. And they're led by someone who knew him when he was small."
