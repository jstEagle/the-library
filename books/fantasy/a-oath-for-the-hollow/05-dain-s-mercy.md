## 5. Dain's Mercy

The twin did not run.

That was the first thing Dain noticed about her, crouched in the bracken where his riders had flushed her from the quarry road like a hare — she stood up out of the ferns with her hands open and empty, as though surrender were a door she had simply chosen to walk through. Her sister was gone, vanished into the gorse somewhere to the east, and the hounds had lost the scent within a hundred paces, which told Dain everything he needed to know about how the two of them worked. One was always the bait. One was always the prize.

"Name," he said, reining in close enough that the mare's breath stirred the girl's hair.

She looked up at him with eyes the color of river ice, and smiled. "You'll have to catch it first."

One of the riders behind him laughed, then thought better of it. Dain did not laugh. He had learned, in nine years of wearing the gray cloak of the Inquisitorial Office, that the ones who joked were the ones who had already decided what they were willing to lose, and that made them either the easiest or the hardest prisoners he would ever take, depending on the day.

He dismounted. He took her wrists himself, bound them with cord rather than iron — iron was for witches who burned, and he had not yet decided what this one was — and walked her back down the toll road toward the ruined waystation where they had made their camp. Ash still sifted through the rafters when the wind came off the hills, settling on everything like gray snow that refused to melt. His men had strung a lantern under the broken eaves, and in its light the girl's face was young and old at once, the way all the gifted looked to him now, after nine years.

"You knew I'd let them take you," he said.

"I knew you'd want to talk," she said. "Everyone wants to talk before they burn someone. It's the talking that makes the burning feel clean."

Dain sat down on an overturned cart, across the fire from her, and studied her the way he studied all of them: for the flicker, the tell, the small wrongness around the eyes where the gift lived. He found nothing. Either she was better than most, or the gift in her ran so deep it had no surface.

"My name is Dain Corvasser," he said. "Inquisitor of the Third Seal."

"I know." She said it mildly, the way one might say *I know it's going to rain*. "You're the one who knew him when he was small."

The words went into him like a splinter driven under a nail. He kept his face still — nine years of practice — but inside, something turned over, the way a stone turns over in a streambed and shows you the pale underside it has been hiding.

"You know Kestrel Iversen," he said.

"We all do, in a manner of speaking. The twins see endings, not beginnings. But we saw his." She tilted her head, and the lantern light moved across her face. "Or we thought we did. Endings are slippery things. They keep changing their minds about him."

"Then tell me what you saw."

"Why would I?"

"Because the alternative," Dain said quietly, "is that I stop being the man who wants to talk."

The girl considered this with what seemed like genuine scholarly interest. Then she leaned forward as far as the cord would allow, and lowered her voice as though the ash itself might be listening.

"He's going to give himself away," she said. "Whole. That's the ending we saw, the night before your riders came. A door opening, and a boy walking into it, and no one on the far side remembering there had ever been a boy at all." She smiled again, softer this time, and it was the softness that frightened him. "Unless someone changes his mind. Unless someone offers him something better than oblivion."

* * *

Dain wrote the letter three times.

The first draft was official — the seal of the Third Seal, the formal terms, renunciation witnessed before a magistrate of the crown, full pardon under writ. He read it by the fire and heard it in the voice of his superiors, cold and correct, and knew it would be read the same way and refused the same way. Kestrel had never obeyed a voice like that. Not even when they were boys, and the voice belonged to schoolmasters, and obedience cost nothing but pride.

The second draft was honest. *I have been looking for you for four months. I have burned eleven people in that time and slept badly after every one. Come in, Kess. Put it down. Whatever it is, put it down.* He read that one and heard his own heart in it, plain as a plucked string, and understood that honesty, in the mouth of an inquisitor, was just another kind of trap — and that Kestrel, who had always been able to read him since they were children stealing apples off the vicar's wall, would hear the trap in every line.

The third draft he wrote the way you write to the dead: without hope of answer, and therefore without anything left to hide behind.

*Kestrel.*

*I don't know if Sable Vane has told you who leads the hunt, or whether she has let you go on pretending it could be anyone. It is me. It has been me since the winter.*

*I want to tell you that I took this office to protect people like you. That may even have been true once. What is true now is simpler: I am good at this work, and the work does not care what I am good for. Every name on my list is a person I have failed twice — once by not finding them sooner, and once by finding them at all. You are the last name. When yours is crossed out, mine can be too.*

*So here is what I am offering, and I offer it only once, and only because it is you.*

*Come home. Renounce the gift — freely, formally, before witnesses; the land will take it back and the writ will be torn up and no one will ever look at you again. You will be pardoned. More than pardoned. You will be ordinary. You will be able to walk into any town in the valley and buy bread and argue about the weather and grow your mentor's garden until your hands ache, and no one will ever again cross a street to avoid you, or kneel to you, or burn for you.*

*I remember you at eleven years old, Kess, standing in the Iversens' field with your arms out, sure the wind was listening to you. Half the time it was. Do you know what I remember most? That you were happy before you ever knew why. The gift didn't make you happy. It only made you different, and everyone told you different was a burden, and you believed them.*

*Be a boy again. Be nobody's miracle. Come home.*

*Dain*

He read it over twice, sealed it with wax and the Third Seal, and gave it to the twin.

"You'll deliver it," he said. "They won't harm you. They need their oracle back more than they need my blood, and they know taking it would prove every word the crown says about them."

"And if they don't believe me?" the girl said. "If they think the letter's poisoned, or spelled, or baited?"

"Then they're wiser than I am," Dain said, "and it changes nothing. Give it to him. Make sure *he* reads it — not the smuggler, not the hedge-witch. Him."

She weighed him with those river-ice eyes for a long moment. "You love him," she said. It was not a question, and it was not kind.

"I knew him when he was small," Dain said, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it.

* * *

Kestrel read the letter alone.

Sable had wanted to read it first — of course she had; trust was a currency she spent carefully and counted twice — but the twin had done as Dain asked, pressing the folded paper into Kestrel's hands with both of hers, saying only, "From the man who knew you when you were small. He said you'd understand." And Kestrel had taken it and walked away up the quarry slope, out of the firelight, and sat down among the flooded terraces where the water lay black and still as poured glass, and broke the seal.

The others watched him from below. They could not help it. Maren stood with her hands folded into her sleeves, very old and very still. The soldier had turned his back, ostentatiously, the way a man turns his back on something he cannot bear to watch. Sable did not turn away at all. She stood with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and she watched Kestrel read the letter the way a woman watches a fire start in a dry field — knowing exactly how it will spread, and knowing there is nothing in her hands to fight it with.

And Kestrel read.

He read it once quickly, the way you flinch past a wound. Then slowly. Then the middle part again — *you will be ordinary* — and felt it move through him like warm water through a cold body, and hated himself for the comfort of it.

Because that was the thing no one else could understand, sitting safe in their hatred of the crown and their righteous fear of the hunt: that the gift had never once, in all his life, felt like a gift. It felt like weather. It felt like a second heartbeat that beat out of time with his own. Every wonder it had ever shown him had come wrapped in a price — the garden that bloomed out of season, the boundary stones that sang to him, the way animals went quiet when he passed and children stared and doors closed. He had fled his valley not only from the hunt but from *it*, from the flicker he had been promised would never follow him, and it had followed anyway, patient as a debt.

And here was a letter offering him absolution. Not pardon — pardon was the word on the paper, but the word underneath was *absolution*. Renounce it. Put it down. Be the boy in the field who was happy before he knew why. Be Kestrel Iversen, gardener, neighbor, ordinary man, instead of Kestrel Iversen, the last name on a list, the reason eleven people had burned while a friend he loved wrote letters by firelight and slept badly.

He could almost feel it already: the silence in his blood where the other heartbeat used to be. The valley in autumn. The garden. Hands in the soil that made nothing happen, that were only hands.

"Kess." Sable's voice, from the foot of the slope. Careful. Too careful. "What does it say?"

He looked down at them — the smuggler, the witch, the soldier with no name, the twin rubbing her freed wrists — hunted, exhausted, bound to him by an oath sworn in a flooded quarry, and he understood with terrible clarity that the letter was not really addressed to him at all. It was addressed to the boy he had been. And the boy he had been was dead, or nearly, and the thing that remained was holding a scrap of paper that smelled of woodsmoke and Dain's ink, and wanting, God help him, *wanting* —

The water in the quarry pools began to tremble.

Rings spread across the black surface, fine as spider silk, crossing and recrossing, though there was no wind. Below, Maren lifted her head sharply. The twin went pale. And Kestrel realized the trembling was coming from his own hands, that the letter had begun to smoke gently at its edges where his fingers gripped it, that the magic he had fled a whole country to escape was rising in him like floodwater answering some call he had never made —

— and that Dain had known. Had known exactly what the letter would do. Had written *be a boy again* knowing that the wanting itself would wake the gift, that hope was the match and the letter only the striker, and that a Kestrel whose power surged at the mere promise of peace was a Kestrel easier to track across a dark country than any beacon.

Below him, in the dark, on the far side of the water, a rider's lantern kindled — one light, then another, then a dozen, silent along the ridge.

The hunt had not needed the twin to find them after all.

It had only needed him to hope.
