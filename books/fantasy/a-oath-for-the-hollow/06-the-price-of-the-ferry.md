## 6. The Price of the Ferry

The river found them before they found it.

Kestrel heard it first as a wrongness in the air — a hush where there should have been birdsong, a damp that clung to the skin like a second, colder body. Then the trees opened, and there it was: the Vell, drowned past all sense of itself. Where the maps showed a ford and a ferry crossing, there was only brown water stretching to a horizon gone soft with mist, swallowing fence posts to their tops, the roof of a barn breaking the surface like the back of some patient beast. A church spire leaned out of the flood at an angle of quiet surrender, its bell still hanging, silent.

"That wasn't a lake three days ago," Sable said. She stood at the waterline with her boots off, trousers rolled to the knee, and even she — who lied so fluently that lies seemed to be her native tongue — sounded shaken. "I've crossed here a dozen times. There was a ferryman. Old woman, smoked fish, charged too much."

"The crown is unmaking the edges first," Maren said. She had come down the bank slowly, one hand on Tam's arm — the soldier who had no name, who answered to nothing but *Tam* because it meant nothing. Her voice was thin but steady, the voice of someone who had decided something on the walk down and would not be argued out of it. "Water remembers what land forgets. When a place starts going, it drowns before it disappears."

The twins stood shoulder to shoulder at the edge of the reeds, heads tilted in that unnerving unison they had, listening to something beneath the world. It was Wren who spoke — or perhaps Sorrel; Kestrel had learned to stop guessing which. "*It ends here,*" she said softly. "This crossing ends here."

"Everything ends somewhere," Tam said flatly.

"Yes," said the other twin, smiling in a way that made Kestrel's stomach turn over. "But rarely so *politely*."

* * *

They searched for another way for most of a day. Sable scouted upstream until the flood simply went on and on, villages standing waist-deep in their own memories. Kestrel flew low along the banks when he dared — his gift flickering under his skin like heat lightning, dangerous now, visible, the kind of light that drew riders from miles away — and came back each time with the same report: water, water, drowned orchards, a dog swimming in circles around the chimney of its own house, waiting for a family that no longer remembered owning it.

By evening they had returned to the spire. The bell caught the last light and held it, a dull gold eye watching them decide.

"There's the ferry," Sable said at last, without hope. "Or there was."

"There is," Maren said.

She had seated herself on a fallen fence post, hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for bread to rise. Kestrel crouched beside her. Up close he could see how old she truly was — older than his mentor Aldous had been, older than any hedge-witch had a right to be, her face a map of small roads worn into herself by decades of small kindnesses. She had known Aldous. She had taught him, once, in a kitchen full of drying herbs, before either of them had names worth hunting.

"Maren." He didn't know what he intended to say after that. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everything.

"I've been spending my magic my whole life on small things," she said, conversational, as if they were discussing weather. "Curing a cow's fever. Sweetening a sour well. Easing a dying man's pain so his children wouldn't have to watch. Little coins, little purchases. You'd be amazed how long a purse lasts if you never buy anything grand." She looked out at the water. "But this river wants more than little coins. It's not really a river anymore, you understand. It's the country forgetting itself, wearing water's shape. And you can't bribe that. You can only *pay*."

"No," Sable said. It came out fast and hard. She had been quiet through all of this, working a splinter of wood with her knife, and now the knife stopped. "No. We find another crossing. We go around. There's always a way around."

"Not for us," Maren said gently. "Not anymore, girl. You know it. I watched you know it two days ago when you counted our food and thought none of us saw your face."

"We could wait for the flood to—"

"To what? Subside?" One of the twins laughed, a sound like small bells falling downstairs. "It isn't going to subside. It's going to spread. That's what forgetting does."

"Then I'll do it." Kestrel heard his own voice and was startled by it. "My magic. Whatever it takes — I'll pay it. I'm the strongest—"

"You are a candle flame in a high wind, boy, and precious for exactly that." Maren's hand covered his, dry and warm and impossibly strong. "And you are the one who resonates with the boundary stones. The Door will want you whole, or it will not want you at all. No." She squeezed once, then released him, and began, unhurriedly, to take off her shoes. "Besides. I've been meaning to see the far side of this water since before you were born. It seems a shame to leave a debt unpaid."

* * *

They carried her to the water's edge in near silence. Sable refused to speak at all; Tam took off his helm — the plain, anonymous helm he wore like a wound — and held it against his chest, which Kestrel suspected was the closest the soldier could come to prayer. The twins sang. It was the first time Kestrel had heard them sing: a low, wordless thing, two voices braided into one, and he understood dimly that it was an ending-song, the only kind of song they knew.

Maren waded in to her knees and stopped, and turned back to look at them, and she was *smiling*. That was the part Kestrel would carry afterward, longer than anything else. Not the light. The smile.

"Aldous used to say," she called to him, raising her voice over the water, "that magic isn't a possession. It's a promise. You spend it where it's needed, and you don't haggle." The water rose up her thighs, her waist, and did not ripple. Did not touch her, somehow; it parted around her hem as though afraid. "He was right about most things. Stubborn about the rest. Tell him — if you ever stand where I'm standing — tell him the well he sweetened ran clear for forty years."

"Maren—" Kestrel's voice broke on the name.

"Hush now. Watch closely. This is the lesson."

She raised both arms, and Kestrel felt it happen — felt it the way you feel a door open in winter, cold rushing through the house. Maren's magic left her. Not in a flash, not in fire. In a long, slow exhalation, like a life ending naturally, like breath given back to the room. The water went still. Not calm — *still*, in the way glass is still, in the way a held breath is still. From the drowned spire to the misted horizon the entire surface of the flood smoothed into a single unbroken sheet, and in it Kestrel could see reflected every star coming out overhead, though the sun had barely set, stars that were not in tonight's sky at all. Stars from somewhere else. Stars the sky had forgotten.

"There," Maren whispered. She was ankle-deep in stillness now, and walking, and her feet did not sink. "There's a road under this, you know. There always was."

They followed her. They had no choice; the stillness held only where she had already walked, a path of glass laid down a stride ahead of her and dissolving behind. Kestrel walked with his heart hammering, staring down at his own reflection floating among impossible stars, and the drowned village slid beneath their feet — streets, doorsteps, a child's hoop rolling to rest against a fence rail, all preserved in the brown glass like flies in amber. Once, looking down, he saw faces. People standing in their flooded parlor, looking up at the underside of the water, mouths open. Whether they were dead or remembering, he could not say. He made himself stop looking.

Halfway across, Maren faltered.

It was small at first — a hitch in her step, a hand pressed briefly to her sternum. Then the stillness around her feet began to tremble, fine cracks spidering out across the surface like frost on a window, and Kestrel saw what the payment looked like as it was collected. Her hair went white where it had been grey. The map-lines of her face deepened, decade by decade, a life being read backward at terrible speed. She staggered, and Tam lunged forward and caught her arm, and she laughed — actually laughed — and patted his gauntlet.

"Steady, soldier. I'm not falling. I'm *finishing*."

"Don't," Sable said. Kestrel had never heard her voice like that. Stripped of all its silk. "Maren. Stop. Keep some back. Everyone keeps some back, that's how—"

"That's how you end up owing," Maren said. "And this country has enough debts." She straightened, and for a moment — the last moment — she was tall again, and young-eyed, and Kestrel saw the hedge-witch who had taught Aldous in a kitchen full of thyme and woodsmoke. "Listen to me, all of you, because I'll only say this once and I mean it as a mother means it. The Door won't take coin. It won't take blood, it won't take years, it won't take your magic alone. Those are little coins, and the Hollow is past bribery. It wants *you*. A self, given wholly — freely — with nothing kept back. Your name, your gift, your memory, your love, all of it poured out like water, and the land drinks and opens." Her eyes found Kestrel's and held them, and he could not look away, and did not want to. "One of you will understand this, when the time comes. I think I already know which. Don't be afraid. Being spent is not the same as being lost. Ask the well. Ask the cows. Ask any soul I ever eased."

The far bank was close now, close enough to see the grass, real grass, ordinary and blessedly unflooded. Maren turned toward it. She took one step, and another, and with the third step the stillness gave out entirely beneath her — not cracking, but simply ceasing, the way a note ceases — and the water took her the way the country takes everything: quietly, thoroughly, without malice.

She did not struggle. The last thing Kestrel saw was her face just below the surface, tilted up toward them, still smiling, growing fainter, growing less — not drowning but *unremembering*, the lines of her smoothing away like writing in rain — until the river closed over the place where she had been, and there was only water again, moving, indifferent, and the four of them standing on the far bank soaked to the ribs, holding each other upright.

Sable screamed. It was short, and raw, and Kestrel knew it was the only true thing she had said in his presence.

Tam put his helm back on.

The twins took each other's hands and said, together, in perfect unison: "*Now we know the price.*"

Kestrel knelt at the waterline. His magic was rising in him uncontrollably, grief-light leaking from his fingertips into the mud, and he let it rise, because hiding it suddenly seemed obscene. Somewhere behind them, downstream, a horn blew — twice, then twice again. Rider-signals. Dain's riders, closing on the crossing, drawn perhaps by the very light he was bleeding into the ground.

He did not care. He knelt in the mud and let himself glow like a dying star, and made a vow to the water, to the country, to the old woman who had bought a river's silence with everything she was:

*I understand now. I understand what it costs.*

*When the time comes, I will not keep some back.*
