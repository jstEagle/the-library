## 7. A Kingdom Eaten from the Edges

The soldier had walked out of the war with no name, and he meant to keep it that way.

He thought of himself as *the soldier*, and when the others needed to call him something they said *Corporal*, which was not a name but a rank, and ranks could be shed like a coat at season's end. He had shed his true one eleven years ago, in a mud cellar outside Vessany, and he told himself every morning that what he'd done there was survival, nothing more. He almost believed it by now.

Almost.

They were three days out of the drowned river where old Maren had stilled the water with her life, and the land was coming apart around them in ways even the soldier's long service could not explain away. He walked rearguard, as he always did — habit outlasting armies — and so it was he who first noticed the road.

It was the same road.

He stopped, boots planted on packed clay gone soft with autumn rot, and looked back the way they'd come. There, fifty paces behind him: the leaning milestone with its crown worn to a smear, the split oak struck by lightning generations ago, the crooked fence rail he'd stepped over not an hour past. Ahead of them, half-hidden by fog off the fields: the same milestone. The same split oak. The same crooked rail, waiting for his boot again.

"Company," he called quietly, because quiet carried further than shouting and drew less attention. "We have a problem."

Sable came back along the column first, quick as spilled water, her smuggler's eyes already reading the road the way she read ledgers and faces. Kestrel followed, and behind him the twins — Vesper and Wren, or Wren and Vesper; even after weeks he couldn't tell, and suspected they rearranged themselves deliberately — drifted up trailing their strange, ending-hungry silence. They had been quieter since Maren died. Quieter since one of them had been taken and returned hollow-eyed from Dain's camp, speaking only in fragments about doors and prices.

The twins stopped at the milestone and spoke together, as they sometimes did, their voices overlapping like two bells struck slightly apart.

"We have seen this hour before," said the left one.

"It ended badly," said the right.

"That's not an answer," Sable snapped. "That's weather reporting."

"It is both," they said, and would say nothing else.

Kestrel knelt at the milestone and pressed his palm flat against the stone. The soldier watched him do it and felt the hair rise on his arms, because the air around the boy's hand shimmered faintly, like heat off a forge though the morning was cold. Magic. The valley-boy hid it poorly and apologized for it constantly, but here — this close to whatever the Door was — it came off him like smoke through a cracked shutter.

"The stone remembers walking," Kestrel murmured. "Or we remember walking. I can't tell which. It's like—" He frowned, searching for words. "Like a word repeated until it stops meaning anything."

"A loop," the soldier said. His voice came out flatter than he intended. "I've seen roads loop before. Near the borders. Near places being unmade." He hadn't, actually. Not like this. But he knew the theory, had heard drunk cartographers talk about it in garrison canteens: the crown's great erasure did not simply delete. It *wore*. A country eaten from the edges grew thin there, and thin things fold back on themselves.

Sable stared at him. "You've seen this and you're only mentioning it *now*?"

"I've heard it described. There's a difference, and I'm telling you I've never stood inside one." He gestured down the road, where the fog was thickening, swallowing the second milestone, then the third. "The way through a loop isn't forward. It's remembering. Someone has to hold the road in mind, all of it, every step, and walk it exactly as it was walked the first time. Any deviation—"

"Any deviation?" Kestrel asked.

"You get folded into the loop. Permanently. You become part of the scenery." He nodded at the split oak. "Ask the oak how it got that way. Maybe someone deviated."

Nobody laughed. That was fine. He hadn't meant it as a joke.

* * *

They walked the loop four times before anyone admitted it was hopeless.

The trick, the soldier explained as best he could, was to fix the first crossing in memory — every footfall, every breath, the exact weight of each pack strap — and repeat it without variation. Sable was good at it; smugglers lived by exact repetition of routes. Kestrel was better than any of them expected, walking with his eyes half-closed and his lips moving silently, counting. But on the fifth circuit the fog thickened, and somewhere in the white a rooster crowed at dawn although it was nearly noon, and Wren — or Vesper — broke stride to look toward the sound.

The road shivered. The milestone appeared ahead of them, closer than it should be, and the fog smelled suddenly of woodsmoke and blood and something older, like a temple sealed for centuries.

"Stop," the soldier breathed. "All of you. Nobody moves."

They froze in the white. And in the silence, the soldier heard it — a sound he had hoped never to hear again, thin and sweet and wrong: a fife playing a marching tune. An army tune. One that had not been played anywhere in twenty years because the regiment it belonged to had been unmade, its name struck from the rolls, its dead struck from the graves, its very flag-fabric unpicked from history.

"Vessany," he whispered.

"What?" Sable hissed.

"Nothing. Keep still. It can't take what doesn't move."

But the twins were moving. They turned slowly, faces tilted into the fog, and the left one said, "There's a village in there."

"There is no village," the soldier said. "That's the point. Whatever's in there is what's *left*."

"There's a village," the right one insisted, gentle and terrible, "and it is having its last day. We see endings. This one is today."

And Kestrel, damn him, took a step toward it.

"No," the soldier said, grabbing his shoulder — and the moment his fingers closed on the boy's coat the fog parted, just for a heartbeat, just enough.

He saw the village.

He saw a market square with stalls hung with onions and dyed wool, a well with three women drawing water, children chasing a hoop between the houses. He saw it all rendered in colors slightly too bright, slightly too warm, like a painting remembered by someone who loved it. And he saw, standing in the doorway of the shrine-house, a figure in grey robes with the crown's white circle stitched at the throat, holding a ledger, reading names aloud in a voice like dry paper tearing.

With every name, something in the village dimmed. A woman set down her water jug and forgot she had ever drawn water. A child stopped mid-chase and became a smear of color, then a patch of ordinary afternoon light. The reader worked methodically down his list, and the village came apart around him not in fire but in *forgetting*, gently, the way snow melts.

"They don't burn them anymore," the soldier heard himself say. His voice sounded very far away. "Burning leaves ruins. Ruins leave questions. They read the places out of existence now. Cleaner."

"How do you know that?" Sable asked. "How do you know *any* of that?"

And there it was. The question he had been dodging since the quarry, since the oath, since Maren smiled herself to death on the black water. It hung in the fog between them, and the twins turned their identical faces toward him, and even Kestrel went very still, the way prey goes still.

"Because I helped write the lists," the soldier said.

* * *

The fog held them all in place, as if the land itself wanted to hear this.

"Eleven years ago I was a quartermaster's clerk attached to the Third Erasure Commission. Not a soldier — a clerk. I counted wagons. I copied manifests." He heard how reasonable he made it sound, how practiced, and hated the sound. "The manifests had columns. Wagons, grain, salt, ink. And names. The names of people in the districts scheduled for reading. The Commission paid a bounty for verified names — full name, parentage, birth-village — because half-names don't erase clean. They need the whole thread to pull it."

"Where did the names come from?" Sable asked. Her voice was ice over deep water.

"From informants. From parish rolls. From—" He stopped. Started again, because there was no version of this that didn't end where it ended. "From me. I grew up in those districts. I knew everyone. My own mother sold me to the Commission when I was fourteen for pointing out that our neighbor harbored a gifted girl. I learned early that names are currency, and I spent mine, and then I spent everyone else's."

The silence was total. Even the fife in the fog had stopped.

"Vessany," Kestrel said quietly. "You said Vessany. What happened at Vessany?"

"Four hundred and six souls. I provided two hundred and ninety-one of the names. Verified. Complete threads." The soldier found he could not look at any of them, so he looked at the bright false village instead, at the woman who no longer knew what jugs were for. "Afterward I deserted. I cut my name out of myself — there are ways, if you're willing to bleed for it — and I've been running since. Running toward the Door, I told myself. Toward freedom. But I think some part of me knew. Some part of me understood that the Door wants a self given freely, and I have been carrying the worst self in this company straight to the altar of it."

"You used us," Sable said. "Every mile, every campfire, every watch rotation you stood — you were mapping us. Learning our names. Our *parentage*. So you could sell us if it kept you alive another week."

"Sable." Kestrel's voice, low.

"Don't *Sable* me, valley-boy. Old Maren died for this company. She spent everything she had, and this man spent *us* a long time ago, we just hadn't received the bill." Her knife was out. He hadn't seen her draw it. Smuggler-quick. "Give me one reason."

"One reason," the soldier agreed, and did not reach for his own blade, because what would be the point. "Is that I'm the only one who knows how the reading works? Is that I'm the only one who knows Dain's commission schedule, his route, the fact that he reads villages in order of their gift-density and yours will be next? No. Those are reasons for keeping me alive, and they're coward's reasons. Here's the real one."

He turned and faced the village in the fog, the last day of it, the grey-robed reader working down his ledger.

"That man is reading my mother's village. Not mine — hers. She sold me to save herself and they read her anyway, three years later, incomplete thread, sloppy work. Everything she was went into a ledger with my handwriting somewhere near it." His voice cracked and he let it. "I want to walk into that fog and tear that reader's throat out with my hands. And I can't. Because if I leave this road I fold into the loop, and if I die here I die a clerk, and the only thing I have left that isn't the Commission's is this oath I swore in the quarry beside people who should never have trusted me. So yes. Sell me to the crown. Cut my throat here. But know that whichever you choose, you'll be doing exactly what the Commission taught us all to do. Spending a person to buy safety."

The knife did not waver. Sable was not a woman who bluffed.

It was Kestrel who moved.

Later — in all the later that followed — the soldier would try to describe what he saw, and fail. The boy stepped between the knife and the clerk, and the magic that had been leaking out of him for days, smoke through a cracked shutter, came through the shutters *all at once*. It rolled off him like a change of weather. The fog lit from within, gold and green, the colors of the valley he'd fled. The milestone under his hand sang — actually sang, a low harmonic like a wet finger on glass — and every stone down the length of the looping road answered it, and the split oak put out one impossible leaf in the dead of autumn.

"Put it away, Sable," Kestrel said, and his voice had harmonics in it too. "Not because he deserves mercy. Because we are standing in a country being erased, and we are the only ones left who remember it, and if we start spending each other we are doing the Commission's work for free."

The knife stayed out a moment longer. Then Sable sheathed it, slow, deliberate, and spat into the fog. "Maren would have cursed him. Something lingering."

"Maren's dead," Kestrel said, "because she chose to spend herself for us. That's the difference. Choice. He gets to make his, once, at the Door. Until then he walks with us and carries it." He turned to the soldier, and his eyes were still glowing faintly, and the soldier understood that he was not looking at the valley-boy anymore. "Do you hear me? You carry it. All of it. Every name."

"Yes," the soldier said. "Yes. I swear it."

"He does carry it," one twin said softly.

"We have seen him carrying it," said the other. "For a very long time. It ends at a door."

"Enough endings," Kestrel said, and raised his hand, and the fog began — slowly, grudgingly, like an animal denied — to pull back from the road.

What it revealed stopped every heart in the company.

The village was gone. Of course it was. But the road beyond the loop ran straight now, clear and cold under a sky that had shifted while none of them were looking — the stars were wrong, the constellations slid a full hand's-width east of where they'd been the night before, and Sable made a small sound of horror when she saw it, because smugglers navigate by stars and hers had betrayed her.

And there, on the clear road ahead, planted upright in the earth like a fence post, stood a single white circle on a pole. The crown's mark. Freshly painted.

And beneath it, weighted with a stone against the wind, a letter. Addressed in a hand the soldier recognized from a hundred Commission documents — precise, educated, merciless — to *Kestrel Iversen, of the valley that forgets.*

Kestrel stared at it a long time before he moved to pick it up.

"Inquisitor's seal," the soldier said quietly. "Dain's own hand. I've seen his correspondence." He hesitated. "Kestrel — I know what those letters contain. I wrote the manifest for one like it, years ago. Pardons. They work, that's the horror of them. Read it, and be careful what you wish you could go back to."

The boy broke the seal.
