## 1. Charms for Copper

The trouble with selling luck was that people expected it to look like something.

Paloma Galloway had learned this the hard way, twenty years ago, when she'd made her first sale and the fisherman had held the knot up to the light like a jewel he suspected of being paste. "What's in it?" he'd demanded. "Bone? Ash? A saint's tooth?"

"Cord," she'd said. "And patience."

He'd bought it anyway, because his nets had been empty for a month and a man that desperate will pay copper for anything shaped like hope. And his nets had filled — not miraculously, not glowing, just filled, the way they might have anyway if the weather turned kind — and he'd told everyone, and Paloma had learned the second great truth of her trade: never let them see you work.

So now, on a grey morning in Brackwater with gulls arguing overhead about who owned which rooftop, she sat behind her little table at the mouth of Cooper's Lane and let her hands do all the talking. Her fingers moved through the knot-work without her mind's permission — over, under, through, cinch — three strands of waxed cord braided into a pattern that meant nothing to anyone watching and everything to the tide.

"Morning, Mistress Galloway." Old Marta Tull came shuffling up the lane with her basket of mending, as she did every third day, though she hadn't bought anything in two years. She liked to watch the knots. Said it reminded her of her late husband's hands, which was either very sweet or very sad depending on how much wine she'd had.

"Morning yourself, Marta. You're early."

"The gulls woke me." She set down her basket and leaned on the table, peering at the half-finished knot with the critical eye of a woman who had darned forty thousand socks. "That one's crooked."

"It's not crooked, it's *asymmetric*. Luck doesn't travel in straight lines."

"Luck doesn't travel at all. That's why it's luck." But she smiled when she said it, the gap-toothed smile that had outlasted two husbands and one fire, and Paloma smiled back, because that was the arrangement: Marta watched, Paloma pretended not to notice how lonely the watching made her.

A widow came by then — young, red-eyed, smelling of the fish-works — and stood at the edge of the table wringing her cap in her hands.

"You're the charm-woman," she said. Not a question. In Brackwater, everyone knew where the charm-woman sat, even if officially there was no such thing, even if officially Paloma sold "cordwork and keepsakes, purely decorative, no promises made or implied," a phrase she'd had painted on her sign in letters so small you needed faith to read them.

"I'm the cordwork woman," Paloma said mildly. "Keepsakes. Decorative."

"My husband's boat went out Tuesday." The widow's voice cracked down the middle like dropped slate. "They found the boat. They didn't find him. The harbor-master says wait. His mother says mourn. I want—" She stopped, swallowed. "I want to do *something*. Is there something?"

There was always something. That was the whole curse of it.

Paloma looked at the cords on her table, at the knot half-born under her fingers, and felt the old familiar itch beneath her skin — the gift stirring like a cat that hears the pantry door. Because here was the secret nobody in Brackwater knew, the secret she kept folded smaller than any knot: the cords were nothing. Pretty, well-made, honestly priced, but nothing. What worked was the word. A swordspeaker could bind an oath into an object, speak her promise into the weave until the weave remembered it, and the thing would hold — against storm, against sorrow, against whatever it was sworn to hold against. One spoken sentence, true and meant, and the luck-knot became luck itself.

One spoken sentence was also, these days, the difference between a shopkeeper and a pyre.

She had not spoken a working oath in twenty years. Twenty years of keeping her voice flat and her craft dumb, of letting the knots be only knots, of telling herself that a small kindness done quietly was worth more than a great power done loudly. Some days she believed it.

"What's his name?" she asked the widow.

"Bren. Bren Oduya."

"And yours?"

"Sefa."

"Well then, Sefa. Here's what I can do." She took up a fresh length of cord, sea-green, and began to work it as she talked, letting the rhythm of the braid settle them both. "I'll make you a mourning-knot. Three strands — one for him, one for you, one for the sea between. It won't bring him back; I don't sell lies, they cost too much to keep. But it gives your hands somewhere to go, when the waiting gets bad. Grief needs a job. This is a job."

Sefa stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a wet, broken laugh, but a laugh. "*Grief needs a job.*"

"It's true. Ask anyone who's buried somebody. The worst hours are the ones with nothing to do but feel it." Paloma looped, cinched, drew tight. "Copper. Two bits if you've got three."

"I've got two."

"Then two, and you owe me a story someday about how he proposed. Widows always have good proposal stories. It's the one payment I insist on."

Sefa paid her two coppers and took the knot and held it the way people hold things they're afraid to believe in, and Paloma watched her go and thought, as she thought every time: *that one almost counted. That one would have been worth speaking for.*

But she hadn't spoken. The knot was cord and patience, nothing more, and Sefa Oduya would find her own way through, the way people did, the way they always had, back before the purges, back before the country decided its own magic was a disease to be cut out —

Back before Paloma had learned to be small.

*Twenty years,* she told her hands, which went on knotting without sympathy. *You've managed twenty years. Don't get sentimental now.*

* * *

Midmorning brought the sun out grudgingly, and with it the crowd, and with the crowd the ordinary commerce of a city that didn't know it was living its last ordinary week.

A dockhand wanted a strength-knot for his bad shoulder — "decorative," Paloma said, "stretch more, lift less" — and paid her extra for the advice, which she found darkly funny. Two children bought friendship-cords with coins pooled from a week of errands, and she threw in a third strand free, "for whoever you haven't met yet," and they ran off arguing about whether that was generous or a swindle. A man in a merchant's coat examined her wares for a long time without buying, asked twice whether her goods were "registered," and left when she blinked at him with perfect, practiced ignorance.

By noon her coin-box had a pleasant weight to it and her throat had that faint dry ache it always got from talking all day without ever once saying the thing that mattered.

Marta was still there. Marta was always still there.

"You know," the old woman said, watching Paloma's hands fly through a knot she wasn't really thinking about, "my grandmother used to say her mother could tie a knot that would hold a boat to its mooring in a hurricane. Just cord. No anchor. She swore it."

"Mm," said Paloma, in the tone of a woman hearing charming nonsense.

"She said the gifted used to do that. Before the burning times." Marta's eyes, milky at the edges but sharp at the center, came up to Paloma's face. "Tied their oaths right into things. So they say."

"So they say," Paloma agreed, and reached for another cord, and her heart was beating very fast and very quietly, the way it had learned to.

Because here was the other thing about Marta Tull, the thing Paloma had never said aloud in fifteen years of buying her bread and mending her shawls and listening to her stories: Marta knew. Had probably known from the first week. Knew and had never once breathed a word, not to the magistrates, not to the neighbors, not even drunk, not even angry. In a city full of informers-in-waiting, Marta had simply decided that the charm-woman was no threat to anyone, and had carried the knowledge around for fifteen years like a stone in her pocket, heavy and harmless.

"Why do you keep coming here, Marta?" Paloma asked suddenly. "You never buy anything anymore."

The old woman considered this. "Company," she said at last. "And someone ought to keep an eye on you. Someone has to remember you were brave, if it ever comes to it." She patted the table. "I'm old. My memory's the last thing working proper. Might as well spend it on something."

Paloma opened her mouth to answer — with what, she didn't know, some mundane deflection, some joke about memory and herring — and that was when the bells started.

Not the harbor bells. Not the chapel bells, which rang honest and plain. These came from the magistrate's hall, deep bronze, arrhythmic, urgent, the sound Brackwater heard perhaps once a decade and always dreaded. Every head on Cooper's Lane turned toward the square. The gulls went up in a ragged white sheet.

"Oh," said Marta softly. "Oh, what now."

They got their answer within minutes, because the criers were already moving through the streets, and the first of them came up Cooper's Lane at a walk that wanted to be a run, unrolling his proclamation with a voice trained to carry over storms:

*"By decree of the Crown! Let all hear! The Edict of Ash is proclaimed throughout the realm!"*

The lane went silent. Even the fishwives stopped mid-haggle.

*"Every person possessed of hidden gifts — of workings, bindings, charms beyond common craft — shall present themselves to the registry of the magistrate's hall within thirty days, to be named, numbered, and marked. Those who register shall be taxed and tolerated. Those who conceal themselves past the thirtieth day shall be sought, and being found, shall burn."*

*A pause, professionally timed.*

*"Harboring the concealed is itself concealment. Informers will be rewarded. The Crown is merciful to those who come forward, and merciless to those who make mercy necessary. Thus speaks the Crown. Thus it shall be."*

The crier moved on. The lane stayed silent a moment longer, the way a room does after a door slams, everyone privately checking that everyone else is still breathing.

Then the noise started — low, everywhere, the sound of a city recalculating. Somewhere a woman said "thirty days" like a prayer. Somewhere else a man laughed, high and wrong. The dockhand with the bad shoulder walked past Paloma's table without looking at it, which he had never once done in six years, and Paloma understood that she had just become, in his eyes, either a criminal or a liability, and possibly both.

Marta didn't move. Marta sat with her basket in her lap and her hands folded on it, looking at Paloma with those milky-sharp eyes, and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of speech.

"Marta—"

"Don't," the old woman said. "Whatever you were going to say, don't. And whatever you're going to do—" she paused, and her voice went gentle and iron at once, "—do it cleverly. Promise me cleverly. Not bravely. Brave gets you burned. Clever gets you out."

"I don't need—"

"You need exactly that and nothing else." Marta rose, joints crackling like green wood, and gathered her basket. At the edge of the table she stopped, and looked down at the half-finished knot under Paloma's frozen hands, and said, quietly enough that only Paloma could hear it: "My grandmother's mother tied oaths into cord. I told you my memory works. Use me while I do."

And she shuffled off down the lane, small and straight-backed, leaving Paloma alone at her table in the loud, frightened afternoon.

* * *

It happened an hour later, and it happened fast, the way terrible things do — no warning bell, no crier, just a change in the texture of the crowd, a rippling outward from the square like water around a dropped stone.

Paloma packed her table. She told herself it was because of the wind coming off the harbor, which was true the way most lies are true. She was coiling her cords into their basket with hands that wouldn't quite steady when the shouting started, and then the running, and then she was in the street with everyone else, pressed shoulder to shoulder at the mouth of the square, watching.

They were dragging old Wenna Corrigan across the cobbles.

Wenna. Everyone's Wenna. The herbwoman who had pulled teeth and delivered babies and brewed the cough syrup half of Brackwater had been raised on, who charged poor folk in eggs and rich folk in apologies, who was seventy-one years old and walked with a stick and had never in her life harmed anyone except, briefly, a turnip. Paloma had known her since girlhood. Half the knots on Paloma's table were wound on thread Wenna had spun.

Four men of the new hunt-wardens had her by the arms, and she wasn't resisting, exactly — she was too old to resist and too proud to beg — but she was talking, talking steadily, reasonably, the way she talked to feverish patients, as though the whole square were one patient and she could talk it out of what it was doing.

"—registered, yes, gladly, take me to your hall, I'll sign whatever—"

"The hall's closed for the day," one of the wardens said, and his voice had the flatness of a man reciting something he'd been given, and Paloma's stomach dropped through the cobbles and into the harbor, because she knew what closed-for-the-day meant, she knew what the second cart at the edge of the square was for, she knew why there was straw in it.

"They're not waiting thirty days," whispered the fishwife beside her. "Look at the cart. Look at the *straw*."

Wenna saw the cart too. Paloma watched her see it — watched the herbwoman's steady stream of reasonable words falter, watched her understand, watched her do the arithmetic every gifted soul in the realm was doing at that moment, the sum of thirty days against a cart already loaded.

Her stick clattered on the stones. One of the wardens picked it up and tossed it aside.

"No," Wenna said then, and her voice changed, went thin and high, and the square, which had been holding its breath, finally heard what it was looking at. "No — I *delivered* you, Tam Wilder's boy, I pulled your tooth, Joss, I sat up three nights with your mother, Annela, you know me, you all *know me*—"

"We know you," someone said from the crowd, miserably, uselessly.

Nobody moved. Paloma's hands were white on the rim of her basket. She was twenty feet away and it may as well have been twenty miles, because between her and the cart stood four armed men and a hundred neighbors who had decided, in the space of an hour, that survival was simpler than courage — and she could not blame a single one of them, and she hated every single one of them, and both of those things were true in her chest at once like two knots pulling opposite ways.

They put Wenna in the cart. They put the straw over her. The cart did not leave yet — there were formalities, a magistrate's clerk with a ledger, a name to be entered, *numbered and marked*, the proclamation said, and the clerk wrote while the square watched and the gulls screamed down at all of them like the world's smallest, cruelest jury.

Paloma stood in the crowd and did nothing, because doing something meant dying, and dying meant the registry stayed where it was, and the registry — she had heard the rumors all week, whispers in the market, a clerk's loose tongue over ale — the registry held names. Every name. Every gifted soul in the realm, written down in the crown's own hand, including children she had taught, including—

Including, she realized with a cold drop, quite possibly her own.

*They'll have a name for you,* Marta's voice said in her memory. *Someone has to remember you were brave.*

The cart rolled. The crowd parted for it the way crowds part for carts, smoothly, shamefully, closing again behind it like water, like nothing had happened, like a hole healing over.

Paloma Galloway walked home the long way, by the water, where the wind could get at her face and hide what it was doing. She passed her table's empty spot on Cooper's Lane. She passed the chapel, where the honest bells were ringing evening now, indifferent and clean. She climbed the stairs to her room above the ropewalk and shut the door and stood in the dark listening to the tide, and let herself shake, quietly, thoroughly, the way she hadn't let herself shake in twenty years.

Thirty days. The whole realm had thirty days to decide what it was.

And somewhere across the city, in the magistrate's hall, in a locked cabinet in a clerk's office, lay a book with names in it — and among the names, if the whispers were true, a column of children, ages seven to twelve, marked with a symbol she recognized because she had invented it, years ago, as a private shorthand in her lesson-books, never dreaming it would outlive her caution.

Paloma lit no lamp. She sat on the edge of her narrow bed in the dark, and her hands, which had knotted all day, went on moving now without cord, over and under, through and cinch, tying nothing, practicing the shape of a decision she hadn't made yet.

Below her window, a patrol went by, torchlight sliding along the wall. Boots. More boots than Brackwater usually had.

"Thirty days," she said aloud, to the dark, to the tide, to nobody. "Cleverly, Marta said. Not bravely."

The tide answered, the way tides do, by coming in.
