## 2. The Registry

The magistrate's hall of Brackwater had been built by men who wanted to be remembered as important, and so it was tall, and cold, and full of stone that echoed. Paloma had sold charms to half the clerks who worked there—little knots against headaches, against ink-stained fingers, against the particular melancholy of copying the same tax rolls for thirty years—and she knew its bones the way she knew her own.

She also knew, from a clerk named Odwin who talked too much when he'd had his third cup, that the registry lived in the warden's office on the second floor, in an iron cabinet with a lock "old enough to have opinions."

Paloma had opinions about locks too. She'd been picking them since she was nine, back when her teacher Sefa had called it *sleight of hand* and smiled in a way that meant *this too is a kind of oathkeeping: what you open quietly, no one has to burn for.*

That had been before the burning started again.

She went in through the records annex, because the annex had a window over the coal store whose latch had been broken since the flood two winters past, and because nobody guarded paperwork. Nobody ever guarded paperwork. The crown could post soldiers at every gate in Brackwater and it would still forget that the true treasure of any tyranny is filed alphabetically.

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and tallow and old ink. She waited by the door for her eyes to adjust, counting her breath the way she counted knots. One for courage. Two for caution. Three for the widow Marta, who had pressed a copper into her hand that morning and said, *Cleverly, girl. Not bravely.*

"I'm being clever," Paloma whispered to the dark. The dark, politely, did not laugh.

* * *

The stairs were the problem. They always were. Stairs creaked, landings opened onto sightlines, and stairwells collected echoes the way gutters collected leaves. Paloma climbed them on the balls of her feet, one hand trailing the wall where the plaster met the wood, moving only when the wind off the harbor pushed against the shutters and filled the building with small honest noises to hide inside.

On the second floor, a lamp burned low behind the warden's door. She heard a chair shift, a page turn, the wet snore of a man asleep sitting up. The warden himself, then. That was either very good or very bad, and she wouldn't know which until she was through the room.

There was no other way. The registry was behind him or nothing.

She eased the door open a hand's width. The office was small, crowded with cabinets, and the warden—a heavy man with his collar undone and a half-eaten pie beside him—was slumped in his chair facing the window, chin on chest, breathing like a tide going out. The iron cabinet stood against the far wall. Between her and it: six feet of floorboard that had not been laid by anyone who cared about quiet.

Paloma stood in the doorway and did the arithmetic every thief does. Weight of body against width of board. Speed against luck. She thought about Marta's copper in her pocket. She thought about the names in that cabinet—the ones she already knew, like old Widow Cress who bought fever-charms and paid in eggs, and the ones she didn't, which frightened her more.

Then she took off her boots.

Stockinged feet on cold boards, she crossed the room in four slow movements timed to the warden's snores, each one longer than the last, like waves between which a gull can land. At the cabinet she knelt and got out her picks—two lengths of stiff wire, worn smooth by twenty years of honest dishonesty.

The lock did have opinions. It grumbled, caught, sulked. Paloma coaxed it the way she coaxed anything stubborn: patiently, with respect, and a running commentary under her breath.

"There you are. No, don't do that. I know, I know, nobody's oiled you since the old king died. There. *There.*"

The lock turned with a click that sounded, in the sleeping room, like a hammer blow. Paloma froze. The warden smacked his lips, muttered something about herring, and slept on.

Inside the cabinet: ledgers, tax rolls, a strongbox she ignored, and there—bound in grey leather, corners reinforced with iron, heavier than any book had a right to be—

**The Registry of the Gifted, by Order of the Crown.**

Her hands shook. She made them stop, because shaking hands drop books, and dropped books wake wardens.

She'd expected a list. What she found, flipping pages by feel and the thin grey light from the window, was a census. Names, parishes, ages. A column headed *Nature of Gift*, written in a clerk's tidy hand: *hearth-blessing. weather-sense. mending-song.* And beside some entries, in different ink, newer and colder: ***confirmed.*** Beside others: ***pending.***

And beside the children's names, nothing at all yet. Just their ages. Seven. Nine. Eleven.

She knew some of those names. She had taught some of those names—quietly, in the back room of her shop, little knots and little rhymes, telling herself she was teaching craft, not gift, telling herself stories while the country sharpened its knives around her.

*Tamsin, age eight, Brackwater parish. Nature of gift: unrecorded.*

Tam. Little Tam, who never spoke, whose silences had a weight to them, who could stand in a room and make the loudest argument go soft as snowfall. She'd told herself his muteness was just muteness. She'd wanted it to be just muteness.

*Pending,* said the registry.

Paloma closed the book. Held it against her chest. It weighed as much as a child.

"Right," she breathed. "Cleverly. Not bravely."

She was three steps from the door when the floorboard betrayed her.

It wasn't even loud. A soft wooden groan, no more. But the warden came up out of his chair like a man scalded, and his hand went to the bell-pull on the wall, and Paloma did the only thing left in her ledger of options: she threw the pie at him.

Not at his face. At his feet. It burst on the flagstones in a spray of crust and eel, and he looked down at it, and that half-second of pure bafflement was all she needed. She was past him, through the door, boots clutched in one fist and the registry hugged under her arm, taking the stairs in leaps she didn't bother to soften anymore because soft was over.

Behind her the bell began to ring, and the whole hall woke up shouting.

* * *

She went out the way she'd come in, because a plan survives contact with disaster only if half of it still works. Through the records annex, over the desk, at the broken window—and stopped.

Torchlight in the yard below. Two soldiers, maybe three, drawn by the bell, standing in the coal-store shadow she'd counted on using.

Paloma pressed herself flat against the wall inside and thought fast and thought badly, because fear makes poor arithmetic. Front door: soldiers. Window: soldiers. Roof: she wasn't twenty anymore.

And then, below her, one of the torches moved away from the others. A soldier walking the wall's edge, alone, unhurried, his light held low. He stopped almost directly beneath her window, and Paloma watched him look at the ground—at her footprints, she realized, printed in coal dust on the sill outside—and she watched him stand there for a long moment doing absolutely nothing about it.

He was young, broad-shouldered, with the tired face of a man who'd been marched too many miles for reasons he hadn't been given. Sergeant's knots on his shoulder. He looked up once, straight at the window, and Paloma looked back, and neither of them moved.

She should have gone. Every sensible instinct said *go*. Instead she stayed, stockinged and book-laden, watching a soldier watch her, and the look on his face wasn't triumph or duty. It was recognition. Worse—it was *memory*. As if he were seeing someone he'd seen before, somewhere that mattered.

His mouth opened. Closed. His hand drifted toward the whistle at his belt and stopped short of it, hovering, like a man reaching for a door he'd decided not to open.

One heartbeat. Two.

Then, from across the yard, another soldier shouted: "Ren! You see anything?"

A pause that lasted exactly as long as a decision.

"No," Sergeant Ren called back. "Nothing here."

He turned away from the window. Paloma was through it and down the coal store and over the wall before her heart finished deciding whether to burst or break, and she ran through the sleeping streets with the registry thumping against her ribs like a second heart, and she did not stop running until she reached the salt marshes north of the city, where she finally sat down in the reeds, put her boots on with shaking hands, and laughed—one wild, cracked sound at the sky.

Then she stopped laughing, because she understood what had happened.

He'd seen her. He'd let her go. And tomorrow, when the hunt gathered itself and came looking, that man would remember her face perfectly, and every hour he delayed would hang him a little higher.

"You fool," she whispered—not sure, even then, whether she meant him or herself. Probably both. Likely both.

She opened the registry one last time in the grey pre-dawn, ran her thumb down the column of pending names, the seven-year-olds and nine-year-olds and eleven-year-olds, and closed it with a snap like a door shutting.

"Cleverly," she told the marsh, the tide, the pale line of coming morning. "Fine. Cleverly, then."

She stood, settled the book beneath her cloak, and turned north, away from everything she'd spent twenty years building and toward everything she'd spent twenty years avoiding.

Behind her, far off in Brackwater, a bell was still ringing.
