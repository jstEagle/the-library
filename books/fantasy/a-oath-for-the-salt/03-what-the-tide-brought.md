## 3. What the Tide Brought

The sergeant's office at Brackwater garrison smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who had signed on to catch smugglers and were about to be handed a very different kind of work.

Ivo Ren stood at attention before Captain Aldous Merrick's desk and told his lie in the flattest voice he could manage.

"Thief was hooded. Dark cloak. Never saw the face."

Merrick looked up from the report he was drafting, quill paused mid-scratch. "You chased her through three streets."

"Yes, sir."

"Close enough to hear her breathing?"

"Yes, sir."

"And you never saw her face."

"No, sir."

Merrick set down the quill and studied him the way Ivo had seen him study maps of coastline—looking for the place where the land didn't match the chart. Ivo kept his eyes fixed on a point six inches above the captain's head, where a moth was battering itself against the lantern glass with more persistence than sense.

"You're a good soldier, Ren," Merrick said at last. "Good soldiers see things. It's the bad ones who don't." He picked up the quill again. "Hooded. Dark cloak. Go on."

Ivo gave the rest of it straight: the magistrate's hall, the broken window on the seaward side, the registry missing from its case, the figure running toward the Tanner's Stair with a book-shaped bundle under one arm. All true. All of it true except for the shape of the face beneath the hood, which had been narrow and brown-eyed and lit from below by the moon off the water, and which Ivo had recognized in half a heartbeat—the way you recognize a song from two notes—and had then spent the length of three streets pretending not to.

Because here was the thing about lies: the best ones weren't inventions. They were just truths with one piece quietly removed, like a knot with a single strand slipped loose. Pull that strand and the whole thing comes apart in your hands, soft as rope.

He'd learned knots from her, in a way. Though she'd have laughed to hear it.

* * *

His brother Tam—no, not Tam, that was another boy entirely, a boy he hadn't met yet and wouldn't for years; his brother was Aldric—Aldric had taken a boar's tusk through the thigh eleven years ago, up past Millbrook, on a hunt that had gone wrong in every way a hunt could go wrong. The tusk had opened him like a purse. By the time they got him down off the ridge, the blood had soaked through two blankets and Aldric's jokes had stopped, which was how Ivo knew it was bad, because Aldric joked when he was frightened the way other men prayed.

The village surgeon was dead. The next nearest was two days away. Their father had sat in the road holding his son's hand and saying nothing, because their father was a man who believed that silence was strength, and Ivo had knelt in the mud thinking, *he is going to die and there is nothing, there is nothing, there is—*

And then a woman had come out of the hedgerow.

That was how he remembered it—not walking, *coming*, as if the hedge had decided to produce her. A charm-seller's pack on her back, travel-stained, with copper charms swinging off it like fruit. She'd looked at Aldric, then at their father, then at Ivo, and said, "I can bind that wound so it holds. But what I do has a price, and the price is that you'll know what I am. Both of those things are forever. Decide quickly."

Their father had said nothing, because silence was strength.

Ivo had said, "Do it."

She'd knelt in the mud beside Aldric and put her hands over the wound, and she'd spoken—not a spell, not the way the traveling players did spells, all thunder and arm-waving. She'd spoken like a person making a promise so large it needed both hands. Ivo couldn't remember the words anymore, though he'd tried for years; they'd been in no language he knew, old and round and salt-flavored, and they'd sounded less like magic than like someone saying *I will not let go of you.*

And the bleeding had stopped. Not slowed. *Stopped*, the way a door stops when it meets its frame.

Aldric had lived. He farmed outside Millbrook now, with a scar he showed off to girls and a limp he blamed for every lost argument. And the woman had packed her charms back onto her back and walked away down the road before anyone could offer her so much as supper, and Ivo had run after her and asked her name, and she'd said, "Better you don't have it," and smiled, and the smile had done something to the inside of his chest that he'd spent eleven years failing to repair.

Paloma Galloway. He'd learned the name later, the way you learn anything in a port city—overheard, half-price, unreliable. Charm-seller off Salt Lane. Keeps to herself. Good luck-knots, if you believe in that sort of thing.

He'd walked past her stall a dozen times over the years. Told himself it was coincidence. Told himself a lot of things.

Last night, in the moonlight under the magistrate's wall, with the registry-shaped bundle under her arm and three streets of running between them, he had raised the alarm horn to his lips—

—and thought of a woman kneeling in mud, saying *I will not let go of you.*

—and lowered it again, and shouted instead, "Stop! In the crown's name!" in a voice pitched to carry exactly far enough to satisfy duty and exactly short enough to be useless, and by the time he'd rounded the corner by the cooperage she was gone, swallowed by the warren of lanes above the fishmarket, and he had stood there breathing hard and lying to himself with the same fluency he would later lie to Merrick.

*Three streets,* he thought now, standing in the tallow-smelling office while Merrick wrote *hooded, dark cloak, face unseen* into the record. *I had three streets to decide who I was.*

He'd used all of them.

* * *

The Edict of Ash changed everything, and it did it with the cheerful efficiency of a fire taking a dry barn.

It had been read out in the square four days ago—a crier with a voice like a rusted hinge, flanked by two of the new inquisition's men in grey, reading the proclamation while the crowd stood very still in that particular way crowds stand when they are memorizing something they hope they'll never need. Every person gifted with the old arts to present themselves for registration within thirty days. Every person harboring such a person liable to the same. Every unregistered gift, upon discovery, to be purged by fire, the property forfeit to the crown, the names struck from the rolls.

Struck from the rolls. As if a name were a debt you could settle by burning the debtor.

The garrison had spent the days since being reorganized. Merrick, poor bastard, had come back from a meeting with the inquisition's local man looking like a man who'd been shown a machine and told to be a gear in it. Squads were drawn up. Streets were assigned. Lists were compiled—Ivo had watched clerks copying out parish records, ferry manifests, tax rolls, anything that might show who lived where and doing what, cross-referenced against complaints, gossip, grudges. Because that was the thing about witch-hunts that nobody put in the proclamations: they ran on grudges the way mills ran on water. Half the names on any list would be there because a neighbor's fence line was disputed, or a sweetheart had married elsewhere, or a fisherman had once gotten better prices at market.

And the other half would be children. Ivo had seen the preliminary figures. Children registered first, the inquisitor's man had said, because the young were most susceptible to correction. *Correction.* Spoken in the same tone you'd use for a misfiled ledger.

So no, Ivo had not especially struggled with his conscience when the chance came to lie about a face.

What troubled him was how little it had felt like a choice at all. It had felt like the tide. You don't decide to be pulled; you just discover you're moving.

* * *

"Sergeant Ren."

The inquisition's man found him at the harbor end of his patrol route, which Ivo suspected was not a coincidence, since the man seemed to have a talent for appearing wherever Ivo was, like weather. His name was Corven, and he had the smooth, unhurried manner of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because everyone around him did it for him, preemptively, out of self-preservation.

"Master Corven."

"You recovered nothing from the Tanner's Stair."

"No, sir."

"The thief went to ground in the fishmarket lanes."

"So it appeared, sir."

Corven smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind that came with a warranty. "The magistrate's clerk tells me the registry case was opened with a key, Sergeant. Not forced. The window was broken *outward*, after. Someone unlocked the case, took the book, and broke the window on their way past to make it look like a break-in." He let that sit between them like a coin on a table. "Which suggests our thief had access. An insider. Someone trusted."

Ivo kept his face at parade rest. "Suggests a clever thief, sir."

"Mm." Corven looked out at the harbor, where the morning boats were coming in with the tide, gulls screaming their usual extortion racket overhead. "You've served in Brackwater long, Sergeant?"

"Six years, sir."

"Know the people, then. The shopkeepers. The charm-sellers." Corven's gaze came back around, mild as milk. "There'll be a house-to-house soon. The Salt Lane quarter first. It would go easier on everyone if the garrison's own men walked point. Men the locals know. Men they'll open doors for."

And there it was. They wanted him to lead them down Salt Lane, past the stalls he knew, to the door of a woman whose luck-knots hung in the window like small braided promises, and knock, and smile, and hold her door open for the grey coats behind him.

"I can do that, sir," Ivo said.

Because refusing would mark him. Because marking him would take him off the lists and put him *on* one. Because a soldier inside the hunt, even a lying one, even a treacherous one, could do things a fugitive never could—and the first of those things occurred to him before Corven had even finished turning away.

* * *

Here is what Ivo began to do, in the small hours and the margins, with the quiet diligence of a man committing treason one stitch at a time:

He wrote reports. Meticulous ones. He reported everything he saw, and he arranged what he saw so that it led nowhere. A light in a window became a lamp left burning. A stranger asking questions at the harbor became a cargo-broker he'd questioned and cleared. A rumor that a charm-seller had been seen leaving the city on the north road became, in his report, an unverified claim from a drunk whose testimony he recommended discounting—he attached the drunk's prior statement about seeing a mermaid, for thoroughness.

He learned the hunt's schedule by volunteering to copy it. Nobody suspects the man doing the tedious work; tedium is a kind of invisibility, and Ivo wrapped himself in it like a cloak. Then he burned his copies, always, immediately, in the garrison stove, and passed what mattered by mouth, in fragments, in places where fragments belonged: a word dropped to the fishwife who fed the harbor cats, knowing it would travel; a note left unsigned in the chapel poor-box, because the poor-box was emptied by a woman who read everything she found there, he was almost sure, on account of how she'd once returned a love letter to him with the spelling corrected.

He was not good at this. He knew he wasn't. He was a soldier, built for orders that came from in front of him, and now the only orders were coming from somewhere behind his own ribs, and they were vague, and they were terrifying, and they boiled down to: *don't let them find her, don't let them find the others, don't let them find the children.*

But he was learning. And the first lesson had arrived already, on the third night, when he'd walked the Salt Lane quarter ahead of schedule—his own initiative, his own risk—and counted the shuttered windows, and noticed that the charm-stall with the luck-knots was dark, and the rooms above it were empty, swept clean as a plate. She was gone. Had been gone, maybe, since the night of the theft, or before. Maybe she'd known about the edict before the crier ever read it. Maybe she'd always had a bag packed, the way sailors do.

He should have felt relief. Instead he felt the specific ache of arriving at a door after the person you needed to thank has already left it.

Because he had never thanked her. Eleven years, and he'd never once stood in front of Paloma Galloway and said: my brother is alive because of you, and I have thought about your hands in the mud every time I've tied a knot since, and I am sorry for every hour I spent wearing this uniform while you sold charms for copper two streets away.

He'd settle for keeping her off the lists. For now. It wasn't gratitude, exactly. It was closer to ballast—you carried it low and deep and it kept you upright in weather.

* * *

On the fifth day, the hunt found its first name.

Not hers. A hedgewitch up past the tanneries, an old man named Fenn who'd been setting bones in the river district for forty years and registering exactly none of them. They took him at dawn. Ivo was assigned to the cordon, which meant he stood at the corner of Tanner's Row and watched them walk the old man past, barefoot, hands bound with—of all things—good navy cord, the kind Ivo himself had coiled a hundred times, and Fenn was looking at the crowd, not pleading, just *looking*, the way a man looks at a shoreline he's about to leave.

And the crowd looked back, and did nothing, and Ivo stood in it, uniformed, armed, complicit in his stillness, and understood that this was how it worked. Not with monsters. With cordons. With men who stood where they were told and lied only in the margins, only in the small hours, only one stitch at a time, while the fire was built in broad daylight by other men's hands.

That night he pulled the first stitch harder.

The inquisition's survey listed four gifted persons suspected in the Brackwater district, pending confirmation. Ivo copied the list, memorized it, burned it—and then, working late on the rosters, made a small adjustment to the patrol assignments for the following week. Nothing dramatic. A rotation shift. The river district squad moved to the north gate; the north gate squad moved to escort duty. Four days of coverage, thinned over one neighborhood, one street, one house where a widow named Marta kept her sister's daughter, and the girl was on nobody's list yet but would be, soon, because the girl could make lamps burn brighter when she sang, and the neighbor had heard her singing.

Four days. Maybe the girl's aunt would use them. Maybe they'd run. Maybe it wouldn't be enough, and Ivo would stand at another corner and watch another pair of bare feet, and hate himself with a fresh and interesting variety of hatred.

But maybe.

He was beginning to understand that this was what mercy looked like from the inside: not grand, not clean, not certain. Just a man in a bad uniform making small wrong entries in a large wrong book, night after night, hoping the arithmetic of it came out on the right side somewhere he'd never see.

* * *

Captain Merrick summoned him on the seventh evening, and Ivo went up the stairs to the tallow-smelling office with the particular lightness of a man who has decided that if this is the gallows, at least the climb will be honest.

Merrick had a dispatch open on the desk and two cups of wine poured, which was either a very good sign or a very bad one.

"Ren." Merrick tapped the dispatch. "Orders from the inquisition's district office. Effective tomorrow, I'm to detach you from patrol. You're reassigned to the registry recovery detail—full time. Tracking the stolen book." He slid a paper across. "Your knowledge of the theft makes you the natural lead."

Ivo looked at the paper. Looked at the wine. Chose the wine.

"Congratulations," Merrick said dryly, "or condolences. I genuinely can't tell which."

"Neither can I, sir."

"They want the registry badly, Ren. There's a name on every page. Hundreds of pages. Whole families." Merrick rubbed his eyes. "You find that book, you're handing the inquisition a map to burn half the coastal towns. You fail to find it—" he shrugged, "—you're a failed sergeant, which is at least a survivable profession."

"What would you do, sir?" Ivo asked, and was surprised to hear the question come out genuine.

Merrick looked at him for a long moment, and then did something Ivo had never seen him do: he turned the dispatch facedown on the desk, as if it embarrassed him.

"My grandmother," he said, "could calm a colicking horse by humming to it. Never told a soul. Died in her bed, eighty-one years old, because nobody ever had reason to look at her twice." He picked up his own cup. "I've been trying, these seven days, to remember whether that's a story about luck or about silence. Find me a third option sometime, Sergeant. I'm told good soldiers see things."

Ivo drank the wine, which was terrible, and took the paper, which was worse, and walked back down the stairs into the lamplit yard where the hunt was assembling its next day's shapes around him like weather coming in.

Full time, then. The recovery detail. Him, hunting her—officially, relentlessly, with resources.

Unofficially, he intended to be the worst hunter the inquisition had ever hired. He would follow every trail one step behind its cooling. He would question every witness until the witnesses forgot what they'd seen. He would become, if it killed him, the most thorough failure in the district's records.

And if, somewhere along that long deliberate road to nowhere, his path happened to cross a narrow-faced woman with brown eyes and a pack full of copper charms—

Well. He had eleven years of thanks saved up, and a warning or two besides, and he was done letting the tide decide for him.

The first thing he did, that same night, was leave a note in the chapel poor-box. Unsigned, unspelled, corrected-spelling optional. It said only: *They've put me on the hunt for the book. I lose slowly. Trust the soldier who doesn't knock.*

Whether it reached anyone, he couldn't say. Whether anyone in Brackwater still had ears small enough and quick enough to hear a thing like that—he couldn't say that either.

But two mornings later, the fishwife who fed the harbor cats pressed a luck-knot into his hand at the market, saying some traveler had paid her to pass it along, no message, no name. Braided in grey cord and sea-green, tight and sure, in a pattern Ivo knew down to his bones because he'd watched those same hands tie its twin in the mud eleven years ago.

He carried it against his chest after that, under the uniform, where no one could register it.

And on the ninth day, a child came looking for him—or rather, a child was *brought* to him, hauled by the elbow into the garrison yard by one of Corven's grey coats, a boy of perhaps ten, silent as stone, with salt-stained cuffs and eyes that went through Ivo like a hook through water. The grey coat said they'd found him hiding in a grain shipment at the north gate, said he hadn't made a sound the whole time, not a whimper, not a cry, said it plain and puzzled, like a man reporting a dog that wouldn't bark.

"Doesn't speak, this one," the grey coat said. "Deaf-and-dumb, likely. What do we do with him?"

Ivo looked at the boy, and the boy looked at Ivo, and somewhere in the space between those two looks, the hunt's arithmetic shifted under Ivo's feet—because the boy's silence had a texture to it, a weight, a held-breath quality that Ivo's skin recognized before his mind caught up, the way it had recognized a woman in a hedgerow, the way it had recognized a face under a hood.

This was no deaf-and-dumb orphan.

This was a gift, walking around unwrapped, ten years old and terrified, one bad afternoon away from a list with his name on it.

"Give him to me," Ivo heard himself say. "Registry detail. He can carry papers. Run messages. Better than wasting a cell on him."

The grey coat shrugged, relieved of a problem. The boy's fingers closed around Ivo's sleeve—small, cold, and gripping like a drowning sailor—and did not let go.

Ivo Ren, hunter, liar, traitor-in-progress, walked out of the garrison yard with the inquisition's newest problem tucked against his side, and headed for the north road, and the dark, and whatever came next.
