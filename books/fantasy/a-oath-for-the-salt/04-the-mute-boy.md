## 4. The Mute Boy

The road out of Brackwater smelled of tar and panic.

Paloma had left by the fish-gate before dawn, when the tide was out far enough to expose the mudflats and the watchmen were too cold to care who shuffled past them. She'd worn her plainest shawl and carried her charms in a basket like any peddler woman going to market, because a peddler woman was invisible in a way that no amount of magic could make a person. Invisibility, she had learned over twenty years, was mostly laundry and posture.

Behind her, smoke rose from the Tanners' Quarter. Not the good kind of smoke, not chimney smoke. The kind that came after.

She did not look back. Looking back was how they caught you; her teacher Maren had said that once, laughing, before the last time anyone laughed about such things. *Keep your eyes on the road ahead, little fish. The past can't chase what won't look at it.*

Maren's name was in the registry. So were forty others from Brackwater alone. So, probably, was hers — though under what name she couldn't guess, since "Paloma Galloway, charm-seller, unremarkable" was the whole of her public self, and the book in the false bottom of her basket held names that would blister your fingers if you thought about them too long.

The salt road went north along the coast, through marsh country where the crown's writ thinned out like broth. Three days' walk to the ferry at Marshend, if the ferries still ran. If the ferrymen hadn't heard about the edict and decided that strangers were more trouble than coin.

She walked. The mud sucked at her boots. Gulls argued overhead about something with great passion and no resolution, which was more civic debate than Brackwater had managed in years.

Near midday she stopped at a roadside shrine to eat. It was one of the old ones — pre-dissolution, the masons called them, though nobody remembered what had been dissolved or why, only that there used to be more shrines than there were now, and more songs, and more words. This one was a squat stone alcove with a basin for offerings and a carving above it so weathered it might have been anything: a wave, a bird, a woman with her arms open. Pilgrims had left shells and copper bits and, in one touching case, half a biscuit gone green with damp.

Paloma ate her bread and cheese and tried not to think about the smoke.

That was when she noticed the silence.

Not quiet. Silence. There is a difference, and every gifted person knows it the way you know a hand on the back of your neck. Quiet is the world resting. Silence is the world being *held*. The gulls had been screaming a moment ago. Now their beaks moved and nothing came out. The wind moved the reeds and made no sound doing it. Even her own chewing had gone strange and distant, as if someone had stuffed wool in the world's ears.

Paloma set down her bread very slowly and said, in the tone she used for skittish cats and frightened customers, "You can come out. I'm not with the hunt."

Nothing. Then, from behind the shrine, a small shape detached itself from the shadow of the stone.

He was maybe ten, thin as a gull's leg, dressed in a coat three sizes too big with a grey cast to it that she recognized with a lurch — garrison issue, cut down. His hair was dark and badly cut, in the way hair gets cut when someone does it with a knife and impatience. He stood with his weight on his heels, ready to run, and his eyes went from her face to her basket to the road behind her and back again, quick as a fish deciding which way the net is coming from.

"Hello," Paloma said.

His mouth opened. Nothing came out. And the silence around him deepened by a degree, the way a room gets darker when a cloud crosses the sun.

Oh, she thought. *Oh, you poor brave little thing.*

"You're the one doing that," she said gently, gesturing at the air, at the soundless gulls. "Aren't you."

He flinched. His hands came up — not defensively, exactly. More like he was bracing to be hit, or to apologize, or both. He shook his head hard, then stopped, then nodded, caught between denying it and knowing denial was useless, and the whole performance was so heartbreaking and so transparently honest that Paloma felt something crack open in her chest that she'd had boarded up for twenty years.

"It's all right," she said. "It's a fine gift. Better than mine, probably."

That got his attention. His head tilted. *Yours?* said the tilt.

"I make things happen when I talk," she said. "Oaths. Promises. When I swear something true, the world... listens. And does it." She shrugged, keeping her voice light, though saying it aloud to another living soul for the first time in two decades made her hands sweat inside her gloves. "Which sounds impressive until you realize you can never promise anyone anything casually ever again. You try telling a customer 'I'll have your order ready Tuesday' when the universe takes notes."

And there — there it was, brief as a spark off flint — the boy's mouth twitched. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, filed away for later use.

Encouraged, Paloma dug in her basket and produced the heel of the cheese and the rest of the bread, plus an apple that had seen better weeks. She set them on the shrine's basin, pushed them across the stone toward him, and stepped back to a respectful distance, the way you'd approach a stray dog.

He looked at the food. He looked at her. He looked at the food again with an expression of profound suspicion, as if it might be baited.

"Eat," she said. "If I wanted to trap you I'd have done it while you were busy holding the whole coastline silent. That's quite a radius, by the way. Are the gulls always this rude when you let them talk?"

The twitch again. He snatched the bread and ate like a small storm, guarding the apple with one elbow, and Paloma sat down slowly on the shrine's low wall and let him, and looked out at the silent marshes, and thought about the smoke over the Tanners' Quarter, and about the list in her basket, and about how many children like this one were on it.

Because she'd taught them. That was the rot at the center of everything: for years, in her other life, her real life, she had been one of the circle that found the gifted young and taught them to hide. Little lessons disguised as games. *Hold your breath when you feel it coming. Blame the cat. Cry, if you can — crying explains everything.* She'd taught a girl who could find lost things to pretend to stumble over them. She'd taught a boy whose touch mended broken crockery to be clumsy with everything else so no one would wonder.

She didn't know this boy. But somebody had taught him to run, and to hide, and to hold his silence like a knife, and whoever it was wasn't here now.

When he'd finished the bread and most of the apple, he wiped his mouth on his enormous sleeve and regarded her with the frank appraisal of the very young, who haven't yet learned to pretend their staring is accidental.

"Paloma," she said, tapping her own chest. "I'm going north. To the marshes, then the ferry." She pointed up the salt road, then north, in case words were too much. "Where are you going?"

He didn't point anywhere. That was answer enough, and it landed in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

"No one?" she said quietly. "There's no one?"

His jaw tightened. He shook his head — once, firmly, the way you refuse something rather than the way you admit it. Then, after a moment, he did something unexpected. He reached into the collar of his giant coat and pulled out a cord, and on the cord hung a wooden bead, hand-carved, shaped like a knot.

A luck-knot. Her pattern. Her maker's mark, small and crude, carved into the base.

Paloma went very still. "Where did you get that?"

He touched his own chest. *Someone like me.* Then he pointed north, vaguely, toward the marshes and whatever lay beyond them, and made a gesture she couldn't read — hands opening, like a bird released, or a door swung wide.

One of her circle. One of Maren's people, or Maren herself, had given this boy a charm and pointed him north, and then — the smoke, the lists, the arrests. The registry in her basket suddenly weighed as much as an anchor.

"You knew them," Paloma said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "The person who gave you that."

He nodded. Then his face did something complicated — grief trying to get out through a face that had no practice letting it, because crying made noise and noise was dangerous, had always been dangerous, even before the edict. What came out instead was a single hitched breath, and the silence around him shuddered like the surface of struck water.

And then he crossed the space between them and grabbed her.

Not her sleeve. Her. Both arms around her middle, face pressed into her shawl, gripping with that drowning-sailor strength, and Paloma sat frozen on the shrine wall with her arms half-raised, twenty years of discipline screaming *don't, don't, attachments are how they find you, don't*—

—and put her arms around him anyway.

"There now," she said, uselessly, warmly, into his terrible haircut. "There now."

He was shaking. She rubbed his back the way you'd soothe a horse in thunder, and felt the silence around them flex and settle, flex and settle, breathing with him. Gulls resumed screaming at the edges of it, indignant. Somewhere a frog remembered itself mid-croak.

After a long time he pulled back and looked at her, red-eyed and resolute, and pointed north, and then at her, and then made a fist around the front of her shawl and did not let go. The meaning could not have been clearer if he'd shouted it.

"I don't know if I can—" Paloma started, and stopped, because it was a lie and she was constitutionally incapable of finishing lies, which was the whole trouble with her life. She took a breath. "Listen. I'm going to say something now, and I need you to understand that when I say it, it's *real*. It's not a comfort. It's not a kindness. It's iron. Do you understand?"

He frowned slightly, then nodded.

So Paloma Galloway, who had spent twenty years swallowing every oath before it could leave her teeth, took his small cold hands in hers and spoke aloud, on an empty road, with the sky listening:

"I will not leave you. Whatever comes — the hunt, the water, the dark — I will not leave you behind. While I walk, you walk with me. So I swear it, and let it be so."

The words went out of her like heat from a forge, and the world *took* them. She felt it happen, the old familiar settling, the promise becoming a fact woven into things. The boy felt it too — his eyes went wide, and the silence around him rang like a bell struck underwater, and every gull within a mile went up off the marshes at once in a clatter of wings and outrage.

He stared at her. Then at the road. Then at her again, with an expression that said, very clearly, *you weren't joking about the universe taking notes.*

"No," Paloma said, standing, retrieving her basket, feeling lighter and more doomed than she had in years. "I never joke about promises. It's my one flaw."

He considered this. Then he picked up the last of the apple, tucked the luck-knot back under his collar, and fell into step beside her — close, shoulder nearly brushing her hip, positioned exactly where a small person walks when they've decided a big person is theirs now.

"What do I call you?" she asked, after a mile. "Can't go calling you 'boy' forever, people will ask questions. Have you got a name?"

He held up one finger — *wait* — crouched, and drew in the mud of the road with a stick. He was quick about it, economical, the strokes sure. A letter. *T.* Then, after consideration, a second letter beside it. *A.* Then a third, drawn with particular care, almost lovingly.

*Tam.*

"Tam," Paloma said, trying it out. It fit him, somehow — short and solid, a stone you could build with. "Tam. Well met, Tam. I'm Paloma, and I talk too much, and between us we should just about balance out."

Tam looked up at her, and this time the smile arrived properly, whole, like a boat coming out of fog.

They walked north. The marshes opened around them, brown and gold and smelling of salt and growing things, and the road ran ahead through country that no one had bothered to burn yet.

It was near dusk, the light going long and honey-colored over the flats, when Tam stopped dead in the middle of the road.

Paloma turned. "What is it?"

He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the reeds to the east of the road, at a stand of them taller than the rest, bending in a wind that — she realized with a slow crawl up her spine — was blowing nowhere else. Just those reeds. Moving against the evening air like grass under a hand.

And Tam had gone rigid, one arm thrown out in front of her, the other hand pressed flat against his own chest, over the luck-knot, over his heart. His eyes were huge. His mouth worked silently, shaping a word over and over, and the silence pouring off him had changed quality entirely — no longer a shield but a *listening*, a held breath, a boy straining toward something only he could hear.

*What word?* Paloma wanted to ask. *What are you hearing, in a country that forgot its own voice?*

But asking was noise, and noise would break it, whatever it was. So she stood in the road with a child's arm barring her chest and watched the impossible wind move the reeds, and waited, and the light died slowly out of the sky, and far to the north — so far it might have been imagination, except that Tam's hand closed tighter on nothing — something answered.

* * *

They walked after dark, because Tam would not be moved until the reeds went still, and after that he kept glancing north with an urgency that needed no translation. Paloma gave up on the idea of camping early and matched his pace, and told herself the prickling at the back of her neck was just the cold.

Around full dark, they passed the first waymarker of the old road — a leaning stone post, taller than a man, carved top to bottom in letters no living scholar could read. Every village had one or two, left over from before, worn smooth by centuries of weather and indifference. Farmers used them to tether goats.

Tam walked straight up to it, laid both palms flat against the stone, and began, very softly, to cry.

The silence around him didn't waver. That was the terrible part. He cried without a single sound escaping, tears running down his face in the moonlight, and the night stayed perfectly, utterly quiet, as if the stone and the boy were sharing something between them that the rest of the world had no ears for.

Paloma stood beside him and put a hand on the stone too. It was cold. Under her palm, faint as a heartbeat, it seemed to hum.

"Well," she said to the dark, to the road, to the drowned country all around them that had buried its own voice and salted the earth over the grave. "I suppose we're going to find out what you're trying to tell us."

North, the marshes went on forever. And somewhere in them, following a mute boy and a humming stone and a stolen book full of names, Paloma Galloway walked deeper into the country's oldest secret — carrying, in the false bottom of a peddler's basket, the list of everyone left alive who could hear it.
