## 5. Roads That Remember

Tam drew the road before they ever found it.

He had been drawing it for two days, actually, in the dirt with a stick, in the margins of Paloma's charm-ledger with a stub of charcoal he'd fished out of a hearth in the last village that would still sell them bread. A line going up and up into hills, then a strange little zigzag, like a bird's footprint, like a stitch.

Paloma had looked at his drawing, then at her map, then at him, and said, "You're telling me there's a road over the Kettleback Hills."

Tam nodded and tapped the zigzag twice.

"And you know this because...?"

He shrugged, which was his answer to most questions, because *I don't know how I know* was not something a person could say without words, and words were the one thing he didn't have. He pointed at his own chest, then at the drawing, then made a motion like water flowing downhill — things just came to him sometimes, the way rain finds the low places. He couldn't have explained it if he'd wanted to. His silence wasn't empty. It was full. It was so full that sounds got lost in it, and other things too — old things, buried things — sometimes rose up in it like bubbles in a pot about to boil.

The zigzag, it turned out, was real. They crested the first hill at midmorning and there it was: a road stitched across the green shoulders of the Kettlebacks, pale as scar tissue, running straight where no sensible road ran straight, climbing where roads weren't supposed to climb.

"Well," said Paloma. She stood with her hands on her hips and her pack straps creaking and squinted down the length of it like it had personally insulted her. "That's the old salt road. Nobody uses the old salt road. The maps all say it fell apart forty years ago."

Tam looked at the road. The road did not look fallen apart. The road looked like it had been waiting.

* * *

They walked it because walking it was better than not walking it, which was Paloma's whole philosophy of travel, and also because every other road had soldiers on it now, and posters, and men with inkpads who wanted to press your thumb into a book of names. The Edict of Ash had followed them out of Brackwater like a smell. Tam had seen the posters nailed to a granary door two villages back: REGISTER OR BURN, in letters big enough to read from a galloping horse. Below the letters was a picture of flames, drawn by someone who had clearly never seen a fire and thought flames looked like curly hair.

Tam could not be registered. There was nothing on any poster about boys whose silence ate sound. But there didn't need to be. The posters didn't list what got burned. They just promised the burning.

So: the salt road. Which is where the wonder started.

It began small. Late in the first day, Tam stopped in the middle of the road and held up one hand — their signal for *stop*, though really it meant *wait, something's happening* — and knelt, and put his palm flat against the stones.

Under his hand, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the road hummed.

Not sound exactly. His gift didn't let sound in; sound was the first thing his silence swallowed, always, whether he wanted it or not. This was older than sound. It was more like the memory of sound — the shape of a thing that had once been loud, pressed flat into stone the way flowers get pressed into books. Cartwheels. Hundreds of cartwheels, thousands, rolling east toward the coast loaded with salt. And under the wheels, footsteps, and under the footsteps, voices, and under the voices—

Tam snatched his hand back.

Paloma had crouched beside him. "What is it? What do you feel?"

He didn't know the word for what he felt, so he did what he always did: he picked up his charcoal and drew it, right there on a flat stone at the roadside. A road. Above the road, wavy lines, like heat-shimmer or like the lines above a candle flame. And inside the lines, tiny figures. Carts. A tower. People dancing, maybe, arms up, skirts out.

Paloma went very quiet, the particular quiet she went into when she was thinking hard and didn't want anyone to know it. "Those are festival lines," she said finally. "Salt-road festivals. My grandmother used to sing about them. Harvest-salt days, when the whole road from the pans to the sea turned into one long market and everyone sang the brine-chants." She frowned. "But those songs are gone. Nobody sings them. They've been gone longer than I've been alive."

Tam tapped the stone. Then he tapped his own ear. Then, carefully, he made the gesture they'd invented between them for *still here*: he laid his palm over his heart and pressed, gently, twice.

Still here. All of it. Under the road, under the years, still here.

Paloma looked at the little dancing figures for a long time. Then she said, mostly to herself, "They told us it died. They told us magic just... faded, after the Dissolution, like a fire left untended." Her jaw did the thing it did when she was angry but keeping it folded up small and portable. "Roads don't remember songs, Tam. Roads aren't supposed to remember anything."

Tam patted the road once, the way you'd pat an old dog, and got up, and they walked on.

* * *

The second day, the echoes got louder. Or deeper. Tam hadn't decided which word fit yet, and he'd spent a fair amount of walking time deciding.

By noon he had filled six pages of Paloma's ledger — she'd surrendered it with the sigh of a woman donating a limb — and Paloma had begun stopping every time he stopped, which was often, which slowed their pace considerably, which Paloma complained about constantly and never once actually minded.

At a ruined toll-arch, he drew the arch standing whole, painted blue, with a bell hanging from it and a man beneath the bell holding a long white horn. When he touched the stones, the horn-note came up through his feet — a single held note, low and round, the kind of sound that must have carried three valleys. He showed Paloma how the note felt by humming it wrong on purpose and then shaking his head and pressing his fist to his sternum, lower, lower, until she guessed.

"A horn call," she breathed. "A warning? No — a greeting. You greet each valley with its own note, so travelers know where they are by ear." She laughed, sudden and startled, a real laugh. "That's clever. That's *lovely*. Who thinks of that?"

People who were not afraid, Tam wrote on his slate. He'd gotten the slate from Paloma's pack; she kept a little store of things for teaching, chalk and slate and patience, though she pretended these were for luck-charm inventory. He underlined *not afraid* twice.

Paloma's laugh died in her throat. She looked up at the arch, at its broken bell-stub, at the long empty road beyond it, and her face did something complicated. "No," she agreed quietly. "They weren't afraid. Not of this." She touched one of the arch's stones the way Tam touched them, palm flat, though Tam suspected she felt nothing through it except cold. Whatever she was, whatever she'd spent twenty years hiding, her gift didn't hear what his did. Hers lived in oaths and iron and spoken truth. His lived in everything that had been made quiet.

Which was, he was beginning to understand, an enormous amount of everything.

* * *

The third day it rained, and the road remembered louder.

Tam figured out why around midday, hunched under an overhang with Paloma while the water sheeted off the world: water woke things up. Rain seeped down between the stones and found the old pressed-flat shapes and plumped them, the way dried flowers swell in a bowl. Every drop that hit the road rang some buried bell of it. To anyone else it would have sounded like rain on rock. To Tam, kneeling at the edge of the overhang with rain streaming off his chin and his hand pressed to wet stone, it sounded like a country talking in its sleep.

He drew and drew. A bridge that leapt a gorge in a single white arc — and when he touched the gorge's rim, the arc hummed too, and the hum said *I held a thousand years of carts and weddings and funerals and I can hold again if anyone would ask me.* A well with two buckets, one for salt water and one for sweet. A shrine where the stones sang back if you knocked on them in the right order, a tune like *knock-knock-rest, knock-knock-rest*, and Tam sat in the rain for half an hour knocking on a shrine that was now just a pile of tumbled rocks, trying to find the order, and when he found it — when the last stone answered and the whole pile rang one clear chord up through his knees — he laughed out loud, a big unpracticed bark of a laugh, his first in longer than he could count.

Paloma stuck her head out from under the overhang. "What happened? Are you hurt?"

Tam shook his head, grinning so wide his cheeks hurt, and knocked the chord again so she could hear it — knowing she couldn't, not really, knowing the rain would eat it before it reached her ears, but needing her to see his face while it happened.

She saw. Something in her expression cracked open, soft and sad all at once. "Oh," she said. "Oh, sweetheart. It's all still in there, isn't it. Everything they erased."

Tam nodded. Then he stopped nodding. Because that wasn't quite it, and he was tired of the not-quite-it, and he was starting — slowly, the way frost forms, crystal by crystal — to understand what the not-quite-it actually was.

Erased things don't ring when you knock on them. Erased things are gone. These things weren't gone. These things were *buried*.

He took up the charcoal. He drew the shrine whole, singing. Then he drew hands — many hands — laying stones over it, covering it, packing earth on top, deliberately, carefully, the way you'd bury someone you loved rather than someone you hated. Then he drew the edict-poster flames over that. Then he crossed out the flames and drew the hands again, because the hands had come first. The hands had come a long time first.

He held it up.

Paloma took the page. She sat down in the mud of the overhang, heedless of her cloak, and stared at it, and Tam watched twenty years of a lie rearrange itself behind her eyes.

"Buried," she said. "Not dead. Not faded. *Buried.*" She looked out at the rain-blurred road, at the drowned hills, at the whole sleeping country. "After the Dissolution, they taught us the magic simply left. Like birds migrating. Nothing to be done, everyone said, that's just how it goes, the world gets a bit grayer, best make your peace." Her voice dropped. "But you don't bury a bird, Tam. You bury a body. Someone decided the magic should die, and when it wouldn't die, they decided it should at least be *silent*—" She stopped. Her hand went to her collarbone, where Tam knew she wore a plain cord with no charm on it, the cord she never explained. "And then someone decided who else should be silent too. Everyone who remembered. Everyone who could hear."

The rain drummed. In it, faintly, through Tam's knees and palms and the soles of his feet, the old road went on murmuring its brine-chants to nobody, patient as salt, sure as tide, waiting for hands that would dig instead of bury.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a coaching inn that had been grand once and was now a roofless suggestion of grandeur. Paloma built a fire in what had been the taproom hearth, a hearth big enough to roast an ox, and they ate barley porridge with the last of the honey, and Tam drew the inn as it used to be on his slate: full of light and noise, a fiddler on a table, a chandelier of driftwood and glass floats.

"You keep showing me what we lost," Paloma said, watching the drawing. She had her knees pulled up and firelight in her hair and the tired look of someone doing arithmetic she doesn't like. "Do you ever show me what we might get back?"

Tam considered this seriously. He took up the chalk.

First he drew the road, unburied, ringing. Then the shrine, rebuilt. Then the arch with its bell rehung. Then — this took a while, and several erasures, because drawing hope is harder than drawing history — he drew a village. Ordinary houses, ordinary people. But above the houses he drew the wavy festival-lines, and in the lines he put everyone: a fisherman's widow with a new luck-knot at her wrist (he gave her Paloma's knot-pattern, five-loop), a soldier with kind eyes and a crooked smile (he gave him Ivo's eyebrows, which he'd only seen for a moment in Brackwater but had apparently decided to keep forever), a tall woman with a sword-captain's stance and her father's frown turned inside out into something kinder (Serah Vane, whom Tam had never met, but Paloma talked in her sleep sometimes and Tam listened the way he listened to everything). And in the middle of the village square, a boy with no voice and a woman with a hidden oath, side by side, both of them drawn with the wavy lines coming *out* of them instead of over them.

He turned the slate around.

Paloma looked at it a long time. Long enough that the fire popped twice and an owl asked its one question somewhere up the dark hillside.

"That's a lot of digging," she said at last, and her voice was rough. "You understand that. If it's buried, somebody buried it, and whoever buries a whole country's magic doesn't leave the shovels lying around for the likes of us. They'll have left watchers instead. Wardens. Inquisitors." She reached over and tapped the drawing of herself, the wavy lines rising out of her hidden heart. "And the moment either of us starts making noise — real noise, old-tongue noise, the kind that wakes things up — every one of those watchers will hear it. Just like you hear the road."

Tam took the slate back. He thought. Then, below the village, he drew one more thing: the sea, and a door in a cliff, and light coming through the crack of it. He didn't know why. He hadn't seen any such door. But his hands knew things his head didn't, and lately his hands kept wanting to draw doors.

Paloma stared at that drawing longest of all.

"Where did you see that?" she asked, very quietly.

Tam shrugged — *I don't know how I know* — and pointed east, down the road, toward the coast he'd never visited and the sea he barely remembered, where the buried country was humming itself hoarse under the rain, and where, come morning, the road would take them whether they understood it yet or not.
