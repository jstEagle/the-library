## 6. The Inquisitor's Daughter

The river town of Millbrook announced itself the way towns do when they're trying too hard to seem harmless: with a crooked sign, a smell of wet flour, and a church bell that rang slightly flat, as if even the bell had given up on precision. Paloma stopped at the crest of the bridge and let Tam catch up, which took a while, because Tam had stopped to examine every single thing between here and there — a snail shell, a knot in the railing, a dog of profound indifference.

He tugged her sleeve and pointed at the bell.

"Flat," she agreed. "Somebody's been ringing it angry for years."

His mouth did the thing that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one. He held up three fingers, then folded two down, then pointed at himself.

"Three days since anyone's looked at us twice? I'd say four." She'd been counting differently than he had, apparently, and his count was better. It usually was. The boy couldn't say a word, but he kept better books than any clerk she'd ever met. "Fine. Four. We eat, we sleep under a roof, we leave before the fishmongers wake up."

Tam considered this, then held up five fingers.

"Five," she conceded. "You drive a hard bargain for someone who can't talk."

They crossed into Millbrook like water finding its level — Paloma with her hood up and her hands looking like nothing more than a charm-seller's hands, calloused from knot-work instead of sword-work; Tam pressed close at her side, wearing the silence around him like a coat two sizes too big. Even now, after weeks on the road, she hadn't gotten used to how sound behaved near him. Birds went quiet not because they were frightened but because their songs simply... didn't arrive. Footsteps softened. Arguments lost their teeth halfway through. Once, in a bad hour on the salt road, a wolf had circled them for half the night and never once managed to growl.

She'd sworn an oath to him, back in the reeds outside Brackwater. *I won't leave you.* She'd said it out loud, which was stupid, which was dangerous, which was the first oath she'd spoken in twenty years, and the sky hadn't fallen and no hunters had come running, and Tam had looked at her like she'd handed him the moon wrapped in brown paper.

So far the moon had mostly meant carrying his drawings and sleeping badly.

* * *

Millbrook's inn was called The Drowned Rat, which Paloma felt showed admirable self-awareness for an establishment whose floorboards were, in fact, damp. The innkeeper was a broad woman named Hess who took their copper without questions and their order without judgment, and for one golden hour Paloma almost believed they might be ordinary travelers after all.

Tam ate bread and cheese with the focused devotion of a boy who remembered hunger, and drew. He always drew. He'd filled half a ledger-book Paloma had stolen along with the registry — she tried not to think about the registry, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, heavier than any book had a right to be — and his pictures were getting stranger. Roads drawn as ribbons with faces. A tree with roots shaped like hands. And lately, over and over, a door.

"Doors again?" she said quietly.

He nodded, tapped the page, then pointed at her, then at the door, then made a walking motion with two fingers that ended at the edge of the table.

"I don't know where it is yet," she said. "That's the truth of it."

He frowned at her, unconvinced, and drew a small circle around the door, and inside the circle he wrote — in his careful, laborious letters — *YOU KNOW.*

"I know what it's *called*," she muttered. "Knowing a thing's name isn't the same as knowing where it sleeps."

But that night, in the room under the slanted roof, with Tam breathing slow and even beside her, she took out the registry and read by candle stub, and the names swam, and among the drowned names of children she had taught — Jeyan, who could warm milk with a hum; little Maren, whose shadows told jokes — she found herself whispering the old words of the Salt Door legend like a prayer she was ashamed of. *Where the salt remembers, the way opens.* Her teachers had sung it when she was small enough to believe songs were just songs.

Somewhere below, the flat bell rang midnight, and Paloma fell asleep with her hand on the book like a mother guarding a child she couldn't decide whether to love or fear.

* * *

They should have left before dawn. That was the plan. Five fingers, Tam's count, out before the fishmongers.

Instead the fishmongers woke them, in a manner of speaking, by going silent all at once — and Paloma came awake the way soldiers come awake, heart first, hand already finding the knife under her pillow, because silence in a market town at dawn is louder than screaming.

Tam was already sitting up. He pointed at the window, then at his own ears, then shook his head slowly: *no sound coming in.* His gift drank noise, yes, but this was different. This was the town itself holding its breath.

Paloma dressed in the dark, strapped the pack across her chest, took Tam's hand, and opened the door onto a hallway full of armed people.

Not many. Six, maybe seven, positioned the way you position people when you've done it a hundred times — angles covered, exits shadowed, no wasted motion. And at the front, a woman perhaps thirty, in hunt-captain grey with the silver badge of the Inquisition at her throat, holding a crossbow pointed at the floor in a way that said *I can raise this faster than you can blink* while also saying *I would rather not.*

Her hair was dark, pulled back severely. Her eyes were the problem. They weren't cruel eyes. Cruel eyes Paloma knew how to handle; you gave them what they wanted and you died a little inside and you survived. These eyes were *tired*, and tired eyes were unpredictable.

"Paloma Galloway," the woman said.

Every name has a weight, and hers landed like a stone dropped down a well. Beside her, Tam's silence deepened until Paloma's own heartbeat sounded distant, muffled, as though the boy were trying to wrap the whole hallway in wool.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Paloma said. "Which is unusual. Usually I'm at several."

"Hunt-Captain Serah Vane." The woman didn't move the crossbow. "You'll come downstairs. Alone. The boy stays here with my people, unharmed, I give you my word."

"Your word." Paloma tasted it. "What's an inquisitor's word worth these days?"

Something flickered across Serah Vane's face — quick, quickly buried — that was not pride and not anger. Something closer to agreement.

"That," she said, "is the question I've been asking myself for eleven years. Downstairs. Please."

*Please*. Hunt captains didn't say please. Paloma looked at Tam, who was staring at Vane with that unnerving stillness of his, head tilted, listening to something no one else could hear. Then he did something extraordinary: he let go of Paloma's hand.

He stepped toward the hunt-captain's soldiers without fear, looked up at the nearest one — a young man gripping his pike like a broom — and pointed at the man's bootlaces, which were indeed untied. Then he looked back at Paloma and shrugged, elaborately, as if to say *these are just people, stop being dramatic.*

"He's right," Paloma sighed. "Lead on, Captain. But if anything touches that boy—"

"Yes," Vane said. "I know what you'll do. Everyone knows what you'll do. That's rather the point of you."

* * *

The common room of The Drowned Rat had been cleared of everyone except Hess, who stood behind her bar polishing a tankard with the aggressive calm of a woman determined to witness everything and admit nothing. Vane dismissed her soldiers to the door, sat across the table from Paloma, set the crossbow down — actually *down*, within reach but pointing at the hearth — and poured two cups of small beer from the pitcher Hess silently provided.

"My father wrote the Edict of Ash," Vane said, without preamble.

Paloma kept her face still. Twenty years of practice. Inside, something cold turned over. *Vane.* Of course. Inquisitor Aldous Vane, architect of the registry, author of the purges' legal machinery, the man whose signature sat at the front of the stolen book like a spider at the center of a web.

"Condolences to your family," Paloma said.

A ghost of a smile. "You're funnier than your reputation suggests."

"I don't have a reputation."

"You have three. Depending on which garrison you ask." Vane leaned forward. "In the eastern camps they say you're a myth. In the southern ones, a cautionary tale. And in my father's study there's a file this thick—" she held up thumb and finger a hand-width apart "—with your name on it and almost nothing in it, because in twenty years you never once slipped. Until Brackwater."

"The magistrate's hall."

"The magistrate's hall." Vane turned her cup slowly on the table. "Do you know what my father says about the theft? He says the thief will run for the coast. He says she'll try for passage north. He has forty men watching every harbor from here to Greyharbor, and every one of them is wasting their time, because I'm the only one who's worked out where you're actually going."

The cold thing in Paloma's chest turned again. "And where's that?"

"Nowhere," Vane said. "Or rather — *out*. You're not fleeing the country, Mistress Galloway. You're fleeing the *story* of the country. You're looking for the Salt Door."

The flat bell above them chose that moment to ring the quarter-hour, off-key, mournful. Tam appeared at the top of the stairs, having evidently decided that promises of unharmed were best verified personally, and descended with the unhurried confidence of a boy who had already assessed every soldier in the building and found them wanting. He planted himself at Paloma's side and glared at Serah Vane with the full force of twelve years old and mute and unimpressed.

Vane looked at him for a long moment. "The silent one. There are reports of a boy whose presence unmakes sound. My father wants him very badly. Do you know why?"

"Because he's a threat," Paloma said flatly.

"Because he's *proof*." Vane's voice dropped. "My father's entire edifice rests on one claim: that magic died out generations ago, that what remains is dregs and trickery, that the gifted are liars and frauds preying on the desperate. That's why registration. That's why the pyres. Because you can't burn what doesn't exist unless you first convince everyone it does." She gestured at Tam. "That boy walks through a market and the noise bends around him like a stream around a stone. My soldiers saw it. Half of them are telling themselves they imagined it. The other half aren't."

Paloma's mouth was dry. "You're telling me the edict is built on a lie."

"I'm telling you my father *knows* it's built on a lie." Vane reached into her coat, and Paloma's hand went to her knife, and Tam's silence thickened until the room felt underwater — but what Vane drew out was not a weapon. It was a letter, creased and worn, sealed with wax that had been broken and broken again. "This is my mother's handwriting. She's been in my father's custody for six years. Not prison — he calls it 'care.' She saw the first purge, twenty-five years ago. She saw what they burned and she saw *why*, and she tried to tell people, and so now she is 'cared for.'" Vane set the letter on the table between them. "She writes me the same sentence every time, at the end, no matter what else the censors allow: *The door was never locked from outside.*"

Paloma stared at the letter. At the worn seal. At the woman across from her, who wore her father's grey and her father's badge and had the look of someone who had spent eleven years carrying a weight and had finally, tonight, decided to put it down somewhere.

"Why?" Paloma asked. It came out rougher than she intended. "Why tell me this? Why not take the registry, take the credit, go home your father's daughter? One word from you and I'm ash on the wind, and you're celebrated."

"Because I burned someone once." Vane said it evenly, the way you report weather. "Early. A charm-seller in Lowmarsh. Old woman, luck-knots, love-charms, nothing that worked, probably. I followed procedure. Procedure said she confessed. Procedure said she refused to recant." Her jaw tightened. "She recanted. I heard her. I chose not to hear her. And afterward I went home and my father praised me, and I understood that I had done exactly what I was trained to do, and that the training was the crime." She met Paloma's eyes. "I am not offering you trust, Mistress Galloway. Trust is for people with clean hands. I'm offering you usefulness. I know where my father's men are. I know the rotation at every checkpoint between here and the coast. I know which harbors are watched and which are theater. And I know — better than anyone alive — how my father thinks. You cannot outrun him. Nobody outruns him. But you can *mislead* him, and I am very, very good at misleading him, because I learned from the master."

Silence. Or rather, Tam's silence, which had settled over the room like snowfall, so that even the fire's crackle arrived muted and gentle.

Paloma looked at the letter. At the woman. At the boy beside her, who was studying Serah Vane with his head tilted, listening to whatever it was he listened to beneath words.

Twenty years of hiding had taught her every shape of trap. This had the shape of a trap. It also had the shape of the only help she'd been offered since the world caught fire.

"If this is a snare," Paloma said slowly, "it's an elegant one. Which worries me. Your father builds elegant snares."

"My father builds snares," Vane agreed. "I build none. I'm a worse liar than he is — it's the one inheritance I escaped." She stood, and for a moment the hunt-captain's armor of composure slipped, and underneath was just a tired woman in her thirties who had hated herself for eleven years. "There's a patrol rotation change at moonrise. After that, the east road closes for three days. If you want my help, we leave tonight. If you don't—" she pushed the letter across the table "—keep that. Burn it, sell it, use it to line a birdcage. But read it first. Her testimony is in there. What really happened at the first purge. Who signed the orders. What was *buried*, and where."

"*Buried*," Paloma repeated. The word snagged like a fishhook. "Buried where?"

Vane almost smiled. "Read the letter."

Paloma picked it up. The paper was soft with handling, and her own hands were not quite steady, and she hated that, and hid it, the way she'd hidden everything for twenty years.

Beside her, Tam reached out and touched the back of Vane's hand — one finger, brief, deliberate — and then looked up at Paloma and raised both eyebrows in a way that needed no translation at all.

*Well?*

Paloma exhaled. "Moonrise," she said. "But Captain — if you betray us, I want you to know I won't even be angry. I'll just be disappointed. And I'm told that's worse."

For the first time, Serah Vane laughed — short, rusty, like a hinge unused for years. "Oh, you *are* going to be trouble."

Above them, the flat bell began to ring the hour, and outside, the first light of the false dawn touched the river, and somewhere far to the south, men with torches were already reading a map and looking for a thief who was, at this very moment, drinking small beer with the inquisitor's daughter.

Paloma unfolded the letter, and read the first line, and felt the ground shift beneath twenty years of certainty:

*My dear Serah — they did not die because their magic failed. They died because it worked.*
