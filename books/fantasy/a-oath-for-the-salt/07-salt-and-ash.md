## 7. Salt and Ash

Serah Vane had grown up on her father's stories the way other children grew up on bread: daily, unquestioningly, and without ever noticing how much of her was made of them.

He told them at the supper table, in his study, on the long rides between garrisons when she was small enough to ride pillion and proud enough to pretend she wasn't asleep against his back. He told them as history, which meant he told them as warnings. The old kingdom had been rotten with magic, he said. Swordspeakers who could unmake a man with a word. Charm-wives who soured milk and curdled love. Children born speaking languages that made walls weep. And so the kingdom had done the only wise thing left to it: it had burned the magic out of itself, root and branch, and called the ashes peace.

She had believed him for thirty years. She had helped him hunt for fifteen.

Now she sat in a leaking boathouse upriver from Millbrook with a stolen registry under her arm, a fugitive charm-seller across the fire, a mute boy asleep against that same charm-seller's shoulder, and the distinct, nauseating sensation that everything her father had ever told her had been true — just not in the way he'd told it.

"You're staring," Paloma said, without looking up from the luck-knot she was retying for the third time. Her fingers worked the cord automatically; it was clearly a habit, like prayer or pacing. "People usually find that unnerving, coming from an inquisitor."

"Former," Serah said. "Former inquisitor. I'd appreciate it if you used the whole title."

"All right. Former inquisitor." Paloma glanced up, and there was something wry and tired in her face that Serah was beginning to suspect was simply her face, the one she wore when she wasn't performing shopkeeper. "You're still staring."

"I'm trying to decide if you're going to kill me for what I'm about to tell you."

That got her full attention. Even Tam stirred, one eye opening — the boy slept like a cat, in snatches, always half-listening even when he couldn't hear anything at all. Which, Serah had gathered, was most of the time. His silence had a texture to it. Sitting near him was like sitting inside a held breath.

Paloma set down the knot. "Try me."

* * *

Serah fed the little stove another twist of kindling before she spoke. It was an old habit too — her father's habit, actually, the pause-before-the-revelation, the courtroom rhythm. She hated that she had it.

"When I was nine," she said, "my father took me to see the Salt Door."

Paloma went very still. Not dramatically. Just — still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"It's real?" Tam wrote on his slate, holding it up. The boy communicated in a quick, economical hand, all angles, no wasted strokes.

"It's real," Serah said. "Or it was. There's a place on the far coast where the maps go blank — officially because the surveyors drowned, unofficially because my father's office paid the surveyors to drown, or at least to stop surveying. Beneath it there's a sea cave, and in the sea cave there's a door. Grey stone, taller than a cathedral door, crusted white with salt. My father stood in front of it with forty men and a wagon of black powder and gave the order to blow it."

"And?" Paloma asked quietly.

"And nothing. The powder cracked the rock around it. The door didn't scratch." Serah turned her hands toward the stove. "I remember my father standing there afterward, covered in dust, looking at it the way a man looks at a debt he can't pay. Then he turned around and told us all it was a natural formation and burned the survey notes. But I saw his face. You don't look at a rock like that."

Tam was writing fast now. *What is it a door TO?*

"That," Serah said, "is the part I've spent twenty years digging out of my father's private archive, one stolen page at a time, and I'll thank you both not to interrupt, because I've never said any of this aloud and I find I'm only brave enough to do it once."

Paloma almost smiled. "Once is enough. Go on."

"The old kingdom didn't fall," Serah said. "It amputated itself. That's the truth under all the official histories. Three hundred years ago the realm was — different. Wider. The gifted weren't hunted; they were *infrastructure*. Swordspeakers swore the border-oaths that kept enemy armies out. Tide-speakers kept the harbors. Oath-menders settled disputes without a drop of blood. And then came the Sundering — the civil war nobody names, because the winners wrote the histories and the winners were the ones who wanted magic gone."

"Why would they want that?" Tam wrote. Then, after a moment, he added, smaller: *Power?*

"Power," Serah agreed. "And fear. The last swordspeaker-queen refused to swear her oath to the new council. She said an oath given to cowards was worse than no oath. So the council did the unthinkable — they sealed the whole gift away. Every oath-line, every speaker, bound behind one final gate. They called it the Salt Door because salt is what remains when you boil the sea down to nothing. Salt keeps. Salt preserves. Salt kills what it touches, if you use enough of it." She looked up. "The Door isn't a wall. It's a lock. And the key—"

"—is an oath," Paloma said.

Her voice had gone strange. Flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening for the crack.

Serah nodded slowly. "An oath spoken aloud, in the old tongue, by a speaker of the sword-line. The registry lists the bloodlines, Paloma. I've read it. I stole it for you, but I've also read it, and there are maybe four families left in the whole realm carrying the sword-gift, and every one of them is dead or registered or—" She stopped. "Or hiding behind a charm-stall in Brackwater selling luck-knots to fishermen for copper."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the river muttered against the pilings.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Paloma said.

"Yes, you do," Serah said, gently, because she had learned — late, at great cost, from watching her father break people — that gentleness opened doors that force only welded shut. "Twenty years ago a girl vanished from the Galloway line the week before her registration. The records say she died of marsh fever. The records say a lot of things. But I watched you bind that cut on Tam's arm in the river town, when we thought the patrol was close. You spoke three words under your breath and the bleeding stopped like a hand had closed on it. I've seen field surgeons work, Paloma. That wasn't surgery."

Tam had gone rigid against Paloma's side. He looked from her face to Serah's and back, and his hand found the hem of her sleeve and gripped it.

"You could be wrong," Paloma said.

"I could be wrong," Serah agreed. "And if I'm wrong, you can laugh at me, and we'll keep walking north until we find some other impossible door. But if I'm right—" She leaned forward. "Then you are the last key this country has, and my father knows it, and that's why the edict isn't really about registration at all. Registration is a net for catching exactly one fish. Everything else is chum."

For a long moment Paloma didn't move. Then she laughed — a short, cracked sound with nothing funny in it.

"Chum," she repeated. "Gods. Twenty years of being careful, and I'm chum."

* * *

She got up then, and walked to the boathouse door, and stood looking out at the dark river. Serah let her. This was another thing she'd learned from the wrong teacher: you couldn't rush a confession, you could only make room for it. Her father had never learned that. Her father had never once in his life made room for anything except himself.

Tam padded over to Serah and tugged her sleeve. On his slate: *Don't push her.*

"I wasn't going to," Serah murmured.

*You were,* he wrote. *But you stopped. That counts.* And then, after a pause, in his smallest handwriting, the letters cramped together like they were hiding: *Is it true? Is she like the stories?*

"What stories have you heard?"

He erased the question with his sleeve and wrote a different one. *My mother hummed and the rats left our house. They took her anyway.*

Serah's throat tightened. "Yes," she said. "It's true. All of it, probably. More than the stories."

Across the boathouse, Paloma spoke without turning around. "The Galloways were tide-speakers originally. Did your father's archive mention that? Before the sword-gift came into the line through marriage, four generations back. We were harbor-folk. We kept the Brackwater lights burning when the fog came in." A breath. "My grandmother could call a lost boat home through a storm with her voice alone. My mother could calm a mob with two sentences. And me—" She turned around. In the stove-light her face was unreadable, and then, deliberately, readable: every year of hiding laid out plain. "Me, they taught to whisper."

"Paloma," Serah started.

"No. Let me finish, because I've been finishing this speech in my head for twenty years and it deserves its ending." Paloma came back toward the fire but didn't sit. "When the edict came — the first purges, before your father's time — my teachers hid me in a charm-shop and told me to be small. Be mundane. Sell knots. Never speak above a market-crowd's noise. Never say the words, not even in prayer, not even alone, because the gift lives in the speaking and dies in the silence, and they needed it alive somewhere in the world. So I buried it. I buried it so deep I forgot which parts of me were mine and which parts were the disguise." She looked at her own hands. "And then a crier read your father's edict off a stage in Brackwater, and a woman I've sold charms to for fifteen years was dragged into the square, and I realized the disguise hadn't saved anyone. It had just made me comfortable while everyone else burned."

Tam crossed the floor and pressed himself against her side. She put an arm around him automatically, the way she seemed to do everything that mattered — without deciding to.

"So ask me," Paloma said to Serah. "Ask me plainly. I think I need to hear it asked plainly, once, by someone who isn't holding irons."

Serah stood. It felt absurdly formal and exactly right. "Paloma Galloway. Are you a swordspeaker of the old lines?"

The silence stretched. Tam's silence wrapped around it, thick and total, so that Serah could hear her own heartbeat and the river and nothing else — the whole world holding its breath with them.

"Yes," Paloma said.

Just that. One syllable, spoken at a volume she hadn't permitted herself in twenty years, and the air in the boathouse changed. Serah felt it pass over her skin like the draft before a storm — a pressure, a listening, as though the old kingdom itself had leaned in through the leaky roof to check whether it had heard correctly. The stove-flame stood up straight. The river went quiet for the space of three heartbeats.

Then everything was ordinary again, and Paloma swayed, and Tam caught her elbow, and she laughed shakily and sat down hard on the crate.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's — it's been so long I forgot it *does* that. Forgot it hears me." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Terrifying. It's terrifying. Why does it feel like breathing?"

"Because it is," Serah said quietly. "It's the first breath you've taken in twenty years."

Paloma lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath the fury, lit from somewhere Serah recognized — the look of a woman stepping out of a prison whose walls, it turned out, had been paper all along.

"Liberating," Paloma said, testing the word. "That's the word you want. Terrifying and liberating. Both at once. Like jumping off a cliff and finding out you can swim."

"Can you?" Serah asked. "Swim? Metaphorically. Can you open it? The Door?"

"I don't know." Paloma said it steadily, and Serah respected her for not pretending otherwise. "I know fragments. Nursemaid-taught fragments, whispered under blankets — the cadence of the oath-tongue, a few words of it, the shape of a binding. My teachers died before they could give me the whole thing. Whatever I open, I'll be opening it half-blind." She looked at the registry under Serah's arm. "But there might be more in there than names. The old registries copied from older books. Bloodlines, yes, but sometimes rites. Sometimes the words themselves, recorded by clerks who didn't know what they were copying."

Serah pulled the registry onto her knees. It was a fat, ugly thing, vellum and iron clasps, her father's life's work reduced to a ledger of the doomed. "Then we read it. Tonight. All of it."

*All of it,* Tam wrote, and underlined it twice.

They bent over the book together, three heads and a guttering stove, while the river ran on outside, indifferent and patient. Serah found the Galloway entry within the hour — four generations, cross-referenced, annotated in her father's tight hand — and beneath it, in faded clerk's script copied from some pre-Sundering original, a fragment of verse that made Paloma's breath catch:

*By name and by wound and by those I keep,*
*I speak the salt, and the salt will speak.*

"That's it," Paloma whispered. "That's the opening. That's the first line of the Door-oath." Her finger trembled on the page. "Serah. This is real. This is actually real."

"It's real," Serah said. "Which means my father will already know you're real. And he doesn't send criers and patrols after things that aren't worth catching."

Outside, far downstream toward Millbrook, a bell began to ring. Not the harbor bell — the church bell, urgent, over and over, the alarm-pattern every town in the realm had drilled since the Edict of Ash.

Tam's head came up. Paloma's hand closed around her luck-knot. And Serah felt the cold slide down her spine as she counted the bells and understood: someone had told them where to look.

"They know about the boathouse," she said. "We have minutes, not hours. Take the book. Run."
