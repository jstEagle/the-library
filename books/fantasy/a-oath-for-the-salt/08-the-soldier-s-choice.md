## 8. The Soldier's Choice

The trouble with lying to the crown, Ivo Ren decided on his ninth day of sabotage, was that it required a great deal of paperwork.

Every lie needed a report. Every report needed a signature. And every signature was another stone in the wall between himself and the man he'd been a month ago—the one who would have dragged Paloma Galloway into the square by her collar and called it duty.

He sat in the hunt's field office at Millbrook, a converted granary that smelled of mildew and lamp oil, and composed his latest masterpiece: *Searched the eastern fens as ordered. Found no trace of the thief or the registry. The fens are extensive.*

All true. All useless. He'd spent two days in those fens walking in enormous, deliberate circles, kicking reeds, and once—his finest work—following a heron for an hour because it looked like it might know something.

"Ren." Captain Aldric Vos stood in the doorway, arms crossed, filling most of it. Vos had a face like a dropped brick and the tactical imagination of one. "Your reports are thin."

"The fens are thin on thieves."

"You've found nothing in nine days."

"I've found mud," Ivo said. "Considerable quantities of it. You're welcome to inspect my boots."

Vos did not laugh. Vos had never once laughed, which Ivo privately considered the man's only consistent virtue. "The Inquisitor's men found charm-sellers in Harrow Fen yesterday. Two of them. Old women." He let that sit. "They were registered. They came forward when the edict was proclaimed, like good subjects. And they burned anyway, because their names were also in a certain stolen book, and the book makes them *evidence*."

Ivo kept his face still. Two old women. He made himself think of them plainly, without flinching, because flinching was a luxury he'd surrendered somewhere around the third forged patrol route.

"That's unfortunate," he said.

"*Unfortunate.*" Vos crossed the room and put both hands flat on Ivo's desk, leaning down until they were nose to nose. "You know what I think, Ren? I think you're a careful soldier who's gotten careless. I think your patrols go where nothing is. I think your reports read like a man describing a dream. And I think tomorrow you'll ride at the head of the column to Greyharbor road, where a fisherman swears he saw a woman with a boy matching the registry descriptions—and if she slips through your fingers again, I'll know exactly why."

"A fisherman," Ivo said. "Swearing things. On the coast. Remarkable."

"Tomorrow, Ren. Dawn." Vos straightened. "And Ren? The Inquisitor has eyes everywhere. Even here. Especially here."

He left. Ivo sat for a long moment, listening to the granary settle, and then he took out his report, added a line about the heron—*local wildlife uncooperative*—signed it, and began, quietly and methodically, to pack the bag he'd been keeping packed for eleven days.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd been doing since Brackwater, the sum that never changed: every day he stayed was a day the hunt moved faster than he could slow it. His little sabotages—a mislabeled map here, a delayed dispatch there—were drops in a bucket with a hole in the bottom. Sooner or later Vos would catch him at it, and then everything he'd bought with his lies would be worth exactly nothing.

Sooner had apparently arrived early.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and mean over the Greyharbor road, and Ivo rode at the head of the column like a man riding to his own execution, which, in a sense, he was.

Twelve soldiers behind him. Vos beside him. Ahead, three miles of hedgerows and ditches where a woman with a stolen book might or might not be hiding with a mute boy whose silence, according to the increasingly hysterical reports filtering up from the south, could swallow sound whole.

Ivo had seen Tam exactly once, from a distance, in the market chaos after the theft: a small figure pressed against Paloma Galloway's side, looking at the world with the expression of someone who had already decided not to trust it. He'd thought then: *that child will slow her down*. He'd thought it with relief. He was not proud of that.

"There." Vos pointed. Smoke, thin and domestic, rising from a tanner's cottage set back off the road. "Fisherman said travelers. Fisherman said a boy."

"Fishermen say a great many things."

"Search it anyway."

Ivo swung down and led four men through the gate. The cottage was poor and clean, and its single occupant was a grandmother with flour on her hands, who looked at six armed soldiers in her kitchen with the weary contempt of someone who had survived three kings and intended to survive this one too.

"Bread?" she said.

"We're searching for fugitives, madam."

"Are you. Well, search. Mind the loaves."

They searched. Ivo himself checked the loft, the cellar, the smoke-house, finding nothing but salt pork, a sleeping cat of tremendous opinions, and—tucked behind a loose stone by the hearth—a luck-knot. Braided cord, cheap wool, the kind sold in Brackwater markets for a copper. He knew the knotting style. He'd have known it anywhere. It was the same knot a woman had tied around his brother's wrist eleven years ago before speaking him back together on a tavern floor, and Ivo stood there holding it while four men overturned a grandmother's mattresses, and felt the last load-bearing wall of his old life crack straight down the middle.

She'd been here. Days ago, maybe hours. She'd left a charm behind a stranger's hearthstone the way other people left coins—for luck, for protection, for no reason except kindness.

"Nothing," reported Corporal Dane, dropping the cat, who expressed its views at length.

"Nothing," Ivo agreed, and put the luck-knot in his pocket instead of on the table, which was, though he didn't fully understand it yet, the moment the door closed behind him forever.

Outside, Vos was waiting, and Vos was smiling, which was worse than anything.

"Interesting morning, Ren," he said. "While you were admiring the bread, a rider came up from the south. Seems a tinker saw our fugitives at the river ford yesterday. Also seems the tinker described the soldier who questioned him there and let him walk." Vos's smile widened by a degree, like frost spreading. "Big fellow. Sad eyes. Very thorough reports about herons."

The world went very quiet and very simple, the way it did before battles. Ivo had always liked that about himself—that under pressure, the noise drained out and left only the next step. Right now the next step was: *run, or talk, and talking is dead.*

"Corporal," Vos said pleasantly, "disarm Sergeant Ren."

Dane hesitated. That hesitation—one heartbeat, the same heartbeat Ivo himself had once wasted outside the magistrate's hall—was the only gift anyone gave him that day. Ivo used it. He drove his elbow back into Dane's breastplate, wrenched the man's sword free, and got his back to the cottage wall just as the other three came at him.

He didn't want to hurt them. That was the absurd, doomed heart of it—he'd served beside two of these men for years, drunk with them, buried one of them's father—but wanting and getting had never been acquainted. He fought the way you fight when you're trying to leave a room, not win a war: shoves, flats of the blade, one man tripped into the pig pen, who would presumably dine out on that story for years if anyone ever asked him.

Then Vos drew steel, and the simplicity ended.

Vos was good. Vos had always been good—fast, joyless, economical—and he came in low with none of the theatrical anger of the others, just the flat professional intent of a man removing an obstacle. Ivo parried twice, gave ground, felt the wall at his back, and understood with terrible clarity that Vos was not going to arrest him. The sword point never wavered toward a disabling cut. It sought the throat, the belly, the thigh.

"Sergeant Ren," Vos said, almost gently, pressing him against the stones, "you're a traitor to the crown. There's only one sentence for that, and I'm authorized to carry it out on the spot. Nothing personal."

"Could've fooled me," Ivo gasped, and turned the next thrust, and the world narrowed to iron and breath—

—and afterward he could never make the memory hold still. Had Vos slipped on the wet cobbles? Had Ivo simply been faster? What he remembered, what he carried, was the moment of impact: the strange lightness of a blade going into a body, the look on Vos's face—not pain, not yet, but *surprise*, genuine and almost childlike, a man discovering the story had a different ending than the one he'd rehearsed. Then the weight of him. Then the blood, shockingly red on grey cobbles, steaming faintly in the cold.

Ivo knelt over his commander in the churned mud of a tanner's yard, and the soldiers stood in a ring around him, and nobody moved, because nobody—including Ivo—knew what this was yet.

"He drew first," Ivo said. His voice sounded far away. "You all saw it. He meant to kill me, not take me."

"He's dead," said Dane, staring.

"Yes."

"You killed the captain."

"Yes."

Silence. Somewhere the cat yowled from a windowsill. The grandmother appeared in her doorway, took in the scene—the body, the blood, the six soldiers and their ruined morning—with the expression of a woman adding a grievance to a long list, and said, "Well. Someone owes me a doorframe," because Ivo's shoulder had gone through it during the fight.

And that—absurdly, unforgivably—was what broke him open. Not the killing. The doorframe. He started laughing, or something adjacent to laughing, kneeling in the mud with his commander's blood on his hands, and Dane took a step back as if the laughter were contagious and fatal.

"Ren," Dane said carefully. "Ren. Listen. Put down the sword. We'll say it was an accident. We'll say—"

"No, you won't." Ivo stood. His legs held, which surprised him. "The Inquisitor doesn't do accidents. You'll report it exactly as it happened, because lying for me gets you hanged too. Go on. Report it."

Nobody moved.

"Go on!" he roared, and they went—backing away, mounting up, one of them weeping silently, all of them leaving Vos's body in the yard because no one wanted to be the man who touched it.

Ivo stood alone with the dead man for a while. He closed Vos's eyes, which seemed the least he could do and was somehow the hardest thing he'd done all year. Then he went inside, ate half the grandmother's bread because she insisted and because his hands were shaking too badly to refuse, accepted her blessing—which consisted of her spitting on her thumb and marking his forehead, muttering something that was either an old-country ward or a curse on the crown, possibly both—and walked out the back gate onto the moor road with a sword, a bag, a stolen luck-knot, and no country.

* * *

He walked all day. Walking was good. Walking kept the thinking from catching up.

By the time the light went amber over the hills, he had assembled the facts of his new life and laid them out like kit inspection: he was a traitor. His face would be on proclamations by week's end. His wages, his pension, his name—all of it belonged now to a man who had died in a tanner's yard, and what remained was a large unarmed problem moving north on foot.

North. Because north was where the roads remembered, according to a drunk ferryman's song; north was where the salt road ran toward Greyharbor and the sea; and north, above all, was wherever Paloma Galloway wasn't, because the last thing she needed was a hunted soldier blundering into her trail and bringing the hunt with him like fleas.

That was the plan. That was a good plan. It survived until the second night, when he took shelter in a ruined chapel off the moor road—a roofless nave, stars where the ceiling should be, an altar some pious soul had swept clean even now—and found it occupied.

A fire burned low in the corner, banked small and careful. Around it: a boy asleep under a cloak, curled tight as a fern; a dark-haired woman in a man's coat sitting bolt upright with a knife already in her hand; and beside her, a severe-faced woman in traveling leathers who had a crossbow leveled at his chest with the bored precision of long practice.

"Don't," said the dark-haired woman, and he realized it was her, older and thinner and harder than the tavern-floor memory, but unmistakably her. Paloma Galloway. "Don't move, don't speak, and don't tell me you're not following us, because I've been watching you walk toward this chapel for two miles, and nobody walks that badly toward something by accident."

"I'm not—" Ivo stopped. Closed his eyes. Started again, because she deserved better than reflexes. "My name is Ivo Ren. I was the sergeant who saw you leave the magistrate's hall in Brackwater and lied about your face. I've been slowing the hunt since. Today my captain caught me at it, and I killed him, and now I'm the thing I used to chase." He opened his eyes. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to let me sleep by your fire and be gone at dawn. That's all."

The crossbow hadn't wavered. The severe woman glanced at Paloma. "He's telling the truth or he's the best liar in the realm," she said. "His hands haven't stopped shaking since he walked in."

"They shake because I killed a man eight hours ago," Ivo said.

"They shake," the woman said, "because you're still holding that sword like it's the only thing between you and the dark, and you need to put it down before the boy wakes up and sees it."

Ivo looked at the boy. Small. Asleep. One hand clutched a stub of charcoal even in sleep, and there were drawings on the chapel floor around him—horses, waves, a door—sketched with the fierce fluency of a child who couldn't say a word and had decided the world would listen anyway.

Ivo unbuckled his sword belt and set it down on the stones, ten feet away, where everyone could see it. It sounded very loud in the silence.

Paloma studied him for a long moment. He braced for hatred—it would have been fair, it would have been correct—but what crossed her face instead was something wearier and sadder, the look of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"The magistrate's hall," she said quietly. "You hesitated."

"One heartbeat."

"One heartbeat's the difference between the man you were and the man you're becoming." She looked at the fire, then at him. "It's not enough. But it's a start, and I'm too tired to be wise about it. Serah, put the bow down. He can have the fire's edge and the cold side of the chapel, and if he moves wrong in the night, I'll know."

"How?" Ivo asked.

"Because I'm a swordspeaker, sergeant," Paloma Galloway said, and the words came out of her like a blade being drawn after twenty years in its sheath, "and this whole ruin is full of echoes, and echoes talk to me. Sleep well."

Serah lowered the crossbow. The boy stirred, blinked at the stranger by the fire, and—Ivo would remember this all his life—simply looked at him, took in the mud and the blood and the shaking hands, and then reached over and pushed half his blanket toward him across the stones.

No way back. Standing at the edge of a stranger's fire with half a blanket and no country, Ivo finally understood what the phrase actually meant. It didn't mean lost. It meant *here*.

Far to the south, unseen, the first proclamations bearing his face were already being nailed to doors.
