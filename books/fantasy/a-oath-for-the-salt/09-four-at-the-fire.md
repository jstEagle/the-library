## 9. Four at the Fire

The chapel had lost its roof to some ancient fire and its god to some older indifference, but it kept its walls, and its walls kept out the wind, and after eleven days of ditches and hedgerows and sleeping in a hay barn with a badger who had opinions, Paloma would have called a leaking stone shell *home* if she'd been fool enough to use that word about anything.

She didn't. She'd learned that lesson twice over.

"Mind the third step," she said, hauling Tam up by the wrist as they came through the broken doorway. "It's a liar."

Tam gave her one of his looks—the flat, unimpressed stare he'd perfected in their weeks together, the look of a boy who had never once spoken and had therefore never once wasted a word—and stepped neatly over the third step on his way to the hearth corner, where he immediately began arranging the kindling with the gravity of a general positioning troops.

The boy could not make a sound. That was the gift the edict wanted him dead for: silence that spread. Stand close enough to Tam when he wished it, and your footsteps went quiet as snowfall, your voice swallowed before it left your teeth, a whole small world hushed. Out on the salt road it had saved them more times than Paloma could count. In here, it mostly meant she couldn't yell at anyone, which she suspected the boy considered a feature.

The others arrived at dusk.

She heard Serah first—of course she did; Serah Vane moved through the world like a woman announcing herself to it, boots striking stone, sword-hilt clinking against her belt buckle, every inch of her a declaration that she had nothing to hide. Which was, Paloma was learning, its own kind of armor.

"I found rabbits," Serah announced from the doorway, holding up two of them by the ears like trophies. "And I found this." She jerked her thumb backward.

Ivo Ren came through the door leaning on a hazel stick, gray-faced, his left leg bound in a bandage that had been white three days ago and was now the color of an honest confession. He looked at the chapel, at the fire Tam was coaxing along, at Paloma, and said, "You're camping in a church."

"It's a ruin."

"It's a church."

"It's a ruin *with a roof-shaped memory*," Paloma said. "Sit down before you fall down, Sergeant. You're bleeding through again."

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of porridge."

"Porridge is a respectable color."

"*Sit down.*"

He sat down. It occurred to Paloma, watching him lower himself against the wall with the careful economy of a man rationing his pain, that three months ago she had been hiding from this exact man, and that now she fussed over him like a hen, and that life was a thing no charm could predict. She'd sold luck-knots for twenty years. Not one of them had ever warned her about this.

* * *

Tam cooked. This had become the arrangement without anyone discussing it: Tam cooked, because Tam cooked better than any of them, because Tam had apparently spent his orphan years charming—no, *earning*—kitchen scraps from half the cooks between Brackwater and the river country, and had absorbed techniques the way other children absorbed songs. He spitted the rabbits on green sticks, rubbed them with dried herbs from Paloma's pouch, and set them turning over the fire with the serene authority of a much older soul in a twelve-year-old body.

Serah watched him work and shook her head slowly. "I trained under a siege-cook for two years. He'd have wept."

Tam shrugged modestly, which from Tam meant he preened thoroughly.

Ivo sat propped against the wall with his leg straight out, pale but present, and Paloma caught him doing the thing he did sometimes—watching Tam with an expression she couldn't name. Not pity. Something rawer. She'd seen it before, the night the fever took him properly, when he'd murmured a name that wasn't his own. *Aldric.* A brother, Serah had told her later, quietly, in the tone people used for graves. Dead six years. The wound that had killed him was the kind Paloma's gift could have closed, if there'd been a swordspeaker there, if there'd been an oath spoken aloud, if the world hadn't spent a century teaching people like her to sew their mouths shut.

She turned back to the fire before the thought could finish sharpening.

"So," Serah said, settling cross-legged near the flames and producing, from somewhere in her pack, a flask. "We're four days from Greyharbor, assuming Ren can ride, assuming the weather holds, assuming my father hasn't—" She stopped. Took a drink. Passed the flask to Paloma. "Assuming."

"Assuming," Paloma agreed, and drank. It was plum brandy, harsh and honest, the best thing she'd tasted since the road began.

They ate. The rabbits were excellent. For a while there was only the fire, the wind worrying the broken rafters above, and Tam making small contented gestures at his own cooking like a craftsman admiring his joinery.

It was, Paloma thought later, almost inevitable that it couldn't last. Four people do not flee a kingdom together, bleed together, bury a lead on the hunters together, and sit around a fire without the old debts surfacing eventually. Debts are like drowned things. Hold them under long enough and they come up gasping anyway.

It was Ivo who broke first, though nobody would have guessed it of him.

"This is strange," he said, to the fire more than to anyone.

"What is?" Serah asked.

"This." He gestured with his chin—at the fire, at the food, at the four of them. "Three months ago I was hunting her." He nodded at Paloma. "Two months ago you were hunting both of us. Now we're sharing rabbits in a ruin and the boy's cooking is genuinely better than anything in the garrison mess, and nobody's said it out loud yet, so I'll say it. It's strange."

"Thank you," Tam signed, with a small bow. He'd taught them all a handful of signs by now, picked up in firelit hours, and *thank you* was the one he used most often, usually ironically.

"I mean it," Ivo said. "How did we get here?"

"Badly," said Serah.

"Slowly," said Paloma.

"Rabbits," Tam signed, and pointed at the spit, and for a moment it was almost easy, and then it wasn't, because Serah set down her flask and looked into the fire and said, in the voice of a woman walking toward something on purpose:

"If we're saying true things. Then here's one." She rolled her shoulders like a soldier squaring under a pack. "When I was nineteen, newly sworn, my father sent me to a village called Millbrook to verify a registry claim. A charm-seller. Old woman. Sold luck-knots." Her eyes flicked to Paloma, held there, didn't flinch away. "Knots exactly like yours."

Paloma went very still. Beside her, she felt Tam go still too.

"She wasn't registered. She wouldn't register. And the law was the law, and I was nineteen, and my father was watching, so I—" Serah's jaw worked. "I burned her stock in the square. All of it. Every knot, every charm, everything she'd made, in front of the whole village. And then I arrested her, and she went to the ash-house, and I told myself for nine years that I'd been merciful, because I burned the charms instead of the woman." She laughed, short and terrible. "That's what mercy looks like in my family. Choosing which fire."

The wind filled the silence above them, rattling loose slate somewhere in the dark.

Paloma found that her hands had curled around each other in her lap, knuckle over knuckle, the way they did when the old fear came. She made herself uncurl them. Made herself speak.

"What was her name?" she asked.

Serah blinked. "What?"

"The charm-seller. What was her name?"

"...Maren. Maren Ostwick."

Paloma breathed out slowly. "Then she isn't one of mine. My teacher was Odile Ferran, and her teacher before her, and our line ran the coast towns—Brackwater, Greyharbor, the salt marshes. We knew every knot-seller south of the river, because you have to, because if one of us slips, the rest of us burn." She met Serah's eyes. "Maren Ostwick wasn't swordspeaker line. She was hedge-gifted. Small craft. Luck and weather-sense, maybe. The kind of gift that never hurt anything bigger than a headache."

"That doesn't make it better."

"No," Paloma agreed. "It makes it worse, actually. Because she was harmless, and you knew it, and you burned her work anyway, because the law was the law and your father was watching." She said it without heat, and was surprised by that. She'd expected rage. What she found instead was something colder and more useful: the plain arithmetic of it. "But you're telling me this at a fire, with your sword across your knees and my knife within reach of you, which means either you're very stupid or you want something from me. And you've never once struck me as stupid."

"I want—" Serah stopped. Started again. "I don't know what I want. Absolution, maybe. Which is a stupid thing to ask a woman whose whole life I've spent trying to end."

"It is stupid," Paloma said. "Ask anyway."

Serah stared at her. Then, quietly, wreckedly: "Can you give it?"

"No," said Paloma.

Tam's head came up. Ivo shifted against the wall.

"Not tonight, and maybe not ever, and certainly not with words, because words are cheap and I sell them for copper, so I know exactly how cheap they are." Paloma leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the grey starting at her temples. "Here's what I can give you, Serah Vane. Work. You carry that woman's name the way I carry mine—out loud, where it costs something. When this is done, if we live, you find her village and you tell them what you did and you let them hate you properly, and then you rebuild what you burned. Knot by knot, if it takes the rest of your life. That's the price. Forgiveness isn't a door that opens because you knock. It's a road, and you haven't walked it yet."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the dark outside, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"All right," Serah said finally, hoarse. "All right. Knot by knot."

"And you'll cook tomorrow," Tam signed, "because I'm tired."

A sound escaped Serah that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Deal."

* * *

Later, when Serah had taken first watch on the broken altar—Ivo had argued it should be him; Ivo had lost the argument to a leg and a vote of two—Paloma sat down beside Ivo against the chapel wall, close enough to share warmth, far enough to be polite about it.

"Heard all that?" she asked.

"Hard not to. Your voice carries."

"It's a curse."

"It's a gift," he said, and then winced, hearing what he'd said, and she watched him decide not to take it back. Good. She was tired of everyone taking things back.

They sat awhile. The fire settled. Tam had fallen asleep sitting up, the way he always did, as if sleep were a place he visited rather than surrendered to, and Paloma reached over and eased him sideways until his head found her shoulder. He sighed without sound. Even his breathing was quiet. Sometimes she forgot he was there, and then his hand would find her sleeve in the dark and hold on, and she would remember, and the remembering was the sweetest ache she owned.

"Ivo," she said. "Your turn."

"My turn for what?"

"Grievances. Confessions. Whatever's eating you besides the fever. Everyone else has paid at the fire tonight. Fair's fair."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he'd refuse. Then: "You know what I did. Before."

"I know you hunted gifted folk. I know you were good at it."

"I was *very* good at it." His voice was flat as slate. "Eleven years. Do you know how you get good at finding people who don't want to be found? You learn to think like them. You learn the knots they tie, the marks they leave, the little lies they tell—a limp that comes and goes, a cough in winter only. I learned it so well that when I finally met one of you face to face, I recognized her in half a heartbeat." He turned his head. His eyes were dark and steady and full of something he'd been carrying since Brackwater. "A woman selling charms by the fish market. Luck-knots. I bought one. I paid real copper for it, because I already knew, and I wanted to see if she'd flinch."

Paloma remembered. Of course she remembered. The tall soldier with the careful eyes, the one customer she'd ever had whose hands checked the knot-work like a man reading a letter. She'd told herself he was just thorough.

"You didn't report me."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because my brother was dying," Ivo said simply, "and you fixed him. An oath, spoken out loud, in a gutter behind the Rope & Anchor, with the watch coming and me begging a stranger in a shawl to save him. And she did. And then she vanished, and I carried that for six years like a coal in my chest, and when the Edict came down and they handed me the hunt, I told myself I'd find her and warn her first. And then I found her stealing the registry, and I stood in that hallway with my hand on my whistle, and I hesitated." He laughed, without humor. "One heartbeat. That's all it took. One heartbeat and I'm lying to my commander, sabotaging patrols, branded, shot at, and eating rabbit in a ruin with the woman I was sent to kill. One heartbeat, Paloma. That's the whole distance between a good soldier and a traitor."

"Or," Paloma said, "that's the whole distance between a man following orders and a man following his oath. Soldiers swear oaths too, Ivo. Most of them just forget which part mattered."

He looked at her for a long moment. "Is that forgiveness?"

"No," she said, and was almost gentle about it. "It's arithmetic. You saw me and didn't sell me—that buys you a debt owed, not a debt cleared. But you've been paying it since. Every patrol you misdirected. Every mile you've limped on that leg. I keep accounts, Sergeant. It's the charm-seller in me." She paused. "You're not clear. But you're not owing anymore, either. You're even. From here, whatever you do, you do it as one of us, not as penance. There's a difference. Penance runs out. Belonging doesn't."

Ivo exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he was carrying. "You talk like a priest."

"I talk like a woman who's had twenty years to rehearse conversations she never got to have." She nudged his good knee with hers. "Sleep. Your watch is second."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat by the banked fire with Tam's weight warm against her side and the registry wrapped in oilcloth in her lap, and she turned the evening over like a knot she was checking for flaws.

Forgiveness argued for, not granted. Roads, not doors. It would hold. It would have to hold, because in four days they'd stand in front of a Door that demanded the one thing she'd refused for twenty years, and she could not afford cracks in the four of them. Found families weren't built from love alone. They were built from debts honestly counted and fires honestly sat at, and she thought—turning Maren Ostwick's name over, and Aldric's, and the heartbeat in the magistrate's hall—that theirs might just hold.

She was still awake when Serah came softly back from watch, crouched beside her, and touched her shoulder.

"Paloma. You need to see this."

The night had gone strangely, terribly quiet—even the owl had stopped—and out past the chapel walls, on the dark line of the road, lanterns were moving. Many lanterns. Moving in the patient, spreading pattern of a search line that had found something.

And at the edge of Tam's silence, faint as a memory, Paloma heard the thing she'd dreaded since Brackwater: a hunting horn, sounding her name.
