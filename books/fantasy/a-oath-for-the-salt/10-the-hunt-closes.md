## 10. The Hunt Closes

The letter came with the morning milk, which was how Serah knew it was from her father. Inquisitor Aldous Vane had never in his life sent a message that did not arrive dressed as something harmless—a summons folded inside a hymnal, a threat tucked into an account of grain prices. He believed, with the same unshakable faith he brought to his interrogations, that cruelty worked best when it wore an apron.

She found it wedged in the crook of the chapel door, beside the chipped jug the dairy cart left for them twice a week. The wax seal was unbroken. The handwriting on the outside said only: *For my daughter.*

Serah stood in the gray light and did not touch it for a long moment. Behind her, inside the ruined chapel, she could hear Ivo arguing with Paloma about whether a stew could be over-salted—Paloma maintaining that it could not, Ivo maintaining that everything could—and Tam's soft, soundless laughter, the kind you felt in the air rather than heard. A small domestic warmth, fragile as frost on a window.

Her father's letters did not come to offer reconciliation. She broke the seal anyway, because her hands were already moving, because twenty-nine years of obedience lived in her fingers whether she wanted them there or not.

*Serah.*

No endearment. He had stopped using them when she was fourteen, the year she first asked him why the registry needed children's names in it.

*You have been seen. A peddler at Millford Cross described a woman of your height coloring traveling with a charm-witch, a mute boy, and a traitor. I confess I hoped the description was wrong. I have prayed on it. God has not answered me clearly, so I will ask you instead, as your father and not your judge: come home. Bring the book. Your mother's health has declined this season. She asks for you at supper every evening. You know how she worries when a place is set and unfilled.*

Serah read the last line three times. *Your mother's health has declined.* Her mother had written to her eight months ago, before Serah's flight became known, complaining—with relish, in her looping hand—that she had never been sturdier and that the inquisitor's cook was wasting good butter on invalid dishes out of pure spite.

Her mother was fine. Her mother was bait.

And her father knew that Serah would know it, and had written it anyway, because that was the message beneath the message: *I know that you know. Come anyway.*

She looked up at the chapel. Through the broken roof, morning sky, pale as skimmed milk. Somewhere beyond the hills, in the city of Corvane with its black-towered inquisitorial seat, her mother was setting tables and worrying about butter and being watched by men with ledgers.

"Bad news?" Paloma had come to the doorway without making a sound, which for a woman who carried two swords under her shawl should have been impossible. Her gray eyes went to the letter, then to Serah's face, and Serah watched her do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic Serah herself had just finished. "Ah," Paloma said quietly. "Family."

"My father knows we're together." Serah's voice came out flat, official, the inquisitor's-daughter voice she hated. "He's offering amnesty if I bring back the registry."

"And if you don't?"

"Then he stops writing polite letters." She folded the paper along its original creases, precisely, because her father had taught her precision before he'd taught her anything else. "There's more. There's always more with him. He wouldn't burn his one chance at negotiation in a letter. He'll want—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. "He'll want leverage. Something I can't refuse in front of witnesses."

"Your mother."

"My mother."

* * *

They gathered around the fire pit in the nave, where the pews had been burned for warmth weeks ago and the saints' niches gaped empty, their statues sold or smashed by some earlier generation of reformers. Ivo sat with his arm bound and propped, still feverish at the edges but lucid, which was more than any of them had dared hope after the bolt. Tam sat close against Paloma's side, sketching in the dirt floor with a stick: quick, sure strokes that somehow always made sense even though he'd never been taught.

Serah laid the letter on a flat stone between them and told them what it said, all of it, including the part about supper.

"He wants us to walk into Corvane," Ivo said. His voice was rougher than it used to be; the bolt had gone through muscle near his shoulder, and fever had scraped it raw. "Into the seat of the inquisition. With the registry."

"He doesn't know we have the registry anymore," Serah said. "He suspects. That's different. My father never moves on suspicion alone—he builds a case. This letter is him building a case. If I come home, I'm either a prodigal or a prisoner, and either way the book comes with me. If I don't come home..." She turned the letter over, as if something might be written on the back. There wasn't. "Then my mother pays the forfeit. Not the pyre—he wouldn't dare, not publicly, she's too well connected—but the cells. The questioning. And my father's questioning doesn't leave marks you can see, which is worse."

Paloma had gone very still. It was a stillness Serah had learned to read in the weeks since Millford Cross: the charm-seller went quiet not when she was afraid but when she was deciding. "How long have we got?"

"The letter says she 'asks for you at supper.' Supper is tonight. That's not a deadline, that's theater. He'll give me three days. Four, if he thinks sentiment runs deep." Serah almost smiled. "It doesn't. He tested that when I was twelve. Left a stray dog I'd adopted in a pen with one of the question-chambers' instruments for a week to see if I'd lie about where I'd been. I lied. He knew I lied. He let me keep the dog."

"Charming man," Ivo said.

"He's the best inquisitor the crown has ever had," Serah said simply. "That's not praise. It's weather. You plan around it."

Tam tugged Paloma's sleeve. When she leaned down, he took the stick and drew, fast: a tower, a table, a woman seated at the table, and then—crossing out the table—a road, leading away from the tower, and the woman walking on the road, free. He looked up at them all, defiant, as if daring anyone to say it couldn't be done.

"A rescue," Ivo translated. "Straight in, straight out."

"It's what I'd want to do," Paloma admitted. She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "It's also exactly what he wants. Every road into Corvane funnels through two gates. He's had twenty years to map every smuggler's path, every cellar door, every sympathetic priest. We wouldn't be walking into a trap. We'd be walking into a trap he built before I was born and has been maintaining ever since."

"So we don't go," Ivo said. "We run. Greyharbor's still ahead of us. The Door is still ahead of us. I hate saying it—" and his face said how much he hated it, this man who had once hunted people like Paloma and now sat bleeding among them— "but four lives against one. Even one we love."

The silence that followed was the worst kind: the kind where everyone agreed and no one could bear to say so.

It was Tam who broke it. He stood up, walked to the flat stone, and picked up Serah's letter. Then he held it out to her, solemn as a priest presenting relics, and pointed—at the words, at her, at the road south toward Corvane, and then pressed both fists against his chest.

"You can't mean to go alone," Serah said.

He nodded. Firmly. Twice.

"No," Paloma said, and the word came out with an edge none of them had heard from her before—not the gentle merchant's voice, not the tired fugitive's voice, but something older, forged somewhere Serah hadn't seen yet. "Absolutely not. You are fifteen years old and your gift is silence, not invisibility. Corvane would eat you."

Tam's jaw set. He crouched, drew again: the tower, the woman, and this time himself, small figure at the base, holding up the walls. Then he drew Paloma, stick-figure with two lines for swords, standing outside the walls, looking up. He tapped each figure in turn. *I go in. You wait. No one else risks.*

"That's not how family works," Paloma said, and then stopped, visibly startled by her own word.

Nobody moved. The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere in the roofless rafters, a dove muttered to itself.

"We're doing this badly," Serah said at last, rubbing her eyes. "All of us. Arguing options like it's a war council, when my father designed this letter to make sure we'd argue. Division is his opening move. He splits the enemy's council before he splits anything else." She stood, pacing the length of the nave, boots crunching on fallen plaster. "So let me tell you what I actually think, and then someone tell me I'm wrong, because I've been wrong about him my whole life and I'd welcome the practice."

They waited.

"He expects grief to make us stupid. Grief, or guilt, or love—any of the levers. So the counter-move isn't to feel less. It's to feel *precisely*. We go—but not through the gates, not down his roads, not on his schedule. My mother isn't held in the inquisitorial seat itself; he'd never risk her near the question-chambers, he's sentimental in ways he'd deny under torture. She'll be at our townhouse. Two guards on the door, maybe three, because he trusts the streets more than the cells. And there's one thing he can't plan for." She looked at Tam. "Him. My father has files on every gift in the realm. He has nothing—*nothing*—on a boy whose presence unmakes sound. Tam walks into that house and the guards won't hear him coming. They won't hear the door. They won't hear each other shout."

Ivo stared at him. "That's monstrous."

"It's a gift," Paloma said sharply, "and he's a child, and he doesn't have to do anything he doesn't choose."

But Tam was already nodding, already drawing it: the townhouse, the guards with their mouths open and no sound coming out, the woman walking free down a street drawn with a confident, joyful line. He looked up at Serah, and his eyes were bright and terrified and certain, and Serah thought: *he's braver than I was at thirty.*

"I taught children like him," Paloma said quietly. It was the first time she'd spoken of it directly since the fire at the chapel, the night the old grievances had surfaced and been argued to exhaustion rather than resolution. "Before the edict. I taught them to hide, because hiding was all I had to teach. And I swore—I swore on salt—that no child of mine would ever be spent buying an adult's escape." Her hands had curled into fists. "So we don't spend him. We ask him. And if he chooses it, we build the plan around keeping him alive, not around using him up. Those are different things. Vane builds plans around spending people. It's why he'll lose."

"And if it goes wrong?" Ivo asked. "If it's already a trap inside the trap? He wrote that letter knowing you'd read between the lines. What if the reading is the trap?"

"Then we're dead in Corvane," Serah said, "instead of dead in a ditch in three weeks when the hunt catches up anyway. At least in Corvane we get to choose the ground." She paused. "And there's one more thing. One thing I haven't told you, because until this morning I wasn't sure it was true."

Paloma's head came up.

"The registry. When I copied certain pages—before I left my father's house, before any of this—I kept a few names back. Names marked for immediate seizure. His first-wave list." She swallowed. "My mother's name is on it. Has been for six years. He wrote her into the registry himself, in his own hand, filed under *assets, unregistered, familial discretion.*"

The fire popped. Ivo said, softly, a curse he probably learned in the army.

"She's gifted," Serah said. "Small craft. Hearth-craft, luck-craft, the kind that looks like a happy house. She's been hiding inside my father's own home for thirty years, and he has *known*, and he has kept her name in his pocket like a coin he might someday spend." Her voice cracked, finally, on the last of it. "This letter isn't bait. It's a bill. He's decided to collect."

Tam crossed the room and put his small hand flat on Serah's arm, and where he touched her, the world went quiet—not silent, but *quiet*, the way snow quiets a field, and Serah realized she was shaking, and that he was giving her his gift the way other children gave a found shell or a smooth stone. Held. Held. Held.

"All right," Paloma said, rising, and the two swords shifted under her shawl like sleeping animals waking. "Corvane. But we do it my way, and my way is this: nobody spends anybody. We go in together, we come out together, and if the arithmetic ever comes down to one life against the rest, we refuse the sum. We find a third answer. That's the oath I'm setting over this company, and I'm told"—the ghost of her old merchant's smile, wry and warm—"my oaths have a way of binding."

Outside, the morning had brightened without their noticing. The road south lay open and ordinary, hedgerows white with dust, utterly innocent of the black towers waiting at the end of it.

Tam released Serah's arm, picked up his stick, and drew one last thing in the dirt before they broke camp: a small house, a woman walking out of its door, and above it, drawn with fierce, careful strokes, four figures standing shoulder to shoulder.

Then he scrubbed it out with his heel, because some pictures weren't meant to last.

They were on the road by midmorning. And behind them, unseen, unremarked, a dairy cart rolled past the empty chapel, and the driver glanced at the letter-sermon he'd been paid to leave in the doorjamb, and memorized which way the fugitives' tracks ran—south, toward Corvane, exactly as the inquisitor had promised they would.
