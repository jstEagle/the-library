## 11. What We Owe the Dead

The grave had no marker. That was the first thing Paloma noticed, and the thing that made her hands go cold on the reins.

She had expected *something*. A cairn, a row of river stones, even a fence post with a rusted bell hung from it—the way country folk marked the places they were ashamed of, so they could avoid them without forgetting them entirely. But the hillside above the village of Marrow's End was just grass, long and silver-green in the autumn wind, sloping down to a creek that chattered to itself as if it had never heard of grief.

"Here?" Tam asked—not asked, really; he tilted his head at her and raised his eyebrows, which was how he asked most things now. Three weeks on the road had taught her his whole vocabulary. She was fluent in the boy's eyebrows.

"Here," she said. "According to Serah."

Serah Vane stood a little apart, arms crossed against the wind, looking at the unmarked slope the way a woman looks at a ledger she knows has been cooked. "My father's first commission," she said. "Before the Edict of Ash there was the Quiet Year, and before the Quiet Year there was this. Forty-one swordspeakers, taken in a single night from the chapter house at Marrow's End. The records call it 'the relocation of a doctrinal irregularity.'" Her mouth twisted. "I used to copy those records out as penmanship practice when I was nine years old."

Paloma looked at her. "You copied death lists for *penmanship*."

"It's a very disciplined hand, my father's." Serah said it flatly, but Paloma had learned to hear the flinch underneath. There was always a flinch under Serah, like a stone under shallow water.

Ivo said nothing. He'd said very little since the chapel, since the meal where old grievances had been laid out on the ground between them like knives nobody picked up. He stood by the horses with his hand on the pommel of a sword he still wasn't sure he was allowed to carry, being a branded traitor and all, and watched the hillside with the particular stillness of a man who has decided that whatever happens next is not his to start.

Tam tugged Paloma's sleeve and pointed at the grass.

At first she saw nothing. Then she did—and her stomach dropped through the earth and kept going.

The grass grew wrong. Not dead, not scorched—*arranged*. It leaned in long, faint spirals, dozens of them, scattered across the slope like fingerprints pressed into dough. Where each spiral turned tightest, the grass grew taller and darker, fed by something underneath.

"They're not graves," Paloma said quietly. "They're one grave."

"The chapter house cellar," Serah said. "Collapsed and filled in after. They didn't even dig separate pits." She paused. "I'm sorry. I thought you should know what you were walking into."

"No," Paloma said. "You were right to tell me." And then, because the alternative was standing there feeling it: "Though I'll note that a warning like that lands easier if you don't deliver it in the voice of a tax assessment."

Something flickered across Serah's face that on another woman would have been a laugh. On Serah it was more like a door opening an inch and then thinking better of it.

Paloma dismounted. Her knees complained; twenty years of hunching over a charm-work bench will do that. She took her satchel off the saddle and held it a moment without opening it.

Inside were the things she'd sworn she'd never use again. Chalk. Salt. A knife with a bone handle that had belonged to a woman named Odile Ferrant, who had taught Paloma the swordspeaker's rites when Paloma was eleven and all elbows and secrets, and who had died in a purge so small and local that no registry ever bothered to record it. Paloma had carried the knife for twenty years the way you carry a splinter too deep to work out.

"You don't have to do this today," Ivo said. He'd come up beside her without her hearing, which should have frightened her and instead just felt like weather. "We can camp. Come back at dawn. Rites are better at dawn, aren't they? Everything's better at dawn."

"That's fishermen's talk."

"I was a fisherman's son before I was anything else."

She looked at him. He looked back, steady, patient, wearing the face of a man who had once lain dying while she spoke an oath over him and had never once, in all the years since, pretended it hadn't happened.

"Dawn's four hours off," she said. "And I've been walking away from this hillside for twenty years. If I wait for dawn I'll find a reason to walk away till spring." She opened the satchel. "Besides. If I do it badly, I'd rather have witnesses who'll lie to me about it afterward."

"We won't lie to you," Tam signed, solemn as a magistrate.

"See, that's the problem with this company," Paloma muttered, and went up the hill.

* * *

She chalked the circle freehand, because Odile had insisted a swordspeaker's hand must know the shapes without guides, and hers had gone soft and uncertain from two decades of drawing only the safe little sigils that sold for copper—luck-knots, calm-the-sea knots, keep-your-husband-off-the-bottle knots. The great spirals came back to her slowly, like a language half-remembered from childhood, and where her memory failed her, her hand seemed to remember anyway, moving with a sureness that made her throat tighten.

Salt next. She walked the circle pouring it from a clay jar, and the wind, which had been worrying the grass all afternoon, went quiet all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters.

Then the knife. Then the words.

She had avoided these words for twenty years. She had told herself it was prudence—oaths spoken aloud carry, carry far, carry to the wrong ears—and that was true, and it had kept her alive, and she did not regret the living. But underneath the prudence there had been shame, plain and simple, and she knew it, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had hidden while others burned. She had sold charms to the widows of men the crown conscripted and called it resistance. She had taught three children letters and small magics in a Brackwater cellar and then stopped teaching them the moment teaching became dangerous, and every one of their names was in the stolen registry under her belt right now, and she had not spoken one word of mourning for any soul the purges had taken, because mourning aloud was the first step toward speaking aloud, and speaking aloud was death.

Well. Death had been following her for weeks now, polite as a debt collector. It could hear what she had to say.

"Sisters of the salt," she began, in the old tongue, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she started again, and cracked again, and Tam made a small motion with both hands—*again, again*, the sign he used when she fumbled a knot or mispronounced a word of his own invented language—and she laughed despite everything, wet-eyed, and started a third time.

This time the words came whole.

She gave them the rite entire: the naming of the ground, the apology of the living, the invitation. *Come up, come up, you who were put down without your names. Come up and be counted.* The salt caught the low sun and glowed along the spiral like veins of light in dark bread. The wind returned, but changed—it moved in circles now, walking the spirals, stirring the tall grass in slow turns.

And then the echoes came.

Not ghosts. Paloma had seen ghosts, or the cheap carnival frauds thereof, and this was nothing like that. These were echoes—the way a struck bell keeps ringing in the metal long after the sound leaves the air. The dead of Marrow's End had been swordspeakers, and swordspeakers were oath-keepers, and oaths, once spoken, do not wholly end. Their last words were still in the ground here, soaked into it like wine into bread, and her rite had given those words permission to finish falling.

She felt them rather than heard them, mostly. Pressure behind the eyes. Grief that arrived pre-owned, worn soft by forty-one other wearers. But some of it resolved into sense, into images pressed against the inside of her skull:

A woman setting her students' names to a counting song so they wouldn't be afraid. An old man laughing—he was actually *laughing*, at the end, telling the soldiers that iron couldn't hold a tide. A young man, barely grown, repeating a name over and over, and Paloma understood with a lurch that it was the name of someone he loved who was already safe, already gone ahead, and he was spending his last breath on gratitude.

And beneath all of it, threaded through it, one recurring thing. A direction. A pull, gentle and immense, like the tug of the moon on water. Every echo bent toward it eventually, the way all conversations bend toward the thing that matters.

*Greyharbor,* the echoes said, in forty-one voices that were not voices. *The drowned place. The door below the bells.*

Paloma came back to herself sitting in the grass, cheek wet, Tam's small hand gripping her sleeve, Ivo kneeling on her other side with his hand hovering an inch from her shoulder, not quite daring to land. Serah stood outside the chalked circle, hat in her hands, head bowed—whether out of respect or old habit of prayer, Paloma couldn't tell.

"How long?" Paloma rasped.

"An hour," Ivo said. "You sang part of it. I didn't know the words, but—" He stopped. Started again. "It sounded like my brother's name was in it somewhere."

"It might have been." She wiped her face with her wrist. "The rite names everyone anyone has ever lost. That's rather the point of it. It's a very thorough guilt-trip of a prayer. Odile designed it, and she was a thorough woman with a cruel streak about making people feel things."

Tam tapped her arm and signed, fast, his hands flying: *What did they say? What did they show?*

"They showed me where we're going." She got her feet under her, stood, swayed, was steadied by Ivo's hand under her elbow this time actually landing, and found she didn't mind. "Greyharbor. The drowned cathedral. The Salt Door isn't a legend somebody misplaced—it's exactly where the stories say, which is why nobody's found it, because nobody wants to wade into a flooded city that eats ships." She looked at Serah. "Your records. Your father's records. Is there anything on Greyharbor? Garrisons? Patrols?"

Serah was already shaking her head before Paloma finished. "Nothing. Greyharbor was written off thirty years ago—the harbor silted, the cathedral bell tower leans, the crown declared it unsalvageable and struck it from the rolls. Which means no garrison, no patrols, no supply lines." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Which also means no one will believe us if we report it, and no one will believe us if we survive it."

"So we're trading certain hunters for uncertain drowning," Ivo said. "That's the best odds we've been offered yet."

"Don't joke," Paloma said, "you'll make me fond of you, and then I'll have to worry about you, and I've got quite enough worries."

"Too late," Tam signed cheerfully. "She's already fond of you. Both of you. She made the face."

"What face?"

"He's inventing things," Paloma said. "He's a mute child with an active imagination and no supervision."

But she was smiling as she said it, and everyone saw, and nobody was cruel enough to say so.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the hill, below the grave, because Paloma refused to leave it behind unfinished. There was one more part of the rite. The part she'd skipped.

She built the fire herself, small and clean, and set the bone-handled knife across her knees, and told them about Odile Ferrant. All of it. The woman who'd found an eleven-year-old girl hiding a gift like a stolen sweet, and hadn't reported her, hadn't even scolded her—had just said, *"Well, child, either you learn to hold it or it learns to hold you, and I know which one makes better company."* The woman who'd smelled of pipe smoke and beeswax and had laughed like a door coming off its hinges. The woman whose last letter to Paloma had said *stay small, stay silent, stay alive, and forgive yourself someday, though not too soon, self-forgiveness given cheap curdles.*

"She ordered me to stay silent," Paloma said, turning the knife in the firelight. "So for twenty years I told myself I was obeying her. That was easier than admitting I was afraid." She looked around the fire—at Tam curled against her side, at Ivo mending a strap with the intense concentration of a man avoiding eye contact, at Serah sitting straight-backed even on the ground, as if the ground might report her posture to her father. "Tonight I did the rest of the rite. The part where you speak the names of your own dead and let them answer. So. Consider yourselves informed: I'm done staying small. It didn't suit me anyway. I have a loud laugh and broad shoulders and opinions, and I've been folding myself into a charm-seller's box for two decades, and the box doesn't fit anymore."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Tam reached over and took the knife gently from her hands, examined it with great seriousness, and handed it back with a nod, the way one craftsman approves another's tool.

"Odile would have liked him," Paloma said, and her voice wobbled, and she let it.

"Greyharbor is six days' hard travel," Serah said, in the tone of a woman changing the subject because the alternative was feelings. "Less if we push. More if the roads have checkpoints. We should decide tonight whether we push."

"We push," Paloma said.

"And when we get there," Ivo said carefully, "and if the Door is real, and if it opens—" He stopped. Everyone knew what he wasn't saying. *Only a spoken oath opens it. Only a swordspeaker's voice, loud enough to reach every hunter in the realm.* "We'll need to talk about that."

"We'll need to talk about a lot of things," Paloma said. "Six days is plenty. Tonight I'm going to sleep on consecrated ground for the first time in my life, and tomorrow we walk toward a drowned city, and somewhere in between I intend to teach Tam to skip stones properly, because his form is atrocious and I refuse to be responsible for a boy who can't skip stones."

Tam signed something rapid and rude that he had definitely learned from Ivo.

"Ivo Ren," Paloma said, "you are a corrupting influence on minors."

"Yes," Ivo agreed placidly, and lay back with his hands behind his head, and for the first time since she'd known him, he looked like a man who slept well.

Above them, the grass on the hillside moved in slow spirals, though the night wind had died. And far off to the east, past hills and rivers and six hard days of road, a drowned cathedral waited with its bells full of saltwater, keeping a door that had been waiting twenty years for a voice.

Paloma fell asleep mid-prayer, for the first time not apologizing for it.
