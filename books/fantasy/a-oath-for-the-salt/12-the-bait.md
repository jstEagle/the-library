## 12. The Bait

My father had always said that a trap was a form of hospitality. *You invite them in,* he'd tell his students in the inquisitory hallways, his voice carrying through the door of our house the way his voice carried through everything. *You set the table. You make them comfortable. Then you close the door.* He said it the way other men talked about gardening.

I had spent twenty-three years telling myself I hadn't listened.

Standing now at the edge of Millbrook, watching lamplight move behind windows where no lamplight should be burning at this hour, I understood that I had listened very well indeed. I simply hadn't agreed.

"He's there," I whispered. "He's already there."

Paloma crouched beside me in the ditch along the mill road, her grey cloak pulled up over her hair, and gave me the look she gave people who stated obvious things as though they were revelations. It was a good look. It had kept me humble for three weeks of hard travel.

"Of course he's there," she murmured. "We knew it was a trap when we decided to come."

"We hoped it might be a badly made trap."

"I've met your father, Serah." She said it gently, but the gentleness didn't soften the truth of it. "The man doesn't make anything badly. Except daughters, apparently, and I'm grateful every day for that particular flaw."

I wanted to laugh. That was the trouble with Paloma Galloway — she made you want to laugh in ditches at midnight while your mother sat hostage in a chapel and your father waited inside it with thirty armed men and a lifetime of practice at being right. The wanting to laugh was how she survived. I was beginning to suspect it was also how she intended for the rest of us to survive, whether we cooperated or not.

Tam pressed against my other side, small and warm, his eyes on the chapel. He couldn't speak — had never spoken, not in any way the world could hear — but he didn't need words to ask a question. His whole body asked it: *Is she in there? Is she afraid?*

"She's not afraid," I lied, because my mother had raised me on the same stubbornness she'd used to survive marrying an inquisitor, and if anyone in that building was calm, it would be her. "She's probably reorganizing his filing system out of spite."

Tam's mouth twitched. Even his smiles were quiet things.

* * *

Here is what I will say about plans, having now made several bad ones: a plan is just a story you tell yourself about the future, and like all stories, it depends entirely on who's listening.

Ours went like this. The chapel of Saint Aldhelm's stood at the center of Millbrook, ringed by my father's hunters, because my father had deduced my betrayal — I still didn't know how; I suspected a letter I'd been foolish enough to send — and had done the arithmetic of my heart quickly and correctly. He knew I would come for Mother. He had put her somewhere I would have to walk into the open to reach.

So we wouldn't walk into the open. Tam would.

That was the part I hated, and I want it recorded that I argued against it for two full days, and that Paloma overruled me, and that Tam himself had drawn the plan — drew it, on the back of a flour sack, in his careful charcoal strokes: a small figure walking up the chapel path, soundless, unseen, because around Tam sound simply stopped, and men who cannot hear their own footsteps forget to be afraid of them. The boy had looked at me while I shouted about it, patient as stone, and then tapped the drawing twice. *This one. Me.*

"You're eleven," I'd told him.

He'd held up four fingers, then folded one down. Eleven. As though I needed reminding. As though the edict cared how many years a person had collected before it came for them.

Ivo had said almost nothing during the argument, which from Ivo was a kind of thunder. He'd stood by the fire with his arms crossed, jaw working, and finally said only: "I'll be closest to him. Whatever happens." And then he'd looked at Paloma, and something passed between them that I wasn't invited into — some old debt, some old oath, the story of his brother that he'd finally told us all one feverish night and never mentioned since.

So: Tam walks in silence past the sentries, who will hear nothing, see nothing until he is already through the sacristy door where the boards are rotten — I'd grown up three houses from that chapel; I knew its bones better than my father ever had. Tam finds Mother. They leave by the river door. We meet at the mill race, cross the weir, and are gone into the marsh country before dawn.

It was a good plan. I want that noted too. It failed anyway, and the reason it failed was not that it was bad but that it was mine, and my father had spent twenty-three years learning exactly how I think, the same way I had learned how he sets a table.

* * *

Tam went up the path like a breath moving through a room.

I watched from the churchyard wall, flat against cold stone, with Paloma beside me and Ivo a shadow further off, near the sacristy corner where he'd sworn to be. The night was clear and cruelly bright, a fat moon sitting over the hills like a lamp left burning by someone careless. I remember thinking: *he chose a moonlit night on purpose.* My father loved moonlight. He said it did half the work of a lantern without the expense.

Tam slipped between two sentries who turned toward each other at precisely the wrong moment — or precisely the right one, I thought later, and my stomach went cold with the lateness of the thought — and reached the sacristy door. The rotten boards gave the way I'd promised they would give. He vanished inside.

One heartbeat. Two. Three.

Then every lantern in Millbrook lit at once.

Not kindled — lit, all together, all at once, which meant oil and wicks prepared in advance, which meant hours of preparation, which meant my father had known not only that I would come but *how*. The whole village bloomed with light like a flower opening in fast motion, and I heard my father's voice roll out across the square, unhurried, almost warm, the voice he used for sermons and for sentencing:

"Serah. You may as well come in. Your mother has kept your seat."

Beside me, Paloma breathed a word I won't repeat here, though I'll admit I was thinking it too.

"Trap within the trap," she whispered. "He let us plan it. He wanted us confident."

"He read my letters." My voice came out flat and strange. "All of them. Since I was a child. He—" I stopped, because there was no time for the shape of that grief, and grief without time curdles into something worse. "Tam's in there. We go now."

"Serah—"

"We *go*."

And here is the thing I have decided to forgive myself for, eventually, in the way one forgives a scar: I went loud. I went straight up the path with my knife out and my mother's name in my mouth, and every hunter in that square turned to look at me, which was, I suppose, a gift of a kind — every eye on me was an eye not on the shadows at the chapel's flank, where Ivo Ren moved like a man settling an old account.

Chaos is a strange country. I crossed most of it in maybe forty steps and remember almost none of them. I remember lantern light jumping. I remember a hunter named Corwin — I'd known him since I was nine; he'd taught me to whistle through a blade of grass — hesitating, his halberd half-raised, his face doing something complicated. I remember shouting at him, "Corwin, don't," and him not lowering it either, frozen in the terrible middle of a choice, and me shouldering past him because I couldn't be the one to make his choice for him.

Then the chapel doors, and the smell of tallow and old incense, and my father standing before the altar with his hands folded, and my mother seated behind him with her hands folded too, in exactly the same way, and I understood in one lurching instant where I had learned everything I was.

"Father," I said. "Let her go. You have me. Isn't that what you wanted? The daughter, delivered."

"Delivered?" He smiled, and it was the smile from the hallway lectures, the hospitable one. "Serah. You weren't invited. You were *bait*." He tilted his head, and behind his shoulder, hunters shifted in the pews like furniture rearranging itself. "Did you truly think I'd trade the woman who bore you for the woman who betrayed me? No. You were the hook, child. I cast you into the dark and waited to see what you'd drag home with you." His eyes went past me, to the doorway, to the night. "Where is the swordspeaker?"

And that was when the altar cloth caught fire.

I never found out how — I have my suspicions, and they involve a certain luck-knot seller's talent for small combustions disguised as accidents — but flame ran up the linen like it had been waiting years for permission, and the chapel filled with smoke and shouting, and in that roiling confusion I saw Tam burst from the sacristy with my mother's hand in his, both of them bent low, running for the river door.

They nearly made it.

I will carry the next ten seconds for the rest of my life, so I may as well describe them properly. A hunter on the gallery — I never learned his name, and I have tried not to invent one, though some nights a name suggests itself and I have to refuse it — raised a crossbow, tracking the small running figure. Tam was fastest thing in the room and the smallest target, and the bolt was faster than both.

Ivo got there first.

I don't mean he arrived. I mean he *arrived*, the way weather arrives, suddenly and completely — one moment the gallery archway was empty, the next Ivo Ren was standing in it with his back to the shooter and his arms spread, and the bolt took him high in the shoulder, punching through with a sound like a door being kicked.

He didn't cry out. That was the worst part. He grunted, once, the way a man does when he drops something heavy, and then he turned and put his fist into the shooter's face with such economy that the man simply sat down and stayed there, and Ivo swayed on his feet with a feathered shaft standing out of him like a quill in an inkpot, and said, in a voice that carried over the whole burning chapel:

"The boy goes free. That's the whole edict tonight. Everything else is negotiable."

Then he fell, and Tam caught him — eleven years old and half Ivo's weight, catching a falling soldier the way a mooring post catches a ship, silently, absolutely, without letting go.

* * *

We got out. I want to be honest that I barely remember how, because honesty is the discipline I owe the dead and the wounded alike. There was smoke, and my mother's grip on my wrist stronger than I'd ever felt it, and Paloma appearing out of the fire-light like something the flames had decided to spare, her knives out and her face set in an expression I'd seen once before, on the night she told me what she was. There was the river door, and the cold, and the mill race roaring under the weir, and Ivo's blood leaving a trail on the stones that I prayed the darkness would drink before morning.

There was the marsh country, and miles of it, and my father's hounds behind us for the first hour until the water confused their noses, and then nothing but reeds and stars and the wet slap of our own footsteps.

We lost everything we couldn't carry, and we could carry almost nothing. The supplies — two weeks of dried fish, the medicine kit, the spare blankets, the rope, the little iron pot Paloma had grown oddly attached to — all of it went into the mill race when the crossing went bad, swept downstream in the dark while we clung to the sluice gate and listened to our future float away. Ivo's wound bled through three bandages. My mother, sixty-one years old and held hostage for six days, walked the marshes without complaint and then, at the first halt, sat down on a tussock and began, of all things, to laugh.

"Mama," I said, kneeling in front of her, checking her hands, her face, her eyes. "Mama, are you hurt?"

"He kept *asking my opinion*," she wheezed, wiping her eyes. "Six days, Serah. About the *furniture*. Whether the interrogation chamber would look better with the chairs facing east. I told him facing east, obviously, and he wrote it down. Your father wrote down my advice about torturing people's *seating arrangements*." She laughed again, and then the laugh cracked down the middle and became something else, and I held her while the marsh wind moved the reeds around us like a congregation shifting in their pews.

* * *

Dawn found us in the ruin of a peat-cutter's hut, and Ivo found the dawn from somewhere far away, grey-faced and shivering under every blanket we had left, which was one.

Paloma worked on his shoulder by firelight, her hands steady, her mouth set. The bolt had gone through clean — meat, not bone, she said, in the tone of a woman reporting weather — but clean wounds still fester, and festered wounds still kill, and we had lost the medicine kit to the mill race along with the pot and the fish and, apparently, my capacity for optimism.

"How is he?" I asked.

"He'll live if he wants to." She tied off the bandage. "Stubbornness counts as medicine. He has a supply."

From the blanket, without opening his eyes: "Heard that."

"You were meant to."

Tam sat at Ivo's side and did not move from it. He had a hand resting, very lightly, on the bandaged shoulder — not pressing, just touching, the way you'd steady a cup you were afraid of spilling. And I noticed something I would spend the rest of the journey thinking about: around Tam's hand, the hut had gone quiet. Not silent — quiet. The wind outside softened. The fire stopped its popping. Even Ivo's ragged breathing seemed to ease, as though the boy's gift, the terrible unmaking sound that the edict would burn him for, had decided to spend itself on mercy.

Paloma noticed too. She watched the boy's hand for a long moment, and then she looked away quickly, the way you look away from something private, and I saw her press her own palm flat against her knee as though holding herself down.

"My fault," Ivo said. His voice was gravel. "The bolt. Should've moved sooner."

"It was meant for Tam," I said. "You know that."

"I know that." He opened his eyes then, and looked at the boy, and something passed over his face that I recognized, because I'd worn it myself since the day I'd burned my father's registry copy: the look of a person who has finally found the thing worth being caught for. "Worth it," he said simply, and closed his eyes again, and slept.

* * *

We counted our losses at first light, because my mother insisted, because she was the sort of woman who believed grief uncounted grew back like weeds.

"One soldier, stabbed," she said, ticking fingers. "One boy, exhausted. One daughter, furious. One fugitive mother, in need of tea. Supplies: none. Lead on the hunters: gone — they'll have picked up our trail by noon. Distance to Greyharbor: I believe someone mentioned a drowned cathedral?" She looked at Paloma. "How far?"

"Nine days," Paloma said. "Ten, wounded. Twelve, without food."

"And Vane is behind us, and Vane's hunters are ahead of us, and the country between here and the sea knows your faces now." My mother summed it up with the brisk cheer of a woman reading a grocery list aloud at a funeral. "Well. I've survived worse dinner parties."

Nobody laughed except Tam, who made no sound at all, which somehow made it funnier, and even Paloma's mouth twitched.

But later, when the others dozed and I sat sharpening a knife I no longer believed in, Paloma came and sat beside me in the doorway of the ruin, and for a while neither of us spoke, because we had learned each other's silences the way travelers learn landmarks.

"He'll come harder now," I said finally. "I embarrassed him. In front of his men. He let me escape with Mother because killing me in front of her would have been untidy, but he'll not make that courtesy twice."

"No," Paloma agreed. "He won't." She turned her grey eyes on me. "Serah. What you did in that chapel — going loud, drawing every eye — that saved Tam's life. I want you to know I saw it."

"It also sprang his trap perfectly."

"Yes. Both things are true. Most true things come in pairs; it's how you know they're true." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Get some sleep. Nine days to Greyharbor, and we've nothing to eat but stubbornness."

"Whose?"

"Mine, mostly. I've been saving it for years."

I slept, eventually, fitfully, with my mother's breathing on one side of me and a wounded soldier's on the other and a mute boy keeping watch over us all without making a sound. And in the last thin hour before waking, I dreamed of my father writing in his ledger, line after line after line, and when I woke I understood what the dream was telling me, and I sat up so fast that Tam startled.

Because I remembered the ledger. I had grown up watching him keep it. Every name, every date, every confession — real or forged — entered in his own hand, in his own cipher, and carried always in a locked case at his saddle.

My father had written the edict. Which meant my father's ledger held the proof it was built on lies — forged evidence, false confessions, twenty years of invented guilt — and that case rode wherever he rode, behind us on the road to Greyharbor, coming closer with every mile.

"Paloma," I said, into the grey morning. "I know how to end the hunt. But we're going to have to rob my father."
