## 13. Fever and Oaths

The fever made a country of him.

Ivo Ren drifted through it the way he'd once drifted through the marshes south of Brackwater as a boy—half swimming, half drowning, entirely lost. The country had roads of fire and rivers that tasted of iron, and everywhere in it, his brother was waiting for him.

"Aldric," he said, or tried to say. His mouth was full of wool and ash.

*You're late,* Aldric said. He looked the way he had at nineteen, before the fever took him—no, before the *arrow* took him, Ivo corrected himself, though in this country the two things kept trading places like soldiers swapping watch. Aldric sat on a fence post that wasn't there, whittling a piece of wood that became, depending on how Ivo squinted, a boat, a bird, a luck-knot. *You were always late. Ma used to say you'd be late to your own funeral.*

"I'm not dead," Ivo told him. "I've decided."

*Bold of you.* Aldric grinned, and the grin had a gap in it from the time he'd fallen off the mill roof, and Ivo's chest hurt worse than the bolt wound did. *Look at you, though. Lying in a chapel that smells like pigeon, letting a witch patch you up. Da would've marched you straight to the inquisitor's office.*

"Da's dead too."

*I know. I'm dead. We compare notes.* Aldric hopped down from the nothing-fence and crouched beside him, and his face went serious in the way it only ever went serious when he was about to say something true. *Do you remember what she said? Over me?*

He remembered. He remembered everything, which was the curse of the fever and the curse of being Ivo Ren: an excellent memory in a man who would have given much to forget.

---

It had been eleven years ago, in the reed marshes below Brackwater, and Aldric had been bleeding out from a boar's tusk gone wrong, and they'd been three hours from any surgeon worth the name. Ivo had carried him toward the one light in all that grey—the hut of a charm-seller who lived at the marsh edge and was rumored to be harmless, or rumored to be a witch, depending on who drank where.

She'd been younger then. Dark-haired, quick-eyed, hands steady as tide-tables. She'd taken one look at Aldric's leg and said, "I can't fix this with knots and copper," and then she'd looked at Ivo, really looked, the way nobody looks at a soldier, like he might be more than a uniform.

"This will cost you," she'd said. "Not coin. Witness. You'll see what I do, and you'll carry it, and someday you may have to decide what it means. Do you accept?"

He'd been nineteen and terrified and he'd said yes.

And she had spoken. Not a prayer, not a spell out of any book he knew—words in an old tongue, rolling and salt-flavored, and her voice had *gone somewhere*, had filled the whole marsh, had made the reeds bow and the water go still and glassy, and the wound in his brother's leg had closed like a mouth deciding not to speak.

Then she'd sagged, white as fish-belly, and said, "Tell no one. If you tell anyone, they will burn me, and your brother will die anyway, because I won't be here to finish the mending." And Ivo, who had already enlisted, who had already sworn oaths to crown and crown's law, had nodded and carried his secret home along with his brother.

He'd told no one for eleven years. And then he'd put on the black-and-silver of the hunt, and he'd told himself that registering the gifted was mercy compared to burning them, and he'd told himself a great many other things besides.

*You were always good at telling yourself things,* Aldric said fondly, in the fever-country. *Remember when you told yourself the mill cat liked you?*

"The mill cat hated everyone equally. That's different."

*Sure it is.*

* * *

Fever has its own weather, and between visits from the dead there were storms. In the storms, Ivo was back in the magistrate's hall, back on the night of the theft, standing in the registry room with its hundred candles snuffed and one window open to the rain, watching a figure in a hooded cloak lift a book that weighed as much as a sin.

He'd had his hand on his sword. He'd had the alarm bell six feet away. He'd had every reason in the realm to shout.

And the hood had slipped, just slightly, just enough, and he'd seen a jaw he knew, a scar on the chin from a fishhook incident she'd never explained, and eleven years collapsed into a single heartbeat, and in that heartbeat he had done nothing. Had stood there like a post while Paloma Galloway—the woman who'd saved his brother with a word—had frozen, seen him seeing her, and then done the bravest stupid thing he'd ever witnessed: she'd *nodded* at him. Like a neighbor passing on the lane. Like they were in on something together.

Which, apparently, they now were.

In the storm-version of the memory, he shouted. Every time, he shouted, and the hall filled with soldiers, and they burned her in the square next to old Marta the knot-maker, and Tam—small, silent Tam—stood in the crowd making no sound at all, which was somehow worse than screaming. Then the storm would break and Aldric would be sitting on the windowsill of the ruined chapel, real-world version, swinging his feet.

*You know what's funny?* Aldric said, during one of these. *You lied about her face. To Captain Dree. To the whole hunt. First lie of your life, and it was a good one.*

"It wasn't a lie. It was—" Ivo groped for it. His tongue felt like a dropped mitten. "Poor lighting."

*Poor lighting,* Aldric repeated, delighted. *Eleven years of honesty and you spend it all in one night on 'poor lighting.' She must be worth it.*

"She burned my whole career is what she's worth. Serah Vane shot me. My own side branded me. I'm lying in a pigeon chapel with a hole in me the size of a sermon, and the woman who saved my brother won't even look at me when she changes the bandages, because—and I want this noted—I haven't told her I'm the soldier from the hall."

*So tell her.*

"She'll hate me."

*She fed you broth last night. I watched. Well—I watch everything now, it's most of what there is to do.* Aldric leaned forward. *Ivo. Little brother. You lied to the hunt for her. You killed Commander Osric for the boy. You're lying in their chapel eating their pigeon-adjacent soup. What exactly are you waiting for?*

"For her to stop looking at me like I'm going to arrest someone."

*Maybe if you stop looking like a man carrying a confession in each pocket.*

* * *

Waking was a slow business, like surfacing through cold harbor water. The first thing Ivo learned about consciousness was that it hurt. The second thing was that someone was singing—very badly, very quietly, under her breath—and the third thing was that the singer was Serah Vane, disgraced inquisitor's daughter, and she was attempting a lullaby in the old tongue and getting roughly half the words wrong.

"That's not how it goes," croaked a voice from across the chapel. Paloma, seated by the cold hearth, mending a shirt that had more holes than shirt. "The third line rhymes with *tide*, not *bride*."

"My father's tutors didn't cover lullabies," Serah said, without shame. "They covered interrogation posture."

"And it shows. You sing like you're arresting the song."

Tam, curled on a pew with his drawings spread around him, held up a fresh one: a stick figure in bed, with wavy lines coming off its head, and a second stick figure holding a bowl. Underneath, in his careful charcoal letters—he wrote; he simply couldn't bear sound—**WHICH ONE IS THE SICK ONE**.

"Very funny," Ivo managed.

All four of them turned. There was a moment of profound silence, the particular silence of people who have spent two days assuming a man might die and are unprepared for him to make jokes instead.

"You're awake," Paloma said. Her voice did something complicated—relief folded over wariness folded over exhaustion—and settled on brisk. She crossed the chapel and pressed two fingers to his forehead with the impersonal efficiency of a farrier checking a horse. "Fever's down. You've been raving."

"About?"

"Your brother, mostly. A mill cat. Something called 'poor lighting.'" Her eyes flickered up to his, sharp. "Also you kept saying *I saw her face, I saw her face*. Which was interesting, since you were looking directly at mine at the time."

The chapel got very quiet. Even Serah stopped butchering lullabies. Tam sat up slowly, charcoal in hand, watching Ivo with the absolute attention of a boy who couldn't speak and so had become fluent in everything else.

Ivo looked at the ceiling beams. Pigeons, as advertised. One of them regarded him with what he chose to interpret as encouragement.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "my brother was gored by a boar in the reed marshes. A charm-seller saved his life with spoken words I wasn't supposed to hear. She made me witness, and she made me promise silence, and I kept both promises." He swallowed. His throat tasted like fever and honesty. "Eleven years later, I joined the hunt, and I told myself registering people was better than burning them. And then, four weeks ago, in the magistrate's hall, I saw a thief take the registry. And I knew her face. And I said nothing. When Captain Dree asked, I told him the lighting was bad."

Paloma had gone very still.

"I've known since the first night," Ivo said. "Before the road. Before Tam. Before any of it. I let you all travel with a hunter, and I let myself believe that keeping quiet was the same thing as being honest, and it isn't. It's just cowardice with better manners." He pushed himself up against the pew, which cost him a white flash of pain he refused to show. "Serah gave up her name for this company. Tam gave up—well, Tam gives everything without even being able to say so. And I've been paying my way with secrets. So. There it is. I'm the soldier from the hall. I'm sorry I didn't say it sooner, and I'm asking—not for forgiveness. Just for a place at the fire until I've earned one."

Silence. The pigeon shifted on its beam.

Paloma set down the shirt she was mending. She walked over slowly, and Ivo braced for anger, for the cold shoulder, for the door.

Instead she picked up the pillow behind his head, fluffed it with unnecessary violence, and shoved it back under him.

"Eleven years," she said, "you kept my secret. Through inquisitor training, through the hunt, through whatever they do to you people to make you stop noticing people are people. Eleven years, Sergeant." Her voice cracked on the rank, and she covered it by glaring. "That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing. But you should have told me."

"Yes."

"Because if I'd known, I wouldn't have spent three weeks wondering when you'd turn us in."

"Yes."

"And because—" she stopped, and something moved behind her eyes, some old tired grief finally setting down a load, "—because Aldric lived. I never got to know that. I left Brackwater the next spring and I never knew if the boy in the marsh made it. Twenty years I've wondered whether that oath bought anything at all."

"He lived thirty more years," Ivo said quietly. "Married a cooper. Argued about everything. Died fat and happy and complaining about the wine at his own retirement dinner." He smiled despite the pain. "You bought all of that."

Paloma turned away fast, busying herself with the shirt, but not before he saw her press the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes.

"Well," said Serah into the quiet, "this is touching, and I refuse to cry in front of the pigeons. For the record, I figured it out days ago. You talk in your sleep like a town crier with a grudge."

"You *what*—"

"Also, your brother sounds lovely and I have questions about the mill cat."

Tam tugged Ivo's sleeve. He held up a new drawing: four stick figures around a fire, and above them, drawn carefully, a fifth small figure on a beam—a pigeon, wearing a tiny halo. Beneath it he wrote: **THE COMPANY OF THE HONEST LIAR AND THE BAD SINGER**.

"I object to both titles," Ivo said.

Tam pointed at him, then at Serah, then shrugged elaborately. Case closed.

Paloma came back and sat on the edge of his pallet, and for a moment she just looked at him—looking, he realized, the way she'd looked at him in the marshes eleven years ago, like he might be more than a uniform.

"There's a thing you should know," she said, "now that we're being honest. Greyharbor. The drowned cathedral. That's where the Salt Door is—that's where we're going, as soon as you can ride. And Ivo—" her voice dropped, and the warmth in it was edged with something like apology, "—opening it requires an oath. Spoken aloud. In the old tongue. Loud enough that every hunter in the realm will know exactly where we are."

Outside, far off, a horn sounded on the road. Once. Twice. Then a third time—the hunt's signal for *prey sighted*.

Everyone looked at the door.

"So rest quickly," Paloma said, and reached for her swords.
