## 14. The Drowned City

Tam had never seen the sea before Brackwater, and he had not liked it then. The sea made noise the way a hundred bells make noise, all at once, forever, and Tam's gift spent itself like a candle in wind just trying to hold a small quiet around his own head.

But Greyharbor was different. Greyharbor was the sea *inside* a city, and that, somehow, was better. Because a city knew how to be quiet. A city had walls, and walls were things Tam understood.

He sat on the last hill with the others — Paloma with her pack of charms and the heavy book wrapped in oilcloth against her back, Serah with her father's face but none of her father's cruelty, Ivo leaning on a cut branch because his wound still wept when he forgot himself — and he looked down at the drowned streets and felt something he had no word for, so he drew it instead.

He took out his slate. He always carried three pieces of chalk now; Paloma bought them for him in every town they passed through, and she never once asked him to talk instead. That was the thing about Paloma. She never once asked.

On the slate he drew: a city lying on its side in the water, rooftops like the backs of sleeping whales, one spire standing up out of the flood with a gull perched on top of it like a hat.

He held it up.

Paloma looked at it a long time. "That's it," she said softly. "That's where the dead told me to go." Her hand found his shoulder without looking, the way she always did, as if her fingers knew where he was even when her eyes were busy. "The spire. We go to the spire."

Serah shaded her eyes. "Half those streets are under more than waist-deep," she said. "And the tide comes in twice a day. If it turns while we're in there—"

"Then we'd better not be slow," Ivo said, and winced, and kept walking anyway, which was the most Ivo thing Tam had ever seen him do.

* * *

Tam went first.

Nobody decided this out loud. It happened the way things happened with their little company: somebody started doing the sensible thing and everyone else fell in behind them like ducklings. And the sensible thing, in a drowned city, was to let the boy who could unmake sound walk at the front — because the tides here were not ordinary tides. Paloma said so. She said the old salt road remembered, and roads that remember have opinions, and Greyharbor's opinion was that visitors should drown.

Tam did not know about opinions. But he could feel the water thinking.

It thought in pushes and pulls against his ankles when they waded into the first flooded lane. It thought in the long groan of a sunken bell somewhere under the surface, ringing a note that had been ringing since before Tam was born, maybe since before anyone was born. It wanted to be loud. It wanted to drag. It wanted, very much, to swallow them.

So Tam gave it silence.

He had learned, over the long gray miles from the chapel ruins, how to spend his gift like coins instead of pouring it out like a bucket. A coin of quiet here, around the worst of the current, so the pull went slack and they could cross. A coin there, pressed flat over the groaning bell, so its note dimmed to something you could ignore. He couldn't stop the tide. Nobody could stop the tide. But he could make it forget, for a few dozen heartbeats at a time, that it was supposed to be doing anything at all.

It was like holding a door shut against something enormous. His arms didn't ache, but something behind his ribs did, a deep tiredness that chalk could not draw. So he drew it anyway. He drew himself as a small stick figure holding up a very large wave with both hands, and showed it to Paloma at the mouth of the second street, and she laughed — quietly, because everything near Tam was quiet whether it liked it or not — and then she looked closer and stopped laughing.

"You're spending too much," she said. "Rest. We'll rest."

She tapped the slate. Then she pointed at a rooftop poking out of the flood ahead, broad and flat, an old customs house by the look of it, its upper story dry as bone.

They climbed up through a window and sat in the dust and ate hard bread and dried fish, and Tam put his head against the wall and let his gift refill itself, drop by drop, the way a well refills when you stop hauling. Ivo slept sitting up with his branch across his knees, which Tam privately thought looked ridiculous, and drew, and showed Serah, who snorted so hard she woke Ivo up, which was worth it.

"What is it this time?" Ivo asked, squinting at the slate.

"A soldier," Tam wrote, beneath the stick figure with the branch-sword. "Sleeping. Very brave."

"I am *resting tactically*."

"You are snoring," Serah said. "In enemy waters."

"It's not enemy waters until the tide turns."

Paloma watched them argue and said nothing, but Tam saw her face, and drew that too: four small figures on a roof, and above them, drawn carefully in white chalk, a shape like a roof but also like hands, holding them up. He didn't show anyone that one. Some pictures were just for himself.

* * *

The tide turned early. Or late. Or sideways. Tam suspected the drowned city simply did what it liked.

They were in the third flooded street, the one Paloma called the processional way because it was wide and lined with statues half-submerged, saints and kings with salt-eaten faces, all of them staring down at the water with expressions of mild historical disappointment. The water came up fast — Tam felt it before he saw it, a cold breath along the stones, a change in the pressure behind his ribs — and suddenly the calm lanes they'd waded were moving, swirling, alive.

A whirlpool opened between two statues. Not big. Big enough.

Ivo was closest. His bad leg found a gap between cobbles and the water grabbed it, pulled, and for one horrible moment Tam watched a grown soldier lean sideways like a felled tree, branch-sword flailing, going down.

Tam threw everything he had at it.

Not at Ivo — at the *sound*. The whirlpool roared as it spun; that was the thing nobody ever told you about drowning places, that they screamed. And the scream was the handle of the thing, the way you could grip it. Tam gripped the scream and squeezed, and the sound died, and when the sound died the spin stuttered, lost its rhythm, faltered like a dancer whose music has stopped.

The whirlpool forgot what it was doing.

Ivo hauled himself free, coughing, soaked to the chest, and lay gasping on the steps of a statue while the water swirled on in confused, aimless circles, a monster with no memory of why it was angry.

"How," Serah began, and stopped, and looked at Tam the way people looked at Paloma's charms — like something small might contain something enormous. "How did you *do* that?"

Tam shrugged. He shrugged a lot. It covered a great many answers.

But Paloma knelt down next to him in the ankle-deep wash and said, "Show me," and he did show her, or tried to: he drew the whirlpool, and then he drew lines coming off it like the strings of a harp, and then he drew his own small figure plucking one string until it snapped.

"The sound is the rope," Paloma said slowly. "Everything that moves makes a sound, and the sound is... tied to the moving. You cut the tie."

Tam nodded vigorously. This was more than he had ever understood about his own gift, explained back to him by a charm-seller in a wet street, and he loved her so much in that moment that he drew a heart on the corner of the slate and showed it to her, and she pretended not to see it, and then kissed the top of his head, which was worse, which made his eyes sting, which he hid by drawing faster.

"We keep him in the middle," Ivo said, getting up, wringing out his sleeve with grim dignity. "Whatever happens, the boy stays in the middle of the line."

"He's not a boy," Serah said. "He's the anchor."

Tam liked that word. He turned it over in his head all afternoon. Anchor. Things anchored did not drift. He had drifted for eight years, from one charity house to another, from one town to another, passed along like a parcel nobody wanted to sign for. Now he was an anchor. Anchors were important. Anchors were what ships trusted.

He drew an anchor on his slate and did not wipe it off for two days.

* * *

Near dusk they reached the cathedral square, and Tam stopped breathing for a moment, which for a boy whose whole life was breath was a serious thing.

The cathedral lay mostly under the water. Only the spire stood clear, rising black and slender out of a flooded nave, and the windows of the spire — the ones still whole — caught the last light and burned orange, so that the whole tower looked like a candle someone had lit at the bottom of the sea. Around it, the drowned roofs made islands. Bells hung silent in the open belfry below, green with age, each one bigger than Tam, and the water lapped gently at their mouths as if drinking.

"That's where the Door is," Paloma whispered. "Underneath. Under the floor of the world."

"Then we climb," Serah said, "before full dark. There's a walkway — roofline, there, see? — if the tiles hold."

"The tiles are four hundred years old."

"So are you, probably, the way you complain."

Ivo laughed, then bent over his wound, then straightened up and laughed again anyway, because apparently that was allowed now. Tam noted it on his slate: *soldier laughs twice. miracle.*

They crossed to the spire island on a fallen beam, Tam in the middle, spending his quiet in small careful coins — a hush over the water where eels moved, a hush over a loose tile that would have clattered and rung the whole roofline like an alarm. At the base of the spire, a door stood ajar, swollen with salt, and Paloma shouldered it open onto a spiral stair that went up into the dark and, presumably, down into deeper dark beneath.

Inside, the silence was already thick. Old holy buildings had a kind of silence of their own, Tam had learned — a held-breath silence, a waiting silence — and his gift settled into it like a stone settling into moss. For once, being near him cost the room nothing. The room had been saving up.

They climbed. Ivo counted the turns aloud in a whisper — forty-one, forty-two — because counting kept his mind off his leg, and Serah corrected him twice out of pure spite, and Paloma carried Tam's pack as well as her own without asking, and Tam drew nothing at all, because some things you just walked inside of instead of drawing.

At the top: a bell chamber open to the sky, and the whole drowned city spread out below them in the blue evening, streets like canals, canals like streets, a thousand windows underwater reflecting the first stars. Somewhere far off, on the shore road, a light moved. Then another. Then a string of them, patient as beads on a thread, coming toward Greyharbor.

Nobody said the word *hunters*. Nobody had to.

"They'll reach the city by morning," Serah said, glassing the shore with a cracked spyglass she'd won off a smuggler in a card game she refused to discuss. "Maybe sooner, if Vane pushed night marchers. He'll want us boxed in among the floods."

"Then tonight we find the Door," Paloma said.

She turned to Tam, and took his slate from his hands gently, and set it down, and took both of his hands in hers instead, which she almost never did, because she said his hands were his voice and you don't cover someone's mouth.

"Tam," she said. "Tomorrow, or the day after, I'm going to have to speak. Out loud. The whole truth, in the old tongue, where anyone can hear it. And when I do—" her voice did something complicated, "—everything will get very loud. Louder than anything you've ever unmade. And I need to know: how much of it can you hold?"

Tam looked at her hands around his. Small, scarred, steady.

Then he picked up his chalk, and drew.

He drew the spire. He drew waves around it, many waves, drawn fast and dark. And then he drew himself, small, at the base of the spire, arms out, and around his little figure he drew a circle — a great sweeping circle of clean empty white, cutting through the waves like a blade, the only untouched space on the whole crowded slate.

Inside the circle, the water was still. Inside the circle, there was no sound at all.

He held it up so all three of them could see.

Paloma's breath caught. Serah said, softly, "That's a lot of quiet, Tam."

Ivo said nothing. Ivo just put his hand on Tam's head, rough and brief, the way soldiers blessed things they weren't sure they believed in.

And far below them, down in the dark under the drowned nave, something answered — a single note from the sunken bells, muffled by fathoms of green water, rising up through the stone and into their bones. Not a warning. Tam was quite sure it wasn't a warning. It sounded almost like a door, somewhere very old, trying to remember how to open.

Paloma looked down through the bell chamber's grate into the black, and her face went strange and bright at once.

"It knows we're here," she said.
