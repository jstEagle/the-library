## 15. Vane's Bargain

They came down from the bell chamber as the tide turned, and the tide in Greyharbor did not turn so much as it changed its mind.

Paloma felt it through her boots — a long, slow exhale beneath the drowned city, water sliding back off rooftops that had spent forty years learning to be reefs. The spire they'd climbed stood on the one hill high enough to keep its chin above the floodline, and around its base the streets lay open now, glistening, cobbled lanes running with ankle-deep silver. Fish moved through doorways where fish had no business moving. A chapel roof broke the surface of a flooded square like the back of a sleeping whale.

"It's beautiful," Serah said, and then, because she was Serah: "It's also a killing ground with better lighting."

"Everything is," Ivo said. "You just have to decide whose."

Tam tugged Paloma's sleeve and held up his slate. He'd been drawing since dawn, quick little charcoal strokes, and now he turned it toward her with the particular gravity he reserved for things his gift had told him and his vocabulary could not.

He had drawn the cathedral below them — the sunken nave, the drowned bells — and rising out of it, a figure. Not detailed. Just a shape in an inquisitor's long coat, drawn with the heavy, pressing lines Tam used for things that were *true*.

"He's coming," Paloma said.

Tam nodded. Then he erased the figure with his thumb, carefully, which was not something Tam usually did. He looked at her while he did it, and Paloma understood the erasure for what it was: a wish, offered the only way he could offer anything.

"I know," she said softly. "I wish too."

They made camp in the cathedral's upper gallery — the dry part, the part where the old kingdom's stonework still arched overhead in ribbed fans like the inside of a shell. Salt crusted everything. The pillars wept white tears down their flanks. Somewhere below, under thirty feet of green-black water, the Salt Door waited in the flooded crypt, and Paloma could feel it the way you feel a tooth you're trying to ignore: a low, patient ache at the root of her.

She'd felt doors before. She'd hidden from them for twenty years. But this one hummed, faint and constant, in the register of her gift — the register she'd kept silent so long that hearing it again was like hearing her own name spoken by a stranger who somehow knew it.

"We should rest while the tide is out," Serah said. She'd spread the registry across two broken pews and was going through it page by page with a soldier's efficiency, marking names. "Vane won't move until he has us cornered. He's a careful man. Careful men build traps and then wait for you to admire them."

"His mother's son," said a dry voice from the gallery entrance.

Serah went rigid. Ivo's hand found his sword. Tam stepped in front of Paloma — small, thin, utterly serious, a boy putting his whole body between his people and the world — and then the voice's owner walked into the salt-light, and Tam relaxed by exactly nothing, because he didn't know her.

She was old. Older than Paloma had expected, though Paloma had never actually expected to meet her. Marta Vane wore traveling clothes gone soft with road dust and a shawl the color of dried seaweed, and she carried herself the way women carry themselves when they have survived a marriage to power: spine straight, eyes level, opinions folded away but not discarded.

"Mother." Serah's voice cracked down the middle. "How — you were supposed to be in Ashfall. You were supposed to be *safe*—"

"I was safe. Safe is a room your father keeps me in." The old woman crossed the gallery, took her daughter's face in both hands, and studied it like a map she'd drawn herself. "You look terrible. You look wonderful. Both are correct." She released her, turned to Paloma, and inclined her head with the precise courtesy of someone greeting an equal she has no intention of pretending otherwise about. "Paloma Galloway. Swordspeaker. My husband doesn't know I know that word."

"Ma'am," Paloma managed.

"I've read your file. All of them, actually — Aldous leaves his papers everywhere; he thinks tidiness is a form of vanity." Marta Vane's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Twenty years of reports saying 'gift unconfirmed.' He wrote 'unconfirmed' eleven times. Do you know what that means, girl? It means he knew. He always knew. He just couldn't prove it, and it *ate* him."

Ivo shifted. "If you followed us here, he can follow you here."

"My dear sergeant, I *am* how he follows us. He let me go because he believes a mother's heart will lead him to his daughter faster than any tracker." She said it without bitterness, the way you report weather. "He was right. Here I am. Here you all are. And here, I'm afraid, comes the rest of it."

* * *

The rest of it arrived an hour later, and it did not arrive quietly.

Horns first — three notes, repeated, the crown's signal for *surrender and be judged* — rolling across the flooded city and coming back doubled off the water. Then torchlight, dozens of points of it, kindling along the ridge of the far shore and on the boats poling slowly through the drowned streets. The hunt had come in force. Of course it had. Vane had never once in his life brought less force than the situation allowed; Serah said he considered restraint a moral failing.

From the gallery's broken windows they watched the boats converge on the cathedral hill. Red-and-white livery. Crossbows racked and ready. And at the prow of the lead boat, standing straight as a mast in a coat the color of dried blood, Inquisitor Aldous Vane.

"He's not attacking," Serah said, frowning. "Why isn't he attacking?"

"Because he wants something," Ivo said grimly.

A single boat came ahead of the rest, bearing only the inquisitor and four oarsmen. It grounded on the cathedral steps with a scrape that echoed up through the whole structure, and Aldous Vane ascended alone, unhurried, hands clasped behind his back like a man arriving slightly early for a dinner party.

Marta met him at the top of the stairs.

For a long moment neither spoke. Husband and wife, framed in the doorway, lit by torchlight from below and salt-faded daylight from above. Paloma, watching from the shadows of the gallery with Tam's hand gripped tight in hers, saw something pass between them that had nothing to do with love or hate — something older and more tired. Recognition, maybe. Two people who had spent thirty years proving different theses about the same country.

"You let her go," Marta said.

"I let her lead me," Vane replied. His voice carried easily; it was a voice built for pulpits and courtrooms, warm and resonant and entirely without warmth. "There is a difference, my dear, and the difference is that I am here, and so is everyone I have hunted for a year." He stepped past her into the gallery, and his gaze went across the ruined pews and broken saints and settled on Paloma with the satisfaction of a man finding the last piece of a puzzle he'd already solved. "Charmseller. Registry thief. And underneath the apron, the last swordspeaker in Brackwater. Eleven reports of 'unconfirmed,' madam. You have cost me more sleep than the war did."

"I'm honored," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Twenty years of practice, that. Twenty years of keeping your voice flat while your heart tries to climb out through your ribs.

Vane smiled thinly. "Where is the book?"

"Safe."

"Everything is safe with you people. That is your tragedy — you believe hiding is the same as winning." He began to walk, slowly, along the gallery, examining the stonework as if appraising a house. "Do you know what I found in the archives at Ashfall? The original edicts. Not the copies — the originals, sealed by the first council after the Dissolution. And do you know what they say?" He turned. "Nothing. They say nothing. There is no crime named. No justification recorded. The purges simply *begin*, page after page, names and dates and ash, with no reason given anywhere — because the reason was never written down. It was spoken. By swordspeakers. And when we killed the speakers, we killed our own explanation." He spread his hands. "We burned the country's memory, madam, and then we built a court system on the ashes and called the ashes law."

Silence. Even the sea seemed to lean in.

"So burn the book," Ivo said flatly. "End it."

"Oh, Sergeant Ren. The man who saw a thief in the magistrate's hall and reported her as 'tall, hooded, unremarkable.'" Vane's smile sharpened. "Yes, I read your report too. Remarkably unremarkable. Almost as if you wanted it that way." Ivo said nothing, and Vane nodded as if he'd expected exactly that silence. "No, I don't want the book burned. Burning books is how we got here. I want—" and here he turned back to Paloma, and for one moment the courtroom warmth dropped away and left something rawer, hungrier, almost pleading, "—a bargain."

"A bargain," Paloma repeated.

"Yourself. Publicly. At Greyharbor, before witnesses, with every hunter in the realm watching." He said it plainly, without theater, which somehow made it worse. "You surrender. You confess your gift. You submit to judgment — the old judgment, the pyre, done properly this time, with rites and record. In exchange—" he drew a folded document from his coat, heavy paper, ribboned and sealed, "—full amnesty. Every name in that registry. Every child you taught, every charm-seller, every hedge-witch and oath-keeper and mute boy who unmakes sound. Pardoned by crown decree, witnessed by the fleet, effective the moment you speak your surrender. They live free. You die known."

The words landed in the gallery like stones into still water.

Tam made a sound — not a sound, really, the *absence* of one, a pocket of dead air that pressed against everyone's ears, his gift flaring with his fear. The torch flames guttered. Paloma pulled him against her side.

"No," Serah said.

"Daughter—"

"*No.* You don't get to dress murder in mercy and call it arithmetic." Serah had her hand on her sword hilt and tears standing bright in her eyes and refused to shed either. "You've spent your whole life building a machine that eats people, and now you want to trade one person for hundreds and call it *statesmanship*—"

"It *is* statesmanship," Vane said, and there it was, the terrible thing: he meant it. "One life, freely offered, against three hundred pardons. Show me the ledger where that loses, Serah. Show me the court that wouldn't call it justice. I am offering the only clean trade this filthy edict has ever produced." His eyes came back to Paloma, and softened — actually softened, horribly, like a man looking at a difficult patient. "You've spent twenty years dying by inches anyway. Hiding. Flinching. Selling luck to widows and calling it living. I am offering you the chance to make that death mean something. Most people never get the offer at all."

And that was the worst part. Not the cruelty. The *sense* of it.

Paloma looked at the sealed decree in his hands. Three hundred names. She could see them without opening the registry — Maren's boy with the fire-hands, old Sefa who blessed fishing nets, the twins in Copperlane who could talk to gulls. Children she'd sworn oaths over in cellars, quiet as prayer. Three hundred lives weighed against one, and the scale didn't even tremble.

That was the trap. Not the crossbows on the water. The arithmetic. Because the arithmetic was *correct*, and knowing it was correct didn't stop her whole body from revolting against it, and the gap between those two facts was where Vane lived.

"She'll say yes," Vane said quietly, to Serah, not to Paloma. "Watch. The good ones always do. It's why they're rare."

Paloma looked down at Tam. He was staring up at her, and his face was doing the thing it did when words failed him completely — jaw tight, eyes enormous, the whole boy become one clenched fist of refusal. He shook his head. Slowly. Then he took her hand and pressed it flat against his own chest, over his heart, and held it there.

*I'm right here. Feel that. Don't you dare.*

"Don't answer him," Ivo said suddenly. He'd moved to stand between Paloma and Vane, wounded shoulder squared, and his voice had the flat calm she'd learned to trust more than most men's courage. "Inquisitor. You've made your offer. She doesn't have to take it today. Or ever. That's the difference between a bargain and a demand — one of them waits."

Vane regarded him for a long moment. "You were a good soldier once, Ren."

"I was a good liar once," Ivo said. "It's better work."

Something flickered across the inquisitor's face — not anger. Something more like grief, briefly, quickly buried. He refolded the decree with great care, as if it were a thing that might bruise.

"The offer stands until the next full tide," he said. "After that, my men come up these stairs, and amnesty becomes a matter of archaeology." He paused at the doorway beside his wife, and for a moment the two of them stood together, an old married couple silhouetted against torchlight, and he said, very low, in a voice only she and perhaps Paloma's sharp ears caught: "Thirty years, Marta. Tell me where I lost the argument."

"You never had it," she said. "You just stopped listening."

He went down the stairs. The boats withdrew. The horns sounded once more — *until the tide turns* — and then the flooded city went quiet, except for the water, and the wind, and far below, faint and patient, the hum of a door.

* * *

They sat around a fire of driftwood in the gallery, because Serah insisted that frightened people should eat, and because the alternative was sitting in the dark doing arithmetic, and none of them could bear it.

The decree lay on the pew between them. Nobody touched it. Everybody looked at it.

"Three hundred people," Serah said finally. "Maybe more. I counted the pages."

"I know how many pages," Paloma said.

"Paloma—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't make the case, Serah. Please. If anyone makes the case, let it be me, because I'm the one who has to live — or not live — inside the answer." She stared into the fire. "Here's what I keep catching myself doing. I keep thinking: twenty years I've been half-dead anyway. Twenty years of flinching. Maybe this is just... finishing the sentence. Cleanly. Loudly. With three hundred people walking free out of the smoke." She laughed, short and awful. "And then I think about Tam learning to write his name, and I think, who teaches him? Who teaches *anyone*? The gifted don't need pardons, they need *teachers*. They need someone who remembers the old tongue. And I'm the last one who knows it whole, and Vane knows that, and that's why the trade sounds so generous — because he isn't buying my death. He's buying the end of the line. Pyre me, and in fifty years some child born with fire in her hands gets registered and burned anyway, because nobody's left to teach her what she is."

Silence around the fire.

"That's the real arithmetic," Paloma said. "Not one against three hundred. One against *every one after*. And he dressed it up in mercy because he genuinely believes he's being merciful, and that's what frightens me. Not the man. The *rightness* of him."

"He's wrong," Tam wrote on his slate, and held it up, fierce.

"About what?"

Tam thought. Charcoal scratched. He crossed something out. Wrote again. Held it up:

**HE THINKS YOU'RE THE ONLY THING LEFT TO TRADE. HE'S WRONG. THE DOOR ISN'T HIS.**

Paloma looked at the slate for a long time.

"No," she agreed softly. "It isn't."

Ivo leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the scar tissue along his jaw. "Then here's my soldier's reading of the field. We have until the next tide — call it six hours. Vane expects one of two plays: she takes the deal, or we run for the crypt and he takes the door by force. So we do neither. We do the thing he hasn't priced in." He glanced at Serah. "Your mother testified at the trial records office, didn't she? Before she was... kept?"

"She was a witness-clerk for thirty years," Serah said slowly. "She knows where every body is buried. Literally. She helped bury the evidence."

"And she's sitting right here," Ivo said, "next to a woman who can bind an oath in the old tongue." He looked at Paloma, and there was something in his face that was almost a grin, tired and crooked and alive. "You wanted arithmetic? Here's better numbers. One testimony, spoken aloud, in front of the fleet, tearing the edict's foundations out from under it — bought for the price of one oath. Vane came here to trade your death for three hundred lives. Let's spend the night making his money worthless."

Marta Vane, who had been silent by the fire, set down her cup. "I have been waiting thirty years for someone to ask me that," she said.

Paloma looked around the fire — the disgraced daughter, the traitor sergeant, the mute boy, the inquisitor's wife, all of them hunted, all of them hers, somehow, in the way that people become yours when you've bled on the same road. The hum of the door rose faintly through the floor, patient as tide.

Above them, out on the black water, the first watch-fires of the hunt burned in their rings, waiting for morning.

Waiting for the tide to turn.
