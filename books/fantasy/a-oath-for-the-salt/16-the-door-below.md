## 16. The Door Below

The drowned cathedral did not so much stand at Greyharbor as kneel in it, half-swallowed by the sea, its spire rising from green water like a drowning man's last outstretched hand. Serah Vane had grown up on her father's maps of the place—inked in red, annotated with the careful hand of a man who catalogued heresy the way other men catalogued birds—and not one of those maps had shown anything beneath it.

Which was fitting, she thought, as she waded through the flooded nave behind Paloma Galloway. Her father had built his life on not looking down.

"Tell me again," Ivo said from behind her, his voice rough with fever he was pretending he didn't have, "why we're following a charm-seller into a basement full of ocean."

"Because," Tam signed, hands quick and certain even in the dark, *because she knows things*, and then, after a beat, *and because you're too slow to run away anymore.*

Ivo made a wounded noise. Serah had learned, over the weeks of their strange company, that Tam's signing had developed something of a wit, and that most of that wit was aimed at Ivo, and that Ivo pretended to mind far less than he claimed. It was, she suspected, the closest thing either of them had to a brother.

"Keep together," Paloma said quietly. She stood waist-deep now where the nave floor dipped, holding a lantern high, and the light caught the water below and turned it the color of old glass. "And whatever you hear down here—whatever you *think* you hear—don't answer it."

"What does it say?" Serah asked.

"That's rather the point," Paloma said. "It doesn't say. It asks."

* * *

They went down through the sacristy, where the tide had carved a stairway out of what must once have been a crypt, and the air changed. Salt thickened until Serah could taste it on her teeth, and the cold stopped being the cold of seawater and became something older—the cold of a room that had been closed for two hundred years and remembered being closed.

Tam went first, which surprised no one who knew him. His gift moved ahead of him like a broom before a sweeper, and in his presence the world simply went quiet: the drip of water, the groan of settling stone, the endless hush-hush of the tide against the walls—all of it fell away, and they walked inside a pocket of silence as complete as the inside of a bell. Serah had hated it at first. Now she found she trusted it more than any sound. Where Tam walked, nothing could sneak up on them, because nothing could make a sound at all.

The stair ended at a landing, and the landing ended at a wall.

Not a wall—a door.

Serah's lantern found it first, and she heard Paloma's breath catch beside her, and Ivo swear softly under his breath, and Tam go very still in the way he did when something mattered.

It was vast. That was the only honest word. Twice the height of a cathedral door, three times the width, set into the living rock like a coin pressed into bread. It was made of salt—not salt-crusted stone, but salt itself, grown dense and pale and faintly luminous, veined with lines like frost ferns or featherwork. The surface was carved, though the carving was worn almost smooth: processions of figures, arms raised, mouths open in shapes that might have been song or might have been screaming. At its center, at the height of a standing man, was a single uncarved circle, wide as a shield, polished by centuries of touching hands.

"It's real," Ivo said. He sounded younger than he had all journey. "My grandmother used to tell a story about a door of salt. I thought it was a story about a door of salt."

"Most true things start that way," Serah said.

Paloma hadn't spoken. She had walked forward alone, boots splashing, and laid her palm flat against the great circle, and Serah watched her shoulders change—the way a person's shoulders change when they come home to a house they'd convinced themselves they'd dreamed.

"It's warm," Paloma said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Two hundred years under the sea and it's warm."

*What does it feel like?* Tam signed, drifting closer, curiosity winning over caution as it always did.

"Like—" Paloma laughed, wet and startled. "Like a hand. Like someone holding my hand back."

* * *

They made camp on the landing, because there was nothing else to do. The Door would not be hurried; Paloma said so, running her fingers along the worn carvings, reading them the way Serah read documents—slowly, twice, lips moving. The tide outside turned and began to fall, and somewhere above them Greyharbor creaked and settled, and Inquisitor Vane's hunters were surely combing the drowned streets street by street, and here they sat, four fugitives and a mother-of-one-inquisitor drying her stockings over a lantern, waiting for a door to decide to be a door.

Serah's mother had taken to the company with the unflappable grace that had let her survive thirty years married to Aldous Vane. She had produced, from some impossible corner of her baggage, a twist of dried figs and a small tin of honey candies, and was distributing the latter with the gravity of a woman administering sacrament.

"Honey," she said, pressing one into Ivo's bandaged hand, "for the fever. My mother swore by it. My mother also swore by leeches, so take it with appropriate skepticism."

"Thank you, Mistress Vane," Ivo said gravely.

"Call me Odile. Anyone my daughter has decided to save is family, and family gets candy whether they deserve it or not." She looked pointedly at Serah. "Especially when they don't."

"Mother."

"You burned a charm-seller," Odile said mildly, to no one in particular, and Serah felt the words land exactly where they were aimed. "In Millbrook. A woman named Ansa Crowe. You were nineteen and following orders and you cried about it for a week, and none of that un-burned her."

"I know," Serah said.

"I know you know. I'm saying it anyway, because someone should say these things out loud in front of doors. Doors are good listeners." She popped a candy into her own mouth and settled back against the salt wall like a queen taking her throne. "Proceed."

There was a silence. Tam broke it by signing, solemnly, *I like her*, and Ivo snorted hard enough to hurt his ribs, and even Paloma smiled—a small, tired, real smile, the kind Serah had learned to count like coins.

But the smile faded as Paloma turned back to the Door, and Serah saw the moment the weight came down on her again, the weight she'd been carrying since the chapel fire, since the mass grave, since the word *swordspeaker* had first been spoken aloud between them like a confession.

"There's a problem," Paloma said.

"There's always a problem," Ivo said. "Problems are how we know we're on schedule."

"The Door opens to an oath. That part the stories got right. But it wants the oath in the old tongue—the swordspeaker's tongue, the one they burned out of the country along with everyone who spoke it. And an oath isn't just words." Paloma's hand rested on the warm salt circle. "An oath is a *claim*. You don't whisper it. You don't sign it. You speak it, aloud, at full voice, and you name yourself while you do it. That's the whole architecture of the thing. The Door doesn't open to a password. It opens to a person declaring themselves."

"All right," Serah said slowly. "So you declare yourself. We're four days ahead of the hunt, we're underground, we're—"

"Serah." Paloma turned and looked at her, and Serah stopped, because she had seen that look before, on the night at the river town, the night Paloma had admitted what she was. "You've spent your life around inquisitors. Think like one for a moment. What happens when a swordspeaker speaks an oath?"

And Serah thought, and her stomach dropped.

Every gifted oath rang. That was the foundation of the entire registry, the entire edict, the entire hunt—an oath spoken with the gift carried, like a bell struck in a quiet country. Wardens' instruments could catch it for miles. Her father had described it to her once, years ago, with something close to reverence: *when one of them truly swears, girl, every listening ear in the realm knows where to look.* The purges had begun, historically, with oaths—weddings, funerals, moments of ordinary human need that no law could forbid, each one a flare over the darkness.

"You speak your oath," Serah said, "and every hunter from here to the capital hears it."

"Not just hears it." Paloma's voice was steady, which cost her something; Serah could see it costing her. "*Locates* it. There are wardens' towers on every coast. They triangulate. By the time the echo fades, my father's men will know this cathedral's name better than I do."

*How long?* Tam signed. *How long do we get?*

"Minutes. Perhaps less. Enough to open the Door. Not enough to—" Paloma stopped. Started again. "The Door will open. We'll get through. But whoever holds it open has to hold it against everything that comes down those stairs, and everything that comes down those stairs will be coming for me specifically."

"No," Ivo said.

"Ivo—"

"No." He pushed himself up off the stones, and the fever made him sway and he ignored it. "We didn't crawl across half the country so you could stand in a doorway and be a hinge. We'll find another way. There's always another way."

"Is there?" Paloma asked gently. "Name it."

Ivo opened his mouth. Closed it. Serah watched him try, honestly try, and fail, and she understood suddenly that this was the shape of loving people—you watched them do arithmetic you couldn't argue with, and you hated the numbers.

"We could draw them off," Serah said. "Split up. Some of us go up and make noise, lead the hunt away from the—"

"And leave you holding the Door alone anyway," Paloma said. "With fewer hands. No."

*A third way,* Tam signed, insistent. *There's always a third way. You taught me that.*

"I taught you that," Paloma agreed, "and I also taught you that some sums don't come out clean. This is one of them." She looked around at them—at Tam, at Ivo, at Serah, at Odile—and Serah saw her doing it, the terrible tender accounting, weighing each of them like coins she couldn't bear to spend. "But not tonight. Tonight we rest. Tomorrow I learn the old tongue well enough to speak it without shaming my teachers. And tomorrow—" her jaw set "—tomorrow we decide together. All of us. Nobody decides this for anybody else. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Serah said.

*Agreed,* Tam signed.

Ivo took longer. Then: "Agreed. Under protest."

"Noted for the record," Odile said, and handed him another candy.

* * *

Serah slept badly, which was normal, and woke before the others, which was also normal, and found Paloma already awake, sitting cross-legged before the Door with her lips moving through sounds that were not quite words—old syllables, round and salt-heavy, practiced soft enough not to wake the stone.

Serah sat down beside her. For a while neither spoke.

"My father wrote the Edict of Ash," Serah said at last. "I've told you that. What I haven't told you is that I helped copy it. Fair-hand script. Three hundred pages. I was proud of my penmanship." She turned the honey tin over in her hands. "I keep thinking about that. How easy it is to be proud of the wrong thing."

"Easy," Paloma agreed. "And expensive."

"If you stay—if it comes to that—I want you to know it won't have been for nothing. Whatever happens on the other side of that door, I'll see the registry burned. Every page. I'll spend the rest of my life making sure no child in that book ever sees a warden's tower."

"I believe you." Paloma reached over and squeezed her hand, brief and hard. "That's why it can't come to that. People who have something to live for shouldn't trade it away. It sets a bad precedent."

Despite everything, Serah laughed. "You sound like my mother."

"Your mother is the finest woman I've met in twenty years of meeting terrible ones. I take it as a compliment."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone and water, a bell began to ring.

Not the cathedral's bell—that had been underwater for two centuries. This was thinner, sharper, wrong: the flat repeated stroke of a warden's signal-tower, and Serah knew its rhythm the way she knew her own heartbeat, having transcribed its codes in fair-hand script in another life. Three-and-two. Three-and-two.

*Gift-sign detected. Location approximate. All units converge.*

Paloma had gone white. "That's not possible. I haven't sworn anything. None of us—"

Then Tam appeared at the top of the stairs, signing fast, face drained, and Serah read his hands and felt the floor tilt beneath her.

*Someone is swearing an oath up in the city. Someone with the gift. Loudly. On purpose.*

"They're flushing us," Serah breathed. "Father's using a decoy—or worse, he's caught someone, someone willing to swear and swear until we come up to see—" She was already moving, grabbing her boots, her sword belt, the registry wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. "Or it's a trap laid for anyone listening. Either way, the arithmetic just changed. If the hunt converges on Greyharbor tonight—"

"Then there's no tomorrow to decide in," Paloma said quietly.

She rose. She put her hand flat against the warm salt circle one more time, and this time she did not pull it away, and the faint light in the Door's veins seemed to lean toward her like grass toward sun.

"Well," said Paloma Galloway, charm-seller, liar, last of the swordspeakers, in the voice of a woman stepping off a ledge, "I suppose we'd best decide fast."
