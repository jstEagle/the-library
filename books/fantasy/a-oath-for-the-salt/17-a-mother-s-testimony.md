## 17. A Mother's Testimony

The plan, such as it was, had been hatched in the dark of the drowned cathedral's bell tower with all the elegance of a tavern brawl.

"We can't fight Vane," Serah had said, pacing a circle worn smooth by centuries of other people's pacing. "He has forty men and the whole city's garrison behind him. We can't run—the Door needs an oath, and an oath takes time we won't have once he hears us speak."

"So we need him deaf," said Ivo.

"We need him *distracted*," Serah corrected, and then she had gone very still, the way she did when an idea arrived that she didn't entirely like the taste of. "We need everyone distracted. The whole city, if we can manage it."

And that was how, on a grey morning smelling of brine and woodsmoke, Paloma Galloway found herself standing in the fish market of Greyharbor between a mute boy holding a slate like a shield and a disgraced inquisitor's daughter adjusting her dead mother's shawl for the fourth time.

"You don't have to do this," Paloma murmured.

"Yes, I do." Maren Vane's voice was thin as winter ice but it did not crack. She was a small woman, sixty-odd years old, with her daughter's stubborn jaw and hands that trembled only when they were idle. "Twenty-two years I have been married to that man. Twenty-two years I have watched what he calls justice. And twenty-two years I have told myself that a wife's silence is a kind of loyalty." She looked down at her own hands. "It is not. It is just silence with better manners."

Tam tugged Paloma's sleeve and held up his slate: **WILL THEY LISTEN?**

"Nobody listens to anything before noon," Paloma said. "That's why we're doing it now. Everyone's tired, everyone's guilty about something, and guilt makes people soft."

"That is a terrible philosophy," said Serah.

"It's a working one."

* * *

The fish market of Greyharbor was built on pilings over what used to be dry land, back before the sea came for the city and the city, in its pride, simply built higher and pretended otherwise. Stalls crowded the boardwalks. Gulls screamed their opinions from every available surface. By midmorning there were perhaps three hundred people buying, selling, gutting, arguing about prices—and one woman in a plain grey dress climbing onto an overturned crab barrel as though she were stepping into a pulpit.

Paloma stationed herself near the edge of the crowd with Tam's hand gripped in hers. Ivo leaned against a piling some ten feet away, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had no opinion about anything happening here whatsoever. He was very good at that expression. He'd had practice.

Maren Vane stood on her barrel and did not shout. That was the first thing Paloma noticed, and the thing that made the hair rise on her arms. In a market full of noise, the quiet voice is the one that turns heads.

"My name is Maren Vane," she said. "You may know my husband. Aldous Vane. Inquisitor General of the Realm."

The name moved through the crowd like a current through water. Fishwives stopped haggling. A boy with a knife and a mackerel froze mid-gut. Somewhere a gull, offended by the sudden silence, screamed alone.

"I have come to tell you that everything he has built is a lie," Maren continued. "Not the kind of lie men tell to get out of trouble. The kind they tell to build empires on."

A big man in oilskins—Paloma marked him as a harbor captain by the chain at his neck—called out, "Woman, if this is a jest, your husband's men will have your head."

"My husband's men already had my head," Maren said placidly. "They held it under water in my own kitchen until I agreed to be bait. So you will forgive me if I am past caring what they do next."

That got them. Paloma felt the crowd lean in, the way crowds do—not toward a speaker, but toward a wound.

* * *

Later, Serah would explain it to Tam with drawings: how her mother had spent three days after the rescue saying almost nothing, sitting by the fire in the ruined chapel, writing. Pages and pages, in a small precise hand, while the rest of them argued about supply lines and Vane's troop movements. Paloma had assumed she was composing a testament, something to leave behind in case things went badly.

She had been composing evidence.

Because Maren Vane, it turned out, had been the Inquisitor General's clerk before she was his wife. She had copied the documents of the first purge with her own hand—every confession, every denunciation, every warrant signed in the old king's name. And she had kept copies. Twenty-two years of copies, sewn into the lining of a winter coat that had traveled with her through four houses and two evacuations, because even then, even as a young bride who loved him, some part of her had known.

"The confessions are forged," she told the market now, and her voice carried clean across the water. "I know because I forged some of them myself, at my husband's instruction, when the real ones would not say what he needed them to say. I know because I watched him dictate a witch's confession word by word to a scribe, and then send soldiers to arrest the witch for words she had never spoken." She drew a folded packet from inside her dress—oilcloth-wrapped, tied with string, worn soft as cloth from handling. "Here are the originals. Here are the drafts in his hand. Here is the order he wrote to burn the village of Saltmere *before* any investigation began, because he had promised the king results by season's end."

She held the packet high.

"Three hundred names," she said. "Three hundred people burned or drowned or broken on the wheel, on paper that was written backwards—confession first, crime invented after. And every gifted soul since, every child on that registry, every family dragged from their beds—they are all of them dying to protect a lie that is older than most of you."

The crowd made a sound Paloma had heard only twice before in her life, both times in the moments before storms came ashore. Not a roar. A low collective intake, hundreds of lungs filling at once.

Then the questions started, and the shouting, and somewhere in the press a man began pushing toward the barrel with murder in his face, and Paloma lost sight of Maren entirely—

—and then Tam did the thing he did.

He didn't decide to. Paloma was sure of that afterward; he wasn't a weapon, whatever anyone tried to make of him. But the crowd surged, and someone knocked against him, and his fear spilled out the way his gift always spilled when he was frightened: a bubble of silence, thirty feet wide, dropping over the market like a glass bell.

Every voice cut off at once. Every gull. Even the sea itself seemed to hold its breath against the pilings.

Three hundred people stood in perfect, impossible quiet, looking at each other, looking at the small pale boy at the edge of the crowd whose mouth was open in horror, looking at the woman on the crab barrel who stood unmoved in the center of the hush as though she had been expecting it.

Maren Vane looked straight at Tam. And in the silence where no one could hear anything at all, she raised the packet of papers over her head again, and mouthed, slowly and clearly, so that half the market could read her lips:

*Read them yourselves.*

* * *

What happened next took the rest of the day and none of it required another word from Maren Vane.

The harbor captain with the chain was the first to move—he waded through the frozen crowd, took the packet from her hands with the reverence of a man accepting a relic, and read the first page aloud in a voice like a ship's bell. Then a fishwife read the second. Then a chandler. They passed the pages hand to hand down the boardwalks, and Greyharbor read its own history aloud in a hundred voices, and Paloma stood pressed against a piling with Tam's face buried in her coat and thought: *this is what a swordspeaker's oath does, except no one has to burn for it.*

Words instead of swords. Serah had said it the night before, half-mocking, half-hoping. Her mother had gone and done it.

By noon, the papers had reached the garrison. By early afternoon, half the garrison had deserted—the registry theft, the purges, the forged warrants, all of it read aloud in barracks where the soldiers' own cousins appeared on lists. By late afternoon, a mob had surrounded the inquisition's chapterhouse on the high street, and smoke rose from it that was not the smoke of burning witches, and nobody, as far as Paloma could see, was sorry.

"They're tearing it apart," Ivo reported, coming back from a scouting loop with salt spray in his beard and an expression Paloma had never seen on him before. He kept trying to arrange his face into professional neutrality and failing. "Chapterhouse, records office, the lot. There's a bonfire of warrants on the high street. Someone put Vane's effigy in it. Poor craftsmanship, frankly. The nose is all wrong."

"Funny man," said Serah, but she was gripping his arm hard enough to leave marks, and neither of them seemed to notice.

"And Vane himself?"

Ivo's face sobered. "Gone. Slipped out the river gate with maybe a dozen men who stayed loyal. Headed—" He hesitated.

"Headed here," Paloma finished. "Toward the cathedral. Toward the Door." She looked out at the smoking city, at the jubilant mobs, at the freedom they had bought with an old woman's courage and a boy's accidental silence, and felt the joy curdle in her chest exactly on schedule. "One day. That's what she bought us. Maybe less. He'll raise the coastal garrisons. He'll come back with force enough not to care what the papers say."

"Then we go tonight," Serah said. "Now, if we can."

* * *

They gathered in the shadow of the drowned cathedral's spire as the light went long and gold across the water. Maren sat on a fallen block of masonry, exhausted, hollow-eyed, wrapped in Ivo's cloak, and Tam sat pressed against her side with his slate balanced on his knees. He had taken to her instantly, the way he took to anyone who was quiet in a way he recognized.

**YOU WERE NOT AFRAID?** he wrote, and showed her.

Maren studied the slate for a long moment. "Oh, I was afraid," she said. "I have been afraid for twenty-two years. This morning I simply decided to be afraid *out loud*, and do you know, it weighs considerably less that way." She smiled at him—a real smile, tired around the edges. "Your friend there taught me that without knowing it. All those years hiding what she is, and look at her. Still standing."

"I'm standing because I'm about to be running," Paloma said. "Vane will be back by dawn with every sword he can rent."

"Then let's not waste the evening." Serah unrolled the sketch of the undercroft they'd drawn from Tam's memory-drawings, weighted the corners with shells. "The Door is below the third chapel. The oath has to be spoken in the old tongue, at the threshold, touching the salt. Once it opens—"

"Once it opens, every hunter within a hundred miles feels it," Paloma said. "Like a bell rung underwater. We've been through this."

"Then we've also been through *this*: you speak, we go, you stay. And the faster we move, the more of Vane's attention you take with us when we go." Serah's jaw set. She looked, suddenly, very much like her father, and Paloma saw her realize it and hate it. "I won't pretend I'm at peace with it. I'm not. None of us are. But my mother gave today to buy your door, Paloma. Don't make her gift cheap by hesitating at the lock."

Maren reached over and touched her daughter's wrist. "Serah."

"Mother."

"When the time comes, you will walk through that door," Maren said, "and you will not look back, and you will teach the old tongue to children who have never been afraid of it. That is your testimony. Mine is finished." She glanced at the smoking skyline, at the gulls wheeling over the ruins of the chapterhouse, and something flickered across her face—grief, maybe, for the man she had married, drowning somewhere out there in the wreckage of his own lies. "Let it be finished."

For a moment nobody spoke. The tide breathed against the pilings. Somewhere across the water, faintly, the mob was singing—an old harvest song, of all things, the words bent slightly wrong the way folk songs always are, and Paloma thought she caught, threaded through it, a phrase in the old tongue that no living singer should have known. The country remembering itself despite everything.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "One day of chaos. One night to open a door that hasn't opened in three hundred years, guarded by an inquisitor with a grudge and nothing left to lose." She rolled up her sleeves, the way she did before knotting a charm, before binding a wound, before every small act of magic she had ever hidden. "I've sold luck to braver fools than us. Let's go spend some."

They gathered their gear in the last of the light. And it was Tam who stopped them—tugging Paloma's sleeve, holding up his slate with both hands, his eyes wide and fixed on the water below the spire.

**THE TIDE IS WRONG,** he had written. **IT IS COMING IN WITHOUT THE MOON.**

Paloma looked. He was right. Far out past the harbor mouth, where the sea should have been lying flat and silver under the fading sky, the water was standing up.
