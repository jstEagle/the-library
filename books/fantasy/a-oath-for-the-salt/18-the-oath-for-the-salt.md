## 18. The Oath for the Salt

The Salt Door did not look like a door.

It looked like the sea had decided, once, to stand still. A wall of brined stone rising from the flooded nave of the drowned cathedral, forty feet of salt-crusted granite veined with channels that might have been carved or might have been wept. The water came up to their knees in the crypt below the spire, black and patient, and the Door stood at its far end like a held breath.

Paloma pressed her palm flat against it and felt—nothing. No hum, no warmth, no whisper of the old tongue sleeping inside. Just cold stone and older salt.

"Twenty years," she said quietly, "and it doesn't even say hello."

"It's waiting for something," Serah said. She stood at Paloma's shoulder, her mother's testimony still ringing somewhere behind them in the city above—the chaos of Greyharbor's turning had bought them this hour, maybe two, no more. "You know what it's waiting for."

"I know." Paloma pulled her hand back. "I was hoping to negotiate."

"You? Negotiate?" Ivo laughed from where he leaned against a fallen pillar, one arm still bound from the crossbow bolt he'd taken for Tam. He'd gone gray around the mouth on the walk down through the flooded streets, but his eyes were clear. "You haggled me out of arresting you with nothing but a luck-knot and a look."

"That was charm, not negotiation. There's a difference."

"There is," Tam signed, quick fingers flickering in the lamplight. *Charm works better.*

Paloma snorted despite everything, and Serah's mother—Maren Vane, who three days ago had been a prisoner of her own husband—laughed outright, a bright sound that bounced off the wet stone and made the dark feel briefly smaller.

Then the dark closed again, because above them, muffled by water and stone but unmistakable, came the sound of boots. Many boots. In step.

* * *

They heard him before they saw him. They always did; Inquisitor Aldous Vane believed silence was a weapon only other people needed.

"Paloma Galloway." His voice carried down the drowned stairwell the way oil carries along water, slick and spreading. "I hold a writ signed by the crown itself. Surrender, and the amnesty stands. Every name in your stolen book. I am a man of my word."

"He is," Maren said softly, bitterly, beside Serah. "That's the horror of him. He'll keep it exactly as long as it costs him nothing."

Paloma looked at the Door. She looked at Tam, standing hip-deep in the black water, his small hands still, his silence wrapped around the four of them like a cloak she could almost feel. She looked at Ivo, who had already shifted his weight forward, sword half-drawn, as if a man with one working arm could stop what was coming down those stairs.

She looked at Serah, who was looking at the stairwell with her father's same gray eyes and none of his patience.

"No," Paloma said. "I don't think I'll surrender."

"Then they burn," Vane called down, conversational now, terrible. "All of them. The boy first. Children burn quickest—you'd know that, wouldn't you, charmseller? You taught half of them their letters before you ran."

Something went very quiet and very large inside Paloma's chest.

It was not anger. Anger she knew; anger was a market-day thing, hot and cheap and gone by evening. This was colder. This was the tide deciding.

"Serah," she said. "Get your mother against the far wall. Ivo—" She turned. The soldier who had been sent to kill her twenty years too late was watching her with an expression she'd learned to read across a hundred campfires: he already knew what she was going to do, and he was already grieving it.

"Don't," he said.

"Ivo."

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. "There has to be another way. There's always—"

"Ivo." She smiled at him, and was surprised to find the smile was real. "You took a bolt for a boy you'd known six weeks. Don't tell me there's always another way. You know better than anyone there isn't. There's just the way, and whether you walk it alone."

Tam grabbed her sleeve. His hands moved fast, urgent, the signs tumbling over each other. *Don't. Don't. We find another door. We go around. We—*

"Little fish." She crouched in the water so they were eye to eye, and cupped his face in both hands, salt and lamplight between them. "Listen to me. You can't hear me, I know, but listen anyway, because you're the best listener I've ever met. When the Door opens—and it will open—you go through. You don't wait. You don't look back. You take Serah's hand and you go, and you live so loudly that no one in that country beyond ever manages to be silent again. Can you do that?"

His eyes were huge and wet and furious. He signed one word. *Why you?*

"Because," Paloma said, "an oath has to be spoken by someone who means every word. And I mean all of mine."

Above them, the boots reached the last landing.

* * *

Vane came down into the crypt with twelve soldiers and a lantern held high, and the light caught the salt wall and made it glitter like a jaw full of teeth.

He looked older than his daughter. Not in years—in certainty. Serah doubted things; doubt had carved lines into her that made her beautiful. Vane had never doubted anything in his life except, perhaps, in this moment, the arithmetic of his own bargain, because his gaze went from Paloma to the registry under Ivo's arm to the Door and back, and for one heartbeat something flickered behind his face that might have been fear.

"Last chance," he said.

"You keep offering me chances," Paloma said. "That's not like an inquisitor."

"The writ requires opportunity for repentance." He drew his blade. It was a long, clean thing, ceremonial and functional at once, the way cruelty always is when it believes itself righteous. "Consider this your opportunity."

"Considered," said Paloma Galloway, and spoke.

Not the old tongue—not yet. First, plain Brackwater common, loud enough to fill the crypt, loud enough to crack against the stone:

"My name is Paloma Galloway. Daughter of Marisol the knotmaker, who was burned in the Ash Year for teaching letters to gifted children. Student of Old Wren, who hid eleven of us in a smokehouse for a winter and never once complained about the smell. Student of Brother Callow, who was drowned in this very harbor for writing down the words I am about to use."

The soldiers shifted. Vane's eyes narrowed. "Guards—"

"And I am a swordspeaker." Her voice dropped into the old tongue then, and it was like watching a river remember which way was downhill. The words came up out of her from somewhere below twenty years of hiding, below the charms and the copper and the careful small life, and they were *loud*, not in volume but in truth, and the water in the crypt began to move.

Every gifted soul within a hundred miles would feel it. Every hunter, every crier, every registered name trembling on a magistrate's list. That was the trap inside the escape, and she walked into it with her eyes open, because the Door demanded truth and truth cannot be whispered.

"*By salt that remembers, by oath that binds, I open what was closed.*"

The Door woke.

Light came up through the channels in the stone like dawn coming up through a cracked egg—not white, not gold, but the pale green-silver of deep sea and old glass—and the whole wall shuddered, and the water around their knees went warm as bathwater, and somewhere in the country above them, Paloma knew, bells were starting to ring in towers that had no reason to ring.

"STOP HER!" Vane roared, and the truce died with the words, and the battle began.

* * *

Later—much later, in the telling, in the songs that would eventually get it wrong—people would say the fight at the Salt Door lasted an hour. It lasted four minutes. Four minutes is a long time when you are holding a door open with your voice.

Ivo met the first three soldiers at the lip of the flooded stairwell, one-armed and furious, fighting less like a sergeant and more like a man settling accounts. Serah put herself between her father and her mother and did not move an inch, though her father's blade was longer than her reach and her mother was screaming at them both to stop, please, please. Tam—

Tam did the thing that no one, not even Paloma, had fully understood until that moment.

He opened his gift all the way.

The boy who unmade sound unmade *all* of it. The clash of steel went silent. The shouting went silent. The roar of the waking Door went silent. Twelve soldiers found themselves swinging swords in a world with no noise in it at all, and men who have trained their whole lives on drums and shouted commands do not fight well deafened by silence itself; they flinched, they hesitated, they looked at each other with wide white eyes, and in that hesitation Ivo and Serah cut them down or drove them back, and Vane stood alone in the ringing nothing with his lantern guttering.

And Paloma kept speaking.

Her voice worked even inside Tam's silence—that was the miracle of it, or the design, the old tongue being older than sound, riding underneath it. She named her dead teachers and felt them arrive, somehow, at her shoulders, a pressure like hands. She named the eleven children of the smokehouse winter, scattered now across the realm, some registered, some run, some ash. She named the widow Marta and her luck-knots. She named Ivo Ren, who chose otherwise. She named Serah Vane, who offered a fragile hand and then gripped hard. She named Maren, whose testimony had bought this hour. She named Tam, last and longest, the smallest bond and the truest, *my son in every way that matters, which is all the ways.*

The Door drank the names and grew brighter, and the salt began to slough off it in sheets, dissolving into the water, and beneath the crust the stone was not stone at all but compressed centuries, pressed-flat years of exile and erasure, and it was opening the way grief opens—which is slowly, and then all at once.

Vane broke through Tam's silence the way a drowning man breaks water, lunging, blade high, straight at Paloma's back.

He never completed the stroke.

He stopped—frozen mid-lunge, blade trembling a hand's width from her shoulder blades—because Paloma, without breaking cadence, without turning, added one more clause to her oath:

"*And let any who raise steel against this threshold remember what they see here, forever, and be compelled to tell it.*"

An oath of witness. The binding she'd been saving. Vane's arm lowered by degrees, like a gate on rusted hinges, and his face was a study in horror—not of death, which he had dealt in for thirty years, but of *memory*. Of being made, permanently, into a man who could not unknow.

"No," he whispered. "No, take it back, I'll—I'll sign whatever—"

"You'll remember," Paloma said, still speaking the old tongue, still feeding the Door. "You'll remember, and you'll tell everyone, for the rest of your life, exactly what happened here tonight. That's not a punishment, Aldous. That's a job. Someone has to carry the history. Your country buried it. You can dig it up with your mouth."

The soldiers who remained upright had stopped fighting entirely. They stared—at the light, at their frozen commander, at the woman in the water who glowed faintly at the edges now, as if the Door's tide had gotten into her blood.

The Door finished opening.

* * *

It opened onto morning.

That was the impossible thing, the thing Paloma would spend the rest of her existence explaining to travelers: beyond the Salt Door there was a coastline, real wind, gulls, the smell of woodsmoke and tar—a country that had never burned anyone, built by the ones who'd escaped the last time. Light poured through the doorway in a long gold lane across the black water, and at the end of the lane, faintly, figures were standing up from beach fires and walking toward the Door, because they'd felt the oath too. They'd felt it for miles.

"It's open," Serah breathed. "It's actually—Mother. Mother, it's open."

"Go," Paloma said.

"Not without—"

"*Go.*" She turned then, finally, and the sight of her stopped Serah mid-word. Paloma could feel what she must look like: lit from inside, her feet already less flesh than salt-stone, the tide climbing her ankles and staying. "The Door holds while I speak. The moment I stop, it closes. I'm not stopping. So you have about ninety seconds to hug me, and then you have the rest of your lives to make me glad you didn't waste them."

What happened next happened fast, because it had to.

Maren Vane went first, pausing only to grip Paloma's hands hard enough to hurt and say, "My daughter comes back to visit you, do you understand? However this works. She comes back."

"If the Door allows," Paloma said, which was honest, and Maren nodded as if honesty were the only currency she'd ever trusted.

Serah hugged her fiercely, quickly, a soldier's embrace, and said into her ear, "I'm going to teach them the old tongue. All of them. Your language won't die with you."

"It was never mine," Paloma said. "That's rather the point. Go."

Ivo came last before Tam. He stood in front of her in the gold light with his ruined arm and his tear-tracked face, and he didn't say anything clever, because he was Ivo, and when the moment was too big for words he simply saluted—the old parade-ground salute, crisp and absurd, knee-deep in a flooded cathedral—and Paloma laughed and cried at once and pushed him toward the light with both hands.

Then Tam.

The boy planted himself in front of her and refused. Signed *no*, over and over, fast and furious, tears cutting through the salt spray on his face. *No. No. No.*

"Tam." She crouched one last time, though her knees protested, though her body was already becoming something that crouched differently. "Hey. Little fish. Look at me."

He looked.

"You gave me your silence once," she said. "Back on the road, the first night, when you thought I was sad. You pressed it into my hand like a stone. I've been carrying it ever since." She held out her palm. "Give it to me properly now. All of it. I'll need it, where I'm going. And you—" her voice caught, and she let it, because the Door wanted truth and she was done hiding anything, "—you won't need it anymore. You're going somewhere no one will ever ask you to be quiet. Somewhere they'll wait as long as it takes for your hands to finish talking. Go be loud, sweetheart. Go be *so* loud."

Tam shook. Then he took her hand, turned it over, and pressed both his small palms flat against hers—and she felt it, the gift passing between them like a smooth cold pebble, like a held breath given away—and he signed, slow and deliberate, one last time:

*Come visit. Somehow. Promise.*

"Promise," Paloma said, and the word rang down through the Door like a bell through water, and oaths made at thresholds bind deeper than any others.

Serah took his hand. He went. He did not look back—he'd promised—but at the last step his free hand rose and signed *love you* over his shoulder, and Paloma watched the most important person she'd ever kidnapped walk out of her country and into the morning.

The Door began to narrow.

On the far side, growing smaller: Ivo planting something in harbor soil—she'd bet her afterlife it was a luck-knot. Serah with her arm around a shaking boy. Maren waving. Figures from the settlements coming down the beach, dozens of them, faces Paloma knew from stories and faces she didn't, all of them looking back at her.

Behind her, in the darkening crypt, Inquisitor Aldous Vane knelt in the black water, weeping and remembering, already composing the first telling of the tale he would repeat until he died.

And Paloma Galloway—swordspeaker, charm-seller, liar, keeper of oaths, door—took a breath, and kept speaking.
