## 19. Who Stays

Ivo Ren had stood in a great many doorways in his life. Barracks doors he'd been afraid to walk through. His mother's door the winter she died, which he'd been too late to knock on. The magistrate's hall, the night a shadow with a stolen book slipped past him and changed everything.

None of them had ever looked like this.

The Salt Door filled the drowned cathedral's crypt from floor to vaulted ceiling—a wall of light and water that wasn't light or water, brined silver at its edges, deep green-gold at its heart, and through it, impossibly, a beach under a morning sky. Gulls wheeled on the far side of the world. The tide came in and went out, patient as anything, as if doors between realms were just another thing the sea did when it felt like it.

And Paloma Galloway stood in front of it with her arms spread like a woman holding open a tavern door for friends, speaking words in the old tongue that Ivo did not understand but felt anyway, the way you feel thunder in your back teeth.

She had been speaking for an hour. Her voice was fraying at the edges. She showed no sign of stopping.

"We're not going," Serah said beside him. It wasn't a question, but she said it like one, the way people do when they're begging the universe to contradict them.

Ivo didn't answer. He was watching Tam.

The boy stood closest to the Door—closest to *her*—and he was doing the thing he did when the world got too loud: pulling sound into himself. Ivo could see it happening. The crash of Vane's soldiers retreating up the flooded stairwells, the groan of the cathedral settling, even the sea itself—all of it dimmed around Tam until there was a pocket of quiet the size of a chapel, and inside that quiet, Paloma's voice came through clear as a bell over still water.

*Hold,* her oath said, in the old tongue, over and over, wearing different shapes each time. *Hold. Hold. Hold.*

"She can't stop," Ivo said quietly. "You understand that, don't you? Now that she's started."

Serah's jaw tightened. "Then we make her."

"With what?" He turned to look at the inquisitor's daughter—at what she'd become in three weeks, mud to the knees, her father's signet ring hanging on a cord around her neck where she could see it and hate it. "You've read the same fragments I have. The Door holds while the oath holds. The oath is spoken by a swordspeaker. There are—" he stopped, because the arithmetic was obscene, and finished anyway, because she deserved the whole ugly sum— "there are no other swordspeakers, Serah. Not here. Not anywhere we can reach. It's her voice or nothing."

"Then we find another way. We wedge it. We anchor it. We—"

"Serah." Maren Vane put her hand on her daughter's arm. The old woman had been rescued two days ago from her husband's trap, had watched her husband kneel in black water and weep, and had borne all of it with the particular iron patience of someone who had been married to an inquisitor for thirty years and had therefore already survived worse. "Love. Listen to me. Some doors only open one way. Your father never understood that either."

"I am *not* my father."

"No," Maren agreed. "He would have let everyone else burn first."

* * *

They argued on the far side of the threshold, which Ivo supposed was fitting. The company had gathered on the beach—himself, Serah, Tam, Maren—and behind them, coming down the dunes in ones and twos, the settlements. People who had gone through this Door twenty years ago, or whose grandparents had. A fisherman with a net slung over one shoulder who kept looking back at the light like a man checking whether a fire was still burning. An old woman carrying a crate of seed potatoes as if the fate of two worlds depended on them, which, Ivo reflected, it possibly did.

Paloma's luck-knots were everywhere. Tied to wrists, braided into hair, knotted onto cart harnesses and boat rigging. He recognized his own work now—the ones he'd planted in harbor soil along the coast road, sprouting into small stubborn shrubs with salt-white flowers. She'd taught him the knots without ever telling him they were anything more than craft. Twenty years of hiding a gift so thoroughly she'd hidden it from herself, some days.

"You're staring at the plants again," Serah said.

"I'm thinking."

"That's new."

He almost smiled. Almost. "When I saw her in the magistrate's hall, I hesitated. One heartbeat. That heartbeat has cost me my rank, my name, and a crossbow bolt through the shoulder, and I want you to know I'd pay it again." He looked at her. "But I also know what it costs to be the one who stays behind. My brother stayed behind at Greyharbor ford. Held the line so the rest of us could cross. They gave him a medal and buried him in it." His throat worked. "I'm not going to stand on this beach and let her choose that alone. Somebody has to argue. That's the job nobody wants."

"And if arguing doesn't work?"

"Then somebody has to stay with her."

Silence. The gulls cried. On the other side of the light, faintly, Paloma's voice wavered and steadied.

"You'd die for her," Serah said. It wasn't mockery. It was inventory.

"I'd live for her, if she'd let me. But she won't, so." He shrugged, and his shoulder screamed, and he ignored it. "Stay with her, then. Argue with her. Die with her. All of it amounts to the same thing: she doesn't get to decide she's worth less than the rest of us. Nobody voted on that."

Tam tugged his sleeve.

The boy held up his slate. He'd been writing on it more since the fever took Ivo's voice for two days and Tam's silence became their whole vocabulary. The chalk letters were careful, blocky, the handwriting of someone who had learned letters late and loved them fiercely:

**SHE ALREADY DECIDED.**

Ivo looked at the slate. Looked at the boy. Tam's eyes were dry, which somehow made it worse; children who cry are still hoping.

"When?" Ivo asked.

Tam wiped the slate with his sleeve and wrote again, slower:

**AT THE GRAVEYARD. WHEN THE DEAD SHOWED HER THE DOOR. SHE ASKED ME IF I COULD QUIET THE WATER ALL THE WAY TO GREYHARBOR. I SAID YES. SHE SMILED LIKE AT A FUNERAL.**

Ivo closed his eyes. Of course. Of course she'd known. Paloma Galloway, who had spent twenty years making sure every kindness she did looked like commerce—who sold luck instead of giving it, who bound wounds with oaths disguised as blessings—had known since the mass grave at Hollowmere that she was walking toward this, and had told exactly no one, because telling people meant letting them carry it, and she had never once in her life let anyone carry anything.

"Well," he said. "Then we'd better go say goodbye properly. And then we'd better disobey her."

Tam's mouth twitched. It was the closest thing to laughter anyone had managed in two days.

* * *

Crossing the threshold was like walking through the memory of swimming.

One moment the beach, the wind, the smell of woodsmoke from the settlement fires. The next, the crypt—cold stone, knee-deep black water, the vast shining wall of the Door filling the space where the cathedral's east window had once shown saints. Vane's soldiers had fled up the stairs hours ago, driven out not by force but by what Paloma's oath was doing to them: every man who lingered began remembering things, true things, terrible things, the purges and the forged evidence and the faces of the burned, until staying became unbearable. An army routed by honesty. Ivo thought his old commander would have found it deeply unfair.

Paloma stood before the Door with her feet planted in the water, and she was *thin*. Not thin in body—though that too, gods, when had she last eaten—but thin in some other way, as if the oath were drawing on more than her voice, spending her the way a candle spends itself, and the light of the Door was partly her, and everyone could see it.

"—and you," she was saying, mid-oath, mid-sentence, not even turning around, because of course she knew they'd come. "Sergeant Ivo Ren, who saw a thief in the dark and chose to see a woman instead. Who took a bolt for a boy he'd known three weeks. Whose brother died holding a line, and who has been holding lines ever since because he doesn't know any other way to love people—"

"Paloma—"

"—I name you, and I keep you, and the Door hears it, and so do I." Her voice cracked on the last word and the Door flickered, and she steadied it with visible effort, teeth gritted. "Don't interrupt a woman working."

"Paloma." He waded closer. The water was cold enough to ache. "Step away from the Door."

"Can't."

"Then I'll pull you."

"You'll try." She glanced back at him, and there it was—that grin, the crooked one, the charm-seller's grin that had gotten half of Brackwater to buy knots they didn't need and the other half to believe in luck they couldn't afford. Except now it was exhausted and lit from behind by a door between worlds, and it was the bravest thing he had ever seen. "Ivo. Sweet, stubborn, terrible liar of a soldier. You know what happens if you drag me off this spot."

"The Door closes."

"The Door closes, and everyone on that beach dies of hope. Or worse—doesn't die, just goes back to hiding. Back to selling charms for copper and biting their tongues until they bleed." Her voice dropped. "I did twenty years of that. I will not hand it back to them. Not when I'm the key."

"There has to be another—"

"There isn't. I've had an hour to look. Believe me, I checked twice." She softened. "Come here. Properly. If these are the last minutes, I'm not spending them shouting across a flooded church."

He went. Standing beside her was like standing beside a forge—heat that wasn't heat, pressure that wasn't wind, the old tongue pouring through her and out of her and into the Door. Up close he could see what it was costing. Fine lines of salt crusted at her temples like frost. Her hands trembled between phrases and stilled when she spoke, as if the words themselves were holding her together.

"Let the others go first," she said. "Maren, then Serah. Then you. Tam last—he'll fight it, so give him my message first."

"What message?"

"'The knot holds because every strand lets go of being alone.'" She smiled ruefully. "It's a charm-seller's saying. Turns out it's true. Tell him exactly that, word for word, or he'll know I garbled it, and he'll sulk at me across the boundary of reality, and I will *not* be blamed for that in whatever comes after."

"Paloma—"

"Ivo." She looked up at him, and for a moment the grin fell away entirely, and underneath it was just a tired woman of forty-odd years who had hidden herself so long she'd nearly vanished, and who had finally, finally said her own name out loud, and was about to lose everything except the saying of it. "Thank you for hesitating. One heartbeat in a dark hallway. Everything after comes from that. Don't you dare regret it."

"I regret plenty," he said roughly. "Not that."

"Good." The grin came back, wet-eyed. "Now go. You're dripping on the oath."

* * *

Maren Vane crossed first.

She paused in front of Paloma, this small fierce woman who had married an inquisitor and raised a better daughter than either of them deserved, and she reached up and tucked a strand of hair behind Paloma's ear with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had been doing it to children for decades.

"My mother used to say the sea keeps what it loves," Maren said. "I always thought it was a comfort. I've decided it's a threat. The sea had better keep you *safe*, girl, or I will come back through this door myself and haunt you personally."

"Maren—"

"No hush now. You've done your talking for a lifetime." She patted Paloma's cheek once, hard enough to sting slightly, which Ivo suspected was deliberate. Then she walked into the light, straight-backed, and emerged on the beach a figure no bigger than a thumb, waving.

Serah lasted longer.

She stood in front of Paloma with her fists clenched and her eyes bright and furious, and Ivo heard her say, low and fast: "This is wrong. This is the worst possible plan. I have written down eleven objections and I will read them to you when I find a way back, and I *will* find a way back, do you hear me? My father built his whole life on the idea that some people are worth less. I am going to spend mine proving him wrong, starting with you."

"Starting with me," Paloma repeated softly. "That's the kindest thing anyone's said to me in twenty years."

"It wasn't kind. It was a threat."

"From a Vane? I'm honored." Paloma leaned forward until their foreheads touched, oath and all, the Door humming around them both. "Undo him, Serah. Teach the old tongue. Burn the registry—no. No, don't burn it. Read every name aloud, once a year, in a loud voice, in a town square somewhere. Let them be remembered loudly. That's the only revenge worth having."

Serah laughed, or sobbed, or something between. "You're impossible."

"I'm a door. Doors are famously difficult." She stepped back. "Go. Before I run out of clever things to say, which, as you know, has never once happened, but there's a first time for everything."

Serah went. At the last step she turned, saluted—clumsy, soldier-fashion, learned from watching Ivo—and was through.

Then it was Tam.

The boy did not move. He stood in the black water with his slate hugged to his chest and his whole small body set like a fence post, and Ivo, who had carried this child through flooded streets and fever nights and one memorable incident involving a smuggler's dog, recognized the posture immediately. It was the stance of someone preparing to refuse.

"Tam," Ivo said gently. "She asked me to give you a message."

Tam shook his head. Wrote on the slate: **NO.**

"She said you'd say that."

**SHE KNOWS EVERYTHING. THAT'S THE PROBLEM.**

"She does," Ivo agreed. "It's insufferable. But listen. Word for word, she said. 'The knot holds because every strand lets go of being alone.'"

Tam went very still.

Ivo watched the sentence land in him—not as words, he realized, but as understanding. The boy's gift was silence; he unmade sound wherever he stood. And Paloma's gift was speech; she made oaths that outlasted her. Two opposites. One knot.

Tam walked forward slowly. He stopped in front of Paloma, and he did something Ivo had never seen him do: he opened his mouth. Nothing came out—nothing ever came out—but the *shape* of it, the effort, the wanting, was louder than any scream.

"I know," Paloma whispered. Her voice was nearly gone now, worn down to silk over gravel. "I know, sweetheart. I wish you could tell me too."

Tam took her hand. Turned it palm-up. And then he pressed his other hand against hers, flat, fingers aligned, the way you'd press a seal into wax—and Ivo felt it happen. Felt the sound drain out of the world. Not gradually. All at once, like a tide going out. The drip of water, the groan of stone, the distant sea, the hum of the Door—even Paloma's fraying voice, mid-word, cut to nothing.

For three heartbeats the crypt was the quietest place in any world.

Then Tam pulled his hand back, and sound crashed in, and Paloma stared at her palm. In the center of it, faint as a watermark, sat a small spiral mark, like a shell seen in cross-section. Like a knot.

"What did you—" she started, and stopped, and Ivo saw her understand it the same moment he did. The boy had given her a piece of his silence. Pressed it into her palm like a stone, like a coin, like a knot. A pocket of quiet she could carry into whatever she was becoming. So that even a door, even a woman made of oath and salt and light, might have one place inside her where nothing spoke, and she could rest there.

Tam wrote on the slate, held it up:

**SO YOU CAN HEAR YOURSELF THINK.**

And then, wiping it quickly, before she could answer:

**AND SO YOU'RE NOT ALONE IN THERE.**

Paloma made a sound Ivo had never heard from her—small, broken, joyful—and Tam turned and ran, actually ran, through the Door before she could see his face, because he was wise, this mute scrap of a boy, wise far past his years, and he knew that some goodbyes are only survivable at speed.

Ivo caught him on the other side. The boy shook, silently, in his arms, facing the light, watching the place where she stood.

* * *

Ivo was the last one left on the near side.

He hadn't planned it. Or rather, he had planned the opposite, and his feet had mutinied. He stood in the cold water with the Door blazing in front of him and Paloma swaying within it, and he said, "Tell me how to help. There must be something. Water, food, a chair, a—"

"A chair," she repeated. "Yes, Ivo, fetch me a nice chair, I'll hold open the gate between worlds and take tea."

"I'm serious."

"So am I." She sagged, caught herself, sagged again. The salt-lines had spread down her cheeks now, glittering. Through her, the Door pulsed steady as a heartbeat. "Here's how you help. You go through. You plant your ridiculous knots in harbor soil until the whole coast smells of them. You teach the settlements to drill like a soldier, because they're soft and kind and the world over that hill is not. You watch Tam grow up and you tell him, every year on this day, exactly what he gave me and exactly why it mattered. And you—" her voice caught, steadied— "you forgive yourself for the hunt. Eventually. It takes years. I'm still working on forgiving myself for the hiding. We can compare notes, someday, if doors ever open twice."

"Doors open twice," Ivo said. "You're proof. You spent twenty years shut and you opened."

Paloma laughed—it hurt her, he could see it hurt, and she did it anyway, because of course she did. "Go, Sergeant. That's an order. Last one I'll ever give you. Disobey all my others, but obey this one."

He stepped toward her. Stopped. "Paloma. When travelers tell stories about the door of salt—what should they call you? Not Galloway. Not the charm-seller. What name do I plant?"

She considered, head tilted, and the light moved through her hair like water through kelp, and she smiled the real smile, the private one, the one he'd seen maybe four times in all these weeks and hoarded every one.

"Tell them the door remembers everyone," she said. "That's name enough. Names are just handles, Ivo. I was never the handle. I was what it pointed at."

He nodded. He couldn't speak. He crossed the last steps, took her free hand—the one without the silence-mark in it—and pressed it briefly to his forehead, the way soldiers greet commanders they'd follow into hell, the way he'd never once done for any officer he'd actually served.

Then he let go, and walked into the light.

The beach hit him all at once—wind, sun, the roar of the surf, dozens of voices, Tam's hand finding his instantly, Serah's shoulder against his other side. Behind them the Door stood tall and burning-bright, narrowing slowly, slowly, held open by a voice growing fainter and fainter across the boundary of the world.

Through it, one last time, they heard her—not the oath, not the old tongue, just Paloma, plain Brackwater accent and all, calling out over the water:

"Mind the tide! And somebody feed that boy, he's all elbows!"

And then the light folded closed like a hand, gentle as anything, and the beach was only a beach, and the doorway was only air, and the sea came in and went out, patient as anything, keeping what it loved.

Tam stood looking at the empty air for a long moment. Then he tugged Ivo's sleeve and held up his slate.

**SHE'S STILL THERE. I CAN FEEL THE QUIET.**

Ivo looked. Saw nothing but morning. Felt, beneath his feet, in the harbor soil where his luck-knots grew, something steady and vast and listening.

"All right," he said, and his voice broke only a little. "Then we'd better give her something worth watching."

Down the beach, Serah was already unrolling a slate of her own—chalk in hand, the old tongue's first lesson taking shape—and the settlements were coming down to the waterline, and the gulls were wheeling, and somewhere beyond the hills, Ivo knew, the rest of the world was waiting to learn that its magic had never died at all.

It had just been waiting for someone to say its name.
