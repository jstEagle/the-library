## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The snow came down the way it always did in the Thornfell passes—sideways, with intent, as if the mountains themselves had opinions about who belonged there and who did not. Sebastian Reyes pulled their collar higher and squinted into the white, counting mules by silhouette. Nine. All nine still moving. Behind them, the caravan's hired muscle trudged in a huddled line, shapes more than people, their grumbling swallowed by wind.

"Keep them tight," Sebastian called back to Doss, the caravan master, a squat man whose beard had frozen into something resembling a weapon. "If we lose the trail at the switchback, we lose the whole night."

"And if we lose the whole night?"

"Then we lose more than that." Sebastian didn't elaborate. The route they were on wasn't marked on any map worth trusting—it was one of the old smuggler's roads, worn into the mountain by generations of men and women who'd preferred not to be counted. Sebastian had learned it before they'd learned to read, back when the company still took contracts this far north, back when the world had seemed like a place you could simply move through without being seen.

Invisibility was a trade, like any other. You paid for it in cold, in hunger, in the particular loneliness of knowing every road and no home. Sebastian had been paying it for twenty-six years and considered the price fair.

Ahead, through the driving white, a shape rose out of the slope: a broken arch of grey stone, furred with frost, half-swallowed by the hillside. Sebastian raised a fist and the column stopped.

"What is it?" Doss asked, coming up beside them, breath steaming.

"Shrine. Old one." Sebastian studied it. The arch was all that stood above ground; the rest had slid into the mountain decades ago, maybe longer. Snow had drifted against it in a smooth ramp, which meant shelter beneath—cellars, crypts, whatever the builders had thought their god needed room for. "We can hole up under it until the wind turns. Mules too."

Doss peered at the arch with the suspicion he reserved for anything older than his grandmother. "Whose shrine? What god?"

Sebastian hesitated. That was the strange thing—they knew these passes the way other people knew their own hands, and yet they couldn't have said who had built the shrines dotted along them. There were dozens. Waystones, cairns, little roofless chapels, all of them bearing the same worn sigil: a circle with a single vertical line descending through it, like a thread dropped into a well. Sebastian had traced that sigil a thousand times without ever asking its meaning. It was simply part of the landscape, like scree and snowmelt.

"I don't know," they said, and found the admission sat oddly in their chest. "Doesn't matter. Stone doesn't care what you believe. Come on."

* * *

The cellar beneath the shrine was dry, which in the Thornfell high country in late autumn was close to miraculous. Someone had built it well—vaulted ceilings, drainage channels cut into the floor, niches in the walls where offerings might once have rested. The mules stamped and steamed in the entrance chamber while the hired blades spread out, grateful for walls, and Doss got a fire going in the old hearth with the efficiency of a man who had committed many small crimes against many cold nights.

Sebastian walked the outer rooms while the others settled. This was habit, not piety: check the sightlines, check the exits, check whether anyone else had found shelter here first. The cellar had three passages off the main vault. Two were choked with rubble. The third ended at a collapsed stairway, and beyond the stairway, wedged behind a fallen slab, Sebastian saw the corner of something that was not stone.

They worked it free with a knife blade and a quarter hour of patience. It was an oilcloth packet, waxed and tied, the kind smugglers used for cargo that couldn't afford damp—and Sebastian knew the knots on it. Company knots. The kind Orla taught every recruit in their first week, the ones meant to be untied blind, in the dark, with cold fingers.

Someone from the company had cached this. Years ago, by the look of the dust. Maybe someone dead now.

Inside the oilcloth was a book.

It was small, bound in leather gone stiff and dark, its spine cracked with age. No title on the cover. Sebastian turned it over twice, feeling the weight of it, telling themselves it was probably a ledger—a cache record, a debt list, nothing. Smugglers left ledgers in holes all the time.

But ledgers didn't come wrapped in company knots and hidden behind altars.

Sebastian carried it back toward the firelight, and that was when they noticed the sigil pressed into the leather—the circle, the descending line. The same mark that was carved into the arch above them, the waystones, every shrine in these passes. The mark that had also, though Sebastian had never told a living soul this, been burned into the skin of their left wrist since the day they were born. A birthmark shaped exactly like it, precise as a brand, which the company's old physicker had once looked at for a long time and then said, carefully, *some marks are just marks, Reyes,* in the voice of a man deciding not to say something else.

Sebastian sat down by the fire, out of the others' earshot, and opened the book.

The first pages were water-stained and near-illegible. A chronicle, or the bones of one—entries in a cramped hand, dates in a calendar Sebastian didn't recognize, names of places that meant nothing. *The Vale of Alderhold. The Court of the Nine Wells. The King Under Cairn.* Somewhere past the middle, the handwriting changed, grew shakier, and the entries stopped being records and started being something else. Warnings. Or promises. The ink turned brown. The sentences turned strange:

*When the name is struck from the stone, the stone will keep it anyway.*

*The Door does not open for armies. It opens for blood, and for grief, and for one who carries both.*

*A child will be born with the sigil unasked-for, in the season the passes close. Mark the year. Mark the day.*

And then, on a page so brittle Sebastian was afraid to breathe on it, a date.

They read it three times. The numerals were written in an old style, but Isolde—no, not Isolde, that was a name from nowhere, from nowhere at all—the merchant factor in Callowmere used the same conventions, and Sebastian could read them fine. Day, month, year. The month was the one that was ending tonight. The day was today.

The year was a hundred years gone.

Below the date, in that same browned, trembling hand, two lines:

*The heir comes down from the high road in the company of liars, seeking shelter from the storm, and finds what was left for them.*

*Their name is Reyes.*

The fire popped. Somewhere behind Sebastian, a mule shifted and blew air through its lips. The wind screamed once against the arch overhead and moved on.

Sebastian sat very still.

It was a trick. Of course it was a trick. Some clever dead smuggler had written a lure into a ledger, bait for superstitious couriers, and the knot was coincidence because company knots were common as dirt, and the sigil was coincidence because the sigils were everywhere in these mountains, and the birthmark—

The birthmark was the thing that wouldn't fold away neatly. Twenty-six years of not thinking about it, and now it sat on their wrist under their sleeve like a coal that had never quite cooled, and the book lay open in their lap saying *mark the year, mark the day*, and outside the wind was doing exactly what the book said the wind would be doing on exactly the night it said.

*Seeking shelter from the storm.* They had taken this route because of the storm. Because the main pass was closed and this was the only road they trusted, and they had stopped here because there was no other shelter for six miles, and every single one of those decisions had felt like their own.

That was the part that made the dread go cold and specific, down in the marrow. Not the prophecy itself—prophecies were cheap, peddled in every market south of the border, good for separating fools from coin. It was the accuracy. Whoever wrote this had known the shape of this night the way Sebastian knew the shape of a pass: not the details, but the truth of it. And the only person alive who knew Sebastian's name was—

No. Plenty of people knew their name. But nobody knew it mattered. Sebastian had spent a lifetime making sure of that. No family records, no birth registry, no debts under their own name, no history anywhere except the company rolls, and those listed them as *Reyes, S., guide, no origin given.* They had built their invisibility brick by brick, and the book in their hands walked straight through the wall as if the wall had never existed.

*The heir.* Heir to what? There was nothing north of the free cities but mountains and the ghost of some old kingdom that even the oldest maps couldn't be bothered to name. Sebastian had asked once, years ago, an ancient toll-keeper at the salt road's end, what country these passes had belonged to. The old man had frowned like Sebastian had asked him to remember a dream.

"There was never a country here," he'd said. "Just the passes."

But somebody had built shrines. Somebody had cut cellars into mountainsides and carved the same sigil over every door. Countries that never existed didn't leave ruins.

"Reyes!" Doss's voice, from across the vault. "You finding anything useful back there, or just playing scholar?"

Sebastian closed the book. Their hands were steady—that was training, that was twenty years of mercenary discipline—but their pulse was hammering somewhere up in their throat. "Old cache records," they said. "Nothing."

They tucked the book inside their coat, against their ribs, where a knife would normally ride. It was warm there, absurdly warm, as if the thing had a body heat of its own.

Sleep didn't come. Sebastian sat with their back to the stone and watched the fire burn down and listened to the hired blades snore and felt the book breathing against them like a second heartbeat. Around midnight, unable to stand it, they opened it again by ember-light, working backward from the date page, looking for anything that would break the spell—some detail wrong, some proof of fraud.

Instead they found the marginalia. A different hand, later, sharper: notes crammed between lines and up the margins in tiny, furious script. A scholar's hand. Someone had studied this book, annotated it, argued with it. Beside the entry naming *Reyes*, the marginalist had written:

*Verified against parish fragments. The sigil-line survived the erasure. If the child lives, the Door can be found. If the Door opens, the cities live. Find them before the Inquisition does. — I.V.*

*I.V.* Sebastian stared at the initials until the embers dimmed. Someone else was looking for them. Had been looking—how long? The ink wasn't fresh, but it wasn't ancient either. Ten years old, maybe less. Someone out there had read this same page, read the name *Reyes*, and started walking.

And below that note, almost hidden in the gutter of the binding, one more line, in the original prophet's hand, as if the dead writer had leaned forward across a century to add a postscript:

*She will find you first. Trust her, though she has ash on her hands.*

Sebastian shut the book.

Outside, the wind had changed. It came down the pass now from the north, carrying with it a smell that didn't belong to snow or stone—woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke something acrid, chemical, like pitch and hot iron. Torch-smell. Army-smell. Sebastian had smelled it enough times in enough wars to know it in their sleep.

They rose quietly, woke Doss with a hand over his mouth, and put a finger to their lips. His eyes went wide, then flat and professional. He'd been a smuggler forty years; he knew what that smell meant too.

"Break camp," Sebastian breathed. "No lights. We move in ten minutes."

As the cellar erupted into silent, urgent motion around them, Sebastian stood apart, one hand pressed flat against the book through their coat, the other curled over the sigil on their wrist. It was warm. Warmer than it had ever been. Warm as if something on the far side of a hundred silent years had finally, finally exhaled.

Down the pass, faint on the wind, came the sound of hooves. Many of them. Moving fast.

And moving, Sebastian realized with a dread that settled over them like the snow itself—not along the main road, but along theirs. Along the smuggler's road. The road that wasn't on any map.

Whoever was coming knew the passes too. And they were coming for the book—or for the name inside it.

Or for them.
