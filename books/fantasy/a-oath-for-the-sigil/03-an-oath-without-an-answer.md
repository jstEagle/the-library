## 3. An Oath Without an Answer

The waystone stood where the smugglers' maps said it would, though the maps were forty years old and the mountain had opinions of its own about permanence. It rose from a shelf of granite above the tree line like a broken tooth—black stone veined with something that might have been silver once, before weather and neglect had eaten the shine away. No road passed it now. No pilgrims climbed to it. The old kings of Thornfell had raised stones like this one along every pass in their kingdom, or so the oldest songs claimed, and when the kingdom died the stones had simply stayed, patient as bones.

Sebastian Reyes knelt before it because they always knelt before it, on the two nights a year the caravan route brought them through the high passes, and because kneeling was the last honest thing they did.

The wind came off the glacier in long, flat shoves, pressing against their back as if the mountain itself wanted them gone. Below, the campfires of the caravan glimmered in the fold of the valley like embers raked from a hearth—twelve fires, thirty-one souls, and a cargo of salted fish and uncut garnets that three separate people would kill for before spring. None of them knew why their guide stopped here. They thought it was superstition, a mercenary's tic, the kind of ritual soldiers invent to keep the dark at arm's length.

They weren't wrong. But they didn't know what the ritual had cost.

Sebastian pulled off the left glove. The sigil sat on the inside of the wrist, just below the pulse—a mark like a closed eye crossed by a single vertical line, inked in silver-grey that no amount of scrubbing or scarring had ever faded. The oath-sigil of Vessia. Every mercenary who swore to the Silent God wore one, and every one of them could tell you the same thing: it burned when the god was near. It burned in battle, when death stood close enough to breathe on you. It burned in the presence of true oaths and true betrayals alike. For eleven years it had been Sebastian's compass, their conscience, the small hot voice at the edge of their skin that never needed words.

It still burned now. That was the problem.

*Vessia.* Sebastian pressed two fingers to the stone, cold enough to bite. *I'm here again. Same stone. Same hour. You know how I keep accounts.*

Nothing answered. Nothing had answered for six years.

They said it anyway, the way they said it every time, because an oath wasn't a transaction and a prayer wasn't a letter requiring reply. *I kept the route clean. Lost no one at the Narrows, though Kestrel wanted to try the ice bridge and I told him no and he sulked about it for two days. The child with the fever lived. I took nothing that wasn't paid for.* A pause. The wind filled it. *You're not listening. You haven't listened since Marrow Ford.*

Marrow Ford. Even the name of the place had a taste—copper and river-mud. Eleven years sworn to Vessia, and it had taken one afternoon to teach Sebastian that silence was not the same as peace. Forty-one of theirs had gone into the water under crossbow fire while Sebastian screamed the god's name until their throat tore, and the sigil on their wrist had burned and burned and burned, bright as a coal, as if Vessia were *attending*, as if the god were watching every drowning with perfect attention and perfect refusal to intervene.

That was the day Sebastian had understood something the priests never said aloud: the silence wasn't absence. The silence was *answer*. Vessia heard everything and spoke nothing, and a person could build a life inside that silence the way a snail builds a shell—slowly, spiraling inward, until the shell was all there was.

Sebastian had built well. Invisible name, invisible past, a trade that rewarded exactly that. A face forgettable enough that innkeepers asked twice if they'd met. A reputation built on competence rather than character, because character invited questions. And underneath it all, the sigil, burning faithfully, faithfully, faithfully—the bond alive even if the god wasn't, or wouldn't speak, or had never existed at all except as the collective wish of ten thousand mercenaries who needed to believe someone was counting their deaths.

*I don't believe in you anymore,* Sebastian told the stone. It was the first time they'd said it plainly, even alone, even here. The words came out smaller than expected. *I think maybe I never did. I think I believed in the burning. I think that's different.*

The sigil flared.

Not gently. Not the familiar banked warmth, but a sudden white heat that made Sebastian hiss through their teeth and snatch their hand back from the stone, cradling the wrist against their chest like a wounded bird. The mark blazed through the skin, through the muscle, a line of light in the shape of a closed eye—and for half a heartbeat, half a breath, Sebastian saw something behind their own eyes that was not their own memory: a door, tall as a cliff face, standing open in a wall of black glass, and beyond it a country wearing autumn like a crown.

Then it was gone. The cold rushed back in. The wind resumed its argument with the ridge line. The sigil dimmed to its usual ember-glow, and Sebastian knelt in the snow shaking, heart slamming, feeling foolish and seen and terrified in equal measure.

*What was that?* they demanded of the stone, of the sky, of whatever listened. *Six years of nothing and now—what, a glimpse? A tease? What do you want from me?*

The stone said nothing. Stones were good at that. But the burning stayed, low and insistent, like a hand closed around their wrist—not painful now, but *present*, the way a grip is present, the way a claim is present. And beneath it, fainter than the wind but unmistakable, came something Sebastian had never felt from the sigil in eleven years of service:

Urgency. Direction. The pull of a compass needle swinging toward the mountains' heart.

"Damn you," Sebastian whispered, and meant it, and knelt a moment longer anyway, because breaking a habit of eleven years took more courage than they had tonight.

* * *

They smelled the smoke before they saw the fire—which was wrong, deeply wrong, because there should have been no fire on this slope. The caravan was a mile below and east, tucked into the lee of the ridge. This light came from higher up, from the saddle between two shoulders of rock where nothing human had any business being after dark, and it moved. It flickered and shifted and threw shadows that walked.

Sebastian went up toward it with their sword drawn and their hood back, because in the high passes you showed your face or you died for the hiding of it. The snow squeaked under their boots. The cold had teeth tonight, the kind of cold that got into the joints of old wounds, and by the time they crested the saddle their breath hung around them in a solid cloud.

A figure sat hunched against the leeward side of a boulder beside a fire so small it was less warmth than apology. Thin. Hooded. One gloved hand stretched toward the flames, fingers curled like frost-killed ferns. At the scrape of Sebastian's boot on stone, the figure jerked upright, swayed, and put its back to the boulder with the desperate economy of someone who had spent their last strength getting here and had none left to run.

"Don't," the figure said. A woman's voice, cracked down to the wire. "Please. I've been walking since—" She stopped. Started again, with the rigid precision of someone reciting something rehearsed on a long, terrible road. "Callowmere. Three days ago. I'm looking for a mercenary named Sebastian Reyes."

The world went very quiet and very large.

Sebastian did not move. Did not lower the sword. Somewhere below, a wolf tested its voice against the wind and got no answer. "Nobody looks for me," they said. "People lose me. It's a professional courtesy."

"I know." The woman laughed, or tried to; it came out as a cough. She pushed her hood back, and the firelight found a face sharp-boned and grey with exhaustion, lips split from cold, eyes so dark and so awake that they seemed lit from behind. Frost had gotten into her hairline, little crystals melting as the fire breathed on them. "Believe me, I know. Finding you took everything I had. Which is why I'd rather you didn't kill me at the finish line. It would feel wasteful."

"Who sent you?"

"No one." She swallowed. Her gaze dropped—to the sword, then up again, steady. "No one sent me. That's rather the point. I came because of a book."

Sebastian's wrist chose that moment to burn.

Not the flare from the waystone—something else, stranger: a slow, rhythmic pulse, like a second heartbeat layered under their own, quickening as she spoke. Sebastian looked down at it involuntarily, and the woman saw them look, and her breath caught audibly in the frozen air.

"The sigil," she said. "Vessia's oath-mark. You're sworn." Her voice changed, dropped its brittle armor, went soft with something that looked almost like awe. "Oh. Oh, it's really you. I half thought—I told myself I was chasing a ghost, a coincidence of names, a hundred-year-old book playing games—but your *name*, it's in the text, the date is *tonight*, and you're real, and you're *here*—"

"Stop." The word came out harder than Sebastian intended, edged with something close to panic. "Back up. What book? What date?"

She hesitated. And in the hesitation Sebastian read her the way they read everyone—years of judging whether the man across the table would reach for steel or coin—and saw fear there, yes, deep and worn smooth like a river stone, but not fear *of them*. Fear of something behind them. Something following.

"You're pursued," Sebastian said.

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"Inquisitors." The word fell between them like a stone into a well. "Malvane's inquisitors. Two of them reached Callowmere four days ago asking after anyone who'd requested restricted texts from the archive. I made such a request. I was careless. I used my own seal." Her mouth twisted. "Arrogance. I'd spent nine years being careful and I got excited about a marginal note and I signed my name like a fool."

"What's your name?"

Another hesitation. Then, quietly, as if handing over the last thing she owned: "Isolde Verane. I'm—an archivist. Was. Callowmere will have struck me from the rolls by now. Excommunication from the archive is a small death, but it's thorough."

Sebastian looked at her a long moment. Half-frozen, three days hunted, sitting beside a fire the size of a cupped hand on a mountainside where no road ran—and she had come anyway, alone, because of words in a book. There was a stubbornness in that which Sebastian recognized the way one recognizes one's own reflection in unexpected places. It was the same stubbornness that made them kneel at dead shrines to a silent god.

And the sigil was still pulsing. Faster now. Toward her.

That frightened Sebastian more than the inquisitors did.

"Get your things," they said. "Fire's out. We move in one minute."

Isolde blinked. "You're—you're taking me with you?"

"I'm taking you downhill to the caravan, where there are walls and heat and thirty-one armed people, and tomorrow I decide what to do with you." Sebastian sheathed the sword and crouched to smother the fire with snow, working fast, methodical, grateful for a task that required nothing but hands. "Understand me, archivist. I'm not your ally. I don't know what you are yet. But nobody freezes to death on my mountain while I watch. That's not mercy. It's accounting."

"It sounded like mercy," Isolde said, struggling upright. Her legs nearly failed her; Sebastian caught her elbow without thinking, and through the contact the sigil flared so bright and so sudden that they both froze—her staring at the glowing mark through the glove's leather, him staring at her staring.

"It burns," she whispered. "When you touch me, it—it burns."

"It does that," Sebastian said, and let go of her arm as if it had bitten them, and turned away to kick snow over the last coals, and hated how much the turning-away felt like flight.

* * *

The descent took two hours that should have taken one, because Isolde's body was failing faster than her will. Twice Sebastian carried her over bad ground, her arm across their shoulders, her weight slight and shivering and utterly trusting in a way that set their teeth on edge. Trust was a currency Sebastian had learned never to accept, because it always arrived with an invoice attached.

They talked, because the dark demanded it and because silence between strangers on a mountain was its own kind of confession.

"Why a mercenary?" Isolde asked at one point, teeth chattering. "In the book. Why you?"

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Sebastian Reyes. Your name. In a text copied a century before you were born." She said it simply, factually, the way she might report a catalog number, and somehow that was worse than drama. "Along with today's date. And a place called the Door of Ash. And a kingdom I couldn't find in any atlas until I realized it wasn't missing from the atlases. It had been *removed*. Deliberately. Every map, every chronicle, every tax roll—struck through, scraped away, rewritten. Do you understand what kind of power it takes to erase a country? Not defeat it. *Erase* it. From paper. From memory. From—"

"Stop talking." Sebastian's voice cracked on it.

Isolde stopped talking. The snow squeaked. Somewhere far below, the caravan fires had grown to a scatter of orange stars in the black fold of the valley.

"I grew up on those roads," Sebastian said finally, to the dark, because the dark didn't answer and neither did anything else. "The passes. My mother sold passage through them. Smugglers' routes, shrine-caves, springs that didn't freeze. She taught me every stone between here and the coast." A breath. "There's a valley two days north of here that isn't on any map either. Not erased. Just never written. The people there don't pay taxes to anyone and no one remembers collecting from their grandmothers. I used to think it was luck."

"And now?"

"Now I think my mother was running from something older than tax collectors." They adjusted her weight on their shoulder. "Now I think the whole country is full of holes shaped like a kingdom."

Isolde was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again her voice had lost its archivist's precision and gained something rawer. "I need to show you the book. Tonight, if we can. Tomorrow may be—" She faltered. "There were riders behind me at the last ford. Two days back. If they've kept my pace, they're a day behind me. Maybe less. They don't tire the way I do. They have remounts and I had a mule that died."

"A day." Sebastian did the arithmetic without wanting to. A day's hard ride from the ford. The passes slowed horses; that was the whole point of the passes. But Halloway's inquisitors—if it was Halloway's, and who else sent pairs into the high country in winter—Halloway's people knew these mountains too. Everyone who mattered knew these mountains. That had always been the trouble with secrets: they were only ever rented.

The caravan's picket lines came into view, lanterns swaying, the smell of woodsmoke and horse and salted fish. Warmth ahead, walls, thirty-one sets of eyes. Safety of a sort—the temporary, rented kind.

Sebastian halted at the edge of the lantern-light, Isolde leaning against them, and looked down at the sigil. It had settled into a steady glow, no longer urgent, no longer directional. Satisfied. Like a dog that had delivered its master somewhere and was now waiting to be told why.

*You wanted me to find her,* Sebastian thought at the silent god, at the stone, at whatever remained. *Fine. She's found. Now what do you want me to do with her?*

The sigil pulsed once. Just once. And for the first time in six years, Sebastian could have sworn the burning formed a shape—not a word, Vessia didn't use words, but something older than words. The shape of a door, opening.

"Reyes!" Kestrel's voice rang out from the picket line, cheerful and oblivious. "You're back late! Who's your friend?"

Sebastian opened their mouth to give the easy lie—the lost traveler, the rescued pilgrim, any of the dozen fictions that would slide this night back into the comfortable shape of their invisible life—and found, to their genuine surprise, that they couldn't remember which lie they'd chosen.

"They're with me," they heard themselves say instead. "Give them the tent by the far fire. And Kestrel—double the watch on the south trail tonight. Riders may be coming."

Kestrel's grin faded into a soldier's frown. "Trouble?"

"Maybe." Sebastian guided Isolde past the fires, past the curious stares, toward the small canvas shelter at the camp's edge, and found that their heart was pounding with something that had no business living in the chest of a man who trusted nothing. "Ask me tomorrow."

Behind them, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, the waystone stood alone in the starlight, and if it answered anyone that night, no one was there to kneel and hear it.
