## 5. The Company Remembers Differently

The Greywater Company wintered at Hollowmere, an old drover's fort squatting in the fold of the hills where the salt road bent south toward the lowlands. Sebastian had known it since before they could hold a sword properly—had learned to walk its walls in the dark, to find the loose stone behind the third buttress where children of the company hid sweets and stolen knives, to read the smoke from the cookfires the way other people read weather. Hollowmere was the closest thing to a home a foundling mercenary was permitted to have, and Sebastian had spent three days walking toward it with a book of impossible prophecies wrapped in oilcloth against their spine, telling themselves that whatever else was true, Orla would know what to do.

Orla always knew what to do. That was the whole architecture of Sebastian's childhood: the world was chaos, and Captain Orla Venn was the fixed point inside it, the woman who had pulled a nameless child out of a burning border village twenty-odd years ago and said, *You're mine now*, as if that settled everything. It had settled everything for a long time.

Sebastian came down off the ridge an hour past dusk, when the fort's torches were lit and the watch would be changing. They did not take the road. They took the smuggler's path instead, the one that threaded through the gorse and came up behind the old byre, because the book against their back felt heavier with every mile and they wanted to see the place plainly before it saw them. A habit of invisibility, drilled into them since childhood: never arrive the way people expect, never be where your name says you'll be.

From the byre wall they could see the main yard, and what they saw stopped them like a hand around the throat.

There were riders in the yard who were not company. Six horses, heavy-boned, shod wide, standing with the weary patience of animals that had covered hard miles and been well cared for anyway. Their riders wore grey cloaks without livery, but Sebastian knew the cut of those cloaks the way a deer knows the shape of a snare. Malvane's inquisitors traveled plain on purpose; ostentation was for soldiers, and inquisitors liked to remind everyone that they were not soldiers, that what they carried was authority rather than steel. One of them stood near the well with his hood back, a lean man turning something small and bright over in his fingers while he talked.

And across the yard from him, arms folded, weight settled easy on her heels, stood Captain Orla Venn.

She was not under guard. She was not being threatened. She was talking the way she talked when haggling a contract—head tilted, one eyebrow up, the posture of a woman holding all the leverage she needed. The lantern light caught the grey coming in at her temples, the scar through her left brow that Sebastian had once asked about and been told, *Ask me again when you've earned it.* Twenty years of loyalty sat in Sebastian's chest like swallowed lead, watching her negotiate.

They should have turned around then. Some cold animal part of them said so, said it clearly: *you have seen enough, go now, go north, go anywhere.* But there is a particular blindness in the betrayed, a refusal that looks exactly like courage from the inside. Sebastian stayed crouched behind the byre wall and listened, and hated themselves for every word they needed to hear.

The wind was wrong—it blew from the yard toward the byre, carrying voices in fragments—and Sebastian eased along the wall until they found the gap beneath the byre door where, as a child, they had pressed an ear to listen to the adults argue about contracts and casualties. The floorboards above their cheekbone were the same ones they'd hidden under during the Kettering raid, aged nine, while the company fought off a rival banner in the yard above. They had learned early that homes had floors, and floors had gaps, and gaps were where the truth lived.

"—the charter," Orla was saying. "Signed under the regent's seal, countersigned by the Office of Records. Not a pardon. A charter."

"A charter," the lean man agreed. His voice was dry and pleasant, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "Legitimacy, Captain. Your company stops being a band of sell-swords tolerated between wars and becomes the Protectorate Free Company of the Marches, sworn retainers of the Crown of Malvane, with winter pay guaranteed and land rights in the valley you've been squatting in for a decade. Your dead get pensions. Your orphans get schooling." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Your foundlings stop being foundlings. They become citizens."

Sebastian's hands had gone numb on the stones. Not from cold.

"And the heir," Orla said.

*The heir.* The words went through Sebastian like a spear through wet canvas.

"The heir is a formality," the inquisitor said. "A coronation requires a body. The prophecy requires a bloodline. We are not barbarians, Captain—we do not intend to put this person's head on a spike. We intend to put a crown on it. There is a throne in the old kingdom that has stood empty for a hundred years, and empty thrones breed rebellion. Fill it, and the rebellion dies of embarrassment. All we ask is that when your wayward child comes home—and they will come home, Captain, they always come home—you send word to us first. Before they reach your gate. Before they speak to anyone else."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the charter dies in committee, and next spring the Crown solves its banditry problem in the Marches the traditional way." The lean man's voice did not rise at all. "You have raised killers for thirty years, Captain Venn. You know precisely how the traditional way goes."

Silence, except for the horses shifting and the wind worrying the torch flames. Sebastian pressed their forehead against the floorboard gap and breathed through their mouth, slow and even, the way Orla had taught them to breathe when a wound was bad and panic would kill faster than blood loss. *Pain is information,* she used to say. *Fear is information. Use both or be used by them.*

So: information. Orla had sold them—not their death, which might almost have been cleaner, but their *crowning*. Their face on coins, their name on treaties written by Malvane's clerks, their body propped on a stolen throne as proof that conquest was really just inheritance arriving late. And she had done it for land rights and winter pay. For legitimacy. For the company.

For the family.

That was the part that made bile climb Sebastian's throat—the understanding, arriving whole and merciless, that Orla had probably not thought of it as betrayal at all. She would have weighed it the way she weighed everything: thirty lives against one, a future for the many purchased with the freedom of the one, and the one in question was a foundling who owed her everything and had already, by deserting after the Vessia shrine business, proven themselves unreliable. Sebastian could construct the entire ledger without effort because they had been raised inside that ledger. Every kindness in the company had come with a column of costs beside it. They had simply never believed they'd end up in the debit line.

Above them, boots crossed the boards. Orla's voice, lower now, addressed to someone else—her sergeant, Bray, by the timbre. "Double the watch on the south road. If my child comes home, I want them brought to me alive and unspooked. Nobody touches them. Nobody *talks* to them. And nobody—" a beat, and something in her voice cracked, just slightly, some hairline fracture running through the captain's certainty, "—nobody tells them anything about tonight. Understood?"

*Aye, Captain,* said Bray, and moved away.

Sebastian lay under the byre floor in the dark and understood two things at once, each canceling the other. Orla still loved them—that crack in her voice was real, older and truer than any contract. And Orla had sold them anyway, and would hand them to the grey cloaks with her own hands, gently, believing to the last that a crown was a gift. Love and treachery had always shared a roof in the Marches. Sebastian had just never had to stand in the doorway between them before.

They began to move. Slowly, the way they'd been taught to move away from a sleeping camp—weight rolled heel to toe, breath timed to the wind, no silhouette against the torchlight. Back along the byre wall, over the fence into the gorse, down toward the streambed that ran below the fort's southern wall. From there they could circle east and pick up the smuggler's track north before the double watch Orla had just ordered ever settled into its rhythm.

They were forty yards from the stream, deep in the gorse, when the dog barked.

Not an alarm—a greeting. Nettle, the brindled lurcher that had slept across Sebastian's feet for six years, came bounding through the dark with her whole ridiculous heart in her throat, tail going, and Sebastian's whispered *down, girl, down* came half a heartbeat too late. Because Nettle's bark had been joy, but the watch heard barking, and the new watch was doubled, and somewhere behind the fort walls a horn sounded—one short note, uncertain, the sound of men not yet sure what they'd seen.

Sebastian ran.

They ran the way the company had trained them to run, choosing ground before speed, putting the streambed between themselves and the gate, using the dark folds of land the way a fish uses current. Behind them torches bloomed along the south wall, and a voice—Bray's voice, cracked and unwilling—shouted the challenge: *"Halt! In the Crown's name!"*

*In the Crown's name.* Bray had served the company for fifteen years and had never once spoken those words. The charter wasn't signed yet, but the words were already in their mouths. That was how legitimacy worked, Sebastian thought wildly, splashing up the streambed. It didn't need ink. It only needed people to start saying it.

An arrow hissed past their left ear and buried itself in a willow root with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Then another, wider. The company's archers were good—too good to miss like that unless they were missing on purpose, shooting wide by inches, and Sebastian understood with a fresh small grief that some of the people hunting them across their own home ground were trying not to hit them. It didn't help. Others wouldn't miss. And Orla's orders were clear: alive, unspooked, delivered.

They came out of the streambed where it bent beneath the old drover's bridge and went up the bank into the black stand of pines, and here Sebastian did the thing that hurt most, the thing they would carry afterward like a stone in the boot: they used the company's own secrets against it. The deadfall trap on the shepherd's path, reset two days ago against wolves—they led the first pursuers across it and heard the crash and cursing behind them. The blind gully behind the pine stand where the ground gave way to scree—they took it at a slide, trusting legs trained on this exact hillside, and heard a pursuer cry out below them. Every trick of ground, every hidden path, every lesson Orla had ever paid for in blood: spent now, all of it, buying distance from the woman who taught them.

At the top of the ridge they stopped, lungs raw, and looked back.

Hollowmere burned orange under its torches, small and stubborn in the cup of the hills, the place where a nameless child had been fed and trained and loved in the only language the company spoke. Torches were spilling out the south gate in a straggling line, dogs giving tongue, the hunt assembling itself with the grim competence of people who knew this country as well as their quarry did. Better, maybe. They'd had longer to learn it.

And on the wall, alone, one figure stood motionless, watching the ridge. Even at this distance Sebastian knew the set of those shoulders, the way Orla stood when she was counting costs. She didn't shout. She didn't signal the archers. She just stood there, and though the night was too dark and too far for faces, Sebastian would swear until the day they died that she was looking directly at them, and that one hand rose—not waving, not cursing. Something smaller than either. Something that might have been an apology, or a salute, or a mother's open palm pressed flat against the air between them.

Sebastian turned north and did not look again.

The book rode against their spine like a second heart. Somewhere ahead, in the high passes, a silent god waited under a sigil that still burned on their wrist; somewhere behind, the family that raised them hunted them with arrows they were refusing to loose straight. And somewhere beyond both, in a city of archives and lamplight, a stranger named Isolde Verane had decoded a century-old text and run into the mountains looking for them—for reasons Sebastian couldn't yet guess, carrying knowledge Sebastian didn't yet want.

The hunt crashed through the pines below. Sebastian ran.

* * *

By the third hour past midnight the pursuit had thinned to nothing—or to the appearance of nothing, which in company country amounted to the same lie. Orla didn't waste hunters; she positioned them. Sebastian chose the worst possible ground on purpose, a flooded stretch of alder carr where horses floundered and dogs lost scent in the black water, and waded it to the chest, book held overhead, teeth chattering, and came out the far side into rocks and heather and the long lonely climb toward the high passes.

Near dawn they stopped in the lee of a boulder, wrung out their sleeves, and allowed themselves, for exactly sixty seconds, to feel it. Not the fear—that could wait, fear was fuel and would keep. The other thing. The image of Orla's hand rising against the torchlight. The crack in her voice: *nobody tells them anything about tonight.* She had meant it as mercy. She had meant to spare them the sight of her selling them, right up until the moment she collected them like a debt. That was the cruelest economy of all: betrayal arranged tenderly, treason with a mother's hands.

"You'd have taken the crown," Sebastian said aloud to the dark, to her, to the memory. "You honestly think you'd have been saving me."

The wind answered, as the god had stopped answering years ago, and the sigil on their wrist pulsed once—slow, warm, patient—like something under the skin turning over in its sleep.

North, then. Into the passes. Toward the waystone, toward whatever waited there, toward the frozen stranger the prophecies insisted was already coming to find them.

Behind, faint on the wind, a single horn sounded from Hollowmere. Not pursuit. Something longer, lower—a call carrying, a message passed from hill to hill, company to company, down every road Sebastian had ever been taught to trust.

Orla was telling the world her child was coming.

Sebastian pulled the hood up and walked into the mountains.
