## 6. Salt Road, Burning Bridge

The salt road ran white beneath the moon, a scar of crushed stone winding along the gorge where the river Vell ran loud and black. Sebastian had walked it twice before in other years, guiding caravans of salt and smuggled silk through the narrow places where the mountains pinched the world down to a single track. It was a road that remembered feet. Tonight it remembered theirs.

They had not stopped since the company's camp. Since Orla's face. Since the arrows.

Sebastian drove them hard through the dark, choosing the path by instinct older than thought—the left fork where the rock overhung, the scree slope that looked impassable but held if you stepped where the frost heaved it apart. Isolde followed without complaint, her scholar's boots splitting at the seams, her breath coming in ragged white plumes. Twice she stumbled. Twice she caught herself on his arm and said nothing, and the silence between them was its own kind of speech.

Behind them, far below and far back, torchlight moved along the valley floor like a slow procession of fireflies. Too many to be hunters. Just enough to be an army deciding where to look.

"They'll send riders up the salt road by morning," Sebastian said. "Halloway doesn't waste men on roads that go nowhere. He wastes them on roads someone might take."

"You know him?"

"I know his kind. I've been paid by worse." Sebastian glanced back at her, at the smudge of ash still on her cheek from the archive fire they'd fled two nights past. "There's a bridge ahead. Old one. Salt traders built it before my grandmother was born."

"And?"

"And bridges are only useful if you're the last one across."

Isolde understood a heartbeat later. He watched it land in her face—that particular archivist's grief, the flinching away from destruction of anything old. "It's a hundred years—"

"A hundred and forty. Ironwood pylons, chain core." He kept walking. "I helped burn a granary once for a man who owed me nothing. This I can do for free."

* * *

The bridge spanned the gorge at its narrowest throat, where the Vell dropped thirty feet into a churning trough of white water. In daylight it would have been beautiful—a single arc of weathered timber and hand-forged chain, sagging like an old animal's back. In moonlight it was only a shape, black against blacker air.

Sebastian tested the first plank with their boot, then crossed to the far anchor point, where the great chains were wound around a stone bollard carved with salt-trader sigils worn nearly smooth. They knelt beside it and began unpacking what remained of their kit: the flask of lamp-oil, the pitch they'd taken from a waystation, the striker.

Isolde stood at the near end, arms wrapped around herself. "You're sure there's no other crossing?"

"None within three days. The gorge walls close up past here—sheer on both sides. An army can't climb what we can't even see a path up." Sebastian worked the oil into the timbers around the anchor, methodical, thorough. A mercenary's craft. They'd set fires before, but never one that felt like closing a door behind themselves. "It buys us until midday. Maybe longer if the rain holds off."

"Midday." She said it flatly, tasting the word. "And then?"

"Then we're in country I know and they don't. That's worth more than any bridge." They struck the striker. Sparks caught the oil-soaked wood; flame licked up the pylon with a soft rushing sound, almost gentle. "Come on. We should be a mile gone before it drops."

They didn't wait to watch it fall. But the sound followed them anyway—a groan of parting iron, a crash like the mountain clearing its throat, and then the river swallowing the wreckage whole. Isolde flinched. Sebastian did not. Some part of them had already learned, long ago, how to walk away from burning things without looking back.

* * *

They made camp in the hollow beneath a fallen slab of granite, a lean-to of pine boughs and canvas that would hide their fire from anything above or behind. The fire itself was small—barely larger than two cupped hands—and Sebastian fed it with dry larch bark that burned clean and nearly smokeless.

Isolde sat with her knees drawn up, hands extended toward the heat. She'd pulled her boots off, and Sebastian saw the blisters had broken, the skin raw and shining. Without a word they took the small tin of goose-fat from their pack and set it beside her.

"Thank you," she said.

"Company habit. Feet are the first thing that kills you in the mountains. Not wolves, not cold. Feet."

She worked the fat into the worst of it, wincing. The firelight moved on her face, and Sebastian found themself studying it the way they'd study terrain—noticing things. The ink stain on the side of her right hand, permanent as a birthmark. The fine lines at the corners of her eyes that said she'd spent years squinting at small script by bad light. The way she sat slightly hunched, shoulders curled inward, like someone who'd learned early that taking up space invited attention.

"You were going to ask me something," she said, not looking up. "Back at the shrine. You started to, and then the dogs started barking, and you never finished."

Sebastian turned a stick in the flames. "Was I?"

"You were. You had the face people get when they're working up to a question they don't want answered."

A long moment. The river murmured far below, patient as time.

"Why excommunication?" Sebastian asked finally. "The archive in Callowmere is a guild institution. Guilds don't cast people out—they fine them, they demote them, they ruin them politely. Excommunication means you did something the guild couldn't forgive. Something that shamed them."

Isolde's hands went still on her blistered foot. For a moment Sebastian thought she wouldn't answer. Then she set her foot down, drew her cloak around her knees, and looked into the fire with the expression of someone reading a page she'd memorized long ago and hated.

"I requested a text I wasn't authorized to request," she said. "The Thornfell codex. Restricted collection, sealed by order of the Archon's council—no one had been permitted to open it in sixty years. I told the registrar it was for a comparative study of borderland liturgies. He believed me, mostly because no one believes an archivist would lie about something so boring." A thin smile, there and gone. "But requesting it put my name on a ledger. And ledgers get read."

"By Halloway's people."

"By his people, yes. Or by whoever feeds them. I never learned which." She shrugged, a small bitter motion. "Three days later I was summoned before the guild tribunal. They asked me who I was working for. I said no one. They asked why a junior archivist with no patron and no standing wanted a sealed codex on a kingdom that officially never existed. I said scholarly interest. They didn't believe me either." Her voice flattened. "So they gave me a choice. Name my accomplice, or be struck from the rolls. Struck from the rolls means no guild will hire me, no city will license me, no library will open its doors. In Callowmere, it's a soft way of erasing someone. They unmake your name the same way Malvane unmade Thornfell's."

Sebastian heard the echo in it and knew she heard it too. "And you chose erasure."

"I chose not to give them a name that wasn't theirs to have." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were steady and very tired. "There wasn't an accomplice, Sebastian. There was just me, and a question I couldn't stop asking. My whole life I was taught that records are sacred—that the archive is memory, and memory is the only thing standing between people and the dark. And then I found out the most powerful archive in the free cities had helped carve a kingdom out of history. Helped make the dark." She laughed softly, without humor. "So now I'm the heretic. The woman who asked too many questions about a country that isn't supposed to exist. Somewhere in Callowmere there's a ledger with my name crossed out in red ink, and somewhere in Malvane's capital there's another one with my name written in, and neither of them is the truth of me."

The fire popped. Sebastian fed it another strip of bark, buying time, because the thing rising in their chest was not a comfortable thing and needed to be handled carefully.

"You could have named someone," they said at last. "Anyone. A dead registrar. A rival. No one checks."

"I know."

"It would have saved you all this. The cold, the running, the—" They gestured vaguely at the mountain dark around them. "This."

"Yes." She said it simply, without drama, and somehow that simplicity was worse than any confession of fear. "But then some innocent person spends the rest of their life looking over their shoulder, and I spend mine knowing I did it. I've seen what being erased does to people, Sebastian. I catalogued the effects for six years. I wasn't going to pass it on."

Sebastian looked at her—at this woman who had lost everything a life was built from and sat straight-backed by a fire the size of a prayer—and felt something shift in their chest, some load-bearing wall giving way.

"My turn," they said.

Her eyebrows rose slightly. "Your turn?"

"Fair's fair. You gave me a confession. Here's mine." They kept their eyes on the fire. It was easier than watching her face. "I deserted."

"I know. You told me—"

"No. You know I left the company tonight, with arrows after me. That's not desertion. Desertion happened four years ago." The words came slowly, dragged up from somewhere deep and packed down. "There was a contract. A garrison town called Merrow's Ford, on the eastern edge of the salt trade. The company took coin to hold it against raiders out of the Ashen Steppe. Simple work. Walls, watch rotations, a payroll. We'd done it a dozen times."

Isolde said nothing. She had the stillness of someone who understands that listening is a form of shelter.

"The raiders came in the spring. Bigger than expected—someone had sold them our dispositions. Someone inside." Sebastian's jaw tightened. "We held the walls for nine days. On the tenth, the paymaster rode out under a parley flag and didn't come back. And Captain Orla gathered the officers and told us the contract was void. The town's magistrates had defaulted on payment—so she claimed. Company law is clear: no coin, no obligation. We marched out at dawn."

"But?"

"But I'd spent ten days on those walls. I knew the baker's name. I knew which cellar the children hid in when the arrows came over. I knew the sound the north gate made when it swung." Sebastian's voice stayed level, but barely; it was the levelness of a plank bearing weight. "I deserted that morning. Walked out of the column before first light and went back. Alone. Held the north gate with eleven townsfolk and a stockpile of salted pork for six more days until a relief militia arrived. Eleven people, Isolde. Six days. And when it was over, the militia captain offered me a commission and I refused it, because going back meant becoming the kind of soldier who stays, and I didn't—I didn't believe I was that. So I came back to the company instead, and I swore Orla to secrecy, and I let her think it was a lapse. A fever. Something that passed."

"Did it pass?"

"No." The admission fell out of them like a stone into deep water. "Every contract since, I've counted the civilians first. Every town we've garrisoned, every caravan we've guarded—I map the cellars and the wells and the weak gates before I map the kill zones. Orla noticed. She never said anything, but she noticed. And tonight, when the inquisitors offered her legitimacy—her own charter, her own lands, a company that answers to no crown—she looked at me and I saw that she'd known for four years exactly what I was. Someone whose oath had a crack in it. Someone who'd choose the eleven over the payroll." They shook their head slowly. "She didn't sell me to Halloway despite knowing that. I think she sold me because of it. Because a mercenary company can't afford a heart, and hers has been calcifying for thirty years, and mine kept... bleeding."

The silence that followed was long and full of river-sound. Isolde reached across the space between them and took the stick from Sebastian's fingers—not to take it away, but so that their hands rested together on it, briefly, before she set it down.

"You went back," she said. "For eleven strangers. Knowing it would cost you everything you'd built."

"It cost me nothing. That's the joke. Four more years of contracts and campfires and Orla's table—that cost me nothing. I was already gone. I just hadn't told myself yet." Sebastian finally looked at her, and the firelight made it possible in a way daylight never would have. "Tonight I told myself."

Isolde's hand was still near theirs. Neither of them moved it away.

"The tribunal asked me who I was working for," she said quietly. "I've been asking myself the wrong version of that question ever since. Not who—but what. What am I working for? Because it was never the guild. It was the record. The true one. The one underneath." Her mouth curved, tired and real. "And you—you've been working for eleven people in a cellar for four years. Maybe that's why the prophecy picked us. Not because we're special. Because we were already pointed the right direction and too stubborn to turn around."

"Prophecies don't pick people, Isolde. People pick prophecies. It's how they justify—"

The sigil burned.

It came without warning, a sudden flare of heat along Sebastian's wrist, sharp enough that they hissed and clutched it through their sleeve. Isolde was on her feet instantly, scanning the dark above the hollow—but the night showed nothing. No torches. No dogs. Only stars, hard and cold, and the black teeth of the ridgeline.

"What is it?" she whispered. "What happened?"

"I don't—" Sebastian pushed the sleeve back. The oath-mark glowed faintly, a thread of pale gold beneath the skin, brighter than it had been in years. Brighter than it had been since before the silence. "It hasn't done this since—Vessia hasn't answered since I was nineteen. Sixteen years of nothing. Prayers falling into a well. And now, tonight, after I—" They stopped. Started again. "After I said it out loud. What I did. What I am."

Isolde crouched beside them, her eyes moving from the sigil to their face. "You think it heard you?"

"I think—" Sebastian flexed their fingers, watching the light pulse gently, like breath. Like something waking. "I think I've been praying wrong my whole life. I've been praying for answers. Maybe it doesn't want to answer. Maybe it wants witnesses."

Far off to the south, beyond two ridgelines, a dull orange glow stained the underside of the clouds. Not dawn—dawn was hours away. The color of something burning on the valley floor. The color of a rearguard village, perhaps, or a granary, or a bridge town that had hesitated too long when soldiers asked questions.

Isolde saw it too. Her face went pale in the firelight.

"That's not pursuit," she said. "That's punishment. He's making examples of the road behind us."

Sebastian rose, pulling their cloak tight, and looked north along the dark spine of the mountains—toward the passes, toward the drowned temple, toward whatever waited at the end of a prophecy written a century ago by hands they'd never meet. The sigil pulsed once more against their wrist, warm as a held hand, and went quiet.

"Then we don't follow the road anymore," they said. "Tomorrow we leave it. There's a shepherd's track above the tree line—hard climbing, but no army on earth moves fast at that altitude. Three days to the high passes." They paused, and something in them—something that had spent sixteen years praying into silence—added, quieter: "Whatever's waking up in this mark of mine, I want to know what it wants before Halloway decides for us."

Isolde banked the fire with practiced care, preserving coals for the morning, and when she looked up at him there was ash-smudge and exhaustion and something else—something kindled, fragile and stubborn, that the dark around them had not managed to put out.

"Then we'd better sleep," she said. "Witnesses need their rest."

And high above the hollow, past the stars, the mountains went on waiting—as they always had—for the ones willing to climb.
