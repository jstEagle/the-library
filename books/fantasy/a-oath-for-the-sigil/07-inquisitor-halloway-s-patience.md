## 7. Inquisitor Halloway's Patience

The rain had been falling on Vhargast for three days, and Inquisitor Halloway found it restful.

It was not a city that rewarded attention. It sat at the elbow of the salt road like a drunkard slumped against a wall, all sagging warehouses and customs houses with their shutters gone gray, and the rain turned its cobbles to a brown slick that smelled of brine and horse. Most men of Halloway's rank would have demanded better quarters—some garrison hall, some magistrate's house with a fire worth the name. Halloway had taken instead a rented room above a chandler's shop, where the tallow stink seeped through the floorboards and the window looked out on nothing but the road and the harbor cranes beyond it.

He liked the room because it taught him things. From this window he could watch what entered the city and what left it, and in nine days of watching he had learned that Vhargast's fear moved along the salt road the way water moves through sand: slowly, invisibly, arriving everywhere at once.

He was watching it now. A carter had stopped his wagon in the middle of the street, arguing with a gate sergeant about some tariff, and half a dozen passersby had slowed—not to help, not even to listen, but simply to look toward the gate. Toward the soldiers. That was new, three days ago. Fear of the wrong thing. It was spreading exactly as he wanted it to spread, and no one had ordered him to spread it, which was the part he enjoyed most.

Behind him, in the room's one chair, the smuggler wept quietly into his own hands.

Halloway did not turn around. He let the man weep. Weeping was useful; it softened a man before you spoke to him, the way rain softens ground before you plant something in it. He counted the carters' glances at the gate—one, two, four—and then, when the smuggler's sobbing had worn itself down to hiccups, he said, without turning:

"Your name is Doss. You run salt and lantern oil out of Callowmere for a woman named Ferren who pays you a third less than she claims to. You have a wife in Vhargast you visit twice a year and a daughter in Callowmere you've never told your wife about. Have I missed anything?"

The silence behind him had a texture to it. Halloway catalogued it: the wet breathing, the small animal shift of a bound man trying to find a position that hurt less, and underneath both, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a man doing arithmetic—trying to work out how much of himself was already known, and therefore how little lying could buy him.

"You—" Doss began, and stopped, and started again. "How do you know about the girl?"

"Because knowing is my trade," Halloway said. "As running cargo is yours." He turned from the window at last.

He was aware of how he appeared to frightened men, and he did nothing to correct it. He was a large man gone soft at the edges, with a clerk's ink-stained fingers and a face that seemed assembled for kindness—the heavy jowls, the drooping eyelids, the mouth that naturally settled into something near a smile. He wore no black. He wore good wool the color of oatmeal and carried no instruments of pain, only a leather satchel full of papers, and this, he had long ago learned, terrified people far more thoroughly than any rack ever built. Men understood cruelty. They could brace against cruelty. What they could not brace against was the sense that they had never once, in their whole lives, been unobserved.

"Here is what I know about you, Doss," Halloway said, drawing the chair opposite and sitting down with the care of a man whose knees troubled him. "I know your routes. I know your debts. I know that last spring you carried a sealed letter from an archivist in Callowmere to a bookbinder in the low town, and that the archivist's name was Verane, and that six weeks later you were paid—generously—to carry a second letter back up into the Thornfell passes, addressed to no one, to be left at a waystone shrine."

Doss's face went through several small weathers. "I don't—"

"No," Halloway agreed pleasantly. "You don't. And that is why you are still alive."

He opened the satchel and took out not papers but a flask, poured a measure of plum brandy, and held it to the smuggler's cracked lips himself. This too was technique. Kindness administered by the hand that held your life was more disorienting than any blow.

"Let me tell you a story," Halloway said. "A true one, which is the rarest kind. Once there was a kingdom called Thornfell. You will not have heard of it. That is not an accident; that is policy. A hundred years ago it existed, and then it did not—not destroyed, understand, not burned, though burning came later—but *removed*. Its name struck from treaties, from tax rolls, from maps, from gravestones. Do you know how much labor that takes? Erasing a country? My predecessors spent forty years on it. Forty years of clerks, Doss. Ink and ledgers. The sword gets the glory, but ink does the killing."

The smuggler's eyes had gone glassy. Halloway continued, warm as a hearth.

"Now. Recently, someone in Callowmere found a book that should not exist—a book written *before* the erasure, which mentions Thornfell by name, and mentions an heir, and mentions a date. This month. Perhaps this very week. And the free cities' councils, being merchants, have done what merchants always do with inconvenient truths: they have argued about whether it is profitable to believe it."

He set down the brandy.

"I am not going to catch this heir," he said. "I want you to understand that clearly, because it is the single most important thing you will ever carry anywhere. I do not need to catch him. Catching him ends the story, and ended stories are forgotten. What I need—" he tapped the floorboard with one soft finger, as if knocking politely on a door—"is for ten thousand people in five cities to believe, deeply, daily, that I *might* catch him at any moment. Because a council that believes the usurper's inquisitor is one step behind a legitimate heir cannot agree on anything. Do they shelter him? Do they sell him? Do they deny the prophecy exists? Every faction wants a different answer, and every answer costs money, and merchants will burn their own granaries before they spend money on a question with no safe reply. I don't need armies. I need their councils to fracture, and they will fracture themselves, given only the belief that I am coming."

"And if they just—give him to you?" Doss whispered. "To make it stop?"

"Then that also serves us. But they won't. Not yet. First they'll fight about it, and while they fight, the roads close, the tariffs rise, the garrisons argue over jurisdiction—and Malvane's real army walks south unopposed." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was the worst thing about it. "You see? I win either way. That is what patience is. Patience is being paid in every currency."

* * *

They walked him out at dusk, when the rain had thinned to a gray drifting mist.

Halloway went down to the chandler's yard personally, past the tallow vats and the stacks of wick-cord, where two of his men waited with the smuggler between them. Doss's wrists were unbound now. His face had the peculiar emptiness of a man who has been shown the shape of his own life from outside and found it small.

"Here is what happens next," Halloway said, conversationally, as though discussing freight rates. "You are going to walk out the north gate tonight. No one will stop you; I have arranged that. You are going to go to Callowmere, because it is where your daughter lives and because you have nowhere else, and you are going to tell everyone who asks—I mean *everyone*, dockhands, tavern keepers, anyone who will buy you a drink—that you escaped the inquisitor's cells in Vhargast. That you saw the heir of Thornfell in chains there. That he is broken, or soon will be, and that Halloway has the prophecy book locked in iron, and that the inquisition's riders are already on the salt road."

Doss stared at him. "You want me to lie."

"I want you to tell a story," Halloway corrected gently. "Lies are brittle; they snap under examination. But a story—ah. A story travels. A story puts on weight as it goes, picks up details from every mouth it passes through, until by the time it reaches the council halls of Callowmere it will be wearing armor I never gave it. And here is the beautiful part: no one can disprove it. Because the councils cannot check my cells without admitting they believe the heir exists, and the moment they admit that, they have already fractured. Do you see? The truth is a door that only opens onto more fear. I have built it so that every road leads inward."

He pressed a small purse into the smuggler's hand—coin, real coin—and closed the man's fingers over it.

"For your daughter," he said. "Consider it payment for services rendered. You have served me very well, Doss, from the moment I first read your name in a shipping manifest eleven months ago. You simply didn't know it yet. Very few people ever know it. That is the mercy of my profession: most of the people I use die without ever learning they were used."

The smuggler's throat worked. "And if I don't say it? If I warn them instead? Tell them the truth—that you let me go?"

Halloway sighed, with genuine regret, the regret of a craftsman forced to point out a flaw in an apprentice's joinery.

"Then ask yourself who will believe you. A known smuggler, released by the chief inquisitor, carrying his coin, telling the cities that the terrible Halloway is *not* to be feared?" He shook his head slowly. "They will think you're mine. Some of them will think it even if you do everything I've asked. There is no version of you, Doss, that arrives in Callowmere clean. That is what I took from you in that room—not your freedom. Your credibility. Everything you say from this day forward belongs to me."

For a moment—only a moment—the smuggler's face showed something other than fear: a flicker of hatred so pure it was almost admiration. Then he turned and walked out into the mist, northward, toward the gate, and Halloway watched him go with the mild satisfaction of a man releasing a carrier pigeon.

One of his men, a scarred lieutenant named Brakka who had served him twelve years and still flinched at certain sounds, spoke at his shoulder. "If he talks anyway. If he finds some way—"

"He won't. And if he does, it changes nothing. By the time any warning of his could matter, the councils will have spent weeks tearing each other apart over whether to trust him, and the argument itself will have done my work." Halloway drew his collar up against the damp. "Fear doesn't need to be accurate, Brakka. It needs only to be *plausible*, and plausible is a very low bar. I could stand in the market square tomorrow and announce I had caught the heir, and a third of the city would believe me, and another third would believe I was lying about having caught him, which frightens them nearly as much."

"The heir himself, though." Brakka's voice dropped. "The captain says the company's turned him out. Orla's made her terms. If we move now, the passes—"

"The passes will keep." Halloway began walking back toward the chandler's shop, unhurried, his boots clicking on wet stone. "Do you know what the difference is between a hunter and an inquisitor, Brakka? A hunter must arrive before the quarry. An inquisitor must merely arrive *believably*. Orla thinks she sold me a mercenary. She sold me a legend, and legends appreciate in value the longer they remain at large. Every day that Sebastian Reyes runs free, the free cities grow more afraid of what he might become—and more afraid of what I might do about it—and more divided about which fear to obey."

He paused at the door of the shop and looked back once at the harbor, where the cranes stood motionless in the mist like a row of patient gallows.

"Send word to Orla," he said. "Tell her the price holds, but the timeline shortens. Tell her that if the heir reaches the mountains' heart, her legitimacy dies with him—and that I will remember, always, exactly how eager she was." He smiled again, softly, to no one. "And have the cells in Vhargast prepared after all. Empty ones. Well-lit, well-visible, near the gate. If the councils send observers to verify the smuggler's tale, I want them to see chains, and a table, and instruments, and a room that looks recently vacated. Let them draw their own conclusions. People always draw the conclusions they are most afraid of. It saves me the trouble of suggesting them."

Brakka hesitated. "And if the heir truly is what the book says? If he opens whatever door they're whispering about?"

For the first time all evening, Halloway was quiet. He stood in the doorway with the mist beading on his wool shoulders, and something moved behind his drooping eyelids—not doubt, precisely. Appetite.

"Then Malvane gets his puppet king," he said, "and I get a kingdom that remembers how to be afraid of me. Either way, Brakka, either way—" he stepped inside, into the tallow-smelling dark, "—the story ends with everyone believing exactly what I need them to believe. Now come. There is a council in Callowmere that meets at dawn, and I intend to be the subject of their first argument before their tea goes cold."

Far to the north, past the passes and the drowned temples and the valley where an old woman kept stories her grandmother had told her, a waystone shrine stood empty in the rain, its fire pit cold, its floor swept by wind—recently, and carefully, by hands that knew smuggler's habits.

Halloway did not know that yet.

But he had built his machine so well that it did not matter. The messenger was already walking north with his poisoned story, the councils were already waking to their arguments, and somewhere ahead of them all, patient as ink soaking into paper, the inquisitor's design was closing like a hand around the map of the world.

By dawn, Doss would reach the first waystation. By noon, the story would have its first new details—someone would swear they'd heard the heir was wounded, another that Halloway had burned a village for harboring him. By week's end it would stand in the council hall of Callowmere, fully armored, undeniable.

And Isolde Verane—who had fled those same halls with a stolen book and a guilty heart, who was even now climbing into the frozen passes after a mercenary she had never met—would return to find the doors of the only city that might have helped her barred by a rumor with her own name caught inside it.

The rain went on falling. Halloway poured himself a cup of tea, sat down by the window, and watched the road.
