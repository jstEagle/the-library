## 8. The Free Cities Refuse to Listen

The council hall of Callowmere had been built to impress, and it did. Isolde Verane stood beneath a ceiling of painted merchants' coats-of-arms—forty families, forty fortunes, each blazoned with the ships and scales that had made them rich—and felt the particular smallness of being one woman with one book in a room built for men who measured everything in cargo.

She had dressed carefully for this. Not her archivist's grey, which would mark her as a servant of the archive, but the deep blue of a scholar with independent means, a fiction she could only afford because she'd spent her last coin on the dye. Her hair was pinned back with the severity her mother had taught her, the severity that said *I am not here to be liked; I am here to be believed.* Under her arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, she carried the prophecy book—the smuggler's cache book, the one whose marginalia she had spent three sleepless nights decoding, the one that named a mercenary called Sebastian Reyes and a date that had already come and gone like a knife sliding home.

The date had passed four days ago. She knew what had happened on it, or enough: somewhere in the Thornfell passes, a man had opened a century-old book and read his own name in it. She did not know how he had taken it. She only knew that she had set this chain moving when she sent her request to the Salt Road factors asking after any text concerning Thornfell, and that the request had reached ears she never intended it to reach, and that a man named Halloway now had her description, her name, and a warrant with her excommunication folded into its justification like a blade wrapped in a cloth.

*You alerted them*, she told herself, not for the first time, not for the hundredth. *You asked the question out loud, and the question itself was the crime.* The censors she had once served—and she had served them, she could no longer pretend otherwise, not even to herself, not since the confession she had made to Sebastian on the salt road—had a word for what she'd done. They called it *unstriking*. Reaching into the record for something that had been struck from it. It was the one sin the archive punished more harshly than theft, because theft took a book and unstriking took a lie apart.

So here she stood, an excommunicated censor's daughter turned fugitive archivist, presenting evidence of an apocalypse to a council of merchants who had scheduled her between a dispute over grain tariffs and a petition from the dyers' guild.

The Speaker of the Council, a heavy man named Aldous Crane with rings on six fingers and none of them inherited, gave her the floor with the courtesy one extends to entertainment. "Mistress Verane. You have a quarter hour. The dyers are waiting."

"I will need less," Isolde said, and unwrapped the book.

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it on the road, walking, speaking aloud to mules and milestones. She knew the temptation would be to begin with Thornfell—with the erased kingdom, the romance of it, the shiver of a country deleted from every map. She had decided against romance. Romance made councils feel things, and feeling was not what she needed from them. She needed arithmetic.

"Three weeks ago," she began, "a caravan came down out of the high passes carrying contraband texts. Among them was a book of prophecies written one hundred years ago, in the years after Malvane's grandfather seized the throne of the old kingdom. I have verified its age by binding, ink, and watermarks. I have cross-checked its prophecies against events. Of the seven predictions in its opening folios that concern dates now past, all seven have occurred. Not approximately. Exactly. To the day, in two cases."

Crane steepled his fingers. A few of the councillors leaned forward—not with belief, but with the interest of men who smelled either profit or fraud and hadn't yet determined which.

"The final prophecy concerns a date three weeks past," Isolde continued. "It names a person. It names a place. And it names a door." She turned to the relevant page and held the book open, but she did not pass it around; she had learned, in her years among records, that a book passed hand to hand becomes a curiosity, and a curiosity becomes a joke by evening. "I will read it."

She read. Her voice carried better than she expected; fear does that, fear is a kind of bellows. *When the last heir of Thornfell stands beneath the mountain's root, the Door of Ash shall know its own, and the way shall open that was shut, and the people who were written out shall be written back.*

Silence, of a sort. The silence of a room deciding whether to be bored.

"Mistress Verane," said a councillor named Fessenden, silver-haired, owner of three granaries and a reputation for reasonableness, "prophecy books are a trade like any other. For every genuine article there are forty forgeries, and the forgers are better businessmen. What you're describing—a kingdom erased from the maps, a hidden heir, a magic door—is a story. A good one. I might almost believe someone wrote it recently and aged it cleverly, because it's exactly the kind of story that sells."

"It has been carbon-dated—" Isolde stopped herself; they had no such instrument here, and she knew the phrase meant nothing to them. "The binding is a hundred years old. The ink oxidation is consistent. The marginalia is in three different hands across sixty years, which no forger bothers to fake, because fakes want to look complete."

"And yet," Fessenden said gently, "none of this proves the prophecy true. Only old."

An older woman on the far bench—Isolde learned later her name was Maren Ostwick, shipping, one of the few women seated—spoke up. "What I want to know is simpler. Say we believe it. Say there's an army coming over those passes. What do you propose we *do*? Callowmere has walls, Mistress Verane. Walls and grain and a fleet. We have survived sieges before. Why should we empty our vaults and alarm our markets on the word of a book?"

This was the real argument, and Isolde had known it would be, and she hated how well she understood it. The free cities were not cowardly. They were *priced in*. Every merchant in that hall had already calculated the cost of Malvane's conquest and found it survivable—tribute, tariffs, a new face on the coinage, business continuing under new management. That was the whole genius of Malvane's approach to the cities: not to storm them but to make surrender look like prudence. Halloway understood this too. It was why he didn't need to catch Sebastian Reyes. He needed only to make the cities believe the heir was caught, or would be, and watch their arithmetic tip.

"You cannot pay tribute to a fire," Isolde said. "Malvane does not want your tariffs. He wants the Door of Ash closed behind him and every claim to legitimacy buried. The prophecy says the free cities escape through the mountain. It does not say the free cities survive *here*. There is no treaty coming. There is no arrangement. There is an army at the valley mouth within the season, and an inquisitor who has already burned a village looking for the book I am holding."

That landed harder than anything else she'd said. Several councillors glanced at each other. Rumors had come down the salt road—smoke seen in the high country, a village that appeared on no map because it had ceased to appear anywhere. Merchants discounted rumors. But smoke was smoke.

Still, the quarter hour ran out as quarter hours do, and Crane called the dyers' petition, and Isolde was given a courteous nod and a suggestion that she lodge her document with the city archive for review—which was, she realized with a cold drop in her stomach, exactly where Halloway's agents would find it, catalogued, indexed, and waiting.

She left the hall through the colonnade, and the rain had started, fine and grey, and she stood under the eaves watching the harbor and thought about Sebastian, wherever he was, holding a sigil that burned toward mountains she couldn't see from here, and wondered if he too was being refused by everyone he tried to warn.

* * *

They gave her a room anyway. Not in the council hall—in the archive annexe, a narrow cell above the map room, offered by a junior archivist named Pell who had attended her presentation and been the only person in the building who didn't treat it as theater. Pell was twenty-three, ink-stained, earnest, and terrified, and he had recognized in Isolde's face the same expression he saw in his own mirror: someone who had found the truth of a thing and discovered the truth was radioactive.

"They'll review it," Pell said, helping her bar the door, which had no lock, only a bolt added sometime in the last century by some other paranoid. "The full council. In nine days. There's a procedure. Documents get referred, and referrals get heard, and sometimes the hearings actually matter."

"Nine days," Isolde said. "Halloway moves faster than committees."

"You've met him?"

"No. I've read his methods. He was trained in the same school I was—before I left it. He doesn't destroy archives. He *compromises* them. He lets them keep existing so that everything in them becomes suspect. Once a council believes its own records may be forged, it stops believing anything, including warnings."

Pell absorbed that with the particular horror of the young encountering institutional rot for the first time. "Then what do we do?"

"We wait," Isolde said, and hated saying it, "and we watch, and we prepare to be wrong-footed. And you should go home to your family tonight, Pell. Not tomorrow. Tonight. If what I think is going to happen happens, anyone associated with this book will need to not be associated with it."

He didn't go. Young men in love with their work rarely do. He slept in the map room, on a pallet among the rolled charts of coastlines that no longer existed, and Isolde lay awake in her cell listening to the rain and the harbor bells counting the hours, and somewhere past midnight she heard footsteps in the stacks below—soft ones, deliberate ones, the walk of people who have practiced walking quietly in places where quiet is suspicious.

She did not investigate. Investigation was how archivists died. She barred the inner door, put the prophecy book inside her clothing where it sat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and waited for dawn with a knife she'd bought from a fishmonger and held wrong.

* * *

The fire came at the hour before dawn, which is the hour fires come when they are meant to look accidental—late enough that the night watch is drowsy, early enough that the blame scatters into confusion.

It began in the map room.

Isolde understood the choice instantly, the way you understand a sentence in a language you used to speak. Not the prophecy book—it wasn't there; she had it. Not the council records. The *maps*. The charts of coastlines, the surveys, the atlases, the whole cartographic memory of the free cities. Fire in the map room said: *your geography cannot be trusted either.* It was a message written in flame, addressed not to the council but to every clerk and archivist in the building: nothing you preserve means anything. We can reach the heart of what you keep. We chose what to burn.

She smelled the smoke before she saw light—old vellum has a smell when it begins to go, sweetish, like hay left too long—and she got Pell out through the annexe stair while the junior night clerks were still forming a bucket line that was mostly ceremony. The city brigade arrived in good time and fought the fire with competence, and by full morning the map room was a blackened rectangle smelling of char and brine, its iron map-presses warped, its contents ash and slurry.

No one died. That was part of the craft of it. Halloway's people did not burn buildings with people in them; they burned buildings beside people, close enough to terrify, far enough to avoid turning the city's grief into fury. An accidental fire with casualties becomes an outrage, and outrage organizes. An accidental fire without casualties becomes a *story*, and stories erode.

By noon the story had done its work. Isolde heard it in the annexe courtyard, in the voices of clerks who had fought the fire side by side and now eyed each other over the buckets: *How did it start? No one knows. Was anything taken before it burned? How would we know? Who requested the Thornfell documents?* — and there it was, the pivot, the exact phrase Halloway must have prayed for. Her name, spoken in the yard, attached to suspicion, the fire quietly becoming hers in the retelling. The excommunicated archivist. The fugitive. The one who brought the cursed book into Callowmere. Of course the archive burned. Of course.

Pell came to her white-faced, his eyebrows singed, his life's work dripping off the drying lines they'd strung up for salvaged charts. "They're saying it was you," he whispered. "Not loudly. In the way people say things when they want you to overhear."

"I know," Isolde said. "That's the design."

"And the council?"

"The council will convene early. Not to hear me—to bury me. My referral hearing will be cancelled. The book will be declared a forgery, possibly by me, possibly planted, and I will be invited to leave the city, or not invited."

She watched the salvaged charts drying on their lines—coastlines of drowned harbors, soundings a hundred years deep, the handwriting of dead surveyors—and something in her that had been despairing since the council hall shifted gear. Despair is a luxury; it assumes you have time to sit in it. She did not have time. What she had was a book against her ribs, a name in her memory—Sebastian Reyes, the passes, the waystone, the shrine—and a certainty arriving not as hope but as arithmetic: if the free cities would not listen to her, then the warning would have to travel another way. Through the man the prophecy named. Through whatever the burning sigil was pulling him toward. Through the Door itself, if it existed, if it could be found, if an heir could reach it ahead of the army that needed it sealed.

"Where is he?" Pell asked, meaning Sebastian, meaning the mercenary, meaning the thread she kept pulling though it frayed her hands. "Do you know?"

"I know where he was," Isolde said. "Four days ago, the high passes, heading east along the salt road. After that—" she thought of the bridge they'd burned, the fire they'd confessed truths over, the company that had sold him, the inquisitors who'd let a smuggler go precisely so his report would spread fear ahead of them— "after that, he's either running toward the mountains or being chased into them, and both roads end at the same place."

She looked at the ruined map room one more time. Under the soot, on one wall that had partially survived, a fragment of an old survey still hung: the high country east of the cities, drawn before the erasure, when the surveyors' ink had still been permitted to name what it saw. And there, faint, half-legible beneath a century of grime and yesterday's smoke, she could read a label that no modern map carried.

*Thornfell.*

The country that had been written out of existence had left one trace in Callowmere, and Halloway's fire had missed it—or had been meant to miss it, or had been unable to touch it, because some things refuse to burn no matter what you feed the flames.

Isolde took the fragment down with her own hands, rolled it against the grain, and put it inside her coat with the book.

"We're leaving tonight," she told Pell, and then, because he would insist on following her into whatever came next and she lacked the heart to stop him and the wisdom to encourage it: "East. Up the salt road. Toward the man the prophecy names, and the door that opens only for him, and whatever waits on the other side of it that neither of us understands yet."

The rain had stopped. The harbor bells rang the evening hour, and the council's messengers were already carrying the news of the fire through the streets with the careful language of official uncertainty, and in nine days a committee would have met to decide nothing, and by then Isolde Verane intended to be so far into the high country that committees would be a rumor to her, and the only thing left to do would be the thing she had been avoiding since she first decoded the marginalia:

Find Sebastian Reyes, and tell him that the cities had refused to listen, and that the silence of a god and the silence of a council were, perhaps, the same silence wearing different clothes—and that somewhere ahead of them both, a door made of ash was waiting to learn whether anyone was still worth remembering.
