## 9. A God's Silence, Examined

The drowned temple announced itself the way all honest things did: without decoration.

Sebastian had walked past it a dozen times in their smuggling years, or rather over it—its roofless colonnade rising from the shallows of Lake Verren like the ribs of something that had lain down to die and never finished the gesture. In summer, when the water drew back, you could cross its flooded threshold on stepping-stones worn smooth by feet that had stopped coming centuries ago. The locals called it the Lady's Bath. They fished from its broken architraves and told their children not to swim past the third pillar, because the third pillar was where the old god had drowned.

Old gods drowned easily, Sebastian had always thought. That was what gods were for.

They came down to the water alone, leaving Isolde asleep under a fisherman's tarpaulin at the edge of the reed-beds, her cheek pressed against the satchel that held the book of prophecies as if she feared it would be taken from her even in dreams. She needed the sleep. So did Sebastian, but sleep had become a country they no longer held papers for. Every time they closed their eyes they saw the page again—their own name in a century-old hand, *tonight* written before tonight had happened—and every time, the silence behind their sternum where Vessia's voice used to live grew one degree colder.

So they walked out onto the stones instead, into the grey hour before dawn when the lake lay flat as hammered tin and the mountains across the water were only a suggestion, a bruise-colored rumor on the horizon.

The sigil on their wrist was quiet.

It had been quiet since Callowmere—quiet through the council hall's contempt, quiet through Isolde's white-faced fury at the merchants who would not listen, quiet through the long walk back into the hills with nothing gained but the certainty that the free cities intended to die politely, one trade agreement at a time. Quiet, and Sebastian had begun to wonder if the burning had been the last of it. If a bond could starve. If oaths, like kingdoms, could simply be struck from the record and leave no wound behind, only the smooth unscarred place where the name used to be.

They knelt on the second step above the waterline, the way they'd been taught to kneel at nineteen, in another life, by a company chaplain with wine on his breath and genuine tenderness in his voice. *Kneel low enough to be beneath her notice, boy. Vessia is the god of things that go unheard. You don't pray up to her. You pray down, into the quiet.*

"Vessia." Their breath fogged over the black water. "I don't know if you're there. I haven't known for years. I've said the words anyway, because an oath isn't a transaction, it's—" They stopped. Swallowed. The chaplain's ghost offered no correction; he was dead these six winters, along with whatever part of Sebastian had believed kneeling was enough.

"I'm going to say it plainly," they went on, quieter now, the way you spoke to someone sleeping whom you didn't want to wake and couldn't bear not to. "I found my name in a book older than my grandmother. It says I'm the heir of a kingdom I've never seen, on a map that never existed. It says there's a door. And I think—I think you've known this whole time. I think that's why you went silent. And I need you to tell me I'm wrong, because if I'm right, then the thing I gave my life to was just a waiting room."

The lake said nothing.

The lake was very good at saying nothing. It had practiced.

Sebastian laughed—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it but exhaustion—and pressed their palm flat against the wet stone. "Fine. Then hear the rest, wherever you are, or don't. There's a woman asleep in the reeds who ruined her life to carry your prophecy to a man she'd never met. There's ten thousand people in cities full of granaries and ledgers who will burn if nobody moves them. And there's me, standing in the ruins of the only conversation we ever had, asking you: *was any of it real? Did you ever actually speak? Or have I been talking to my own wrist for fifteen years?*"

The water rippled.

Not wind—there was no wind; the morning was holding its breath—but a single concentric ring, expanding outward from a point perhaps twenty paces out, where the submerged outline of the temple's inner sanctum made a darker shape beneath the surface. The ring reached the shore, touched the stone beneath Sebastian's hand, and passed on. And in its wake the sigil woke.

Not burned. *Sang.* A thin, high heat that ran up the bones of their forearm and settled behind their breastbone like a struck bell still choosing whether to fade. Sebastian hissed and clutched their wrist, and in the grey light saw the oath-mark's lines had shifted—not the closed knot they had carried since initiation, but open now, unfurled, each stroke pointing inward toward the lake's dark center. Toward the sanctum. Toward the drowned place.

Toward, they realized with a lurch that had nothing to do with balance, the mountains beyond it. The heart of the range. The same bearing the book's marginalia had traced in Isolde's careful reconstructed hand.

"Oh," Sebastian said softly, to no one, to everyone. "*Oh, you absolute bastard.*"

"You shouldn't curse at the water." The voice came from the colonnade behind them, dry as moth wings. "She takes it personally. Always has."

Sebastian was on their feet with a knife drawn before the echo settled—which was habit, and also, lately, wisdom. An old woman stood between two leaning pillars where no one had stood a moment before, small and bent and wrapped in layers of undyed wool gone the color of the mist off the lake. Her eyes were closed. They had been closed, Sebastian understood at once, for a very long time; the lids had the settled, finished look of shutters on a house whose owner has moved away.

"I heard you praying down," the old woman said. "That's rare, nowadays. Most people shout. Shouting is how you talk to gods you don't trust. Praying down is how you talk to one you're afraid of losing." She tilted her head. "You're afraid of losing her. How strange. Most people lost her a century ago."

"The priestess." It wasn't quite a question. The smugglers' gossip about the Lady's Bath had included, among the warnings about the third pillar, mention of a blind woman who lived in the ruins and spoke to the drowned god, and who had been old when the current fishermen's grandmothers were girls. Nobody believed it. Smugglers didn't believe anything they couldn't drink or spend.

"People call me that." She smiled without much humor. "Priestess implies a priesthood. There hasn't been a priesthood since the Striking. There's only me, and the water, and whoever wanders in asking questions at dawn." Her blind face turned toward him with unsettling precision. "You asked her a good question, though. Whether she ever truly spoke. Shall I tell you what the water tells me? It tells me you already know the answer, and can't afford it yet."

Sebastian sheathed the knife slowly. Every mercenary instinct they owned said this was exactly the kind of encounter that ended with a blade between the ribs—old women in ruins, voices from nowhere, omens arranged too neatly. But the sigil was still singing, warm and open against their pulse, and it had never once lied to them. Misled them, abandoned them, possibly—yes. Lied, never.

"Who are you?" they asked.

"A keeper of an inconvenient congregation." She gestured, one wool-wrapped arm sweeping to take in the flooded nave, the sunken sanctum, the far mountains. "Come inside properly, heir of Thornfell. The water is shallowest where it's deepest. You'll see."

* * *

They waded in to the third pillar.

The water rose to their thighs, cold enough to make their breath shorten, and the old woman moved ahead of them through it without hesitation or splash, barefoot beneath her robes, finding the stepping-stones by some sense that had nothing to do with sight. Up close the temple revealed itself differently than it had from the shore. The carvings on the pillars—worn nearly featureless by water and time—were not, Sebastian saw now, decorative. Under the slime and the centuries, faint figures spiraled downward around each column: processions of people, ranks of them, walking into the stone itself. Not drowning. *Entering.*

"They carved it as they left," the priestess said, without turning. "Or as they thought they were leaving. Look at the faces, if you can find one clear enough. They aren't frightened."

"Who are they?"

"The last congregation of Vessia. The ones who stayed when Malvane's censors came with their ledgers and their ink." She paused on a stone, small and steady amid the black water. "You know what the Striking was, I think. Your archivist will have told you. Thornfell wasn't conquered, child. It was *edited*. Its name pulled out of every record, its borders reassigned, its kings declared mythical. A country doesn't survive that by fighting. Armies fight, and edited countries have no armies, because armies require a flag and flags require a name."

"So they fled."

"Some fled. The Door was opened once before—you knew that? Of course you didn't. No one knows anything anymore; that was rather the point of the exercise." Something moved across her closed lids, a flicker, like light behind curtains. "But the Door opens one way and admits few, and Malvane's men watched the passes. So others stayed. The stubborn ones. The ones who thought a country was more than its name on a map—that it was the stones and the songs and the graves, and none of those could be struck out, because the censors' ink didn't reach that deep." Her voice thinned. "They came here, to the oldest shrine of the god who hears what goes unsaid, and they did the only thing left to do. They stopped speaking of Thornfell aloud, so the censors would have nothing more to strike. And they kept it *inward*. Kept it in memory. Kept it the way you keep a coal alive under ash."

"And the god?" Sebastian asked, though their heart had begun to hammer against the singing sigil as if answering a knock. "What happened to her?"

The priestess turned. For a moment, in the flat pre-dawn light, with the water dripping from her sleeves and her blind eyes aimed at him with terrible gentleness, she looked less like an old woman than like something the country had grown out of its own grief, the way a tree grows a scar over a lost limb.

"That is the question, isn't it. The one you knelt on the shore to ask." She beckoned, and waded onward, toward the sunken sanctum. "Everyone asks it eventually. The fishermen ask it as a joke. The scholars asked it as a scandal, once upon a time. But you—" her head cocked, listening to something beneath his skin, "—you get to ask it as an heir. Come and see where she went. Mind the fourth stone. It lies."

* * *

The sanctum was a circle of nine pillars, eight standing and one fallen, around a floor of fitted black stone that the water barely covered—a hand's depth, no more, so that the whole space seemed less flooded than polished, a mirror set into the lakebed. The old woman stepped onto it and stood, motionless, while Sebastian hesitated at the edge.

"It won't take anything from you," she said. "That's not what it does. That's not what *she* does. Whatever else you've been told about gods and prices, unlearn it here."

Sebastian stepped down onto the black stone.

The moment their boot touched it, the sigil flared—truly flared this time, a band of heat from wrist to elbow, and the vision came with it, unasked, undeniable:

A throne room. High vaulted halls of pale granite, banners of green and silver hanging motionless in air that smelled of cedar smoke and rain. A door at the far end—vast, dark, seamed with veins of grey like cooled lava, and *breathing*, the seams glowing and dimming with a slow rhythm, as though the mountain behind it inhaled and exhaled. Figures knelt before it in ranks. And above the door, cut deep into the living rock in letters taller than a man, a single word in a language Sebastian had never studied and read without effort:

VES-SI-A. NOT A NAME. A THRESHOLD.

The vision broke. Sebastian staggered, caught themselves on the fallen ninth pillar, and found the priestess watching them with her head inclined, patient as the water.

"You saw her," the old woman said. It was not a question.

"I saw a door."

"You saw *her*." The priestess's voice dropped, and the whole drowned temple seemed to lean in to listen. "Listen to me, Sebastian Reyes, and believe it, because I am the last person alive who was taught it properly. Vessia did not die. Gods of her kind don't die—they *spend*. She spent herself the night the censors came, poured everything she was into the only vessel no ledger can edit and no army can burn." She lifted one hand, dripping, and pointed—not at the lake, not at the sky, but east, toward the mountains, toward the bruise-colored mass now catching the first thin gold of sunrise along its spine. "She became a *place*. The Door of Ash is not a door to somewhere. It is Vessia. Her body, her memory, her patience—all of it folded into stone and seam and threshold, waiting in the heart of the range for the one thing she cannot do for herself."

Sebastian's mouth had gone dry. "Which is?"

"To be entered." The old woman smiled, and it was the saddest expression Sebastian had ever seen on a human face. "Places wait to be entered, child. That is what places are *for*. A house waits. A grave waits. A country waits. She has waited a hundred years in the dark with the whole of a kingdom folded inside her—every road, every harvest, every song, every name the censors struck—and she cannot open herself, because a door cannot turn its own handle. She needs hands. Heir's hands. Hands that remember being hers."

The sigil pulsed, and this time Sebastian felt the direction of it unmistakably, a compass-needle of heat drawing a line from their wrist through their chest and out into the world—east, and up, into the mountains' blind white heart. Toward the Door. Toward the god who had not gone silent at all, but had simply *moved*, and been waiting all these years for them to stop shouting at the empty air and follow.

"She wasn't silent," Sebastian whispered. "All those years. All those unanswered prayers. She was—"

"Answering. Constantly. With the only voice she had left." The priestess nodded toward his wrist. "That burn you call silence? That was her speaking. Every flare, every ache, every pull toward a road you hadn't chosen yet—she was steering you the only way a place can steer anything. Toward herself." Her blind eyes gleamed wet in the growing light. "You thought you'd been talking to your own wrist for fifteen years. Dear child. You've been talking to a country."

The words landed in Sebastian's chest like stones dropped down a well—falling and falling, striking depths they hadn't known they contained. Fifteen years of prayer in the dark. Fifteen years of feeling foolish, faithful, forsaken. And all of it answered, all of it *heard*, by something vast and patient and buried in the roots of the mountains, keeping faith with an heir it had never met, in a kingdom that officially had never existed.

"Why me?" The question came out rough. "There must be others. Bloodlines, descendants, anyone—"

"There are no others. That is what 'last heir' means; the book does not flatter." The priestess's voice gentled further, which Sebastian would not have believed possible. "And there is a price. There is always a price, though not the kind your ballads sing about. A door admits by exchange. To open her fully—to open her wide enough for ten thousand souls to pass through in a single season—she needs not your blood and not your death." The old woman's hand closed over Sebastian's wrist, over the singing sigil, cool and dry as paper. "She needs your memories. The life you chose. The roads, the company, the years, the people—the self you built when you thought you were nobody. All of it, surrendered at the threshold, to feed the remembering heart of a country that has forgotten how to remember."

The lake lapped softly at the pillars. Somewhere behind them, in the reeds, Isolde would be waking soon, and the day's running would begin again, and none of it—not the pursuit, not the councils, not the burning cities—would wait for grief.

Sebastian looked east. The mountains stood gold-edged and indifferent, and beneath their snowfields, in the dark, something vast exhaled.

Hope arrived, and it arrived wearing its true clothes: not triumph, not rescue, but a door that would open only if they walked through it and left themselves behind.

"How far?" Sebastian asked quietly.

"Three days, if the passes hold. Less, if they don't." The priestess released their wrist and stepped back, folding into the shadow of the pillars like a figure returning to a fresco. "Go and wake your archivist, heir of Thornfell. Tell her the god was never silent. And tell her—" the blind face tilted one last time, and the smile returned, sad and knowing, "—tell her to love you thoroughly in the days remaining. Doors keep excellent accounts."

The water stilled. When Sebastian turned to speak again, the colonnade stood empty, and only the ninth pillar's reflection trembled on the black mirror of the sanctum, where the carved procession entered the stone forever, unafraid, faces upturned toward a threshold no one had named until now.

Sebastian waded ashore alone, the sigil burning a steady line toward the mountains' heart, and began, with hands that almost shook, to count what they would have to forget.
