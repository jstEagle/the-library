## 10. Orla's Terms

The snow on the high road had gone grey with ash by the time Orla caught the scent of her own company's smoke, and she knew then that something in the world had tilted past mending. She had ridden alone for three days, leaving the column at the ford with a lie about scouting the eastern ridges, and for three days she had followed a trail only she could read: a heel dragged here, a campfire banked too carefully there, the particular way Sebastian Reyes broke trail through scree—always choosing the harder line, the one no one sensible would take, because no one sensible would be looking.

She found them at dusk in the lee of a fallen watchtower, an old Thornfell ruin that the maps had forgotten along with everything else. Two figures against a small fire. One hunched over a book. The other feeding sticks to the flames with the nervous economy of someone who counted every resource twice.

Orla did not dismount at once. She sat her horse at the edge of the light and let them see her—let Sebastian see her—because there was no version of this that ended with a knife in the dark. Whatever came next had to happen face to face, in the open, where the oaths between them could be spoken aloud or broken aloud. She owed him that much. She owed herself that much. Twenty-two years of raising a child who was never hers, and she would not end it like a thief.

"Sebastian," she said.

The figure by the book went still in a way that was worse than flinching. It was the stillness of an animal deciding whether it had already been caught.

"Captain." His voice was flat as a frozen lake. He didn't stand. The woman beside him did—the archivist, Verane, thin and sharp-eyed even half-starved, positioning herself not behind Sebastian but beside him, which told Orla more than any interrogation could have.

"You're alone," Sebastian said.

"I am."

"Your scouts aren't in the trees?"

"No scouts. No Halloway. Just me and a horse that hates this mountain and a flask of brandy I've been saving for an occasion." She swung down, deliberately slow, deliberately loud, and held up both hands empty. "This is an occasion."

* * *

They drank from the same tin cup, which was either a gesture of trust or an accusation, depending on how you looked at it, and Orla suspected Sebastian was looking at it both ways at once. She had taught him that. She took a small pride in it even now, sitting across the fire from a deserter she'd been ordered to bring back dead.

The archivist watched her like a hawk watching a snake. Smart girl. Orla made a point of meeting her eyes when she spoke, because the words were for her almost as much as for him.

"Do you know what they promised me?" Orla said.

Sebastian said nothing.

"A pardon. Not just mine. The whole company. Letters of marque under Malvane's seal, back pay going back nine years, land grants in the lowlands. We stop being mercenaries and start being soldiers. Legitimate. Our names in the rolls instead of the warrants." She turned the cup in her hands. "You know what we were before I bought us that contract? You remember what it cost to keep forty people fed through the winter Halden's Crossing burned?"

"I remember," Sebastian said quietly. "I carried the grain."

"You carried the grain. And I signed the paper. That's the trade we always made, you and I. You do the bleeding; I do the signing." She looked up at him across the fire. "So understand me when I tell you I signed another one. And understand that I hate myself for it in a way I will carry to whatever grave I get."

"Then un-sign it."

"It doesn't work that way, boy." The old name slipped out before she could stop it—*boy*, though he was thirty-one and had buried men older than himself—and she saw him flinch, and hated herself a little more. "Contracts don't un-sign. Not with Halloway holding the other copy. You've met his work by now, I expect."

Sebastian's hand moved, without seeming to decide to, to the wrist where the oath-sigil lived beneath its wrappings. Orla tracked the motion. She had seen that sigil since he was nine years old and some dying priest pressed it into his skin in payment for a rescue. She had never understood it. She was beginning to.

"The inquisitor sent me alone," she said. "That part's true. But hear all of it, because you've earned all of it, and then decide what kind of fool you want to be."

She set down the cup.

"Halloway doesn't want you dead, Sebastian. I need you to hear that properly, because everything you've been running on—the fear, the flight, the burning bridges—it's all built on the wrong foundation. Dead men make poor kings."

The word landed in the firelight like a dropped coin.

*Kings.*

Beside Sebastian, Isolde Verane drew a breath sharp enough to cut.

"They want you crowned," Orla said. "Thornfell has an heir. A living, breathing, oath-sworn heir with a god's mark on his arm and a prophecy in his pocket naming him by name and by date. Do you understand what that's worth to a man like Malvane? He hasn't conquered the free cities yet. He's *buying* them—one council, one charter, one frightened merchant at a time—and the purchase goes faster if it isn't conquest at all. If it's restoration. If the lost kingdom comes back from the dead on its own, willingly, under its rightful king, and the rightful king happens to owe his crown to the empire that found him." She smiled, and it hurt her face. "You wouldn't be their prisoner, boy. You'd be their signature. Your name on the proclamation. Your god's blessing on the annexation. Every holdout in every free city lays down arms the day their prophesied king says the words they've been waiting a hundred years to hear. And you'd say them. Because Halloway is very good at making people say things."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the fire to settle twice.

Sebastian stared into it. Orla watched him the way she used to watch him over maps, reading terrain: the jaw tight, the thumb moving over the wrapped wrist again and again, the glance sideways—not at escape routes, she noted, but at the woman beside him. Checking on her. Measuring the distance between her and danger, and finding himself the danger.

There it was, then. She'd known it would be. She had raised this stubborn, self-erasing child for two decades and she knew exactly which lever moved him, and she despised Halloway for finding it too, and despised herself most of all for being the hand on it.

"So that's the offer," Sebastian said finally. His voice was steady. Too steady. "I come in. I put on the crown. And she—" his chin tipped toward Isolde—"walks free."

"She walks free. Full pardon, escort to Callowmere or anywhere else she names, her archive sealed and protected under imperial writ. No one touches her. On my life." Orla said it clearly, formally, the way terms were given in the old companies, so there could be no pretending later that anything had been implied. "That's the deal, and it's a good one, and if you turn it down out of pride I will spend the rest of my life knowing I offered you the door and watched you choose the window."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Halloway stops sending mothers." She said it without drama. "He sends the ninth column, and the archivist becomes a lesson rather than a hostage, and you learn the difference between a leash and a grave too late to matter."

Isolde spoke for the first time. Her voice was hoarse from cold but level. "And if he accepts—if he lets himself be crowned—how long does he live after the last city signs?"

Orla held her gaze. She would not lie to this one. Lying to her would be like lying to a mirror.

"Ask your own books," Orla said. "Usurpers burn their instruments. Malvane keeps no puppets longer than the puppeteering takes. But you already knew that. You worked for his censors once—you'd know better than anyone what he does with pens he no longer needs."

She regretted it the moment she said it. She saw it land—a small detonation behind Isolde's eyes, some private ruin collapsing further—and Sebastian's head turning slowly toward the archivist with an expression Orla had never seen on his face before, and never wanted to see again.

"That's not why I'm telling you," Orla said quickly. "I'm not here to break you two apart. Gods know the world will try hard enough on its own."

"Why *are* you here, Captain?" Sebastian asked. Still that terrible steadiness. "Truly. Strip away the contract and the pardon and the threats. Why ride three days alone into the cold to sit at my fire?"

And there it was—the question she had been carrying up the mountain like a stone in her chest for three days, the one she'd rehearsed answers to and discarded all of them, because there was only one true answer and it served no strategic purpose whatsoever.

"Because twenty-two years ago I pulled a starving child out of a ditch outside Vessia's drowned temple, and I fed him, and I taught him to fight, and somewhere in the middle of doing my job I became his mother in every way that matters except the one that requires saying it aloud," Orla said. "And because I have spent my whole life trading pieces to keep my people alive, and I am so tired, Sebastian. I am tired down to the marrow. And the only honest thing left in me refuses to watch you walk into Halloway's machine believing it was ever going to let you—or her—out again."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the ruined tower, sighing through stone like an old woman remembering.

"So there's my answer," Orla finished. "No strategy. Just an old woman's confession, worth exactly nothing to the empire, and everything to me."

* * *

Sebastian stood. Walked to the edge of the firelight. Stood looking north, toward the passes, toward wherever the Door of Ash waited in whatever shape it took, and Orla gave him the time because some decisions can't be hurried without breaking them.

When he turned around, his face had settled into something she recognized from the worst days of the company—the look he wore when the price was already paid and all that remained was arithmetic.

"If I go with you," he said, "you swear on the company's colors—on every man and woman we buried—that she reaches Callowmere untouched. Not escorted. *Untouched.*"

"On the colors. On the graves. On my mother's name."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I ride back alone and tell Halloway you're beyond reach, and buy you whatever days my credibility can purchase, and pray they're enough." She paused. "Which they won't be. So don't make me do that."

But Sebastian wasn't listening anymore. He was looking at Isolde, and Isolde was looking at him, and between them passed something silent and enormous that Orla felt like an intruder witnessing—the same current she'd seen once, years ago, between two soldiers holding a breached wall, deciding together who would live.

It was Isolde who broke it.

"He's not going with you," she said. Quietly. Without heat. As though reporting a fact about the weather.

Orla turned to her. "You'd gamble his life on the mountains and Halloway's patience?"

"I'd gamble it on *him*," Isolde said. "Which is more than the empire's willing to do. They don't want Sebastian Reyes. They want a crown-shaped hole where he used to be. And you know it, Captain. You said it yourself—he's the signature, not the signatory. They'll wear his hand out writing proclamations and then burn the hand." She stepped forward, into the firelight fully, and Orla saw that she was trembling—with cold, or fury, or the specific courage of people who have already lost everything once. "You want to give him a door. Fine. But check whose lock is on it."

For a long moment nobody spoke. The wind sighed through the tower. The fire sank.

Then Orla began, very quietly, to laugh—not the cruel laugh of a negotiator whose gambit failed, but something rawer, nearer grief. She rubbed her face with both hands.

"Gods help me, you sound like me at thirty," she said to Isolde. "Before the signing started. Before I learned that every door has a lock and somebody owns it." She looked up at Sebastian, and her voice gentled into something neither of them had used with each other in years. "Last chance, boy. Come home. Bring her. We'll figure out the rest the way we always figured out the rest—together, badly, and alive."

Sebastian crossed back to the fire and knelt, slowly, so that his eyes were level with hers—the way he had knelt at her feet as a child learning sword-grip, the way no mercenary kneels to a captain unless it's family.

"I can't," he said. "You know I can't. The book has my name in it. The sigil burns toward those mountains every hour of every day. And Vessia—" his voice caught, and Orla heard, beneath twenty years of discipline, the abandoned child in the ditch—"Vessia is finally answering something. Even if it costs everything. *Especially* if it costs everything."

Orla closed her eyes.

When she opened them, she reached into her coat and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, and inside it a letter bearing the black wax of Malvane's chancery, and she held it out to him.

"Then take this," she said. "Halloway's full terms, written in his own hand. Routes, garrisons, the disposition of the ninth column—all of it. I copied it three nights ago while the camp slept." Her mouth twisted. "Consider it the last thing your mother ever stole for you. Because in three days' time, when Halloway asks me where you've gone, I'm going to have to point east and lie convincingly, and I'd rather you were far away and armed with his own handwriting than close and trusting mine."

Sebastian took the letter as though it weighed more than the sword at his hip.

"You'll hang for this," Isolde said softly.

"I've been hanging for thirty years," Orla said. "It's called command." She rose, joints cracking, and gathered her horse's reins, and paused with one foot in the stirrup.

"Boy."

He looked up.

"When you find whatever door you're looking for—and you will find it, because you were always too stubborn to die and too honest to quit—remember that some of us stayed behind in the world that forgot your name. Somebody has to remember it *out here*. Somebody has to keep saying *Thornfell* in the taverns and the muster-rolls until the words stop sounding mad." She swung up into the saddle. "That's your mother's job now. Let me do it."

She turned the horse north, toward the column, toward the lie she would have to tell, and then—because she was who she was, and had earned one indulgence in twenty-two years—she looked back once.

The fire was small against the vast dark of the pass. Two figures beside it, leaning into each other like trees grown too close to separate. Behind them the ruined tower of a kingdom no map remembered, and above it, faint as breath on glass, the mountains glowed the color of old embers.

Orla rode down into the dark, carrying her stolen letter's twin, her forged report, and the particular loneliness of a woman who has just chosen, forever, which side of a door she will die on.

Behind her, she did not see Sebastian open Halloway's letter by firelight—nor the way his face changed as he read, nor the single line at the bottom, in the inquisitor's careful clerk's hand, that had not been in any version Orla had copied:

*We know about the village. We will be there before you.*
