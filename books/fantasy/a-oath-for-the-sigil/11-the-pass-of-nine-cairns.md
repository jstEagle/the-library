## 11. The Pass of Nine Cairns

The pass began where the maps ended, which was the only reason Sebastian trusted it.

They had left the salt road before dawn, cutting north through a fold in the hills that no surveyor had ever bothered to name—a smuggler's track worn into the scree by generations of feet that preferred not to be counted. Sebastian knew it the way they knew the backs of their own hands: the flat stone where you could take a mule at a trot if you didn't mind the drop on your left; the split boulder shaped like a broken tooth that meant water within half a mile; the place where the mountain's shadow pooled so deep and cold at midday that frost survived the summer. They had run brandy and uncut opium and fugitives along this route for eleven years, and never once thought about what it meant to be the fugitive instead.

Now they knew. It meant listening to Isolde's breathing.

She was holding up better than he'd feared. The archivist who had fled Callowmere with nothing but a satchel of stolen notes and a coat too thin for altitude had turned out to have a mountaineer's stubbornness buried under all that ink-stained gentility. She climbed when he told her to climb, froze when he told her to freeze, and asked questions only when the questions wouldn't kill them. But her breathing told him everything—the shortness of it on the steep pitches, the way she rationed her words now like a woman counting coins. Two days out from the burned bridge and the fire where they'd confessed their small treasons to each other, and already the pursuit behind them had changed shape. These weren't the company's riders anymore. Orla's people knew these hills almost as well as he did; what followed them now moved like something that had been *told* about the hills, secondhand, by someone who wanted very badly to be believed.

Halloway's outriders. Or worse—Halloway himself was patient enough to send only his map.

Sebastian crouched at the lip of the first cairn and let Isolde catch up. The stones rose waist-high against the grey sky, nine of them strung along the pass like a rosary someone had abandoned mid-prayer, each one stacked by hands long gone. Smugglers' superstition said you added a stone to the third cairn for luck and took one from the seventh for honesty, and that anyone who did both would come through the mountains owing the dark a debt it would collect eventually. Sebastian had done both, once, at nineteen, running a cargo of stolen reliquaries over the top in a whiteout. They still owed the debt. They could feel it accumulating interest.

"The ninth cairn," Isolde said, coming up beside him, her voice thin with the altitude. "In the marginalia. The prophecy references 'the nine silent witnesses.' I always assumed it was metaphorical."

"Everything in that book is metaphorical until it tries to kill you." Sebastian touched the cairn's topmost stone. It was warm. Not sun-warm—they were standing in shadow, and the air had the bite of coming snow in it—but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, faintly, from somewhere underneath. Their wrist answered before their mind did. The sigil flared against the leather bracer, a line of heat like a coal pressed to the pulse point, and beneath the flare came the whisper again, the one that wasn't a whisper because Vessia had no voice, the pressure of meaning arriving without language: *closer.*

"Sebastian." Isolde's hand closed over theirs on the stone. Her fingers were cold. "Your eyes just changed."

"They do that now."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." They pulled the bracer tighter and stood. "The sigil's been burning since the drowned temple. The priestess said Vessia became a place, and places wait to be entered. Every mile we climb toward whatever's under this pass, the bond gets... louder. Not sound. More like—" they searched for it, "—like standing next to a door and hearing, not knocking, but the person on the other side leaning their weight against the wood. Waiting for you to open it."

"And what happens when you do?"

"I don't know." They met her eyes. She deserved the truth even if the truth was useless. "I know what the book says. Heir of Thornfell. Door of Ash. I know what the priestess said. And I know that every vision comes with a price tag attached, and I've never yet seen a god offer anything for free."

* * *

They made the fourth cairn by midafternoon, and that was where the mountain decided to test them.

The track narrowed to a ledge barely wide enough for one, cut across a face of black rock that wept meltwater in slow silver threads. Below, the valley floor was already distant enough that the pines looked like moss. Sebastian went first, testing each foothold, calling the holds back to Isolde in the clipped shorthand of the trade—*left foot, crack, weight right, don't trust the grey one*—and she followed with a focus so total it looked almost like prayer. Halfway across, the sigil detonated.

Not flared. *Detonated.* Heat lanced up Sebastian's arm and into the shoulder, the neck, behind the eye, and the world doubled. For three heartbeats they were in two places at once: clinging to a wet ledge above a valley of pine, and standing in a throne room.

It came whole, the way drowning comes whole. A hall of black stone veined with gold, pillars carved as trees with their branches interlocking overhead into a canopy that had once been gilded and was now mostly grief. Dust lay over everything in a fine grey fall, and the dust was ash—that registered with terrible certainty, the way some things register without evidence—ash settled over a long table set for a feast no one had eaten, ash in the cups, ash on the thrones. Two thrones. One tall, one smaller, side by side, because whoever built this had believed rule was something you shared. On the wall behind them hung a sigil the size of a door, and it was Sebastian's sigil, the oath-mark on their wrist rendered vast, and it was *burning*, burning without consuming, and beneath it stood a door of grey stone with no handle and no hinge and no seam except the shape of a tree split down the middle.

Someone was standing in front of the door. Sebastian couldn't see the figure's face, only the outline, and the outline was wearing their own posture, their own tired set of the shoulders, and the figure raised one hand—

"—Sebastian." Isolde's voice, sharp with fear, close. "Sebastian, your hand."

They came back to the ledge with their fingers numb and their arm shaking and Isolde's grip locked around their wrist, anchoring them to the wet rock, to the cold, to the present tense. The sigil's heat drained away slowly, leaving the ache of a burn that left no mark.

"You went somewhere," she said. Not a question. Her breath fogged between them. "You were here and you weren't. Your eyes went silver-grey, like slate underwater, and you said—" she hesitated, "—you said *two thrones*. Twice."

"Did I tell you anything else?"

"No."

"Good." Sebastian leaned their forehead against the rock and breathed until the ledge stopped tilting. "Because I saw a door, Isolde. Under the mountain. Grey stone, no seam, shaped like a split tree. And I saw myself standing in front of it, and I don't think I was knocking. I think I was being *asked*."

"Asked what?"

"That's the part I'm afraid of." They pushed off the rock and kept climbing, because the alternative was standing still and thinking about it. "Every smuggler learns one law early: nothing under a mountain is free. Not shelter, not passage, not silence. Everything costs, and the price is always named after you've already taken the goods."

Isolde caught up and matched their pace, and after a while she said, quietly, "You're talking about yourself like you're already spent."

"I'm talking about myself like I've read the same marginalia you have." The wind picked at their words. "Heir of Thornfell. You showed me what that means. Not a bloodline—a *debt*. An inheritance isn't what you're given, Isolde. It's what you owe the dead."

* * *

The storm found them at the sixth cairn.

It came off the high ice without warning, as storms do up there—one moment the sky was bruised iron, the next the world was white noise and horizontal sleet that hit like thrown gravel. Sebastian got them into a hollow beneath the cairn's base, a smuggler's cache from some earlier decade, lined with rotted sacking that still held a ghost of its old purpose. They wedged together shoulder to shoulder, shared the last of the dried plums and half a skin of melted snow, and waited for the mountain to finish shouting.

In the dark, the sigil burned steadily now, no longer spiking but constant, a low fever in the bones of the forearm. And in the fever, the visions came easier—which was the frightening part. Sebastian had expected resistance. What they got instead was welcome.

The throne room again, but closer this time, walking through it step by step while the body sat shivering in a hole in the mountain. Ash on the feast table. Ash in the two thrones. And now details the first vision hadn't given: a child's cloak folded over the smaller throne's arm, moths having taken everything but the shape of it. A sword on the wall whose hilt bore the same split-tree device as the door. And written into the gold veins of the black stone, visible only when the burning sigil-light swept across it, words—not letters exactly, more like the memory of letters, the grooves where meaning used to live:

*We did not fall. We were unwritten. Unwrite us back.*

"Sebastian." Isolde's voice in the dark, very quiet. "You're speaking. In your sleep or whatever this is—you're speaking words I don't recognize, and then you say the same phrase over and over. *Unwrite us back.*"

Their eyes opened. The hollow was pitch-dark, but the sigil threw a faint ember-glow through the bracer's seams, enough to see her face by, close, watchful, afraid for them and not of them, and somehow that was worse than fear would have been.

"It's the country," Sebastian said. "Or what's left of it. Malvane didn't conquer Thornfell—you proved that. He erased it. Struck the name from every record, every map, every memory he could buy or burn. But you can't erase a thing completely. It goes *somewhere*. All those unwritten names, all that struck-out history—it went down. Into the stone. Into the door. It's been waiting a hundred years for someone with the right scar to come read it back aloud."

"And the price?"

"You keep asking that."

"Because you keep not answering."

Sebastian was silent a long time. Outside, the storm screamed and the cairn stones ticked as they froze and cracked. Inside, the sigil glowed, patient as a banked fire.

"When I was fourteen," they said finally, "the company took me in. Orla fed me. Taught me routes and knots and how to lie with my whole face. Before that I had nothing—no name that mattered, no past worth keeping. I built everything I am out of what the company gave me. Roads. Loyalties. Eleven years of being *useful*, which was the closest thing to loved I knew how to be." They flexed the burning hand. "And now I'm starting to understand that the door doesn't want my blood. Blood's cheap. It wants the other thing. It wants what I *chose*. Every road I know. Every face. The whole architecture of the life I built on top of the ruins of the one that was taken from me. That's the toll, Isolde. I can feel it in the way the visions work—they show me the throne room, and each time, a little less of *me* comes back from watching it. Yesterday I couldn't remember the name of the innkeeper at Marrow's Rest who saved my life twice. Today I had to think hard to remember what Orla smells like—pipe smoke and horse liniment—and I've known that woman twenty years."

Isolde's hand found theirs in the dark. Held on.

"Then we find another way," she said. "There's always another way. I've spent my life in archives, Sebastian. There is *always* another text, another reading, another door behind the door."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then I'll stand in front of it with you and we'll refuse together, and we'll die refusing, and it will be the most honest thing either of us has ever done."

Something in Sebastian's chest cracked along an old fault line. They turned their hand over and gripped hers properly, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the storm shouted itself hoarse outside, and the sigil burned on between them like a third presence neither had invited.

"There are three cairns left," Sebastian said at last. "Past the ninth, the track drops into a valley the maps call nothing at all. There are people there. People who remember."

"Remember what?"

"Thornfell. Without knowing why. My grandmother's grandmother's stories kind of remember. If any part of this inheritance is going to be survivable, I think it starts there." They closed their eyes. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we spend the last of the easy miles."

* * *

The eighth cairn nearly killed them.

The storm had passed but left the mountain treacherous, glazed with ice, and the track beyond the seventh cairn crossed a field of old avalanche debris where the footing shifted under every step. They were halfway across when the slope below Sebastian's boot let go—a slab of ice-crusted scree the size of a table, sliding, taking them with it. They went down hard, rolled, slammed into the eighth cairn itself, and the ancient stones held—held, and *burned*. The sigil flared so hot through the bracer that Sebastian smelled their own singed hair, and the vision came like a blow to the skull: the throne room, the door, and this time the door was *open a crack*, a line of light the color of dawn falling through the split-tree seam, and from inside the light a voice that was not a voice said, in the pressure-language of the silent god:

*Almost. One more witness. Come and be remembered—or go and be forgotten. Those are the only doors left, heir.*

They woke on their back against the cairn with Isolde kneeling over them, snow melting on her face, her hands pressed to either side of their jaw, saying their name over and over like a rope thrown to someone in deep water. Above her the sky was clearing to a hard, clean blue, and the ninth cairn stood on the ridge ahead, the last of the nine silent witnesses, wearing a cap of fresh snow like a mourner's hood.

"How long?" Sebastian rasped.

"Minutes. Maybe ten. You fell and then you just—stopped. Breathing shallow, eyes open, seeing straight through me." Her voice shook and she hated it shaking and he could see her hating it. "I felt your pulse and it was steady. Steady, Sebastian. Like your body belonged to something else while it was gone."

They sat up slowly. The ache in their arm had changed character overnight—no longer a burn now but a *pull*, a tidal tug toward the ridge, toward whatever slept past the ninth cairn. And with the pull came a clarity that frightened them more than the pain ever had: they could feel the door now. Not imagine it. *Feel* it, the way you feel a lit window across a dark field. It knew they were coming. It had always known. The book had known a century ago. Somewhere in the ash and the unwritten names, a kingdom had been keeping a seat warm.

"Isolde." They got to their feet, tested their legs, found them willing. "When we get past the ninth cairn, there'll be a descent into the valley. Half a day, maybe less. Whatever happens after that—whatever I become or stop being—I need you to promise me something."

"Don't ask me that."

"I need you to write it down. Everything. The true record, the one you said you'd start. Not the prophecy—your account. What happened. Who I was. Because if the door takes my memories, someone has to keep them, and I'd rather it be you than a god who's spent a hundred years learning patience."

Isolde looked at him for a long moment. Then she took out the little oilskin packet where she kept her notes, and her charcoal stub, and she wrote one line on the last clean page without showing it to him, and folded it away.

"What does it say?" Sebastian asked.

"It says I'll keep your memories," she said. "But I'm writing them in pencil, Sebastian. Because I intend to give every single one of them back."

They crested the ridge together as the afternoon light went long and gold. The ninth cairn stood at the top, taller than the others, older, its stones fitted with a care that smugglers' luck-stones had never needed—and set into its crown, half-buried by centuries, was a capstone carved with the split-tree sigil, the same mark that burned on Sebastian's wrist, the same mark that sealed the door in the ash-filled throne room. As Sebastian laid their palm against it, the pull in their arm resolved at last into something unmistakable, something almost gentle:

Below them, the valley opened—green, sheltered, threaded with woodsmoke. And from somewhere beneath its roots, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the Door of Ash was knocking back.
