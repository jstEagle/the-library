## 12. What Isolde Erased

The pass narrowed as they climbed, and the world narrowed with it.

Isolde had never thought of herself as someone who feared small places—she had spent half her life in the archive stacks of Callowmere, in corridors of shelving so tight two scholars could not pass without turning sideways—but this was different. Here the mountain itself pressed close on either side, walls of grey granite streaked with old rust-colored seams, and the sky was a ribbon of pale blue overhead, thin as a blade. The wind came down through the notch ahead of them with a sound like a long exhaled breath, and every time it touched her she flinched, though she would not have admitted it.

Sebastian walked ahead. They always walked ahead now. Since the visions had begun—the sigil burning on their wrist, their eyes going distant and terrible mid-stride—they had taken point without discussion, as if the mountain were speaking to them and only to them, and Isolde was permitted to follow in the wake of that conversation like a servant outside a council door.

She hated it. She hated how much she understood it.

They made camp at dusk in a hollow where the rock curved back against the wind, a pocket of shelter no larger than a chapel, floored with dry pine needles that smelled of resin and cold. Sebastian built the fire small—a smuggler's fire, cupped in stone, invisible from more than twenty paces—and sat across from it sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Isolde watched the blade whisper over the whetstone and knew, with the particular dread of an archivist who has read enough histories to know how confessions end, that tonight was the night.

There would not be another. She could feel that too. Tomorrow they would reach whatever the sigil was dragging Sebastian toward—the Door, or the ruin of it, or the truth beneath it—and after tomorrow there would be no version of this story in which her silence survived. Either she told him, or the mountain told him, or Halloway's people dragged it out of her corpse. Those were the only pages left unwritten.

"Sebastian." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. She tried again. "Sebastian. There's something you need to hear before we go any further."

The knife stopped. He didn't look up—not at first. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadow, the face of someone who had been bracing for bad news since the day he found his own name in a dead man's book. "You've been rehearsing that for three days," he said quietly. "I've watched you open your mouth and close it eleven times. I counted."

Of course he had. He was a mercenary; counting was what mercenaries did. "Then you know it isn't nothing."

"I know it isn't nothing." He set the knife down at last, deliberately, the way a man lays down a weapon before a parley, and looked at her across the fire. His eyes were the worst part—steady, patient, already grieving something he hadn't been told yet. "Whatever it is, Isolde. Say it."

*Say it.* As if it were one thing. As if it could be said the way weather is reported, briefly, and then moved past. She wrapped her arms around her knees and stared into the flames because looking at him while she said it would be impossible, and began the way she always began—with the record, with the facts, with the safe dry architecture of a document.

"You know I worked in the Verane Archive. My family's archive. You know my father was its keeper, and that I was trained from childhood in paleography, in cipher, in the reading of marginalia." The words came out level, recited, a litany. "What you don't know—what I have never told anyone—is how the archive stayed open. Through the war years. Through the purges. When Malvane's censors came to Callowmere and every other collection in the city was sealed or burned, ours kept its doors open. Our scholars kept their stipends. Our lamps stayed lit."

Sebastian said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the rocks with small patient fingers.

"There was a man," Isolde said. "He came in the winter I was nineteen. He didn't wear a uniform—he wore good wool and a scholar's satchel, and he spoke to my father like a colleague, which was worse than any threat would have been. He had a list. Not a long one. Names of books. Titles, mostly histories, some genealogies, a few maps. And a single instruction that ran underneath all of them like a watermark: *these works are to be corrected.*"

"Corrected," Sebastian repeated. The word came out flat.

"That's what they called it. Correction." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It's such a gentle word, isn't it? You correct a student. You correct a proof. What it meant, in practice, was this: you took the volume, and you turned to the page, and you took your knife—" her hand rose unconsciously toward her own throat— "and you scraped. Ink lifts off vellum beautifully if you're careful. Papyrus takes a damp cloth and salt. Paper is harder; paper wants pumice. And when the name was gone, you wrote what they wanted in its place, in a hand matched to the original, in ink mixed to match the original, and then you aged it with smoke and oil of gall until even *I* couldn't have told you which line had been there first."

The firelight moved on Sebastian's face. He still hadn't spoken. She wished he would shout. Shouting would have been a mercy.

"My father refused," she went on, faster now, the litany breaking apart into something rawer. "My father was a good man, Sebastian, he was the best man I ever knew, and he told the censor that the archive's purpose was preservation, not revision, and that he would burn the whole collection himself before he let it be falsified. And the censor smiled—I remember the smile, I will *always* remember the smile—and thanked him for his principles, and left, and three days later my father was arrested for sedition. For a letter. A forged letter, in his own hand, matched by experts, aged with smoke and oil of gall. Do you understand? They used our own craft against us. They had learned it from us."

"And they gave him back," Sebastian said. It wasn't a question. His voice had gone very quiet, very controlled, the voice of a man walking carefully across ice he can hear cracking. "In exchange for the work being done."

"In exchange for the work being done *by me.*" She heard her own breath shake. "They didn't want him. He was old; killing him bought them nothing but a martyr. What they wanted was a Verane. Someone whose name opened every private collection in the free cities, someone whose skill no one would question, someone young enough to do the work for thirty years. They wanted the daughter. So I sat in my father's chair, under his seal, with his ransom folded into the terms like a knife between the pages, and I corrected."

"How many?" Sebastian asked.

And there it was. The question she had built her whole confession around avoiding, and he asked it gently, which was infinitely worse than if he'd screamed it.

"I don't know," she whispered. "That's the honest answer. I stopped counting in the second year because counting was a way of keeping score and keeping score was a way of pretending I might still win. Hundreds of documents. Thousands of lines. Every mention of certain names, certain battles, certain—"

She stopped. Because she had known, from the moment she decoded the prophecy in the Callowmere reading room, from the moment she saw the word *Thornfell* standing intact inside a century-old text like a bone unbroken in flesh, that this conversation was coming. She had known it on the road, watching him sleep. She had known it an hour ago, walking behind him up the pass. But knowing a thing and saying it were separated by a distance no map measured, and crossing it now, she found her voice simply failed.

"Thornfell," Sebastian said.

Not loudly. Just the name, set down between them on the pine needles like a blade laid flat.

"Yes," Isolde said.

"How many times?"

"I don't—" She closed her eyes. Made herself be exact, because exactness was the only penance she had ever been permitted. "In the Verane Archive alone? Four hundred and six instances across ninety-one documents. Chronicles. Tax rolls. Marriage registers. A hymnal. There was a children's primer, Sebastian. A *primer*, with Thornfell in a list of kingdoms children were meant to memorize, and I scraped it out and wrote *Vhalen* in its place, and some child learned Vhalen instead, and grew up, and taught it to their own child, and that is a wound I put in the world that will never close, because you cannot recall a lesson once it has been learned. That is the thing about erasure that no one tells you. It doesn't kill the past. It kills everyone who *would have remembered*, forever downstream, generation after generation, and you are the one who swung the knife, and the knife keeps swinging after you put it down."

The wind came down the pass and the fire bent and righted itself. Sebastian had gone very still. Stillness, she had learned on the road, was the most dangerous thing about him. Orla's people shouted and swore and laughed; Sebastian went still, and the stillness meant the part of him that had been raised to survive was doing arithmetic.

"The book," he said at last. "The prophecy. The one with my name in it. It wasn't corrected."

"No."

"Why?" And now something moved under the quiet, something with heat in it. "If you were so thorough. If you scraped four hundred and six names out of ninety-one documents. Why does that one survive? Why does the one book in the world that says *Thornfell* and gives me a birthright and a door—why is that the one thing your knife missed?"

"Because I hid it." The words tore coming out, and she let them tear. "Because by the third year I couldn't sleep, and I started—there's a practice among archivists, an old one, forbidden, called *countermarking*. When you're forced to destroy a text, you copy it first, in cipher, in fragments, and you scatter the copies where no censor would think to look. Inside bindings. Under floor stones. In the margins of innocuous ledgers. I told myself it was scholarship. I told myself I was preserving evidence for a future restoration. But I knew what I was really doing, Sebastian. I was building myself a door out of the room I'd locked myself in. The prophecy was the last thing I countermarked before I ran. I copied it entire, ciphered it, and slipped it into a consignment of smuggled goods bound for the mountain passes, because I thought—if there's anywhere in the world Malvane's hands haven't reached, it's the roads men like you walk. And then I requested the original through the archive network, to verify my cipher, and the request itself was the signal. That's how they found me. That's how Halloway found *you*. Everything that has hunted us since the day we met—"

She stopped. Swallowed. Finished it.

"—started with my knife. Or ended with it. I don't know anymore. I genuinely don't know whether I'm the reason Thornfell died or the reason it gets to live, and I have lain awake every night on this road trying to decide which, and there is no answer, and I am so tired, Sebastian. I am so tired of carrying it alone."

The silence that followed was the longest of her life.

Sebastian rose. Slowly, the way men rise when they aren't sure their legs will hold, and stood at the edge of the firelight with his back half-turned, and Isolde watched the muscles of his shoulders and understood that he was not deciding whether to forgive her. He was deciding whether she was still the person he had crossed a country beside, or whether that person had been, all along, one of Malvane's instruments wearing a scholar's face. Whether every kindness she had shown him had been a censor's patience. Whether the woman who decoded his name out of a dead book had also, with her own hands, killed everything that name belonged to.

"You should have told me at the waystone," he said finally. His voice was terrible in its mildness. "When you found me. Before I trusted you. Before I—" He cut himself off, and Isolde felt the unsaid word land between them like a dropped stone: *before I loved you.*

"If I'd told you at the waystone, you'd have left," she said. "Or worse—you'd have stayed, and hated yourself for staying, and I would have watched you do it. I told myself I'd tell you when we were safe. Then I told myself when we reached the pass. Then I told myself after the Door. I am an archivist, Sebastian. Lying is my native language. I don't mean the false records—I mean the true ones, the ones I keep about myself, the ones with the dates adjusted and the inconvenient pages scraped clean. I have been correcting my own history for six years and I am apparently no better at it than I ever was for them."

"Four hundred and six." He turned then, and his eyes in the firelight were the eyes of a man looking at a battlefield. "I grew up on Orla's knee hearing stories about the border kingdoms. About the wars. There was a hole in every telling, do you understand? A shape. Songs that repeated a chorus about a kingdom none of us could name. Roads that ran to nowhere and everyone pretended not to notice. I thought I was mad. I thought the whole world was mad. And it turns out the world was fine—it was *corrected*—and the hand that held the knife was teaching me to read prophecy by firelight."

"I know what I am," Isolde said. Her eyes were burning now, and she let them. "I am the reason your country has no grave. I am the reason your name fits in no register. If the Door needs an heir, it needs one because people like me made heirs impossible. I inherited the guilt the same way you inherited the crown, and I would give anything—do you hear me, *anything*—to have refused them. To have let my father hang. To have been the daughter who chose four hundred and six dead names over one living man. But I wasn't that daughter. I was nineteen and afraid and they had him in irons, and I scraped, and I scraped, and I scraped, and every night I tell myself the ledger balances because I saved the prophecy, because I saved *you*, and every morning I know better. Ledgers don't balance. That's the first thing they teach you. You just keep the accounts honestly, and carry them, and die owing."

Sebastian crossed the hollow in three strides.

She flinched—she would deny it later, but she flinched, full-body, a flinch six years in the making—and his hands closed not on her throat but on her shoulders, gripping hard, shaking her once, and his face was inches from hers and wet-eyed and furious and lost.

"Don't you *dare*," he said, low and shaking, "tell me about ledgers. Don't you dare stand there and make it clean. My company sold me two days ago. My god has been silent for six years. My name belongs to a country that exists because you *unmade* it, and I have been walking toward a door that opens for me because of a crime committed by the only person in this world I—" His jaw locked. The word fought him. She watched it fight him, watched him lose. "I love you," he said, ragged, like something torn out rather than given. "I love you, and I don't know if I'm allowed to anymore, and that is the cruelest thing anyone has ever done to me, Isolde, and Malvane's inquisitors burned a village around my ears last week. Do you understand what you've done? You've made it so that every time I look at you I see the reason for everything, and every time I turn away from you I lose the only thing that's real. You didn't erase Thornfell. You erased the person I was three days ago, the one who trusted easily, and *he's* not coming back either."

"Then hate me," she said. "Hate me and keep walking. Use me. I'm the best map you'll ever have—I know the censorship patterns, I know which records Halloway trusts, I know how Malvane thinks because I was his pen for six years. Take all of that and leave the rest of me at the treeline. I won't follow you past the Door if you tell me not to. I swear it on my father's grave, and my father's grave is one of the few true things I have left."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The fire burned low. Above the notch of the pass, the first stars were coming out, cold and indifferent, and somewhere far below and behind them—too far to hear, too near to forget—the world they had fled went on believing in a map with a hole in it.

Then Sebastian's grip changed. It loosened, finger by finger, and became something else—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not for a long time, but a decision. His forehead came down to rest against hers, and his breath shuddered out of him, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and raw and final.

"We keep the accounts honestly," he said. "Both of us. From here to the Door. No more corrections. Whatever the sigil shows me, whatever the mountain asks, you hear it first, and I hear your guilt, and neither of us gets to scrape a single line. Can you live like that?"

"No," Isolde said honestly. "But I can walk like that."

Something that was almost a laugh broke out of him, brief and wounded, and he pulled back just far enough to look at her, and in his eyes the fury was still there—it would always be there now, she understood that; it was part of the account—but beneath it was the other thing, the stubborn, unkillable thing, the thing that had carried him through betrayal and desertion and burning bridges and had somehow, impossibly, chosen her twice.

"Then we walk," he said.

He was turning back toward the fire—toward the bedroll, toward the small mercy of exhausted sleep—when he stopped. All of him stopped, the way he stopped when the sigil spoke, and his hand rose slowly to his wrist, and the light that leaked between his fingers was not the fire's orange but a deep ember-red, pulsing, urgent, faster than any heartbeat.

"Isolde," he said, and his voice had gone strange. "It's never done this before. It's pulling. Downhill. Toward the valley."

Toward the green valley below the pass, threaded with woodsmoke, where faint lights were beginning to kindle in the dusk—one window, then ten, then dozens, a village lighting its lamps against the dark in a country that officially did not exist.

Toward the village with no name.
