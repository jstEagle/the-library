## 14. Halloway Arrives Early

Inquisitor Halloway had learned, over thirty years of service to the Usurper's truth, that the most efficient cruelty was the kind that arrived early.

Everyone expected him at the valley mouth. The free cities' councils had been told—by his own careful leaks—that Malvane's vanguard would reach the border in eleven days. Eleven days gave the merchants time to argue, to hoard, to betray one another in committee. Eleven days gave the heir time to run, and running men made mistakes, and mistakes left trails.

But Halloway himself did not travel with armies. Armies were for nations. He traveled with forty outriders and a wagon of lamp-oil, and he moved through the high passes like a rumor with a schedule.

He stood now on the shoulder of the southern ridge, watching the village below through a spyglass of blackened brass. It was a poor thing of a place—thirty roofs, maybe forty, smoke rising thin and domestic from chimneys cut into the mountainside as if the houses had grown there rather than been built. No name on any map he owned. No name on any map at all, which was precisely why it interested him.

"Nothing," said Sergeant Vess, beside him. She was a solid woman with a crossbow slung across her back like a farming tool, which in a sense it was. "Scouts say no soldiers, no sigil-bearers, just farmers. And an old woman who talks too much."

"Farmers keep granaries," Halloway said. "Granaries feed fugitives. And old women—" he lowered the spyglass, "—old women are where countries hide when they think no one is looking."

Vess frowned. "You believe the book's here, then."

"I believe," Halloway said mildly, "that a blind priestess sent two people into these mountains with nothing but faith and a burning wrist, and that faith requires shelter, and shelter in winter means villages." He handed her the spyglass. "I also believe that whether or not the book is here does not matter. What matters is what happens after we burn it."

"Which is?"

"The heir arrives to smoke and ash and dead friends instead of a map and open arms. Grief narrows a man, Sergeant. A grieving heir runs for the Door alone, or not at all—and either way, he stops being a symbol the free cities can rally around. A martyr in the mountains is worth less than a living puppet on a throne. We simply need him small again."

He turned from the ridge. Below, the first lights of evening were kindling in the village windows, warm and gold and utterly unaware.

"Begin at dusk," he said. "Take the old woman alive. She'll know things the grandmother knew. Burn the rest."

* * *

Sebastian smelled the smoke before they saw it.

They had gone up, following the humming stone deeper into the valley—the old woman, Maren, had pressed its location on them at moonrise, saying *the country keeps its heart where the snow never melts*—and for three hours they had climbed through pine dark with the sigil singing against Sebastian's wrist like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Isolde walked close behind, one hand on Sebastian's pack strap, where the wrapped book rode. Their only map. Their only proof. The only text in the world that named the Door of Ash and the road to it.

Then the wind shifted, and brought them the smell of burning thatch and burning wool and something underneath both that Sebastian knew from a hundred battlefields and had hoped never to smell again in a place with chimneys lit for supper.

"No," Isolde breathed.

Sebastian was already running.

The village came apart below them in pieces: a roof collapsing in a gout of orange; riders moving between houses with torches, unhurried, methodical, the way men work when they have done this many times and been paid by the house; figures stumbling into the square with their hands full of children instead of weapons. And at the center, tied to the wellpost, Maren—alive, they could see even from the ridge, alive and upright, while a woman in Malvane grey held a brand near her face and asked questions the wind tore away.

Sebastian's hand went to the sword. Every muscle, every year of training, every oath screamed down the slope toward the riders.

Isolde caught the pack strap. "There are forty of them."

"There are thirty of them down there and I've killed more with worse odds."

"And I'm not a soldier, and the book burns if you drop the pack, and if you die on that hillside the Door stays shut forever." Her voice cracked but held. "Sebastian. *Please.*"

For one long breath Sebastian stood in the falling snow with the firelight painting the whole valley red, and understood—with a clarity that felt like a blade sliding between ribs—that Isolde was right, and that being right had never felt so much like cowardice.

Then Maren looked up.

Whether she saw them or simply knew—they would never learn—she turned her face toward the ridge, toward the dark where they crouched, and she shouted something into the wind. One word, thrown like a stone:

"*GO!*"

And then, before the soldier with the brand could stop her, before Sebastian could move or speak or do anything at all, the old woman twisted toward the well behind her and threw herself into it. The sound carried up the mountain. So did the silence after.

Something in Sebastian broke cleanly, the way ice breaks off a roof edge—no warning, all weight. They did not go down the slope with a sword. They went down the slope along the goat track Maren had shown them that morning, the one that let out behind the granary, because grief without direction is just screaming, and Sebastian Reyes had never once in their life permitted themselves the luxury of screaming when there was work to be done.

* * *

The granary was already burning at one corner. Inside, half the village had done what mountain folk have always done when soldiers come: gone to ground. Cellars. Root caches. The hollow spaces under floors that a land which has been erased once learns to dig without being taught.

Sebastian came through the back wall's broken door with Isolde behind them and found twenty-three people huddled in the dark among sacks of barley, eyes shining, children gagged with their own mothers' scarves so they wouldn't cry out.

"The tunnel," Sebastian said, to the oldest man there—a stooped fellow with a smith's forearms. "Maren said there was a way out under the north field. Where?"

The man stared. "Who—"

"*Where?*"

"Under the cairn. Third stone from the left has an iron ring. But the field—there are riders on the field—"

"Then you'll wait until there aren't." Sebastian turned to Isolde, and everything they said next was said fast and low, in the voice of someone dividing a body into tasks because feeling would come later, much later, or never. "Take them into the far cellar. When I pull the riders off the field, count to a hundred, get them out, take the track east to the shrine ruins. Don't wait for me. Don't come back for me."

"You're going to give me the book first." Isolde's hands were already at the pack straps—not asking. "Because you're going onto that field to die, and the book can't die with you."

"It's our only map—"

"It's the *country's* map, and I'm the archivist, and this is the one thing I know how to do better than anyone alive." She pulled the wrapped bundle free and pressed it inside her coat, against her heart, and looked at Sebastian with eyes that had cried all night two days ago and would not cry now. "Go be stupid. I'll keep the words safe."

Sebastian kissed her—hard, brief, like sealing a letter—and went out through the smoke.

What followed took four minutes and cost Sebastian two fingers' worth of skin, a rib that would ache until spring, and something quieter than blood. They came out of the granary's burning side like something the fire itself had coughed up, took the first rider off his horse with the flat of the sword and the second off his feet entirely, and then ran—loudly, visibly, sword ringing off stones, shouting in the company cant that every mercenary in Malvane's pay would know—straight across the north field, drawing six riders after them like sparks after a struck flint.

They led those riders a mile into the pines using roads that existed only in Sebastian's feet, dropped two of them into a ravine that had no bottom anyone had ever measured, lost the rest in a streambed, and circled back through the dark with blood in their mouth and Maren's last word still lodged under their sternum like a swallowed coal.

By the time they reached the north field, the cairn stood open and empty, the tunnel breathing cold air into the night, and the village above burned freely, thoroughly, completely—thatch and timber and the shrine to no god that had stood in the square since before anyone could remember, all of it going up together into a column of orange visible, Halloway noted with professional satisfaction from the ridge, for ten miles in every direction.

* * *

Halloway watched the column of smoke and permitted himself a small, dry pleasure, the way a miser permits himself one coin.

Sergeant Vess stood at his shoulder, wiping soot from her gauntlets. "No sign of the heir or the archivist. The old woman's dead—took her own life at the well before we could question her. Villagers unaccounted for, likely fled east through some tunnel. And the granary cellars were empty except for grain." She hesitated. "One thing, Inquisitor. A rider swears he saw a figure on the north field drawing us off. Company cant, sword-work like a professional. If the heir was here—"

"He was here." Halloway folded the spyglass with a click. "And now he has nothing. No book, no map, no village, no allies, and a graveyard where his welcome used to be. Tomorrow he will be exactly what I need him to be: a frightened man with a burning wrist, running blind toward a door he can no longer find." He smiled without warmth. "Grief, Sergeant, is the most reliable instrument ever forged. It never misses, and it always points inward."

Vess looked at the burning village a moment longer. "And if he finds another way? The country under the map—it woke, didn't it. The stone was humming when we rode in. My scouts heard it."

Halloway paused. For the first time that night, something moved behind his eyes that was not satisfaction—something older and colder, the look of a man who has read every forbidden text in the Usurper's archives and remembers one passage he pretends not to.

"Stone hums," he said finally. "Ash remembers. That is why we burn, Sergeant. Fire is the only argument ash respects." He turned away from the smoke. "Break camp. We ride for the barrow-marks before dawn. If the heir wants a door, let him find it with my shadow already standing in it."

* * *

They buried what they could bury at dawn.

Twenty-three villagers had come out of the tunnel alive. Three had not come out at all—an old man's heart, a child's fever that the flight had stoked, and a young shepherd whose name Sebastian made themselves repeat aloud four times so they would never lose it. The others stood in the snow among the smoking foundations, silent in the way of mountain people, who have inherited grief the way lowlanders inherit land.

Isolde stood beside Sebastian at the graves, the book heavy against her chest beneath her coat. Neither of them had spoken since the field. There was nothing yet to say that wasn't ash.

It was the smith, Harl, who finally broke the silence. He came to stand before Sebastian, and he did not kneel, and he did not curse, and somehow that was worse than either.

"Maren kept stories," he said. "Her grandmother's grandmother's stories. About a kingdom under the snow, and a door, and a family with our faces." He looked at the ruin of the village, at the shrine's one surviving wall, blackened, on which—now that the plaster had burned away—old carved letters stood revealed in the morning light, worn nearly smooth, legible only to someone who had spent her life reading erased things.

Isolde saw them at the same moment Sebastian did.

She crossed the square slowly, snow crunching, and put her bare fingers against the carved wall, tracing lines that had waited under whitewash for a hundred years for exactly this fire to strip them bare. Her lips moved. Reading. And then she stopped reading, and went very pale, and very still.

"Sebastian," she said, and her voice sounded like someone speaking from the bottom of a well. "It's not a prayer. It's not a memorial." She turned, and the light of the burning village's last embers was in her eyes. "It's directions."

She looked back at the wall—at the ruined houses, the collapsed roofs, the fallen beams lying in the snow in patterns no one had chosen and everyone had survived.

"The village isn't destroyed," Isolde whispered. "It's *written*. They burned the map to save it. Sebastian—the Door of Ash was never in the book. The book was only the key to *reading this*."

Behind them, in the deep of the valley, beneath the snow that never melted, the humming stone sang one clear note into the waking dark—and far to the south, riding hard through the passes with forty outriders and a wagon of lamp-oil, Inquisitor Halloway felt the note pass through his chest like a cold hand, and spurred his horse faster.
