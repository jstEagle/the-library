## 17. Malvane's Army at the Valley Mouth

The vanguard came down out of the high pass in the last grey hour before dawn, and the valley below them was still asleep.

Inquisitor Halloway sat his horse at the head of the column and did not sleep, because he had not slept in four days and had found, long ago, that sleep was a habit of men who believed the world would still be there in the morning. He watched the free cities' border stones emerge from the dark one by one—waist-high granite markers, moss-eaten, half-forgotten—and thought about how borders were only a kind of sentence. *This far and no farther.* Sentences could be edited. He had built a career on editing.

Behind him, two thousand soldiers moved with the discipline Malvane's coin bought: outriders fanning wide along the valley walls, supply wagons groaning on the old salt road, torches hooded against discovery that no longer mattered. The border was behind them now. They were inside the lands of the free cities, and by the time any council in Callowmere finished arguing about whether to believe it, the vanguard would be three days closer to everything Halloway wanted.

He did not want the cities themselves. Cities fell. What he wanted was already in this valley, somewhere ahead—a mercenary with a burning sigil on her wrist, an archivist with ink-stained fingers, a book of prophecies, and a door. Halloway had read enough of the recovered texts to know what the Door of Ash was worth. Kingdoms could be conquered twice: once with armies, and once with stories. Whoever controlled the door controlled which story got told.

"Scouts report smoke to the northwest," said Captain Veyren, reining up beside him. "A village. Small. No name on our maps."

"No name," Halloway repeated softly. "Of course." Everything in this valley had been struck from maps a century ago; that was rather the point of it. "We'll stop there. Armies march better after they've eaten, and I find villages are excellent teachers." He smiled at Veyren's blank look. "They teach people what happens to those who shelter fugitives. The lesson travels faster than we do."

They rode down into the sleeping valley, and behind them the sky over the pass began, very faintly, to lighten—the color of ash, the color of things about to burn.

* * *

Orla Venn had made her peace with damnation years ago, or told herself she had, and it took until midmorning for her to understand that she had never made peace with anything at all.

She rode at the rear of the column because she could no longer bear the front of it. Her company—what remained of the Grey Talons, forty-three riders who had followed her banner for eleven years—rode in loose formation around her, and they had been quiet for two days in the way soldiers are quiet when they have seen something their oaths cannot digest.

It had happened at the ford, yesterday evening. Three of her own scouts—Brannic, Sefa, and young Tam with the crooked smile—had gone ahead to map the valley roads, as she'd ordered them to weeks ago, back when she still believed she was playing both sides. Halloway's outriders had taken them at the river. And when Orla rode up demanding answers, Halloway had not even bothered to lie.

"They were carrying messages," he'd said, in that reasonable voice he used the way other men used knives. "To your former charge. You understand I can't allow lines of communication to remain open."

"They're my scouts."

"They were," Halloway agreed pleasantly. "Now they're evidence."

She had stood there while they bound the three of them—her people, children she'd fed from her own stores in hard winters—and Halloway had watched her with that patient, terrible attention, waiting to see what she would do. And she had done nothing. That was the wound she kept returning to, probing it like a loose tooth: she had done *nothing*. She had turned her horse away and ridden back down the column, telling herself that forty-three lives outweighed three, that Sebastian had chosen their path, that legitimacy for the Talons was worth this price and more.

Then this morning, at the village with no name, Halloway had burned the prisoners.

Not hanged them. Not ransomed them. He had built a pyre in the village square in front of the assembled villagers, called it a purification, and put Brannic and Sefa and Tam on it alive, because—as he explained to Orla afterward, almost gently, as if she were a student who had nearly grasped the lesson—mercy was a currency, and spending it carelessly devalued it. Fear held its value forever.

Orla had watched the smoke rise. She had watched Tam's screaming stop. And something in her that had been bending for months, bending under the weight of every compromise she'd dressed up as strategy, finally broke clean through.

She didn't announce it. Announcement was for people who wanted applause. She simply walked to her tent, took the sealed pardon Malvane's emissary had given her—the document promising the Grey Talons land, title, a place in the new order—and fed it into her cookfire. Then she went among her riders, one by one, and told them the truth: that she had sold them, that the sale had bought nothing, that she was riding northwest after two fugitives to warn them, and that anyone who wanted to stay and collect Malvane's coin instead was free to do so and would hear no word of blame from her.

Forty-three saddles creaked. Not one rider moved toward the column.

"Hells," muttered Doss, her sergeant, a scarred woman who had once broken Orla's nose over a card debt. "Took you long enough."

They slipped away in the afternoon, cutting north along a shepherd's track Halloway's maps wouldn't show, riding hard through the long light, and Orla did not let herself think about what waited at the end of the ride. She knew what she was. She knew what she had done. Redemption was a word for priests and archivists. All she had was speed, and guilt, and the hope—thin as winter ice—that she could reach Sebastian before Halloway's patience reached its end.

She owed them a warning. She owed them more than a warning. But warnings were all she had left, and so she spent them like a dying woman spends gold.

* * *

Sebastian felt the Door before they saw it.

They had come down off the Pass of Nine Cairns into the hidden valley as the sun set, Isolde silent beside him, both of them hollowed out by the road and by the things confessed between them—her pen, his desertion, the whole ledger of small betrayals that two people carry when they love each other honestly. The barrow-mound rose ahead of them above the heather, older than the maps, older than the erasure, and the sigil on Sebastian's wrist had stopped burning and started *singing*, a low note felt in the bones rather than heard.

And beneath the mound, in a chamber where the first king slept in stone, they had found it: the Door of Ash, vast and seamless, black stone shot through with veins of faint gold, waiting. It had opened for no prayer, no key, no blood. It had opened, the priestess's words coming back to him now like a tide, *for an heir willing to give up what they chose.*

Sebastian stood before it with his hand raised and understood, finally, why Vessia had been silent all these years. Not absent. Waiting. A god who had become a place, and places wait to be entered, and entry cost everything a person was.

He had begun to turn—to say Isolde's name, to say goodbye properly, to give her the dignity of being refused to her face—

And then he saw them. Riders cresting the eastern ridge. Small, distant, moving fast.

Halloway. Hours, not days.

"Isolde." His voice came out cracked. "Isolde, we're out of time."

* * *

Halloway arrived at the valley mouth as the light died, and he arrived the way he did everything: without hurry.

He drew up the vanguard in parade order on the flat ground where the valley narrowed, two thousand men forming ranks in the dusk with torches kindling down the line like a lit fuse, and he did not advance. Advancement was for officers who needed battles. Halloway needed only an audience.

"The valley has one mouth," he observed to Veyren, gesturing with one gloved hand at the narrowing walls. "One road in. Whatever door they've found is at the far end, and doors, Captain, are chokepoints—for whoever holds the ground outside them." He dismounted, accepted a cup of wine he would not drink, and settled onto a camp stool as comfortably as a man at a wedding. "Send a herald under a white banner. Offer terms. The heir comes to me freely, the archivist walks away untouched, and every soul in this valley lives to see harvest. Let the offer stand until midnight."

"And if they refuse?"

Halloway looked out at the darkening valley, at the distant smudge of the barrow-mound against the last light, and smiled the smile that had unmade councils and broken kingdoms without a single sword drawn.

"Then at midnight we begin burning the valley from this end to that one, farmstead by farmstead, and every refugee these free cities sent to shelter here will learn precisely how much their councils' caution cost them." He sipped nothing. "I don't need to catch them, Captain. I need only to be *believed*. Belief does the rest."

The herald rode out under the white banner, and the valley held its breath.

* * *

Orla hit the eastern ridge at full gallop in the blue hour after sunset, and below her she saw it all at once: Halloway's camp strung across the valley mouth like a barbed necklace, two thousand fires, the white banner of parley fluttering—and beyond it, deep in the valley, a glow that was not fire. Gold-grey, sourceless, steady. Light like remembered things.

The Door was opening. Or trying to.

"Faster," she breathed, and drove her horse down the scree.

They cut wide around the army's picket line, forty-three riders moving like smoke along the valley wall, and when a picket finally challenged them out of the dark, Doss put a crossbow bolt through his shoulder and they were past, committed now, arrows singing after them into the night. One took her mare through the neck. Orla felt the animal stumble, felt herself falling, rolled through the heather and came up running, and ran the rest of the way because there was no time for anything else.

She crested the last rise before the barrow and saw them: two figures silhouetted against that impossible golden seam in the mountainside, a man with his hand raised toward a door, a woman reaching for him.

"SEBASTIAN!" Her voice tore out of her rawer than any battle-cry she'd ever given. "HALLOWAY'S IN THE VALLEY! HE'S OFFERED TERMS AND HE'LL BURN IT ALL AT MIDNIGHT!"

They turned. Even at this distance, even in that strange light, she saw Sebastian recognize her—saw the whole history of it cross their face, the raising, the training, the betrayal, the arrows. Isolde stepped halfway in front of them, arm out, a scholar putting her body between a soldier and her own.

"You brought them here," Isolde shouted back, and there was no accusation in it worse than grief. "You led them—"

"I led them AWAY!" Orla stumbled down the slope, lungs burning, and behind her her riders spread out along the ridge with bows drawn, facing down the valley toward two thousand fires. "Forty-three of us broke from the column tonight. We're all that stands between Halloway and this door, and I am not asking your forgiveness—I'm asking for a post. Give me a wall to hold and I will hold it until my last rider falls, and then I'll hold it myself."

Sebastian came forward slowly, the sigil blazing on their wrist, the Door humming at their back, and looked at the woman who had raised them and sold them in the same breath.

"Why?" was all they asked.

Orla laughed, and it came out broken. "Because I watched a boy burn today who carried my rations and laughed at my jokes, and I did nothing. Because I sold you for a piece of parchment, and the parchment's ash, and I'm the only thing left of what I promised to be." She pulled her sword and drove it point-down into the earth between them—an old gesture, older than the companies, the gesture of a commander surrendering command. "Judge me after. Let me die useful first."

For a long moment Sebastian said nothing. The light from the Door moved across all their faces like water.

Then they looked past her, down the valley, at the torch-line of the army, and Orla watched the child she'd trained become, in the space of a breath, something older than any of them.

"Then we hold the mouth of this valley," Sebastian said quietly, "until everyone who can run, has run. Isolde—" their voice caught, steadied— "the Door opens for me. Only me. Get the villagers ready. Everyone Halloway would burn—they go through."

"And you?" Isolde whispered.

Sebastian turned back to the Door, and the gold-grey light washed over them, and they smiled the saddest smile Orla had ever seen.

"I'm the toll," they said.

Down the valley, a horn sounded—long, cold, deliberate. Midnight was coming. And at the valley mouth, the first torches began to move.
