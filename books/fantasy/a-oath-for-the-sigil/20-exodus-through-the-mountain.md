## 20. Exodus Through the Mountain

The Door did not look like a door anymore.

That was the first thing Isolde understood, standing in the barrow's throat with ash sifting down around her like grey snow: a door is something you open once, and then it is behind you. This was not behind anyone. It stood open in the mountain's heart, and it was *wide*—wider than any gate in Callowmere, wider than the harbor mouths of Vell and Sarnholt—and through it came not darkness but a light like late afternoon in a country she had never seen, gold falling on stone that had waited a hundred years for someone to walk on it.

And through it, already, came the people.

They had been arriving all night. Isolde had gone down into Callowmere with Sebastian's last words still ringing in her ears—*lead them, Isolde, you're the one who knows how to read a road*—and had done the only thing an archivist could do: she had made copies. She had written the truth of the prophecy onto forty sheets of good vellum and nailed them to the doors of every guildhall, every temple, every counting-house in the free cities, and beneath the prophecy she had written three words in letters tall enough to be read from horseback:

THE DOOR IS OPEN.

She had expected disbelief. She had prepared arguments, citations, the whole weight of her training marshaled against the skepticism of merchants who trusted nothing they could not weigh. She had not needed any of it. Because Halloway had done her work for her. The fires, the arrests, the inquisitors walking openly through streets that had been free for three hundred years—the people of the free cities had been waiting, without knowing it, for permission to be afraid in a useful direction. All Isolde had given them was somewhere to put it.

Now she stood at the threshold and watched them come.

They came up the smuggler's roads in the dark, ten thousand of them and more, by ones and twos and whole families with their goods strapped to their backs. Farmers who had abandoned fields their grandfathers had plowed. Glassblowers of Vell carrying nothing but their blowpipes and their children. A Sarnholt shipwright who had burned his own half-finished hull rather than let Malvane's men take it, and whose hands were bandaged so thickly he could not hold his daughter's, so she walked holding his sleeve. Old women. Infants wrapped in quilts. A man carrying a beehive on a pole, the bees drowsy with cold, because he said a country without bees was no country at all, and who was going to argue with him now?

Isolde stood where the tunnel widened into the antechamber before the Door, and she directed them. That was her work tonight—not decoding, not deciphering, but the simpler older art of telling frightened people where to go. Keep moving. Don't stop in the narrow places. Leave the heavy things; the other side has soil. She said it four hundred times, five hundred, until her voice was a rasp and she was saying it in her sleep while awake, and always there were more faces coming out of the dark behind her, lit by torchlight, smudged with ash, looking past her at the golden light with expressions she would carry for the rest of her life.

Hope, she thought, is not a feeling. It is a logistics problem. It is knowing which way to walk.

* * *

Halloway's army reached the valley mouth at dawn.

Isolde knew it before any rider brought word, because the sound changed. For two days the mountain had hummed with footsteps and cartwheels and the low constant murmur of ten thousand people being brave in motion, and then, underneath all of it, from far away down the passes, came a new sound—a deep, rhythmic knocking, like a fist on a door the size of the sky. Drums. War-drums, and the belling of horns, and beneath them a vibration she felt in her back teeth: the massed movement of an army that had stopped pretending to be patient.

The refugees heard it too. Isolde watched it move through the column like wind through wheat—that ripple of heads turning, of mothers' arms tightening, of men setting down their bundles and looking back down the road they had come. And she watched what happened next, which was the thing she would write about later, when she began the true record: nobody ran. They tightened their grip on their children and they kept walking. Faster, yes. But toward the light, not away from it.

A rider found her in the antechamber—one of Orla's scouts, a lean woman with a crossbow slung across her back and blood on her cheek that was not hers. "Inquisitor's vanguard's in the lower pass," she said. "Two thousand foot, lancers behind. Halloway himself with the second wave." She spat. "He's not hurrying. He thinks we're rats in a sack."

"Are we?" Isolde asked.

The scout looked past her, through the antechamber, at the Door standing open and golden, at the line of people filing through it without pause or panic. Something moved across her face that might have been awe trying to disguise itself as professionalism.

"No," she said. "We're a country. Rats don't form columns."

Then she was gone, back down the tunnel, and Isolde turned to the work of moving ten thousand souls through a door between worlds.

* * *

What happened in the valley mouth, Isolde saw only afterward, in fragments, told and retold by witnesses until the story had the polished inevitability of something prophesied—which, she supposed, in a book a century old, perhaps it had been.

Halloway's vanguard came up the salt road expecting stragglers and found instead the Pass of Nine Cairns held against them. Not by soldiers—the free cities had never had enough soldiers—but by the country itself. By Thornfell. Because the refugees had walked through the village with no name, and the old woman had touched each of their foreheads as they passed, as her grandmother had taught her, and whether it was blessing or memory or simple stubbornness, something had gone with them. When the inquisitors' arrows fell among the cairns, the stones remembered how to fall back. When the lancers charged the narrows, the mountain that had guided Sebastian Reyes through a hundred smuggler's roads chose, suddenly and comprehensively, to forget every path but one—and that one led the charge onto ground that crumbled like bread, taking thirty horses down into a gorge that had been waiting, patient as an archive, to be consulted again.

The scout who told Isolde this part was laughing, half-hysterical, her hands shaking around a cup of tea someone had pressed on her. "It wasn't a battle," she said. "It was a *reading*. The whole valley read us, and it read them, and it decided."

"And Halloway?" Isolde asked.

The scout's laughter died. "He pulled back. Set up camp at the valley mouth like he owns it. He's not beaten." She looked at Isolde with the flat honesty of a soldier. "He's reading *us* now. Working out where the door is. He'll find it. Men like him always find it eventually."

Isolde thought of Halloway's patience, which she had studied in captured dispatches the way she'd once studied dead languages—as a system with rules, and therefore a weakness. Patience was a strategy built on the assumption that the patient party could wait longer than everyone else. It assumed time was on his side.

But time, Isolde thought, looking at the Door, was the one thing Halloway had fundamentally mispriced. He thought he was fighting people fleeing through a mountain. He was actually fighting a country that had spent a hundred years learning how to wait.

* * *

She went through the Door herself on the third night.

Not because the exodus was finished—it wasn't; the columns were still coming, thinner now, the last stragglers and the stubborn and the dying carried on litters—but because Sebastian was on the other side, and she had not seen them since the threshold, since the moment when they had turned from her with their memories already loosening in their eyes like stars going out, and had said, in a voice that was already becoming someone else's voice: *Write it down. Write all of it down. Someone has to remember me correctly.*

So she went through, into the light.

Thornfell, on its own side of itself, was not ash at all.

That was the second great unmaking of everything she had believed. She had imagined a dead kingdom, a hollow country, a land like the inside of a sealed tomb. Instead she stepped out of the barrow's mouth onto a hillside under a real sky—clouds moving, wind smelling of rain and green things and woodsmoke—and below her spread a valley with rivers in it, terraced fields gone wild with a century of self-sufficiency, orchards, the ruins of towns being slowly reclaimed by ivy and by something else, something more deliberate. The land was not dead. The land had been *keeping*.

And everywhere, as the refugees poured out behind her and stood blinking in a light they had never expected to see, the land began to answer them.

She saw it first at the wells. Refugees, thirsty from the passage, went looking for water and found the old wellheads uncovered, ropes coiled neatly on the stones as if someone had drawn water yesterday. She saw it in the orchards, where trees that should have been choked by a century of neglect stood pruned and bearing, fruit ripe out of season. She saw it in the way the paths through the wild fields were worn clear, as if feet had walked them recently—or as if the ground itself remembered feet, and shaped itself to expect them.

Vessia, the silent god. The god who had not died but become a place. Isolde stood on the hillside and understood, with the vertigo of a scholar watching her life's framework dissolve and rebuild itself in a single afternoon, that they were not refugees entering a ruin. They were heirs entering an inheritance. Every well, every path, every fruiting tree was a sentence in a language the country had been speaking to itself for a hundred years, waiting for someone fluent enough to arrive and answer.

And at the center of it, at the mouth of the barrow where the Door stood open behind him, sat Sebastian Reyes.

* * *

They did not know her.

That had been the price, and she had known it would be, and knowing had not prepared her. Sebastian sat wrapped in a blanket some refugee had given them, watching the crowds come through with an expression of gentle bewilderment—the expression of a person waking in a house they've never seen but which fits them perfectly. The oath-sigil on their wrist was visible where the blanket slipped: no longer burning, no longer black, just a pale scar in the shape of a closed eye.

Isolde approached the way you approach a deer, or a fire, or a sentence you're afraid to finish translating.

"I'm Isolde," she said. "I'm an archivist. I—" Her voice failed and restarted. "I knew you. Before."

Sebastian looked up at her. And here was the thing she would set down carefully, precisely, in the true record, because it mattered more than anything else she would ever write: Sebastian looked at her for a long time, with nothing in their face—no recognition, no warmth, no history—and then their brow furrowed slightly, the way it furrows when a scholar almost remembers where they left a book.

"Do I know you?" Sebastian asked. "You seem... familiar. Like a word I used to know the meaning of."

"No," Isolde said, and her heart broke along a seam she'd already prepared for it, cleanly, the way she'd broken it in advance every night since the threshold. "Not yet. But I'm going to tell you everything. I have the book. I have your whole life written down. I'm very good at my work."

Sebastian smiled—a small, tired, courteous smile, offered to a stranger—and something in the smile's shape was so exactly, so unbearably *them*, some specific asymmetry Isolde had memorized across a hundred nights of firelight, that she had to turn away and pretend to check the column counts.

Grief carried forward, she thought. That was the cargo. Everyone passing through the Door was carrying some version of it—burned houses, dead kin, a kingdom lost twice over. Hers was simply more particular than most. She had gotten everyone through alive except the one person she had crossed a mountain to save, and he was sitting right in front of her, alive, safe, and gone.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and when she turned back she was an archivist again, because that was the armor that fit.

"Sebastian," she said. "The heir of Thornfell. There are ten thousand people coming through that door who owe you their lives, and a country under our feet that's been waiting a century to meet you. You don't remember why any of that matters. So I'm going to do the only thing I know how to do." She held up the prophecy book, smoke-stained, water-swollen, saved from the burning village in a smuggler's oilcloth wrap. "I'm going to teach you yourself. From the beginning. It may take years."

Sebastian looked at the book. Looked at her. And then did the thing that gave Isolde the first breath of real hope since the threshold: they reached out and touched the book's cover, lightly, the way you touch a scar that's yours.

"It hurts," Sebastian said wonderingly, "in a good way. Like a name on the tip of the tongue." Their eyes came up to hers, and though there was no memory in them, there was something older than memory—something the silent god had kept safe in the place where memories go when they're surrendered. "Start with the parts about you," they said. "I think—I think those are the ones I want back most."

Behind them, the Door stood open, and the last of the columns came through into the light, and down in the world they had left, Halloway's drums were still beating against a mountain that had already decided.

Isolde opened the book to the first page, and began.
