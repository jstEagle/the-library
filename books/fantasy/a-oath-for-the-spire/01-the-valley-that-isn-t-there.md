## 1. The Valley That Isn't There

The dead of Hollowmere complained about the cold the way living people complained about the weather—constantly, and with no expectation that anything would be done about it.

*Cold down here,* said old Marta Brask, who had been dead eleven years and had not once stopped remarking on it. *Cold in the bones, and me without any bones worth mentioning. Theo. Theo, are you listening?*

"I'm listening," Theo said, and kept their chisel moving along the grain of the grave-marker. Birch, this one, because the family had no coin for stone. The letters were coming out clean: *ALDIS WEBB, BELOVED HUSBAND.* The *B* was giving them trouble.

*You never make them warm enough,* Marta said. *The blankets in the ground. You could say a word over them, couldn't you? You could—*

"I don't do that anymore."

*You never did that. That's the trouble with you.*

Theo blew a curl of birch shaving off the marker and did not answer, because the answer was the same as it always was, and Marta knew it, and asked anyway, the way the dead asked everything—endlessly, hopefully, and without any expectation that anything would be done about it.

The workshop sat at the crook of the mill road where it bent around the Hollowmere river, close enough to the water that in spring the floorboards sweated and Theo's glue pots went sour. It had been their mother's workshop before it was theirs, and their grandmother's before that, and somewhere in the smoke-dark of the rafters hung three generations of carving knives, each handle worn to the shape of an Underhill hand. Theo's hand fit the oldest one best. That was the sort of thing that was supposed to feel like belonging. Most days it did. Most days.

Outside, the valley was doing what the valley always did in the last week of harvest: being beautiful in a way that felt like a held breath. The terraces above the town were gold to the ridgeline, the border stones stood in their ancient circle on the pass road with their worn faces turned outward, and the light came down long and amber and forgiving. Hollowmere was a bowl of light. Everyone said so. Everyone said it the way you'd say a charm against misfortune—*we're lucky, we're safe, the valley keeps us*—and Theo had said it too, all their life, without ever once wondering why a place needed so much reassuring.

*The new one's here,* Marta said suddenly. *The fresh one. By the lych-gate. Oh, he's confused. They're always confused.*

Theo set down the chisel. "Which grave?"

*Not a grave. He's standing at the gate. He's standing up.*

Theo's hands went still. The dead of Hollowmere lay in the ground, all of them, in the tidy rows behind the meetinghouse or out under the ash orchard where the old pyres had been. They lay down and they stayed down and they talked, oh, they talked, but they never stood. Standing was for the dying, sometimes, in the last hour—the dead said so, in the voices of those who had stood themselves, briefly, on their way out. A standing dead man at the lych-gate meant a dying man at the lych-gate.

Theo wiped their hands on their apron, took the lamp down from its hook, and went out into the dusk.

* * *

The man was leaning against the lych-gate the way a coat leans when it's been thrown over a chair. He was tall, or had been, and was now folded down into himself like something crumpled for burning. Blood had soaked through his riding coat from the left side, a dark bloom still spreading, and his horse—a good horse, a courier's horse, lathered and blowing—stood loose-reined in the road behind him as if it had carried him the last mile out of sheer professional obligation.

"Workshop," the man said. His voice came out in pieces. "You're the—the carver. Underhill."

"I'm Theo Underhill."

"I need—" He coughed, and the cough was wet and wrong. "Not a marker. Not yet. I need a door."

Theo looked up and down the mill road. Empty. The harvest lamps were being lit in the town below, little orange eyes winking on one by one, and no one was coming, and no one would, for an hour at least. The dying man had chosen his moment with a courier's instinct for timing.

"Come inside," Theo heard themselves say. "Careful on the step. It's warped."

Later—much later, in the bad hours that followed—Theo would try to find the moment where staying small had stopped being possible, and it would always come back to this: not the blood, not the map, not the whisper of *the door opens at the dark moon.* It was the step. It was saying *careful on the step, it's warped* to a stranger bleeding out in their doorway, because that was what you said to people in Hollowmere, because that was what your mother had said, because the words were worn smooth as a knife handle and they fit your hand. Kindness as habit. Habit as a kind of fate.

* * *

He would not give his name. He lay on the workbench where Theo had swept the birch shavings into a corner with shaking hands, and he would not give his name, and that was the first wrong thing, because everyone in Hollowmere gave their name; it was the whole currency of the place. *Underhill, Brask, Webb, Fen.* Everyone knew everyone's name back to the flood. A man without a name in Hollowmere was like a letter without an address.

The second wrong thing was the wound. Theo had stitched the occasional gash—country work, scythe work, boar work—and this was none of those. The cut was long, deliberate, and placed low across the ribs by someone who had known exactly where to put a blade so that death would take its time. It was a wound made by a professional, for a purpose.

The third wrong thing was the leather tube, which he would not let go of. Not when Theo peeled back the coat to see the wound. Not when they pressed a rag to it and felt him shudder. He held it against his chest with both hands, like a child with a stolen loaf, and his eyes—gray, fever-bright, terribly awake—stayed on Theo's face the whole time, measuring.

"You're the bone-carver," he said again. It wasn't a question this time. It was an accusation, or a prayer.

"I carve grave-markers."

"Markers." He laughed, and the laugh cost him something, and blood came up with it. "Markers. Yes. That's what they'd have you do." His hand came off the tube—only far enough—and caught Theo's wrist. His grip was still strong. Dying men are strong the way rivers are strong just before the freeze. "Listen to me. Listen. There's a country. There's a country and you're standing in it."

"I'm standing in Hollowmere."

"You're standing in the *shell* of Hollowmere." His eyes were wet now, and not only from fever. "The valley. The border stones. You think they keep the world out? Sweet bones, carver. They keep you in. There was a country here before there was a valley. It was erased. Wiped—out of the maps, out of the records, out of *memory*, and the people in it, all of them, made into a story about a safe little valley where nothing ever happens." His breath hitched. "The door opens at the dark moon. One night. Once a generation. And I am the last courier who knows the way, and there are men on the pass road, and I am not going to make it, am I. I'm not going to make it."

Theo's hands had stopped moving. The rag against his ribs was soaked through. "I should fetch the physic—"

"Don't." It came out flat and final. "They'll be watching the physic's door. They're watching every door. That's what a Warden does." He pulled Theo closer, close enough that Theo could smell the blood and the road dust and under it something stranger, like the air off a deep cellar. "You hear them, don't you. The dead. Don't lie. I've crossed four borders to find the one carver in the world who *hears them*, and I don't have time for your lie."

The workshop was very quiet. Outside, a nightjar called, and the river kept its small talk going, and Marta Brask's voice came thin through the floorboards—*Theo? Theo, who is it? What's happening?*—and Theo looked at this dying stranger who had just said aloud, in a valley where saying it aloud would mean the pyre or the river or worse, the one thing Theo had guarded since the age of seven.

"I don't know what you mean," Theo said.

It was a bad lie. Theo knew it was a bad lie the moment it left their mouth, the way you know a crooked letter the instant the chisel bites. The courier knew it too. Something in his face gentled—not pity exactly. Recognition.

"No," he said. "You wouldn't. Not even to yourself, maybe. That's how they built it. That's how they built all of it." He pressed the leather tube into Theo's hands then, both his hands over theirs, folding their fingers closed around it, and the tube was warm from his body and lighter than it should have been. "The dark moon. Nine days. The map shows the door. The map shows everything. You hear the dead—so hear this: *they remember.* The dead remember the country. They've been trying to tell you for years, and you've been carving their words into birch and calling it a job."

*Theo,* said Marta, from under the floor, and for the first time in Theo's life her voice sounded frightened. *Theo, there are riders on the pass road. Four of them. They're not stopping at the stones. Theo, the stones are letting them through.*

The courier heard it too—saw it in Theo's face, whatever it was that a face does when a voice arrives that no one else can hear. He nodded, slow, almost peaceful.

"That'll be the Warden's men," he said. "Quick now. The back window. The tube—hide it, don't read it by lamplight, the ink's not ink. And carver—" His hand found Theo's sleeve as Theo turned, and held it, and his gray eyes were going dim at the edges but his voice came out clear as a bell over frozen ground.

"Whatever they tell you the valley is—it isn't. It never was. You were never *safe*. You were *kept*."

Then the door of the workshop banged open against the wall, and the lamplight swung, and the night came in wearing boots.
