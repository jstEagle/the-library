## 2. A Map Written in Someone Else's Blood

The courier lasted until the third bell of the night, which was longer than Theo expected and shorter than they hoped.

They had done what could be done in a bone-carver's workshop, which was not much. A pallet of wood shavings dragged before the hearth. A kettle of water heated too slow. The man's wound was in his side, low and deep, the kind that wept rather than gushed, filling the room with the smell of copper and something underneath it, sour and wrong, like a well that had gone bad. Theo had pressed a clean rag to it and the rag had gone red, then dark, then red again, and somewhere in that repetition Theo understood that the arithmetic was not going to work out.

The man knew it too. That was the strange part. He lay on the shavings with his boots still on, muddied to the knee, and he watched the ceiling beams with the patience of a man reading a familiar book, and when he spoke his voice was dry and even, as though he were discussing the weather.

"Name's Aldric," he said. "In case it matters. It usually doesn't."

"Theo." They hesitated, then added, "Underhill."

"Underhill." He turned his head, and his eyes were gray and very clear, clearer than eyes had any right to be in a man dying. "Bone-carver. The Warden's men said a bone-carver. They said it like it was a dirty word."

Theo's hands went still on the rag. "The Warden's men said that? When?"

"This afternoon. On the road past the ash orchard." A thin smile. "They were asking after me before I ever reached your valley, carver. I didn't lead them here. I was followed."

Outside, Hollowmere lay quiet under a fat white moon. Theo could hear the valley the way they always heard it: the creek muttering over its stones, a dog three lanes over dreaming in high yips, and beneath all of it, faint as breath on a winter window, the voices of the dead in the churchyard hill, complaining of the cold. They had heard those voices every night of their life. They had never once told a living soul. And now a stranger was dying on their floor, and the first thing he had given them was a reason to be afraid.

"Who are you?" Theo asked. "What did you carry?"

Aldric laughed, and the laugh cost him; he curled around the wound and breathed through his teeth for a long moment before he could speak again. "A letter. A map. A message twenty years old that somebody finally decided was worth killing for." His hand crept along the floor, over the shavings, until it found Theo's wrist. His grip was cold. "You hear them, don't you."

Theo went very still. "Hear who."

"The dead. Don't insult us both." His gray eyes held them, and there was no accusation in them, only a kind of tired recognition. "The valley's full of them. That's why it was built here. That's why the border stones hum at night if you put your ear to them. You've heard the stones, haven't you? You've heard everything. You just never knew what it was you were hearing."

The dead in the churchyard stirred, as if they had heard themselves mentioned. *Who is that,* whispered old Maren Cobb, dead forty years. *He smells of the outside.* Theo's mouth had gone dry.

"I don't know what you're talking about," they said, and it came out too fast, too high, the worst lie they had ever told, and Aldric had the grace not to point at it.

"Fine," he said. "Keep your secrets. I'll give you mine instead." He fumbled at his belt with his free hand, and his fingers were clumsy now, the cold coming into them. The leather tube came free and he dragged it between them, and Theo saw the dark stain soaking through the wrapping, blood that had dried and dried again over miles of road. "The door opens at the dark moon. Eleven days from now. It only opens once in a generation, and the last time it opened, the Warden was there to close it, and he'll be there again."

"The door," Theo repeated. "What door?"

"The door out." Aldric's breath was going shallow, each word a small expenditure. "Out of Hollowmere. Out of the country that isn't in any country. You've lived your whole life inside a prison, carver, and the walls are so old and so kind that everyone inside calls them home."

*He's mad,* Theo thought. *He's dying and he's mad, and I'll bury him in the churchyard and the dead will ask me who he was, and I won't know what to tell them.* But their hands had already taken the tube. They noticed this only afterward — that they had reached for it without deciding to, the way a drowning man reaches for a rope he doesn't believe in.

"Take it," Aldric said. "Not to the Warden. Not to anyone with a uniform or a tithe-book or a kind face and questions. Take it to—" He stopped. His brow furrowed, and for a moment Theo watched a whole life pass behind his eyes, every name he'd ever trusted, and none of them surviving his scrutiny. "Take it to the door yourself. Or give it to someone who hates the Warden more than they fear him. That's the only instruction that matters. Hate more than fear. Everything else is on the map."

"The map's in blood," Theo said. "It's soaked through—"

"It was always in blood." His smile again, fainter. "Everything worth carrying is. Listen — the dark moon. Eleven days. The cliff on the north face, past the standing stones, where the rock doesn't cast a shadow. You'll know it because you'll feel it. The silence in the stone. You of all people will feel it."

His grip on Theo's wrist tightened, then loosened, then was gone.

He died a little before dawn, between one complaint of the churchyard dead and the next, so quietly that Theo only knew because the room had stopped smelling of copper and started smelling of morning. Theo sat on the floor beside him until the light came gray through the shutters, the leather tube in their lap, and realized their hands had not stopped shaking since midnight.

* * *

The riders came at midmorning.

There were three of them, in the Warden's gray-and-black, and they filled Theo's little workshop the way crows fill a tree. The one in front was broad and soft-spoken, with a face like a closed door, and he took off his gloves finger by finger before he spoke, which was somehow worse than shouting.

"Courier came through the valley yesterday," he said. "Man on a lathered horse, riding north. You'd have seen him. Everyone sees everyone in Hollowmere."

"Didn't see anyone," Theo said.

The man looked at the fresh-turned earth visible through the window, at the spade leaning against the workshop wall, still crusted with yesterday's dirt, and then at Theo, and said nothing for a long, long time.

"You dug a grave this morning," he said at last.

"Old Cobb's plot needed a marker. I'm the carver. I dig when I carve." Theo heard their own voice coming out wrong, too quick, the words tripping over each other like children running from a bell. *You are lying badly,* said a cold clear voice in their head that sounded like their mother. *You have never once had to lie and it shows like a stain.*

"Mm," said the rider. He walked the length of the workshop, slow, his boots loud on the floorboards. He stopped at the hearth. He looked at the wood-shaving pallet, at the dark stain on the stones that Theo had scrubbed twice and not well enough. He crouched and touched it with one fingertip and looked at his fingertip.

"Blood," he said, conversationally. "Not more than a few hours old."

"Killed a chicken," Theo said.

"Where's the feathers?"

Theo looked at the rider, and the rider looked back, and the silence stretched until Theo could hear the churchyard dead murmuring like a crowd before a hanging. *He knows,* they whispered, though the dead knew nothing of the kind; they only echoed Theo's own terror back at them, the way they always echoed whatever was loudest in the living.

The rider stood. He put his gloves back on, finger by finger.

"The Warden pays for information," he said. "He pays well, and he pays forever, if you understand me. And he collects from those who hold it back." He set a gray coin on the workbench, on the half-finished grave-marker Theo had been carving for old Maren Cobb, right in the hollow of the name. "Eleven days. If a certain item passes through your hands, carver, you'll bring it to the Warden's house. And if it doesn't — well. Then it never did, and we're all of us friends."

They left. Theo stood in the doorway and watched the three riders walk their horses up the lane at an unhurried pace, past neighbors who bowed their heads and asked no questions, because in Hollowmere everyone had learned long ago that the Warden's men asked questions and never answered them.

Theo went back inside and picked up the leather tube with hands that had finally, finally gone still. Not calm. Still, the way a bowstring is still.

* * *

Sable came at dusk, because Sable always came at dusk, when the light made her look less like what she was.

She was a hedge-witch, which in Hollowmere meant she sold charms that half worked for prices that always worked, and she owed money to people in three villages and possibly a fourth, and she had a face like a fox that had been caught in a henhouse once and had enjoyed it. She let herself in without knocking, as she always did, and stopped just inside the door with her nose lifted like a hound's.

"You've got a corpse in here," she said. "Recently. And you've got Warden's men on you like flies. Theo Underhill, what in the name of every saint nobody remembers have you done?"

"I haven't done anything," Theo said. "It was done to me." And because it was Sable, and because the alternative was carrying it alone, they told her. The courier. The blood. The tube. The dark moon, eleven days off. The rider's finger in the old blood, and the gray coin sitting where they had not dared move it.

Sable listened the way she did everything — leaning, sharp-eyed, one hand already in her pocket. When Theo finished she was quiet for a moment, which from Sable was more alarming than shouting.

"Show me the map," she said.

Theo unrolled it on the workbench, and even Sable went still. It was drawn on skin — not vellum, skin, fine and pale — in ink that had gone brown with age and red with the courier's blood, and the blood had not ruined it. The blood had *finished* it. Rivers that had been faint lines now gleamed. Roads Theo's eye kept sliding off of now held, the way a word holds when you finally sound it out. And at the top of the map, where a title should be, there was a country's shape with no name, only a border drawn twice, once in ink and once — newer, steadier — in something dark and dried.

"That's not a map of Hollowmere," Sable said slowly. "That's a map of everything *around* Hollowmere. And Hollowmere's—" She stopped. Her finger hovered over the space where their whole life was, and Theo saw her find it, or rather find its absence: a blank, a gap in the skin, a hole shaped like home.

"There's no way out drawn," Theo said. "There's a door. He said the door's on the north face, past the standing stones. Eleven days. The dark moon."

Sable looked at the map for a long time. Then she looked at Theo, and something moved behind her eyes that Theo had never seen there before — not the usual quick arithmetic of profit, but something older and hungrier and, strangely, more honest.

"I owe people," she said. "Bad people, the kind that collect in fingers. And you, carver, owe the Warden a map you can't give him, which means in eleven days you're a dead man or a fugitive, and there's no third thing." She tapped the map once, lightly, as if it might bite. "I can get us out. I know the ways through the ash orchard that the riders don't watch, and I know a woman past the stones who owes me, and I know how to move without leaving a shadow. I'll take you and your map to the door."

Theo's heart did something complicated. "And in exchange?"

"In exchange, the map comes with me. It's mine after that. Whatever it opens, whatever it's worth — mine." She held out her hand, fox-faced and unblinking. "That's the price. I don't dress it up pretty. You'll be free and I'll be paid, and neither of us will ever have to see Hollowmere again."

Theo looked at the map — at the hole in it shaped like their whole life — and at the gray coin on the bench, and at the fresh grave through the window where a stranger who had hated the Warden more than he feared him was already going quiet in the ground.

*Invisibility was a plan,* they thought. *It was a plan right up until it wasn't.*

They shook her hand.

Outside, far off toward the north face, the border stones began to hum — low and long, a note Theo had heard every night of their life and never once listened to. Sable's head came up sharply. "They don't usually sing this early," she said.

"I know," Theo said. "I think they know we're coming."

And beneath the hum, faint and cold, the churchyard dead had gone silent — all of them, all at once — as if they were holding their breath.
