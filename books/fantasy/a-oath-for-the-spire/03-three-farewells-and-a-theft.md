## 3. Three Farewells and a Theft

Sable had stolen things before—rings off sleeping fingers, a psalter from a chapel that no longer existed, an entire afternoon of another woman's life—but she had never stolen from someone who was trusting her. That was the difference, she told herself, turning the leather tube over in her hands in the blue hour before dawn. That was the whole difference, and it wasn't much of one.

Theo slept in the chair by the cold hearth, chin dropped to chest, hands still curled as if around a chisel. Even asleep they looked like someone braced for a scolding. They'd given her the pallet; she hadn't asked for it. They'd fed her nettle soup and bread gone hard at the crusts, and when she'd named a price for passage—half up front, the map as surety—they'd simply nodded, the way people nodded at weather.

*Half up front,* she thought now, weighing the tube. *And the other half is whatever I decide it's worth.*

The map inside was written in blood that had gone brown as old rust, and it was worth more than everything Sable had lost, which was to say: everything. Her name on the hedge-witch rolls, struck through in red ink after the Vell business. Her cottage at Marrow's Edge, seized against debts she still couldn't count honestly. Her reputation, which had been small but hers, like a garden of weeds. Twenty years of being useful to people who needed cures they couldn't buy and curses they couldn't admit to—and all of it gone because she'd once been kind to the wrong man.

She could sell this map. She knew who bought such things; she'd spent enough years in the gray markets to know the shape of the trade even if she'd never held anything this bright. A map out of Hollowmere. A door that opened once a generation. There were men in Corvane's Reach who would pay a house for that. Pay a life.

And there were men who would pay less and ask fewer questions, and those were the ones she meant to find.

Sable looked at Theo sleeping upright like a dog that expected to be kicked, and she felt something move in her chest that she refused to name, because naming it would mean owing it.

*I'll come back,* she lied to herself, and went out the window into the dark.

* * *

The buyer's name was Ferren Hale, and he kept a counting-house on Tallow Street with shutters that never quite closed, as if the building itself were squinting at its own accounts. Sable had done business two streets over, years ago—a love-charm, a forgetting-potion, nothing that left marks—and she remembered the neighborhood the way you remember a scar.

Hale received her in a back room lit by one lamp turned low. He was a soft-bellied man with ink-stained cuffs and the patient smile of someone who had never once been surprised by what people would sell.

"A map," he said, when she laid the tube on his table.

"Out of Hollowmere." She kept her voice bored. Boredom was armor. "Blood-inked. The real thing."

"And how does a hedge-witch come by the real thing?"

"Does it matter? You're not buying my story. You're buying paper."

He unrolled it with fingers surprisingly gentle, and Sable watched his face do something complicated—the flicker of a man reading scripture in a language he'd been told didn't exist. His smile stayed fixed but his eyes went wet-bright, and for one absurd moment she thought he might weep over it.

Then the lamp behind him moved, and she saw the shadow it threw: a second man, standing by the door she hadn't noticed, wearing the gray-and-black of the Warden's riders. The badge on his chest was a key crossed with a bar. The sign of the lock.

Her mouth went dry. "You said fewer questions," she managed, though he had said nothing of the kind.

"I did say nothing," Hale agreed pleasantly. "He did the saying." He rolled the map closed with the same tenderness. "Mistress Sable Vane, formerly of Marrow's Edge. Struck from the rolls four years past. Debts outstanding to—" he consulted a card he clearly already knew by heart "—six parties, totaling rather more than your craft can cover. You see why I assumed you'd come eventually. People always bring me the interesting things. Eventually."

The rider stepped forward into the lamplight. He was younger than she expected, with a face like a closed ledger, and he looked at her the way you look at a tool that has performed adequately.

"The carver," he said. "Underhill. Where?"

"I don't—"

"You brought us the map. Which means you took it from them. Which means you know where they sleep." He said it without heat, the way a man reads coordinates. "You'll tell me, and you'll be paid, and you'll be forgotten. Or you won't, and you'll be remembered. In our work, mistress, being remembered is very bad."

Sable thought about the window at Theo's workshop. The pallet she hadn't earned. The soup. The nod, easy as weather, when she'd named her price—as if betrayal were just another toll on the road, as if everyone charged it.

They probably did expect it. That was the worst part. Somewhere in that tired carved face, Theo had already priced their own trust and found it cheap.

"No," Sable heard herself say.

The word came out before she'd decided on it, and she stood there feeling it hang in the air like smoke, astonished at her own mouth.

Hale sighed. The rider's hand moved toward his belt, unhurried, and Sable did the only thing she had ever truly been good at: she moved first. The lamp went over under her palm, and in the dark and the spreading oil-flame she was through the back shutter and into the alley before either man finished standing, running with the particular grace of a woman who has fled many rooms and learned all their exits.

Behind her, Hale's voice followed, mild as milk: "We know where they live anyway, mistress. You've only cost yourself the payment."

She ran anyway. She ran until Tallow Street was a memory and the ash orchards rose black around her, and then she stopped, bent double, and was sick into the roots of a tree grown from somebody's burned bones.

*You've only cost yourself the payment.*

They'd known. They'd let her run because she was leading them nowhere they weren't already going. And every step she'd taken away from Theo's workshop tonight was a step the Warden's men were taking toward it, at their leisure, with her theft as their warrant.

Sable wiped her mouth and turned back toward Hollowmere, ashamed in a way that sat in the body heavier than any fever. Guilt, she discovered, had a taste. It tasted like nettle soup she'd eaten without thanking anyone.

* * *

She caught the boy at the edge of the market square, or rather he caught himself—tripping over his own feet in front of her with the practiced clumsiness of a pickpocket, small quick fingers already sliding toward the pouch at her hip.

Sable closed her hand around his wrist.

He froze. He was maybe ten, all elbows and hollow cheeks, hair cut raggedly as if by his own knife, and when she yanked him close he didn't cry out or struggle. He just looked up at her with eyes too old for the rest of him, and waited to see what the world would do.

"Fast hands," Sable said. "Bad feet."

His mouth pressed flat. He didn't answer, and after a moment she understood—he wasn't sulking. He tapped two fingers against his throat, twice, and shook his head.

*Mute.* She'd seen him around the valley fringes, she realized. Sleeping in the woodstacks near the tannery. The other children called him Pip, when they bothered to call him anything.

"Give it back," she said, holding out her free hand.

He opened his fist. In it lay not her coin purse but a button—one of the bone buttons off her coat, worked with a tiny whorled pattern, stolen sometime in the last minute without her feeling a thing. He set it in her palm carefully, almost ceremonially, and then, because he was apparently incapable of leaving well alone, he also produced her coin purse and set that down too, and then, after visible internal debate, a copper bit that was almost certainly not hers.

Sable stared at the little pile in her hand. Something about it—the honesty of the theft returned, the terrible etiquette of it—cracked something loose in her chest that the Warden's rider hadn't managed.

"How would you like to be paid to run away?" she said.

Pip tilted his head.

"There are bad men coming to a workshop on Tanner's Lane. Two travelers there who need waking and warning, and I need—" *to look them in the face,* she didn't say. "I need fast hands and bad feet. Both yours."

He considered her for a long moment with those old eyes, doing some silent arithmetic of his own. Then he pointed at her coat pocket where the bread was, and held up three fingers.

"Greedy," Sable said, and gave him the whole loaf.

They went together through the graying streets, the boy ghosting along beside her, and Sable rehearsed apologies the whole way and discarded every one. What was she going to say? *I stole from you and sold you to the men hunting you, but don't worry, I changed my mind?* There was no version of it that wasn't filthy. All she could do was arrive, and keep arriving, and hope that showing up counted for something in a world where it so rarely did.

The workshop's window was dark. Too dark. The door stood open a hand's width, swinging gently, and the smell of lamp-oil smoke hung faint on the morning air.

Sable stopped dead in the lane, her stomach dropping through the ground of Hollowmere itself.

Inside, the hearth was cold, the chair empty, and on the floor where Theo had slept lay the leather tube—empty, unrolled, its blood-brown lines gleaming in the gray light. Someone had taken the map out and left the casing behind, neat as a calling card.

Pip slipped past her into the room. He crouched by the cold hearth, touched the ash, and went very still. Then he looked up at her, urgent, and began pulling scraps of charcoal from his pocket, drawing fast on the floorboards: a road, a line of trees, a shape like a broken tower—and beneath it all, scratched hard enough to bite the wood, a single wordless spiral that Sable recognized, with a chill, from the corner of the map.

The boy knew where Theo had gone. The boy knew the way.

Sable looked at the empty tube, at the open door, at the child drawing maps on a stranger's floor with charcoal-stained fingers, and understood that her debts had just gotten worse in every direction except the one that mattered.

"Well," she said, to nobody, to herself, to the dead of Hollowmere. "Best go collect what's mine."
