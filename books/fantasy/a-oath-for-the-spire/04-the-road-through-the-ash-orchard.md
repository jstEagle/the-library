## 4. The Road Through the Ash Orchard

The trees were wrong. Theo had known that from the moment they crossed the first ridge out of Hollowmere and saw the orchard spilling down the far slope in rows too straight for anything wild, too crooked for anything planted by living hands.

"Pyre-orchard," Sable said, when Theo asked. She walked with her staff slung across her back like a yoke she'd grown used to carrying, her hood up against a wind that smelled of cold iron. "After the Burning Years. They didn't have ground left to bury people—too many dead, too fast—so they burned them and worked the ash into the soil. Apple stock grows deep roots." She said it the way she said most things: flat, practical, daring anyone to make it sentimental. "Every tree in this valley is standing on somebody."

Theo stopped at the nearest tree and put their palm flat against the bark.

The voices hit them like cold water.

In Hollowmere the dead murmured. They complained about the damp, about the weight of the stones above them, about grandchildren who never visited. Their voices came through bone and earth worn smooth by centuries, softened to something almost companionable. Theo had spent twenty-two years learning to live inside that murmur the way you live inside the sound of a river.

These dead did not murmur. They *shouted*.

*Cold,* said the tree, in a voice like a struck bell. *Cold cold cold, they burned us green, we weren't even dry, we screamed while we burned and the screaming is still in the wood, can't you hear it, can't you hear it—*

Theo snatched their hand back as if the bark had bitten.

"What?" Sable had turned, sharp-eyed. "What did you hear?"

"Nothing." The lie was automatic, worn smooth from use. "Just checking the fruit."

"It's the wrong season for fruit and we both know it." She studied them a moment longer than was comfortable, then let it go—or seemed to. Behind her, Pip stood very still among the trees, his head tilted, listening to something too. But he had no gift; Theo would have felt it if he did. Wouldn't they?

They walked on through the orchard as the light thickened toward evening. The dead grew louder with every row. Not just the one tree—all of them, hundreds of voices braided together into a rope of sound that Theo could not stop hearing, because hearing was not something they did with their ears. It came up through their boot soles, through the bones of their ankles, into the marrow where all their secrets lived.

*Behind you,* the dead said. *Behind behind behind. They come behind you.*

Theo stopped so abruptly that Pip walked into their back.

"They're coming," Theo said. "Riders. Behind us. Half a day, maybe less."

Sable's face went through several expressions in quick succession—annoyance, calculation, and something that might have been fear wearing calculation's clothes. "You're sure?"

"The trees are sure." Theo looked back the way they'd come, at the long gray avenue of trunks fading into dusk. "They say there are four of them. They say—" Theo hesitated, because the next part was ugly, and saying it aloud made it real. "They say the riders stop at every farmstead and ask after us. And the ones who answer wrong don't get asked twice."

Sable swore under her breath, a long practiced string of words that suggested a misspent youth in dockside towns. "Off the road, then. We cut through the orchard and take the shepherd tracks on the north slope. It'll cost us a day."

"A day we may not have."

"A day we'll wish we'd spent when Corvane's men ride us down in open country." She was already moving, cutting left between two gnarled trunks. "Come on. Both of you. And Underhill—if your trees say anything else useful, I'd rather hear it before it kills us."

*Your trees.* Theo fell into step beside her, Pip trotting at their heels like a small determined shadow. Something about the phrase sat strangely warm in their chest, and they decided not to examine why.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an ancient tree whose trunk had split and healed and split again, a knot of scar tissue the size of a barrel. Sable built no fire. Instead she produced a stub of candle, cupped it against the wind, and divided their provisions with the grim precision of a woman who had gone hungry enough times to hate waste: a heel of black bread each, a smear of honey gone sugary at the edges, three shriveled apples picked from the pyre-trees despite Theo's warning look.

"They're only apples," Sable said, biting into hers. "The dead don't begrudge you an apple."

"You don't know that."

"I know I've eaten worse from worse places." She tossed one to Pip, who caught it against his chest with both hands and held it like treasure before eating it in small, careful bites, saving the skin for last.

Theo turned their own apple over in their fingers. Up close, in the candlelight, the flesh of the pyre-fruit was faintly pink, as though blushed from within. When they bit it, the taste was sweet and underneath the sweetness something smoky, like a memory of fire. The dead in the tree above them went quiet, watching. Theo felt their attention the way you feel sunlight through a window.

"I'm sorry," Theo said quietly, to the tree, to all of them. "For taking. I'll plant the seeds. Somewhere with clean soil."

Sable stared at them across the candle. "Did you just apologize to an apple tree?"

Theo chewed. Swallowed. Said nothing.

"No," Sable said slowly, sitting forward. "No, you talked to it. You've been talking to them this whole time—the graves in Hollowmere, the markers you carve. That's your secret, isn't it? That's what makes you flinch every time we pass a fence post." Her voice wasn't mocking. That was somehow worse. It was interested. "How long?"

"Since I was six." The words came out of Theo like teeth being pulled. "I touched my grandmother's headstone and she told me my mother was lying about where father went. I thought everyone could do it. I thought—I thought they were just being polite, pretending they couldn't hear her too." A laugh escaped them, thin and awful. "I told my mother what grandmother said. She went white as chalk and never spoke of him again, and I learned very quickly which parts of myself to keep behind my teeth."

The candle flame bent in the wind. Above them the old tree creaked, and its voice, when it came, was gentler than the others—older, maybe, or simply tired. *Little listener. Little listener, you carry our words like eggs in a basket. Be careful which ones you break.*

"And the map?" Sable asked. "The courier. Did the dead tell you to take it? Is that why you're here?"

"The courier told me himself. He was dying and he grabbed my wrist and he said, *the door opens at the dark moon, someone has to carry it out, promise me.*" Theo looked at their hands, ink-stained and chipped-nailed, hands built for carving names into stone. "I promised. I didn't even know what I was promising. I just couldn't let him die holding nothing."

Pip had stopped eating. He was watching Theo with those dark, unblinking eyes, and something in his expression made Theo's skin prickle—not pity, not fear, but recognition, as if Pip understood promises made in desperation better than any child should.

Then Pip set down his apple core, dug into the ragged satchel he carried everywhere, and pulled out his charcoal stub and the folded sheet of cheap paper he drew on when words failed him—which was always.

He drew quickly, furiously, tongue caught between his teeth. Theo and Sable leaned in without meaning to, drawn together by the scratch of charcoal like moths.

A door. Tall, narrow, carved directly into a cliff face, with a keystone shaped like a sunburst split down the middle. Around it, Pip sketched seven symbols in an arch, quick confident strokes, and then—he paused, frowned, erased one with his thumb, redrew it.

Theo's blood went cold.

They had seen that door. They had seen it an hour ago, unrolled by firelight in Sable's stolen glance at the courier's map: the door at the valley's edge, the one marked with a broken-sun sigil and ringed by seven glyphs that the cartographer had annotated in cramped handwriting—*sequence unknown; mechanism presumed keyed.*

Pip had never seen the map. Theo was certain of it. They had kept it wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of their pack since the workshop floor, and Pip had been asleep both times they'd checked it.

"Pip," Theo said carefully. "Where did you see this door?"

The boy looked up. His eyes were wet—not crying, exactly, but full, the way a cup is full right before it spills. He pointed at the drawing. Then he pointed at himself. Then he pressed his fist flat against his own chest, over his heart, and held it there until his hand trembled.

"He's seen it," Sable breathed. "Or he remembers it. Underhill, look at the glyphs—that's not a child's scribbling, that's *order*, that's a sequence. He knows the sequence." She grabbed Pip's chin gently and turned his face to the candlelight, searching. "Who are you? Whose boy are you?"

Pip pulled away from her touch, not angry but firm, and did something Theo had never seen him do. He took the charcoal and wrote, in small, careful letters at the bottom of the page—letters too practiced, too schooled, for a gutter orphan:

*I forgot on purpose. I'm sorry. It's starting to come back.*

The wind rose through the orchard, moving through a thousand burning-memory trees, and the dead began to speak all at once—not shouting now, but chanting, low and rhythmic, like a congregation finding an old hymn. Theo caught fragments through the roar:

*The heir returns. The heir returns. Light the lamps, the heir returns—*

And beneath it, farther off, threaded through the chant like wire through wool: hoofbeats. Four sets of them. No longer half a day away.

Sable snuffed the candle with her fingers. In the sudden dark, her voice came low and hard.

"We move. Now."
