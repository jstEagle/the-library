## 5. What the Warden Wants

The interrogation room in the Warden's station at Grayharrow had once been a tithe barn, and it still smelled of old grain and older fear. Corvane had kept the beams. He liked the way they darkened overhead like ribs, so that a man brought in bound to the chair beneath them might feel, without knowing why, that he had been swallowed.

The man in the chair was called Ferren Moss. He was a broker of curiosities out of Lowmarsh, with soft hands and a merchant's habit of smiling before he spoke, and he had been smiling when the riders dragged him in an hour ago. He was not smiling now. The lantern on the table between them had burned down to a thumb's width of oil, and in its shrunken light his face had begun to look like something left out in weather.

"Again," Corvane said. "From the beginning. Slowly."

"I've told you everything." Moss's voice cracked on *everything*, which interested Corvane more than the words themselves. "A woman came to my rooms. Hedge-witch by the look of her—Sable, she gave her name, though I'd wager coin it wasn't her mother's. Said she had a map for sale. A map of the interior."

"The interior," Corvane repeated.

"You know what I mean." Moss wet his lips. His eyes went to the door, then to the window, then to the beams, as if escape might be found in any of them if only he looked hard enough. "The country that isn't there. Everyone in the trade has heard whispers. A valley inside a border no surveyor will draw. I told her I'd pay. She said she'd return by morning with the document itself. She never came."

"And why would a hedge-witch from Hollowmere have such a thing?"

"How should I know? These valley folk—" Moss stopped himself, wisely, and started again. "I don't know. I buy and sell. I don't ask where things come from. That's the whole of my trade."

"That," said Corvane, "is the whole of your problem."

He rose and crossed to the window. Beyond the glass, Grayharrow lay under a sky the color of dishwater, its rooftops furred with early frost. Twenty years he had watched this town, and the eleven others ringing the silence, and in twenty years he had learned that people did not need to know a thing to endanger it. They needed only to want it. Wanting was enough. Wanting was how the last catastrophe had begun—not with malice but with appetite, with men who looked at a neighboring country's power the way Moss looked at merchandise, and reached out their hands.

"Describe the map," he said, without turning around.

"There wasn't one to describe. She showed me a corner of it through the leather tube. Just a corner." Moss's breathing had gone shallow; Corvane could hear it, the little whistle of a man whose body understood his danger before his mind did. "But I know vellum. It was old. And there were markings on it I've never seen—place-names in no script I recognized, and a symbol at the margin. A door. Drawn as a door, cut into a hillside, with a keyhole like—"

"Like what?"

"A keyhole like a person. A small figure, standing."

Corvane closed his eyes.

He had seen that symbol once before, twenty years ago, on a document that no longer existed because he had fed it into a stove with his own hands while its author wept across the table from him. The cartographer Aldous Vane had spent nine years mapping the erased country from rumor, memory, and the testimony of three survivors who had wandered out before the borders closed—and when Corvane asked him, gently, why he would do such a thing, Vane had answered: *Because a country shouldn't be allowed to die twice.*

It had been a decent answer. It had also been wrong. Countries did not die. They waited. And the thing sleeping behind the border stones had been waiting long enough.

* * *

Afterward, alone, Corvane unlocked the iron cabinet in his study and took out the ledger no one else was permitted to see. It was not an official record. Official records could be subpoenaed, stolen, read. This one was written in his own hand, in a cipher of his own devising, and it contained the true history of the Warden's office—the history that existed nowhere else, because he had made certain of it.

He turned to the earliest pages and read what he had already memorized, the way a man reads scripture, not for information but for absolution.

*In the year the calendars break, the country now unrecorded opened its doors to trade, and what came out of it was not goods. The mages of that place had learned to bind the dead into service—to carve keys from bone, to open ways that ought to stay shut, to make doors of people. For six months the traffic continued before the neighboring courts understood what they were buying. By the time they did, the dead of four provinces walked as laborers, and the living had begun to sell their own deaths in advance, the way one sells timber standing in a forest.*

*The catastrophe, when it came, took eleven days. What remained afterward was not ruins. Ruins can be mapped. What remained was absence—a wound in the world that pulled at every magical working within a hundred leagues, that unraveled spells cast near it, that whispered. The courts convened. The mages convened. And in the end they did the only thing left: they buried it. Border stones raised in a ring. Memory softened by decree and by craft, until the country passed out of record, out of song, out of the mouths of mothers warning their children. A hole in the world, papered over.*

*And at the center of the hole, still alive, still trading, still carving: the valley that had been the heart of it. Hollowmere.*

Corvane set down the ledger. The fire had burned low. In its ember-light the room seemed to contract around him, the shelves of confiscated books leaning in like witnesses.

People imagined, when they imagined him at all—and mostly they did not imagine him, which was the point—that the Warden's work was cruelty. It was not. Cruelty was easy and common. His work was arithmetic. Every year, a handful of souls stumbled against the truth of the borders: a courier with a map, a scholar with a theory, a child born too perceptive. And every year, Corvane weighed those lives against the lives beyond the ring—the millions who slept soundly because the wound stayed bandaged—and the arithmetic never changed. It had never once changed in twenty years.

He was not a cruel man. He was a merciful one, and mercy, done properly, required a stomach.

* * *

At dawn, the reports came in.

His riders had swept the ash orchards west of Hollowmere and found sign: three travelers, moving fast, one of them bleeding. A hedgerow camp abandoned with bread still wrapped in cloth. And at a farmstead where they had begged water, a girl of eight described them with the merciless precision of children—one tall woman with a limp, one young person with ink-stained hands, and a boy who did not speak but drew.

The boy drew, the farmer's wife reported, a picture of the farmhouse they stood in, before anyone had told her what it looked like.

Corvane read the report twice.

Then he sent for his lieutenant, Brannoc, a gray man with a gray voice whom Corvane trusted precisely because he had never once shown initiative. "Take six riders," Corvane said. "The drowned chapel road, if they're making for the old causeway—they'll want shelter, and there's only one roof between here and the river that doesn't ask questions. Bring me the witch and the boy alive. The carver—" He paused. Outside, frost was burning off the fields in slow white ribbons, and somewhere a lark was singing with the wholehearted stupidity of things that have never read ledgers. "The carver is the one that matters. Underhill. The bone-carver of Hollowmere."

Brannoc waited. He was good at waiting.

"The courier who died in Underhill's workshop carried a map," Corvane said. "Vane's map, or a copy of it. But maps are only paper, Lieutenant. Paper burns. Paper lies. The old texts are clear on this: the door at the dark moon does not open for ink. It opens for a key—and the key is not carried. It is *made*. Carved, by a hand that can hear the dead, from the bone of a willing corpse." He folded his hands on the desk. "There is exactly one such hand inside the ring. There has only ever been one. I let it live because a gift for listening seemed harmless next to a gift for opening. I was wrong, or I was lucky, and either way my luck has run out."

"You believe the Underhill carver can open the door," Brannoc said. Not a question. Brannoc did not ask questions; he restated conclusions so his superior could correct them.

"I believe the courier chose them. I believe the witch tried to sell the map and failed, which means the map is still with them, which means they know what they carry. And I believe"—and here Corvane felt the old tiredness settle over him, the weight he wore beneath the uniform, the weight of two decades of arithmetic—"that somewhere on the drowned chapel road, right now, a bone-carver is deciding whether my life's work ends this month."

He dismissed Brannoc with a nod and sat a long time after the door closed.

He thought about Theo Underhill, whom he had never met, and knew anyway: someone who had grown up hearing the dead complain of the cold, and had carved their markers kindly, and had told no one. Someone small, careful, practiced in staying invisible. Someone who would understand, better than most, the value of a quiet life.

That was the tragedy of it, Corvane thought. He and the carver wanted the same thing. Peace. Silence. A world that held its shape. If they could sit across a table, he could show them the ledger, the true one, and say: *I am not your enemy. I am the bandage. Tear me off and the wound breathes again.* He could offer what he had offered so few times and meant every time—forgetting, safety, a workshop in Hollowmere until the end of a long and unremarkable life.

Most people took that bargain. That was the saddest thing he knew about people, and the most reassuring.

He took up his pen and wrote the order that would put riders on the chapel road by midday, and his hand did not shake, because it never had.

Mercy, done properly, required a stomach. His had held for twenty years.

It would hold a little longer.
