## 6. The Drowned Chapel

The boy had no name for the chapel, so he made one up as they came down through the reeds: the singing house, because the wind moved through its broken bell tower and made a low, mournful note that never quite stopped. He could not have said the word *mournful*. He did not know the word. But he knew the feeling of it, the way he knew the feeling of most things—somewhere behind his ribs, in the place where other people kept their words.

Pip walked last, as always. It was easier at the back. He could watch both of them without being watched: Sable ahead with her staff cutting the reeds like a scythe, muttering about wet boots and the general treachery of the world; and Theo just behind her, carrying the map tube slung across their back, head tilted in that listening way of theirs that Pip had decided was his favorite thing about them.

They were all three soaked to the knee by the time they reached it. The chapel sat in the middle of a drowned meadow, half-swallowed—the water had taken the nave up to the windowsills years ago, and black water stood perfectly still inside the empty window frames, so that each arch held a rectangle of reflected sky. Only the east end still rose above the flood, a squat apse of grey stone with a door hanging by one hinge.

Sable tested the threshold with her staff and pronounced it, "Dry-ish, haunted-looking, and better than sleeping in a bog," which Pip understood perfectly, because it was what she said about everything, and he had learned that this was how she loved things. She complained them into being safe.

Inside, the apse smelled of stone and old water and something faintly sweet, like flowers left too long in a vase. There was a floor above the waterline—a raised platform where an altar must once have stood—and just enough room for three people to lie down if none of them minded elbows. The stained glass above the altar had mostly fallen out, but one panel survived: a figure with its hands open, palms up, giving or receiving, impossible to tell.

Pip put his palm against the glass. Cold. He looked at his own hand beside the painted one, smaller, chapped, the knuckles scabbed from the ash orchard. Then he took out his stub of charcoal and, on the flat of a loose floor tile, drew the figure with open hands. It came easily. Most things came easily to his hand that would not come to his mouth.

He was still drawing when he felt it—that prickle at the nape of his neck, the one that meant *watched*. He turned toward the broken doorway and the dark water beyond it, and for a moment he saw nothing. Then a ripple crossed the flooded nave, long and straight, against the wind.

He tugged Theo's sleeve and pointed.

Theo looked. Listened—not with their ears, Pip noticed, but with their whole face, eyes going distant and unfocused, mouth slightly parted. Whatever they heard, it made them go pale.

"Out," Theo whispered. "Pip—*out*, now—"

The first crossbow bolt took the altar rail where Sable's head had been a heartbeat before.

* * *

Afterward, Pip would remember it in pieces, the way you remember a dream you woke from too fast.

Sable moving—not away from the bolt but *into* the doorway, staff whirling, shouting words that were half spell and half pure fury. The water of the nave erupting as men waded out of the reeds on either side, six of them, seven, dark-cloaked, faces masked against the cold. A second bolt. Sable catching it somehow on her staff's iron shod end, the wood cracking, her laughing—actually *laughing*—"Is that all Corvane pays for? I've been robbed by better!"

Then the third bolt, and no laughter.

It went into her below the collarbone, high on the left side, and she went down against the altar steps with a sound that Pip felt in his own chest, a punched-out grunt, and then she was trying to get up, trying to keep herself between the doorway and the two of them, and failing, sliding down the stone with her hand pressed to the wound and blood coming through her fingers black in the moonlight.

Pip screamed. No sound came. Nothing ever came. He screamed anyway, silently, his whole body one clenched knot of *no*, and grabbed the first thing his hand found—a shard of fallen stained glass, heavy as a knife—and hurled it at the nearest man. It caught him across the brow and he staggered, cursing, and then Theo was there.

Pip had seen Theo use their gift exactly twice, and both times small: a grave-marker murmured over until the dead man's name surfaced in the grain of the wood; a pauper's skull asked politely which way a lost goat had wandered. Both times Theo had flinched afterward, hunched, ashamed, like someone caught talking to themselves.

This was not that.

Theo stepped past Sable's body—she was moving, breathing, cursing weakly, but down—and raised both hands over the flooded nave, and *spoke aloud* to the drowned chapel's dead.

"You," they said, and their voice came out strange, doubled, as though the water itself were speaking with them. "You who were baptized in this water and buried under this floor. You who watched this place drown. I am Theo Underhill, bone-carver of Hollowmere, and I am asking."

The water stopped rippling. Every ripple, everywhere, all at once—as if the whole flooded nave had become a single held breath.

"The men who are coming," Theo said. "And the way out that isn't the way in. Show me. *Tell* me."

For a moment, nothing. The masked men in the reeds hesitated, looking at one another, and Pip understood that they had come expecting fugitives, not whatever this was. One of them raised his crossbow again—

—and every corpse in the drowned nave rose at once.

Not walking. Not lurching. Just *rising*, gently, the way silt rises when a pond is stirred: bones lifting out of the black water in slow spirals, dozens of them, hundreds, ribcages and femurs and small delicate hand-bones, turning in the moonlight that came through the window frames. They did not threaten. They simply *were*, everywhere, between the hunters and the altar, a slow galaxy of the dead turning around Theo's voice.

One of the masked men made a sound Pip had never heard a grown man make and ran. Another fired a bolt that passed through a drifting ribcage and hit nothing. And Theo stood with their arms up and their eyes gone white-pale and said, in that doubled voice, quiet enough that only Pip, closest, could hear the words beneath the words:

"*Under the altar. The founders' walk. Stone that sounds hollow. The water remembers the road.*"

Then Theo's knees buckled, and Pip caught them—one arm around the bone-carver's waist, dragging them back toward Sable—and the bones sank, all together, sinking back into the black water like a breath let out, and the hunters fled, crashing away through the reeds, because there are things even paid men will not stand inside of.

* * *

Sable was still conscious, which she announced by saying, "If either of you tells anyone I fainted, I will haunt you personally, and I have references."

She was grey-faced, and the bolt-head was still in her, snapped off near the fletching, sunk deep below the collarbone. She talked constantly while Theo worked—talking, Pip realized, to keep them calm, to keep *herself* calm—while Theo cut the head out with the small sharp knife they used for carving names into bone, and packed the wound with moss, and bound it with strips boiled briefly in chapel water over a fire of broken pew-wood. Sable told a story about cheating a tax collector in Marrowford with a cursed pig. Pip did not know the words for most of it, but he knew the shape of it: she was telling them *I am fine, I am fine, look how fine I am,* the way people sing when they are afraid of the quiet.

By the time it was done, dawn was a grey suggestion through the broken windows, and Sable had finally gone truly unconscious, breathing shallow but steady, propped against the altar where the painted saint watched with open hands.

Pip sat between the two of them—Sable's hot forehead against his shoulder, Theo's head bowed over their own hands—and he took out his tile and his charcoal and drew what Theo had said, because he had listened, really listened, the way he always listened, with everything except his ears.

*Under the altar.* He drew the altar. An arrow down.

*Stone that sounds hollow.* He drew the altar again, cracked open like an egg.

*The water remembers the road.* He drew the flooded nave, and then, beneath the water line, a path of stones going out the far side, dry-topped, hidden, a road wearing the flood like a blanket.

He tapped Theo's shoulder and held it up.

Theo stared at the drawing for a long time. Then at Pip. Then at the drawing again, and something moved across their face that Pip had no word for either—wonder, maybe, or fear, or the exact place where those two feelings meet and can't be told apart.

"The map shows a causeway here," Theo said slowly. "I thought it was a mistake. A drawing error." They looked at the boy properly, searching his face as if seeing him for the first time. "You've never seen the map. You couldn't read it if you had. So how do you know what's under this chapel, Pip?"

Pip shrugged. It was an honest shrug. He didn't know. He only knew that places sometimes spoke to him the way they supposed they spoke to Theo, not in voices but in *shapes*, in the itch of a line that wanted drawing. He pointed at himself, then at the map tube on Theo's back, then spread his hands: *I don't know.*

Theo was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind found the broken bell tower again, and the singing house sang its one low note.

"When we get out of here," Theo said at last, and their voice was rough, and they put their hand briefly, clumsily, on top of Pip's head like a blessing, "I'm going to teach you words. All of them. Every single one, if that's what you want. Someone should have done it years ago."

Pip blinked hard and looked down at his tile and drew, very small, in the corner where no one would see it unless they knew to look: three stick figures, standing close together, under one roof.

Then he pointed at the altar, and at his drawing of the hollow stone, and raised his eyebrows.

Together, quietly, while Sable slept her fevered sleep, they worked the altar loose. Beneath it, exactly as the dead had said and the boy had drawn, a slab of stone sat proud of the others, and when Theo rapped it with the staff's broken haft, it rang hollow as a bell. Carved into its face, worn nearly smooth, was a spiral—the same spiral, Pip realized with a jolt that went through him like cold water, that appeared on the corner of the courier's map, the mark he had drawn once himself, weeks ago, on a wall in Hollowmere, without knowing why.

Theo pried the slab up. Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth that crumbled at the touch, lay a bundle: a second map, older, vellum gone amber, marked in faded ink with a route that began at the drowned chapel and ended at a cliff face with a door drawn in it—and beside the door, written in a dead language that Theo nevertheless read aloud slowly, haltingly, as if remembering rather than translating:

*"Only the heir may turn the key."*

Theo looked at Pip.

Pip looked at the drawing of the door, and his hand, all on its own, ached to copy it.

Behind them, Sable moaned in her sleep, and the fever climbed.
