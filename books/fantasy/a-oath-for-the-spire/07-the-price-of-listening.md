## 7. The Price of Listening

The fever came with the rain.

It arrived quietly, the way debts do—Sable woke shivering under the lean-to they'd built against a fallen wall, and by noon she was burning, her skin hot as a hearthstone, her jokes gone thin and glassy. Theo knelt beside her with a damp cloth and watched the hedge-witch's hands, those quick, clever hands that could coax a spark from wet tinder and a smile from a suspicious stranger, tremble against the blanket.

"It's nothing," Sable said, and then ruined it by laughing, a dry, cracked sound. "Nothing. A scratch. I've had worse from a cat."

The wound in her side was not a scratch. It had gone the color of storm clouds at the edges, and the skin around it was tight and shining. Theo had cleaned it, packed it with the last of the moss-poultice Sable kept in her coat, whispered every charm Sable had taught them on the road—little borrowed things, hedge-craft, the kind of magic that was really just persuasion aimed at the body. The charms slid off the wound like water off wax.

Because of the border stones.

Theo had understood it intellectually when Sable explained, weeks ago, sitting by a fire: magic pooled in Hollowmere like rain in a bowl, but out here, past the stones that ringed the valley, it thinned to nothing. The stones didn't just keep people in. They kept the world's magic out. Everything inside the ring was preserved, pickled, sealed away from anything that might change it. Including sickness. Including cures.

"The feverfew," Theo said. "The white-flowered kind you showed me. It grows past the stones, doesn't it. You said so."

Sable's eyes opened. They were too bright. "I said a lot of things. Most of them were lies, and the rest were exaggerations."

"You said it grows on the ridge past the western stones. Where the old road used to run."

"I said that," Sable admitted. "I also said I'd never sell you out, and look how that went." Her hand found Theo's wrist, grip weak but insistent. "Theo. Listen to me. The fever will break or it won't. But if you go past the stones—"

"The stones repel magic. I know."

"You don't know. Nobody knows, because nobody's tried, because everyone with any sense stays inside them." Her breath caught. "The stones aren't a wall, Theo. They're a wound in the world. The whole country on the other side of them is a wound. You'd be walking into the place where the catastrophe happened, and you—" She stopped, and Theo watched her decide not to finish the sentence.

"You'd be carrying me into it," Theo said. "That's the plan. Pip carries the packs. I carry you."

Pip, who had been sitting cross-legged at the mouth of the lean-to, sketching in the dirt with a stick, looked up. He didn't say anything. He never said anything. But he looked at Theo with that steady, unnerving attention he gave everything, as if he were memorizing it, and then he went back to his drawing—and Theo saw, with a cold turn of the stomach, that he was drawing the ridge. The western ridge, with its crown of stones, though they were still two days' walk from it and Pip had never been there in his life.

The boy drew places he'd never seen. He had been doing it since the ash orchard. Theo had stopped pretending not to notice.

"We rest tonight," Theo said. "Tomorrow we move."

* * *

The dead found Theo that night, as the dead always did.

They came with the dark, out past the firelight, a rustling at the edge of hearing like wind through dry stalks. Theo sat up, careful not to wake Sable, and listened. This was the gift they had never asked for and never been able to put down: the voices of the dead, thin as smoke, persistent as weather. In Hollowmere they had been a constant complaint—the cold, the damp, the neglect of their graves, the terrible smallness of being remembered by no one. Out here they were different. Out here they were *hungry*.

*Carver,* said the nearest voice. It was an old voice, a man's, worn smooth like a river stone. *Carver of bone. We smell the knife on you.*

"I don't have a knife," Theo whispered.

*You have the knife in you. The one your grandmother's grandmother carried. The one that made the first marker in Hollowmere. It sleeps in your hands. We can feel it dreaming.*

Theo's hands curled closed. They had carved grave-markers since they were nine years old, small stones and smaller bones, names and dates and little comforts scratched into the surfaces of death. They had never thought of it as a lineage. They had thought of it as a chore, a secret, a burden.

"What do you want?"

*The same thing you want,* the voice said, and others gathered behind it, a congregation of whispers. *We want the door open. We want out of this pickle-jar of a country. We want to stop being the past tense of a place that was erased.*

"I don't know how to open any door."

*You do. You've known since the courier died in your workshop and put his blood on your hands.* The voice was gentle, horribly gentle. *The map is half the key, carver. The other half is bone. Bone carved by a willing hand from a willing dead. That is the old bargain. That is the only bargain. The door out of nowhere opens for nothing less.*

The fire popped. Sable murmured in her fever and turned her face toward Theo, and even asleep she looked like someone bracing for a blow.

"I'm not killing anyone," Theo said.

*We know,* the dead said, and there was something almost like pity in it. *That's why we're offering.*

* * *

The dead man's name was Aldous Vane, and he had been dead for sixty years, and he would not stop talking.

They found him the next evening, or rather Theo found him—Pip led them to a shallow fold of hillside where the old road had once run, and there, under a cairn of stones no bigger than a fist-pile, Aldous Vane lay folded like a man who had lain down to rest and simply never gotten up. His bones were clean. His boots had rotted. His femurs were long and pale and whole.

*Here,* he said, cheerfully, the moment Theo knelt. *You found me. Took you long enough. I've been waiting on this hill since before your grandmother was born, and let me tell you, the waiting is the worst part. Worse than the dying, even. The dying only takes a moment. The waiting takes everything else.*

"You're the one who spoke to me. Last night."

*I'm the one who's been speaking to everyone for sixty years. You're the first one who answered.* A pause, and then, softer: *You're the first one who could.*

Theo looked at the cairn, at the clean bones, at Sable lying gray-faced on a bedroll ten feet away with Pip sitting beside her, one small hand resting on her arm as if holding her down against the wind of her own fever. The rain had stopped. The light was going amber and long, and somewhere past the western ridge, past the border stones, a white flower was growing that could save her, and the stones would not let Theo's magic cross to reach it, but a door—a door cut from a willing dead man's bone, opened at the dark moon, three nights from now—would let *them* cross. All of them. The living and the dead and the whole erased country besides.

"You understand what I'd be taking," Theo said. "Your femur. Part of you. Carved into a key."

*Carver, I have been a pile of stones on a hill for sixty years. Do you know what I wouldn't give to be a pile of stones that *did* something?* The voice laughed, a dry rustle like leaves. *Take the leg. Take both legs. Take the story with it—my name was Aldous Vane, I was a surveyor, I mapped the old road before they erased the roads, and I died walking it, and I have regretted nothing except the waiting. Carve me into a door and I will be the most useful dead man in the history of dead men.*

"And the price," Theo said. Their throat was tight. "The dead said—the bargain takes something from the carver. From me."

The whispers went quiet. All of them, the whole rustling congregation of the hillside, every voice Theo had heard since childhood—the complainers, the cold ones, the lonely ones, the chorus that had been the only constant company of their whole small buried life—silent, listening.

*Yes,* Aldous Vane said at last, gently. *That's the other half of it, isn't it. The knife cuts both ways, carver. You take bone from a willing dead, and the dead take something from you in return. The hearing. The voices. All of them. Forever.*

Theo sat back on their heels. The amber light lay on the cairn stones like honey, like something sweet and sealing.

All their life, the voices had been the thing that made them strange. The thing they hid. The reason they stayed small, stayed quiet, stayed in the workshop with the shutters closed, carving markers for the dead while the living passed by without a glance. They had dreamed—hadn't they?—of silence. Of waking in the morning to nothing but birdsong and the ordinary noise of the world, unhaunted, unburdened, *normal*.

And now that silence was being offered as the price of a door, it felt like being asked to go deaf. It felt like being asked to give up the only family they'd ever had—the dead, who had kept them company through every cold night of their childhood, who had complained and grumbled and gossiped and, in their own gray way, loved them.

*You're crying,* Aldous Vane observed. *Dead men notice these things. We have a lot of time for noticing.*

"I'm not crying." Theo wiped their face. "I'm pricing something."

*Ah.* A long pause. *That's what it is, then. That's what no one tells you about staying alive. It's not free. It never was. Every year in that valley, every marker you carved, every voice you answered—someone was keeping a ledger. And now the bill's come due, and you get to choose how to pay it.*

Theo looked at Sable, who had lied to them and stolen from them and taken a knife-wound meant for them, and who was burning alive with fever ten feet away. They looked at Pip, who was watching them with those memorizing eyes, who drew places he'd never seen as if the map lived in him, as if the erased country had left its coordinates in his blood.

*Three nights,* the dead murmured, all together now, a tide of whispers. *The dark moon. The door in the cliff. Carve, carver. Carve, and pay.*

Theo took out their knife—the small one, the grave-marker's knife, the one they'd carried since they were nine—and knelt at the cairn, and began, gently, to move the stones.

Aldous Vane began to hum.

It was, Theo thought, the first time in their life they had ever heard a dead man sound happy—and the thought broke something open in them, some door inside their own chest, and they kept working through the tears because the work was the prayer and the prayer was the price, and the price, at last, had a name.

Behind them, faint and feverish, Sable stirred and whispered a word they couldn't make out.

But Pip heard it. And Pip went very still, staring north, toward the cliffs—where, on the far horizon, three nights early, a light had begun to burn that was not any light Theo had ever seen.
