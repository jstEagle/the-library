## 8. Corvane's Mercy

Warden Corvane had learned long ago that the best way to catch a frightened animal was to stop running after it.

His men did not understand this. They were young, mostly—boys from the garrison towns who had never seen the border stones glow, who thought the Warden's office was a matter of chains and shouting and the honest pleasure of the chase. Let them ride hard, he told them. Let them crash through the ash orchards with their torches held high and their voices carrying for a mile in every direction. Frightened things run from noise. And when they run, they run along the only roads that exist.

So while Sergeant Hale and his six riders thundered up the river road, Corvane took two men and went the slow way: down through the terraced fields where no one looked at him twice, past the shrine at Coldbrook where an old woman sold honey cakes and asked no questions because she had been paid not to, and into the hills above the drowned chapel, where he sat on a flat stone like a man resting his feet and waited for the world to deliver three fugitives into his open hand.

It took four days. He had expected five.

He watched them come up the goat track in the grey light before dawn—the bone-carver first, moving with that careful, listening hunch he had seen in others of their kind, as though the ground itself spoke too loudly; behind them the hedge-witch, one arm bound against her ribs, her face the color of tallow but her mouth still set in the shape of someone planning to argue; and last the boy, barefoot, silent, turning his head constantly like a bird that has heard a cat it cannot yet see.

The boy was the one who saw him. Of course he was.

Corvane rose from the stone slowly, hands open and empty, and watched the boy's whole body go rigid—not with fear, he thought, but with recognition. That interested him. Children who had never left the valley should not know his face. Unless someone had told them stories about the Warden. Unless the boy had been told to run from him before he could even speak.

"Theo Underhill," he said, and the name landed on the carver like a thrown stone. "You'll want to hear what I have to say before you decide to run. The witch can't run far, and you know it."

* * *

They sat, because there was nowhere better to sit—a hollow below the ridge, out of the wind, with the chapel's broken spire showing above the water in the valley's throat like a drowned man's finger. Corvane had his men build a small fire, which was either hospitality or a message, and he was content to let it be both. The hedge-witch sat as far from him as the hollow allowed, her good hand resting near a knife she would not reach for, and the carver sat between them all, turning their cap over and over in their fingers, and the boy sat pressed against the carver's side, watching Corvane with eyes too old for his face.

"You killed a courier," Theo said at last. Not an accusation. A question wearing one.

"I stopped a courier," Corvane said. "What happened after that was between him and the road." It was true, or nearly true, and he had found that nearly-true things carried further than lies. "He was carrying something that doesn't belong to him. Or to you."

"The map."

"The map," Corvane agreed. "And I'd like it back. Along with the boy."

The hedge-witch made a sound low in her throat, half laugh, half snarl. "That's your offer? Hand over a child and walk home?"

"That is my offer, Sable of the Fenmarch. Yes." He let her name settle over her like cold water; he wanted her to understand how thoroughly he knew her, how little of her life was hidden from him. Her debts in Marrowgate. The master whose license she'd burned rather than surrender. "I know you're waiting for me to threaten you. I'm not going to. Threats are for people who need them, and I don't. I need only to show you something, and then you'll make the right choice on your own."

He reached into his coat and brought out a locket—not silver, nothing so kind as silver. Iron, black with age, its hinge fused shut by heat. He opened it with his thumbnail, the way he had opened it perhaps a hundred times in twenty years, always alone, always at night, and laid it in the firelight so they could see what was inside.

Not a portrait. A scrap of painted silk, scorched at every edge, still holding the ghost of a color that might once have been blue.

"What is that?" the carver asked.

"A piece of a banner," Corvane said. "From the country you're trying to open the door to. There were ten thousand banners like it, once. You've never heard of the country it belonged to. No one has. That isn't an accident, Master Underhill. That is the single most expensive act of magic ever performed on this continent, and I am one of eleven people alive who knows why it was done."

* * *

He told it plainly, because plainness served him best. He had rehearsed this telling for twenty years and never once been able to use it, and now that he finally could, he found he wanted them to understand—not to obey, not yet, but to understand. It mattered to him, in some stubborn chamber of himself, that they understand.

"There was a country east of the Spire mountains called Vessany. Rich, old, proud. Its mages had a gift the rest of the world envied: they could fold space, move a door from here to there, walk a hundred leagues in a step. For centuries they traded on it. Then, ninety years ago, the folding began to fail—and no one knew why until it was too late. The doors started opening onto wrong places. Onto fire. Onto the space between spaces, if there is such a thing, and what came through—" He paused. His voice did not shake; he had spent twenty years making sure it could not. "Eleven thousand people died in the first month. Not soldiers. Bakers. Children. Whole towns simply ceased, folded out of the world, and the ones that survived did worse than die. They came back changed, and they spread the change, and the mages of Vessany could not close what they had opened because closing it required a door, and every door was a wound.

"So they did the only thing left. They sealed the whole country inside a shell of unremembering. Every border stone from here to the coast. Everything inside the shell was erased—not destroyed, hidden. Pulled out of every map, every ledger, every memory outside the stones. The rest of the world woke one morning unable to say what lay beyond the Spire mountains, and slept soundly ever after. And the survivors inside—" he tapped the locket, "—were given a mercy so complete they don't even know they received it. Hollowmere. Ashford. Coldbrook. All of it. A valley full of the descendants of Vessany, living quiet lives, planting turnips, carving grave-markers, never knowing that the ground under their feet is the lid on a coffin."

The wind moved through the grass above the hollow. The hedge-witch had gone very still. The carver's hands had stopped turning the cap.

"And the door," Theo said quietly. "The door in the cliff."

"Opens once a generation. The seal thins at the dark moon—it can't be helped; the magic breathes, even caged magic. And each time it opens, everything Vessany was comes pushing at the crack. Ninety years of pressure, Master Underhill. Do you know what happens if it opens wide?" He leaned forward. "The erasure breaks. Not gently. Every door in the old country reopens at once, onto whatever is on the other side of them now, and the catastrophe finishes what it started—except this time it spills out here, into your valley, into the world that paid so much to forget. Eleven thousand dead became a hundred thousand. I've read the reports from the last opening, sixty years ago. One family slipped through before the Warden of that day closed it. Three weeks later, a village in the lowlands folded out of existence with everyone in it. That family meant no harm. They were just going home."

* * *

He let the silence do its work. This was the part the boys with torches never understood: the work was not done with iron. The work was done sitting on cold ground in the hour before dawn, telling the truth to someone who had the courage to hear it.

"I could take the map from you," he said. "I could put you in irons and carry you back to Hollowmere, and in a year you'd be a story children tell each other, and in ten years no one would remember there'd ever been a story. But I'd rather not. So here is my offer, and I give you my word—which I have kept for twenty years, ask anyone—that I will honor it exactly.

"Surrender the map. Surrender the boy. Go home to Hollowmere. Keep your workshop. Keep your neighbors. In the spring the Warden's stipend will find its way to you, as it finds its way to others who serve without knowing they serve, and you will live out your life in the safest place on this earth, because Hollowmere is not a prison, Master Underhill. It is a shelter. The walls around it are the only reason you have ever heard the dead whisper instead of scream."

He watched the carver's face as he said it, and he saw the thing he had known he would see. Not resistance. Longing.

He knew that face. He wore it every morning.

Because here was the truth Corvane had carried for twenty years, the truth that kept him at his desk past midnight reading reports no one else was permitted to read: he was not a jailer. He was a man standing on a lid. And every fugitive, every courier, every fool with a map was a hand reaching up from underneath, asking him to lift it—not knowing what lifting it would cost. He had buried friends in the lowlands village. He had held the scorched banner of a country that no longer existed and sworn that no one else would die of remembering it.

"You think I'm cruel," he said softly. "I am the most merciful man you will ever meet, and mercy is heavy, and no one thanks you for carrying it. Your gift—the voices. I know what they cost you. I know you've never slept a whole night through. Give me the map and the boy, go home, and I will give you this in return: a draught, brewed by the last mage inside the stones, that will quiet the dead forever. Silence, Master Underhill. Real silence. A night with nothing in it but your own breathing. You've wanted that since you were a child. Don't pretend you haven't."

The carver's breath caught. Beside them, the boy had gone white, and the hedge-witch's hand had closed over the child's shoulder—but it was the carver Corvane watched, the carver whose eyes had gone somewhere far away, somewhere warm and dark and utterly silent, and stayed there a moment too long.

There it is, he thought, with something almost like tenderness. There is the part of you that wants exactly what I want. We are not so different, you and I. We are both tired of hearing.

"So," he said, and spread his empty hands. "The map. The boy. And in exchange—safety, silence, and a life where no one ever again asks anything of you at all."

Above the hollow, the sun crested the ridge, and the light fell across the drowned chapel, across the water, across the faces of the three people who had one night left to decide whether the world would end or begin—and Theo Underhill looked at the Warden for a long, long moment, and then looked down at the mute boy clinging to their side, and Corvane felt, for the first time in twenty years, the lid beneath his feet shift.
