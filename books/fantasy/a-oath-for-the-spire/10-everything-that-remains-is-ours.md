## 10. Everything That Remains Is Ours

Sable had seen crowds before—market days in Vell, the press of bodies at the ferry landings—but she had never seen a crowd *arrive*. They came through the door in the cliff like water finding a crack in a dam: first a trickle, a woman with a child on her hip and ash still in her hair, then a family of four blinking against the grey dawn light as though they'd never seen weather before. Then more. Dozens. Then so many that Sable stopped counting and simply stood on the wet grass with Pip's hand locked in hers and watched a country pour itself back into the world.

They were thin, most of them. Their clothes were fine once—embroidered collars gone soft with age, silver buttons polished to nothing by decades of nervous thumbs—and they moved like people who had spent a very long time being quiet. A man at the front of them carried a lantern that burned without oil, some last ember of the old country's craft, and its light was the color of honey and looked, to Sable's eyes, like the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

Behind it all, the door ground slowly shut.

No—not shut. It settled into the cliff face like a scar closing, leaving only the faintest seam, a line of paler stone where a keyhole had been. Theo stood before it with their hands hanging empty at their sides, and Sable knew, watching the set of their shoulders, that the silence inside them must be absolute. The dead had been Theo's constant company since childhood—the complaining, the grieving, the endless cold murmur of Hollowmere's bones—and now there was nothing. Sable had lost things before. She knew what it was to reach for a habit and find it gone.

She went to stand beside them. She didn't say anything. There wasn't anything worth saying that wasn't cheaper than the moment deserved.

* * *

Corvane's men formed up on the ridge an hour after dawn, and for one long, sickening minute Sable thought the story would end the way stories like theirs always ended—in fire and arithmetic.

There were perhaps forty riders, black-liveried, the Warden's white hand stitched on their breasts. Below them, spilling down the hillside from the open door, were three hundred souls at least, and still coming: old men leaning on younger shoulders, children carried because they could not walk, a whole erased nation remembering how to be visible. The riders sat their horses and did not move.

Corvane rode down alone.

He looked older than he had in the chapel ambush—no, not older, Sable corrected herself. *Lighter*, somehow, as if twenty years of carrying a secret country had finally been set down, and he hadn't yet decided whether to miss the weight. He reined in ten paces from Theo and looked out over the crowd, and his face did something complicated that Sable, who had made a career of reading faces across card tables, couldn't entirely parse.

"Three," he said quietly. "I came for three."

"You came for a secret," Theo said. "It got bigger."

He was silent a long time. His gloved hand rested on his rein; his men waited above him, motionless, waiting for the order that would make them butchers or would never come. Sable found Pip's shoulder and pulled the boy half behind her, though there was nowhere in the world to hide from forty crossbows.

"I buried this country once," Corvane said at last, and his voice was not cruel. That was the worst part. It was tired, and certain, and kind in the way that does the most damage. "I buried it because what it woke, the last time its doors opened, ate two provinces. I have spent my life being the mercy no one thanked me for." He looked at the woman with the lantern, at the children, at the thin faces turned up toward a sun they'd been denied. "And now I am asked to kill three hundred people to bury it again."

"No one's asking you anything," Theo said. "You can ride away. Or you can stay, and learn their names, and help them plant. But you don't get to erase them twice. Not today."

The wind came off the new country and stirred the grass, and somewhere in the crowd a baby began to cry—a small, furious, living sound—and Corvane closed his eyes as if it struck him physically.

When he opened them, he turned his horse.

"Hollowmere will hear that the fugitives died in the ash orchard," he said, not looking back. "It's cleaner that way. For everyone." He paused. "Tell the carver—" He stopped himself. Started again. "Tell Underhill the valley's stones will stand unwatched. Let the dead whisper to whoever's left to listen."

Then he rode up the ridge, and his riders parted around him like water around a stone, and they were gone, and the country kept pouring into the morning.

Sable let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for three hundred miles.

"Well," she said. "I've been run out of six towns, and that's the first time anyone ever *withdrew* from me. I almost feel insulted."

Theo laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound, half sob—and then Pip slammed into them both at once, arms around whatever he could reach, and the three of them stood there in the trampled grass while a nation streamed past and nobody, for one whole minute, asked them for anything at all.

* * *

They buried the courier two days later, on a south-facing slope above the settlement that was already calling itself New Vell, though the survivors had other names for it that Sable suspected would win.

His bones had traveled the whole way in the leather tube, rattling against the map that had killed him, and Sable had privately thought it a grim sort of luggage. But Theo had cleaned each bone with oil and cloth on the journey's last nights, careful as a jeweler, and wrapped them in linen, and now knelt at the graveside with the small bundle and would not let anyone else touch it.

That was the thing about Theo, Sable had learned. They gave everything away and called it nothing.

The soil here was real soil—dark and loamy and smelling of rain instead of ash—and Theo dug with their bare hands rather than use the spade Pip offered. Around them the settlement went about its business: hammers knocking together the first true houses, someone arguing about a well, children running shrieking between the tent rows because children, apparently, recovered faster than anyone. The woman with the honey-colored lantern had hung it outside her doorway and let it burn out at last, its work done.

"He never told me his name," Theo said, when the grave was ready. "He just bled on my floor and made me part of something. I've been trying to decide if I should be angry about that." They lowered the bones gently down. "I'm not. I think it might be the best thing anyone ever did to me."

Sable crouched beside them and took a handful of soil. "For what it's worth, I've had worse introductions to people. I stole from half of my best friends before I liked them."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest. Different service entirely."

Theo smiled—small, tired, real—and covered the bones together with her, handful by handful, until the mound was done. Then Theo pressed both palms flat against the fresh earth and held them there, and Sable understood they were listening. Listening for a voice that wasn't there anymore. Waiting for a whisper that would never come, because Theo had traded every whisper they'd ever heard for this: a door, a people, a slope of dark dirt under an honest sky.

Their hands trembled once. Then they stilled.

"There's nothing," Theo said quietly. "Just... quiet. Like snowfall." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and clear at the same time. "It doesn't hurt the way I thought it would. It hurts like an amputation hurts. Clean. Finished." A breath. "I keep reaching for them and finding *me*. Just me. Just us."

"Just us," Sable agreed, and found, to her mild horror, that she meant it warmly.

Pip appeared then, as Pip always did exactly when silence became unbearable, waving a stick of charcoal and the slate he'd carried since the ash orchard. He drew with fierce concentration: a pot, steam rising, three stick figures seated around it. Then he pointed at Sable with the accusing authority only a mute child can muster.

"He says we missed breakfast," Sable translated. "He says if we let the porridge burn again he's running away to live with the lantern woman, who he claims can cook."

Theo wiped their face with a dirty sleeve and laughed, really laughed, and the sound went up off the hillside into the wide morning air of a country that existed again.

"Then we'd better go," Theo said, standing, brushing the grave-soil from their knees. "We wouldn't want to lose him to better porridge."

They walked down the slope together—three people who had chosen each other out of desperation and theft and blood, and stayed by choice, which was the rarer thing. Behind them the courier's grave settled into the earth. Ahead, smoke rose from cookfires, and someone was singing in a language that had not been sung aloud in a generation, and the border stones of Hollowmere stood far away and unwatched, keeping their whispering valley safe for whoever needed hiding next.

Sable walked between them, a disgraced hedge-witch with no debts left in the world, and thought: *everything that remains is ours.*

It was, she decided, the first honest fortune she'd ever owned.
