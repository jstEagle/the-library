## 2. Charms for a Lawless City

Morning came to the Lamplight Quarter the way it always did: gray water sliding down gray glass, and Zephyrine Sandoval sweeping his doorstep before anyone could see what the night had left there.

This morning it left nothing but leaves and a dead moth. He was grateful for that. Some mornings it left things he had to burn.

The boy was still asleep on the cot behind the counter when Zephyrine opened the shutters. He'd checked three times in the night — once with a candle, once without, and once simply standing in the doorway listening to the small, even sound of the boy's breathing, as if confirming that children who spoke the First Tongue in their sleep were permitted to keep breathing at all. The wards under the floorboards had hummed until nearly dawn, a low thread of sound like a struck glass, and then gone quiet.

Zephyrine did not let himself think about why.

He swept. He put the kettle on. He turned the sign in the window — SANDOVAL'S CHARMS & CURIOSITIES, the letters re-gilded so many times they stood up from the glass like little golden scars — and prepared to be harmless.

It was a craft, being harmless. He had practiced it for twelve years, and he was very good at it now, the way a man who has cut off his own hand becomes very good at doing things with the other one.

* * *

The first customer came at half past eight, which meant she'd been waiting outside since half past seven. Widow Anselm, third door down, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, her breath fogging the shop's cold air.

"Master Sandoval." She said it the way she always did, with a small bow of the head that was either courtesy or irony; after eleven years he had never decided which. "The rheumatism's back in my hands. Terrible this year."

"Terrible," Zephyrine agreed, and went to the shelf.

Here is what he sold: copper wire wound three times around an iron nail, sealed in wax, sold as a charm against joint-ache. It worked exactly as well as any copper wire wound around any nail, which is to say not at all, except for the part where a person believed it might, and belief loosened the grip of pain better than most medicine in Ashfall. There was no magic in it. He made sure of that. He sanded every charm smooth before it left the shop — ran his thumb along each knot and whisper and intention until nothing remained but object. A candle stub blessed for safe travel that was only a candle stub. A knotted red cord against nightmares that was only cord. A tin whistle that was, genuinely, just a whistle, though he charged extra for it because people trusted expensive whistles.

Once, these shelves had held real charms. Once, a knot of his tying could hold a door shut against a flood, and a word of his speaking could calm a spooked horse at forty paces. Now the magic lived in him like swallowed coals, banked and buried, and he sold people beautiful lies at two coppers apiece, and they loved him for it.

"The usual?" he asked.

"The usual." Widow Anselm counted out her coppers one at a time, slowly, the way old women do, making the transaction last. And then, as she always did, she lingered. Her eyes went past him, into the dim interior of the shop, cataloguing. "You've a light burning late these nights."

"I sleep poorly."

"Mm." She drew her shawl tighter. "My nephew's on the watch, you know. Corner of Tanner's Row. If ever anything troubled your sleep, Master Sandoval — anything at all — you'd only have to say."

There it was. The same sentence she'd been offering him for eleven years, wearing a different coat each season. *I know what you are. I have chosen not to know it harder.*

"You're kind," said Zephyrine, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

She left. Behind the counter curtain, a small face watched her go with an intensity that would have frightened Zephyrine if he'd had any fear left over after everything else.

* * *

The boy ate like the famine was personal. Zephyrine set out bread and dripping and a boiled egg, and the boy demolished all of it in the time it took Zephyrine to butter a single slice, then looked up with those enormous dark eyes and the particular shame of someone who has learned that hunger is a crime.

"There's more," Zephyrine said. "Eat."

The boy ate more. Slowly this time, watching Zephyrine between bites as if the permission might be revoked mid-chew.

He was perhaps nine years old. Thin as a lath, with the fine wrists and hollow cheeks of a child who had walked a long way on short rations. His hair was black and rain-curled, and his eyes — Zephyrine kept catching himself looking at them and forcing himself to stop — his eyes were the deep amber-brown of resin held up to firelight, and there was something in them that did not belong to any child of Ashfall. Something patient and old and slightly sideways, like a reflection in water that takes a half-second too long to move.

He hadn't spoken since the words in the night. Those words. Zephyrine had lain awake turning them over: four syllables in the First Tongue, the language of the realm beyond the Thorn Gate, a language that existed in this world now only in forbidden books and in the memory of one man who wished it didn't. The boy had spoken them in his sleep, clear as a bell, and the wards beneath the floorboards — wards Zephyrine himself had laid, twelve years ago, woven into the foundations of the shop directly above the gate — had sung back.

*Answered,* was the word his mind kept offering, and he kept refusing it.

"You'll need a name," Zephyrine said that morning, while the boy watched him grind dried lavender for the sleep-sachets that were, this month, his best seller. "Can't keep calling you 'boy.' The neighbors will talk, and talking is how people end up on corner posts."

The boy tilted his head.

Zephyrine looked at him — small, quick-eyed, perched on the stool like something ready to fly at the first loud noise — and heard himself say, "Wren."

Something flickered across the child's face. Not recognition. Recognition of the *shape* of the thing, maybe. A name is a small cage and a small gift at once, and the boy seemed to weigh both, and then he nodded once, decisively, the way a magistrate signs a writ.

"Wren," Zephyrine repeated, and felt the strange vertigo of having made a decision about another person's life that felt, absurdly, like the most important thing he'd done in years.

Wren spent the day learning the shop.

That was the only word for it. He trailed Zephyrine from shelf to shelf with his hands clasped behind his back like a tiny tax assessor, watching everything — the way Zephyrine measured wax, the way he tested knots by feel, the way he paused before each finished charm with his thumb pressed flat against it for three full seconds, sanding off whatever couldn't be seen. Wren watched that pause most hungrily of all. On the fourth charm, his lips moved, silently, counting the seconds. On the seventh, his hand drifted toward the workbench and Zephyrine said, quietly, "Don't touch," and Wren snatched the hand back as if the bench had teeth.

But he never stopped watching. That was the thing Zephyrine couldn't shake, all through the long gray morning, all through the trickle of customers. The boy watched the way starving people watch bread. As if every small trick of Zephyrine's hands were a meal being eaten in front of him.

* * *

At noon, Mrs. Pell from the pie shop next door sent over two meat pasties "for the poor orphan you've taken in, not that I'm saying you've taken in an orphan, Master Sandoval, I'm saying nothing of the kind." She delivered this while looking meaningfully at Wren, then meaningfully at Zephyrine, then meaningfully at the space between them, which contained the entire unspoken treaty of the Lamplight Quarter.

"Thank you, Mrs. Pell."

"She's got her mother's chin, whoever she belongs to." Mrs. Pell squinted at Wren. "Or his father's. Whichever. Children are all chins and eyes anyway." She departed with the satisfied air of a woman who had committed an act of charity and an act of treason in the same breath and found both agreeable.

By afternoon, half the street had invented a story about the boy. Zephyrine heard fragments of it through the open door: a nephew from the coast. A charity case from the Foundling Office. A ghost, said the baker's youngest, with the confidence of the very stupid. Nobody said the true thing, which was that nobody knew, and that in Ashfall nobody-knowing was the safest thing a street could own collectively.

Zephyrine understood, with a gratitude that sat in his chest like a stone he couldn't swallow, that they were protecting him. All of them. Badly, loudly, with pies and gossip and invented nephews — but protecting him. Twelve years of two-copper charms and free advice and mended hinges and remembered birthdays had bought him a wall made of neighbors, and he had never once let himself think of it as a wall, because walls implied there was something to be defended against, and thinking about that led to thinking about the gate, and he did not think about the gate.

He thought about the gate constantly.

* * *

The notice appeared sometime between four o'clock, when Zephyrine stepped out to buy tallow, and half past, when Tam Voss from the cooperage came in for a luck-charm for his daughter's apprenticeship and mentioned it with elaborate casualness.

"New posting on the corner," Tam said, examining a shelf of candle-stubs with tremendous interest. "Corner post. By the well."

"Was there?"

"Was there," Tam agreed. "Thought you'd want to know. Being as you're a man who reads everything and goes nowhere."

Zephyrine wiped his hands on his apron. Walked to the door. Stood in it a moment, feeling the whole street's attention settle on his back like birds on a wire, and crossed to the corner post by the well.

The notice was fresh paste and crisp paper, which meant a rider had come through within the hour, which meant the Office of Inquiry was moving faster than usual, which meant — his mind supplied, helpfully — that somewhere a report had already been filed. *Unregistered child. Lamplight Quarter. Possible irregularity.*

The notice read:

**BY ORDER OF THE OFFICE OF INQUIRY**

Be it known that the Crown offers a reward of fifty crowns for information leading to the recovery of any child found unsanctioned within the city walls, such child being suspected of foreign origin. Persons harboring unsanctioned children commit a felony against the security of the realm. Informants' names shall be held in confidence.

Inquiries directed to Inspector C. Hale, Office of Inquiry.

Below the text, where such notices usually bore the Crown's seal, there was instead a small stamped sigil Zephyrine had never seen on a public posting before: a circle bisected by a vertical line, rendered in iron-gray ink.

He knew that mark. Every child of the old circles knew it, the way sheep know the smell of the abattoir. It was the witch-finders' mark. The Office of Inquiry was the respectable public face — licensing, census, paperwork, the slow bureaucratic machinery of a state that had outlawed magic by degrees rather than decree. But the iron-gray circle belonged to the Order of the Broken Line, and the Broken Line did not do paperwork.

Fifty crowns. Enough to buy a family a year of bread. Enough to turn a neighbor.

Zephyrine read the notice twice, memorized it, and walked back to his shop at a pace he carefully calibrated to look unhurried. Behind windows and half-open doors, he felt the street watching him go, and he understood that they had all read it too, and that tomorrow the invented stories about the boy would grow one layer thicker, and that this was the neighborhood arming itself with fiction the way other streets armed themselves with bars on the windows.

Tam was still in the shop when he returned, holding the luck-charm and not looking at the curtain behind which Wren sat.

"Fifty crowns," Tam said to the shelf.

"A lot of money," Zephyrine said.

"It is." Tam set two coppers on the counter, deliberately, one at a time. "For some people. Depends what a man's used to, I expect. A man who's had good neighbors his whole life — that's worth more than fifty crowns, isn't it. Hard to price."

"Very hard," Zephyrine said. "The charm's on the house."

They looked at each other for a moment, two men conducting an entire negotiation in the language of candle-stubs and coppers, and then Tam nodded, touched his cap, and left.

* * *

That evening Zephyrine closed early. He bolted the door, dropped the blackout screen over the window, and lit a single lamp behind the counter, and sat for a long time doing nothing at all, which was the hardest work he knew.

Wren sat cross-legged on the floor amid the day's leavings — wax shavings, lavender stems, a length of red cord — and played. Or what looked like playing. He was winding the cord around his fingers in patterns, over-under-over-under, and Zephyrine realized with a slow, cold drop of the stomach that the patterns weren't random. They were knots. Binding-knots. Child-simplified, clumsy, the way a child's drawing of a horse is still recognizably a horse — but binding-knots nonetheless, from a tradition that had not existed on this side of the Thorn Gate for twelve years.

"Where did you learn that?" Zephyrine asked, before he could stop himself.

Wren looked up. Held his gaze. And then, slowly, deliberately, unwound the cord and began again — showing him. Over, under, through. The gesture was unmistakable even without language: *watch, I'll show you, watch again.* Teaching. The child was teaching him, patiently, the way you'd teach someone who had forgotten.

"I haven't forgotten," Zephyrine said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "I remember perfectly well. That's the problem."

Wren cocked his head. Then he pointed — at the floorboards. At the exact spot, Zephyrine realized, directly above the sealed hatch that led down to the Thorn Gate. The spot where the wards hummed.

"No," Zephyrine said.

Wren pointed again, insistent now, and his mouth opened, and Zephyrine braced for the First Tongue to pour out of him again, for the wards to wake and sing —

— and what came out instead was a single word, small and cracked from disuse, in plain, ordinary, market-day Ashfall common:

"Please."

Zephyrine stared at him. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, the city went about its lawless business, and on the corner post by the well, the paste of a fresh notice dried over older notices, and beneath the floorboards something vast and patient waited under seal, counting.

"Not tonight," Zephyrine said, which was not no, and they both knew it, and that frightened him more than the notice on the post, more than the iron-gray circle, more than fifty crowns and the name Hale written in a clerk's careful hand.

Because he had spent twelve years building a life out of refusals, and tonight, for the first time, he had answered a question about the gate with *not yet* — and *not yet*, he knew better than almost anyone alive, is the sound a seal makes in the moment before it breaks.
