## 3. What the Gate Remembers

The cellar door had always been the hardest part of Zephyrine's house.

Not because it was heavy — though it was, oak and iron, older than the shop above it and older than the street — but because of what waited at the bottom of the stairs. Twelve years of discipline lived in that door. Twelve years of walking past it every morning with his tea, every night with his lamp, never once letting his hand linger on the latch longer than a heartbeat. The city above had laws against magic; Zephyrine had made himself a stricter law against remembering.

But tonight he stood before it with a candle in one hand and Wren's small fingers wrapped in the other, and the boy was pulling.

Wren didn't pull hard. That was the strange thing. He pulled the way water pulls — patiently, inevitably, as if the stairs had always been his to descend and Zephyrine were merely late in following. He had been pulling all evening, actually, ever since the rain had come back and the lamps in the shop had guttered in some wind Zephyrine couldn't feel. Every time the draft moved through the room, the boy's head turned toward the floor. Toward *down*.

Zephyrine told himself it was nothing. A child's fancy. A fear of cellars, common enough in any city where cellars held rats and worse.

He told himself this while unlocking a door he had sworn never to open with witnesses present, and the telling did not help at all.

"Stay behind me," he said, and heard how foolish it sounded — giving orders in a language the boy didn't speak, to a boy who had already stepped past him onto the first stair.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the house should have allowed. Everyone who built over a gate learned this eventually: the Thorn Gate did not sit beneath the city so much as the city sat upon it, like a coat laid over something sleeping, and the space below obeyed the sleeper's proportions rather than the builder's. Forty steps by the count Zephyrine had made once, drunk on grief and cheap wine, the night after the sealing. He had counted them again sober and gotten forty-one, and had decided not to think about why.

Tonight he lost count entirely, because Wren's grip tightened at step twenty-something and did not loosen, and Zephyrine's whole attention narrowed to the small warm weight of the boy's hand and the way his breathing had gone quick and shallow, like a bird's.

The air changed first. It always did. Cold, but a living cold, the cold of deep water or high stone, carrying a smell that had no business beneath a charm shop in a rain-soaked city: pine resin, snowmelt, woodsmoke from fires no one here had lit. The far realm's breath, seeping through twelve years of seal. On bad nights — nights when the moon was wrong — Zephyrine could smell it even through the floorboards, and had convinced himself each time that he'd imagined it.

He had not imagined it. He had simply been very good at pretending.

The candle flame leaned sideways, toward the dark, as if drawn.

And then the last stair, and the chamber.

It was round, hewn from living rock by hands that predated the city's founding and possibly the city's gods. No torch brackets, no shelving, none of the practical clutter Zephyrine had installed elsewhere in his life to make spaces bearable. Just the walls sweeping up into shadow, and at the center, rising from the floor like the stump of a tree that had once held up the sky —

— the gate.

Even sealed, even scarred, even after everything, it stopped his heart for a moment, the way it always did. A ring of grey-white stone twice the height of a man, its inner face carved with the covenant-script in spirals so fine they looked like frost grown deliberate. And across that face, sealing it, the oath-work: interlocking lines of power that Zephyrine himself had cut, twelve years ago, with blood and grief and a certainty he no longer owned. They had been white once. Clean. Whole.

They were not whole now.

"No," Zephyrine said, and the word came out of him without permission, small and cracked in the vast dark.

He crossed the chamber without feeling his legs move. The candlelight found the damage and made it worse by making it specific: not one wound but dozens, scores, a constellation of chips and gouges scattered across the seal-lines like moth-holes in cloth. Each chip was shallow — a fingernail's depth, a coin's width — but they were everywhere, and they were old, some of them, their edges worn smooth by time. Months of time. Perhaps a year.

Someone had been coming down here. Someone had been chipping at the covenant, patiently, night after night, the way a prisoner scrapes at a wall with a spoon.

Or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome, settling into his chest like swallowed river-water — someone had been sending something down here. Something small enough to pass unnoticed. Something patient.

Zephyrine pressed his palm flat against the stone, over the worst of the scarring, the way a physician presses a wound to judge its depth. The stone was warm. Not stone-warm. *Skin*-warm. And beneath his hand, faint but unmistakable, he felt it: a pulse. Slow, arrhythmic, arrhythmic the way a dying thing is arrhythmic — a beat, then a long absence, then two beats crowding together as if making up lost time.

Twelve years he had kept this gate. In all that time, the seal had hummed under his palm like a struck bell fading — steady, diminishing, obedient. It had never pulsed. It had never *skipped*.

"It's failing," he whispered. "Oh, you stupid, stubborn, beautiful thing. You're failing."

Behind him, small feet pattered across the flagstones. He turned to warn Wren back — the chamber was no place for a child, the chamber had never been a place for anyone — and the warning died unspoken.

Because Wren wasn't afraid.

* * *

The boy walked to the gate the way other children walked to their mothers' knees. No hesitation, no wide eyes, none of the wary circling that even grown oath-keepers had done in the old days, approaching the covenant with the respect owed to a sleeping giant. Wren simply arrived, tipped his head back to look up the ring's full height, and then — before Zephyrine could cross the distance between them, before he could even draw the breath to shout —

— Wren put both hands flat against the stone.

The chamber lit.

Not with firelight, not with lamplight, but with a luminance that came from everywhere and nowhere, silver-blue, sourceless, the exact color of moonlight on snow in a country Zephyrine had spent twelve years teaching himself to forget. The shadows fled upward into the vaulting. The covenant-script woke line by line, spiral by spiral, each carved character filling with light like veins filling with blood, until the whole gate blazed with writing and the air itself seemed to lean inward, listening.

And Wren spoke.

"*Sethra vael, sethra vael, iorun kethis maren.*"

The First Tongue. Not broken, not halting, not the stumbling imitation of a child repeating sounds half-heard. Fluent. Rounded. The vowels long and clean as a bell-stroke, the consonants placed precisely where they belonged, in rhythms Zephyrine's body knew before his mind caught up — call-and-response, question-and-answer, the ancient cadence of the covenant-liturgies. His skin prickled from wrist to shoulder. His mouth went dry.

The boy was six years old, perhaps seven. He had arrived mute three nights ago, had spoken no word in any language since, and now he stood with his palms against the oldest working of power in either world and addressed it like family.

"*Iorun kethis maren,*" Wren said again, softer, almost coaxing. Almost pleading. *Wake, wake, I've come home.*

The gate answered.

It began as sound — a tone below hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, rising until the flagstones shivered and dust sifted from seams in the rock overhead. Then the light changed. It gathered, drew inward, concentrated itself into the air before the boy, spinning slowly, and out of that spinning silver came shapes. Not quite images. More than images. Memory given form, the gate's own memory, poured out like water from a cracked vessel:

A sky. A sky like torn silk, ribbons of violet and ash-grey streaming across a darkness that was not night, shot through with stars that burned green and gold and colors Zephyrine had no names for anymore, though once he had known them all. Mountains floating. Rivers running upward. And threaded through it all, unmistakable even rendered in the gate's ghost-light: the thirteenth moon.

Every realm-bound soul knew the thirteen moons. Twelve kept their faithful rounds, waxing and waning in their long slow harmony. The thirteenth was different — the covenant-moon, the oath-keeper's moon, the one that rose only when the covenant itself required witnessing. Old stories said it had hung full and constant in the far sky since the First Accord, unmoving, a promise pinned to heaven.

In the vision, it was moving.

It swelled, dimmed, swelled again — and with each swell, Zephyrine understood with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, that lived in the marrow rather than the mind: *one turn*. The moon was counting. One turn of the thirteenth moon, and then another, and another, each cycle faster than the last, each one leaving the moon smaller, cracked, redder — a wheel of blood rolling down a darkening sky.

Thirteen turns.

The vision showed him nothing else. No faces, no armies, no explanation. Just the moon, turning, turning, turning, and beneath it the gate — this gate — its seal-lines flickering like a guttering lamp, and around its base, small and dark and numerous, figures gathering. Coming home. Or coming to claim.

Then the light collapsed, all at once, like a fist closing, and the chamber fell back into candle-dark and silence, and Zephyrine found himself on his knees on the cold flagstones without any memory of falling.

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Wren stood with his hands still raised, hovering an inch from the stone now, as if unsure whether touching it again would be welcome. In the returned candlelight his face was very pale and very calm, and there was something in the calm that frightened Zephyrine more than the vision had — the calm of someone who has finally been understood, after a long silence, and is bracing for the understanding to be taken away.

"Wren." His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Wren, look at me."

The boy turned. Silver light still clung faintly to his irises, fading like embers.

"What did you say to it?" Zephyrine asked, knowing it was useless, knowing the boy couldn't answer in words — and then hating himself, because the boy *could*. Hadn't he just heard it? Fluency pouring out of a child the city said was empty?

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then he opened his mouth, and what came out was not the First Tongue.

"*Maren.*" One word. Careful, rounded, offered like a gift held out on an open palm. *Home.*

"I know," Zephyrine whispered, and discovered he was crying, which hadn't happened in twelve years, which he had believed himself incapable of, which he had privately counted among his life's quiet achievements. "I know it's home. Mine too. Once."

The boy crossed the floor and sat down beside him on the cold stone, close enough that their shoulders touched, and did not move away, and Zephyrine did not have the strength left to pretend that meant nothing.

* * *

Later — much later, upstairs, with the candles burned to pools and Wren asleep at last in the cot behind the counter, one hand curled around a wooden sparrow Zephyrine had whittled him — Zephyrine sat alone at his workbench and forced himself to do arithmetic.

The scarring was months old. Which meant whoever — whatever — had been working at the seal had started long before Wren appeared at his door. The boy was not the cause. The boy was a symptom, or a messenger, or — and here the thought turned over like a knife going in — the boy was the *answer*, sent ahead of something, the way a fever precedes the sickness or a dawn-star precedes the sun.

The gate had shown him thirteen turns. He didn't know how long a turn of the thirteenth moon lasted; no one alive in this city knew; the knowledge had been sealed away along with everything else, locked behind his own blood-work, carried off by the keepers who'd scattered, buried with the keeper who'd died. It might be years. It might be seasons. It might be weeks.

But the pulse under his palm had skipped. The seal had been chipped in a hundred places. The thirteenth moon was counting.

Denial, he realized, was a luxury he had purchased with twelve years of careful numbness, and the bill had just come due, and he could not pay it.

He looked at the notice tacked to the corner post that afternoon — *UNREGISTERED PERSONS TO BE REPORTED*, in the witch-finders' neat bureaucratic hand — and at the sleeping boy beyond the curtain, and at the trapdoor in the floor that led down to a dying god-door, and he understood, finally, with total clarity, that the peace he had built was already over. It had been over for months. He had simply been the last to know.

Tomorrow, he would have to decide what to do about Inspector Hale's questions.

Tonight, he blew out the candles, and lay awake listening to the house settle, and beneath the settling, faint as a heartbeat heard through a pillow, he could have sworn he heard the gate — pulsing. Skipping. Counting.

One turn down. Twelve remaining.

And somewhere out in the rain-dark city, someone else knew it too.
