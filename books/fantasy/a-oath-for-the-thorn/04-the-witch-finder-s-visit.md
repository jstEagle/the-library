## 4. The Witch-Finder's Visit

The knock came at midmorning, which was wrong in itself. Zephyrine's customers came at dusk or after, hooded and apologetic, sliding coins across the counter like contraband — because that was what they were buying, whatever the charms said on the little paper labels. Nobody official knocked on the door of Sandoval's Charms & Curios at midmorning with three crisp raps and the patience of a man who intended to be let in.

Zephyrine set down the spool of copper wire he'd been winding and looked through the shop's warped front glass.

Inspector Corvane Hale stood on the wet cobbles in a gray watchman's coat, hands clasped behind his back, studying the shop's sign as though it might confess something. He was younger than Zephyrine had expected from the voice that had asked questions at the licensing office last autumn — thirty perhaps, with a long, unremarkable face and hair the color of dishwater, combed with a precision that felt like a threat. He looked like a man who had been assembled from spare parts of other, more interesting men.

Behind him, two actual watchmen waited by the corner post where the notice had gone up yesterday. They were looking anywhere but at the shop. Zephyrine filed that away: even the watch didn't like standing next to Hale.

He unlocked the door. "Inspector. We're not open till evening."

"I know." Hale stepped inside without being asked, and his eyes did a slow circuit of the room — the shelves of bottled charms, the hanging bundles of dried rowan, the counter, the curtain over the back stair, the small figure sitting cross-legged on the floor between the workbench and the wall, sorting buttons into piles by color. "I'm here about your license renewal. The office sent me. Apparently there's a discrepancy."

"A discrepancy," Zephyrine repeated, keeping his voice pleasantly bored. Twelve years of practice made boredom easy. Boredom was armor you could wear in public.

"Your premises are registered for one occupant." Hale's gaze came down to Wren, who had not looked up from his buttons. "I see two."

"He's my sister's boy." The lie arrived so smoothly it frightened Zephyrine a little; it had been waiting behind his teeth, fully formed, since the night the wards hummed. "She's ill. Fevered lungs. I took him in until she's well enough to care for him again."

"And her name?"

"Marta Sandoval. She lives out past Tanner's Row."

Hale produced a small black notebook and wrote nothing in it, which was somehow worse than writing. He simply held the pen against the page and watched Zephyrine over the top of it. "Tanner's Row. And she couldn't send him to the parish foundling house? That's the lawful arrangement for an orphaned or displaced child. Registration, examination, assignment."

"She's not dead, Inspector, only coughing. The parish doesn't take children with living mothers."

"No," Hale agreed mildly. "They don't." He drifted along the shelf of charms, trailing one gloved finger an inch above the bottles without touching them, the way a man trails a finger above a sleeping animal's spine. "Curious trade you have. Little bottles. Little knots. What do they do?"

"They do what the labels say. Luck for travelers. Calm for colicky babies. A good-night charm that's really just lavender oil and suggestion." Zephyrine smiled the smile he used on suspicious neighbors. "Magic's a hanging offense, Inspector. I sell comfort. There's a difference, and the licensing office agrees, or I'd have been closed years ago."

"Oh, I've read your file." Hale picked up a bottle — *Safe Roads*, the label read, in Zephyrine's own careful hand — and turned it so the light caught the sediment inside. "Very clean file. Almost too clean. A man keeps a shop in Lowmarsh for twelve years and generates no complaints, no citations, no questions? Either you're very good—" he set the bottle back exactly where it had stood, a half-inch truer than before, "—or very careful. I find those are usually the same thing."

Wren's button-pile had stopped growing. Zephyrine could feel the boy listening the way stone listens to water — utterly still, taking everything in. He willed him not to look up. Children who wouldn't meet an inspector's eye were children inspectors remembered.

"Hale," Zephyrine said. "That's a northern name, isn't it? Corvane, too. Old family?"

Something moved behind the inspector's face — not warmth, but the place where warmth would have gone if the room had been built for it. "Old enough. My grandfather kept records for the Assize of Cleansing. My father audited confiscations. I audit licenses." He turned from the shelf at last and gave Zephyrine his full attention, and Zephyrine understood suddenly why the watchmen wouldn't stand near him: being looked at by Corvane Hale was like being weighed. You could feel yourself getting lighter, as if something were being skimmed off the top of you. "Which brings me to the discrepancy, Mr. Sandoval. Unregistered children are a matter of civic interest. Especially these days."

"These days?"

"There have been... irregularities." Hale said the word the way other men said *plague*. "Persons moving through the city who shouldn't be here. Sounds in the underways that the engineers can't account for. And now a notice from the capital — new protocols for any child of unknown provenance found within city walls." He opened the notebook at last and made a single unhurried mark. "Examination is mandatory. It's painless, mostly. Iron doesn't hurt them unless they're carrying something."

The word *iron* went through the shop like a cold draft. Zephyrine felt every ward he'd woven into the walls lean toward it and flinch.

"Iron," he said lightly. "Like a smith's touchstone. Clever."

"It's old cleverness." Hale closed the notebook. "May I see the rest of the premises? Cellars especially. Licensing requires an inspection of foundations every third year, and yours is..." he consulted a paper he hadn't needed to consult, "...overdue."

There it was. Zephyrine had known it would come — had known since the man walked in — but knowing didn't stop the drop of his stomach, the sudden vivid image of the stair behind the curtain, the door beneath it, the gate humming its cracked and counting song twelve feet below the flagstones. If Hale stood over that stone with iron on his wrist—

"The cellar floods," Zephyrine said. "Spring melt comes up through the marsh clay. There's six inches of standing water down there eight months of the year. I keep my stock up here precisely because of it — ask any of my neighbors, they'll tell you I've complained to the city about drainage for a decade." He let a note of grievance creep in, the small pettiness of an honest tradesman. "You're welcome to go down and ruin your boots if you like. Mind the third step, it's rotten."

It was a gamble. It was also true, which was the best kind of lie's foundation. Hale studied him for a long moment, and Zephyrine met the look with the mild, put-upon patience of a man with nothing under his floor but water.

Then Hale smiled. It was a well-made smile, fitted to his face like the coat fit his shoulders, and it reached his eyes the way lamplight reaches the bottom of a well.

"Another day, perhaps. When it's drier." He moved toward the door, then paused beside Wren as if remembering him. The boy sat among his buttons, head bowed, and Zephyrine saw his small hands curl slowly into fists against the floorboards. "Your sister's boy. How old would you say he is?"

"Seven. Perhaps eight. Marta was never precise."

"Does he speak?"

"He's shy with strangers." Zephyrine kept his voice level and hated how much effort it took. "Trauma does that to children, I'm told. Whatever brought him to my door, he wasn't talking much when he got here."

"No," Hale said softly. "They usually aren't." He crouched — smoothly, without hurry — until his face was level with the boy's, and Zephyrine's heart slammed once against his ribs. "Hello," Hale said, in a gentle voice that fit him worse than the smile. "My name is Corvane. Can you tell me yours?"

Wren raised his head.

For one terrible instant his eyes met the inspector's, and Zephyrine saw the boy's lips part — saw the shape of the First Tongue gathering behind his teeth like a bird deciding whether to sing — and Zephyrine crossed the shop faster than he had moved in years and put his hand on the boy's shoulder.

"Wren," he said. "His name is Wren. He's my nephew, he's seven, and he's tired. It's past his dinner hour."

The silence stretched. Hale looked at the hand on the boy's shoulder, then up at Zephyrine, and for a moment the well-made face went still and unreadable as the gate itself. Then he rose, brushed off his knee, and tucked the notebook away.

"Wren," he repeated, tasting it. "A bird name. Small birds get everywhere, Mr. Sandoval. Through cracks. Under doors. People rarely notice them until they hear the singing." He settled his coat. "I'll have the renewal papers sent round. No need to come to the office. I like to handle my own discrepancies."

He let himself out. Through the glass Zephyrine watched him pause at the corner post, say something brief to the watchmen — who both nodded too quickly — and then walk away down the wet street without hurrying, a gray man in a gray coat, leaving no ripples at all.

* * *

Zephyrine threw the bolt, dropped the shade, and stood with his forehead against the door until his breathing remembered how to be ordinary.

Behind him, small feet pattered across the floorboards, and a warm weight pressed against his side — Wren, leaning into him, the way a cat leans, all trust and no apology. Zephyrine put an arm around the boy automatically and then froze, because tenderness still startled him, arriving like weather.

"You did well," he murmured. "Not answering him. Very well."

Wren tilted his head back and looked up, and his eyes were the color of the sky through torn silk — Zephyrine had never managed to describe them better, even to himself — and in them was a question the boy had no words for. His mouth worked. His fingers plucked at Zephyrine's sleeve, urgent, frustrated, the whole vocabulary of him trapped behind his teeth.

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I know you have things to say. We'll find another way to say them. Ink, maybe. Or signs. We'll—"

"*Thirteen.*"

The word fell into the room like a coin into a well.

Zephyrine went absolutely still. Wren was watching him with fierce concentration, lips still shaped around the echo of it, and he said it again — clumsy, sanded rough at the edges, but unmistakable, common-tongue, *his first word in Zephyrine's language* — "*Thirteen.*"

"How—" Zephyrine crouched down to the boy's level. "How do you know that word? Who taught you—"

Wren shook his head. Not *no one taught me*. Something else. He pointed at the floor. At the boards. Down, down, at what lay beneath them. Then he held up his hands and began to move them — counting, Zephyrine realized, fingers folding down one by one, thirteen of them though he only had ten, and the boy's face as he counted was the face of someone reading a sentence written in fire.

Then Wren did something worse. He took Zephyrine's hand, pressed it flat against his own small chest, and spoke in the First Tongue — soft, fluent, sorrowful — and though Zephyrine had spent twelve years refusing to remember that language, the body remembers what the mind buries, and the words landed in him like stones dropped down a shaft:

*It counts. It is almost done. The moon will turn, and turn, and turn.*

And then, in the boy's halting, brand-new common tongue, one more word, offered like a gift and a wound at once:

"*Soon.*"

* * *

That night Zephyrine lit no lamps. He sat in the dark at the workbench with the shop's ledger open in front of him, and on a fresh page he wrote, in the cramped cipher he hadn't used in twelve years:

*Hale knows. Not everything — not yet. But iron, and questions, and a cellar he means to see when it's dry. Dry season starts in six weeks.*

*Thirteen turns. Six weeks. The arithmetic does not care which runs out first.*

He stared at the page until the ink dried, and beneath the shop, faint and patient, the gate pulsed. Skipping. Counting.

Twelve remaining.

He thought of Odile Ferran, three days north, who had sworn she'd gut him if she ever saw his face again. He thought of the others, scattered like dropped stones across half the map. He thought of a boy pressing Zephyrine's hand to his chest, saying *soon* in a dead language, and understood with terrible clarity that the choice he had been postponing since the rain-lashed night at his door had just run out of road.

In the morning, he would close the shop.

In the morning, he would go north, and start asking his family — what was left of it — for forgiveness he had no right to expect.

Because the thirteenth moon was turning, and Corvane Hale was already counting too.
