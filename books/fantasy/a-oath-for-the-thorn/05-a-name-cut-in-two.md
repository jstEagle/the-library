## 5. A Name Cut in Two

Before the shop, before the rain, before the man with the kind hands and the wards that hummed like a struck bell — there was the sky.

Not this sky. Not the gray lid that hung over the city, leaking its patient water. The other sky. The one like torn silk.

Wren did not have words for it in the tongue of this world, and in his own tongue he had no voice at all, so he kept it where he kept everything: behind his teeth, in the hollow of his chest, folded small as a moth in winter. But at night, lying on the pallet above the charm shop with the rain writing its long letters on the glass, he opened the fold and let it out.

The silk sky had been red-gold at the seams and pale between them, ripped in long ribbons that never mended, that drifted and tore further even as you watched. The moon there — he counted them sometimes, in the dark behind his eyes — there had been twelve moons, all of them whole then, all of them rising in their slow procession like a ring of lamps carried by a line of mourners. He remembered thinking, once, that they looked like a funeral. He had not known whose.

He knew now. He was learning, night by night, what a funeral looked like when it took twelve years to arrive.

* * *

He remembered the woman's hands best.

Hands came first, always, in the memories he could bear to touch. The woman's hands were brown as river clay, cracked at the knuckles, trembling. They had held his face the way you hold something already breaking — gently, and too tightly, both at once.

She had called him *key*.

Not his name. His name had gone into the gate with everything else, or perhaps it had gone earlier; he could not find it anymore. When he reached for it inside himself he found only the shape of the space where it had been, the way a missing tooth leaves a smooth place the tongue returns to again and again. The woman had said *key*, and the word had fit into that space so perfectly, so terribly, that he had believed for a long time it *was* his name. That he had been born to be turned in a lock.

Her tears had fallen on his face. Warm. He remembered being surprised they were warm. In the stories the old ones told, the ones who went through the gate wept cold tears, tears like frost, because grief on the far side of a door is a different substance than grief on the near side. But hers were warm, and they ran down his cheeks and into his mouth, and they tasted of salt and of something green, something growing, and he understood — without understanding, the way you understand falling — that she was giving him the last living thing she had.

*Go,* she had said, in the First Tongue, the language that lived in his chest like a second heartbeat. *Go, and do not look back, and do not come back. Little key. Go and live.*

And he had thought: *that is not living. That is being used.*

But he had gone. Because her hands were shaking, and because behind her the silk sky was tearing faster, and because a child knows, in the marrow-places where knowing cannot be argued with, when an adult is asking him to carry something too heavy for them. He had stepped backward into the gate's cold mouth, holding her eyes, refusing to blink, so that the last thing she saw would be him seeing her.

Then white. Then stone. Then rain.

This city's rain. Falling on him in an alley that smelled of fish and smoke, in a body suddenly heavy, suddenly *single*, suddenly only itself. On the far side he had been part of things — the way a thread is part of cloth, felt everywhere at once, belonging to the whole weave. Here he was a thread cut loose on a floor. One thread. Nothing.

He had cried then, silently, the way he did everything. No sound came out. It hadn't come out since the gate. He had screamed going through — he thought he had screamed; he could not be sure; memory on the far side of a door is a different substance than memory on the near side — and the scream had stayed behind, or been taken, or spent itself paying whatever toll the crossing charged. What remained was a boy-shaped silence walking through a strange city, looking for a door that hummed.

He had found it. Of course he had found it. He was the key; keys know their locks.

* * *

In the day, in the shop, Wren watched.

Watching was what he had instead of speaking, and he had gotten very good at it, the way a blind man gets good at listening. He watched the man — Zephyrine, though Wren didn't know the name yet, only *the keeper*, because the wards around the man's heart were the same make as the wards beneath the floor, and that told Wren what he was — watch the customers come and go. He watched the charms change hands: little knots of thread, little chips of stone, little bottles of nothing much, sold for coins and smiles. And underneath his watching, quiet as breath, he translated.

Because here was the thing no one in the city seemed to know, the thing that made Wren want to laugh and weep at once: the charms were not empty. They were nearly empty — sanded down, planed thin, magic pared away like rind from fruit until only the faintest ghost of it remained, safe enough to pass any law, faint enough that maybe even the maker had convinced himself it was nothing. But magic is never nothing. Magic is a language, and these charms were whispers of it, and Wren heard every whisper.

The knot of red thread on the fishwife's wrist still remembered how to hold. The chip of gray stone in the chimney-sweep's pocket still remembered warmth. Small memories. Harmless memories. But memories — and memories were the one thing the far realm was bleeding out of itself, the one thing dying there faster than light dies at dusk.

Wren would hold those whispering objects in his cupped hands when Zephyrine wasn't looking, and feel them like the warm bodies of small animals, and ache.

*Ache* was another word he had no sign for. He had invented signs for most things, in the years alone — a tap on the collarbone for *hungry*, two fingers dragged under the eye for *sad*, a fist pressed flat over the sternum for the big ones, the ones that had no neat edges. But ache — the specific ache of feeling a whole world starving through a wall too thick to climb — that one he kept wordless even in his own tongue. Some things should not be made portable.

* * *

At night the dreams came, and the dreams were the gate's way of talking, and lately the gate had a great deal to say.

It spoke in moons.

Twelve of them, hanging in the torn silk sky, each one round and silver and whole. And beneath them, thirteenth — a dark place in the procession, an empty lamp, a socket waiting. Every night the dream was the same except for one detail, and the detail was this: each night, one of the twelve moons was a little thinner. A little more eaten. A little more like a coin worn past reading.

First night, the smallest moon had a shadow on its edge, no bigger than a thumbnail.

Last night, three of them were more shadow than silver.

Wren woke from that one with his hands pressed flat against the floorboards, as if he could feel through the wood, through the stone, down into the deep place where the Thorn Gate sat in its chamber like a heart in a chest, and hold it steady by pressure alone. The floor was cold. The house was silent. Somewhere below, faint as a pulse felt through a wrist, the gate hummed.

And the humming was wrong. He had known gates his whole short life — had been raised among them, had been *made* for one, if the woman's word *key* meant anything — and he knew the sound a healthy gate made versus the sound of a failing one. A healthy gate hummed like a full hive, like a choir holding one long note, like a mother humming over a cradle. This one hummed like a singer running out of breath. Like a note being held past endurance by a throat that was starting to tear.

Someone was chipping at it. He had felt that too, in the nights before the shop, before the keeper — a rhythmic wrongness, tiny taps of damage, patient as dripping water. Someone on one side or the other was picking the lock from outside.

Or — and this thought arrived some nights and sat on his chest like a cat, heavy and uninvited — someone was picking it from *inside*. From the anchor-place. From wherever the one who held the gate had been holding it for twelve years.

Wren pressed his palm to the floorboards and sent what he had down through the wood: not words, not yet, but the shape of attention. *I hear you,* the shape said. *Whoever you are. I hear you holding on.*

For a moment — one heartbeat, two — the humming changed. Warmed. Turned toward him like a face turning toward a candle in a dark room.

Then it steadied, and went back to its long note, and Wren lay in the dark with his heart hammering and understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first: whoever held the gate was almost gone. Twelve years was past the end of what a soul could give. The note would break soon, moon-thin or not, chipped-at or not. The covenant was ending the way an old rope ends — not with a snap but with fibers letting go, one by one, quietly, until the load swings free.

The second: they would want him for it. The woman had known it; that was why she had wept. A child of both realms, born on the seam of the worlds, could slide into the anchor-place without dissolving — could hold the covenant the way no full-blooded soul of either side could. Could hold it *and survive*, which was more than anyone else could promise.

Survive. But not stay whole. The gate would take his childhood like a toll. His voice — what little the crossing had left of it. His self, spoonful by spoonful, year by year, until a boy became a door.

Going back meant dying. Staying meant becoming the lock he was cut to turn.

And there was a third thing, a small third thing, that he told no one, not even the dark: some nights, exhausted, lonely past the bottom of loneliness, he caught himself *wanting* it. Wanting to matter that much. Wanting the gate's arms around him instead of the cold. Wanting to be needed so completely that no one could ever send him away again.

That wanting frightened him worse than the witch-finders with their iron cuffs. Because doors do not choose to open. Keys do not choose to turn. And if he let himself become a thing that was only useful —

— then he would be exactly what the woman's trembling hands had made him, and her tears would have been wasted, and he would rather die than waste them.

* * *

Morning came gray through the shutters. Downstairs, the keeper moved through his rituals: kettle, counter, charms laid out in rows like a sentence being carefully mispronounced. Wren sat on the top stair, chin on his knees, watching through the banister rails.

The keeper glanced up — he always glanced up, quick, guilty, as if checking whether the boy was still real — and their eyes met through the rails.

And Wren, who had not spoken in four years, who had crossed a dying world and a locked gate and a hostile city without making a sound, felt the word rise in him like a bubble through deep water. Not in the First Tongue. In theirs. The hard, blunt, honest language of this side, the language of markets and laws and rain.

He didn't say it. He held it. He was saving it, the way the woman had saved her tears — spending nothing until the moment it mattered.

But he shaped it, silently, against the roof of his mouth, and the shape of it was this:

*Thirteen.*

Below, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the gate hummed — and somewhere out in the wet streets, a bell began to ring.
