## 6. The Exile at the Market

The market on Cordwainer's Row smelled of wet wool and frying onions, and Zephyrine hated how much he loved it.

Twelve years ago he would have walked this street with his hood down, laughing at Odile's haggling, letting Merrin drag him toward the honey-cakes while Tam argued with a fishmonger about the freshness of something that had clearly been swimming yesterday. Now he walked it with his collar up and his eyes moving — always moving — cataloguing exits the way other men catalogued faces. The corner post where the watch nailed its notices. The alley behind the tanner's that smelled too foul for anyone to follow into. The gap between the cooper's shed and the wall, just wide enough for a boy.

Wren walked at his side, one hand hooked in Zephyrine's belt-loop the way he'd done since the second night, when Zephyrine had tried to leave him sleeping by the hearth and found the boy standing silent in the dark doorway, watching him with those grave, unblinking eyes. *I go where you go,* the grip said. It was the only conversation they had most days, and Zephyrine had begun, to his quiet alarm, to find it sufficient.

"Stay close," he murmured, though the boy always did. "And if I squeeze your shoulder twice, you run for the shop and don't look back."

Wren looked up at him. He didn't nod — he never nodded, Zephyrine had noticed; agreement from Wren was expressed in stillness, refusal in motion — but his fingers tightened once in the fabric of Zephyrine's coat, which was as close to *yes* as the boy ever gave.

The morning was doing its best. Rain had come in the night and washed the soot off the eaves, and now the sun was attempting the cobblestones with limited success, turning every puddle into a scrap of tarnished silver. Vendors called out over baskets of late apples and bolts of grey cloth. A woman sold roasted chestnuts from a brazier whose smoke curled up sweet and sharp. Ordinary life, ordinary noise. Zephyrine had spent twelve years learning to move through it like water through reeds — present, unnoticed, gone.

He was buying apples — three, because Wren ate them whole, core and all, with an intensity that suggested fruit was a novelty — when he heard it.

The First Tongue.

Not from Wren. From behind him, low and musical, spoken the way you'd speak a language you dreamed in: two words, soft, addressed to no one. Or so it seemed.

Zephyrine went cold all over, the way he imagined a man feels the assassin's blade before he hears the footstep. Twelve years since he'd heard that language outside his own head. Twelve years since the gate had last breathed it against his palms. The words were simple — *the sky is torn* — a child's phrase, a traveler's phrase, and they fell into the market-noise like a coin into a well.

He turned slowly, apples forgotten in his hand.

The woman stood by the chestnut brazier, and she was watching him with an expression he recognized because he wore it himself every day of his life: the careful blankness of someone listening for their own name in a stranger's mouth. She was perhaps forty, lean as a whippet, with close-cropped black hair going iron at the temples and a scar running through her left eyebrow like a signature. Her clothes were good wool, city-cut, deliberately forgettable. But her boots were wrong. City boots didn't have soles stitched in that flat, overlapping pattern — a pattern Zephyrine knew, because he'd resoled a pair exactly like them once, twelve years ago, for a friend who'd crossed over and back and crossed over again until the gate took its toll.

She said, in common speech, mild as milk: "You dropped your apple."

Zephyrine looked down. One of his three apples lay in the gutter, rolling gently against a cobblestone. He hadn't felt his hand open.

"No matter," he said. "It was bruised."

"Most things are." She smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the corners, practiced everywhere else. "Lovely morning, though. After the rain."

"It is," Zephyrine said, and turned back to the stall, and put a coin in the vendor's palm, and did not run, because running would confirm everything, and did not stay either, because staying would confirm more. He moved down the row with Wren tucked against his hip, and let the crowd take them, and counted in his head the way he hadn't needed to count in years. Thirty steps to the corner. Forty to the alley. If she followed—

She followed.

Of course she followed. She didn't hurry; that was the frightening part. She simply drifted along the edge of the market like a leaf in an eddy, and when Zephyrine paused at a ribbon-seller's cart for no reason except to see if she'd pause too, she paused, and examined ribbons with genuine interest, and said to the seller, "That green would suit my sister," and bought it.

Zephyrine walked. She walked. The market swallowed and released them, swallowed and released, and finally, near the mouth of Tanner's Alley where the smell ran interference for any listener, she caught up and fell into step beside him as naturally as if they'd arranged it.

"You shouldn't speak it here," Zephyrine said quietly, eyes forward. "Whatever it is you want."

"I want nothing dangerous." Her voice stayed pleasant, pitched under the market's murmur. "A conversation. You know how rare those are, for people like us. People who can't talk about the weather without checking the wind first."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." She unwrapped the green ribbon, considered it, wound it around two fingers, unwound it again. "The boy speaks it fluently. Not learned-fluent. *Born* fluent. His accent has the old river-bend in it — the way they rounded the vowels before the Sundering, before the dialects split. You can't teach that. You can only be from there." She glanced sideways at Wren, and her expression did something complicated — hunger and grief fighting for the same ground. "Hello, little one. *Vesh-ka orun?*" — *are you well?*

Wren stopped walking.

Zephyrine felt it through the hand on his belt — the sudden dead weight of the boy going rigid — and then Wren turned, slowly, and looked up at the stranger, and his lips parted, and what came out was a flood: fast, low, urgent, the First Tongue pouring out of him like water from a cracked jar, words tumbling over words, more than Zephyrine had heard him say in total since the night at the door.

The woman's face broke.

There was no other word for it. The careful pleasant mask cracked straight down the middle, and underneath was something raw and starving and devastated, and she crouched right there in the filthy alley-mouth among the fish-guts and cabbage leaves and took the boy's hands in hers and answered him — answered him in kind, long sentences, her voice shaking — and Zephyrine stood frozen with the apples and understood that whatever game he'd imagined, this part was real. Whatever she wanted, she wanted *him*, this child, this voice, this language, the way a drowning woman wants air.

The flood slowed. Wren's answers shortened. And then he did the thing that made Zephyrine's chest ache in a way he refused to examine: he pulled his hands free, stepped back, and came to stand pressed against Zephyrine's side, facing the woman, silent again. Chosen. He had chosen.

The woman rose. She looked at Zephyrine over the boy's head, and her face rebuilt itself piece by piece, the mask settling back into place, though the cracks still showed through like kiln-lines in glaze.

"Well," she said. "He trusts you. That's rarer than you know."

"Who are you?" Zephyrine asked.

"Sable," she said. "Sable Vane." A beat. "That's the name I wear here. The one I was given on the other side doesn't translate."

"And the other side is—"

"Don't." Her voice sharpened, then softened itself with visible effort. "Don't make me say it in the open street. Please. I've waited twelve years to say it to anyone, and I'd rather not do it standing in fish guts." She nodded down the alley. "There's a tea-house two streets over. The Gilded Perch. Back room, the owner owes me. Ten minutes of your time, keeper, and then you never have to see me again."

The word hit him like a slap he should have seen coming. *Keeper.* He kept his face still — twelve years of practice — but inside, everything lurched.

"I'm a charm-seller," he said. "Harmless trinkets. You've mistaken me."

"Have I." She wound the green ribbon around her fingers again, unwound it. "Then you won't mind if I say this plainly, and you can go on being mistaken. There are others coming. Not like me. I ask. They take. They've been chipping at something under this city for months, and when it breaks, it breaks for everyone, and the boy—" her voice caught, actually caught, and she mastered it — "the boy will be standing closest to the break. So. Tea. Ten minutes. Or don't, and spend the rest of your life wondering if ten minutes would have saved him."

She turned and walked away down the alley, unhurried, and the rain-washed light took her, and Zephyrine stood with his apples and his silence and the boy's hand finding his again, gripping hard.

* * *

The Gilded Perch was the kind of place that survived by being beneath notice: low ceiling, greasy beams, tea stewed to the color of varnish. The owner — a vast, placid man named Brannoc who owed Sable Vane, according to her, "more than money" — showed them to the back room without meeting anyone's eyes, which told Zephyrine the debt was an interesting one.

The room held a table, two benches, a candle stub, and a window the size of a hymnal, fogged with grime. Sable sat with her back to the wall, facing the door, and Zephyrine noted it and sat opposite anyway, with Wren beside him and the boy's apple — the surviving one — quartered and shared between them because Wren refused to eat unless Zephyrine ate too, another small unbearable thing he was accumulating daily.

"Talk," Zephyrine said.

Sable turned her teacup in a slow circle. Up close, in the bad light, he could see the toll the twelve years had taken: fine lines around her eyes like cracks in old porcelain, a tremor in her left hand she kept hiding by keeping both hands busy.

"My name isn't Sable Vane," she said. "On the far side of the Thorn Gate, I was called Sel. Third daughter of the Vane line, keepers of the western ford. I was twenty-two when I came through, on a trading errand — before your covenant, before the sealing. There was traffic then. Small traffic. Honest traffic, mostly." Her mouth twisted. "I was meant to be back before the moon turned. Then five people I'd never met decided to seal the only door home, and I've been on this side ever since. Twelve years. Do you know what it's like to be homesick for a sky?"

Zephyrine said nothing. His tea sat untouched.

"There's a moon there," she went on, quieter. "You'd know that, if you're what I think you are. Ours counts differently. Thirteen turns to its year, and each turn it waxes and wanes out of order, like it can't make up its mind. When I was a girl we sang to it. *Thirteen sisters, none alike, hold the door through day and night.*" She laughed, short and bitter. "Children's nonsense. Except it wasn't, was it? Because your covenant runs on that count. Thirteen turns. And the seal your family of heroes slapped across the gate — it's failing on that same count. Thirteen turns from the night it was made, the covenant comes due. Renews or dies." She looked up. "You knew that. I can see you knew that."

Zephyrine's hands were very still on the table. "Go on."

"The exiles — there are forty-one of us left on this side, scattered from here to the coast — we've spent twelve years trying to get home. Petitions, pilgrimages, digging. One man spent six years tunneling under the harbor and found nothing, because the gate isn't *under* anything, it's *elsewhere*, and you know that too." She leaned forward. "But lately the seal's been thinning. We can feel it. Like hearing music through a wall. And some of us decided we were done waiting for it to fail politely. There's a group — call them the Unbound — who've been working at the stone directly. Months. Every mark they cut weakens it further, and every weakening bleeds magic *out* of the far realm like blood from a wound, and they don't care, because they figure a dying realm's better spent coming home than bleeding dry behind a locked door."

"And the boy?" Zephyrine's voice came out lower than he intended. "Where does he fit in your arithmetic?"

Sable was quiet a moment. She looked at Wren — really looked, the way you look at something you're afraid to hope for — and when she spoke, her voice had lost its polish entirely.

"He's a bridge-child. Born of both realms — you must have worked that out, or guessed. There haven't been many. Ever. A bridge-child can stand in the gate's threshold and not burn. Can carry the covenant's weight without dissolving. The old texts say the first anchor was a bridge-child, before anchors were volunteers." Her jaw tightened. "The Unbound found out about him. I don't know how. But they want him, Zephyrine Sandoval — I know your name, yes, I've known it since I saw your wards flinch at his voice — they want him because a bridge-child can *open* the gate from this side. Willingly or otherwise. And the ones who won't ask first are already on the road."

The candle flame leaned in a draft that hadn't been there a moment ago. Somewhere in the front room, Brannoc laughed at something, a great easy boom of sound, and the ordinariness of it made the back room feel like the bottom of a well.

"If what you say is true," Zephyrine said carefully, "then helping you means opening the gate. To a realm I sealed shut for reasons that cost me—" He stopped. Started again. "The gate is sealed because it had to be. What's beyond it nearly ended this world. I was there."

"Were you?" Something flickered across her face — not disbelief. Recognition. "Or were you there when five frightened people decided five opinions mattered more than forty-one lives? I'm not saying you're a villain, keeper. I'm saying I've read the histories on both sides of that door, and history is always written by whoever stayed." She spread her hands. "I'm asking for a door. That's all. A door, and the choice to walk through it. Is that so monstrous?"

And here was the trap of it, Zephyrine thought, staring into his untouched tea: it wasn't monstrous. That was what made it dangerous. She was reasonable. She was grieving. She was a woman who sang children's songs to a moon she'd never see again, and every word she said landed on the exact fault line he'd been standing on for twelve years — the doubt he'd buried under routine and harmless charms and the careful numbness that had kept him upright since the blood and the shouting and the body he still couldn't think about without his hands going cold.

*Maybe we were wrong.*

The thought rose in him like groundwater, and he pushed it down, and it came back, and he pushed again.

"No," he said.

Sable closed her eyes briefly. "No to tea, or no to everything?"

"No to everything. The gate stays sealed. The boy stays out of it. If the Unbound come for him, they'll find—" He almost said *me*. He heard himself almost say it, and heard, underneath, the ghost of a younger man saying the same word in a different crisis, alone, certain, and the memory arrived with its usual taste of ash. He chose the sentence differently this time. "They'll find us difficult."

Sable studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw seemed to settle something in her, because she nodded slowly, and reached into her coat, and set something on the table between them: a sliver of pale stone, no bigger than a finger joint, veined with faint silver that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. Oath-stone. Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the floor.

"A fragment of the original binding," she said. "Torn loose the night you sealed it. I've carried it twelve years like a splinter." She pushed it an inch toward him. "Keep it. Not as a gift — as a warning. When the thirteenth turn comes due, that stone will tell you things your pride won't. And when the Unbound arrive — and they will arrive, soon, within the turn of a moon — remember that I warned you. Remember that some of us knocked."

She stood, dropped coins on the table for a tea nobody drank, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. The gray window-light cut her face in half.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning around, "the boy chose you. In the alley. Out of everyone in that market, he walked to you." Her voice roughened. "Bridge-children don't choose lightly. Whatever you think you lost twelve years ago, keeper — you've got something now. Try not to lose it the same way twice."

Then she was gone, and the door swung, and the candle steadied.

* * *

They walked home the long way, doubling back twice, Zephyrine's senses stretched wire-tight. Wren kept glancing up at him — reading him, the way the boy read everything, weather and faces and the hum of hidden things — and halfway down Chandler's Lane he tugged Zephyrine's sleeve and pointed at the coat pocket where the oath-stone fragment rode against Zephyrine's ribs.

"It's nothing," Zephyrine said. "A rock."

Wren gave him a look of devastating skepticism, then reached up and touched the pocket, and through the wool Zephyrine felt it — faint as a heartbeat, faint as a pulse under snow — the fragment answering the boy's touch. Silver veins brightening. A thread of warmth.

And beneath his feet, deep under the city, under the cellar and the false floor and the twelve years of careful silence, the Thorn Gate hummed — low, patient, counting.

Zephyrine stood in the middle of Chandler's Lane with a boy and a splinter of a broken promise, and understood that the countdown he'd glimpsed in the gate's vision had already begun, and that somewhere on the roads out of the dark, people were walking toward his door who would not knock.

He squeezed Wren's shoulder once — *stay* — and hurried them both home.
