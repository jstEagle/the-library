## 7. Twelve Years Ago

The rain came sideways that night, driven off the mountains like something fleeing, and it found them all gathered beneath the Thorn Gate where no rain had ever fallen.

Zephyrine remembered that afterward — the strangeness of it. Five of them standing in a dry stone chamber while the world above drowned, and the gate before them humming like a struck bell that would not finish its note. The Thorn Gate was older than the city, older than the kingdom that hanged witches in its squares, older than any covenant sworn upon it. It was a seam in the world, tall as a cathedral arch, its stone veined with living silver that pulsed slow as sleeping breath. Through its closed center they could feel the other realm pressing: a warmth like a hand laid flat against the far side of a wall.

Five of them. They had sworn together eleven years before that, young and certain and stupid with love for one another, that they would keep the door between worlds — not lock it, not throw it wide, but *keep* it, tend it, decide its turning as one body with five hearts.

Odile stood nearest the gate, arms crossed, jaw set in the way that meant she had already decided and was waiting for the rest of the world to catch up. She was a shipwright's daughter from the salt coast, broad-handed, loud, incapable of lying even when lying would have saved them all. Beside her, Cassian turned his signet ring around and around his finger — he did that when he was afraid, though only Zephyrine had ever learned to read it. Yeva leaned against the far wall with her hood still dripping, saying nothing, watching everything, the way she always did. And Merrin —

Merrin was laughing.

That was the thing Zephyrine could not make peace with, twelve years on. In his memory of that night, Merrin laughed. Not cruelly, not madly — Merrin had the kind of laugh that made rooms warmer, that made strangers confess their troubles over shared bread. He was laughing because Sable Vane's exiles had sent up another petition through the gate's keyhole of a crack, written in the First Tongue on silk so fine it dissolved if you breathed on it wrong, and the petition began, in translation, with a joke.

*They are asking again,* Merrin said, holding the silk up to the silver light. *Politely. As always. Listen to this — 'Honored keepers, we remain your servants across the seam, though our sky continues to tear.' A joke, do you see? 'Our sky continues to tear.' They joke while their world dies.*

*It isn't dying,* Cassian said. It was not quite an argument. It was a man setting a plank across a gap and hoping it would hold.

*Their magic bleeds through the seal every hour,* Yeva said quietly, from the wall. *You've seen the readings. The gate drinks it like a wound drinks blood. Every year the far side is thinner.*

*And every year we open it wider, we risk what came through last time.* Cassian's ring stopped turning. *We risk another Hollowing. We risk the city. You all remember what came through before we sealed it tight.*

Silence, then. Because they all remembered. Even now Zephyrine could not think of the word *Hollowing* without tasting ash.

*Then we share it,* Odile said. *Controlled. A door with rules, not a wall with cracks.* She spread her hands. *The covenant was never meant to be a tomb, Zeph. Read the first oath. 'The gate shall turn as the keepers turn, together.' Together. All five voices. We were meant to govern it, and instead we've been starving it shut for a decade because we're afraid.*

*I'm not afraid,* said Cassian, who was.

*I'm afraid,* Merrin said simply, and the laugh had gone out of him, and somehow that was worse. *I'm terrified. But terror isn't a policy, Cass. We can't hold this door shut forever out of fear and call it keeping faith. The exiles have children over there. Children born under a tearing sky.*

*And we have a city full of children over here,* Cassian snapped, *who will burn if the gate fails — not maybe, not someday, but the moment the seal slips. You've felt what presses against it at night. You've heard it.*

*I've heard it,* Yeva said, and her voice was so soft that everyone stopped. *Every night. For years. Do you know what it says?*

No one answered.

*It says 'let us home,'* Yeva said. *It isn't a monster, Cassian. It's people. It has always been people. That's what makes it unbearable.*

Zephyrine had not spoken yet. He was aware of that the way a man is aware of a splinter working toward his heart. Four faces would turn to him soon — they always did eventually, though no oath had ever named him first among them, though he had never wanted it. He had built the ward-lattice that held the seal. His craft, his geometry, his sleepless months of work. If the gate was a lock, he was its locksmith, and somewhere in the marrow of him he had begun to believe that made the decision his.

*Zeph,* Odile said. *Say something.*

He looked at the gate. The silver veins pulsed. Behind the stone, patient as tide, the other realm waited.

*The vote splits,* he said at last. *It always splits. Two and two, and me in the middle.* A pause he would regret for the rest of his life. *So I'll break the tie.*

*Good,* said Odile, relieved, already reaching for the ritual chalk.

*Not tonight,* Zephyrine said. *I need to check the lattice first. The northern anchor-stone has been humming flat since midsummer. If we're going to widen the covenant, the seal has to be perfect. Give me three days.*

Merrin smiled at him, warm as bread. *Three days. Then we do it right, all together.*

*Together,* Zephyrine agreed, and lied.

* * *

He told himself, walking home through the drowned streets with his collar soaked and his heart hammering, that he hadn't decided anything yet. He told himself he was only going to look at the lattice, run the calculations, weigh the risks honestly, the way a keeper should.

But his hands already knew.

That was the horror he would carry into every year that followed: his hands had known before his mind consented. While his mouth said *three days*, his hands were already sketching the revised ward-geometry on the fogged window of the ferryman's boat, the one that didn't share the burden across five anchors but collapsed it into one. One anchor-stone instead of five. One voice holding the whole weight of the covenant, forever, so that no vote would ever be needed again. No arguments. No splits. No risk of four hearts deciding wrongly and dragging the fifth along.

His heart. His weight. His alone.

He told himself it was mercy. That was the cruelest part — how easily mercy fit itself around ambition like a glove. If he anchored the gate alone, then Odile never had to stand in front of a failing seal. Then Cassian's city stayed safe by *his* sacrifice, not by committee. Then Merrin — gentle, laughing Merrin, who joked about tearing skies — could go back to teaching charm-craft to slum children instead of standing watch over a wound between worlds.

He told himself all of it, and some of it was even true, and none of it excused what he did next, and he knew that too, even then, even as he climbed the stairs to his workshop and pulled out the blood-chalk he had sworn never to use except in concert with four other voices.

* * *

He went down at moonset, when the tide of the city's dreaming was lowest and the wards would feel least disturbance. The gate chamber received him with its familiar hush. Silver light. Dry air. The hum.

He knelt at the central anchor-point and began to cut the new geometry.

It should not have worked. That was the doctrine they had all sworn by: the covenant was a chord of five notes, and no single voice could sound it. But Zephyrine had spent eleven years learning the lattice's every thread, and he had found — in the small hours, in the shameful private hours he had never confessed — that the chord could be forced. Compressed. One will playing all five parts at once, badly, brutally, but playing them. The gate would not know the difference at first. The gate would only know that the seal had deepened, hardened, gone quiet.

The blood-chalk took his wrist-blood greedily. He drew the closing lines of the new pattern, and the silver veins in the great stone began to dim, one by one, like windows going dark in a house where everyone has died.

The gate screamed.

Not in stone-voice, not in the low bell-hum they knew. It screamed in the First Tongue, in a register that bypassed the ear entirely and seized the spine, and Zephyrine felt the lattice resist him — truly resist, for the first time, as if the covenant itself had understood what he was doing and recoiled. The compression fought him. Five voices' worth of burden pouring into one vessel. He gritted his teeth and poured his will down the lines of chalk and felt his own edges begin to fray, and he kept going, because stopping now would mean the seal half-made, worse than either alternative—

Footsteps on the stair.

*ZEPHYRINE.*

Merrin's voice, raw with a horror Zephyrine had never heard in it before. Lantern-light. Then Odile behind him, then Cassian, then Yeva, all of them awake, all of them feeling it — of course they felt it, they were keepers, the covenant lived in their bones, and their bones knew their brother was strangling it.

*What have you done.* Odile crossed the chamber like a storm making landfall. *What have you DONE—*

*It's done,* Zephyrine said, and his voice came out strange and thin. *Look at it. Look how strong the seal is. No one has to argue anymore. No one has to risk—*

*Merrin, get away from the anchor!* That was Yeva, sharp as a blade, because she had seen what Zephyrine had refused to see: that compressing five anchors into one did not erase the other four. It made them loose ends. Loose ends in a living covenant, whipping free, seeking ground—

Merrin was closest to the gate. Merrin had come straight to the gate, hands out, the way he always did, the way he greeted everything in both realms, with open hands and a warm heart, and the discharged anchor-burden — the four-fifths of a covenant with nowhere left to live — found him.

Zephyrine saw it happen and could not stop it. The silver light turned and poured. Merrin made a sound that was not a scream, that was worse than a scream, a sound like a name being unwritten, and the light took him the way the sea takes a lamp, folding him in and closing over.

*No—* Odile lunged. Yeva caught her. Cassian was on his knees. The lantern lay guttering on the stones, and nobody had dropped it, which meant somebody had, which meant time had gone strange.

When the light released, the gate was sealed — utterly, perfectly, terribly sealed, harder than it had ever been, exactly as Zephyrine had designed. The hum was gone. The warmth behind the stone was gone. The pressing presence, the voices, the *let us home* — silence.

And Merrin was gone with them. Not dead on the floor. Simply absent, the way a note is absent when the chord stops. The anchor-stone at the gate's heart glowed faintly where he had stood, and Zephyrine understood, in the last instant before he made himself stop understanding, what he was looking at.

He had not killed Merrin.

He had spent him. Poured him into the lock like solder.

* * *

Afterward there was shouting, and there was grief, and there was Odile's fist against his jaw, and there was Cassian weeping without noise in the corner, and there was Yeva looking at Zephyrine with an expression he would see behind his eyelids for twelve years — not hatred. Disgust was hotter than what she wore. What she wore was the look of a woman revising everything she had ever believed about someone, all at once, in silence.

*You swore,* Odile said. Her voice was wrecked. *Eleven years ago, on this stone, you swore. 'Never alone. Never without the five.' You looked me in the eyes and you swore it, Zeph, and I believed you, God help me, I believed you.*

*The gate is safe now,* Zephyrine said, and heard, as the words left him, how hollow they rang — how much they sounded like the lie they had been from the beginning.

Cassian lifted his head. His face was terrible. *Safe,* he repeated. *You sealed it so hard it can never be opened. You understand that. Whatever happens over there — whatever dies over there — it stays sealed until the day the covenant breaks, and when it breaks, it breaks all at once, catastrophically, because there's no give in it. There's no give anywhere. You haven't saved us, Zephyrine. You've built us a dam with no spillway.*

*The exiles,* Yeva said quietly. *Everyone on the far side. Their world is bleeding out through a locked door, and we're the ones who locked it.* She pulled up her hood. *I want no part of what's under this city anymore. I want no part of him.*

She went up the stairs. Nobody stopped her. That was the scattering, though it happened slowly enough at first that they mistook it for anger — Cassian leaving within the month for the capital, taking his signet and his silence; Odile staying one bitter winter before the coast called her north to a lighthouse and a life with no doors in it at all. Five keepers became four, then three, then one, and the one was Zephyrine, because he deserved it. Because he was the anchor now in every way but flesh. Because someone had to sit above the gate and sell harmless charms and feel the covenant humming in his teeth every hour of every day, and if anyone had earned that sentence, he had.

Before Odile left, she came to the shop one last time. She stood in the doorway with her traveling pack on and her face like flint over fire, and she said the words that would become, without her knowing it, a prophecy waiting twelve years for its hour:

*One day that seal will crack, Zephyrine. Everything you buried will come back up the stairs. And when it does — when you're drowning in what you did — remember that you did it alone. That was the crime. Not the blood. Not even Merrin. The crime was that you decided none of us were strong enough to be trusted with the choice.* Her voice broke, finally, just once. *We were your family. You treated us like a risk.*

She left. The bell over the shop door rang and settled, and Zephyrine stood among his harmless charms in the gray morning light and began the long labor of becoming numb.

Twelve years he kept that numbness like a second oath, careful and complete, sanding the magic off his charms and the feeling off his days. Twelve years, and he almost succeeded. He might have succeeded entirely, if the covenant had held.

But dams with no spillways crack. Locked wounds fester. And on a rain-lashed night in the twelfth winter, when the knock came at his shop door and a silent child stood dripping on his threshold speaking the First Tongue, Zephyrine Sandoval felt the numbness split down its whole length like river ice in spring — and beneath it, rising fast and cold and inevitable, everything he had buried.
