## 8. Finding Odile

The road north smelled of salt and rotting kelp, and Zephyrine hated every mile of it.

Three days out of Grayharbor, the cobbles gave way to packed clay, the clay to gravel, the gravel to a coastal track that wound between wind-bent pines like a scar. Wren rode on his shoulders for the first day because the boy's boots had split at the seams, and rode on his back for the second because the boy's feet had blistered anyway, and by the third morning Zephyrine carried him without being asked, the child's chin hooked over his shoulder, watching the sea appear and disappear between the trees with an expression Zephyrine was beginning to recognize as hunger — not for food, but for *more*. More world. More words. Since the night he'd said *thirteen*, Wren's common tongue had been arriving in pieces, like crates washed ashore: *light*, *cold*, *go*, *why*.

*Why* most of all.

"Why her?" Wren asked now, muffled against Zephyrine's collar.

"Because she's alive," Zephyrine said. "Because she's angry enough to still be alive."

He did not say: *because I have spent twelve years not saying her name, and the not-saying has worn a groove in me so deep that everything else rolls toward it.*

The lighthouse stood on a fist of black rock at the end of the track, white-washed, stubborn, its lamp dark against the grey morning. A cottage clung to its side like a barnacle. Nets hung from a rail, drying. A rowboat sat upturned on stones. And in the garden, hands buried to the wrist in a bed of something green and furious, a broad-shouldered woman with iron-grey hair cropped short worked with her back to them.

Zephyrine stopped at the gate. His heart performed a maneuver he could only describe as climbing.

Twelve years. The last time he'd seen Odile Ferran, there had been blood on her sleeves and Merrin's name in her mouth and her hand raised — not to strike him, though he'd deserved it, but to hold the door of the shrine shut while he did what he'd done. She had screamed at him through the stone. He had never let himself remember the exact words. He had made a discipline of it, the way he'd made a discipline of sanding the magic off every charm he sold: smooth it down, make it harmless, make it nothing.

Wren slid off his back and stood beside him, small and solemn, looking up at the tower.

"You're afraid," Wren said. Not a question. The boy had learned *afraid* two days ago and deployed it with devastating accuracy ever since.

"Yes."

"Of her?"

"Of what I have to ask her."

Odile straightened, pressed her lower back with both fists the way old soldiers and old gardeners do, and turned.

For one long moment neither of them moved. The wind came off the water and shoved between them like a third party with opinions. She looked older — of course she did; they all did — but the change that struck him wasn't age. It was that her face, which had once been all quickness, all weather, was now carved into something patient and hard, like driftwood the sea had given up trying to move.

Then she crossed the garden in six strides, hauled the gate open, and slapped him so hard his ear rang.

"*Twelve years,*" she said.

"Odile—"

She hit him again, open-palmed, less a blow than punctuation. "*Twelve years, you absolute — you couldn't write? You couldn't send a bird? You couldn't crawl here on your knees, which is what you owed me, and knock?*"

"I didn't know where you were. For a long time I didn't know if—"

"If I was dead." Her voice dropped, went flat and terrible. "You mean like Merrin. Yes. Well. I wasn't. Congratulations on your continued ignorance."

And then, before he could assemble any defense, she seized him by the collar and pulled him into a hug so fierce it cracked something loose in his chest — a hug with fury still in it, grief still in it, twelve years of both compressed into the space of three breaths. Zephyrine stood rigid as a fence post. He could not remember the last time anyone had held him. He could not make his arms work.

Over her shoulder, Wren watched with wide eyes.

When she released him, she stepped back and looked him over the way she used to look over their gear before a crossing — quick, thorough, unsentimental. "You look terrible. You look like a man who sells spoons."

"I sell charms."

"Worse." Her gaze snagged on Wren then, and changed. "Whose is this?"

"He's mine," Zephyrine said. "I mean — no one's. He came to me. In the rain, four months ago. He speaks the First Tongue, Odile. Natively. Fluently. Like it's the only language he was ever sung to sleep in."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes — a shutter half-opening onto a lit room. She crouched slowly, bringing herself level with the boy, and Zephyrine saw her hands tremble and saw her hate them for trembling.

"What's your name, little one?"

"Wren," he said. Then, carefully, in the First Tongue, in a voice like a bell rung underwater: *"The gate remembers the ones who swore."*

Odile went very still.

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below the rock, waves worried the stones with a sound like slow applause. Zephyrine watched his oldest friend's anger — the anger she had built her whole exile out of, plank by plank — meet those five soft syllables and fail to hold.

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's the oath-formula. That's the *binding verse*. Where did you learn that?"

"He didn't learn it," Zephyrine said quietly. "It learned him. Odile, the gate under my shop has been chipped at for months. Someone's been working at the seal. And when Wren touched the stone, it showed us a moon counting down. Thirteen turns. When the thirteenth turns full, the covenant fails. Everything we bound comes loose."

"The covenant failed twelve years ago," Odile said. "You made sure of that yourself."

"No. We *broke* it. Breaking isn't failing. Breaking was — it was the wound. Failing is the wound opening." He heard his own voice shake and let it. "Merrin died holding the seal together. Or — whatever happened that night, someone or something has been paying the interest ever since, and the principal's coming due. I need the keepers, Odile. All of them. Whatever's left of them. You were first on my list because you were always first at everything."

Her jaw worked. She looked at Wren again — really looked, the way you look at a thing you've prayed for and stopped expecting — and when she spoke, her voice had gone rough.

"Do you know what I did after that night? After you sealed it with our — with *blood*, without asking, without—" She cut herself off, breathed. "I walked south for a month. I got as far as the salt marshes and I stood in the water up to my waist and I tried to drown the gift out of myself. Held my own head under. Because if I couldn't feel the far side anymore, I thought, maybe I'd stop hearing Merrin."

Zephyrine's stomach dropped. "You heard her?"

"Every night for a year. Faint. Like singing through a wall." Odile laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard from her. "I told myself it was guilt. Guilt makes music too, if you feed it. But it wasn't, was it. It was her. She's *in* it. Isn't she. Whatever you did that night — she's part of the seal."

Zephyrine said nothing, because he had spent twelve years refusing to know the answer, and the refusal had calcified into a kind of knowledge all its own.

Odile read his silence fluently. She had always been able to read him; it had been the best and worst thing about them. She closed her eyes. When she opened them, the fury was still there, banked now rather than blazing, coals instead of fire — and coals, Zephyrine knew, burn longer.

"Get inside," she said. "Both of you. There's fish stew and it's terrible and you'll eat all of it. And then you're going to tell me everything, Marchetti, in order, starting with the night you decided you were the only one of us who got to be brave."

* * *

The cottage interior was exactly Odile: spare, scrubbed, fiercely ordered. Dried herbs in labeled bundles. A whetstone worn to a smile. On the mantel, arranged with a care that contradicted the room's plainness, a row of small objects Zephyrine recognized with a lurch — river-glass from the crossing places, a brass compass with a cracked face, a wooden bird Merrin had carved. Relics. A shrine to a family that no longer officially existed.

They ate the terrible stew. Wren ate three bowls and fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew almost, his head tipping against Odile's arm. She froze, then very carefully did not move, and Zephyrine watched her decide to let it stay.

"So," she said, low. "Tell me about the chipping."

"Clean cuts. Tool-work, not erosion. Months of it, patient, from the near side. Which means someone over here wants it open."

"Hale's people?"

"Witch-finders don't want doors. They want nails." He turned his cup. "More likely exiles. Sable Vane found us at the market. Crossed over before the sealing. She wants the gate thrown wide — says the far realm is dying, bleeding out through the damage, and reopening it is survival. For them."

"And for us?"

"She didn't say. That's what frightens me. She wasn't lying, Odile. That's what frightens me more."

Odile was quiet a moment. "There were others like her, before. Families that straddled the border. Traders, singers, the marsh-clans. When we sealed it, we didn't just close a door on monsters. We closed it on people mid-step." Her voice hardened. "We told ourselves it was clean. Nothing is clean. You least of all should believe that."

"I don't."

"No," she said. "You believe you're filthy. That's different, and it's nearly as useless." She shifted Wren gently onto a bench, draped a blanket over him, and stood. "Show me your hands."

"What?"

"Your hands, Zeph."

Confused, he held them out. She took them, turned them palm-up, and ran her thumb across the calluses — charm-maker's calluses, sander's calluses, the hands of a man who had spent twelve years filing magic down to harmlessness. Something in her expression cracked further.

"You stopped," she said. "You stopped practicing. Entirely."

"A man was hanged in Copperlane for a cantrip. I adapted."

"You *buried* yourself. We swore oaths on standing stones, you and I, and you buried yourself in a shop selling luck-knots to people who'll never know what you used to be." Her grip tightened. "Do you know what I thought, all these years? I thought you were building something. Gathering strength. Waiting. Because Zephyrine Sandoval doesn't retreat, he repositions — that's what Merrin always said. And instead you were just... enduring. Enduring isn't keeping an oath. It's just a slower way of breaking it."

"I kept everyone *alive*," he said, and heard how small it sounded.

"Did you?" She released his hands. "Keep them alive, or keep them away?"

Before he could answer — before he even knew whether he had an answer — she crossed to the mantel, took down the wooden bird, and pressed its belly. A seam opened along the grain with a click that Zephyrine felt in his teeth, and inside, nested in oilcloth, lay a shard of grey stone the size of a knife-blade, veined with faint gold light that pulsed, slow as a sleeping heartbeat.

An oath-stone fragment. One of five, once, each keeper carrying a piece of the binding. He hadn't seen one since the night the world broke.

"You kept yours," he whispered.

"I kept *mine*," she said. "Tam threw his in the harbor — or lost it, depending which story you believe. Sera's went with her when she went west, if she's even living. And Merrin's..." She looked at the fragment, and the gold light seemed to lean toward her hand like a plant toward sun. "Merrin's was on her body. Whatever was left of it. At the shrine."

"We have to go back there. To the shrine. If the seal's anchored anywhere, it's anchored there, and if it's failing—"

"I know where the shrine is, Zeph. I'm the one who showed *you* the mountain path, if you recall." She wrapped the fragment back in its oilcloth with brisk, practiced motions, but her hands slowed at the last fold. "I spent ten years telling myself I'd never go back. Then two years telling myself I'd go alone. Then I stopped telling myself anything, which is its own kind of death." She tucked the bundle into her coat. "So. Terms."

"Terms."

"You get my help, my stone, and my boat — there's a sloop at the winter anchorage, she's ugly and she floats. In exchange: you stop apologizing. Not because you're forgiven. You are nowhere near forgiven, and you may never be, and I need you to understand that clearly before you start feeling better." Her eyes were bright and wet and absolutely steady. "Forgiveness isn't a door I open for you. It's a road we build, both of us, and it's going to be slow, and there will be tolls. What you did — acting alone, spending Merrin without asking — that wasn't courage, it was arrogance wearing courage's coat. And the only penance that means anything is the opposite. Never alone again. Whatever comes. Swear it."

The words landed in the groove twelve years deep, and fit it exactly.

"I swear," Zephyrine said. "Not alone. Never again alone."

"Not good enough. Say it like an oath. You remember how."

He did remember. He closed his eyes, and the old formula rose in him unbidden, in the First Tongue, in the cadence they had all spoken together on a night when they were five and whole: *the gate remembers the ones who swore.*

"I swear it," he said in the First Tongue. "By stone and season. By the ones who paid. By the ones who remain."

Behind them, from the bench, a small sleepy voice finished the verse.

*"By the key that opens, by the lock that keeps."*

They both turned. Wren sat up in his blankets, blinking, not fully awake, reciting words he could not possibly have been taught — the closing lines of the binding verse, the lines that had never been written down, that existed only in the memories of five people, four of whom had scattered and one of whom was dead.

Or not dead. Zephyrine's skin went cold. *Like singing through a wall.*

Odile sank slowly onto the bench beside the boy. Her hand hovered over his hair without touching, the way you hover over something hot, or holy.

"Zeph," she said, and her voice had gone strange and soft. "Where exactly did you say this child came from?"

"The door of my shop. In the rain. With no name and no voice."

"And the gate under your shop — the seal — when he arrived. Did anything change? Anything at all?"

He thought of the wards humming. Of the crack in his numbness. Of thirteen turns counting down beneath his floorboards, and a boy who might be a message, or a weapon, or a wound given legs.

"It started humming," he said. "Like something answering."

Odile looked at the sleeping-awake child, then at the mantel of relics, then out the window toward the south, toward Grayharbor, toward the mountain beyond it where a shrine held whatever remained of Merrin Ferran.

"Then we'd better sail with the evening tide," she said. "Because I think your boy isn't knocking on the gate, Zeph. I think the gate sent him out — like a hand reaching through a burning house. And I think whatever it's reaching for..." She picked up the oath-stone fragment, and its gold pulse quickened, urgent now, like a heartbeat before running. "...it's reaching for *us*."
