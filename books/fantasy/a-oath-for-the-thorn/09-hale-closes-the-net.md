## 9. Hale Closes the Net

The cuffs were cold before dawn.

Corvane Hale sat in his office above the Hall of Inquiry with his sleeves rolled back, both iron bands laid out on the blotter like surgical instruments. They were beautiful things, if you knew how to look. Most people saw a witch-finder's badge and thought of shackles — punishment, restraint, the law's heavy hand. Hale saw them for what they truly were: ladles. Scoops. The fine filigree along the inner rim wasn't decoration; it was a lattice of capture-runes, each one tuned to drink deep when pressed against living magic. A cuff on a witch's wrist didn't silence them. It *harvested* them, slow and steady, drawing the power up through the iron and into the reservoir at the Hall's foundations.

He turned one cuff over. In its polished curve he caught his own reflection, warped slightly, as though the metal remembered what it had swallowed.

Twelve years ago, when he was a junior inspector with ink-stained cuffs and nothing to harvest but the dregs of street hedge-witches, an old finder named Aldous Vetch had shown him the truth over cheap brandy. *We don't fear it, boy,* Vetch had said, turning his own cuff so the lamplight ran along the runes. *Fear is for priests and grandmothers. We are not exterminators. We are collectors.* He'd tapped the iron. *Every hanging is a waste. Every pyre burns fuel we should be bottling. The cuffs were the smartest thing this city ever built, and the fools who wear them think they're weapons.*

Hale had understood then that he would run the Hall one day. Not because he hated magic. Because he loved it — with the clean, patient love of a man who hoards gold he never intends to spend. Or rather, intends to spend all at once, on a single purchase no one else can afford.

He set the cuff down and pulled the morning's reports toward him.

* * *

Three items on the docket, and two of them bored him.

A fire-singer in the Tannery Quarter, drunk, had lit her own eyebrows at a wedding. Harvest value: negligible. A tinker in Lowbridge with a charm against rust that actually worked — worth a visit, perhaps, next week. And third:

*A complaint of unlicensed charms, premises of one Z. Sandoval, Thornmarket Row.*

Hale read the name twice. Sandoval. The charm-shop keeper. Twelve years licensed, twelve years inspected, twelve years of inventory ledgers so immaculate they were almost an insult. Every charm registered as inert. Every component logged. Every inspection passed with the bland perfection of a man who had learned exactly how much imperfection made a ledger believable.

For eleven of those years, Hale had believed him. That was the honest accounting, and Hale prided himself on honest accounting even when it convicted himself. For eleven years he had filed Sandoval under *harmless*, alongside the fortune-tellers and the bone-readers and the old women who sold knotted thread against nightmares. The city needed its small superstitions. Let them have their sanded-down trinkets; the real work lay elsewhere.

Then, eight months ago, the resonance maps had changed.

The Hall kept instruments most finders never saw — brass orreries and mercury spirals in a cellar beneath the Hall, tracing the faint tides of power that moved under the city like blood under skin. And beneath Thornmarket Row, the tide had begun to move differently. Not rising. Not falling. *Pulling.* As though something down there had begun to breathe.

Hale had walked Thornmarket Row four times since, in different coats, at different hours. He had bought a luck-knot from Sandoval's shop — five coppers, charmingly crooked, registered as decorative twine. He had held it in his palm and felt nothing, which was itself interesting, because the knot was too well-made to be nothing. A fraud sells you garbage. A craftsman selling nothing has hidden the something.

And there was the matter of the child.

* * *

"Unregistered minor, age approximately nine or ten, observed entering the Sandoval premises on the evening of the ninth," said Inspector Dree, standing at parade rest before Hale's desk. She was his best hound: patient, humorless, gifted at becoming furniture. "No record of the child exists. No birth registration, no foundling-house intake, no work permit. He appeared roughly two months ago and does not appear to leave."

"Neighbors?"

"The widow at the corner says he's Sandoval's nephew. The cooper across the row says the same. But the widow told the cooper the boy came from 'down south,' and the cooper told the widow he came from 'his sister,' and neither story matches anything about Sandoval, who has no sister and no family on any record going back three generations." Dree's mouth twitched, which for her was a laugh. "They're lying for him. Badly. Affectionately."

Affectionately. Hale turned the word over. It was the tell that mattered more than any ledger. A man with something to hide buys silence. A man with something to protect earns loyalty. Sandoval had earned it — years of small kindnesses, of charms sold at cost to those who couldn't pay, of remembering names and children's birthdays and which door hinge squeaked. Hale had read the neighborhood interviews. Half of Thornmarket Row would lie under oath for the charm-seller, and the other half would lie badly enough to be useful.

"And the child himself?"

"Silent. Doesn't speak. Watches everything." Dree hesitated, and Hale watched her decide to give him the thing she'd saved for last. "One detail. On the night of the ninth, during the patrol pass, the boy was visible through the shop window for perhaps four seconds. Officer Brandt — who wears a cuff, standard issue — reported afterward that his cuff went cold. Not warm. Cold. Like iron drinking from the wrong direction."

Hale sat very still.

Iron cuffs drank magic. That was their whole doctrine. Pressed against a source, they drew; held near a void, they chilled. A child whose mere proximity reversed the flow —

"Brandt reported this?"

"He did. I've sealed his report."

"Good." Hale rose and took his cuffs from the desk. "Seal it and forget it. If Brandt talks, he'll talk himself into a reassignment to the northern bogs."

Dree's eyes flicked up. "You believe the child is the source."

"I believe," Hale said, sliding the first cuff onto his wrist and feeling it wake against his pulse, "that the child is either the source or the door to it, and that after twelve years of nothing, something under Thornmarket Row has started breathing. Coincidence is a word for people who haven't yet finished counting." He shrugged into his grey coat. "We're going to close the net. Quietly. No raid, no arrest, nothing that spooks the fish."

"Then what?"

"A neighbor," Hale said.

* * *

Tam Wicker's name surfaced the way useful names always did: through debt.

The cooper across the row owed money to three creditors and the city tax office, and his workshop's fire-license had lapsed twice. More usefully, Tam Wicker was the loudest of Sandoval's defenders — the man who'd told the widow the boy was family, who'd been seen carrying a crate of Sandoval's stock during the spring floods, who'd once, memorably, told a passing patrolman that the charm-seller was "the most honest man on this street, which isn't saying much, but still."

Hale studied the file and felt the particular satisfaction of a lock yielding. You didn't need to break a community to take it apart. You needed only to find the load-bearing member and apply pressure.

"We take Wicker tomorrow," he told Dree. "Publicly. Loudly. Charge: harboring an unregistered person suspected of arcane ability. It carries the rope, so make sure the street sees the irons go on."

"And Sandoval?"

"Sandoval will do what loyal men do. He'll come forward. He'll confess, or bargain, or testify — some version of *it was me, not him*." Hale smiled without warmth. "Men who've spent twelve years hiding will spend their final coin to save a friend. And the moment he opens his mouth in my hall and claims connection to that child, I'll have him on the register, the cuffs on his wrists, and the truth draining out of him drop by drop."

Dree lingered. "And if he doesn't come forward? If he runs?"

"Then I burn the neighborhood's goodwill out of him one arrest at a time until he surfaces to stop me." Hale closed the file. "Either way, the net closes. The only variable is how much of the street comes apart in the tightening."

He almost added *and that's acceptable*. He didn't, because Dree already knew. They all knew. The law was not a shield in this city; it was a net, and nets were not cruel or kind. They simply closed.

* * *

That evening Hale walked Thornmarket Row himself, one last time, unaccompanied, in a dockworker's coat.

Rain had come again, the thin persistent kind that made the cobbles shine like scales. Lamps bloomed in windows. From the cooperage came the knock of mallets, steady as a heartbeat. And from the charm-shop, light — warm, gold, ordinary — and through the fogged glass the silhouette of a tall man moving behind his counter, and a smaller silhouette beside him, still as a painted figure, watching the street.

Watching *him*.

Hale felt the child's attention land on him through the glass and rain like a physical weight, and the cuff on his wrist — dormant, passive, merely worn — shivered. Not cold this time. Something stranger. A sensation like the iron being *considered*. Weighed. Found, perhaps, wanting.

He stopped in the middle of the street, rain running off his jaw, and let the moment stretch. Let the child look. There was no harm in it now; the trap was set, the warrant drafted, the timing fixed. Tomorrow the cooper would be taken in front of God and the whole gawking street, and the day after, or the day after that, the charm-seller would crack, because loyal men always cracked, because loyalty was just guilt with better posture.

*You're breathing down there,* Hale thought, toward the shop, toward whatever slept beneath it. *Twelve years quiet, and now breath. Something changed. Someone came. And everything that comes through a door can be collected.*

The small silhouette did not move. But Hale had the distinct, unreasonable impression that it heard him — that behind those dark eyes, in whatever tongue the child kept silent, something answered. Not in words. In temperature. The rain between them seemed to fall a half-degree warmer, as though the air itself had taken a side.

Hale smiled into the dark and touched the cuff at his wrist, once, the way a gambler touches a loaded die.

Tomorrow, the cooper.

Tonight, he allowed himself the luxury of certainty. In his experience, certainty was the most valuable substance in the world — rarer than magic, harder to harvest, and always, always found in men who were about to be wrong.
