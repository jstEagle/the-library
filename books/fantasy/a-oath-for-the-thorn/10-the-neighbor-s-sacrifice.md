## 10. The Neighbor's Sacrifice

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Zephyrine knew every honest knock in the lane — Tam's double rap with the flat of his palm, the widow Anselm's timid tap-tap-tap, the baker's boy kicking the doorframe because his hands were full of bread. This was none of them. This was three measured blows, evenly spaced, delivered by someone who had practiced the sound of authority until it came naturally.

He was already dressed. He had not slept. Wren was awake too — Zephyrine could feel him through the floorboards, a small held breath in the loft above the shop, the way you can feel a candle burning in an unlit room.

"Stay there," Zephyrine whispered to the ceiling, though he knew the boy couldn't hear him. "Whatever happens. Stay."

He opened the door on the grey light and found the lane full of watchmen.

Six of them, in oilcloth cloaks beaded with rain, and behind them — inevitable as weather — Inspector Corvane Hale, dry under a black umbrella he hadn't needed yet but carried anyway, the way other men carried pistols. His badge caught the early light. So did the cuff beneath it, that sliver of iron at his wrist that Zephyrine had spent two nights telling himself he'd imagined.

"Master Sandoval." Hale's voice was pleasant. That was the worst of it; the man never raised his voice, never sharpened it. He spoke the way a surgeon speaks over an open body. "Forgive the hour. We're executing a warrant."

"A warrant." Zephyrine kept his hands loose at his sides and his face bored. Twelve years of practice. "At this house?"

"Oh, no." Hale smiled and stepped aside, gesturing down the lane with the umbrella like a man presenting a gift. "Not this house."

They were dragging Tam Voss out of the cooperage across the way.

Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. Tam — barrel-chested, gray-bearded Tam, who bought charms for his wife's rheumatism and never asked what was in them, who had once stood between Zephyrine and a drunk watchman with nothing but his bulk and his stubbornness, who had taken Wren in for two days last month when the boy had a fever and Zephyrine was laid low with a chill of his own. Tam, who was now face-down in the mud of his own doorstep with a knee between his shoulder blades, shouting — not pleading, *shouting* — "I've done nothing! I've done *nothing*, you bastards, ask anyone—"

"We have," Hale said mildly, to no one, to everyone. "We've asked everyone."

*Everyone.* The word went through Zephyrine like a splinter seeking bone. The lane was filling with neighbors drawn by the noise, and every one of them wore the same expression: horror braided with relief, the terrible arithmetic of a crowd watching someone else be chosen. Old Marta clutched her shawl. The baker's boy stood white-faced with a basket of rolls going cold in his arms. Nobody spoke. Nobody would meet anybody else's eyes. This was how it worked, Zephyrine thought. Not with fire and brimstone. With arithmetic. With the quiet transaction by which a whole street agreed, one heartbeat at a time, that it was better them than us.

And then Tam lifted his head out of the mud, saw Zephyrine standing in his doorway, and did the thing that broke Zephyrine's careful numbness open like a struck bell.

He winked.

Bloody, muddy, sixty years old, pinned under a watchman's boot — Tam Voss looked at him and winked, quick and sly, the same look he gave when slipping an extra honey-cake into Wren's pocket. As if to say: *I know why they're here. I know whose door they should be knocking on. And I'd rather it were mine.*

"No," Zephyrine said. It came out before he could stop it, barely a breath.

Hale heard it anyway. Of course he did. The inspector turned, and his eyes — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who harvested things — settled on Zephyrine with the weight of a hand on a scale.

"I'm sorry," Hale said courteously. "Did you say something, Master Sandoval?"

*Say nothing. Say nothing. You have a boy upstairs who cannot run fast enough, a gate below your feet that must not be found, twelve years of survival balanced on the point of your silence.* Every instinct Zephyrine owned screamed the same lesson, the one written in Merrin's blood and Odile's exile and his own long gray solitude: *survive, survive, survive.*

Tam was hauled upright. The cooper's wrists were bound before him, and he swayed, and steadied himself, and looked at Zephyrine again — not winking now, just looking, with the calm of a man doing sums he'd already finished.

"The notice said unregistered children," Tam said loudly, to the street, to the watchmen, to anyone. "Harboring. Well, I harbor plenty. Half the cats in Copperlane sleep in my shavings. If a child ate at my table now and then, is that a crime? Then arrest the whole street, because you all fed him soup when his keeper here was sick—" He stopped. Zephyrine watched him hear his own words, watched the old man realize he'd just tied their names together in front of six witnesses and a witch-finder, and watched him — deliberately, gloriously — keep talking anyway. "Aye, I said it. The charm-seller's boy. What of it? He's a mute child who likes honey-cakes, not a demon. Unless the law's changed while I wasn't looking."

"It hasn't changed," Hale said. "It's merely being enforced." He drew a folded paper from his coat. "Warrant of seizure, Tam Voss, cooper, for harboring an unregistered minor suspected of foreign origin. He'll be questioned at the Bastion. Standard procedure."

*Standard.* The word was a lie so smooth it might have been polished. Zephyrine had seen what came back from the Bastion's standard procedures. Sometimes it was people. Rarely it was all of them.

The watchmen marched Tam down the lane. The crowd parted and closed behind them like water, and the lane was ordinary again, except for the shape of a man missing from it.

Zephyrine stood in his doorway until the cold made him shake.

* * *

Upstairs, Wren was pressed against the window, both palms flat on the glass, watching the empty lane where Tam had been.

"He took it," Zephyrine said. His voice sounded strange to him, scraped hollow. "He took the fall. For you. For me. He knew — gods know how he knew, but he knew, and he chose—"

Wren turned. His face was the face Zephyrine had learned to read these past weeks: not blank, never blank, just *elsewhere*, tuned to a frequency common speech couldn't reach. But today something human and simple moved across it, something any mother in any city would have known at a glance.

Guilt. A child's guilt, enormous and unmanageable, the kind that has no language yet and therefore no bottom.

"Don't," Zephyrine said, crossing the room. "Don't you dare carry this. This isn't yours. This is mine — do you understand? Every kindness anyone has paid us, I earned it or I borrowed it, and either way the debt lands on me. Tam fed you soup because I asked him to. Whatever happens to him, I put him in its path."

Wren shook his head slowly. Then he reached up and touched Zephyrine's chest, just over the heart, and said — in the First Tongue, soft as falling ash — a word Zephyrine didn't know but felt anyway, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm.

Then he pointed at himself, and at the window, and made a gesture Zephyrine understood across every border of language: *run.*

"Yes," Zephyrine admitted. "That's the sensible thing. Pack the boy out the back, down through the cellar, past the gate without touching it, north to Odile, and let this city keep its rot. By nightfall we're smoke. By week's end we're a lighthouse and a story."

He said it all in one breath, the plan he'd already built in the dark hours, the plan that was clean and correct and survivable.

"And Tam rots in the Bastion," he finished. "And when they're done questioning him — and they will question him, Wren, they'll take him apart joint by joint looking for the child he swears he only fed — whatever's left of him gets to die knowing he saved us. Knowing we ran. Knowing his sacrifice bought exactly what it cost."

Wren's jaw set. It was uncanny, sometimes, how much stubbornness could live in such a small face.

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I know. Stay silent, stay hidden, stay safe — that's been the whole catechism for twelve years, and it worked, it *worked*, right up until it started costing other people their lives instead of costing me mine." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Do you know what I told myself, all these years? That my silence was penance. That hiding was the price I paid for what I did at the gate. But it wasn't penance. Penance costs. Hiding is just — rent. I've been paying rent on my guilt, Wren, and letting others make the payments."

The boy studied him for a long moment. Then, deliberately, he sat down on the edge of the cot, folded his hands in his lap, and looked at Zephyrine with the patience of stone.

Waiting. Refusing to leave. Making his small body into an argument.

"You're a menace," Zephyrine told him. "You know that? A mute, foreign, illegally existing menace, and I ought to have drowned you in the rain gutter the night you showed up speaking the end of the world on my doorstep."

Wren almost smiled.

Zephyrine closed his eyes. Behind them: Tam's wink. Tam's voice, carrying down the lane, tying their names together on purpose. *Ask anyone. Ask anyone.* Twelve years ago he had acted alone to save the world and broken everything worth saving. He had sworn — silently, uselessly, to no one — never again.

"Well," he said. "Damn it all."

* * *

The Bastion's hearing hall had been a granary once, in some more generous century. Now the great timber roof held up nothing but judgment, and the grain-smell had long since been scrubbed out and replaced with ink, iron, and fear. Benches for spectators lined the walls. At the front, behind a table of black oak, sat three examiners in gray, and beside them, taking notes in a beautiful hand, Inspector Corvane Hale.

Zephyrine walked in through the public door at the appointed hour, in his good coat, with his license papers in his breast pocket, and felt every eye in the room slide toward him and away, the way eyes do around a lit fuse.

He had spent the afternoon writing and destroying letters. To Odile — burned, too dangerous, too revealing. To the neighbors — pointless, cowardly. In the end he had written nothing at all, and had simply kissed the top of Wren's head in the dark of the cellar, told him *stay, wait, trust me,* and left the boy guarding a gate with instructions and a knife he hoped to heaven the child would never need.

Now he stood in the queue of petitioners and waited his turn, heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't felt since the night the covenant broke. Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, terrifyingly, something else. Something almost like relief. As if the mask he'd worn for twelve years had grown so heavy that the prospect of finally setting it down outweighed the axe waiting behind it.

"Name and business," said the examiner on the left, when his turn came.

"Zephyrine Sandoval. Charm-seller, licensed, Copperlane ward." He laid his papers on the black oak. "I'm here about the prisoner Voss. I'm here to testify."

The examiner's quill stopped. Somewhere to the side, unhurried, Hale turned a page.

"Testify to what, Master Sandoval?"

"To the fact that Tam Voss is innocent of the charge against him." Zephyrine's voice held. He was almost proud of it. "The child you're hunting was never harbored by Tam Voss. He was harbored by me. From the first night. He slept under my roof, ate at my table, and if there's a warrant for unregistered children in this city, then it ends at my door — which is where I'm standing."

The hall went very quiet. The particular quiet of a room recalculating.

"That is a grave confession," the examiner said slowly.

"It's a true one."

"Master Sandoval." Hale rose from his chair, folding his notes with a soft snap, and approached the table smiling his surgeon's smile. Up close, Zephyrine could see the iron cuff clearly now — no disguise, no pretense, worn openly beneath the watchman's badge like a second, truer rank. "You understand what you're claiming. Unregistered minors of foreign origin are presumed vessels. Presumed vessels are remanded to the Bastion for examination. Examination is... thorough." He tilted his head. "You'd be surrendering yourself to protect a neighbor. Most men find the arithmetic runs the other way."

"Most men haven't owed as long as I have."

Something flickered behind Hale's pale eyes — appetite, Zephyrine realized. Pure, patient appetite. The inspector had come fishing for a charm-seller and hooked, apparently, something far larger, and he was reeling with exquisite care.

"Then let's not waste the court's time," Hale said pleasantly. "Examiners — the witness confesses to harboring. Take him into custody. And send riders to Copperlane." His smile widened by a degree, a man savoring a vintage. "There's a shop on the lane with a cellar I've been meaning to inspect."

*He knows.* The realization landed cold and complete. He'd known about the cellar. Perhaps about the gate. Perhaps all along — perhaps Tam's arrest had never been about Tam at all, but about watching which doors opened in the night, which faces broke, which confession crawled out of the woodwork. Zephyrine had walked into the trap with his eyes open, and the trap had a second jaw he hadn't seen.

Two watchmen took his arms. Their grip was professional, impersonal, and final.

And that was when the lights went out.

Not all of them. Just the great cressets along the eastern wall — six flames snuffed in a single breath, as if pinched by one enormous invisible hand. In the sudden half-dark, the crowd surged and cried out, and somewhere a woman began to scream that it was witchcraft, which was, Zephyrine reflected even as the watchman's grip tightened, technically accurate.

Then the western wall exploded inward.

Not with fire. With *light* — a lance of pure white radiance that punched through the timber like a spear through wet paper, and through the smoking hole climbed a figure Zephyrine would have known anywhere in the world, in any century, in death itself: broad-shouldered, silver-streaked, wrapped in a traveling cloak still crusted with three days of northern road-dust, and holding in one outstretched hand a fragment of gray stone that blazed like a captive star.

Odile Ferran surveyed the screaming, stampeding, thoroughly disrupted hearing hall of the Bastion with the expression of a woman deeply unimpressed.

"Twelve years," she announced, to the hall, to the heavens, to Zephyrine specifically, "twelve *years* I waited for you to write to me, you absolute *idiot*, and instead I get a fishmonger's letter saying you got yourself arrested. So I came myself." She leveled the blazing oath-stone at the remaining examiners, who were wisely deciding to be elsewhere. "Nobody move. Especially not you, Zeph. We're going to have a conversation, you and I, and it's going to be long, and you are going to hate every word of it."

"Odile," Zephyrine managed, "the back wall—"

"I see the back wall."

"There are guards outside the back wall."

"Not anymore," said a small voice from the shattered doorway — and there was Wren, breathless, mud to his knees, having disobeyed every instruction Zephyrine had ever given him, holding a dead man's dropped keys in one fist and wearing the ferocious triumph of a general who has personally won the war.

For one absurd heartbeat, in the middle of the collapsing hearing hall of the Bastion, with witch-finders regrouping and Hale's calm voice cutting through the chaos — *"Seal the exits! The child! TAKE THE CHILD!"* — Zephyrine Sandoval stood between the woman he'd betrayed and the boy he'd failed to protect, and laughed.

It was a terrible time to laugh. He did it anyway. Because for the first time in twelve years, he was not alone in a burning building of his own making.

"Run," Odile said, grabbing his collar. "Muse later. *Run.*"

They ran — out through the hole in the wall, into the rain, into the labyrinth of the lower city where Odile had already, with characteristic thoroughness, arranged horses, bribes, and one extremely offended smuggler. Behind them the Bastion's bells began to swing, and above the rooftops, briefly visible through tearing cloud, the moon hung thin and hungry, counting down.

Nine turns left. And now the whole machinery of the witch-finders knew exactly where to hunt.
