## 11. Road of Small Mercies

The road north out of the city was a scar of mud and cart-ruts, and for the first day they walked it like criminals, because that was what they were now — or near enough that the distinction had stopped mattering to Zephyrine's heart, which kept up its frantic drumming long after the walls of Corvane's jurisdiction had sunk behind the hills.

He had expected pursuit. He had not expected kindness.

It came at dusk on the second day, in a village called Lowmarsh where the houses leaned together like conspirators and the ditches smelled of peat and woodsmoke. Zephyrine had planned to pass through without stopping — hood up, eyes down, Wren's hand in his, Odile ranging ahead like a gull scenting weather. But Wren stopped dead in the middle of the lane, tugging his sleeve, staring at a doorway.

A woman stood there with a child on her hip, and above her door hung a charm Zephyrine knew intimately, because he had made it: a twist of river-reed and blue thread, the magic sanded off until only the shape of a blessing remained. A charm against bad dreams. He'd sold it to this woman — no. To her mother, four years ago, when the child had been an infant screaming through every night, and the mother had been so tired she'd wept into her coins.

"You're the charm-man," the woman said. Not a question. Her eyes went from his face to Wren's to Odile's, and something moved behind them — calculation, yes, but also something warmer underneath, banked like a coal. "Mara said you came through Millbrook last year and fixed her well-rope for nothing."

"I don't recall," Zephyrine said carefully.

"Course you don't." She almost smiled. "That's how you do it." She stepped back and held her door wide. "There's stew on. And if anyone asks, I've seen no one but my own shadow all week."

Odile looked at Zephyrine with one eyebrow raised, the look that said, *this is your doing, whatever you want to call it.* Zephyrine wanted to say it wasn't, that he had only been surviving, that every charm sold had been a transaction, coin for goods, nothing owed in either direction. But the woman was already ladling stew, and Wren was already inside, drawn across the threshold as if by a thread, and there was no honest way to call any of it nothing.

They ate barley stew with leeks and a heel of hard cheese, and the woman — Bess, her name was — talked about her neighbors the way people talk about weather, constant and unremarkable, and twice she mentioned charms she owned, and each time Zephyrine recognized his own work hanging in some corner of her house. The reed-and-thread against dreams. A knotted cord over the hearth for safe childbirth, bought by her sister. A small iron nail wrapped in wool, nailed above the byre door, which had cost a farmer two eggs and which Zephyrine had made knowing full well it held no magic at all — only the idea of watching-over, which sometimes worked better than the real thing.

Wren sat close to him and watched everything with those dark, patient eyes, and when Bess laughed, Wren's mouth twitched at the corners, trying the shape of it.

Afterward, in the loft where Bess spread blankets for them, Odile lay with her hands laced behind her head and said quietly, "You didn't know her name. You didn't know any of their names. And still they're feeding us."

"I sold them trinkets."

"You sold them being seen." Odile turned her head. In the dark her voice was rougher. "Twelve years, Zeph. You've been building something without noticing. A web. Every knot a person who half-knows what you are and decided it didn't matter." She was quiet a moment. "I spent twelve years deciding it did matter. That you'd betrayed us past forgiving. And here I am eating stew off your reputation."

Zephyrine stared at the rafters. "It wasn't a web. It was just — getting by."

"Everything's just getting by," Odile said, "until you look back and see the shape of it."

Below them, faintly, Wren's breathing had gone slow and even, and Bess was moving about her kitchen, humming, and Zephyrine lay awake a long time listening to the ordinary sounds of shelter, feeling the first warmth in twelve years that he hadn't built himself.

* * *

The second keeper, Odile told them, was a man named Tammas Reeve who lived beyond the fens, in a market town called Harrowgate, where the roads crossed and the tolls were cheap and nobody asked where you'd come from. Three days' walk, if the weather held. The weather did not hold.

Rain came in off the fens sideways, cold as a grudge, and by the middle of the second day Wren was shivering so hard his teeth chattered, though he never once complained — had never once complained, in all the weeks since the night he'd appeared at the shop door, silent as a held breath. Zephyrine carried him on his back for a while, then Odile took him, wrapping him in her oilskin, muttering curses at the sky in two languages.

They came down toward a crossroads inn as the light failed, its windows glowing like embers, and Zephyrine's stomach dropped, because inns meant questions, questions meant names, names meant the witch-finders' net drawing tighter. But Odile was already striding toward it. "Harrowgate's a market town," she said. "Half these people have bought something off you too, whether you remember it or not."

Inside, the inn was low-beamed and crowded, smelling of wet wool and onions, and for one terrible moment every face seemed to turn toward them. Then a broad man behind the bar squinted at Zephyrine, and his squint softened into recognition.

"The charm-man." He said it loudly enough that heads turned, and Zephyrine's blood went to ice — and then the man grinned. "My wife swears by your headache ribbons. Says she'd pay double." He banged the bar. "Room and fire for the charm-man and his folk!"

What followed, Zephyrine could hardly piece together afterward. A farmer insisted they take his table. A drover pressed a cup of hot spiced wine into Odile's hands and refused payment. An old woman with a knitted shawl — Zephyrine knew that shawl, had sold her the pattern-charm stitched into its hem, a charm for warmth that was mostly good knitting and a little bit of something else — patted Wren's wet hair and clucked over him like a hen. Nobody asked their names. Somebody asked if the boy was his son, and before Zephyrine could stumble, Odile said, "Yes," with such finality that the question died on the spot.

Wren, wrapped in a blanket by the fire, watched it all with his chin on his knees. And when the old woman gave him a bowl of pottage and he took it, he looked up at her and said — hoarse, rusty, like a hinge unused for years:

"Thank you."

Two words. Common tongue. The whole room might as well have gone silent for what it did to Zephyrine's chest.

The old woman beamed as if he'd recited poetry. "Polite little thing, isn't he," she said, and Wren, emboldened, tried again: "Warm." He pressed his hands to the bowl. "Is warm."

"Is warm," the old woman agreed solemnly, and Wren's face did something complicated and bright, the face of a boy discovering that words were doors and everyone around him was holding them open.

Later, when the common room had thinned and the rain hammered the shutters, Wren climbed onto the bench beside Zephyrine and leaned against his shoulder, heavy with sleep, and said, in that new, careful voice, working the words like stones across a stream:

"You. Gave. Warm. To. Them." A pause, gathering. "Now. They give. Back."

Zephyrine's throat closed. He put his arm around the boy and looked into the fire and thought about what Odile had said in the loft, about webs and knots, about looking back and seeing the shape. Twelve years of small transactions — a ribbon here, a blessing-shaped nothing there — and he had told himself it was commerce, survival, nothing. But the boy was right. It had never been nothing. Kindness moved through the world like water through chalk, slow and invisible, and surfaced where you least expected it, exactly when you needed it most.

"Is that how it works?" Zephyrine murmured. "Where you come from?"

Wren was quiet so long Zephyrine thought he'd fallen asleep. Then, barely audible: "There. Everything. Is taken." His fingers curled into Zephyrine's sleeve. "Here. People. Give." He looked up, and in the firelight his eyes were the color of the gate-stone, deep and veined with silver. "I like. Here."

Zephyrine held him and did not say what they both knew: that the far realm was dying, that Sable Vane's exiles wanted the gate thrown open, that the covenant was unraveling turn by turn toward a cracked red moon, and that the boy leaning trustingly against his shoulder was the key to all of it — sent through by a weeping woman, hunted by men with iron cuffs, carrying a language older than the law that would hang him for speaking it. Zephyrine held him and said none of it, and gave the boy the only thing left that was true: "Then we'll keep you here. Whatever it costs."

From across the room, Odile watched them over the rim of her cup, and said nothing, and smiled like a woman watching a debt finally coming due in the right direction.

* * *

They reached Harrowgate on the fifth morning, under a sky scoured clean by the storms, the fens glittering behind them like hammered tin. The town was loud with market-day — carts and hawkers, geese complaining, the smell of frying fish — and Odile led them through the crush with the confidence of a woman following a scent only she could smell.

Tammas Reeve, she'd said, kept a stall at the edge of the fish-market. Sold knives. Kept his head down. Hadn't answered a letter in twelve years.

They found the stall easily enough, and found it shuttered, and found the shuttered stall surrounded by a knot of townsfolk talking in the low, urgent way people talk when something has happened that everyone knows and no one will say aloud.

"What's happened here?" Odile demanded, in her lighthouse-keeper's voice, the one that brooked no fog.

A fishwife jerked her thumb. "Watchmen took him. Yesterday. Said he was harboring unregistered persons." She lowered her voice further. "But Tammas hasn't harbored so much as a stray cat in ten years. Man's careful as winter."

Zephyrine felt the ground tilt beneath him. Unregistered persons. The phrase from Hale's licensing visit, smooth as oil. *We've had reports of unregistered children.*

"They took him where?" he heard himself ask.

"Magistrate's house. There's a witch-finder come to town." The fishwife spat, discreetly, into the gutter. "Iron badge, soft voice. Been asking after travelers. A man, a woman, a mute boy."

The market noise receded. Zephyrine became aware of Wren's hand finding his, small and cold, and of Odile stepping slightly in front of them both, shoulders squared, an old oath-keeper's instinct rising through twelve years of salt and silence.

"He'll hold," Odile said quietly. "Tammas always was the stubborn one. He'll hold for days before he gives a description." Her jaw tightened. "But he won't hold forever, and Hale won't stop at one arrest. This is a net, Zeph. He's cutting the strands ahead of us, one keeper at a time, herding us toward—"

She stopped. Her eyes had gone past Zephyrine's shoulder, to the far end of the market square, where a man in a watchman's coat stood very still among the crowd, not shopping, not talking, not moving at all except with his eyes — and his eyes were on them.

On Wren.

The man touched two fingers to the iron badge at his collar — a small, courteous gesture, almost a salute — and turned, unhurried, and disappeared down the lane toward the magistrate's house.

Behind them, unnoticed by anyone, a hawker's wind-chime stirred in the breeze, and Wren — who alone of the three of them could read the old currents running under the world — went rigid, and spoke his clearest sentence yet, in a whisper meant only for Zephyrine:

"He. Knows. What I am."

And somewhere beneath their feet, deeper than cellars, deeper than memory, something vast and patient counted another turn of the moon.
