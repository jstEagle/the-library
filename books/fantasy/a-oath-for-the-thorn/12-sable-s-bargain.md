## 12. Sable's Bargain

I had been watching them for two days before I finally let them see me.

Not because I was afraid. Fear is a luxury the far realm burned out of me years ago, along with most of my softer organs. I watched because Zephyrine Sandoval travels like a man who has spent twelve years teaching himself not to be followed — and a man like that deserves to be studied before he is approached. He walks with his shoulders curled inward, as if he could fold himself small enough to slip through the world unnoticed. He sleeps in shifts, one eye always toward Wren. He counts exits in every room he enters, and he does it without moving his lips.

But he has forgotten how to read a road.

Odile Ferran hasn't. The old lighthouse woman walks at the rear with a boat-hook she uses as a walking staff and a scowl that has probably turned back smugglers on its own. She checks their backtrail every quarter hour, and twice she caught my spoor — a bent reed here, a footprint pressed too deep there — and frowned at it without understanding. Her eyes are going soft at the edges. Twelve years of salt wind will do that.

It was the boy who felt me first.

Wren stopped dead in the middle of the forest track on the second morning, so suddenly that Odile nearly walked into him. He turned his face north-northeast, directly to where I crouched beneath a hawthorn with my hood up and my breath held, and he *looked* at me. Not with his eyes. With whatever it is that lives under his skin — the covenant-stuff, the key-thing, the reason a dying woman on the other side of a sealed door had wept over him and pushed him through.

He couldn't see me. But he knew I was there, and he said nothing, and he walked on, and I understood then that the boy already knew more about what was coming than any of them did.

Children born of both realms are rare enough that the exiles have songs about them. None of the songs end well.

* * *

The road they traveled ran along the spine of the Greymarsh hills, following an old drover's path toward the mountain passes and the shrine where — if my information was still good, and it usually was — Merrin's bones were supposed to lie. They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen standing stone, a ring of low fire, Odile's stew gone thin from stretching. Zephyrine sat with his back against the stone and Wren tucked against his side, and he was teaching the boy words. Common words, mouthed slowly, fingers tracing shapes in the dirt: *river*, *bread*, *home*.

Home. The word sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

I stepped out of the dark with my hands open and empty, the way you approach a wild animal or a man who once sealed a door with blood.

Zephyrine was on his feet before I finished my third step. I'll give him this much: twelve years behind a counter selling charms with the magic sanded off hadn't dulled him entirely. There was a knife in his hand — a plain one, bought honestly, but his knuckles went white around the grip in a way that told me the blade wasn't the weapon. The weapon was what he'd do with it, and how little he wanted to.

"Sable Vane," he said. My name came out flat as a coin slapped on a table.

"Charm-seller." I kept my hands wide. "Liar. Oath-breaker." I let him hear each word land. "And tonight, something worse. A petitioner."

"Odile," Zephyrine said quietly, and the old woman rose with her boat-hook leveled, planting herself between me and the boy with the ease of long habit. Guarding people was in her bones. It always had been.

"You won't need that," I told her. "If I wanted the boy dead, I'd have done it while you slept. If I wanted the boy *taken*, I'd have done it while you slept too. I've crossed worse ground than your backtrail, Odile Ferran, and I did it alone."

"Then why are you here?" Zephyrine asked.

"Because I'm tired," I said, and heard my own voice crack on it, and hated myself for the honesty even as I understood it was the only coin this man would accept. "And because you're walking toward a grave that isn't a grave, and someone who knows the truth should tell you before you find it the hard way."

* * *

They didn't invite me to the fire. I sat down anyway, three paces out, close enough to share its warmth and far enough to honor the shape of their distrust. Wren watched me the whole time with those eyes that were too old for his face — watching, weighing, listening with more than his ears.

"I was born in Vessarin," I began, "on the far side. Have you ever seen it?"

"No one here has," Zephyrine said. "That's rather the point of the gate being shut."

"The point," I said, "is that it's dying."

I let that sit. Let him hear the way the fire popped and the night insects went on with their indifferent business around the sentence.

"When your covenant held, magic moved between the realms like water between connected pools — high tide here, low tide there, back and forth, balanced. Your scholars never understood it because your realm runs thin; the deep magic pools on our side. We were the reservoir. You were the riverbed. And when the gate sealed—" I spread my hands. "You dammed a river and called it peace."

"We sealed a wound," Odile growled.

"You sealed a *valve*, woman, and you sealed it crooked." My voice came out harder than I intended. I reeled it back. "Twelve years, and the pressure has nowhere to go. So it bleeds. Through the cracks in your seal, through every scar some fool has chipped into the gate-stone, through the boy himself — why do you think he can speak the First Tongue? Why do you think the wards sing when he walks past? He leaks. We all leak. Our realm is bleeding out through a door you locked, and there is no one left over there with the strength to stop it."

"What happens," Zephyrine said slowly, "when a realm bleeds out?"

I looked at him for a long moment. This was the question. Everything I had crossed the veil-shadow for, everything the exiles had argued over in our camps of ragged tents and failing lantern-light, lived inside this question, and I found that I could not give him the clever answer. Only the true one.

"The sky goes first," I said. "Ours looks like torn silk now. Holes in it. Through the holes you can see... nothing. Not stars. Not dark. Nothing, the way a blind eye sees nothing. Then the land goes. Grass forgets how to be grass. Rivers run uphill looking for magic that isn't there anymore. Children born after the sealing come out wrong — hollow-eyed, quiet, half-finished. We call them the unbright." My throat tightened. "There are four hundred of us left in the exile camps on this side, Zephyrine. Four hundred who got out before the door shut, or slipped through the cracks since. On the other side, there were millions."

The fire cracked. Odile had lowered her boat-hook somewhere in the last minute without noticing she'd done it.

"Millions," she repeated.

"And falling. Every year the bleed gets faster. Every year another valley goes silent. Another town wakes up gray." I pulled my hood back so the firelight could find my face — the scars, the sunken cheeks, the white streak in my hair that I'd earned at thirty and wore at forty-five. "Look at me. I'm fifty years younger than the oldest exile and I look older than you, Odile. That's what living downstream of a dying well does. That's what your seal did."

"It saved this realm," Zephyrine said. But his voice had no iron in it. It had the sound of a man reciting a creed whose god had stopped answering.

"It saved *your* realm by murdering mine," I said. "I don't say that to wound you. I say it because you deserve to carry the whole arithmetic, not the comfortable half. You were five oath-keepers. You voted — all but one of you — to share the gate, open it under law, bind both worlds to the same covenant. And then you decided, alone, in the dark, that you knew better. And Merrin died for it. And the rest of you scattered. And my world began to die."

"How do you know that?" Zephyrine whispered. "What happened that night. How could you possibly—"

"Because we've had twelve years to dig," I said. "Exiles on both sides of the seal. People who were near the gate that night. People who heard the argument through the stone, who saw who struck the blow, who carried the story out piece by piece until we had it whole." I met his eyes and did not blink. "You want truth instead of threat, charm-seller? Here is truth. You killed Merrin. Not the witch-finders, not the betrayal you've spent twelve years flinching from — *you*. The blood on the seal-stone was yours. Everyone over there knows it. Everyone over there has always known it. We named the wound after you."

Wren made a small sound. I glanced at him and found him staring at Zephyrine with an expression I couldn't read — not horror, not quite. Something closer to recognition. As if the boy had carried a picture of this man's guilt inside him since the day a weeping woman called him *key* and pushed him through a closing door.

Zephyrine had not moved. He sat very still, the way men sit when the ground has opened under them and movement means acknowledging the fall. When he spoke, his voice was almost steady.

"Merrin's body was never found. I told myself—"

"You told yourself what guilty men tell themselves." I softened, because cruelty past the point of use is just cruelty. "I know. I've stood where you're standing. Half my life ago I did a thing I've spent every day since trying to unmake, and I know exactly how the floor feels when the truth finally comes through it." I drew a breath. "So here is my bargain, Zephyrine Sandoval. No threats. No knives in the dark. Truth, freely given, for truth freely given."

"Say it."

"The exiles want the gate open. All of us. Even the ones who talk of force — and yes, there are others coming behind me who mean to break the seal whether you help or not, and they are less patient than I am, and they have been chipping at your gate-stone for months, which you already know because you've seen the scars." His face flickered; I pressed on. "We don't ask you to throw it wide. We ask you to help us forge a *new* covenant — controlled, governed, a border instead of a wound. Tides managed. Both realms fed. Your witch-finders kept off, your city kept safe, my people kept alive. That is what we want. What we have *always* wanted."

"And what do you offer?"

"Everything I know." I counted it on my fingers. "The full map of the damage to your seal. The names and numbers of the exiles, the patient ones and the impatient ones. The route to the shrine you're walking toward — and the warning that goes with it, because what waits at that shrine is not a grave, Zephyrine, and when you understand what it actually is, you will wish someone had told you before you arrived." I paused. "And one thing more. The last piece of that night. The part you've never known, because no one on this side survived who saw it."

His breath caught. "Merrin—"

"Died with your blood on their hands and a message on their lips," I said. "We found a witness. Someone crossing the veil-shadow that night, someone who reached the chamber door too late and hid, and heard, and lived, and has carried it for twelve years because carrying it alone was killing them." I leaned forward, and the firelight moved across my face, and I gave him the only gift I had that was truly mine to give. "Merrin's last words were not curses, Zephyrine. They were *forgiveness*. 'Tell him it was the right door,' the witness heard. 'Tell him I chose it.' You have spent twelve years building your prison out of a murder. It was never a murder. It was a sacrifice you never agreed to accept — and refusing to accept it has cost you everything, and it has cost *my world* everything, and I am asking you, on behalf of everyone I have ever loved, to put it down."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and texture, that presses against the ears. Zephyrine stared into the fire and I watched twelve years rearrange themselves behind his eyes — watched a man discover that the cell he'd built was built on sand.

"That's not possible," he said hoarsely. "That can't—"

"It is," Odile said quietly.

We both turned. The old woman had tears cutting lines through the grime on her weathered cheeks, and her voice, when it came, was rough as rope.

"I found a witness too, Zeph. Three years back. Never told you because — because I thought you'd break, and I needed you whole to raise that boy." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at her own tears. "Same words. Same witness. I checked it twice. It's true. It's been true the whole time."

"You *knew*?" The word tore out of him. "You knew, all this time, and you let me—"

"I let you live," Odile snapped back, and then gentled, the way a storm gentles when it spends itself. "Aye, and I'd do it again. But you're right to be angry. We've all been keeping each other's prisons locked, haven't we. Twelve years of kindness used as bars." She looked at me across the fire, and something passed between us — two women who had both chosen protection over truth and were only now learning what it had cost. "Maybe it's time somebody picked a lock."

Wren moved.

He crawled out from Zephyrine's side, crossed the space between the fire and me, and sat down beside me as simply as if we were old friends. Then he took my scarred hand in his small warm one, turned it over, and traced one finger along the white streak-marked lines of my palm — reading it, or writing on it, I couldn't tell. And he spoke.

"*Sister*," he said, in the First Tongue, in a voice rusty from disuse but clear as a bell over water.

I am not ashamed of what happened next, though I have never told anyone. I wept. Openly, ugly, the way I had not wept since the night I climbed through the closing gate and left my mother shouting my name into a sky that was beginning to tear.

Because that word — *sister* — was the word the unbright children used for the women of the exile camps who fed them. The word for kin not of blood but of shared hunger. And the boy had learned it over *there*, in the dying realm, in the years before a woman wept over him and sent him through. Which meant he remembered. He remembered the camps, the gray valleys, the torn silk sky. He remembered what we were losing.

He was not their key. He was their witness. And he had chosen, out of everything he might have said first, to claim *me*.

"Boy," I managed, when I could speak. "Why did you come through? Do you know?"

He shook his head slowly. Then he pointed — at himself, at the ground, at the direction of the distant mountains and the gate beneath the city beyond them — and pressed his small hand flat over his own heart, and closed his eyes, and made a gesture I knew from the death-tents of the exile camps.

The gesture for *giving what cannot be taken back*.

Zephyrine went white. "No," he said. "No, whatever that means, no—"

"He doesn't fully know yet," I said quickly, catching Wren's shoulder. "But he knows more than you do, and less than he will. And that is precisely why my bargain matters, charm-seller. Because things are converging on that gate — the exiles, the witch-finders, the thirteenth moon — and the boy intends to put himself between all of it, and the only way anyone saves him is by getting there first and knowing more than he does." I stood, brushing the dirt from my knees, and offered Zephyrine my hand across the fire. "So. Truth for truth. Help for help. Will you take my bargain, Zephyrine Sandoval? Or shall I leave you to learn the rest the hard way, at the shrine, with the moon turning above you?"

He looked at my hand. He looked at Wren, who was watching him with those old, old eyes. He looked at Odile, who nodded once, slow and grim, like a judge passing sentence.

Then Zephyrine Sandoval took my hand, and his grip was cold and shaking and absolutely certain.

"Start with the shrine," he said. "Tell me what's really waiting for us in Merrin's grave."

I held his grip a moment longer, and I let him see the pity in my face, because he deserved the warning written plainly.

"Brace yourself," I said. "Because Merrin isn't in that grave at all. And when you reach the shrine — you're going to hear their voice."
