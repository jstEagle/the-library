## 13. The Keeper Who Died

The mountain did not want them there.

Zephyrine felt it in the way the path narrowed and doubled back on itself, in the pines that leaned too close, in the cold that came down off the peak not like weather but like intention. Three days they had climbed since leaving the low country behind — three days of Odile's oath-stone fragment growing warmer in its wrappings until she stopped carrying it in her pack and carried it against her chest instead, "like a bird with a fever," she said.

Now the stone was hot enough to feel through wool.

"It knows where we are," Odile said. She stood at the lip of the shrine's approach, breath steaming, gray hair whipping loose from its braid. Below them, half-swallowed by scree and lichen, lay a ring of standing stones no taller than a man's shoulder, arranged around a hollow of black earth. Beyond the ring, the mountainside simply *stopped* — a sheer drop into cloud, as if the world had been cut away with a knife.

"This is where we buried her," Zephyrine said.

It had been twelve years, but his body remembered the climb down better than his mind wanted it to. He remembered Merrin's weight on his shoulders for the last stretch, lighter than she should have been because the blood had already done its work. He remembered Odile digging. He remembered Sable — no. Not Sable. Sable had been gone by then, crossed over or fled; he'd never learned which. He remembered the four of them who remained lowering their fifth into this hollow while the gate beneath the city screamed across a hundred miles of stone, and he remembered deciding, alone, that Merrin would be buried here rather than carried home, because here was where her oath had been sworn and he could not bear to look at her face on the road.

He had made so many decisions alone. That was the whole shape of his sin, when he finally let himself see it: not the sealing itself, but the solitude. Every wound in his life traced back to a door he had closed without asking anyone else to hold the handle.

"Zeph." Odile touched his arm. "Look at the stones."

He looked. And his stomach dropped through the mountain.

The ring of standing stones was carved. It hadn't been, twelve years ago — they had been raw granite, rough as bread. Now every surface was covered in script, dense and flowing, spiraling inward toward the hollow at the center. The First Tongue. Someone had written an entire covenant into the rock around Merrin's grave, line upon line upon line, and the lines were *glowing*, faintly, the way embers glow under ash.

Wren slipped past them both before Zephyrine could catch his sleeve. The boy crouched at the ring's edge, laid both palms flat on the nearest stone, and read aloud — not translating, just reading, his voice small and clear in the thin air, words that made the hair on Zephyrine's arms rise because he understood them without wanting to.

*She who holds. She who is held. She who waits between.*

"No," Zephyrine said. The word came out flat and stupid. "No, that's not — she died. I carried her body. I *buried*—"

"Zephyrine." Odile's voice had gone very quiet, which was worse than shouting. She had unbound the oath-stone fragment from her chest and was holding it out on her open palm, and the fragment was moving. Trembling. Turning slowly, like a compass needle finding north, toward the hollow at the center of the ring. "What do you think an anchor *is*?"

* * *

They dug for an hour in the frozen ground, and found nothing.

No bones. No shroud. No body. Only a cavity shaped like a sleeping woman, pressed into the black earth and then filled again, as if the mountain had taken what it was given and kept it. As if the grave had been a door all along, and someone had walked through it backward.

Zephyrine sat back on his heels in the empty grave and laughed, once, a sound with nothing in it. "I buried her," he said. "I *carried* her. I felt her go cold."

"You felt what the gate wanted you to feel." Odile stood over him, and her face was terrible with pity. "Or what *she* wanted us to feel. Think, Zeph. Think about what happened that night. The covenant broke, the gate was tearing itself apart, five keepers and one dead — and then suddenly, impossibly, the seal held. For twelve years it held. You thought your blood did that. Your betrayal, your solitary sin, your grand stupid sacrifice." She shook her head slowly. "You flattered yourself. Something held that gate, and something has held it every hour since, and anchors don't come free."

Wren tugged at Zephyrine's coat. When Zephyrine looked up, the boy pointed — not at the hollow, but past it, past the ring, to the place where the mountainside ended and the cloud began.

At first Zephyrine saw nothing. Then the cloud moved, or the light did, and he saw that the cliff face was not cliff at all. It was a door. A second gate, high on the mountain, older than the one beneath his shop — the original, maybe, the first seam ever cut between the worlds — and it was sealed with the same scarred lattice of oaths he knew by heart, except here the lattice was whole, unchipped, tended. And bound into its center, woven through the stone like a thread through cloth, was a figure.

A woman. Standing upright inside the rock, arms out, palms flat against the inner faces of the gate, as if she were holding two worlds apart by main strength. Her hair had gone white as frost. Her eyes were open.

Her eyes were open, and they were looking directly at him.

"Merrin," Zephyrine whispered.

The mouth moved. No sound crossed the distance — or perhaps sound did cross, and it was simply not sound meant for ears, because Zephyrine heard it anyway, arriving somewhere behind his sternum, in the voice of a woman he had mourned for twelve years:

*You're late, Zephyrine Sandoval. But then, you always were.*

* * *

Getting to her took the rest of the day. There was a path, once Wren found it — of course there was a path; the boy saw things in the First Tongue's architecture that Zephyrine's eyes had been trained to skip — a goat-track switchbacking along the void, narrow enough that they went in single file with their backs to the drop. Odile sang the old walking-songs under her breath, the ones from before, the ones that used to make Merrin tell her to shut up. Zephyrine did not sing. His hands would not stop shaking, and he needed them for the rock.

Twelve years. She had been awake for twelve years.

Every night he had lit the lamp above his shop and told himself the worst was paid for. Every morning he had woken into the numbness he'd built like a wall around the memory of that night, and the numbness had held, and he had called it survival. And the whole time, forty miles north and three thousand feet up, Merrin had been standing in a door, holding it shut with her hands, aware, *awake*, watching seasons wheel past the gap in the stone, watching the moon count down from thirteen.

How many times had she seen him in dreams? How many times had she called his name into the rock?

The path ended at a ledge wide enough for the three of them, directly before the gate. Up close, the lattice of oaths blazed like a hearth, and the heat coming off it was wrong — not fire-heat but *pressure*-heat, the warmth of something straining. Zephyrine raised his hand toward the stone and stopped an inch away, because now that he was near enough to truly look, he could see what the glowing hid.

Cracks. Hairline fractures running through the oaths like cracks in glaze, hundreds of them, and through each crack seeped a fine golden dust that the wind took and scattered into the cloud below. Magic bleeding out of the seal. The same sickness Sable had described, the far realm dying of leakage — except now he understood it wasn't only the far realm. The covenant was hemorrhaging from both sides, and the woman holding the wound closed was the bandage, and bandages fray.

"Merrin." His voice cracked on the name. "Merrin, it's me. It's Zeph. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I didn't know, I—"

*I know what you didn't know.* The voice arrived in his chest again, and it was frayed at the edges now that he listened closely, like a rope rubbed near to parting. *I've had twelve years to be angry at you, and I spent the first six on it, and it was a waste. Anger needs somewhere to go, and there's nowhere to go in here. So I spent the next six letting it go. You should try it sometime. You still carry yours like luggage.*

"I'm getting you out." He pressed his palm flat to the stone beside her hand, and the lattice burned cold against his skin. "Odile has an oath-stone fragment. We have Wren. We can take the anchor-load, divide it, the way the original five were meant to—"

*Zephyrine.* Gentle. Terribly gentle. *Look at my hands.*

He looked. And understood, finally, why the gate had shown him a vision of thirteen moons instead of simply calling for help. Why the chipping at the city seal had gone unanswered. Why the covenant was unraveling everywhere at once, quietly, patiently, like rot.

Merrin's fingers had grown into the stone. Not metaphorically. Flesh and rock had negotiated a long truce and then forgotten where one ended, root and branch, and where her wrists met the gate the boundary was seamless. She was not trapped in the door. She *was* the door. Pulling her out would be pulling the keystone from an arch.

"The thirteenth moon," Odile said hoarsely. She had come up beside him, and her hand found his without either of them deciding it. "When it turns full, the covenant completes its cycle. Either it renews or it breaks. That's what the vision meant. That's what Wren's been trying to tell us since the shop."

*Yes.* Merrin's eyes moved to Wren, and softened, and broke a little. *Hello, little key. I felt you the day you came through. I've felt every crossing since the seal went in — every smuggled charm, every whispered word, everything your Zephyrine sanded the magic off of and sold to neighbors. He thought he was hiding. He was keeping the pilot light lit. Clever, stubborn man.*

"Don't," Zephyrine said. His eyes were burning and he let them. "Don't be kind to me right now. Tell me how to fix it. Tell me what replaces you."

The silence stretched long enough that the wind changed. When Merrin spoke again, the fraying in her voice was audible, threads of her coming loose mid-word, and it was the most frightening thing he had heard in twelve years — worse than Hale's iron cuff, worse than the scarred seal beneath his shop — because it was the sound of a mind being worn away by its own duty, grain by grain, and there was no one to blame for it and no one to stop it.

*A new anchor. That is the only arithmetic the covenant accepts. One for one. A life poured into the stone, and the stone pours back purpose, and the purpose eats whatever it touches first. In me it started with sleep, then hunger, then the faces of everyone I loved. I have been rationing your faces, all four of you, one hour a day, so I wouldn't lose them before I lost myself. Today I spent two hours on yours, Zephyrine. I regret nothing.*

"Then we find another way," Odile said fiercely. "There's always another way. Five keepers built this thing — five, together, shared. If the load is divided—"

*Divided, yes. Diluted, even. The original covenant was never meant to rest on one spine; that was the flaw, and the flaw is mine — I let it rest on me because I trusted no one else after the betrayal, and trust withheld is a debt that compounds.* Merrin's gaze slid back to Zephyrine, and in it was neither accusation nor absolution, only the terrible clarity of someone with very little time left spending none of it on comfort. *You could share the anchor. All of you. It would cost each of you less than it has cost me. Perhaps it would cost each of you almost nothing. But someone must stand in the stone first, at the turning of the moon, and hold the door while the others bind themselves in — a keystone goes in before the arch closes, Zephyrine. Someone stands alone in the dark first. That is the price of doing it rightly, and there is no oath clever enough to waive it.*

The wind came up off the cloud and cut through Zephyrine's coat, and he barely felt it, because inside his chest a familiar machinery was already turning, gears he knew by their grinding: *me, I'll do it, I'll stand in the dark, it's mine to do, my betrayal bought this, my blood opened the account—*

He caught himself. Physically caught himself, fist closing hard enough to bite nails into his own palm, because he had promised — he had promised Odile on the road, in the rain, after Tam's arrest, after the testimony that burned his life down — *not without us. Never again without us.* And he would keep that promise if it killed the impulse in him, whatever it cost.

But he looked at Merrin's hands in the stone, and at the golden dust streaming off the cracks, and at the moon — visible now through a tear in the cloud, swollen and pale, one turn short of full — and he heard himself say, very quietly:

"How long do we have?"

*Until the thirteenth moon turns,* Merrin said. *Three nights.*

And then her head tilted, sharply, like a deer scenting wolves, and her voice changed — flattened, hurried, scraped thin with alarm:

*And you must decide faster than that. Because something is climbing the mountain behind you, and it does not walk like any of my keepers. Iron, Zephyrine. I smell iron on the wind.*

Zephyrine turned. Far below, winding up the goat-track they had climbed that morning, torches were moving through the dusk. A dozen of them. Two dozen. Moving with the unhurried discipline of people who knew exactly where they were going, and among them, catching the torchlight and throwing it back cold, glinted the unmistakable curve of iron cuffs.

Beside him, Wren made a sound he had never made before — not a word, not yet, but the shape of one, the first syllable of a sentence the boy had been holding behind his teeth since the night he appeared at the shop door. He was staring at the gate, at Merrin, at the anchor-stone, and his small hands had curled into fists, and his eyes were wet and absolutely certain.

Zephyrine had three nights to find another way.

He was beginning to fear the boy had already decided what it would be.
