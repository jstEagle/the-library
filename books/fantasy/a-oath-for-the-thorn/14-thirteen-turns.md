## 14. Thirteen Turns

The mountain speaks in a language Wren knows better than his own mouth.

It is not the First Tongue, though the wind up here carries words in it sometimes — thin syllables scraped off the shrine stones by weather, half-eaten by frost. It is older than that. It is the language of things that stand still for a very long time: stone, snow, the crooked pine bent sideways by a hundred winters of one particular wind. Wren has spent most of his life unable to speak, and so he has learned to listen to everything that cannot speak either, and the mountain is the loudest quiet thing he has ever met.

He sits on the shrine steps with his knees pulled up and counts.

Not sheep. Not stars. Moons.

There are thirteen of them now if you know how to look — and he knows how to look, because the gate taught him before Odile's cart ever reached this valley. Thirteen moons wheeling around the Thorn Gate like beads on a wire, twelve bright and one dark, and every night since they left the city the dark one has been a little less dark. A little more *there*. Like an eye opening behind the sky.

Twelve turns gone. One turn left.

Wren holds up his hand against the rising moon and spreads his fingers. Five. He does this sometimes when the counting gets too big. Five fingers, five keepers, five names stitched into the covenant like colors into cloth. Zephyrine. Odile. Sable, who was never meant to be a keeper but became one anyway, the way a river becomes a border. Merrin, who is not dead, who was never dead, who is *inside* — and thinking about Merrin makes something cold move through Wren's chest, because he has been inside too, in a way, and he knows what the inside of a door feels like when it starts to forget there was ever anyone knocking.

And himself. The fifth. The key.

*Key.*

The woman said it the night she pushed him through. He remembers her hands — long hands, ink-stained, shaking so badly she had to grip his shoulders twice to make them hold. He remembers her voice breaking on the word the way a branch breaks under snow: slowly, then all at once. *You are the key, little one. Forgive me. Forgive me.* And then the light, and the tearing, and the rain-lashed street on the other side where a man with tired eyes opened a shop door and looked at Wren as if Wren were a wound he'd forgotten he had.

Wren did not understand then. He understands now, and he wishes with everything in him that understanding worked the other way — that you could un-know a thing by learning it too well.

Because here is what the shrine showed him today, in the hour between noon and dusk, when Odile went down the path to argue with a goatherd about lodging and Zephyrine sat inside the broken chapel copying oath-words onto paper he kept burning afterward:

Here is what the covenant is.

It is not a lock. Wren had always thought of it as a lock, because everyone spoke of sealing and unsealing, keys and gates. But locks are simple. Locks only want to be closed or open. The covenant is a *knot* — a knot tied between two worlds so that neither bleeds into the other, and a knot must be held. Someone must hold it. From the inside. With their whole self wrapped around the rope.

Merrin held it for twelve years. That is what being dead actually meant. That is what the anchor-stone was. Not a grave. A pair of hands.

And here is the part the shrine showed him in a flash of white light that left him on his back in the snow, gasping, his heart going like a bird in a cupped fist:

A grown keeper dissolves.

That is what happened to Merrin, or began to happen, or will finish happening when the thirteenth moon turns. An adult self is already finished, already set like fired clay, and the covenant's pull finds no seam in it to hold — so it takes the whole. Twelve years of holding, and Merrin's mind fraying like wet rope, because the knot eats what holds it unless the holder has somewhere for the eating to go.

But a child —

A child is not finished. A child is still soft clay, still growing, still full of seams. A child born of both realms — a child whose blood already belongs to both sides of the rope — can wrap around the knot without dissolving into it. Can hold it the way a young tree holds a fence wire: growing around it, growing *with* it, the two of them becoming one shape over years.

Years.

His childhood. His voice. His self, poured out slowly, season by season, until there was nothing left in him but the holding.

That is why she wept. That is why she called him *key* and could not look at him while she said it. She had done the arithmetic every adult in this story has been doing — Zephyrine with his face going gray at the shrine, Odile gripping her oath-stone fragment until her knuckles went white, even Sable with her careful kind eyes — and she had chosen him, because he was the only answer that didn't destroy someone *finished*.

Someone finished. Someone who had already lived. Someone like—

Wren's breath stops.

He looks down at his own hands, small in the blue evening light, and he sees it. He sees the whole shape of it, the way you see a pattern in frost once someone points and says *look, a fern.*

Zephyrine means to do it.

Wren has watched him for days without having a word for what he was watching, and now the word arrives and it is terrible. Zephyrine has been memorizing things. Wren knows what memorizing looks like; he has done it his whole silent life, cataloguing faces and exits and which neighbors' windows catch lamplight at which hours. Zephyrine has been looking at Odile's laugh like a man memorizing a room he is about to leave. He has been touching the shop-charms in his pocket — the little brass sun, the knotted red thread — the way you touch things to fix them in your hands forever. Last night, by the fire, he wrote something on a scrap of paper, read it, and burned it, and the look on his face while it burned was the look the woman had. The *forgive me* look.

Zephyrine means to take Merrin's place. Zephyrine means to put his finished, grown, already-lived self into the knot and let the covenant eat him from the edges inward, and everyone will call it noble, and it will work, for a while, the way a dam works, and then —

No.

Wren stands up so fast the world tilts.

*No.*

The word doesn't come out. Words rarely come out. But it fills him anyway, all the way down, bigger than his body, and for one wild moment he thinks the mountain itself must hear it, because the wind stops, just for a breath, as if listening.

They don't get it. None of them get it, because they are all adults and adults think sacrifice is a coin you pay once. They think the choice is *who*, not *whether*. They line themselves up like stones for a wall and argue about which stone goes first. Zephyrine will not let Wren do it — Wren has seen that too, seen the man flinch every time Wren comes near the anchor-stone, seen him position his own body between Wren and the shrine's inner door like a shield made of tired flesh. And Wren cannot *say* any of it. Cannot argue. Cannot beg. His voice is a locked room and the key to it is the very thing they're fighting over, and the cruelty of that is so perfect it might be funny if it weren't killing him.

So he will not say it.

He will *do* it.

* * *

Night settles over the shrine like ash over a fire.

Inside the chapel, Odile has won the argument with the goatherd and lost the argument with sleep; she dozes upright in a corner, her oath-stone fragment cradled against her chest like a second heart. Zephyrine sits at the trestle table with papers spread before him, and Wren watches him through the doorway and sees it plainly now, the way you see a face in a map once you've seen it once. The copying. The burning. The counting. Zephyrine is building his goodbye out of parchment and oath-words, and he thinks no one can see it, and maybe no one else can, but Wren has spent his whole life reading people who thought silence made them invisible.

Wren crosses the room without sound. He is good at that. He has always been good at that.

He stands beside Zephyrine's chair, and after a moment — because he wants this, because he is stealing this, and thieves should at least be honest — he puts his hand flat on the table next to the papers.

Zephyrine startles, then softens, the way he always does. "Wren." His voice is rough from the altitude and from something else. "You should be sleeping."

Wren shakes his head. Then, carefully, he takes the topmost paper — the one with fresh ink, the one with the opening line of the anchoring rite copied out in Zephyrine's cramped, careful hand — and he holds it up between them.

Zephyrine goes very still.

"It's nothing," he says, too quickly. "Preparation. Understanding the rite so we can — so we can plan around it. That's all."

Wren looks at him.

He has one face and he has used it his whole life, and right now he pours everything he has into it. *I see you. I see what you're doing. You are not as hidden as you think.* He lets the paper drop back to the table and taps it once, twice, with one finger — *this, this* — and then he presses his palm against his own chest, over his heart, and looks at Zephyrine with an expression he hopes is as clear as glass.

Zephyrine's face does something complicated. Guilt first, flickering, then denial, then a terrible gentleness that is worse than either. "No," he says quietly. "Whatever you're thinking — no. You're a child, Wren. You don't understand what—"

Wren stamps his foot.

Once. Hard. The floorboards jump. Odile snorts awake in her corner and mutters and sleeps again, and Wren doesn't care, because for the first time in his life he needs to be loud and this is the loudest he can be. He points at Zephyrine. He points at himself. He mimes it, all of it, fast and furious — wraps his arms around himself like a rope, staggers, clutches his throat, lets his eyes go empty, lets his body sag like something hollowed — *you, dissolved, eaten, gone* — and then he jabs his finger at Zephyrine's chest again, hard enough to rock him.

*Don't you dare.*

"Wren—" Zephyrine catches his wrists, gently, and kneels so they're eye to eye, and his hands are shaking. "Listen to me. Listen. What happened to Merrin — what the covenant does — I have lived my life already. Do you understand? I have had my years. I had—" his voice catches on something, some name, some memory Wren can see him choosing not to say, "—I had people. I had a family once. Whatever the gate takes from me, I've had the spending of it. You haven't. You're eleven years old. You haven't even begun."

*I have begun,* Wren thinks fiercely. *I began the night you opened the door.*

He pulls one wrist free and touches Zephyrine's chest, right over the heart, the way Zephyrine once touched his own when he told the story of the boy at the door — the story Odile told back to him on the road, laughing and crying at once, the story Wren keeps like a stone in his pocket. *You looked at me like I was a wound you'd forgotten you had,* the story says. *You hid me from the patrol. You fed me soup you pretended not to watch me eat. You named me after a bird because I wouldn't sing.*

"You gave me a name," Zephyrine whispers, as if Wren had said it aloud, as if the touch carried it. "Of course I did. Of course I—"

Wren shakes his head. Slowly. Deliberately. *No.*

Then he takes both of Zephyrine's hands in both of his, small hands around large ones, and he bows his head over them the way he saw Odile bow over her oath-stone, and he holds them, and he does not let go, and the meaning is so plain it hardly needs a tongue:

*If you go into that stone, I go in first. If you spend yourself, I will spend myself faster. You cannot save me by drowning, old man. I will dive in after you.*

Zephyrine understands. Wren watches him understand, watches it land like a blow, watches the color leave his face. "You would," Zephyrine says hoarsely. "You stubborn, brave, impossible boy. You absolutely would."

Yes, Wren thinks. Yes, I would. Gladly. Before you can blink.

Because here is the thing none of them know, the thing he learned tonight in the snow with his heart hammering like a trapped bird, the thing he will carry alone because carrying it alone is the first act of the holding:

Children born of both realms can hold the covenant without dissolving. That part is true. But it is not free. Nothing the gate gives is free. The holding will still take — take his childhood, take his voice, take his self, season by season, year by year, until there is nothing left in him but the knot and the hands around it. He will grow *around* it instead of past it. He will become a door that was once, briefly, a boy.

He knows. He chooses it anyway.

Because the alternative is Zephyrine. Zephyrine, who opened a door in the rain. Zephyrine, who lied to a witch-finder with steady eyes while his knee bounced under the table. Zephyrine, who burned his own cover to testify for a neighbor, who walked three days north to be slapped by a woman he betrayed, who is sitting right now in a freezing chapel writing his own ending in careful handwriting because he thinks love means paying alone.

Wren learned love in a shop full of harmless charms. He learned it from soup and naming and a hand held in the dark of a cellar while patrols passed overhead. And love, as far as he can tell — as far as his eleven years of watching have taught him — is not a coin you pay once. It is a charm you renew daily. It is showing up again tomorrow. It is *not without us*, which is what Odile shouted at Zephyrine on the mountain path two nights ago, and which Wren has decided, silently, permanently, is also his.

So: he will do it first. Or he will do it instead. Whichever the night allows.

He releases Zephyrine's hands and steps back, and he lets his face go smooth and sleepy, a child ready for bed, nothing to see here. Zephyrine watches him with haunted eyes and finally, reluctantly, herds him toward the pallet by the fire, pulling the blanket up to his chin the way he has every night since the city, and Wren closes his eyes and breathes slow and lets the adult in the room believe the child has surrendered.

Under the blanket, his fingers move.

He has been practicing. In the dark, in the cart, under tables — tracing shapes on his own knee, on the wood, on Odile's oblivious shoulder. The anchoring rite is a sequence of signs as much as words; the shrine showed him that much before the light knocked him down. He has most of it. Not all. Enough.

Enough, if he slips away before dawn. Enough, if he climbs to the inner chamber while the others sleep. Enough, if the thirteenth moon — one turn from full, hanging fat and faintly cracked above the peak like a coin bitten by a thief — rises to find him already wrapped around the knot.

Let the moon find a key in its lock.

Let it find him.

* * *

He wakes in the black hour before dawn, the hour the woman sent him through, the hour that has always belonged to doors.

The fire is embers. Odile snores softly. Zephyrine sleeps sitting up, his head tipped back against the wall, the burned-smelling ghost of his last paper still in the air, and even asleep his hand rests near his pocket where the brass sun charm lives, guarding his small magics even in dreams.

Wren sits up. Puts his bare feet on the cold stone. Stands.

At the doorway he stops — one last time, one stolen look back at the fire, at the man, at the strange crooked family assembled out of guilt and forgiveness and road-dust. *Mine,* he thinks, with a ferocity that hurts. *All of you. Mine. Which is exactly why.*

Then the boy who was named after a bird slips out into the snow, silent as flight, and begins to climb toward the chamber where the thirteenth moon is waiting to see who answers when it calls.

Behind him, far below in the valley, iron rings against stone — once, twice — like a knock at a door.

Like many boots, marching.
