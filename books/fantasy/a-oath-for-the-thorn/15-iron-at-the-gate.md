## 15. Iron at the Gate

### Corvane Hale

The cuff had been singing to him for three days.

Not a sound, exactly — nothing his ears could catch. It was deeper than sound, a vibration in the marrow of his forearm, in the teeth of his jaw, a hunger that had finally found something worth wanting. Hale had worn the iron for eleven years, and for eleven years it had whispered to him of charms and hedge-craft, of granny-women's knots and love-sick boys' paper talismans — small things, bitter things, magic like table scraps.

This was not table scraps.

He stood at the head of the column in the pre-dawn dark, twelve witch-finders in watchman's gray behind him, and beneath his boots, through six feet of clay and cellar stone and older stone still, he felt it: the Thorn Gate, waking. The cuff drank from it across all that distance like a root finding water, and Hale's whole body shivered with the taste of it.

*There you are,* he thought. *Twelve years under my feet.*

"Inspector." Sergeant Brack, breathing hard from the stairs, rain streaming off his oilskin. "The boy's gone. The charm-seller's gone. Neighbors say they left two nights back with a woman off the north road."

"I know where they've gone."

Brack blinked. "Sir?"

Hale turned and let him see the cuff. He never let anyone see it — that was doctrine, that was discipline, the harvest was not to be shown to the reaped. But tonight he wanted them to understand what they marched toward. The iron was glowing. Faintly, sullenly, the color of a coal remembered rather than seen, but glowing, and the light pulsed in time with something far below the city.

"The Sandoval man is a keeper," Hale said. "One of the old covenant. The gate under his shop isn't sealed anymore — it's *failing*, and when it fails, everything on the other side comes through, and everything on this side burns. Unless someone holds it." He smiled, and felt the smile fit his face poorly, like a borrowed coat. "Someone will hold it. Someone always does. And whoever holds it will be the most powerful creature this realm has ever produced."

"And we arrest him, sir?"

"We *thank* him," Hale said. "We thank him very much. And then we put the cuff on him, and we own both worlds."

He said it plainly because he had learned long ago that ambition spoken plainly frightened men less than ambition hidden. Brack swallowed and nodded and passed the word down the column, and eleven faces went carefully blank in the way of men who have decided not to think about a thing until it is done.

They moved out along the river road as the sky went the color of dirty pewter. Hale walked at the front and did not feel the rain. The cuff sang louder with every step, and beneath the song, fainter, he could hear the other thing now — the thing he had been hearing since the moon began to swell wrong overhead, cracked along one edge like dropped porcelain, red as an old wound.

A voice. Thin, frayed, patient beyond all reason.

*Twelve years,* it seemed to say. *Twelve years I have held the door with my hands, and no one came, and no one came, and no one came.*

Hale did not believe in ghosts. He believed in assets. Whatever was down there, it was an asset approaching failure, and failure, in Hale's ledger, was simply opportunity arriving late.

By full dark they would reach the Thorn Gate. By midnight, he intended to reach through it.

### Sable Vane

The far side of the gate smelled of ash and roses, and Sable Vane had missed it so badly for twelve years that the smell alone nearly brought her to her knees.

She stood on a ridge of glassy black rock — the land here had been glass for a generation, fused by the bleeding of magic through the wounded seal — and looked down at the valley where the Thorn Gate's far face stood: a ring of white thorn-stone in a hillside that used to be orchards and was now a desert of glittering cinders. Two hundred exiles camped around it. Children born on the wrong side of a door their parents had shut. Old men who remembered rain that fell upward. Women who had forgotten the color of the sky over the other realm and taught their daughters songs about it anyway.

Behind her, the sky hung like torn silk — literally torn; there were rents in it now, wide as rivers, and through them showed not stars but a slow grey nothing that ate whatever drifted too close. The realm was dying. Not metaphorically. It was coming apart at the seams, and every seam ran back to the gate, and every year the leaks grew wider.

"Vane." Doss, her second, limping up the ridge with his staff of thorn-wood. "The scouts say the seal is almost through. There's a light under the hill tonight. First time in years."

"I know. I can feel it." She could. Everyone could. The whole camp had been awake for two days, pressed against the ground like animals before a quake.

"And the other side? Your contact's last message said the iron-men were moving."

"They're moving. They'll be there tonight or tomorrow night." She watched the white ring of the gate below, catching the red light of the broken moon. "And the keeper's coming too. Sandoval. With the child."

Doss spat, which in the thin air of the far side was more effort than it sounded. "You trust the iron-men less."

"I don't trust anyone," Sable said. "That's why I'm still alive. But hear me, Doss, and carry it down the camp: we do not touch the child. We do not touch the keeper if it can be helped. We go through the door when it opens, and we fight only those who try to close it on us. The witch-finders want to own both realms. We just want to live in one of them. When those two wants collide—" she shrugged, "—I know which side I'd rather be standing on when the dust settles. But I'd rather it didn't settle into a graveyard."

"You were a soldier once," Doss said. It wasn't a question. Everyone knew. Everyone knew she had been one of the five, once, before the covenant broke, before she'd crossed over on the last night the gate stood open and spent twelve years learning what she'd abandoned.

"I was many things once." She started down the ridge, and the camp rose around her as she passed — two hundred faces turning, hope and terror wearing the same expression. "Tonight we're one thing. Tonight we're the people who are going home."

Below, under the hill, the light under the seal brightened. Somewhere on the other side of the world's thin wall, she felt the anchor strain — felt it the way you feel a rope go taut in your own hands — and heard, or imagined she heard, a voice that had been holding on for twelve years finally begin to slip.

"Hold on," she murmured, to Merrin, whom she had mourned, to the gate, to all of it. "Hold on one more night."

### Zephyrine

They reached the shrine at dusk, and Zephyrine knew before they descended the stair that they were too late.

It was in the air — a pressure like the moment before lightning, but constant, unrelenting, pressing on the eardrums and the heart. It was in Odile's face as she held her oath-stone fragment up and watched it glow without being asked. It was in Wren, who had stopped speaking altogether again, who walked with his small hand flat against the shrine wall as though reading it by touch, and whose eyes when he looked up at Zephyrine were full of a knowledge no child should carry.

"He's slipping," Odile said quietly. "Merrin. Can you feel it?"

Zephyrine could. Twelve years ago he had bound the gate with blood and betrayal and told himself it was necessary, and the price of his necessity had been a friend's life — so he had believed. Three weeks ago he had learned Merrin lived, bound inside the gate as its anchor, aware, alone, dissolving by degrees into the covenant's machinery. Every day since, walking north with Wren and Odile, he had felt the tether between them thinning like a candle burning down.

"Merrin!" he called down the stair, into the dark that breathed cold and thorn-scent. "Merrin, we're here! We found them! Odile's here!"

The answer, when it came, was not a voice. It was the gate itself — a shudder through the mountain, a groan of stone, and then words rising through the floor like bubbles through deep water, arriving already broken:

*...zeph...rine... is that... I dreamed... I dreamed you all forgot...*

"We didn't forget," Odile shouted, and her voice cracked clean in half. "We *never* — do you hear me, Merrin Ferran, we never forgot, we were *ashamed*, which is different, which is worse, and I'll apologize properly when I can see your face—"

"*Odile.*" The name came clearer this time, threaded with wonder. Then, faintly, terribly, amused: *you still shout like a fishwife.*

Odile laughed and sobbed in the same breath.

Wren tugged Zephyrine's sleeve. The boy pointed down the stair, then held up his fingers — thirteen, then twelve, then, slowly, deliberately, folded one finger down. Eleven.

Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "How long?"

Wren spread his hands: he didn't know. Hours, perhaps. The gesture said. Perhaps less.

And then the mountain shook again — not from within this time. From without. A sound rolled down from the pass above the shrine, a sound Zephyrine had hoped never to hear again and had heard twice in the last month: the baying of witch-finder horns, iron-mouthed instruments designed to make magic flinch and reveal itself.

Odile was at the arrow-slit window in an instant. "Torches on the pass road. A dozen at least. And—" she went very still, "—Zeph. Look at the sky."

He looked. The thirteenth moon was rising early, swollen past reason, its crack now a jagged canyon of red light, and beneath it, on the far horizon, the air itself was rippling — a shimmer like heat-haze in winter, spreading outward from a point he couldn't see but knew in his bones.

The far side of the gate. The exiles, gathering at the other face of the same door.

"Three armies," Odile said softly. "The iron-men above us. The exiles beyond the wall. And us." She turned, oath-stone in her fist, firelight and moonlight fighting across her face. "Zephyrine Sandoval, I have waited twelve years to say this, so listen well: whatever you're planning to do alone — don't."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You have the face. You've had the face since the shrine." She stepped close, and her voice dropped to the register she'd used on him when they were young and stupid and invincible together. "Whatever happens tonight, we do it together, or we don't do it at all. Swear it."

Above them the horns sounded again, closer. Below them Merrin's voice rose one final time through the stone, faint as breath on a winter window:

*they're coming... zephyrine... everyone is coming... and I am... I am so tired...*

Wren took Zephyrine's hand. His grip was small and fierce, and when Zephyrine looked down, the boy was staring at the stair into the dark with an expression Zephyrine recognized with horror — because he had worn it himself, twelve years ago, the night he had decided that he alone would pay the price and spare the others.

"No," Zephyrine said to him, though the boy had said nothing. "No, Wren. Whatever you're thinking — *no*."

But the boy only squeezed his hand tighter, and began, silently, to walk down the stairs toward the gate, and the thirteenth moon climbed higher, cracked and red, over three converging armies of want.
