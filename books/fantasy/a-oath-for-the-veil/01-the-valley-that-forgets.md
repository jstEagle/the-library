## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley of Hollowmere began where the world's magic ended, and everyone said it like a blessing.

*Magic cannot follow you here,* the grandmothers said, pressing the words into children like coins into clay. *Here you are safe. Here you are only yourself.* They spoke of the valley's borders as a kindness — the mist that pooled in the mountain passes, the strange stillness that settled over travelers at the ridge line, the way charms went dull and wards went blind and blessings curdled into ordinary air. Pilgrims came sometimes, hollow-eyed with grief or guilt, to be emptied. To stand in Hollowmere was to be no one special, and for most people, Noor supposed, that was a mercy.

Noor Thackeray had spent twenty-three years learning that it could also be a sentence.

They knelt in the straw of Maera's barn, one hand flat against the heaving flank of a ewe gone wrong with her lambing, and whispered.

*Easy. Easy now. Turn her, little mother. He's turned wrong inside you, but you're strong, stronger than you know. One more push. Give him the road.*

The ewe's dark eye rolled toward Noor — not the blank rolling of pain, but something attentive, something *listening* — and she strained, and the lamb came slithering into the lantern light, wet and furious and alive. The ewe sagged against Noor's shoulder with a groan that needed no translation.

"I know," Noor murmured, wiping their hands on the straw. "I know. You did beautifully." A pause, and then, because there was no one to hear: "Don't tell anyone I helped."

The ewe snorted, which Noor chose to take as agreement.

That was the shape of their life, folded small as a moth in a closed fist. By day, Noor Thackeray was Maera's apprentice at the healing house — competent, quiet, unremarkable, the sort of person whose name neighbors had to think twice before remembering. They ground willow bark and packed wounds and knew when a fever would break and when it wouldn't, and if they were better than most at reading what ailed a body, well, that was craft, not sorcery. Anyone might learn it.

By night — by the gray hours before dawn, by stolen minutes in paddocks and coops and stables — Noor was something else entirely. Something that had no name in Hollowmere except the one written in old law books: *beast-speaker*. The gift ran in their blood like a river under ice. Their grandmother had carried it before them, all the way to the grave, and had never once told them what it cost her to carry it here, where even a whisper of it meant pyres.

Because that was the other thing about the valley's blessing, the part the grandmothers' saying left out. Magic could not follow you into Hollowmere. But if magic was already *in* you — born in you, rooted in you like a second heartbeat — then the valley did not drive it out. It only made sure no one ever saw it. The mists kept watchers out; the stillness kept secrets in. And the law of the Church of the Silent Ledger, enforced these hundred years by nothing more than habit and fear, held that any soul found speaking with beasts was a soul owed to the fire.

So Noor hid. It was the first thing they had ever learned and the last thing they were likely to unlearn. They hid the way the valley itself hid: thoroughly, patiently, without hope of being found.

Most nights, that was enough. Most nights, Noor could almost believe the hiding was a life.

* * *

Maera found them at dawn on the step outside the healing house, two cups of barley tea in hand, her gray braid coming loose from its pin the way it always did by the end of a long night.

"The ewe?" she asked.

"Lamb's breathing. She'll keep." Noor took the cup. Their hands were still trembling faintly, though whether from the birth or the whispering, they couldn't have said.

Maera lowered herself onto the step with the careful economy of a woman whose knees had opinions, and looked out over the village waking below — smoke rising from chimneys, the mill wheel turning its slow brown circle, geese arguing their eternal grievances in the common. Four orphan children slept upstairs behind them, worn out from being woken to see the lamb. Maera had taken in every stray Hollowmere produced, and the valley had produced a great many strays.

"You look like a woman deciding something," Maera said.

"I'm always deciding something."

"No. Tonight you look like you've decided." She sipped her tea. "I've known you since you were six years old and bit the tanner's son for drowning kittens. Whatever it is, you'll do it badly and then tell me after."

Noor laughed despite themselves, and the laugh cracked something open, just slightly. "Do you ever want more than this?"

Maera didn't answer right away. Below, the baker's boy was chasing his dog through the wheat stubble, both of them making the same sound at different pitches.

"Wanting more is how people end up burned," she said at last, gently, the way she said everything that mattered. "Or worse — how they end up leaving. And then who packs my comfrey?"

It was an old joke between them, and Noor let it close the conversation, because that was also a skill the valley taught: how to fold a longing up small enough to fit back inside your chest. But walking the morning rounds afterward — lancing a boil on the cooper's thumb, listening to the miller's wife describe her dreams at length — Noor felt the folded thing shifting, restless, like a bird turning in a too-small box.

Because the truth was that Noor wanted *more* the way a drowned man wants air: constantly, quietly, without any expectation of getting it. They wanted a world where the ewe's gratitude wasn't a crime. Where the wolves they'd spoken with at the tree line hadn't flinched from them like something cursed. Where they could say the word *yes, I hear them* out loud, once, in daylight, and not die for it.

They wanted, most shamefully of all, to be *found*.

Be careful what you long for. The valley forgets many things, but it does not forget to punish wanting.

* * *

The crow came at dusk, three days later.

Noor was closing the coop when it dropped out of the sky like a thrown stone — not landing so much as *crashing*, wings askew, striking the frozen mud beside the fence with a sound Noor felt in their teeth. They crouched over it before thinking, hands cupped around its body, and the crow's eye found theirs, and Noor's whole body went cold.

Because the eye was *looking* at them. Not the flat black indifference of a bird, not even the bright animal attention Noor knew so well. This was recognition. This was a creature that had been sent.

*Speaker.* The voice in Noor's head was thin as wire, fraying with every word, and it came in the old tongue — the deep speech of ravens and river-things, the language Noor had never been taught and had never needed to be, the one that lived in the blood alongside the gift. *Speaker, I flew past the mists. Do you understand? Past the mists. Nothing stopped me. Nothing—*

A shudder went through the small body. Blood was seeping from its feathers in places feathers should not bleed, as if something inside had been torn on the way through.

*I carried a message. I was told: find the bloodline. Find the oath-keeper. I don't know what an oath-keeper is, speaker. I only know the name I was given to carry.*

"Don't talk," Noor whispered, and hated how useless it sounded. "Save your strength. Who sent you? What name?"

The crow's beak opened. What came out was not a word in any mortal mouth — it was a *name*, a god's name, spoken in a tongue dead a thousand years, and hearing it was like hearing a bell rung inside your own skull. The syllables went through Noor and kept going, out into the darkening valley, and somewhere far off a dog began to howl, and then another, as if the name had passed through them too.

Then the light went out of the crow's eye, and the name hung alone in the cold air, unfinished, waiting.

Noor knelt in the mud holding a dead messenger, and understood, with the clarity of ice water, two things at once.

First: the valley's protection was failing. The mists had let this creature through wounded and dying, and the name had traveled where no name should travel. The walls around their whole hidden life were coming down grain by grain, and Noor could feel the draft already.

Second — and this was the shameful thing, the thing they would carry like a stone — beneath the dread, beneath the grief for the small broken body in their hands, some starved and secret part of them was *singing*.

*More*, said the singing part. *At last. More.*

Noor buried the crow under the rowan tree by the coop, digging through frost with bare hands, and laid a sprig of Maera's dried lavender on the little mound because it seemed owed. Then they stood in the dark, looking north toward the passes where the mists lived, toward the river that climbed out of the valley and into the wide world where prayers were being answered again — they'd heard the rumors, everyone had, hushed and hungry: the old gods waking beyond the hills, shrines warming, wells granting wishes.

Something had sent a dying bird through the failing wall of their sanctuary, carrying a god's name and a title Noor had never asked for.

*Oath-keeper.*

"No," Noor said aloud, to the night, to the name, to whatever was listening. "I'm a healer's apprentice. I bury crows. That's all."

The wind moved through the rowan branches, and far away, upstream, something answered — not in words, but in the low, patient way a river answers a stone that has just begun to move.

Noor gathered their cloak around themselves and walked back to the healing house, and did not sleep, and in the morning told Maera nothing at all — which was, as she had predicted, doing it badly.

But that night, and every night after, they left the coop door unlatched, and stood a while in the cold, watching the northern sky.
