## 3. The Tithe of Small Gods

Vess had been a river once.

Not for long — a decade, perhaps two of mortal reckoning, back when the world still had room in it for gods who wished to be water — but the habit of it had never left. Rivers did not run uphill. Rivers did not argue with their banks. And rivers, above all things, knew when they were being followed, because everything that fell into them was carried along at the same speed, and hunters were never content to be carried.

These ones were dragging.

Vess felt them the way a river feels a dropped anchor: a stutter in the current of the world, three miles downstream and closing. Men with iron shoes and dogs whose thoughts were simple and loud — *smell, chase, please, master* — and behind them something worse, something that thought in ledger-lines and cold arithmetic. A censor-priest, or near enough. The Church of the Silent Ledger did not send its censors out for small debts.

It sent them for this.

"Keep to the stones," Vess said, not turning around. "The wet ones. Your feet make less noise on wet stone, and the dogs will lose you at the ford."

Behind, Noor Thackeray picked their way along the shingle with the expression of someone who had been given far too many answers before breakfast. They had the crow riding their shoulder like a black judgment, and the crow's name — Vess had finally pried it loose — was Sorrow-of-the-Ninth-Winter, which was either an old god's courtesy or a very dramatic bird.

"You said," Noor called up the bank, low and furious, "that we'd be safe past the boundary stones."

"I said the valley's protection was failing." Vess paused on a flat rock, testing it, feeling the water speak beneath. "I never said anything about safety. Safety is a story mortals tell each other so they can sleep. I deal in debts."

"So you've mentioned." Noor scrambled up beside them, breath ragged, and glared. "You haven't actually explained any of it. You washed up on my river half-dead, called me an oath-keeper, and now men with dogs are hunting us through my own hills, and the sum total of your explanation is *debts*. If I'm going to die out here, godling, I'd like to know what for."

There it was again — that voice. Vess had heard ten thousand mortal voices plead and bargain and curse, and none of them sounded like that. None of them made the air sit up and listen. The covenant had chosen its keeper well, or cruelly; with the old gods, those had always been the same thing.

"All right," Vess said. "Walk. I'll talk. And if I stop talking, run."

They ran.

* * *

The truth of it was older than Vess, and Vess was old enough to remember when the mountains had names that hurt to say.

"Mortals think the gods went silent out of spite," Vess said, leaping a fallen birch without breaking stride. "That we abandoned you. That some war or wandering took us away and left the world dark. It's a comfortable story. Stories about being abandoned always are — they require nothing of the teller."

"And the truth?"

"The truth is a contract clause." Vess bared teeth that were not quite a smile. "Every prayer ever answered cost something. Not blood, not gold — nothing so crude. Memory. That was the tithe. For every miracle, the one who prayed gave up a piece of remembering. The widow whose drowned son came back to her forgot his face. The farmer whose blight lifted forgot the taste of bread from the year before. Small payments, mostly. The gods took only what was owed, and the covenant recorded every entry, because even gods must keep honest books."

Noor had stopped running. Stopped dead, in fact, standing among the ferns with the color gone out of their face. "You *ate* memories."

"We *collected* them. There's a difference." Though Vess had watched enough faces to know how little comfort there was in the distinction. "Where do you think all those forgotten things went? They went into the ledger. Every memory ever tithed, every debt ever forgiven or defaulted, every promise a god ever made and broke — written down, sealed, kept. The covenant isn't a book, Noor. It's the account of everything the divine world owes yours. And everything yours owes ours."

"The valley," Noor said slowly. "Hollowmere. Where magic doesn't work. Where prayers go unanswered. My grandmother came from outside — she never spoke of where. She built wards nobody could see and told me never to leave, never to—"

"—never to be found by anything that could smell what she'd hidden in your blood." Vess gentled, as much as a godling could gentle. "She wasn't hiding *you*, oath-keeper. She was hiding the key. Someone in her line was meant to carry the covenant's voice, and she decided no one would carry it at all. She stole you into a place where even the gods' eyes slid off. Clever woman. Cowardly. Both can be true."

The crow shifted on Noor's shoulder and said nothing, which from a bird named Sorrow-of-the-Ninth-Winter was practically a eulogy.

Downstream, faint as a heartbeat, the anchor-drag of the hunters stuttered closer. Vess turned and walked, and this time Noor followed without being asked.

* * *

They made the ridge by late afternoon, where the land folded into a hollow full of old stones — standing stones, weathered smooth, carved with script that predated the Church by a thousand years. Vess sank down against the largest of them and let the wound in their side have its say. Mortal form was inconvenient that way: pain arrived with opinions.

"Now," Noor said, crouching across from them, "you'll tell me why the prayers started again. Why now. And why everyone suddenly wants whatever it is I'm carrying."

"Because the tithe stopped being paid." Vess closed their eyes. "Three generations ago, give or take. The Church of the Silent Ledger rose and taught a beautiful doctrine: that memory is sacred, that forgetting is theft, that no one should pay the gods another copper of themselves. And they were *right*, Noor. That's the filthy joke of it. The tithe was cruel. We were cruel. Whole villages forgot their dead so thoroughly they walked past their own mothers' graves. So the people stopped praying, and the gods, starved of what fed them, went quiet — and the Church wrote itself into history as the faith that slew the gods without spilling a drop of blood."

"But the prayers are answered again. I've seen it. Past the valley — rain where priests prayed for rain, fevers breaking—"

"Because someone found a loophole." Vess opened their eyes and looked at the boy — no, not a boy, a healer, an heir, a loaded weapon wearing a person's face — and felt the bitter humor curdle into the fear it had always been. "A prayer answered without payment is a debt unpaid. And every unanswered debt goes into the ledger. Do you understand? Every miracle the Church has been quietly engineering, every 'saint's blessing,' every answered prayer they've performed while preaching that the gods are dead — it's all been written down. Somewhere. In something. Waiting for a voice authorized to read it aloud."

The wind moved through the standing stones, and the old carvings seemed almost to breathe.

"The covenant," Noor whispered.

"The covenant. The ledger of all divine debts — theirs and ours, every forgery included. And yes, *forgery*. The Church didn't just preach against the tithe. Their founders altered the covenant itself, struck entries, added others, made gods owe what they never owed and erased what mortals were owed in turn. If the true text were read aloud — every line, every crime, every forged signature — it wouldn't just embarrass the Church. It would unmake their authority from the first stone. And it would hand the old gods a bill of particulars against mankind long enough to justify burning the world twice over."

Noor sat down slowly in the bracken, as if their legs had filed a formal complaint. "So the Church wants to destroy it. Or bury it deeper."

"The Church wants to *control* it. There are factions. There are always factions — the censors who want it burned, the archivists who want it corrected, the ones like Serah Ives who—" Vess stopped. Some names were better saved. "And the gods want it too. Odran most of all. He wants the debts read aloud so he can collect. All of them. At once. With interest compounded over three centuries of silence."

"And me? What am I, exactly, besides bait?"

"You're the voice. The covenant doesn't answer to gods anymore — too many of us cheated. It doesn't answer to the Church — they forged it. It answers to the bloodline that was sworn to keep it honest. Yours." Vess leaned forward, and let the god show through the mortal face for just a moment, the way light shows through deep water. "You could end the oldest war in the world with a sentence, Noor Thackeray. Or start its last chapter. That's what you're carrying. That's what the dogs are smelling."

For a long moment the healer said nothing. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of everything: "You should have left me in the valley."

"Yes," Vess said honestly. "I considered it. But the hunters were already coming for Hollowmere, oath-keeper. The Church has known about your grandmother's line for years. They were simply waiting until they could take the valley without anyone noticing a few missing healers." A pause. "I didn't wash ashore by accident, either. I was sent. By whom, I'm not yet certain — and that frightens me more than the dogs do."

Noor looked at them then, really looked, and Vess saw the moment the dread in the mortal's face hardened into something with edges. Good. Dread was useless. Edges were currency.

Downstream, the dogs had found the ford.

And far off, beyond the ridgeline, toward the valley Noor still called home, a smudge of smoke rose against the evening sky — thin, deliberate, and far too orderly to be an accident.

"They're already there," Noor said.

Vess got to their feet, ribs screaming, and offered the only honesty left. "Then we'd best decide what kind of debtors we're going to be."
