## 4. Bones for Bread

The valley had changed in three days, and Noor felt it before they saw it.

It began at the tree line, where the beast-paths thinned. The hares that usually scattered from Noor's boots stood their ground instead, ears flat, eyes too bright. A doe watched them pass with the fixed attention of a creature waiting for orders. And beneath everything — beneath the wind and the water and the small rustling business of the woods — there was a hum now, faint as a struck tuning fork buried in the earth. Magic, leaking into Hollowmere like damp through a foundation.

*Three days,* Vess had said, curled in the crook of a fallen log like a child in wet clothes. *Three days since the first prayer answered past your borders. It will spread. It always spreads.* The godling's voice had been thin with pain and thinner with certainty, and Noor had believed them, because Noor had spent a lifetime feeling exactly this — the wrongness of a place that refused to listen to anything living.

But believing it was different from walking home to find it true.

Noor crested the ridge above Hollowmere at dusk, and stopped so suddenly that the crow on their shoulder — the same impossible crow, riding along like a debt collector — dug its talons in with a sound like a key turning.

Below, in the square where Maera sold her tinctures and the children played knucklebones against the well's stone lip, stood a wagon. Not just any wagon: a white one, canopied in white cloth, drawn by two white oxen whose coats gleamed like fresh milk. The Church of the Silent Ledger did not believe in color. Color was vanity; vanity was debt; debt was theirs to collect.

And around the wagon, orderly as beads on a string, stood half of Hollowmere.

Noor went cold from the scalp down. They knew every face in that gathering. There was Tam Broadfoot, who mended shoes and cheated at cards and once, when Noor was nine, had carried them four miles home with a broken ankle without asking a single question about why a child had been alone on the fells talking to sheep. There was Widow Cress, who paid for her cough syrup in eggs and gossip. There were the Hollis brothers, who between them owned most of the valley's grain and none of its kindness.

Every one of them faced the wagon. Every one of them held a small white slip of paper.

*Amnesty,* Vess had warned, back in the dark by the river, with the hunters' torches still guttering downstream. *That is how they hunt in settled country. Not with fire first. With paper. They make confession cheaper than silence.*

Noor had thought, then, that Hollowmere would be different. That was the whole point of Hollowmere. The valley forgot; the valley kept no ledgers of its own; the valley let a beast-speaker live thirty years unburned because nobody thought to look. Noor had built their whole life inside that forgetting — the healer's apprentice by day, hands busy with poultices and pulse-counts, and by night the whisperer, kneeling in barns, easing a foaling mare's terror with words no human ear could catch.

They had thought the valley's blindness was a wall.

Standing on the ridge, watching their neighbors hold out their hands for grain, Noor understood it had been a cage all along — and someone had finally brought the key.

* * *

Getting down into the village unseen was easy. Getting to Maera's cottage unseen was harder, because the crowd had spilled toward it, drawn like iron filings to the white wagon, and Noor had to move through back gardens with their shoulders up around their ears, praying to gods they'd never trusted that no dog would announce them. The dogs didn't. That was the worst part, almost — the dogs looked at Noor as they passed and said nothing, and Noor could feel their attention like warm breath, patient, waiting to be asked something.

Inside the cottage, the shutters were closed and the air smelled of feverfew and scorched linen. Maera sat at the table with her back very straight, which was how she sat when she was frightened, because Maera had been frightened her whole life and had long ago decided fear was a private matter. Three of the children were with her — Pip and Sable and little Wren, pressed together on the bench like kittens in a basket. The other four, the older ones, were not there.

"Where are they?" Noor whispered.

Maera's eyes flicked to the shuttered window. "In line," she said.

The word landed in Noor's stomach like a swallowed stone. "Maera—"

"They're not confessing." Her voice was low and fierce and shaking only slightly, the way a rope shakes under weight. "I told them to take the slips and say nothing. Paper isn't a sin, Noor. Paper is what you do when the wolf is at the door and you can't fight it — you feed it and you count your children after."

"And if the wolf asks them questions?"

"Then they'll lie," Maera said, "because I raised them to lie beautifully."

Noor wanted to laugh. What came out instead was closer to a sob, bitten off fast, because outside, a voice had risen — the smooth, oiled voice of a man trained to be heard in churches.

"—and the Ledger is merciful, friends. The Ledger does not want your suffering. It wants only balance. Confess what you have harbored, knowingly or unknowingly, and receive amnesty, bread, and protection. Harbor it still, and when it is found — and it *will* be found — the debt passes to you entire."

Through the gap in the shutters, Noor saw him: a lean man in gray-white vestments, ink-stained fingers, a face like a well-dressed shovel. Beside the wagon, clerks were writing names in a ledger whose pages caught the last light and threw it back strangely, too white, like bone.

Tam Broadfoot stepped forward. Noor's heart lifted for one stupid, reflexive second — *Tam will tell them nothing, Tam carried me home* — and then Tam said, clear across the square:

"The Tarrow woman. Old Bess Tarrow, up past the mill. My brother saw her talking to her geese, and the geese *answered*. Formation flying, mind, like soldiers. Been going on years."

The clerk wrote it down. Somewhere in the line, a woman made a small sound, quickly smothered.

Widow Cress went next. "The Fenner boy talks to rats. Everyone knows it. His mother pays him in bread crusts to keep the granary clean, but it's not cleaning, it's *conversing*."

The Fenner boy was eleven. He was fourth in line, holding his white slip, waiting to confess to something he had never once thought of as a crime.

Noor's hands found the windowsill and gripped it until the wood creaked. This was the trap, then. Not the fire — the fire was for later, for the ones who didn't come in from the cold. The trap was this: make mercy lawful, and watch neighbors weigh each other against a sack of grain. Nobody in Hollowmere was wicked. They were *hungry*, and winter was coming, and the Church had counted on exactly that, because the Church had always understood people better than people understood themselves.

"I have to go out there," Noor breathed.

"You will do no such thing." Maera's hand closed on their wrist, herbalist-strong, callused from mortar and pestle. "You walk out that door and open your mouth, and everything I've spent thirty years burying comes up out of the ground with you. You think I don't know? You think I took in a foundling who hummed to the goats and never wondered?" Her grip tightened. "I knew, Noor. I've always known. That's my confession, and I'm making it here, where it costs nothing but truth between us."

Noor stared at her. Thirty years. Thirty years of perfecting the hiding, and the hiding had never hidden anything at all.

"Why?" was all they could manage.

"Because you were a child who talked to animals, and animals loved you, and I decided that was worth more than safe." Maera's eyes were wet and furious. "So don't you dare spend that now on a wagon full of ink and flour. You get those children out the back way, and you keep them out, and you let me handle the square."

"Handle it how?"

Maera smiled, and it was the smile she wore over a dying patient's bed — gentle, hopeless, absolutely resolved. "I'm an old woman with a shop full of medicine and no family name worth taking. I'm worth more to them confessed than burned. I'll buy you the night."

"No—"

"*Go.*"

Outside, the clerk called a name, and it was one of the older children — Jory, sixteen, steady-handed, the one who helped shoe the ponies. Noor heard his voice carry across the square, pitched carefully bored: "Nothing to confess. Just here for the bread."

A pause. Then the smooth voice again, pleasant as oil: "Of course. Though our records note your household shelters seven orphans under one roof, and no registered guardian. Irregular. We'll need to speak with the herbalist."

Noor was already moving — Wren hoisted onto their hip, Sable and Pip herded ahead, Maera pressing a bundle of dried herbs and a knife into their arms with the brisk violence of a woman packing a lifetime into ten seconds.

"Beast-paths," she murmured against Noor's ear. "Whatever that crow of yours knows, use it. And Noor—" She pulled back, and for one moment her composure cracked clean through, showing the frightened girl underneath the iron old woman. "Come back for me. Don't be noble. Be *late*, be slow, be careful — but come back."

The door opened onto the square's noise, and Maera walked out into it with her spine straight and her chin high, an old woman going to sell herself dear, while Noor slipped out the back with three children and a heartbeat like a war drum.

Behind them, the clerk's pen scratched. Ahead, the dark hills waited, full of listening things.

And on Noor's shoulder, the crow bent its head and spoke in a dead tongue — one word, soft as ash falling.

Vess's name.
