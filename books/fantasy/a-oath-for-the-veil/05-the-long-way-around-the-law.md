## 5. The Long Way Around the Law

Noor had never thought of the valley as having a back door. It had always been a tomb with good lighting — walls of stone on three sides, the river cutting the fourth, and every path in or out watched by someone who would sell your name for a sack of flour. But beasts did not use paths. Beasts used *wants*, and wants went everywhere.

The deer-trail behind Maera's smokehouse was the first door. It climbed through bracken so thick it swallowed sound, and Noor walked it at dusk with six children strung out behind them like beads on a fraying cord, each one carrying what they could: a bundle of dried yarrow, a doll with one button eye, a knife too big for the hand that gripped it. Maera came last, her herb-basket repurposed into a pack, her mouth set in the flat line she wore when she had decided to be furious later, at a more convenient hour.

"Tell me again why we're trusting the deer," she whispered.

"I'm not trusting the deer. I'm trusting that the deer are afraid of exactly the same things we are." Noor pressed a palm to the cold earth and let the gift unfurl, quiet as breath. The valley's deadness pressed back against it like a hand over a mouth — it always had — but here at the edge, where the stone grew thin, something answered. A doe, two ridges up, moving fast. Something behind her moving faster.

"They've started already," Noor said. "Dogs. Four of them, maybe five."

Vess, walking in the middle of the column in a borrowed coat three sizes too large, looked less like a god than a sick child playing dress-up. Their mortal body was still wrong in small ways — joints that bent a half-degree too far, eyes that caught lamplight like a cat's when they forgot to make them not. "I can carry two of them," Vess said. "If we run."

"You can barely carry yourself."

"I carried a river once."

"And look how that ended." Noor hoisted the smallest child, Tam, onto their hip. He was feverish from the walk and smelled of woodsmoke and fear. The Church had come to Hollowmere with grain wagons and a pulpit on wheels, and by the second day the neighbors who had eaten Maera's bread were lining up to confess their neighbors' sins. Old Hobb had pointed at Maera's garden and said the word *witch* like he was spitting out a seed. Noor had stood in the crowd and said nothing, and the nothing had weighed more than any word ever had.

"We go down to the water," Noor said. "There's a spirit under the mill-race. I owe it a favor."

"What kind of favor?" Maera asked.

"The kind you don't ask about until it's paid."

* * *

Vess hated rivers now. That was the joke no one laughed at — that a godling of the deep water had nearly drowned in one, washed up on a mudbank like a landed fish, stripped of everything but a name and a debt. Walking beside the Hollowmere current, Vess felt the old pull of it, the way a burned hand feels fire across the room.

The children were loud in the way only frightened children are loud: silently, constantly, a shrieking under the skin. Vess had forgotten how much noise mortals made just by being alive. Hearts hammering. Blood rushing. Little lungs working like bellows. Gods heard prayers; they were not built for proximity.

Noor walked ahead, and Vess watched them work. It was an education. At the ford below the ruined mill, Noor crouched at the bank and spoke — not words, exactly, but the shape of words, a grammar of pressure and pitch that made the water itself lean in to listen. Vess felt the spirit rise before it showed itself: a long cold attention uncoiling beneath the surface, older than the mill, older than the village, the kind of minor divinity the Ledger had never bothered to record because no one had ever prayed to it twice.

"It says the ford is watched," Noor translated. "Two men with lanterns, upstream of the crossing. It will hide us if—"

"If?"

Noor's jaw tightened. "It wants my voice. A full night of it. Whatever I say to it, it keeps."

"That's theft," Vess said sharply. "That's your *gift*, Noor. You can't hand pieces of it away like buttons."

"No," Noor agreed, standing, brushing mud from their knees. "But I can spend it. There's a difference, and I'm learning it." They looked back at the children, at Maera, at the glow of lantern-light beginning to stain the clouds upstream. "Everything costs. The Church taught this valley that better than any god ever did. At least the river pays fair."

Vess wanted to argue. Instead Vess knelt at the water's edge and added something of their own — a thread of true divinity, thin as spider-silk, spun into the bargain. The spirit shivered through the whole reach of the stream, delighted, greedy, and the water went mirror-still and dark as poured ink.

They crossed in silence, six children and two liars and one old woman, and the lantern-men upstream saw nothing but night.

* * *

The wolf met them at the treeline above the high pasture, and she was not pleased to see them.

Her name — the name she allowed — was Greymuzzle, and she was old the way mountains are old, gray to the shoulders, one ear torn to lace, and she regarded Noor with the flat yellow patience of a creature who has outlived every promise ever made to her.

*"You,"* she said, in the speech that lived under speech. *"The whisperer. You want the pass through my country."*

"I do."

*"Last time you came to me, you asked about a lame ewe. I told you. You paid me a haunch of mutton. Fair trade. This time you bring—" * her nose wrinkled toward the children, toward Vess — *"—small screaming things and something that smells like lightning in a skin bag. This is not a ewe, whisperer."*

"No. It's seven lives, and dogs behind us, and men with fire ahead of us."

Greymuzzle sat. The whole pack — nine shadows in the bracken — sat with her, a parliament of eyes. *"Everything is lives, with your kind. Everything is fire. I am old. My hips ache. Give me a reason that isn't a tragedy."*

Noor opened their mouth to plead, and Vess watched them stop — watched the moment land, visible as a swallow. *Loyalty is a currency spent best deliberately.* They hadn't learned it yet, but they were learning it now, here, in front of a wolf who could smell desperation like blood in water.

"A reason," Noor said slowly. "All right. Not a tragedy. A trade. Winter's coming. The valley's going to war, and war means carcasses — livestock abandoned, deer pushed out of the deep woods by soldiers, gut-piles nobody claims. Your pack eats fat while every other pack in these hills starves. And I'll throw in the mutton besides. But you guide us true, and you stand between us and the dogs if they close the gap."

Greymuzzle's tail moved once, a slow metronome. *"You bargain like a wolf."*

"I was raised by worse."

The old wolf laughed — a sound like gravel pouring — and rose. *"Then run, little trader. Run well. My country is steep, and I have no patience for crying."*

* * *

They ran all night. By the grey hour before dawn they had put the mill-ford and the high pasture behind them and come down into the gully country, where the hills folded around streams like cupped hands and the beast-paths braided together into something no mapmaker had ever survived to chart. The children slept in shifts, slung between adults' backs, and Maera walked with her chin up and her hands steady and only Noor knew that her knees had been shaking since the first mile.

Vess brought up the rear and listened to the world behind them.

Gods hear prayers. That was the truth of Vess's nature, even diminished, even half-drowned and mortal-wrapped: prayer was a sound like a struck bell, and it carried. All night, from the direction of Hollowmere, the bells had been ringing — not church bells, but the raw, ragged bell of human need. Farmers praying for rain. Mothers praying their sons wouldn't inform. One voice, over and over, praying for the death of a neighbor whose name Vess now knew because the prayer named it forty times.

And underneath it, fainter, stranger: a single prayer aimed like an arrow. Not at any god. At *them*. At the oath-keeper. A woman's voice, disciplined and dry as ledger-paper, saying: *Show me where you cross the water, and I will let the children live.*

"She's found us," Vess said quietly, at the next halt. "The inquisitor. Serah Ives. She's not searching. She's *praying*. She's using prayer as a hound."

Noor went very still. "Can she find us that way?"

"She could. If I answered her." Vess flexed their too-flexible fingers. "Every prayer wants answering, Noor. That's the trap of being what I am. Answer one, and you belong to the answerer."

"So don't answer."

"It's not that simple. Silence is also an answer. Silence tells her we're afraid."

Maera, wrapping Tam in her own cloak, snorted without looking up. "Are you afraid?"

"Yes," said Vess, and then, because gods lie beautifully and this was no time for beauty: "Terrified. She's the most dangerous kind of believer. The kind who reads."

* * *

Serah Ives caught them at noon, in the narrow place where the gully pinched between two stone ribs and there was nowhere left to be except exactly where they were.

She came alone, which was somehow worse than a squad. Grey riding coat, plain boots, a sword worn like a tool rather than an ornament, and a face that might have been carved for a coin — honest, severe, forgettable, which was its own kind of dishonesty. She stepped into the gully's throat and stopped, and her eyes went past Noor entirely, past the children, past Maera, and fixed on Vess with the particular hunger of a scholar finding a page everyone else had torn out.

"Oath-keeper," she said to Noor. Her voice was level. Almost kind. "You have seven civilians, a wounded godling, and perhaps four miles of start. Behind me are thirty men who burn villages for a living. Ahead of you is the Karveth waste, which kills the unprepared. I want you to understand your position precisely before we speak."

"I understand it," Noor said. Their heart was a fist against their ribs. Beside them, Vess had gone taut as a drawn bow, and Noor could feel the godling gathering whatever remained of their divinity for one blinding, useless gesture.

"Good." Serah Ives looked at the children — really looked, cataloguing fevers and blisters and the button-eyed doll clutched in a small fist — and something crossed her face that was gone before it could be named. Then she stepped backward, out of the pinch of the gully, and turned sideways, clearing the path.

"Go," she said.

Noor did not move. "Why?"

For a long moment the inquisitor said nothing. Wind moved the bracken. Somewhere above, a crow called once — the same three notes, Noor realized with a chill, as the dying bird that had started all of this.

"Because I've read the founding archives of my own order," Serah Ives said at last, quietly, "and I know what's written in the covenant you're carrying. And because when the time comes, I will need you alive, and free, and owing me nothing — because debts between us should be *chosen*." She glanced once at Vess. "Keep him breathing. He's evidence."

Then she was gone, uphill and away, and the gully stood open like an unbarred gate.

Noor let out a breath they felt they'd been holding since the grain wagons came. Maera touched their elbow. "Do we trust her?"

"No," Noor said. And then, because the week had taught them at least that much: "But we *use* her. There's a difference, and I'm learning it."

They walked on toward Karveth, toward the conclave of ash and crow-feathers waiting at the end of the beast-paths — and behind them, faint as a struck bell, Noor could still feel the inquisitor's prayer hanging in the air, unanswered, patient, *kept* — like a debt entered in a ledger no one had yet agreed to open.
