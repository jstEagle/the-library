## 6. A Court of Crows and Ash

The road to Karveth was paved with the bones of a promise.

Vess felt every stone of it through the soles of the godling-shape they wore, that borrowed mortal flesh which ached and blistered and bled the way true flesh did. Once, they had crossed this road in a single stride, one foot falling in the age of bronze and the other in the age of iron, and the road had been white marble inlaid with gold prayers. Now the prayers were gone — scraped out by mortal chisels, or perhaps by shame — and the marble had gone grey and cracked, and wheat grew up through the seams as if the earth itself were trying to forget what had been built here.

Noor walked beside them, leading the mule. The beast-speaker had not spoken in three hours, which Vess had learned was not silence but weather; something gathering behind their eyes.

"You're afraid," Vess said. It was not a question. Godlings were poor at questions. They had spent too many millennia being answered.

"I'm a healer's apprentice from a valley where nothing magical has survived since my grandmother's grandmother," Noor said. "In an hour I'm going to stand in front of gods. Plural. Actual gods." A dry, unamused breath. "No. Fear would be simpler."

* * *

Karveth had been the first city of the covenant, raised in the years when gods and mortals still shook hands over treaties and meant them. Vess remembered its consecration the way mortals remembered their own weddings — hazily, with more fondness than accuracy. Now the temple-city stood open to the sky, its domes collapsed into bowls that caught the rain, its colonnades like broken ribs around a heart that had stopped beating centuries ago.

The conclave had already gathered by the time they climbed the great stair.

Vess felt them before they saw them: the pressure of old attention, the way the air thickened near divinity the way it thickened before a storm. In the ruined amphitheater at the city's center, half-sunk in ash and wildflowers, the survivors of the old pantheon had taken their seats.

There was Yara-of-the-Roots, who had once been a forest and was now a single stubborn oak growing through the floor of her own temple, her face in the bark like a memory of a face. There were the Twin Currents, river-gods who had merged into one body to survive the famine of prayer, arguing with themselves in two voices. There was a hearth-goddess no larger than a candle-flame who had once warmed ten thousand homes and now warmed only her own hands. And there, at the head of the broken circle, where the high altar had been —

Odran.

The storm-god had not diminished. That was the first thing Vess understood, and the understanding sat in their stomach like a swallowed stone. Where the other gods had shrunk with the shrinking of belief, Odran had grown *lean* — stripped down to lightning and grievance, all the soft weather burned away. He wore the sky like a cloak and the sky did not fit him well. Thunder muttered in the clouds above Karveth though the day had been clear at the valley's edge.

"Little Vess." Odran's voice came from everywhere, the way storms do. "The drowned whelp returns. And it brings —" his attention swung to Noor, and Vess saw the mortal flinch under the weight of it — "a thief."

"They bring the oath-keeper," Vess said, and was proud of how steady their borrowed voice came out.

"Thief," Odran repeated. "The covenant was kept by a bloodline sworn to the gods. Sworn to *us*. And that bloodline hid itself in a dead valley for four generations while our names were chiseled out and our altars plowed under and our faithful burned by the Ledger-men. That is not keeping. That is *hoarding*."

"The valley was built to hide the covenant," Vess said. "From exactly this."

"Then it has failed," said Odran, "because here we all are. And here is the covenant, wearing a mortal face and a healer's smock." He rose, and when the storm-god rose, the ruins rose with him — dust lifting in spirals, wildflowers bowing flat. "So let us do what should have been done a century ago. Let us open the ledger. Let us read the debts. And let us *collect*."

* * *

The conclave fractured the way old families fracture, along lines worn smooth by centuries of the same argument.

The Twin Currents wanted the covenant read aloud, all of it, every debt — but only so that the gods could finally know who owed them what, a reckoning of grief more than of punishment. Yara-of-the-Roots said nothing at all, which from a god was its own kind of scream. The hearth-flame wept, quietly, in the corner of her ruined shrine, and her tears smelled of woodsmoke.

And Odran wanted blood.

Vess stood at Noor's shoulder and listened to the storm-god lay it out with the terrible patience of a thing that had been rehearsing this speech to empty skies for a hundred years. Every god burned at the stake. Every shrine defaced. Every name struck from every record. The Church of the Silent Ledger had not merely forgotten the old gods; it had *executed* them, and called the executions peace.

"The covenant is a ledger of debts," Odran said. "Debts cut both ways. Let the mortals read what was done to us. Let them read it and *tremble*. A tithe of memory, yes — but a tithe of fear as well. Fear is the only coin they have never stopped minting." His lightning eyes found Noor. "You will open it, oath-keeper. You will read the debts of punishment, and we will collect them from every city that raised a pyre. Starting with the one that burned your own grandmother."

The silence after that was very long.

Vess watched Noor's hands. That was the trick of watching mortals, the thing gods forgot: not their faces, which lied, and not their words, which lied more beautifully, but their hands. Noor's hands were steady on the mule's lead, and then they were not, and then they were again — and Vess understood that the mortal had just fought a war inside themselves and won it, or lost it, and was not going to say which.

"No," Noor said.

The word was small. Mortal-sized. It should not have been able to stand in that place, in that silence, against a storm-god's wrath.

It stood anyway.

"No," Noor said again, louder, and this time Vess heard something under it — the same voice that gentled spooked horses and talked fever out of dying livestock, the voice that had never once in a lifetime of hiding been raised above a whisper. "I didn't ask for this. I didn't know your names a season ago. I have spent my whole life being told that what I am is a thing to burn for. And now you want me to use the only power I've ever been given to do the same to others. To make children tremble for what their great-grandfathers did." Noor's jaw tightened. "I've seen what that looks like from the ground, lord of storms. It looks like grain carts and amnesty and neighbors denouncing neighbors. It looks like *justice*, from up where you're standing. It looks like a pyre from everywhere else."

"You dare —" Odran began, and the clouds above Karveth split with light.

And the covenant *answered*.

Vess felt it happen — felt it the way one feels a bone set, sudden and right. The ledger was not a book. Vess had known that much, had tried to explain it to Noor on the road and failed, because there were no mortal words for a thing that was part vow and part law and part living memory. It lived in the oath-keeper's blood. And when the storm-god's power came down toward the mortal who refused him, the covenant rose up around Noor like a wall of unspoken words, and the lightning *broke* against it, sheeting off into the ruins, scorching a hundred years of moss off the amphitheater stones and leaving Noor untouched in a circle of clean white ash.

The gods went still. Even the Twin Currents stopped arguing with themselves.

"You see," Vess said softly, into the ringing silence, "it answers to their voice alone. I did try to tell you all. The bloodline chose its keeper. The covenant chose its terms. It was never a ledger you could seize." They looked at Odran, and let a little of what they truly were show through the mortal shape — the old, deep, patient thing beneath the drowned whelp. "It was a door. And she is the only one who can open it. Or not."

Odran looked at Noor for a long time. The storm-god's face was the sky's face, and the sky's face said many things — fury, hunger, and beneath them, so faint it might have been imagined, something that in a smaller creature might have been called relief. It is a terrible weight, being the only one willing to say what everyone is thinking. Even gods grew tired of it.

"Keep your refusal, then, thief," Odran said at last, and his voice was silk stretched over a blade. "But hear the arithmetic of it. Every week you keep that ledger sealed, the Ledger-men burn a village looking for it. They have begun already. You have seen their carts, their grain, their amnesty." He leaned close, and his breath was ozone. "Your silence has a body count, oath-keeper. Count it. And when the counting breaks you — and it will — Karveth will be waiting."

He withdrew into his clouds. The conclave broke apart like a fist unclenching, gods dissolving into their remnants — the oak, the river, the candle-flame — leaving only ash and wildflowers and the smell of scorched moss.

* * *

Noor did not speak until they were halfway down the great stair, the mule's hooves clicking on the bone-paved road.

"The village," Noor said. "The one he mentioned. He wasn't lying. He wanted me to think he was lying, so I'd doubt everything else, but he wasn't." Their hands had gone steady again, which was worse than trembling. Steady hands meant a decision had been made somewhere, and Noor did not yet know what it was. "Vess. How long does it take to burn a village?"

Vess looked at the mortal who carried the most dangerous truth in the world in their blood, and thought: *Odran is not the danger. He never was. The danger is that the keeper is kind, and kindness can be led by its own leash.*

"A day," Vess said. "Perhaps two, if the inquisitor in charge likes to read confessions first."

Noor turned and looked back up at Karveth, at the broken bowls of the domes full of rain and sky, at the ash circle where a mortal word had broken a god's lightning.

"Then we don't have much time," Noor said, "to decide how many people my silence is worth."

And far behind them, at the head of the broken stair, Vess saw a figure in the grey vestments of the Church of the Silent Ledger, standing very still among the wildflowers — watching them go, making no move to follow, and writing something in a small black book.

Vess knew that silhouette. They had let it escape once on the riverbank, and had told themselves it was mercy.

It was beginning to look like fate.
