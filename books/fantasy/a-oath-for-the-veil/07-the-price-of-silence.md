## 7. The Price of Silence

The storm-god did not sleep, and so neither did Karveth.

Noor lay on a pallet of temple wool in a chamber that had once belonged to some priest or priestess whose name the ash had eaten, listening to Odran's presence roll through the ruined city like weather that had learned to think. Thunder without clouds. Pressure in the teeth. The old gods had taken the great hall for their conclave and never quite left it; their arguments seeped through the broken walls the way damp seeped through stone, and Noor had learned in three days that divinity was mostly noise.

Vess slept curled at the foot of the pallet, small as a starved cat in mortal form, feathers gone dull along one arm where the hunters' iron had bitten deep. Even sleeping, the godling's fingers twitched — counting debts, maybe. Counting the ways this could still go wrong.

*All the ways,* Noor thought. *Every single one.*

They rose before dawn, because sleep had become a country they could no longer afford to visit, and walked out onto the temple terrace where the valley below Karveth unrolled like a scroll someone had dropped: fields, roads, woodsmoke, the tiny stubborn lights of villages that had no idea what was being weighed above them.

Odran was already there.

He wore his storm like a cloak these days, but when he wished to be seen he took the shape of an old man with river-grey eyes and hands too large for any human frame, hands that had once held lightning the way farmers held seed. He stood at the terrace's broken balustrade looking down at the world as if it were a ledger he was auditing.

"You don't sleep," he said, without turning.

"Neither do you."

"I am a god. Sleep is for creatures who can be surprised." He turned then, and his eyes were kind, which was the worst thing about him. Kindness from something that powerful felt like a hand closing gently around your throat. "Walk with me, oath-keeper. I have an offer, and I would rather make it honestly than let you hear it secondhand from the crows."

The crows, at least, Noor trusted. They had carried messages for them since Hollowmere; they asked only bread and gossip. But they went where Odran told them to go, same as anyone.

They walked. The terrace stones crumbled softly underfoot, and below them the morning mist was peeling off the fields, and somewhere a rooster was announcing a dawn that didn't know it might be its village's last.

"The Church of the Silent Ledger burned Tallow's Ford two nights ago," Odran said. "Forty-one souls. The inquisitors called it an amnesty gone wrong — a village that harbored a confessed witch who recanted her confession. There will be another in six days. Then another. One a week, until the covenant is surrendered to the Ledger's keeping." He said it the way a man reports rain. "I want you to understand that I am not threatening you. The Church does not need my permission to burn mortals. It has never needed anyone's permission. That is rather the point of institutions."

"And your offer?"

Odran stopped walking. When he spoke again, his voice had the low rolling quality of a sky deciding.

"Keep it sealed. Speak nothing. Let the ledger stay shut behind your voice, where it has been since your grandmother's grandmother swore it shut. In return, the gods — all of us who remain — will ward Hollowmere. Not for a season. Forever. Walls of storm and fog that no inquisitor's iron can cross. Your Maera will grow her herbs until she is old and bent and complaining. The children will grow up never knowing how close the fire came. And you—" the grey eyes softened, "—you may finally rest, Noor Thackeray. You have been carrying this since you were a child hiding a gift in a dead valley. Put it down. We will carry it. We are very good at carrying things. It is practically all we do now."

The offer hung there, beautiful and terrible, like fruit made of glass.

And beneath it, coiled and patient, the secret itself — the thing Noor had read in the covenant's first pages and understood immediately why every god in Karveth wanted it sealed, and why Serah Ives would burn her own order to publish it:

*The war between gods and mortals did not end. It was ended — by forgery. The Church's founders wrote themselves into the covenant as creditors, and the gods' oldest crimes were struck from the record by gods who preferred mercy to memory. Neither side ever agreed to peace. Both sides merely agreed to forget, and the forgetting was written in a dead tongue, in a book that answered to one bloodline.*

Noor's bloodline. Noor's voice. Noor's choice.

If the covenant was opened truly — every debt, every forgery, both sides' sins laid bare — the old war would not simply resume. It would *justify itself*. Every god would have proof of what was done to them; every mortal order would have proof of what the gods had done first. Odran knew this. That was why he wanted it sealed. That was why the gentlest god at the conclave was also the most desperate.

"You're asking me to buy forty-one lives a week with my silence," Noor said quietly.

"I am asking you to spend your silence *well*," Odran corrected. "There is a difference. Silence that saves a valley is not a sin, oath-keeper. I have watched mortals for ten thousand years, and I will tell you what I have learned: justice is a fire, and fires do not ask permission about whose house they warm and whose they eat. You hold a fire. The question is not whether it burns. The question is whether you aim it."

* * *

Maera found them at midday, in the herb garden the gods had let her plant among the ruins — because even a conclave of dying divinities apparently feared her scolding more than each other.

She was on her knees in the dirt with her sleeves rolled, thinning the row of feverfew, and she did not look up when Noor sat down beside her. That was Maera's way. She gave people time to say the hard thing by pretending, magnificently, not to notice them arriving.

"Tallow's Ford," Noor said.

"I heard." A weed came up, roots and all. She flicked it into the basket. "Forty-one."

"They'll do it again. Odran says one a week. He says if I keep the covenant sealed, Hollowmere is warded forever. Storm walls. Nothing gets in. The children grow up safe."

Maera's hands went still in the dirt.

For a long moment there was only the wind moving through the ruined colonnade, and the far-off argument of gods, and the small green smell of crushed feverfew rising between them.

"And the villages?" she asked at last. Her voice was very level, the way it got when she was holding something down with both hands. "The ones that aren't ours?"

"They burn."

"Yes." She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, thoroughly, as if the dirt were something she could wash off and the question wasn't. "And you'd live with that. Behind your storm walls. With Pip asking why you flinch when the bread comes out of the oven black, and little Sefi learning to braid hair, and every week, somewhere, forty-one someones." She finally looked at Noor, and her eyes were wet and furious and older than the ruins around them. "Don't you dare open it, Noor. Don't you *dare*."

"Maera—"

"No. You listen to me. I have buried children. Before you, before the others — I buried them, in the bad years, when the fever came through and there was nothing in my baskets but hope and boiled nettle. Do you know what I learned?" Her voice cracked and she hated it, Noor could see her hating it. "I learned that justice is a luxury of the safe. I learned that when you are drowning, you grab the rope that's nearest, and you don't ask whether the rope was stolen. Those children upstairs — Sefi, Pip, Tam, all nine of them — they are not pawns in your grand reckoning. They are *children*, and they are mine, and I will trade every secret in that cursed book for their whole boring ordinary lives, and I will never once lose sleep over it, and neither should you."

"Someone has to pay the debt," Noor said. "That's what a covenant *is*. If I stay silent, I'm not choosing safety. I'm choosing whose blood buys mine."

"And if you speak?" Maera laughed, short and bitter. "You shatter the thing, the war starts again, and the first field it burns is probably ours. You said it yourself, the night you came back from Karveth shaking. No one wins that war. Everyone just loses slower." She reached out and gripped Noor's wrist, hard, herbalist's fingers finding the pulse like she was checking whether Noor was still alive in there. "You are not the only person in the world, child. But you are the only person those children have. Stay quiet. Be their wall. Let the world outside solve its own arithmetic."

She released him and went back to the feverfew, hands trembling now that she thought no one could see.

Noor sat in the dirt beside her for a long time, saying nothing, doing the arithmetic she refused to do.

One a week. Fifty-two a year. How many years did a covenant last? How long before the silence outweighed the saving? And underneath it all, whispering like Vess in fever: *the truth belongs to everyone it was stolen from.* The gods who'd been edited. The mortals who'd been lied to. Forty generations of the forgotten, waiting in a dead tongue for one living voice.

* * *

Evening came down over Karveth like ash falling upward. Noor stood on the terrace again, alone this time, watching the lights of the next doomed village flicker on in the dark — six days away, five now, the hours draining out of the hourglass whether Noor moved or not.

Behind them, footsteps: light, uneven, favoring the wounded arm. Vess settled onto the balustrade beside them, small and sharp-eyed, and for a while neither spoke.

"He offered you the walls," Vess said at last. Not a question. Crows had ears everywhere, and Vess had been a crow longer than anything else.

"He offered me the walls."

"And Maera offered you her fury. And the children are asleep upstairs, dreaming about nothing." Vess picked at the bandage on their arm. "You know what I think? I think Odran made you a genuine offer. That's the horror of it. He meant every word. He would ward your valley forever and grieve honestly for every village, and call it wisdom, and history would call him merciful." The godling looked up, and their eyes caught the last light like oil catching flame. "But I was there when the covenant was forged, Noor. I was small even then, but I remember what was struck from the record. And I am telling you, as the only other soul alive who knows: silence doesn't keep that secret. Silence just decides *who* finds it. And whoever finds it unannounced will use it as a weapon, not a wound dressed in daylight."

Below, the village lights burned on, patient and unaware.

Five days.

Noor closed their eyes, and in the dark behind them, the covenant waited — vast, patient, written in a tongue only their blood could speak — and for the first time since the crow had fallen out of the sky into their life, Noor Thackeray heard a sound they could not name: the faint, unmistakable creak of a door that only they in all the world could open, beginning, very slowly, to swing.
