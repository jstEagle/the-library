## 8. Serah Ives, Believer

The road to Hollowmere was a wound in the world, and Serah Ives had been walking it for eleven days.

She had left her horse at the valley's edge, as the Church's own maps instructed — *no beast of burden passes the forgetting line* — and gone in on foot, boots sinking into a soil that seemed to resent her weight. Behind her, the world hummed with the old gods' returning voices: prayers answered, omens flaring, rivers running warm with something that might have been blessing or might have been appetite. Ahead of her, the valley lay silent as a held breath. Magic could not follow her here. Neither, she suspected, could forgiveness.

Her order had taught her that silence like this was holy. The Church of the Silent Ledger kept its founding creed stitched into every novice's memory: *What is written may be weighed; what is weighed may be judged; what is judged may be buried.* They were accountants of the soul, auditors of the divine. And Serah had been their best auditor for twenty years — until the day she opened the sealed archive beneath the Cathedral of Tally and found the first entry in the Ledger of Debts.

It was not a record of mortal sins against heaven.

It was a forgery.

She had sat with it for six hours before she let herself believe what she was reading. The covenant between gods and mortals — the great binding, the oath-structure that all doctrine claimed had been freely sworn at the dawn of the world — bore the ink-hand of a mortal scribe. Not any scribe. Her order's founder, Saint Aldous the Reconciler, whose gentle face watched from stained glass in every cathedral Serah had ever prayed in. He had not recorded the covenant. He had *written* it. Altered terms, struck clauses, inserted debts that had never been owed and erased ones that had. The war between gods and mortals had not ended in reconciliation. It had ended in fraud — and the Church had spent two thousand years guarding the fraud with fire.

Serah had reported it, because she was a believer, and believers believe the truth will be received with joy.

Her superiors had thanked her, promoted her, and given her a new assignment: track down the last oath-keeper before anyone else did, and ensure the covenant never surfaced. She understood the instruction perfectly. It was not an errand. It was a burial, and she was to be the gravedigger.

So here she was, walking into the valley that forgot, carrying her order's seal in one pocket and a copy of the forged first page hidden in the lining of her coat, over her heart, where she told herself it was evidence and not scripture.

* * *

Hollowmere received her the way a body receives a splinter — with inflammation.

The village was small, poor, and watchful. Word of the Church's arrival had spread ahead of her; grain wagons stood in the square, and a line of neighbors snaked toward the tithe-tent where her junior clerks offered amnesty in exchange for confession. Serah watched from the tavern's shadowed doorway as a woman named Odell — she would learn the name later, along with everything else about her — pointed a trembling finger at the herbalist's house and said, loudly enough for the clerks to hear, that strange lights had been seen there. Strange lights. Strange herbs. A stranger who'd come and gone.

The herbalist — Maera, gray-streaked, iron-spined — stood in her own doorway and said nothing at all, which was more courage than most managed under a pointing finger.

Serah had ordered this. That was the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. The grain-for-confession program was hers, drafted in her own hand, designed to flush out magic-users without spilling blood. *Mercy first,* she had written. She had believed it, once. Watching Odell trade a neighbor's safety for a sack of barley, watching the clerks write the woman's name in a ledger — always a ledger — Serah felt the machinery of her faith close around her own throat.

She asked the tavern-keeper where the Thackeray farm lay. He looked at her the way men look at executioners' horses and told her: upriver, past the mill, follow the crows.

* * *

Noor Thackeray was waiting for her.

Not at the farmhouse — at the river bend below it, standing on the flat grey stone where the water pooled slow and glass-clear, and Serah knew before she introduced herself that this meeting was no accident. Beast-speakers heard things. Crows gossiped. Noor had known she was coming since the moment she crossed the forgetting line, and had chosen the ground: open sky, running water, nowhere to hide soldiers or traps.

Good, Serah thought. She respected a person who chose their terrain.

"You're the inquisitor," Noor said. Younger than Serah expected, with a healer's calm hands and eyes that had already catalogued her — sword, seal, the telltale bulge of documents beneath her coat. "You let us go at the ford. Three days ago. Your men were half a mile behind us and you called them off."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I wasn't hunting you." Serah walked to the edge of the stone and looked down into the water. A fish hung motionless there, regarding her with ancient indifference. "I was hunting the same thing you're carrying."

Noor went very still. It was a stillness Serah recognized from interrogations — the stillness of someone deciding how much they could afford to know. "I don't have anything."

"The covenant." Serah turned to face them. "The ledger of divine debts. Vess carries it, or you do, or it lives somewhere between you — I don't care which. My order wants it found and destroyed. Buried again, deeper this time, with everyone who's read it." She let that land. "I'm not my order anymore. I want it read. Publicly. Every clause, every debt, every forgery — spoken aloud where gods and mortals can both hear it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstream, a crow made a remark that sounded, to Serah's untrained ear, distinctly editorial.

"And why," Noor said carefully, "would an inquisitor want her own church exposed?"

"Because my church was built on a lie, and I found the lie, and when I brought it to the men who taught me to love truth, they handed me a shovel." Serah pulled the folded page from her coat — careful, deliberate, showing her empty hands doing it — and held it out. "This is the first entry of the covenant. Read it if you can. I can't. But I've compared the hand to Saint Aldous's authenticated correspondence for two years, and it matches stroke for stroke. Our founder forged the covenant that ended the god-war. Everything since — the tithes, the burning, the ledgers, me — has been bookkeeping for his crime."

Noor took the page but didn't look at it. They were looking at her instead, the way they must look at frightened animals: reading what the voice didn't say. "You're afraid," they said. "Not of me. Of them finding out you came here."

"My superiors gave me a month to return with the covenant's location. When I don't — when they learn I met you and let you walk away twice — they'll declare me corrupted. There are inquisitors who hunt their own. I trained two of them." Serah smiled, and it was not a kind smile, mostly because it was an honest one. "So yes. I'm afraid. Fear is just information. Here's mine: I am a dead woman walking unless the truth comes out first. If the covenant surfaces publicly, my order can't bury it without burying itself. That's the only shelter left for people like me. People who chose truth over the institution that raised them."

* * *

They talked for two hours on the flat stone while the river ran and the light moved.

Serah learned things she had not come to learn. That Noor had inherited the oath through a grandmother's bloodline, unknowing. That the godling Vess was wounded and hiding in a barn, convalescing on goat's milk and stubbornness. That Maera had nearly been denounced, that children slept in the herbalist's house who had nowhere else, that the whole fragile architecture of mercy Serah had built in this valley was currently crushing the very people it claimed to protect.

And Noor learned things too. Serah watched them absorb each detail of the forgery with a dawning, terrible comprehension — the sense of a person realizing the cage they'd lived in had a name, and a builder, and a paper trail.

"If I speak it aloud," Noor said finally, "at Karveth, before the conclave — the storm-god Odran wants the covenant for punishment. The Church wants it destroyed. You want it published. Everyone wants to hold it. Nobody wants to *hear* it."

"That's why it has to be spoken, not surrendered." Serah crouched, drew a line in the wet stone with one finger. "A document can be stolen. A sealed ledger can be forged again — Aldous proved that. But words spoken publicly, witnessed by armies and gods together, remembered by ten thousand minds? You can't forge that. You can't burn it out of every head. It becomes the one thing my order has feared for two thousand years." She looked up. "Irreversible."

"You understand," Noor said quietly, "that if you're wrong, we start the war again. All of it. Gods against mortals, and this time everyone knows exactly who cheated whom."

"Yes." Serah rose, brushed the water from her knee. "And if we do nothing, Odran burns a village a week and my church burns the rest, and the lie sits safe under both fires forever. I've done the arithmetic, Thackeray. I was made for arithmetic. There is no column in which silence saves anyone. There is only a column in which silence saves *the lie*."

* * *

She saw the hesitation she needed — not agreement, not yet, but the shift in the shoulders, the recalibration behind the eyes. Noor Thackeray was deciding whether to trust her, and Serah knew better than to rush a decision like that. Trust rushed was trust broken.

So she offered the only currency she had left.

"Here is my proposition. Mutual blackmail — the honest kind." She held out her hand, palm up, empty. "You give me nothing today. But you know what I am now: an inquisitor who betrayed her order, carrying proof of its founding crime. If I abandon you, you can destroy me with a word to Karveth or a word to my own hunters. And I know what you are: the last oath-keeper, hiding a godling and a secret worth kingdoms. If you fall, I fall with you — because I let you go, twice, and my order knows it. We are tied together whether we like it or not. The only question is whether we hang separately or build something."

Noor looked at her hand for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, they laughed — short, startled, real. "Maera always says the Church sends either fire or paperwork. She never mentioned it might send a co-conspirator."

"We contain multitudes. It's in the doctrine. Page four hundred." Serah kept her hand extended. "I don't need your answer now. I need it at Karveth, in front of the conclave, when Odran makes his offer and the Church makes its threat. When that moment comes — and it's coming, Thackeray, within days — I need one witness on the mortal side who isn't holding a torch or a ledger. Just one. Me."

Noor took her hand. Their grip was a healer's grip — dry, steady, practiced at holding things together.

"One condition," Noor said. "When I speak it — if I speak it — you stand where they can see your face. Your order sent you to bury the truth. Let them watch you refuse."

"It would be my honor," Serah said, and discovered, saying it, that she meant it — that some cold, starved chamber in her chest had warmed for the first time in twenty years. This was what belief was supposed to feel like. Not the ledger's arithmetic. This: standing in open air beside a stranger, gambling everything on a truth neither of them fully owned.

She turned to go, then stopped. Downstream, the light had changed — gone bruised and violet, though the sun stood high. The river had begun to run backward, gently, as if reconsidering.

Noor had gone rigid beside her. "That's not weather," they breathed. "Something's coming up the valley. Something big. And the beasts—" They pressed a hand to their ear, listening to a conversation Serah couldn't hear, and their face drained white. "The crows say the storm-god's herald crossed the forgetting line an hour ago. Serah — the valley's protection isn't failing. Someone on the *inside* told Odran how to break it."

Serah's hand went to her sword hilt, then, slowly, away from it. Swords wouldn't matter. She looked at the backwards-running river and understood, with her auditor's certainty, that the hour of arithmetic was over.

The moment she had promised to stand witness for had arrived early — and it was bringing an army.
