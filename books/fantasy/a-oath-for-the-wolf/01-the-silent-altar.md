## 1. The Silent Altar

The fire was small on purpose. Hazel Thackeray had built it in a hollow of stone behind the ruined waystation, where the wind off the Thornfell highlands couldn't find it, and she'd banked it low so that no light would climb above the broken walls and advertise her presence to the road. Ten years of smuggling had taught her that the difference between a good fire and a dead one was mostly a matter of pride. Pride made people build fires they could see from a mile off. Hazel had stopped being proud a long time ago.

She knelt now on the cold flagstone, her pack set aside with its straps buckled — always buckled; a loose strap was a loose tongue — and laid out the offering on a square of grey cloth: a cup of dark beer, a heel of black bread, and a single copper coin, worn nearly smooth, stamped with a sunburst that might once have been a face.

"Evening," she said to the coin. To what the coin stood for. "Quiet night. Cold one. The pass is clear as far as Greywater Ford, if you're keeping count."

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered.

She waited anyway, the way she always waited — three breaths, no more, because more than three meant hoping, and hoping was a debt you paid with interest. On the fourth breath she drank half the beer herself, ate the bread, and pocketed the coin. That was the rite entire. Offer, wait, take back. Her god had been silent for ten years, but Hazel had never once skipped the offering, and she'd have cut out the tongue of anyone who asked her why.

Because it wasn't about being answered. That was what she told herself, kneeling there in the dark with beer on her lips and frost forming in her hair. It was about *keeping the appointment*. Anyone could pray when the god was listening. It took a certain kind of stubbornness to keep showing up for a conversation where the other party had walked out a decade ago and never said goodbye.

*You expect nothing,* she reminded herself, folding the cloth. *That's why you're still alive.*

It was true, or near enough to true that the difference didn't matter at two in the morning in wolf-country. She expected nothing from the road except the road's dangers, which she knew by heart. She expected nothing from her employers except betrayal at the worst convenient moment, which she priced into every contract. She expected nothing from other people, full stop — not loyalty, not gratitude, not even honesty beyond the length of a handshake. And she expected nothing from heaven, which had made its position on the subject of Hazel Thackeray abundantly clear.

A woman who expects nothing can't be disappointed. A woman who expects nothing can't be ambushed by hope. She moved through the world like water through cracks, finding every gap, trusting none of them to hold, and that was why she was the best smuggler working the Thornfell passes and why she slept alone and why, some nights, the silence inside her chest was so loud she could hear her own heartbeat like footsteps following her.

She was banking the fire when she heard them coming up the trail — boots, many, careless, laughing. Six men at least, moving without any attempt at quiet, which in the Thornfell highlands after dark was either stupidity or a declaration. Hazel pinched the coals dead between two stones, shouldered her pack, and slid into the shadow of the waystation's fallen lintel with her knife already loose in its sheath.

They came around the ruin's corner carrying lanterns like they owned the night, and she recognized the colors before she recognized the faces: red sashes over mail, the badge of the Corvane crew, who ran salt and stolen relics down from the northern shrines and were famous in four counties for two things — their prices and their cruelty. Their leader was a broad man called Dree Harrow, whose beard was braided with little silver bells that jingled when he laughed, which was often, because Dree Harrow found everything funny, especially other people's misfortunes.

"Well," he said, stopping, lantern raised, bells shivering. "Look what the mountain coughed up. Hazel Thackeray herself."

"Dree." She didn't come out of the shadow. "Nice bells."

"You're camped in our stretch, Thackeray. This is Corvane road this season."

"The road belongs to whoever's paying the tolls, and I paid mine at Greywater. Yours is a different purse." She let that sit. "But I'm leaving anyway. You go ahead and guard your dirt."

One of the younger ones — a lean fellow with a crossbow slung carelessly backwards — snickered and said, too loudly, "Leaving? Or going to say her prayers?"

Hazel went very still.

Dree's grin widened. He'd caught it. He was the kind of man who could smell a bruise through leather. "That's right," he said, stepping closer, lantern swinging, casting her shadow long across the broken wall. "We passed your little altar spot. Beer and bread and a bit of copper, all laid out pretty." He clicked his tongue. "Ten years, they say. Ten years and nothing. You know what we call that, boys?"

"A waste of good beer," said the crossbowman.

"We call it praying to a corpse." Dree spread his hands, magnanimous, delighted. "No offense meant, Thackeray. But your god's dead, or deaf, or never existed, and you're out here every night talking to the dark like a widow visiting a grave that's got nobody in it. There's a word for that."

"There are several," Hazel said quietly. "Pick one carefully."

"Faithful," Dree said, and spat the word like gristle. "Faithful. Faithful to *what*, though? That's my question. What's it get you? You sleep cold, you work alone, you've got no crew, no family, no god — nothing holding your line but your own two hands. And your own two hands don't love you back either, do they?" He laughed, and his crew laughed with him, bells jingling, and the sound filled the ruin like flies filling a wound. "You know what I think? I think you keep the little altar because it's the only thing in the world that can't leave you. Dead gods make loyal company."

Something in Hazel's chest went white-hot and very, very calm.

Later — much later, in another life — she would remember how close it came. How her hand had already found the knife's grip, how she had already measured the distance to Dree's throat, six feet, easy, before his lantern even dropped. How the only thing that stopped her was arithmetic: six against one, a cargo to deliver, a reputation that ran on reliability rather than corpses. She did not kill Dree Harrow in the ruins of the old waystation. But she thought about it with a clarity that frightened her more than the wanting did, because it wasn't rage doing the measuring. It was something colder and older, something that felt almost like relief — the part of her that had been waiting ten years for permission to stop expecting anything from anyone, including herself.

Instead she stepped out of the shadow into the lantern-light, slow, letting him see her whole.

"I'll tell you what faith gets me, Dree," she said. "It gets me up before dawn. It gets me through the pass when your boys are still puking up their ale. It gets me contracts while you're fighting over scraps, because the merchants of Thornfell trust a woman who keeps her appointments." She looked at the bells in his beard, each one a little trophy of someone else's bad day. "You wear your victories where everyone can hear them. Mine don't jingle. They just hold."

"Pretty speech."

"It's free. The next thing I say won't be." She turned, unhurried, and picked up her pack. "The road's yours, Dree. Guard it well. It's the only thing that'll have you."

She heard him start to answer and kept walking, and the dark took her the way it always did, easily, like a door opening for someone it knew.

* * *

She made four miles before she let herself stop, in a hollow beneath a leaning rowan where the wind couldn't follow. Her hands were steady. Her breathing was even. Everything about her was exactly as it should be, and that was the problem — because underneath the steadiness, in the place where the silence lived, something was rattling its bars.

*Dead gods make loyal company.*

She sat down with her back to the rowan and unbuckled the pack and took out the grey cloth, though it was past midnight and the rite was a dusk-rite and she had already kept it. She laid out the last of the bread. She had no beer left, so she used snowmelt in the cup, and the copper coin, and she knelt in the frost with her knees aching and spoke to the dark.

"That wasn't for you," she said. "What I said to him. In case you were listening. Which you weren't."

Three breaths. One. Two. Three.

"Ten years," she said, and her voice cracked on the number, just slightly, just enough that she was glad there was no one in the world to hear it. "I've kept every appointment. Every night, rain, snow, fever, blood loss. I never missed. Not once. So if you're there — if any piece of you is anywhere — I would take *anything*. A dream. A cold spot. A coin landing wrong. Just once. Just so I know I haven't been talking to myself for a decade like a madwoman on a mountainside."

The rowan creaked overhead. Somewhere far off, a wolf began to sing — long, low, patient, a sound with centuries in it, a sound that seemed less like an animal calling and more like something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the hills.

Hazel froze, cup halfway to her lips.

The wolf sang on, unhurried, and the note of it worked its way under her skin and settled somewhere behind her sternum, familiar as her own name, and she could not have said why. She only knew that her hands had begun, very slightly, to shake — the first unsteady thing about her all night — and that the silence in her chest had changed shape. It was no longer empty. It was the silence of a room in which someone has just, very softly, closed a door.

She sat until the singing stopped. Then she packed the offering away, unfinished for the first time in ten years, and lay down with her knife in hand and did not sleep.

At first light she broke camp and took the eastern fork instead of the western, toward the high country she avoided every season of every year, toward the deep shrines and the old stone, telling herself it was a shortcut and knowing it was a prayer.

Two days later, a stranger would stumble into her camp with his guts opened and a map in his bleeding hands, and everything she believed about her silence would begin to die.
