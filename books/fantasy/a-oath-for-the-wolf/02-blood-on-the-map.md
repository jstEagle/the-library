## 2. Blood on the Map

The fire was small on purpose. Hazel had built it in a hollow of stone behind a fallen fir, where the light would climb no higher than her knees and the smoke would tangle itself in the branches above before any watcher's eye could find it. She was three days out from Grayharbor with a pack of smuggled salt-iron and a route she knew by the shape of its silences — where the road bent out of sight of the tollhouse, where the stream ran shallow enough to ford at night, where the king's riders grew lazy because nothing worth taxing had ever come this way.

She was good at this work. Not because she was brave, though she could be, and not because she was fast, though she was faster than most men believed until it cost them. She was good because she expected nothing. No partner would betray her if she never let one close enough. No patron would abandon her if she never waited for one. And no god would fail her if she asked for nothing but the courtesy of being heard.

Still, every night, she made the offering.

She knelt now at the edge of the firelight with her knife drawn across her left palm, a thin line that welled and then stilled. She tipped the blood into the dirt — three drops, as the rite demanded, though she'd learned the rite from no one living and could not have said where the number came from, only that her hand knew it the way her hand knew how to gut a fish or throw a blade.

"Old One," she said quietly. "Keep the road under me. Keep my name when I cannot."

The words went up into the dark like sparks into wet wool. Nothing answered. Nothing had answered for ten years — not a warmth, not a whisper, not so much as a change in the wind. Other mercenaries had gods who spoke: Maren of the Salt Company swore her river-god told her which bridges were rotten, and she'd grown rich on it. Even the superstitious fools who worshipped whatever lived under Thornfell's mountain claimed their shrines still hummed.

Hazel's god said nothing. She made the offering anyway, wiped the knife on her thigh, and told herself — as she did every night — that faith without answer was simply discipline by another name.

It almost worked, tonight.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him, which saved her life, or at least her sleep. A sound off the road — not a footfall exactly, more the failure of one, a body catching itself against a tree and sliding. Then a wet, dragging breath.

Hazel put out the fire with a sweep of her boot, took her bow, and moved into the dark along the line she'd already chosen, downwind, low. The moon was a fingernail paring over the ridge; enough to see by, not enough to be seen.

He came stumbling through the bracken like a man walking into wind only he could feel. Tall, gray-cloaked, one hand pressed to his belly where something had opened him — she could smell the blood even over the cold pine, copper-sweet and wrong, mixed with something else beneath it, something burnt. He fell twice between the treeline and her camp, and both times he got up again, and both times she drew and did not loose, because a dying man crawling toward you with empty hands is either a trap or a message, and traps were usually better organized than this.

"Mercenary." His voice was gravel dragged through water. He'd seen her — somehow, in the dark, he'd seen exactly where she stood. "I was told. On the road. A woman alone, gray eyes, salt-iron cargo. I was told you'd be here."

"Who told you?" Hazel kept her voice flat. "And who cut you?"

"No one cut me." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that brought him to his knees at the edge of her dead fire. "No blade does this. It's what happens when you carry something too long that doesn't want to be carried." He looked up at her, and even in the thin moonlight she saw that his eyes were wrong — not feverish, not mad, just *spent*, like lamps burned down to the last film of oil. "You're the one they said. The one who makes offerings to a silent altar."

Her blood went cold in a way the night hadn't managed. "Who are you?"

"A courier," he said. "Once. Before there was nothing left to carry me but this." He fumbled at his cloak, and she watched his hands shake, watched them fail, watched him try again with the stubbornness of a man who had decided he would die only after one specific thing was done. "Here. Take it. Take it *now*, before I forget why I crawled all this way."

What he pressed into her hands was a roll of leather, stiff and old, tied with a cord that had been dyed red once and was dark now with older stains. The moment it touched her skin, cold climbed up her arms — not the cold of night air but a cold with weight to it, a cold like holding a key that has been buried in winter ground. She nearly dropped it. She didn't know why she didn't.

"There's a map inside," the stranger said. "Don't open it here. Don't open it where anything can see you reading it. There are things on that road that count, mercenary. They count everything. Every face, every name, every year. And when the count is finished—" He stopped. His breath caught, held, failed.

"Don't let him finish the count," he said, and each word came slower than the last, as though he were paying for them out of some purse running dry. "That's all. That's the whole message. Don't let him finish the count. Find the stones. Find—"

His eyes fixed on her face, and something moved through them — recognition, sudden and total, the way a man recognizes home.

"Seryth," he whispered.

Hazel went very still. "That's not my name."

"It was." A ghost of a smile crossed his ruined face. "In the temple. Before. You used to braid your hair wrong, Seryth, always wrong, and the old priestess said the god liked it, said it looked like roots growing sideways, like something that wouldn't stay planted where it was put—" His voice frayed apart. "He kept it. Your name. He kept it safe all this time, in the dark, in the place where he—"

"What temple? What god? Who kept it?" The questions came out of her faster than she could weigh them, and she hated the sound of her own voice — hungry, cracked, a girl's voice. "Look at me. What temple?"

But the light in him was going out. She saw it happen the way you see a tide turn — no single moment, only the sureness after that it had gone. His mouth moved once more. What came out was not a word, or if it was, it belonged to no language she knew; it sounded like wind through a hollow place, like bells rung underwater.

Then he was only a body in the bracken, and the night was only the night, and Hazel Thackeray — who did not flinch at wounds, who had sewn her own arm shut with sail thread, who had watched men die in nine different counties and slept well afterward — sat down hard on a stone and discovered her hands would not stop shaking.

*Seryth.*

She turned the word over the way you'd turn an unfamiliar coin, checking for the mark of a forgery. It fit nothing. She had been Hazel since — since always. Since Grayharbor, since the first ledger anyone had ever written her into. She had no mother she remembered, no childhood, no temple. She had woken up at fourteen in a harbor town with calluses she couldn't account for and a scar on her palm shaped like a crescent, and she had built herself from that blank page onward, brick by competent brick, and she had been *proud* of it.

Hadn't she?

The leather map burned cold against her skin through her shirt, patient as ice.

* * *

She buried the stranger at first light, because leaving him for the crows felt like agreeing with whoever had done this to him. Digging gave her hands work and her mind leave to circle. By the time she piled the last stones, she had assembled the morning's arithmetic:

One: the map existed, and she had not opened it, partly from caution and partly from a superstition she didn't examine too closely — that once read, a thing could not be unread, and she suspected this thing would read *her* back.

Two: a dying man had crawled, by his own account, a very long way, carrying something that was killing him, to give it specifically to a woman described well enough that he'd found her camp in the dark. That meant someone had sent him. Someone knew her route, her face, her habits — and knew about the offerings. Knew about the silent altar.

Three: he had called her Seryth, and spoken of a temple, and of a god who had *kept* her name — kept it somewhere, hidden, the way you hide a thing from a thief. And the only god Hazel had ever served had gone silent ten years ago, the same year, she realized with a slow vertigo, that she had appeared in Grayharbor with no past and a crescent scar.

Coincidence was a currency she'd stopped accepting years ago. Nobody paid in it honestly.

She should sell the map. That was the sane move. There were collectors in Grayharbor who paid gold for old leather and older mysteries, and mysteries out of the Thornfell interior fetched triple. She could be rid of the cold weight of it by week's end, richer, unburdened, back to roads she understood and a life she had built with her own two hands.

She got as far as taking it out and weighing it in her palm.

The leather was scarred with age, soft as skin at the edges, and stitched along one border with thread that glinted faintly silver when the sun touched it — not metal, she decided, not quite. The cord was stained dark, and now, in honest daylight, she admitted to herself what the stain was. Blood. Old blood, renewed often, the way you'd renew an offering.

An offering. To keep a thing safe on its journey.

The same rite she performed every night to a god who never answered.

Hazel stood in the thin morning light with the map in her hand and felt the two halves of her life grind together like millstones: the woman who expected nothing from heaven, and the girl someone had stolen and named Seryth, waiting all these years in the dark for a courier to come crawling out of the trees.

She tied the cord around her neck and tucked the map beneath her shirt, against her sternum, where it lay cold as a second heartbeat.

Then she broke camp, shouldered her pack of salt-iron she no longer cared about, and turned north — away from Grayharbor, away from the delivery that would pay her quarter's rent — toward the interior of Thornfell, following nothing but a dead man's last instruction and the pull of a name that was not hers and might be.

Behind her, far down the valley, a church bell began to ring. She counted the strokes without meaning to.

Nine.

She did not know why the number made her walk faster.
