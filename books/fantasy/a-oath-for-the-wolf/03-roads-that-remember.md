## 3. Roads That Remember

The map did not behave like a map.

Hazel had owned charts that smelled of salt and tar, surveyor's drawings crisp as frost, smugglers' scraps annotated in three inks and one blood. She knew what paper did when it aged — curled, yellowed, went soft at the folds like old skin. This thing stayed supple as a living hide, and it was cold. Not winter-cold. Cold the way a coin is cold when you pull it from a dead man's pocket: the cold of something that no longer has any business being either warm or cold.

She'd tried to sell it twice.

The first time, in Marrowgate, a fence named Osk took one look at the leather and pushed it back across the table with two fingers, the way you'd return a knife someone had dropped point-up. "Not that," he said. "Whatever it is, not that." He wouldn't say more, wouldn't meet her eyes, and when she left his shop she noticed he'd already turned the sign to closed though it was barely midday.

The second time she didn't even get inside. The broker's apprentice met her on the steps, glanced at the tube slung across her back, and said, "Lady, we don't deal in Thornfell goods," before she'd said a word about Thornfell or anything else. She hadn't mentioned where she was headed. She was sure of it. Almost sure.

So she kept it, and after four days of telling herself she was merely holding it until she found a buyer stupid enough, she stopped lying to herself and followed it instead.

The roads it described were wrong. That was the first thing. Hazel Okonkwo knew the Thornfell interior the way other women knew their own hands — every goat track, every ford, every burned-out tollhouse where the tollmen had died and nobody bothered replacing them. She could walk from the Salt Gate to the high passes without consulting so much as a cairn. But the map showed roads she had never seen, running through country she had crossed a dozen times, and where they ran there should have been nothing. A road through the middle of the Grayfell moor, for instance, where she had once spent three miserable days finding water. The map drew a highway there, straight and confident, marked with waystones.

Nobody built highways across the Grayfell. Nobody would.

She went anyway. Because the stranger had died pressing it into her hands, because he had said *don't let him finish the count* in a voice like a man watching a door he couldn't reach, and because — this was the part she examined least — he had called her by a name she did not know, and she wanted to know why.

* * *

She found the first village on the evening of the second day out of Marrowgate, exactly where the map promised, in a fold of the moor that Hazel's memory insisted was empty grassland.

It wasn't empty. It was a proper little town — forty roofs, maybe fifty, thatch and grey stone, smoke rising straight in the windless dusk, sheep pens, a well with a carved cover, a shrine-house with a bell. A good bell, bronze, the kind of bell a parish saved ten years to buy. It hung silent in its arch.

Hazel stood on the rise above it for a long moment, doing the arithmetic that had kept her alive for fifteen years of mercenary work. There was no ledger in Marrowgate, no tax roll, no guild registry, no smuggler's chart — and she had seen them all, copied half of them — that listed a settlement here. The Grayfell was supposed to be waterless. Yet below her, fields glowed green in the last light, irrigated off some stream she had never once stumbled across in three days of desperate searching.

Her skin crawled. Not fear, exactly. Something worse: the feeling of being wrong. Of having trusted a thing — her own knowledge, her own map of the world drawn from her own feet — and finding it false.

She went down anyway. Competence, she had learned long ago, meant walking toward the thing that frightened you while your hands stayed steady.

A woman met her at the edge of the first field, a broad-hipped farmer with flour to the elbows, and smiled at her like weather coming home.

"Hazel! You're early — we thought not till the week's end."

Hazel's hand went to her knife before her mind caught up. "I don't know you."

The woman laughed, easy, unbothered. "Everyone knows everyone here. Come in, come in, there's barley bread still warm. You look half-starved, same as always."

*Same as always.* As if Hazel had been here before. As if there were an *always* between them.

She let herself be led in, knife loose in its sheath, eyes working. The village was real — that was the horror of it, how real it was. Real children chasing a hoop down the lane. Real dogs. An old man mending a net by his door who nodded to her with the particular nod owed to someone familiar. Inside the farmer's house — her name was Sela, apparently, though she never introduced herself; she simply assumed Hazel knew — there was bread and honey and a fire, and everything was warm and ordinary and utterly impossible.

"Sela," Hazel said carefully, over the bread. "How do you know my name?"

Sela looked at her with mild surprise, then mild confusion, then — Hazel watched it happen — nothing at all. The warmth drained out of her face not like suspicion but like water out of a cracked cup. She blinked. She looked at Hazel sitting at her table, at the strange armed woman eating her bread, and her brow creased with polite puzzlement.

"I'm sorry," Sela said. "Have we met? You looked — I thought for a moment—" She shook her head, smiling apologetically. "Forgive me, I must be tired. Are you lost, mistress? Travelers rarely come this far off the road."

Hazel set down the bread. Her mouth had gone dry.

"You just told me I was early. You said I looked half-starved, same as always."

"Did I?" Sela laughed again, but thinner now, uncertain. "I don't think I did. I'd remember."

*I'd remember.* Hazel stood, thanked her for the bread in a voice that came out steady, which she counted as the finest piece of work she'd done all year, and walked out into the lane.

The old man with the net looked up. "Evening, Hazel. Back again?"

She stopped. "You know me?"

"Course I do." He squinted, and she watched the same tide go out of his face, the same slow blink. "Sorry, mistress. Thought you were someone else. Can I help you?"

By the well, a boy of eight waved at her cheerfully and called her name, and by the time she'd crossed the distance between them he had forgotten he'd done it. At the shrine-house, the bronze bell hung silent, and beneath it, scratched small into the stone lintel where no one would look, someone had cut a tally. Rows of marks. Nine rows full, and a tenth begun.

Hazel stared at it until the dark came down.

*Don't let him finish the count.*

* * *

She camped that night on the rise above the village, no fire, watching the windows glow below. She made her offering anyway — habit older than sense. A coin from her purse, pressed into the dirt, the whispered words to a god who had not answered in ten years. *Hear me. See me. Remember me.*

Nothing. As always. The silence of heaven, familiar as her own heartbeat.

But tonight the silence felt different. Tonight it felt less like absence and more like theft.

She thought about the villagers. About Sela's face going smooth as poured wax. They remembered her — greeted her by name, warmly, with the ease of long acquaintance — and then it drained away in minutes, like they were cups with holes in the bottom. And yet the names stayed. *Hazel.* They'd known *Hazel*, even after they'd forgotten her face. As if the name itself had been left behind, too cheap or too strange to bother taking.

Or as if it had been taken before, long ago, and what lived in their memories was only the echo of the taking.

She sat with that thought and did not like where it pointed.

Because here was the thing she kept circling: the villages weren't on any ledger. Not hidden — *unrecorded*. Whole towns, generations deep, bronze bells and carved wells, and no clerk in Marrowgate had ever written them down. But that wasn't possible unless the forgetting went both ways. Unless it wasn't just the villagers whose memories leaked. Unless somewhere, in offices and archives and the heads of cartographers, records had quietly emptied themselves of these places, and no one had noticed the gaps, because noticing required remembering that there had been something to notice.

Memories were being harvested here. Taken like wool or grain or ore — systematically, in quantity, by someone with infrastructure. The tally under the bell. Nine rows full.

And the map — the dying man's map — drew these roads as if they mattered. Drew them confidently, lovingly even, the way you draw the veins of a wound.

Hazel lay back against her pack and stared at the stars and felt, for the first time in years, genuinely out of her depth. She had built her whole life on knowing things. Roads, prices, faces, exits, which lord's men took bribes and which took heads. Knowledge was the one wealth no one could take off her body, the one asset that couldn't be stolen in the night. She had trusted it the way other people trusted gods, and unlike them, it had never gone silent on her.

Until now. Until a country where the roads themselves forgot where they went, and grandmothers forgot her between one sentence and the next, and her own perfect memory of the Grayfell moor turned out to be a memory of a hole where a living town should be.

If they could take a village out of every ledger in Thornfell, what else had they taken? What else was missing from her — not around her, but *in* her? How would she even know? A gap doesn't ache. A gap is just smoothness. She thought of the stranger calling her by a name she didn't recognize, and her chest went tight and cold.

*You're not the first thing they've emptied,* said a voice in her head that might have been her own and might have been older. *You might be full of holes yourself, mercenary. You just can't feel them.*

"Shut up," she said aloud, to the dark, to herself, to whatever listened.

Below, the village lights went out one by one, house by house, like memories being snuffed in order.

* * *

She left before dawn and followed the map deeper, and over the next six days she found five more villages that did not exist. Each one greeted her by name. Each one forgot her within minutes. Each shrine-house held its silent bronze bell and its tally scratched into the stone, and the tallies were longer the further north she went — nine full rows everywhere now, the tenth row growing mark by mark, and in the last village, a hamlet of a dozen houses huddled under a black crag, the tenth row had only three marks left empty.

Three.

She stood in that shrine-house doorway with her finger hovering over the stone, counting, recounting, feeling the cold of the map through her coat like a hand pressed flat between her shoulder blades.

Behind her, on the road she'd come by, she heard hooves. Many horses, moving in formation, unhurried, certain. She turned and saw riders cresting the hill in the king's colors — royal livery, royal escort, a banner she knew from proclamations nailed to every gate from here to the coast.

At their center rode a herald bearing a scroll sealed with the crown of Thornfell, and when he saw her standing in the shrine-house door, he did not ask who she was.

"Hazel Okonkwo," he called, voice carrying across the empty morning. "You are summoned to the capital. His Majesty commands your presence — and says to tell you: *the count is nearly finished.*"
