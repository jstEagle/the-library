## 4. The Court of Hollow Bells

The summons came on the third morning after the stranger died, riding into Hazel's camp on a white mule with a courier who smelled of ink and fear.

"By order of His Majesty King Aldric, sovereign of Thornfell and keeper of its bounds," the courier recited, holding out a scroll sealed in grey wax. "You are commanded to present yourself at the capital within seven days."

Hazel didn't take it at first. She looked at the seal — a wolf's head crowned in thorns, pressed so deep into the wax it had nearly torn through — and felt the map shift against her ribs where she'd sewn it into her coat lining. It had been cold ever since the man died. Cold as riverbed stone. Some mornings she woke and thought she could feel it ticking against her heart like something counting.

"I'm a smuggler," she said. "Kings don't summon smugglers."

"The king summons whoever he pleases." The courier's eyes flicked to her camp — the neat fire pit, the bedroll squared away with military precision, the long knife lying in plain view because hiding it would have been an insult to them both. "He knows what you carry."

She took the scroll then. The wax was warm from his hand; everything else about that moment was cold.

* * *

The capital of Thornfell had no proper name anymore, or if it did, no one used it. People called it the Bell City, because its towers were hung with bells cast in bronze and silver and one — high on the palace spire — in a pale metal no smith would claim. They never rang. Not for coronations, not for funerals, not for the festival of first thaw when every other bell in the kingdom was swung until the rope-hands bled. The hollow bells hung silent over the streets, and the people of the city had learned not to look up.

Hazel looked up. She'd been looking up for three days, since she passed through the outer gate and heard the gate-warden greet her by name — *Mistress Thackeray, welcome back* — though she had never in her life come here before. Since the innkeeper had known her favorite drink without asking. Since the cobbler's boy had waved at her like a friend and then, mid-wave, gone blank as a wiped slate, staring at her with polite incomprehension before shuffling off to other customers.

The villages on the map had done this. Greeted her, remembered her, forgotten her between one breath and the next. But here the forgetting had a different texture. In the villages it had been hunger — quick, animal, done. Here it was bookkeeping. Here, someone was keeping accounts.

She presented herself at the palace on the seventh day, because arriving early was a weakness and arriving late was a challenge, and Hazel Thackeray had survived ten years of mercenary work by giving no one either satisfaction.

They took her weapons at the door. All of them, including the boot knife and the garrote wire braided into her belt, which earned the searcher a look of genuine professional respect. Then four guards in thorn-crowned livery walked her up stairs of white stone through halls where the silence pressed against her ears like deep water.

No servants. That was the first wrong thing. A palace this size should have been crawling with them — sweepers, stewards, pages, cooks, the whole sweating machinery of a living court. Instead the corridors were empty, the sconces lit themselves with flame that burned without flickering, and the only sound was the four guards' boots and her own heartbeat and, faintly, from somewhere far above, the sound of nothing at all.

The throne room doors were oak banded in iron, tall as a chapel's, and they opened without being touched.

King Aldric sat on a throne of black wood at the far end of a hall built for a thousand petitioners, alone except for a single figure standing at the foot of the dais steps: a woman in dove-grey, silver-haired, hands folded, watching Hazel with eyes that missed nothing.

Hazel walked the length of the hall. She counted her steps out of habit — forty-one — and noted that her boots made no echo. The hall swallowed sound the way the hollow bells swallowed ringing.

Up close, the king was younger than his coinage. Thirty-five, perhaps forty, with a broad honest face and a beard trimmed short in the southern style, and he rose from the throne when she approached, which kings did not do.

"Hazel Thackeray," he said, and his voice was warm as fresh bread. "The road-walker. The woman who expects nothing from anyone." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and for one unguarded moment Hazel thought: *this is a good man, tired, carrying too much.* "I've wanted to meet you for a long time."

"Your Majesty honors me," Hazel said, and bowed exactly as deep as required and no deeper.

"Do I?" He came down the steps. The guards did not follow him. "I wonder. Do you know what I honor, Mistress Thackeray? I honor competence. I honor a person who looks at the world plainly and takes it as it is." He stopped an arm's length away, close enough that she could see the fine lines at his eyes, the calluses on his sword-hand — a king who still trained, then, or had once. "You gave a dead man a grave. You buried a stranger you owed nothing to. That is rarer than loyalty. Loyalty can be bought."

"You have me at a disadvantage, Majesty. You know a great deal about me."

"I know everything about you." The warmth held, but something behind it shifted — the way light shifts on water when a large fish turns beneath the surface. "That is rather my function."

And there — for half a heartbeat — his eyes changed. Not darkened. *Deepened.* As if she were looking down a well instead of into a face, and something at the bottom turned over and regarded her back. Hunger lived in that depth, patient and vast, the hunger of a thing that had waited centuries between meals and intended to enjoy this one.

Then it was gone. Aldric blinked, and he was a tired good man again, rubbing the bridge of his nose like a soldier with a headache.

"Forgive me. Long nights. There are matters of state—" He gestured vaguely upward, toward the silent bells. "Tell me. The map you carry. Where did you get it?"

"A dying man gave it to me on the Salt Road."

"What did he say to you?"

Hazel weighed the lie and found it wouldn't hold. Something in this hall ate lies; she could feel it leaning toward her tongue, tasting. "He said: don't let him finish the count."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them, impossibly, one of the hollow bells trembled — not ringing, just trembling, like a throat clearing around a word it must not speak.

"Yes," Aldric said softly. "That is what he would say." And his face, for a moment, was full of such grief and such helpless horror that Hazel's mercenary instincts — the ones that read men like ledgers — told her she was looking at a prisoner asking a visitor to carry a message past his captors. "Mistress Thackeray. If I give you an order, will you—"

"Your Majesty." The woman in dove-grey spoke for the first time, quiet as snowfall. "The hour grows late. Your petitioners."

Aldric closed his eyes. When he opened them, the well was back — not fully, just a gleam at the bottom, watching — and his voice when it came was pleasant and cold and belonged to no tired father of a nation. "Of course. Forgive me, Lady Vess keeps my hours better than I do." He extended his hand, and Hazel, having no choice, took it.

His grip was cool and dry. And in the instant their skin touched, she felt it — a sensation like fingers leafing through the pages of her, unhurried, appraising. *The Salt Road. The stranger's blood on the snow. The name he called you. Ah. Ah, yes. We will come back to that page.*

She wrenched her hand free. Aldric smiled with all the warmth of a drawn blade.

"Rest tonight. You'll be feasted tomorrow." He turned back toward the throne, then paused. "Oh — and Mistress Thackeray. The map. Keep it safe. Things carried carelessly have a way of... finding their way to those who count them." The well-eyes glittered. "We would hate for you to lose your place in our ledger."

* * *

Lady Vess found her that night, or perhaps had always been going to find her, in the guest chamber where the window overlooked the palace spire and the great pale bell hanging in it, huge and silent against the stars.

"They watch the doors," Vess said, without preamble, sliding the bolt home behind her. "Not the windows. The windows they consider beneath consideration." She was older than she'd seemed in the hall — sixty at least, with a scholar's stoop beginning and a duelist's balance still in how she stood. "Drink this." She pressed a small clay cup into Hazel's hand. It smelled of bitterroot and iron. "It won't stop him reading you. Nothing stops that. But it blurs the margins."

Hazel drank it. It tasted like swallowing a cold coin. "You're his advisor."

"I am his advisor. Which means I am the last person in this palace who remembers why the job exists." Vess moved to the window, silhouetted against the pale bell. "You saw him today. Both of him."

"The man is real."

"The man is real." Vess's voice cracked on it, very slightly, like ice underfoot. "Aldric Vane, third of his name, who fed his household guard during the famine winter from his own granaries, who once spent a whole night talking a bridge-keeper out of drowning himself. He is in there. He watches through whatever window the thing leaves him. And some days he fights hard enough that I get thirty seconds of my king back." She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "But the thing wearing his crown, Mistress Thackeray — the thing that counts memories like coins and spends villages like small change — that thing has been wearing him for eleven years. And it is very nearly finished counting."

"The stranger. On the Salt Road. He said—"

"Don't let him finish the count. Yes. He was one of mine." Something old and exhausted moved through Vess's face. "One of the last of us who still kept the old rites. He stole that map out of the vaults beneath the crown itself, and he ran, and he was hunted, and he knew he would die. So he gave it to a stranger. To you." She studied Hazel with unsettling attention. "He named you before he died. A name you didn't recognize. Tell me I'm wrong."

Hazel said nothing. The map against her ribs had gone so cold it burned.

"He chose you for a reason," Vess said quietly. "People like him don't waste their last breaths on chance. Tomorrow the court will feast you, and the thing in the king will flatter you, and offer you things — it is very generous, that creature, generosity costs it nothing but stolen goods. Whatever it offers you, however real it looks, however much you ache for it—" Vess gripped her wrist, hard, an old woman's grip with iron in it. "Understand what you're standing in, Hazel Thackeray. This isn't a court. It's a larder. And every soul in this city is on the shelf."

Above them, against the stars, the great pale bell began — very softly, so softly that only two women in one high room heard it — to ring.
