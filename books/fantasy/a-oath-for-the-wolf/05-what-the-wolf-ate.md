## 5. What the Wolf Ate

Lady Vess Corvane had learned, over thirty years of court service, that the truth was best delivered the way a surgeon delivers a blade: quickly, cleanly, and with the hand already braced for what comes after.

She had not, however, learned how to deliver this one.

The chamber beneath the east wing had no name on any official plan of the palace. The stewards called it the Cold Archive, and it was cold indeed — a round room sunk into the mountain's shoulder, its walls lined with slate tablets that predated the dynasty, its single lamp burning oil that smelled of juniper and something older, something like snow on stone. Vess lit the lamp herself. She did not trust the palace's new servants with this room, because the palace's new servants could not remember, from one week to the next, why they had been hired.

The mercenary arrived at the appointed hour, which Vess noted with approval. Punctuality in a sellsword was rarer than honesty in a courtier, and this woman had both in suspicious measure. Hazel Thackeray — if that was even the name the world had given her first — stood in the doorway with the posture of someone who had already counted the exits.

"You said you'd tell me what the map opens," Hazel said. "You have until my patience runs out. It's a short walk."

"Sit anyway." Vess gestured to the stool beside the lamp. "What I have to say takes longer than your patience. You'll want to be sitting when you stop believing me."

The mercenary did not sit. Vess had expected that too. She turned to the wall of tablets and drew her finger along them, slate after slate, each one incised with lines of the old script — the binding-script, the priests had called it, before there were no more priests.

"Three hundred years ago," she began, "there was a god in this country whose name was Fenmar. Not a gentle god. Not a kind one. He was the wolf at the edge of the firelight, the winter that takes the weak and the old so the strong may keep the spring. The first people of Thornfell did not love him. They *fed* him, the way you feed a thing you cannot kill, and in exchange he stayed at the edges and let them remember themselves."

Hazel's eyes narrowed. "Fed him what?"

"Memory." Vess let the word sit in the cold air. "Not the way your smugglers' tales have it — not a bite out of the skull. He ate the past. A village would give up its dead, its griefs, its old hatreds, and in the morning the village would be lighter. Happier. And Fenmar, fed, would sleep another year beneath the mountain." She touched one tablet, its lines worn nearly smooth. "That was the first binding. Not chains. A *meal*, served on schedule, by consent. The oldest contract in Thornfell, and the only one that has never once been broken by the wolf."

"Broken by the other side, then."

"You are quicker than the king's council, which is a low bar and yet." Vess allowed herself the ghost of a smile, then let it die. "The royal line took over the rite six generations in. The sacrifice changed — no longer the village's griefs, freely given, but a tithe. A census. Every household in Thornfell recorded, and a portion of every record burned at the binding-stones each midwinter. Names, births, marriages, the small histories of small lives. The crown decided what the country would forget. The crown decided what the wolf would eat." Her hand closed. "It was monstrous. It was also *contained*. I want you to hold both of those truths at once, mercenary, because the failure to hold both is how we arrived at this room."

"And the king broke it."

Vess looked at her for a long moment. "You've spoken with him."

"I've spoken with something wearing him."

"Yes." Vess turned back to the tablets. "Then you know the sound of him. The warmth that comes and goes like a draft under a door."

* * *

She told it in order, because order was the only mercy left in the story.

"Aldric was not always what he is now. I served his father, and I will tell you that the father was a hard man and the son was harder, but hardness is not hunger. Aldric came to the throne at thirty-one with a mind like a ledger — everything entered, everything balanced, everything *counted*. The priests of the rite called that a virtue. The binding-stones answered to counting, you see. Nine stones, nine tithes, nine burnings. The whole apparatus ran on arithmetic, and Aldric was the finest arithmetician the throne had ever produced."

Vess poured watered wine from the ewer she'd brought, more for her own hands than her throat. They were not quite steady.

"The tithe was expensive. That was its great flaw, in Aldric's ledger. Burning a tenth of the country's records every year — do you know what that costs? Not in coin. In *coherence*. Land disputes with no deeds. Heirs with no proof. A kingdom perpetually half-amnesiac, and the crown perpetually paying for the privilege. He began looking for a cheaper rite within a year of his coronation. He found one."

"The stranger with the map," Hazel said quietly. "He was a priest."

"He was the last of them." Vess's voice did not waver, but it cost her. "The order of the Nine Stones. And yes — the man you watched die had spent twenty years trying to reach the throne with warnings, and when the throne would not hear him, he did the only thing left. He gave the key away. To a stranger. To *you*, though the gods alone know why the lot fell to you, and I have begun to suspect the gods had less to do with it than the dead man's own desperation."

"You said the king found a cheaper rite."

"He did." Vess set down the cup. "He found that the binding did not require a tithe at all — that the stones would accept any offering of memory, freely surrendered, and that the *size* of the offering determined only the length of the wolf's sleep. A tenth of the kingdom's records bought a year. A king's whole reign, freely given — bought a century. And Aldric, who counted everything, counted the years of his own life and the years of his kingdom's peace and concluded that one man's memory was the cheapest price ever paid for a nation's safety."

Hazel had gone very still. "He offered himself."

"He offered himself the way a miser offers a coin he's already bitten. He performed the rite. He spoke the surrender. And at the last moment — at the very threshold of the offering — he flinched." Vess heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used for death notices. "He could not give up his mother's face. That was the memory he could not burn. Everything else, he would surrender; that one, he kept back, hidden behind a false count, a lie in the ledger of his own soul. And Fenmar — who has been fed by kings for three hundred years and knows every trick in the arithmetic of sacrifice — *tasted the lie*."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

"A binding is a door held shut by a true word," Vess said. "Aldric's word was false. The door opened. And the wolf, who had been sleeping at the threshold for three centuries, did not come out into the world. He came *in* — into the nearest vessel, into the richest vessel, into the king who had invited him with a lie. Fenmar put Aldric on like a coat. And he has been feeding ever since — not on tithes, not on records, but on the living country. Villages. Whole parishes, unwritten from the ledgers, unwritten from their neighbors' minds. He eats, and he grows, and the more he grows the wider he reaches, and there is no rite left to stop him because the last priest died in a ditch with his map in a stranger's hands."

* * *

The silence afterward had weight, like snow on a roof.

Hazel stood by the door, arms crossed, and Vess watched her process it — watched the mercenary's mind move the way a soldier's mind moves, cataloging, pricing, looking for the tactical shape of the catastrophe. It was the wrong way to hold this story and the only way Hazel knew, and Vess felt something like tenderness for her, and something like shame.

Because there was more, and the more was hers.

"You're wondering why I'm telling you this," Vess said. "Why not the council. Why not the army."

"The council can't remember last week. And the army takes orders from the thing on the throne."

"Both true. But neither is the reason." Vess walked to the far wall, to the last tablet, the newest one — only a century old, its script still sharp. "The reason is that Aldric did not find the cheaper rite alone. He found it because someone in his father's old council confirmed for him that it *could* work. Someone who had studied the binding all her life. Someone who told herself she was only answering a question of scholarship, only confirming an arithmetic, only — " Her voice caught, and she let it, because hiding it would be one more lie in a story already rotten with them. "Someone who was me."

Hazel turned. For the first time since entering the room, she looked at Vess not as an obstacle or an asset but as a person, and it was somehow worse.

"You helped him break it."

"I handed him the knife and told him it was a key." Vess pressed her palm flat against the tablet, against the names of the dead rite. "I have had eleven years to make my peace with it and I have not managed a single day of it. So understand me, mercenary, when I say what I say next. I am not asking you to save Thornfell. I am not asking you to save the king, though the king is still in there, drowning, and every year the water rises. I am asking you to do the thing I was too much a coward to do: to hold the whole truth at once. The binding was monstrous *and* it was containment. Aldric is a fool *and* he is a victim. And the wolf — " she turned, and the lamplight made her shadow enormous on the slate — "the wolf is not a beast in a story. He is patient, and he is clever, and he has already begun to look at *you*."

"Me."

"You carry something he wants. I don't know what. The stranger's map chose you; the forgotten villages know your name; and three days ago the king's own guard was ordered to learn your habits, which means Fenmar has given the order, which means you are no longer a curiosity to him. You are a meal he has decided to savor." Vess stepped closer. "So I will teach you what the rite was, all of it, every word of the old script, and you will go down under the mountain before he finishes his count — the priest's dying words, mercenary, and I finally understand them. Fenmar is counting the binding-stones. Eight have fallen silent. When the ninth goes, there will be nothing left in this country old enough to hold a chain."

Hazel was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was the flat, competent voice Vess had first heard in the throne room — the voice of a woman who expects nothing from anyone, not even heaven.

"Then we'd best move before he reaches ten."

"No," Vess said, and took the lamp from its hook, and led her toward the stair that went down. "You misunderstand. The count doesn't reach ten. There are only nine."

The lamp flame shivered.

"And last night, mercenary, I felt the ninth stone die."
