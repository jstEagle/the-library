## 6. A Name I Never Gave

The road out of the forgotten villages ran north along a river that had no name on any map Hazel owned, and the river was the only thing in Thornfell that seemed to remember anything at all. It remembered the shape of its banks. It remembered the salmon that returned each autumn to the same gravel beds their mothers had used before Hazel's grandmother's grandmother had been born. It flowed the way it had always flowed, indifferent to the theft happening quietly along its shores, carrying its memory the way a body carries blood — not because it chooses to, but because it cannot do otherwise.

Hazel envied it.

She had made camp two nights past in a hollow beneath an old watchtower, one of those squat stone rings the old kings of Thornfell had raised against raiders who no longer came. The tower was roofless now, open to the sky like a broken cup, and she had chosen it for exactly that reason: from inside, lying on her bedroll with her back against the south wall, she could see the whole bowl of heaven. She told herself it was good practice, keeping watch. A mercenary who stopped watching the sky was a mercenary who woke up dead.

But the truth was uglier, and she knew it even as she lied to herself about it. She lay there looking up because she did not know what else to do with what was happening to her. For three days — since the stranger had died in her camp with his blood soaking into her cloak, since he had pressed the leather map into her hands and spoken a name she did not know — she had been carrying a question the way you carry a stone in your boot. Not sharp enough to stop you walking. Just heavy enough that every step reminded you it was there.

*Who am I?*

It should have been an easy question. She had answered it a thousand times, to customs men and hiring captains and rival crew leads and once, memorably, to a magistrate in Corroway who had wanted her full name for a contract and gotten instead a flat look and the words "Hazel Thackeray, and if you don't like it, find another blade." She had built herself out of that name the way a mason builds a wall, course by course, stone by stone. Hazel Thackeray, mercenary. Hazel Thackeray, oathbound. Hazel Thackeray, the woman who expected nothing from anyone, not even heaven, and who had made her competence out of that emptiness like armor hammered over a wound nobody could see.

And now some dying smuggler had looked at her across three feet of trampled snow and said *Sera* — said it gently, the way you say a name to someone you have been waiting your whole life to find — and the wall had cracked.

* * *

She spent the first day after leaving the villages doing what she always did when something rattled her: work. She checked her gear twice. She re-waxed her boots. She cleaned her blades until they threw back the grey winter light like mirrors, and then she cleaned them again because the first time hadn't made the feeling go away. Work was the medicine she trusted. Work had never lied to her. Work had never gone silent for ten years and left her talking into a dark altar like a fool shouting down a well.

But by the second evening, the work had run out of places to hide the question, and the question found her anyway.

It happened at dusk, in the hollow under the tower. She had built up her fire small — a mercenary's fire, low and disciplined, the kind that warmed without announcing you to every bandit within four miles — and she was sitting in front of it eating hard bread and salted fish when the word surfaced again. Not from outside. From inside. From somewhere in her own mouth, her own memory, her own body, which was precisely what made it unbearable.

*Sera.*

She heard it the way you hear a song you've forgotten you know — half-remembered, humming just below thought, rising up unbidden the way water rises through chalk. And this time, unlike the night the stranger died, when the name had slid through her like a knife and left nothing behind but dread, this time something answered it. Something rose to meet the word from deep inside her, some fragmentary thing, some splinter of recognition that did not arrive as a memory at all.

It arrived as a reflex.

Her hands moved. Without deciding to move them, without any thought passing through her mind at all, her hands moved the way they had moved around the fire — and they did not make the sign she had made every night for ten years at her silent god's altar. They made a different one. Her right palm turned upward. Her fingers curled inward, all five, closing toward the wrist like a cupped hand holding something invisible. Then the thumb crossed over, sealing it. Then the whole gesture folded inward toward her chest, and there — at the moment it touched her sternum — something in her flinched. Some part of her body remembered a weight that was supposed to be there. A warmth. A presence. Something that had lived in that gesture once, the way a flame lives in a lamp, and was not living there now.

She sat very still afterward, the fish untouched, the bread going cold in her lap, staring at her own hands as though they had betrayed her.

They had, of course. That was the whole horror of it. Hands were supposed to be hers. Hands were the one part of her she had never doubted — a mercenary's hands, scarred and capable, hands that had earned her name honestly, grip by grip, cut by cut. And now here they were performing a rite she had never learned, a liturgy she had never been taught, folding themselves into shapes that belonged to some other life, some other faith, some other woman — and performing them *fluently*, the way you speak your mother tongue, without effort, without thought, the way water finds the channel it has always run in.

Muscle does not forget what the mind has been made to forget. That was the lesson of the evening, and it arrived in her body before it arrived in her understanding, and it sat in her chest like a coal.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She tried. She went through the whole ritual of it — banked the fire, checked the perimeter, set her blades where her hands would find them blind in the dark, lay down with her back to the stone. But sleep would not take her, and around the third hour of the dark, with the cold pressing down through the tower's broken crown and the stars wheeling slow above her, she gave up trying and sat up instead, and let herself think the thing she had been refusing to think.

Let herself follow the thread.

The stranger had called her Sera. Her hands, unprompted, in the dark, alone, with no one watching and nothing to gain — her hands had made a gesture of offering. Of worship. Of *oath*. And the gesture had ended at her chest, at the place where a person keeps the things they cannot put down, and something in her had flinched at the emptiness there the way a dog flinches at a missing limb.

Ten years. Ten years she had knelt at altars and made offerings and spoken her nightly oath into silence, and told herself the silence meant abandonment, and built her whole character on the back of that abandonment — the woman who expected nothing, who trusted nothing, who needed nothing, because heaven itself had proven that needing was a fool's economy. She had worn her godlessness like a badge. She had mocked softer people than herself, gently, privately, the way you mock weakness you're grateful not to share. *They need their gods. I don't. Look how well I do without.*

And now the ground under all of it had opened, and what was underneath was not emptiness.

What was underneath was evidence. Physical, bodily, undeniable evidence that she had once belonged to something — that the gestures of devotion lived in her hands like scars live in skin, that she had performed them thousands of times, that they had been carved into her so deeply that even whatever had been done to her mind had not reached them. Whatever had taken her memories had taken the record of her life but not the life itself. It had taken the story but left the muscle. It had burned the library and left the reader standing, illiterate, holding habits she could not explain.

And the stranger — the stranger had known. Had looked at her, gutted and dying, and seen through ten years of blank page straight down to whatever was written on the page beneath, and named it. *Sera.* Not Hazel. Not Thackeray. Something older. Something that fit the gesture in her hands the way a key fits the lock it was cut for.

She got up. She walked out of the hollow, out from under the tower's shadow, into the open starlight, because the walls had started to feel like they were part of the lie and she wanted sky above her while she thought. The frost crunched under her boots. Her breath hung white. And she stood there in the middle of a nameless field in a country that was forgetting itself, and she said it out loud, to no one, to the dark, to whatever might still be listening:

"I was someone before."

The words fell into the cold and did not echo. Nothing echoed anymore in Thornfell; she had learned that in the villages, where her voice went into the valley and came back as nothing, swallowed the way everything was swallowed. But saying them changed something anyway. Saying them was the first honest act she had committed in ten years, because every other sentence she had ever spoken about herself had been built on a foundation she now knew was false — not wrong, exactly, but incomplete, the way a map is incomplete when half the country has been torn off it.

She had believed she was self-made. She had taken pride in it. The loner's pride, the survivor's pride, the cold clean pride of a woman who owed her shape to no one — not to family, not to faith, not to fortune, only to her own two hands and her own unbending will. And it had all been true, and it had all been a lie, both at once, the way a scar is true and is also a lie about the flesh beneath it. She was self-made the way a survivor is self-made: out of wreckage. Out of a catastrophe she had no memory of surviving. Every trait she treasured — the discipline, the distrust, the refusal to hope, the competence that expected nothing — every one of them might be not a virtue but a symptom. Not character. Scar tissue. The shape a soul takes when something has bitten a hole in it and the soul has grown around the hole, the way a tree grows around a nail.

She stood in the frost until her feet went numb, and then numbness spread up her legs, and she realized she was shaking, and she could not have said whether it was cold or grief.

Both. It was both. Grief for a woman she had never met — the acolyte, the temple girl, whoever Sera had been before the world broke her open and poured her out and handed the empty vessel a new name and a sword and told her to get on with it. Grief for the god whose gestures still lived in her hands while his voice had been scraped out of the world. Grief, strangest of all, for Hazel Thackeray herself, who was not a lie exactly, who had fought real fights and kept real oaths and bled real blood under that name — but who was suddenly revealed as a room built over a cellar, and the cellar had a door, and the door had been locked from the outside by someone who did not want her ever finding what was stored down there.

*Someone.* Or something. The thought arrived with the frost, sharp and unwelcome: memories are not lost the way a coin is lost, slipping through a hole in a pocket into the grass. Memories are *taken*. Taken requires a taker. And the villages along the river had shown her what a taker looked like — showed her whole communities dissolving like sugar in water, names going out of ledgers, faces going out of minds, history going out of the world — and the dying man's last words had pointed north and east, toward the mountain, toward the capital, toward a king whose eyes flickered between a man and something older and hungrier than any man.

*Don't let him finish the count.*

She had carried those words for days without weighing them properly, the way you carry a loaded phrase, feeling its weight but not opening it. Now, standing in the frozen field with her hands making shapes she couldn't explain and a name in her mouth that wasn't hers, she opened it. And what she saw made her breath go short.

If memories were being harvested — if the wolf-thing in the crown counted them like coins, as Lady Vess had whispered, if it ate villages whole — then her stolen past was not an accident. It was not decay. It was not the ordinary cruelty of time. Her memories had been *eaten*. Taken the way grain is taken from a granary, the way lambs are taken from a fold. Which meant that somewhere, in whatever dark place such things are kept, there existed a record of her. A store of her. Ten years of a woman's life, sitting in some ancient belly like coins in a vault, and the thing that held them had held her god down first and swallowed him, and then turned to the congregation, and she was what remained. The last survivor of a parish that no longer knew it had existed.

Her oath hadn't been abandoned. Her oath had been *swallowed*. Swallowed whole, god and vows and memories together, and she had spent a decade kneeling at empty altars cursing a silence that was not silence at all but the sound of digestion.

She laughed. Standing alone in the frozen field, shaking, she actually laughed — a short, ragged, horrible laugh, the kind that comes when the truth is too large for weeping and the body doesn't know what else to do. Because it was funny, in the way terrible things are funny. All those nights. All those offerings. All that faithful, furious, stubborn devotion poured into a silence she had interpreted as desertion — and the whole time she had been praying directly into the throat of the thing that ate her god, feeding her little offerings down into the dark like a woman dropping coins into a well that had no bottom, and the well had a bottom, and the bottom was a stomach.

* * *

Dawn came grey and thin, the way winter dawns come in Thornfell, more a suggestion of light than light itself. Hazel packed her camp with the mechanical efficiency of long habit, and then stood over her packed gear for a long moment, unable to decide which way to walk, because the question of direction had become the question of identity and she knew it.

South lay the capital, and the court, and Lady Vess's warning, and the king with the flickering eyes. North and east lay the mountain, and the shrines, and whatever the dying man had been racing toward when death caught him. The map pulled east. Her oath — the living remnant of it, the fragment of habit and gesture and stubborn refusal to quit that had somehow survived the swallowing — pulled east too. Everything in her pulled east except fear, and fear pulled hardest of all, and she had learned long ago that fear was a compass worth reading but never following.

She struck east.

But she did not strike east as the same woman who had walked out of the forgotten villages. Something had shifted in her during the night, some load-bearing wall removed, some floor opened onto a cellar, and she felt the difference in the way her pack rode on her shoulders — lighter, somehow, though she had thrown nothing away, because the weight she'd lost was not in the pack. She walked lighter because she finally understood why she had always walked heavy. She had been carrying a question shaped like a hole, and holes weigh nothing, but the bracing you build around them weighs everything, and she had been building bracing for ten years.

Now the bracing was coming down, plank by plank, belief by belief, and underneath it she could feel the shape of something she did not yet have words for. Not Hazel. Not Sera, quite — Sera was a word the stranger gave her, a clue, not an answer. Something else. Something that had survived the swallowing the way her hand-gestures had survived: reduced, fragmented, but *present*. Alive in her the way embers are alive in a banked fire, invisible, warm, waiting.

She caught herself wondering — really wondering, for the first time not with dread but with a strange fierce hunger — what else had survived. What other fragments slept in her hands, her spine, her knees, her tongue. Whether somewhere in the wreck of her there might be enough of the old faith left to matter. Enough of the old oath left to keep. Enough of the old god left to find.

Because that was the thought that had come to her in the last hour of the night, the thought that had finally let her stand up and walk east without trembling: if her god had been swallowed rather than killed — if the silence was a stomach and not a grave — then the god was not gone. He was *held*. Held inside the thing that ate him, the way a swallowed seed is held inside the belly of a beast, enclosed, preserved, buried alive in the dark — and seeds have a way of doing in the dark what they cannot do in the light.

She did not know if it was hope or madness. She suspected, with the flat honesty she prided herself on, that it was both, that in Thornfell the two had become indistinguishable, that hope itself had been eaten and what passed for hope now was just hunger wearing hope's face.

But she walked east. And on the second morning of the eastern road, as she came up out of a river valley into high pine country where the frost lay blue in the shadows and the air smelled of resin and stone, she saw the shrines.

Three of them, strung along the ridgeline like beads on a broken necklace — old waystones, moss-eaten, each one crowned with a weathered carving she recognized now, recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd after years, suddenly and completely and with your heart in your mouth. A wolf. Not Fenmar's wolf, not the devourer — the other one. The rival. Her god's sigil, carved by hands that had believed, worn by centuries into something soft and patient and almost kind.

And on the shrine-stone of the middle one, fresh-cut, the moss peeled away, the granite still white at the edges of the strokes, someone had carved new words. Words in a hand too steady to be old, too deliberate to be accidental. She climbed to it, and knelt before it without deciding to kneel — her body choosing devotion before her mind could veto it, the old reflexes firing one after another like lamps coming on in a house everyone thought was empty — and she read what was written there.

It was a count. A tally. Eight marks, deep and clean, and beside them, in letters that seemed to glow faintly with a cold inner light, a single line:

*Nine comes with the moon.*

She looked up. The moon was three days from full.
