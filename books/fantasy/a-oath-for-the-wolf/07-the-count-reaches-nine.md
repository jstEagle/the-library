## 7. The Count Reaches Nine

Hazel did not sleep. She sat by the fire with the map unrolled across her knees and the tally-stone in her palm, watching the moon climb through the black teeth of the pines, and she counted too — not marks, but days, hours, the dwindling coin of time she had left to spend.

Three nights until full.

She had tried to leave. That was the honest truth of it, the truth she'd tell no one: at dawn she had saddled her mare, ridden two miles down the valley road toward the border, and then stopped, because the road had *changed*. Not washed out, not blocked — changed. The ruts were wrong. The milestone that should have read THORNFELL — 40 LEAGUES read instead THORNFELL — 12, in fresh-cut letters, as if the distance itself had been eaten. She had turned around not from courage but from a colder instinct: when the roads begin lying to you, you stop trusting your feet and start hunting for what's doing the lying.

So she made camp again in the shrine-village of Hollowmere, where the locals still greeted her by name and forgot her before she finished answering, and where the little stone chapel at the village heart stood open and empty, its altar swept clean as a table set for a guest who never came.

The fire cracked. Somewhere up-slope, a wolf howled — one long note, wrong somehow, too steady, like a bow drawn across a single string and held there.

Hazel got up, walked to the chapel, and knelt at the empty altar because it was the only prayer she knew how to make anymore: showing up. She pressed her palm to the cold stone and said the words she'd said every night for ten years, into silence, into nothing.

*"Keeper of the last light, I am yours. What I have, I hold for you. What I am, I spend on your road."*

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered.

But tonight, something else did.

"You still say it beautifully," said a voice behind her, warm as banked coals. "They taught you well, whoever they were. Or perhaps no one taught you at all. Perhaps it was simply always in you."

Hazel came off her knees with a knife already in her hand, spun, and found the chapel occupied by a man who had not been there a heartbeat before. He stood by the door as if he'd been leaning there all evening — broad-shouldered, grey-cloaked, ordinary in every line except his eyes, which caught the firelight and gave none of it back. They were the color of a winter river under ice, and they moved like water.

"I know you," she said, which was a lie, and then, worse, "I've seen you," which was true — in the flicker of King Aldric's face, in the moment the warmth drained out of the royal eyes and something ancient looked through them. This was that something, walking around outside the crown now, wearing a body like a borrowed coat.

"Fenmar," she said.

"At your service." He inclined his head with genuine courtesy. "Though *service* is such an ugly word between friends. And we are friends, Hazel. We are nearly the same age, you and I — did you know that? Both of us orphans of a god."

Her knife hand didn't waver, but her breath did. "My god isn't yours."

"No," Fenmar agreed, stepping closer, unhurried, the way weather is unhurried. "Yours is dead. Mine merely sleeps below. You've been praying to a corpse for ten years, child, and I confess it moved me. All that devotion, poured into a grave. Most people would have stopped after the first year." His head tilted. "You never stopped. Do you know why?"

"Because I keep my oaths."

"Do you?" Something flickered behind the ice-water eyes — hunger, yes, but also, horribly, something like tenderness. "You can't remember making them. Isn't that the truth of it? You are a woman built entirely out of discipline over a hole. You're excellent at everything because you trust nothing, not even your own past. I could show you why. I could show you *what*."

"I don't take bargains from things that eat villages."

"I don't eat villages." He sounded genuinely wounded. "I eat forgetting. There's a difference, though I grant it's a fine one. The memories would rot otherwise — do you know what happens to a memory no one holds? It thins. Fades. Becomes a story about a story. I keep them. Perfectly. Forever. Ask me anything about anyone this land has lost, and I can give it back to you whole, down to the smell of the bread." He smiled, and the smile was Aldric's smile, the king's own warmth, worn like a mask over the dark. "I am not the thief here, Hazel. I am the *vault*."

"And the vault has a lock," she said. "Nine stones. Eight fallen."

His expression cooled by a single degree. "You've been talking to Vess."

"Vess talks. I listen."

"Vess is an old woman clutching an old mistake. The binding was cruelty dressed up as holiness — do you know what the rite required? A life, every generation. A soul fed into the mountain so the rest of you could sleep easy. Your gentle Keeper demanded it. Your dead god, whose last light you serve — the whole altar-system was built on sacrifice." He spread his hands. "I only want what was taken from everyone. To be remembered. To be *known*. Is that so monstrous?"

"Yes," Hazel said. "When knowing costs other people their selves."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The fire in the chapel brazier had gone down to embers; the moonlight through the broken roof fell in a silver bar across the floor between them, like a drawn line.

Then Fenmar sighed, and everything about him changed. The menace drained away, and what remained was almost kind. Almost human. That was the most frightening thing yet.

"Let me show you," he said softly. "No bargain. No price. Just a gift, freely given, so you can judge me fairly. You strike me as someone who judges fairly."

"I really don't."

He raised one hand, palm up, and the moonlight bent.

* * *

It was summer.

Hazel knew it was summer because the air smelled of cut hay and hot dust, and because the temple bells of some high courtyard were ringing the noon hour in a pattern she knew *in her bones* — three quick, two slow, one long — though she had never heard it in her life.

Except she had. She was seven years old, and she was running.

Not running from. Running *to* — down a cloister walk with whitewashed walls and blue-painted beams, past novices hanging washing, past a fat orange cat asleep in a herb pot, toward the refectory where the morning bread was handed out and where, if she ran fast enough, she could get the heel-end with the burnt crust that nobody else wanted. Her feet slapped stone. Her braid — thin, brown, coming loose — whipped against her shoulder blades. Someone laughed and called a name after her:

"Hazel! Slow down, little wren!"

And she turned, breathless, grinning gap-toothed, and shouted back, "You can't catch me, Brother Anselm!" and the old monk's laugh rolled down the cloister like warm water, and the sun was on her face, and she was *happy*, wholly and stupidly happy, in a way the adult Hazel had not been since—

Since ever. Since nothing. Since the blank page where a childhood should be.

The vision held her gently, like hands cupping water. She saw the refectory, the burnt crust, the monk Anselm with flour on his sleeves. She saw the chapel where she'd first learned the offering-words — *Keeper of the last light, I am yours* — kneeling beside a girl named Sefa who used to braid flowers into Hazel's hair when the teachers weren't looking. She saw the night the bells rang wrong, the night the sky went the color of a bruise, the night the older acolytes wept and burned the records and told the children to forget, *forget, it's safer if you forget* —

And then she was standing in the ruined chapel at Hollowmere again, on her knees without remembering falling, tears cutting cold tracks down her face, the embers breathing red.

"It may be real," Fenmar said quietly, from somewhere above her. "That's the cruelty of it, isn't it? I don't need to fabricate anything. I *hold* those memories. They came to me the night your god went silent — swallowed mid-meal, as it were. Every detail true. Brother Anselm. Sefa. The burnt crust. The name they called you before you were Hazel-the-mercenary." He crouched, bringing the ice-water eyes level with hers. "I can give you all of it back. Whole. Every year the blank page ate. You would know yourself at last — completely, finally, the way no living person knows themselves. And all I ask—"

"Don't." Her voice came out ragged. "Don't say it."

"—is that you stop fighting the count. Nine stones, nine locks, and the ninth falls with the full moon whether you will it or not. Stand aside. Walk away, rich and remembered. Serve me, and be *whole*."

Hazel knelt in the wreckage of her own longing and understood, with terrible clarity, that this was the trap — not the offer but the ache beneath it. Ten years of competence built over emptiness, and here was the emptiness offered back to her filled, warm, smelling of bread and summer. He wasn't tempting her with power. He was tempting her with *herself*.

And the worst part — the part she would carry like a splinter — was that she believed him. It was real. Some frightened acolyte had chosen to forget rather than be hunted, and the forgetting had landed in the wolf's belly, and the wolf was right, it was all still there, perfect, preserved, *his*.

She got up. Her legs shook. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and when she spoke, her voice was flat and even, the mercenary's voice, the voice that expected nothing from anyone.

"When the ninth stone falls," she said, "where will you be?"

Fenmar smiled, and for once he didn't disguise it. "Beneath the crown. Where else does a king belong?"

"Then I'll see you there."

She walked out of the chapel into the moonlight, and behind her she heard him say, softly, almost fondly: *"You'll change your mind, little wren. They all do, at the end."*

* * *

Dawn found the mountains burning.

Not with fire — with light. From the ridge above Hollowmere, Hazel watched the peaks of the Thornfell range one by one go strange and pale, each summit crowned with a shrine she'd passed on her way in, and above each shrine the air itself seemed to tear, thin veils of silver peeling upward like smoke. The binding-stones. She could feel them going — not see, exactly, but feel, the way you feel a tooth loosen. Each veil that rose and unraveled took something with it, some deep structural hum in the world, and the land shuddered faintly underfoot like a sleeper turning toward waking.

By noon, riders came down the valley — not soldiers, not wolves, just villagers, hollow-eyed and placid, walking in perfect unison toward the capital, carrying tools. To help, their faces said. To build. To count.

At dusk, the eighth veil tore free and dissolved, and the howling started in the high places, one voice at first and then many, that same sustained wrong note she'd heard before, rising and holding, a chord with no resolution.

Hazel packed her gear in the dark, checked her blades, and took out the dying stranger's map one last time. In the moonlight the ink had shifted again; new lines crawled where old ones had been, and now the whole western range was marked in that cold-glowing script, converging on a single point beneath the capital — a chamber drawn deep under the crown itself, with a word beside it she could suddenly read, though the letters were in no alphabet she knew:

*THE LAST STONE.*

The ninth. Still standing. Still unbroken — the only one, and she understood in her marrow that it was the one the stranger had died carrying news of, the one Vess had whispered about, the one thing between the wolf and the world.

*Nine comes with the moon.* But the ninth stone hadn't fallen yet. Which meant the count could still be spoiled.

Hazel rolled the map, tied it down, and swung into the saddle as the full moon broke the ridgeline behind her, huge and white and merciless, flooding the valley with light bright as day.

She rode north, toward the capital, toward the crown, toward the vault beneath it — and as she rode, the wind carried down from the shrines a sound that froze the blood in her: the voices of the hollow-eyed villagers, thousands of them, chanting in unison as they climbed toward the last stone.

Counting.
