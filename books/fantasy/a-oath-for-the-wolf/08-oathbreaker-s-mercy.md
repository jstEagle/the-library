## 8. Oathbreaker's Mercy

Lady Vess had spent thirty years learning the exact weight of a lie.

She knew how much falsehood a candle flame could hide — how a single taper in a servant's corridor made shadows honest enough to pass inspection. She knew how many words of flattery it took to make a guard look elsewhere (three, if he was young; one and a title, if he was old). She knew that the most dangerous lies were not the ones you told but the ones you arranged: a misplaced ledger here, a delayed summons there, until the truth found itself standing alone in an empty room with no witnesses.

Tonight she was arranging the largest lie of her life, and its name was Hazel Okonkwo.

"You're staring," Hazel said. The mercenary sat on the edge of Vess's writing desk as though desks were made for sitting, boots muddy from the drainage culvert, turning a silver hairpin over in her fingers. It was Vess's hairpin. It was also, now, a key — or would be, once bent to the shape Vess had memorized forty years ago and never once needed until tonight.

"I'm deciding whether to tell you something," Vess said.

"Decide faster. Your king's wolves doubled their patrols at moonrise."

"They're not his wolves." Vess crossed to the window. Below, the capital of Thornfell lay quiet under a fat, bruised-colored moon, its bells hanging silent in every tower — hollowed, all of them, on Aldric's orders years ago, so that no true ringing could ever call the old rites back to life. "That's what I need to tell you. What I did."

Hazel looked up. In the candlelight her face had the stillness of a woman who had learned to take bad news like weather. "Then tell it fast."

* * *

Vess told it slowly anyway, because some confessions deserve to be walked, not run.

She told her about the binding beneath the mountain — nine stones, nine names, renewed each generation by a rite the royal house had kept for six hundred years. She told her about the cost of it: that the rite asked for memory, a tithe of the sacrificer's own past, poured into the stones to feed the chain that held Fenmar under the world. A childhood summer. A dead parent's voice. Small things, first. Then larger.

She told her about the night, eleven years ago, when King Aldric had come to her — then only the second-most ambitious person in the room — and said, *Vess, why should my grandfather's memories buy my safety? Why should I pay for a wolf I've never seen?*

And she had agreed. God forgive her, she had helped him draft the argument. They had gone before the council and dressed greed up as reform: *the old rite is barbaric, the old god is dead, the treasury of memory is better spent on living men.* They had let one binding lapse. Just one stone. Just to see.

The wolf had been waiting eleven years for exactly that.

"The stranger who gave you the map," Vess said, "was the last warden of the ninth stone. He ran because everyone else who remembered the rite was already eaten. He gave it to you because—" she hesitated, and hated herself for the hesitation, "—because your god was Fenmar's rival, and its last fragment is hidden where Fenmar can't reach without help. Inside someone. Inside you."

Hazel was very quiet. Then: "You knew. When I came to court. You knew what I was and you let me walk into that hall."

"I didn't know what you were. I knew what you *might* be. And yes." Vess met her eyes. "I let you walk in. I wanted Fenmar to see you and grow careless. He has been so careful, Hazel. Eleven years of careful eating. Careful men are hard to kill."

"And if he'd decided to eat me instead?"

"Then I'd have failed twice." Vess turned back to the window. "I'm rather hoping tonight makes up for it."

* * *

The deep vaults lay beneath the crown itself — not metaphorically, though Vess supposed it was that too. Beneath the coronation chamber, down stairs cut before Thornfell had kings, before it had a name, into rock that the first stonemasons had sworn was older than hunger.

There were three ways down. Two were watched. The third was watched too, but by a man named Corvin, who owed Vess a debt so old it had fossilized into loyalty, and who had the one weakness she could spend: he could not remember his daughter's face anymore, and it was breaking him, and Vess had promised him — promised him, knowing the price — that after tonight she would find a way to give the memory back.

She would not. That promise was part of the lie too. By morning she would no longer remember making it.

"Hold out your hand," she told Hazel, in the dark of the old cistern passage.

Hazel held it out. Vess pressed the bent hairpin into her palm and closed the mercenary's fingers around it. For a moment neither of them moved. The water dripped somewhere ahead of them, patient as arithmetic.

"When we reach the ninth gate," Vess said, "there will be a moment when I stop being useful. You'll know it because I'll ask you who you are. Don't answer kindly. Answer quickly, and go through."

"What do you mean, you'll stop being—"

"The vault eats, Hazel. That's what it's *for*. It's the wolf's larder, the place where everything Aldric's rite ever swallowed is stored — every tithe, every stolen village, every name. And it doesn't distinguish between what belongs there and what walks in carrying it." Vess smiled, and found, to her surprise, that the smile was almost easy. Thirty years of lying had at least taught her face honesty under pressure. "I have sixty-one years of memories in me. Some of them are even worth eating. I intend to spend them the way one spends a purse in a burning house — loudly, and all at once, to buy thirty feet of corridor."

"No." Hazel's voice came flat and immediate. "No. There's another way. There's always—"

"There isn't, and you know it better than anyone. You've walked the roads that remember. You've seen what a half-fed wolf leaves behind." Vess reached out and, before she could think better of it, touched the mercenary's jaw — briefly, the way one touches a thing one is about to lose. "I broke the binding. Eleven years ago I stood in a warm room with a charming man and helped him argue that the past was optional. Every village that's been erased since — the ferry-town on the Sorrel, the shepherd holds in the Gray Teeth — that ledger has my handwriting in it. Not my signature. My *handwriting*. Do you understand the difference? I didn't order it. I just made it possible, and then I stayed, and told myself staying was penance." Her breath went out slow. "It wasn't. Penance is a debt paid. This is a debt *spent*. There's a difference, and the difference is that spending hurts."

Hazel caught her wrist. Her grip was a soldier's grip, strong and practical, and her eyes in the dark were furious and wet at once. "You don't get to decide this alone. That's what you people never understand — you crown-wearers, you oath-breakers, you decide alone and call it mercy—"

"Yes," Vess said gently. "We do. And then someone like you comes along and decides better, and we get to be the lesson instead of the warning. Go on. Move. We've wasted enough light."

* * *

The descent took two hours, and Vess measured them in losses.

It began small, the way the rite had begun small for the kings. At the third landing she paused before a niche in the wall — a votive hollow, ancient, holding nothing — and found herself unable to recall what offering she had left there on her only previous visit, forty years ago. A flower? A coin? The absence sat in her mind like a tooth pulled: not painful, precisely, but whistling.

At the fifth landing she lost the smell of her mother's kitchen. She knew she had loved it. She knew it had been rosemary and woodsmoke and scorched barley. But the knowing was all that remained — a fact where a sense had been, a label on an empty jar. She said nothing. Hazel glanced back at her twice, and the second time Vess nodded briskly, *keep moving*, and the mercenary, to her credit, kept moving.

At the seventh landing, the vault noticed them.

It did not send wolves. It sent warmth. The air softened; the dripping water sounded almost like voices; and Vess felt, rising through the soles of her boots, a sensation she recognized with horror from the council chamber years ago — the feeling of being *listened to appreciatively*. Of being counted.

"It knows we're here," Hazel whispered.

"It's known since the cistern. It's choosing which of us to open first." Vess kept her voice level. Somewhere behind her eyes, a door she had not known was a door swung quietly shut. She could no longer remember the color of the dress she'd worn to her first presentation at court. She had liked that dress. She thought she had liked it. The thought was growing thin.

"Vess." Hazel stopped, turned, grabbed both her shoulders. "Look at me. What am I called?"

"Mercenary," Vess said. "Trouble. The woman with the map." And then — the vault pressing in, sweet and cold, taking as it pleased — she heard herself add, honestly, helplessly: "I don't remember your name. Did I ever know it? I think I knew it an hour ago. I think—" She frowned. Something important was leaking away and she could feel the shape of the hole it left, like a hand pressed into snow. "I brought you here for a reason. I'm certain I brought you here for a reason. Tell me the reason."

"The ninth gate," Hazel said. Her voice cracked only slightly. "You're buying me the corridor. Sixty-one years, spent loudly. Your words."

"My words." Vess smiled at her — and meant it, whatever else the vault stripped away, she would mean this — "Good girl. Listen well, then, while I still own the words."

She straightened. The passage opened ahead of them into a final gallery, and at its end stood a door of black stone banded in silver, and above the door, faint as breath on glass, burned a mark she had spent eleven years pretending not to recognize: the sigil of the silent god, the rival, the devoured. The fragment's prison. The way in.

"The oath is the chain," Vess said, fast, spending herself like coins. "Not memory — memory is what the wolf eats, memory is *currency*, it buys nothing that lasts. But a vow spoken alive, in the present tense, by a throat that means it — that the old law cannot digest. That's why your god hid its fragment in a person instead of a stone. Stones hold memories. People hold *promises*. Say it aloud down there. Whatever the original words were, say them new. Mean them bleeding. And when Fenmar offers you your past back — and he will, he will make it beautiful, he will show you a childhood with sunlight in it — you remember this gallery. Remember me forgetting why I'm standing here." Her voice frayed. "Because I am, Hazel. It's going now. I can feel it going, and I want you to know that I don't regret it, I only regret that soon I won't be able to."

"Don't," Hazel whispered.

"One more thing." Vess gripped her hands, hard, transferring everything through her palms that her mouth was losing. "Corvin. The guard at the third turn. His daughter's name was—"

The name was gone. She reached for it and found smooth stone where it had been, and heard herself laugh — a small, shocked, genuine sound — at the sheer efficiency of it.

"Well," she said. "Well. Isn't that tidy."

She turned to the black door, and put her shoulder against it, and pushed, and as the seal broke and cold air rolled out smelling of snowfall and centuries, Lady Vess of the Hollow Court smiled at the mercenary whose name she had already spent, and said, with perfect sincerity:

"Go on, whoever you are. Finish the count."

Behind her, in the dark, something vast turned over in its sleep — and opened one eye.
