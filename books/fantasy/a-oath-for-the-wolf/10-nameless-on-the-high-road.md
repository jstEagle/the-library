## 10. Nameless on the High Road

Spring came late to Thornfell that year, and it came quietly.

Hazel stood in the high gallery of the crown-vault's antechamber and watched the light move through windows that had been dark for a century. The glass was old work — temple work, someone had told her, though no one could say which temple. It caught the morning and scattered it across the floor in colors she had no names for: a green like river shallows, a gold like honey held up to the sun. Once, she thought, she would have known these names. Once there had been a girl who knelt on floors like this one and learned the liturgy of light.

That girl was not her. That girl was gone, or rather — Hazel was still learning how to say it — that girl had never been taken from her at all. She had simply been set down somewhere else in time, and Hazel had walked away from her without looking back, because looking back was not something survivors did.

"You're leaving today."

Aldric stood at the gallery's far end. He looked smaller than he had in the throne room, though he wore the same body, the same face. The crown sat on a cushion beside him rather than on his head; he had not touched it since the night the wolf went down beneath the mountain, and Hazel suspected he never would again. His hands were folded like a man at a funeral. In a way, he was at one.

"I'm leaving today," she agreed.

"The roads are open now. All of them." He said it carefully, as if the words might break. "The villages are remembering themselves. Slowly. A woman in Hollowmere woke last week and wept because she suddenly recalled her mother's face — her mother has been dead forty years, and until that morning she'd believed she'd never had one. A shepherd near Thornfell's edge remembered where he'd buried his father. The ledgers are filling in. My clerks can hardly write fast enough."

"Good," Hazel said. And it was good. It was the only thing in all of this that was unambiguously, cleanly good, and she held onto it the way a drowning woman holds a plank.

"It will take years," Aldric said. "Decades, maybe. Some things won't come back at all. Memory isn't a storehouse you can raid and restock. It's more like—" he gestured vaguely at the colored light on the floor, "—like this. You can shatter it, and you can gather the pieces, but the picture never quite fits together the way it did."

"I know something about that."

He flinched. She hadn't meant it as cruelty, but she didn't take it back either. There was a debt between them that apology couldn't settle — nine binding-stones fallen, a god devoured, a decade of silence that had been, all along, a scream swallowed whole. Aldric had broken the rite out of greed, and Fenmar had worn him like a glove for longer than anyone could count, and the man standing before her now was what remained when the glove was pulled off: diminished, honest, and hollowed in ways that would never fill.

"I'm rebuilding the bindings," he said. "Real ones, this time. No sacrifices. Vess left notes—"

Vess. The name landed in Hazel's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash and heard nothing.

"—Vess left notes," Aldric continued, steadier now, as if saying the name were a duty he intended to perform daily for the rest of his life. "She understood the old rites better than any living soul. Better than me. I intend to honor that."

"She forgot why she helped me," Hazel said. "At the end. She looked at me in the vault corridor and there was nothing behind her eyes. Just a kind old woman helping a stranger for reasons she couldn't name."

"Yes."

"And then the wolf took the rest of her when it fell. What was left."

"Yes," Aldric said again, softer. "We buried her under the rowan on the north slope. I'm told she liked rowans. I don't know how I'm told that. Someone must have known her once."

They stood in silence while the light crawled another handspan across the floor. Below them, through the gallery's open arches, Hazel could hear the city waking: cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, children running somewhere with the particular shrieking joy of children who did not yet know they lived above a chained god. The bells of the hollow court rang the hour, and for the first time since she'd entered Thornfell they sounded like bells instead of warnings.

"There's a place for you here," Aldric said. "In the new order. Whatever you want it to be. Warden of the vaults. Advisor. Anything."

"No."

He nodded as if he'd expected it. Perhaps he had. Kings learned to read people, even hollowed ones. "Then take whatever you need from the treasury."

"I need nothing." She paused. "One thing. When the villages remember — send word to a temple order, if any still exists, that kept altars to a god whose name began with an open vowel and ended in silence. If anyone remembers how to address him. If anyone remembers him at all."

"He was devoured," Aldric said gently. "You told us yourself. The fragment—"

"The fragment is chained under the mountain with the wolf. I know what I did." Her hand rose without her permission to the center of her chest, where the oath lived now, bound into her ribs like a second heartbeat. "But devoured isn't destroyed. I've seen what remains of devoured things. Ask your clerks to ask their ledgers. That's all."

"That's all," he echoed, and something in his ruined face suggested he understood better than she expected. Men who had been worn like armor knew about fragments.

* * *

She left by the east gate, on foot, because horses made her feel like a passenger in her own journey and she had spent enough of her life being carried by forces she didn't choose.

The road climbed out of the capital through terraced orchards just breaking bud, and by midday she had passed the first of the forgotten villages.

It had no name yet. The people called it nothing, or called it the-place-by-the-river, and they were patient about it, the way you're patient with a wound. But there were signs. A woman had painted a door blue because, she said, it felt right, and then three days later had burst into tears because she suddenly remembered her grandmother's door had been blue, and her grandmother had died believing herself forgotten by everyone alive. A boy of ten had started humming a song no one taught him, and an old man had dropped his rake and stood in the middle of the field listening, because it was a harvest song from a village that no ledger recorded, and the old man had known it once, before he knew nothing.

Hazel ate bread and hard cheese with them at a table under a rowan tree — rowans everywhere now, planted or remembered into being, she couldn't tell which — and when she rose to go, the woman pressed a heel of bread into her pack and asked her name.

And here was the strange thing, the thing she turned over on the road for the next two days like a stone in her pocket:

Her name had come back.

It had returned the moment the oath re-bound itself through her, riding the chain down into the dark — *Hazel Okonkwo, acolyte of the silent altar, keeper of the ninth stone* — flooding back with the force of a dam breaking. She had stood in the vault with the wolf screaming around her and her own past pouring in, every stolen year of it, the temple and the teachers and the small devout girl who had sworn herself to a god of thresholds and memory-keeping, and she had used all of it, every scrap, as fuel for the binding.

And then, when the mountain closed its mouth, she had found that the memories fit strangely. Like Aldric's shattered window-glass gathered up and pieced together. The picture was there, but the seams showed. She remembered loving the temple. She remembered the specific weight of incense smoke and the sound of her teacher's voice. But the love arrived secondhand now, reported rather than felt, the way you remember a dream's emotion after the dream has dissolved. The girl who had lived those years had loved them with her whole self, and Hazel carried the record of that love the way a courier carries a letter addressed to someone else.

So when the woman by the river asked her name, Hazel had opened her mouth and found two answers waiting.

*Hazel Okonkwo* — true, recovered, hers by right of everything that had been stolen.

And also: *no one*. Nameless on the high road. The mercenary with no god and no past worth the word, who was good at her work precisely because she expected nothing from anyone, even heaven.

She had given the woman neither answer. She had said, "Call me whatever's useful," and the woman had laughed and called her Traveler, and that had been enough, and Hazel had walked on feeling lighter than she had any right to feel.

Identity, she was beginning to understand, was not what was taken from you. The wolf had proven that beyond argument — it had eaten a decade of her life and a god entire, and here she stood anyway, intact in the ways that counted. Identity was what you chose next. It was the oath renewed, not the oath remembered. It was the road ahead, unwritten, which meant — for the first time in her life, for the first time in the life of the devout girl whose pages had been torn out — unwritten by anyone but her.

No god to serve. No king to save. No map burning cold against her skin. No dying stranger's command ringing in her ears.

Just the road, and spring, and her own two feet.

* * *

On the fourth day out of the capital, at a crossroads marked by a leaning post with no sign remaining, Hazel stopped.

Not because anything was wrong. Because the light was doing something — slanting low and gold through the birches, catching the dust of the road so the air itself seemed lit from within — and because she had decided, somewhere in the last four days, that she was allowed to stop for such things now. That noticing was not a luxury reserved for people with unbroken lives.

She stood in the gold light and took inventory, the way she'd once inventoried blades and rope and coin.

Two knives, well kept. A pack with bread from a nameless village. A leather map, creased soft with handling, its ink faded almost to nothing now that its prison lay sealed — she kept it anyway, the way you keep a letter from the dead. An oath in her chest, silent as its god, but hers, sworn freely, holding firm. And a name, folded up and put away like winter clothes in summer: *Hazel Okonkwo*, available whenever she wanted it, belonging to her whether she wore it or not.

Somewhere behind her, under the mountain, a fragment of a dead god slept in the dark beside the thing that had eaten it, and perhaps someday someone would find a way to speak to it, and perhaps not. Somewhere behind her, a diminished king wrote letters to villages that were remembering their own dead. Somewhere behind her, a grave under a rowan tree held a woman who had bought a stranger's future with her own past.

Ahead: nothing. Everything. The high road going on and on into country she'd never crossed, toward towns that had never heard of Thornfell or its wolf or its hollow bells, where no one would know her name because she hadn't chosen to give it yet.

Hazel smiled — small, private, real — and turned her face to the open road.

She chose walking.

She chose onward.

And the road, which remembered everything and demanded nothing, carried her out of Thornfell and into the world, nameless and unbroken, writing itself beneath her feet one step at a time.
