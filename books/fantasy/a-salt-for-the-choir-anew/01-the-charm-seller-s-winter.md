## 1. The Charm-Seller's Winter

The frost came up through the flagstones of Marrowgate Market before dawn, and Emrys Nystrom learned the day's weather by the ache in his knuckles before he ever saw the sky.

He had been in Greyharrow eleven years — long enough that the city's grey had worked itself into him like dye into wool. Grey stone, grey river, grey light straining through a lid of cloud that never quite lifted and never quite broke. Even the gulls were grey here, wheeling over the fish-stalls with their ragged cries, and even the bells of the Magistrate's tower rang in a key that sounded, to Emrys's ear, like a question no one wanted answered.

His stall was the third from the corner, wedged between a tanner who smelled of lime and lye and a woman who sold candles by the pound and secrets by the inch, though only one of those trades was written on her sign. His own sign read simply: *KNOTS & TRINKETS — E. NYSTROM*. Beneath it, laid out on scarred boards with the care of a man arranging an apology, sat his wares.

To any passerby they were nothing. Cord twisted into pretty loops. Tin charms stamped with dull suns. Dried herbs bundled in brown paper and tied with string, labeled *for the pot* as though he were selling soup fixings rather than feverfew and yarrow and a pinch of something rarer that had cost him three days' walk into the hills and a night spent shivering under a hawthorn waiting for the moon to be right.

But the knots were not merely pretty. Each one held a turn of luck folded into its loops the way a letter holds words folded into its creases — a charm of finding for the fisherman who kept losing nets, a knot against cramp for the laundress whose hands seized in cold water, a small tight braid of patience for a mother whose son would not stop crying at night. The tin suns carried warmth in them, faint as breath on a window. The bundles did what bundles of that herb had always done, if you knew how to cut them and what to whisper over the scissors, and the whisper was the crime.

Magic had been outlawed in Greyharrow for twenty years. Not discouraged — outlawed. The Edict of Amnesty hung in every public square, inked on vellum gone amber with age, promising the city peace in exchange for purity: no hedge-craft, no hearth-spells, no whispered words over birthing beds or burial shrouds. The Magistrate's hunters enforced it with ledgers and iron and a thoroughness that had outlasted everyone who remembered why the bargain had been struck in the first place. Emrys had been fourteen when the edict went up. He had been his mistress's apprentice then, her hands still soft with learning, and he had run — south along the river road with a satchel of stolen grimoire pages and a heart full of the particular cowardice that masquerades, for years afterward, as prudence.

Now he sold trinkets. And beneath the trinkets, quietly, at prices that barely fed him, he sold the other thing, because a man can starve honestly for only so long before he begins to lie with his hands.

"Two coppers for the blue one," he told the fisherman's wife, who came every seventh morning and never asked why the blue knot worked better than the red. "It's just cord."

"It's just cord," she agreed, pressing coins into his palm with the solemnity of a woman taking communion. Her fingers were cracked from brine. She tucked the knot inside her mitten and walked away standing a little straighter, and Emrys watched her go and felt the familiar double weight of it — the small warm satisfaction of craft done well, and beneath it, cold as the flagstones, the knowledge that every knot he sold was a small treason, and every treason made the rope around his neck a little longer.

The market filled around him by degrees, the way water fills a lock. Voices layering. Fish-gut shine on the cobbles. A fiddler tuning somewhere out of sight, testing the same four notes over and over as though hoping one of them would improve. Smoke from the chestnut cart, sweet and bitter at once. Emrys wrapped his hands around a cup of cider gone lukewarm and let the noise wash over him, and for a moment — a moment he would remember later, when remembering was all he had — he let himself imagine that this was enough. A stall. A trade. A life measured out in coppers and small mercies, hidden so thoroughly that even he sometimes lost track of where the hiding ended and he began.

Then the bells changed.

It was not a sound so much as a shift in the air, the way a field goes quiet before lightning. The fiddler stopped mid-note. The chestnut-seller's lad froze with his scoop halfway to the coals. Down the length of Marrowgate, heads turned in unison toward the tower, and Emrys felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world, because he knew that peal. Everyone knew that peal. Three strikes, a pause, three more — the hunters' call, and it meant they were already in the district, already moving, and the pause between the third strike and the fourth was the width of your whole life in which to decide what to do about it.

He did not decide. His hands decided for him, hands trained by eleven years of raids and near-misses, hands that swept the herb-bundles into their hiding drawer and dropped the false bottom over them while his eyes scanned the crowd for grey cloaks. The knots stayed out — knots were innocent, knots were cord — but the tin suns he swept into his apron pocket, all nine of them, and their borrowed warmth pressed against his thigh like small accusing hearts.

They came from the east end of the market, which was wrong, because they always came from the west, and Emrys understood in one sick flash that someone had talked. There was always someone. The tanner's boy, maybe, who'd watched too closely last month. The candle-woman, hedging her bets with the Magistrate the way other people hedged with saints. It didn't matter who. What mattered was the grey cloaks moving down the row now, unhurried, terrible in their unhurriedness, six of them flanking a figure in black with a ledger chained to her wrist.

Captain Ilsa Vane. He knew her by reputation the way children know wolves — by shape, by silence, by the way other people went quiet around the idea of her. She was said to have closed forty-one workshops in the Tanners' Quarter alone. She was said to read handwriting the way priests read scripture, divining guilt from the angle of a descender. She was walking toward his stall, and she was looking at the stalls ahead of his, working her way down the row like a woman reading a page.

Emrys did the thing he had practiced in mirrors until it bored him: he became boring. He slouched. He let his face empty of everything but the mild resentment of a tradesman interrupted. When Vane reached the candle-woman's stall two down from his, he began loudly restacking his cord, muttering about custom and wasted mornings, performing the small theater of a man with nothing to hide and a grievance about it.

Vane paused at the candle stall. She did not speak. She picked up one of the woman's tallow dips, turned it in her gloved fingers, and set it down again, and the candle-woman's face went the color of her own wax. Then the captain's gaze slid sideways — one heartbeat, two — and passed over Emrys the way winter passes over a stone.

It moved on. Down the row. Toward the fishmongers.

Emrys breathed. That was his mistake, breathing, because in the same moment a hunter at the rear of the party — young, eager, carrying his authority like a borrowed coat — stopped at the tanner's stall beside Emrys's own and knocked over a rack of hides reaching for something behind it, and the hides fell across Emrys's table, and when Emrys shoved them back off, the false-bottomed drawer stood open half an inch, and the young hunter's eyes, quick as a rat's, caught the gleam of dried yarrow through the gap.

"What's in there?"

"Bundles." Emrys heard his own voice come out steady and marveled at it from a great distance. "For the pot. Soup herbs. You want to smell one? Smells like my grandmother's attic."

The hunter looked at him. Emrys looked back with the flat, patient stare of the utterly mundane, and for three full seconds the morning balanced on a knife-edge finer than any he had ever tempered steel on. Somewhere behind the hunter, Captain Vane had stopped walking. She had not turned around. But she had stopped, the way a hawk stops when a field mouse moves wrong in the grass.

"Open it," the hunter said.

And Emrys — weary, cold-fingered, eleven years tired — understood with perfect clarity that the morning had tipped, and that whatever happened next would happen fast. So he did the only thing left in his repertoire, the trick his mistress had taught him first of all, before knots, before charms, before anything: misdirection, the oldest magic, the one that needs no law to ban it because it lives in the watcher, not the worker.

He sneezed. Enormously. Twice. And in the violence of the second sneeze he knocked his own table over — cords, tins, the works, clattering across the cobbles in a spill of honest junk — and in the half-second of every eye in earshot going to the noise, he palmed the yarrow bundle out of the drawer, crushed it in his fist, and let the wind take the dust.

It wasn't much of a spell. It wasn't a spell at all, really — just dried yarrow and a scrap of intent, the magical equivalent of a shrug. But yarrow scattered on wind does what yarrow does: it makes eyes water and noses itch and tempers fray, and in the chorus of sneezing that erupted down the market row, Emrys Nystrom stepped backward out of his own life.

He left the stall standing. He left the stock, the sign, the coppers in the dish, the whole careful architecture of eleven years. He pulled his cap low and joined the stampede of shoppers fleeing the raid — because everyone flees a raid, guilty or innocent; fleeing is the most legal thing a person can do — and he walked, fast but not running, out of Marrowgate Market and into the warren of tenement streets beyond, where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators and the light never quite reached the ground.

Behind him, faint through the crooked streets, he heard the crash of his table being overturned properly, officially, by men with warrants. He heard the candle-woman start to cry. He did not look back. Looking back was how they caught you; some lessons the body learns so deep the mind need never trouble itself.

* * *

His room was on the top floor of a tenement on Cindervine Lane, one room, one window, one chair, and a hearth he was legally permitted to use for cooking and illegally used for everything else. He climbed to it by the outside stair because the inside stair had eyes, and he locked the door behind him with a lock he had charmed himself, a lock that would rattle and groan like a dying man if anyone but him touched it.

Then he sat down on the floor, because the chair seemed suddenly too dignified, and he put his head in his hands, and he shook.

Not with fear, exactly. Fear he knew the way he knew his own teeth. This was something older and more corrosive — the shaking of a man who has just watched the sum of his life get kicked apart in a market square and found, examining the wreckage from a distance, that it amounted to very little. Nine tin suns lost. Eleven years of goodwill among the fishwives and laundresses, burned in an afternoon because one boy knocked over one rack of hides. A name — E. NYSTROM — that would be chalked into a ledger tonight, in a column headed *persons of interest*, and Captain Vane did not strike names from columns; she grew them, patiently, like a gardener of gallows.

"They'll come," he said aloud, to the empty room, because saying it made it ordinary. "In a week. Maybe less. They'll knock on doors down Cindervine Lane with the ledger open, and someone will point, because someone always points."

The hearth was cold. He hadn't lit it that morning — no time, the market calling — and now the room's chill settled into him like a tenant with a lease. He should pack. He had a go-bag sewn into the mattress lining, had had it for eleven years, refreshed twice a year like a bandage on a wound that never healed: dry clothes, a knife, thirty crowns sewn into the hem, and a oilskin packet of pages he had stolen from his mistress's cottage when he ran, pages he had never once dared read past the first line because the first line was in her handwriting and her handwriting could still, after all these years, make him feel fourteen and ashamed.

He should pack the bag. He should walk out of Greyharrow tonight, north or south, anywhere, and begin again in some other grey town with some other grey market, selling cord to strangers until the hunters found him there too. That was the sensible thing. That was the thing he had always done, the whole architecture of his adulthood: run early, run quiet, run small.

Instead he sat on the cold floorboards as the light failed, and he thought about Maren Nystrom — no relation, whatever the shared surname meant, and it had meant everything once — and about the last thing she had said to him at the garden gate twenty years ago, when the edict was fresh and the hunters were burning hedge-cottages in the hills and he had told her he was leaving.

"You'll be safe," she had said, and her voice had been strange, flat as frost on glass, as though she were reciting something she'd bought rather than something she felt. "That's the whole point of what I've done. You'll be safe, Emrys. All of you will be safe." And she had turned back to her herbs in the dark, and he had run, and he had spent eleven years being exactly as safe as she had promised, and it had felt, every single day of it, like drowning slowly in shallow water.

Safe. Hidden. Small. A life spent folded up so tightly it fit in a drawer with a false bottom.

Outside, the first flakes of the season's snow were coming down through the lamplight — early this year, weeks early, fat and slow and silent, settling on Cindervine Lane like ash settling after a fire no one had admitted to. Emrys watched them through the warped glass and felt the city holding its breath around him, ten thousand chimneys, ten thousand shuttered windows, a whole population practicing the same art he had mastered: the art of being no one, thoroughly, until the winter passed.

A fist hammered on the door below. Then another door. Then voices, several, moving up the inside stair with the unhurried tread of men reading names off a list.

Emrys was out the window and onto the roof tiles before the knocking reached the second floor, the go-bag over his shoulder, the stolen pages against his ribs, his breath smoking in the dark. Below him, through the gap between chimneys, he could see the street: hunters in the snowfall, lanterns swinging, and at their head a figure in black with a ledger chained to her wrist, looking up at the tenement windows with the patient attention of a woman who has all winter and knows it.

She could not see him. He was certain she could not see him.

She smiled anyway — a small, precise movement of the mouth, visible even at that distance, even in the falling snow — and turned to give some order to her men, and Emrys Nystrom fled across the rooftops of Greyharrow with his heart hammering out the rhythm of the hunters' bell: three strikes, a pause, three more, and in the pause, the width of a life in which to decide what he was going to become.
