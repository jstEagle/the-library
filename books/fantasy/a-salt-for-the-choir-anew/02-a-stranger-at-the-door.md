## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at the hour when Greyharrow's lamps burned lowest and its watchers drank their second cup of something warming and illegal.

Emrys knew the sound of a knock the way a hare knows the sound of a snare closing — by what it promised. Three raps, evenly spaced: a neighbor wanting tallow or company. A fist, hard and repeated: the Magistrate's men, who never learned to knock softly because they had never needed to. And then there was this knock, which was barely a knock at all — a slurred thump, as of someone leaning their whole weight against the wood and letting gravity do the asking.

He sat very still in his chair with his mending in his lap, a luck-knot half-tied around a river stone, and listened to the building breathe around him. The tenement on Candlemaker's Row was old enough to have opinions. It groaned when the wind came off the harbor; it ticked and settled in the frost like an old man's knees. It had never once, in the four years Emrys had lived inside its walls, made a sound that meant *someone is dying on my threshold*.

But he heard it anyway, underneath the thump. A wet, rattling breath, drawn through teeth clenched against pain.

He should not open the door. This was the first law of his life now, older than any his mistress had taught him: *keep small, keep shut, keep nothing worth finding.* He was a seller of trinkets, a mender of kettles, a man whose hands were clever but whose name was clean. The hunters had swept the market quarter only three days past. Captain Vane's people did not sweep twice in one week without reason.

The breath rattled again, thinner this time. Closer to stopping than to starting.

Emrys set down the knot, untied it out of habit — never leave a working half-finished where a searching eye might find it — and went to the door.

* * *

The man was already sliding down when Emrys opened it, and only Emrys's quick grab at his coat kept him from folding into the stairwell. He was heavy in the way of tall men gone gaunt, all bone and sinew under wool that had once been good and was now stiff with frost. His beard was crusted white. Not grey — *white*, rimed, as though he had walked through a blizzard that no one else in the city had seen, and the cold had followed him in like a dog.

"Inside," Emrys said, which was stupid, which broke every law he lived by, but the man's boots were black at the toes and everyone in Greyharrow knew what black frost-toes meant, and there are limits to what even a careful coward can step over.

He hauled him across the threshold and got him onto the floor by the little iron stove, and the door he shut behind them with a hand that shook more than he liked.

Up close the stranger was worse. Frostbite had taken his ears and was working on his cheeks, the flesh gone waxy and strange. His lips were split. His eyes, when they found Emrys's face, were the color of weak tea left too long, and they were *urgent* in a way that frost could not touch.

"You're him," the man said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "Nystrom's boy."

The words went through Emrys like a splinter driven under a nail. Nobody called him that. Nobody living called him that. He had buried the name Nystrom twenty years ago along with everything else from before — the cottage above the fen road, the smell of beeswax and dried yarrow, the woman with ink-stained fingers who had taught him to braid wind into cord and then, one morning, simply wasn't there anymore.

"You've mistaken me," Emrys said. "I'm a tinker. I mend—"

"I watched you sell your knots." The man coughed, and the cough had blood in it somewhere deep; Emrys could hear the place it came from. "Market day. The red-haired woman bought a fever-charm off you for her daughter and you told her to hang it over the east window and she laughed at you because it looked like nothing, and you said—" he drew a shuddering breath, "—you said *the best magic looks like nothing, that's how you know it's good.*"

Emrys crouched there, frozen worse than the stranger.

That was Maren's phrase. That was Maren's *exact* phrase, worn smooth by use, passed from her mouth to his in the years when his whole world was the width of her kitchen table. He had said it ten thousand times since without thinking of her at all, the way you say your own name. Hearing it come back to him out of a dying stranger's mouth was like hearing his own voice call to him from a grave.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

"Aldous Crane." The man tried to sit up and failed, and seemed to decide it didn't matter. "I was a clerk. Before. Before I understood what the ledgers were *for*." His tea-colored eyes fixed on Emrys with terrible focus, the focus of a man rationing what remained of himself. "Listen to me. There isn't time to make you believe anything. So I'll just say it, and you can disbelieve it after I'm dead, if you like."

"That's morbid."

"I'm past morbid. I'm past everything but the message." His hand crawled across the floorboards, slow as a spider in winter, and closed on Emrys's wrist with a grip that had surprising heat in it, a fever-heat, a burning-from-inside heat. "Maren Nystrom is alive."

The stove ticked. Wind worried the eaves. Somewhere below, the Widow Sarn's cat complained at the cold.

"She sold her soul," Crane said. "Twenty years ago. To the Court Beneath the Mountain. And she is still alive, and she is still *there*, and she has been there every single day that you have spent selling fever-charms to red-haired women and telling yourself she abandoned you."

Emrys heard himself say, very calmly, in the voice he used for haggling: "You're mad. Or you're a hunter's bait. They've used grief before. They've used—"

"The amnesty." Crane's grip tightened. "Ask yourself the question you've never let yourself ask, boy. Why does Greyharrow outlaw magic? Every city in the world has its hedge-witches, its charm-sellers, its cunning folk in the fens. But Greyharrow — Greyharrow alone wrote it into iron law, hunted it with fire and ledger, twenty years ago, all at once, out of nowhere. Ask *why*."

"Because the Magistrate—"

"The Magistrate was paid." Crane's breath was failing between phrases now; he spoke the way a man walks uphill in deep snow, pausing at each sentence to gather strength. "Not in coin. In something better. In *safety*. One soul, freely given, in exchange for the city's protection — and the price of the protection was that no other craft would ever be practiced openly within the walls again. Her bargain. Her terms. She wrote the law herself, boy, in exchange for herself. Every witch-hunter's warrant in this city bears the shape of her signature, whether the Magistrate knows it or not."

The room tilted. Emrys put a hand on the floorboards and the floorboards seemed further away than they should be.

Because he had done the arithmetic long ago, in the bitter years, and hated himself for doing it. If she had been taken — if the hunters had caught her — there would have been a trial, a pyre, a public thing, a *name*. Greyharrow burned its witches loudly; it was the one theater the Magistrate permitted itself. But Maren Nystrom had vanished without a ripple, without a warrant, without so much as a confiscated bundle of herbs. As though the law had parted around her like water around a stone.

As though the law had been built *around* her absence.

"No," Emrys said. It came out very small.

"She's in the choir," Crane said. "They sing. The ones who are sold. They sing the King's mass, and they don't stop, and they don't sleep, and they don't remember why they're singing. Twenty years, boy. She has been singing for twenty years while you tied your little knots and flinched at every knock."

Emrys's hands had gone numb. He noticed it distantly, the way one notices weather in another country. "Why," he managed. "Why are you telling me. Why me. Why not — anyone. A priest. The Magistrate. Someone who *matters*."

Crane smiled, and the smile cracked the frozen skin of his lips, and a bead of blood welled there, shockingly red, the warmest thing in the room.

"Because of this."

With fingers that moved like something underwater he worked at the front of his coat, at an inner pocket sewn against the lining, and drew out a roll of oilcloth tied with waxed string. Inside the oilcloth — Emrys felt the weight of it before he saw it, felt it the way he felt a charged charm at forty paces, a hum in the back teeth — was a scroll. Heavy paper. Dense with ink. Sealed at its heart with a disc of dark wax stamped with a sigil Emrys did not know and yet recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face in a crowd twenty years on: a mountain inverted, its peak driven downward into darkness, roots upward like a drowned tree.

A map. Even rolled, even unopened, Emrys could feel that it was a map — roads, distances, a geometry that pulled at the eye.

"It shows the way down," Crane said. "Through the bone-roads. Under the mountain, to the court. I made it myself, over eleven years, from the accounts of everyone who ever went down and came partway back. It cost me—" he coughed again, wetter, "—it cost me most of what I had. My wife's soul is in the choir too. Sold before mine was ever at risk. Before my daughter was old enough to lose her. I went to buy her back. That was the errand. That was always the errand."

"And you didn't go down."

"I got as far as the tollmen." Something moved behind his eyes that might have been shame, or might have been terror wearing shame's coat. "They don't take coin, boy. They don't take blood. They take *names*, and memories, and years, and other things I won't describe to a man who still sleeps soundly. I stood at the first gate and I counted what I'd have left at the end, if I succeeded, and I found I couldn't do it. I turned around." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "I am the only man alive who knows the road and lacks the courage to walk it. Remember that about me, when you're tempted to be kind."

Emrys took the map. He hadn't decided to; his hands simply did it, the way they tied knots, the way they had always known things a beat before his mind caught up. The oilcloth was warm. Nothing that had traveled inside a frozen man's coat had any right to be warm.

"Why give it to me?"

"Because you're hers." Crane sagged back against the wall, and the urgency in him was guttering now like a candle in a draft, and he seemed to know it, and to be spending his last coins quickly. "Her apprentice. Her craft is in your hands — I saw it in the market, the way you handle a working, the way you *apologize* to the thread before you cut it. She taught you that. Only she apologizes to thread." A ghost of the smile again. "And because the debt comes due. Solstice. The longest night. The court renews its contracts at solstice, and the King grows hungry before a renewal, and this year — this year the hunger is different. I've read the signs. The winters are coming earlier. The snow doesn't melt. He's reaching past the walls of his bargain, past the terms she set. Whatever she bought with her soul, it's running out. And when it runs out—"

He stopped. Not for breath. For emphasis, spending his final clarity like a miser's last gold piece.

"—when it runs out, the amnesty dies with it. And every charm-seller, every hedge-witch, every cunning man and herb-wife in Greyharrow goes on the pyre in a single season, and the King gets a city's worth of souls instead of one. Unless someone goes down there before solstice and unmakes the bargain at its root."

The stove ticked. The wind sang in the chimney with a note Emrys had never noticed before, a thin high note, almost like a voice holding one long vowel.

"Solstice," Emrys repeated dumbly. "That's — that's nineteen days."

"Eighteen," said Crane, "and a bit."

* * *

He died an hour before dawn, and it was not dramatic, and Emrys thought afterward that this was somehow the worst part — that a man could carry a message across half a frozen country and up six flights of a tenement and deliver it entire, and then simply stop, mid-sentence, his head tipping against the wall as though he'd only meant to rest his eyes.

Emrys sat beside him until the light came grey through the shutters, and did not sleep, and did not weep, though his eyes burned as though he might. He went through the man's pockets with the detached thoroughness Maren had drilled into him — *grief is for later; first learn what you hold* — and found little: a tin locket with a miniature portrait of a girl of perhaps twelve, painted twenty years ago and carried until the paint wore soft; three coppers; a wedding ring on a cord around his neck, sized for a woman's hand.

A daughter, then. Alive, presumably. Who would wake one morning to learn her father had died in a stranger's rooms chasing ghosts beneath a mountain.

*Like mistress, like apprentice,* whispered something sour in the back of Emrys's skull. *Runaways, both of us.*

He laid Crane out decently on his own bed, folded the big frost-stiffened hands, and covered the ruined face with a sheet. Then he stood in the middle of his small clean hidden life — the mending basket, the false-bottomed trunk, the shelf of honest kettles — and unrolled the map on the floor.

It was beautiful. That was his first coherent thought, kneeling over it like a man at a graveside. Eleven years of work lay in those lines: the bone-roads rendered in fine brown ink, mile-marked, annotated in two hands — Crane's cramped clerk's script, and beneath it, here and there, corrections and additions in a hand Emrys knew better than his own. Slanted. Confident. The letters that had written *the best magic looks like nothing* across the whole architecture of his life.

She had annotated the map. Which meant she had seen it, or dreamed it, or sent knowledge up through whatever hollow channel still connected the choir to the world. Which meant some part of her, somewhere under all that frost, was *reaching*.

And then he saw the blank.

In the heart of the map, where the roads converged — where every line, every annotation, every careful measured mile ran inward toward a single destination — the paper showed nothing at all. Not empty space. *Nothing.* He tilted it toward the lamp and the lamplight slid off it strangely, refusing to settle, and the region where the Court Beneath the Mountain must surely lie was simply absent, as though the map itself had been forbidden to know its own ending.

At the margin of the blank, in Maren's hand, in letters small enough to be missed, someone had written four words:

*Bring salt. Bring nothing else.*

Outside, far earlier than it should, the snow began to fall on Greyharrow — the first true snow in decades, fat and silent and wrong, falling straight down through windless air as though the sky itself were listening.

Emrys knelt on his floor with a dead man's map and his mistress's handwriting, and felt the last twenty years of his carefully buried life shift, groan, and begin to collapse inward like a mine shaft.

Eighteen days. And a bit.
