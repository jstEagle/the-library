## 3. What the Map Refuses to Show

Emrys did not sleep.

He sat at his tenement table with the dead man's map unrolled before him, weighted at the corners with a candle stub, a chipped cup, a river stone he'd once charmed for luck and never had the heart to sell, and the stranger's own boot-knife, which Emrys had taken from the body because leaving it seemed worse than keeping it. The snow had stopped an hour past midnight. Now the city made the particular silence it made after snowfall — a hush like a held breath, like the whole of Greyharrow leaning in to listen.

The map was drawn on skin.

He had known it since the moment he'd turned it over and seen the pores. Not vellum, not the waxed linen the cartographers on Lantern Row used for their licensed charts of the harbor and the coal roads. Skin, cured thin and pale as onion paper, stitched at the edges with sinew gone yellow with age. Someone had loved this map enough to carry it against their heart for a long time, or someone had been made into it without being asked. Emrys could not decide which possibility sickened him more, and so he held both, the way he held everything now, in the same numb hand.

The ink was iron-gall, browned to the color of dried blood, and it showed roads.

Not roads above. Roads *beneath*. They crawled across the skin-page like veins seen through a wrist — branching, forking, rejoining, some drawn in confident strokes and some in lines so faint they might have been traced from memory, or from fever. A mountain dominated the upper third of the map, rendered in fine hatching, its roots torn open to display what lived inside it: galleries, stairways, a river drawn in a shivering line that someone had labeled, in a cramped and careful hand, *the water that remembers*. Roads spilled out of the mountain's roots and ran down and off the edges of the skin, running, Emrys realized with a slow cold turn of his stomach, *under Greyharrow*. Under the market quarter. Under his own street. Under, very possibly, this very room.

He had lived his whole life above roads he'd never known existed. He had walked to market ten thousand times over whatever lay beneath, and the city had never once creaked to tell him.

*The debt comes due at solstice.*

The dead man's words sat in him like a swallowed stone. Solstice was nineteen days away.

He lit the candle properly, cupped his hands around the small heat, and made himself look at the margins.

There was writing there — he'd seen it by the light of the stranger's dying lantern, glimpsed it as the man pressed the map into his hands, but grief and terror had blurred it. Now, in the honest yellow of a tallow flame, he saw that the margins were crowded. Notations in three hands, maybe four: some in the iron-gall brown, some in a graphite that had smudged to shadow, some in a red-brown that Emrys refused, for as long as he could, to identify as anything but rust. And one hand above all the others — small, slanted leftward, the letters leaning like women whispering behind a fan — that he knew.

That he would have known anywhere. That he had spent ten years of his childhood copying out, letter by patient letter, until his own hand had learned its rhythm the way a riverbed learns the river.

Maren Nystrom's hand.

His mistress's handwriting was in the margins of a dead stranger's map to the Court Beneath the Mountain, and Emrys sat very still for a long time, breathing the tallow smoke, while something in his chest that had been frozen for twenty years began, painfully, to thaw — and what thawed first was not love. It was anger. Anger came first, the way water comes first through a dam, before anything kinder can get through.

*You told me you had no family,* he thought at her across the years. *You told me the road behind you was burned. You taught me that the first rule of the craft was to leave no thread trailing back to what you loved, and then you —*

Then you what?

He unrolled the map fully and began to read.

* * *

The margins were a conversation between the dead and the disappeared.

The oldest hand — the confident one, the cartographer's, the one that had drawn the roads themselves — belonged, Emrys decided, to whoever had flayed or been flayed for this page. That hand wrote in a tidy merchant's script: distances, elevations, the locations of the tollmen's stations. *Toll at the third fork: one name, freely given.* *The lichen eats what you carry in your head. Travel light.* *Do not drink the remembering water unless you mean to be remembered.*

The second hand was frantic, cramped, the letters of a man writing by bad light in bad conditions — the dead stranger, Emrys understood, or the dead stranger's father, or both, the same obsession passed down like a debt. This hand had annotated the tolls with costs. *Third fork — paid. Lost: the smell of my mother's kitchen.* *Seventh gallery — paid. Lost: the year of my wedding.* And once, pressed so hard the skin-page had scarred beneath it: *IT DOES NOT GIVE BACK. IT ONLY TAKES. WHOEVER TOLD ME THIS WAS A BARGAIN LIED.*

And the third hand. The small slanted hand. Maren's hand.

Her notes were different from all the others. They were not warnings and they were not costs. They were corrections.

Where the cartographer had drawn a stair, she had written *no — the stair moves; it is a tongue, and the mountain swallows with it.* Where the frantic hand had recorded a toll, she had written beneath it, in letters fine as frost-fern: *there is a way past the tollman if you come at the hour between — but you must carry nothing he can weigh, not even hope, and I could not, and so I paid.* Where the map blanked — and the map did blank, Emrys saw now, had seen it from the first and refused to understand it — where the mountain's deep heart should have been, where the court itself must lie, the skin-page showed nothing at all. Not empty parchment. Not smudged ink. The skin simply went smooth and pale and featureless, like the palm of a hand, like the face of someone who has decided to tell you nothing.

Maren had written in that blankness anyway. Her words sat on it the way writing sits on water — legible for a moment, then fading, then surfacing again somewhere else on the page, so that her notes migrated across the map like birds over a sea. Emrys watched one sentence travel the length of the mountain while he held his breath: *the court cannot be mapped because it does not consent to be known. It is not a place. It is a contract, and contracts have no shape, only terms.*

He read her scattered terms until the candle guttered, relit it, and read again. And slowly, in the way of a man wading into cold water inch by inch because leaping would stop his heart, he assembled what she had tried to tell him across twenty years of silence and one migrating sentence at a time.

The Court Beneath the Mountain was old. Older than Greyharrow. Older, she wrote, than the word *old*. It had been a king's hall once, then a king's grave, then something that kept the shape of both the way a glove keeps the shape of a hand. Its power was not power as Emrys understood it — not the hedge-craft he'd been beaten for, not the fever-charms and luck-knots and small warm mercies of his stolen trade. Its power was *holding*. It held things. Debts. Souls. Seasons. It held the winter the way a miser holds a coin, and it had been holding this region's winter for a very long time, letting it out in small grudging increments, taking it back with interest.

And twenty years ago — here Emrys's hands began to shake, and he set them flat on the table, on the dead man's skin, and read on — twenty years ago, the plague had come down out of the fells into Greyharrow, and it had not come alone.

He knew this part. He had lived this part. He had been nine years old, and he had watched the death-carts run at all hours, and he had watched the Magistrate's men nail proclamations to the church doors declaring that the plague was the work of witches, that hedge-craft was poison, that any soul found practicing the old arts would be tried and burned and their houses salted. He had watched his mistress stand in the market square and argue law with a magistrate until her voice gave out, and he had watched her lose. He had watched the first pyre built in Tanner's Field. He had run that night, a coward of nine, and he had never stopped running, and he had told himself for twenty years that Maren Nystrom had died in the purges with the rest, and he had built his whole small hidden life on the grief of that.

The margins told him she had not died.

The margins told him what she had done instead, in the winter the pyres were built, in the hour between, carrying nothing the tollman could weigh, not even hope.

*The court buys,* she had written, in her migrating frost-fine hand. *It does not steal; stealing is beneath it, or perhaps above it. It buys. And what it buys dearest is a soul freely offered, for a soul freely offered can be held without breaking, the way a door held open is held longer than a door kicked in.*

*I offered mine.*

Emrys read the sentence eleven times. The candle burned down. The snow-silence outside deepened. Somewhere below the floor, below the street, below the coal-cellars and the buried streams, the roads on the map ran on toward a court that was not a place but a contract, and in that court, if the margins were true, a woman with small slanted handwriting had been singing in a choir of the hollow for twenty years.

*I offered mine,* she had written, *and I set the price myself, which is the only power the seller keeps. I asked for the city. I asked that Greyharrow be spared the purge — not the plague; the plague was never theirs to give; but the burning, the hunting, the law that makes a fever-charm a hanging crime. I asked that the witches of Greyharrow be left to live small and hidden and afraid, but alive. And the court granted it, because the court loves a bargain, and because what I asked for was so small to it and so vast to me.*

*Here is what I did not understand, boy, and what you must: the court grants wishes in the shape of wounds. It did not strike the purges down. It made them permanent. It wrote my bargain into law, into the Magistrate's own mouth, into the iron of the city. The outlawing of magic in Greyharrow is not the Magistrate's law. It is my flesh. Every hunter's warrant is drawn on my soul. Every charm-seller who runs is running from me. I bought my people's lives and paid for them with their freedom, and the interest compounds, and the debt comes due at solstice, and I have had twenty years to learn what I should have known at the tollman's fork:*

*Nothing given to the court is ever given. Everything is lent, and lent at ruinous rate, and the court always, always collects.*

Emrys sat back. The chair creaked. His breath came out of him in a long plume in the freezing room, and for a while he could not have said whether he was weeping, because his face was so cold that tears would have frozen before they announced themselves.

* * *

Betrayal, he discovered, is not a stable substance. It curdles.

For the first hour he hated her. He hated her cleanly, thoroughly, with the clean thoroughness of a child — because that was what he became, sitting in the dark with her handwriting migrating across a dead man's skin: a nine-year-old boy again, standing in a market square, watching the only person in the world who mattered argue law with iron and lose, and then — and this was the wound, this was the salt in it — *not* lose. Not die. Not burn in Tanner's Field with the others while he ran. She had *won*. She had won in the one way the court permits, the way that costs everything and buys less than nothing, and she had done it without telling him, without leaving him so much as a thread, because the first rule of the craft was to leave no thread trailing back to what you loved.

He had thought that rule was wisdom. It was not wisdom. It was a woman planning her own disappearance and dressing the plan as a lesson.

*You taught me to hide,* he said to her across the years, and his voice cracked on it in the empty room. *You taught me to be small. Twenty years I have sold luck-knots out of a stall like a man selling buttons, and flinched at every red cloak, and told myself it was survival, and it was — it was your bargain. I have been living inside your wound. I thought it was my life.*

The hatred held for an hour. Maybe less.

And then it curdled, as it had to, because Emrys had spent his whole life reading the fine print of other people's desperation — it was half his trade, reading what a customer's hands said beneath what their mouth said — and the fine print of Maren's margins said what her fury would not. She had been alone. She had been nine-and-forty and alone in a city building pyres, with an apprentice too young to carry the truth and no one else at all, and she had gone down under the mountain in the hour between carrying nothing, not even hope, and she had set her price, and she had known even then — the margins said so, in the last note, the one that had migrated to the very edge of the skin, half off the page, as if she had written it reaching for a door — she had known even then that the bargain was a wound, and she had paid it anyway, because the pyres were already built, and the children of Greyharrow were already being named in the Magistrate's ledgers, and someone had to stand in the fork of that road and choose which horror to purchase.

She had chosen. She had chosen badly, or the only way it could be chosen, and she had carried the knowing alone for twenty years in a choir of the hollow, singing without self, while the city she'd bought lived small and hidden and afraid and called it peace.

*And I called it cowardice,* Emrys thought. *I built a whole life on the story that she abandoned me. I made her death a grievance and wore it like a charm against feeling anything.*

The guilt came in behind the hatred the way the flood comes in behind the broken dam, and it was worse, because guilt was earned. He had run. That was the plain fact under all his fine feelings. A nine-year-old could be forgiven for running. A twenty-nine-year-old who had spent two decades not looking, not asking, not spending one silver coin of his small cunning on the question of what had become of Maren Nystrom — that man had chosen the story that let him sleep, and the story had been a lie, and the lie had been comfortable, and comfort, the court's margins seemed to say, was always bought from someone.

The candle drowned in its own wax. Emrys did not relight it. He sat in the dark with the map, and the map's blank heart sat with him, and the migrating writing surfaced one last time, faint as breath on glass, right at the edge of the page where her final note had come to rest:

*If anyone ever reads this: the debt comes due at solstice, and the court will come to collect the city entire, because that was always in the terms, in the fine print, in the part I could not read. What was bought for a soul can be reclaimed only by a soul. I am sorry. I am so sorry. Do not come for me. Come for the city. And whatever you carry into the mountain, carry nothing it can weigh.*

Emrys read it by the light of the snow through the window, which was a blue light, a light with no warmth in it, and when he looked up from the page he saw that the frost on the glass had grown while he read — grown in patterns, in letters almost, in fine slanted ferns that leaned like women whispering behind a fan — and that the cold in the room had a direction now, and the direction was down.

Beneath the floor. Beneath the street. Nineteen days.

He was still sitting there, still not moving, when the knocking began at his door — three slow blows, deliberate as a magistrate's seal, and a voice through the wood, a woman's voice, cold and even as the frost:

"Emrys Nystrom. Open in the name of the Magistrate's hunt. We know the map came to this district, and we know whose hand wrote its margins — and we have a ledger page, twenty years old, with your name already in it."
