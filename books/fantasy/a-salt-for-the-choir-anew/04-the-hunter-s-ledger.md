## 4. The Hunter's Ledger

The snow had not yet come to Greyharrow, but Captain Ilsa Vane could smell it in the air the way a butcher smells blood before the animal is even led up the ramp — something metallic waiting behind the grey of the sky, patient, owed. She stood over the dead man in the alley off Cordwainer's Row and let the smell of the coming winter settle into her lungs like a promise she intended to keep.

He lay on his back with his arms flung wide, as if he had been mid-embrace when death took him, or mid-surrender. Frost had gotten into his beard first, whitening it from the chin upward, so that he seemed to be aging in reverse, the ice erasing him from the bottom of his face toward his eyes. His eyes were still open. They were brown, ordinary, and fixed on the strip of sky above the alley with an expression Vane had learned to read across nineteen years of service: not terror, not pain, but *recognition*. He had seen what was coming for him and had known its name.

"Found him at dawn," said Corporal Brech, her youngest, breath steaming. "No purse taken. Boots still on his feet. Whoever he was, nobody robbed him."

"Nobody needed to." Vane crouched. Her knee cracked — she was forty-one years old and the hunt did not care for knees — and she studied the corpse the way she studied everything, in order, without hurry, because hurry was how hunters died and how evidence died faster.

Frostbite, old and deep, gone black at the fingertips. He had walked a long way through hard cold. But frostbite hadn't killed him; she pressed two fingers beneath his jaw, more from ritual than hope, and felt nothing but the chill of meat. No wound. No poison smell. The lips were faintly blue-tinged, and there — she tilted his head with a gloved hand — a thread of frost had crept out from between his lips and down his chin, delicate as lace, as if the last thing inside him had been winter itself.

*Cold-taken,* the coroner would write. *Exposure.* And it would even be true, in the way that most lies are built on true stones.

But Vane turned the man's right hand over and saw what the frost had preserved better than any embalmer could have: wax. A crumb of dark green sealing wax wedged under the thumbnail, and a smear of it across the heel of his palm, as though he had been clutching something sealed until the very last, and then — what? Given it away? Had it taken from him?

She looked at his eyes again. Recognition, yes. But also, she thought now, relief.

"Search him properly," she said. "Every seam. And find out who saw him last. A man doesn't freeze to death ten feet from a warm street without someone noticing which door he was begging at."

Brech hesitated. "Captain — the Magistrate's schedule. You're meant to report on the market quarter by noon. The raids—"

"The raids will keep. Dead men don't." She rose, brushing the frost-dust from her coat. "And neither do ledgers. That's rather the point of them."

* * *

The hunt's offices occupied the old customs house on Anchorstone Quay, a building of black granite that had been raised to count cargo and now counted sins instead. Vane liked it for its honesty. Nothing in it pretended to be anything other than what it was: iron shelving, iron lamps, iron drawers, and paper — thousands upon thousands of pages, each one a small arrest waiting to happen.

Her own office was the coldest room in the building, which her subordinates assumed was oversight and she knew was preference. Cold kept the mind clean. Warmth made men sentimental, and sentiment was the first symptom of the disease she was employed to root out. Magic began, always, in warmth — in the warm hand of a stranger offering you a charm against fever, in the warm lie that some things need not be paid for.

She hung her coat, sat, and opened the morning's intake log with a pen knife's edge of attention.

The dead man had been identified by noon: one Aldous Wren, formerly of the tallow-chandlers' guild, struck off the rolls eleven years ago for — she read the entry twice, savoring it the way other men savored wine — *unlicensed practice of remedial arts*. Remedial arts. The guilds had such gentle words for it. Hedge-craft. Hearth-magic. The little warm treasons.

Wren had left Greyharrow after his striking-off. Where he had gone, the record did not say. What he had done out there in the world for eleven years, the record did not say either. But he had come back, walking through hard cold with frostbitten hands, carrying something sealed in dark green wax, and he had died within sight of the city walls with relief in his eyes.

Vane pulled the second drawer of her desk toward her. It stuck, as it always stuck, and gave, as it always gave, with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside lay the ledger.

Not the great public ledger of convictions — that was for show, for the Magistrate's annual readings, for the burning of names in the square. This was the other ledger, the private one, the one no clerk touched but her. It had been begun by Captain Sorrel before her, and by Captain Maud before Sorrel, back and back into the black granite past, each hunter adding to it, each page a debt the hunt carried so the city didn't have to.

She turned to the page marked with a slip of grey ribbon. Twenty years old. She had inherited it along with the office and the cold room and the sticking drawer, and in twenty years — no. In the eleven years since she'd made captain, she had looked at it perhaps a hundred times, and every time it had shown her the same thing: an open wound in the paperwork.

*Incident 411. The Amnesty Winter.*

The handwriting was Sorrel's, sharp and slanted, a hand that wrote the way a blade enters. Vane knew the account by heart, but she read it again anyway, because reading it was a kind of sharpening.

In the plague winter twenty years past, when the fever came up the river and took one soul in five, the hedge-witches of Greyharrow had broken the law en masse — openly, flagrantly, healing the sick with charms and cantrips while the licensed physicians died at the same rate as everyone else. The Magistrate had faced a choice: prosecute half the city, or look away. And into that deadlock, according to Sorrel's informants, had walked a woman named Maren Nystrom, a hedge-witch of considerable reputation, and had offered the Magistrate a bargain.

The terms had never been written down. That was the wound. Sorrel's page recorded only the outcome: within a fortnight, the plague broke. Within a month, the Magistrate issued the Amnesty Proclamation — full pardon for all magical acts committed during the fever months — followed, three weeks later, by the Permanent Edict: magic outlawed absolutely, forever, on pain of the harshest penalties the law could devise. Mercy and iron in the same breath, and no explanation why the iron had to be so total.

Sorrel had investigated for six years. Her final entry on the page read:

*The witch Nystrom bought the amnesty with something. The Edict's severity is not policy; it is payment. Whatever she sold, she sold it to buy our leash, and the leash is on all of us. She vanished the same season. Her apprentice, a boy, E. Nystrom, age approx. 14, fled the city or died in it. UNRESOLVED. The debt remains open.*

Below it, in Maud's older hand, faded almost to nothing: *All debts remain open. That is what makes them debts.*

Vane closed her eyes briefly. Then she laid Aldous Wren's effects on the blotter: the boots, the belt, the small knife, the guild token worn smooth, and — recovered from a seam in his coat lining, folded into a square the size of a coin — a scrap of parchment bearing a single word in a careful, educated hand:

*Nystrom.*

Just the name. Not Emrys. Not Maren. Just the surname, like a key labeled only *door*.

And beneath the scrap, wrapped in oilcloth, the thing that had made her send Brech away before she allowed herself to touch it: a fragment of dark green sealing wax, broken, bearing half a seal.

She held it to the lamp.

Most seals in Greyharrow were civic — the Magistrate's scales, the guilds' marks, the temple's wheel. Smugglers used blank wax or borrowed sigils. But this fragment showed the curve of a design she had seen exactly once before, in the archive copy of a twenty-year-old evidence report she had never been able to get out of her head: a mountain with a doorway in its heart, and above the doorway, a crown.

The report had concerned a raid, twenty years ago, on a charm-seller's cellar. The cellar had been empty except for candle stubs and one discarded letter, sealed in green wax, unsigned. The raid's target — a woman named Maren Nystrom — was never found.

Vane set the wax fragment down with the care one gives a live coal.

So. Aldous Wren, a struck-off hedge-practitioner, had spent his last strength walking back to Greyharrow carrying something sealed with Maren Nystrom's mark, and the only word he'd bothered to preserve was *Nystrom*, and he had died looking relieved. Which meant he had delivered it. Which meant it was in this city now, in someone's hands, moving through someone's life like a splinter working toward the heart.

And there was only one Nystrom the hunt had ever wanted and never found.

*E. Nystrom. Age approx. 14. Fled the city or died in it.*

Twenty years. If the boy lived, he was a man of thirty-four now. He would be careful. He would be small. He would have spent two decades learning to be invisible in the one city where invisibility was a survival trade. He might sell ribbons. He might mend kettles. He might stand in a market with a stall full of perfectly mundane trinkets and hands that remembered everything his mouth denied.

Or he might be dead in a ditch outside Vell's Crossing, and she would spend another eleven years opening this drawer.

Vane did not believe in ditches. Ditches were what people hoped for, and hope was not evidence. Evidence was a name on a scrap and a seal on a fragment and a dead man's relieved eyes, and evidence said: *he came home. He brought it home.*

She took a fresh sheet of hunt paper and began, in her own sharp hand, to write the warrant request. Name unknown — no. She crossed that out. Names could wait; districts could not. Wren had frozen ten feet from a warm street, which meant he had been refused shelter, which meant the poor streets, the tenement warrens where doors stayed shut against beggars in a hard season. She pulled the district map toward her and let her finger walk it: Cordwainer's Row, the Shambles, Tanner's Fold, and — her finger stopped — Cindermarket and its surrounding lanes, the cheapest roofs in Greyharrow, the quarter where a runaway apprentice grown into a careful man would hide in plain sight among a thousand careful people.

It was also, she noted, the quarter scheduled for tomorrow's sweep. The Magistrate's raids were routine, bureaucratic, a showing of the flag. But flags, Vane thought, could be pointed.

She finished the warrant request. She wrote the name *Nystrom* in the subject line and felt the old ledger page seem to lean closer over her shoulder, twenty years of unresolved ink exhaling against her neck.

Then she did something she had done only four times in eleven years of captaincy. She unlocked the bottom drawer, lifted out the iron case, and removed the hunt's oldest instrument: not a sword, not a brand, but a pair of scales, palm-sized, brass gone dark with handling, that had belonged to the first captain of the hunt. Legend said they weighed guilt. Vane's predecessor had told her the truth, which was better: they weighed nothing at all. They were a reminder. *Everything balances eventually. Your work is only to make sure you're standing on the right side of the scale when it does.*

She set the scales on her desk beside the wax fragment and the scrap of parchment, and the little brass pans hung level and empty, and she looked at them for a long moment.

Somewhere out in the gathering cold, a man named Nystrom was going about his evening — banking his fire, counting his stock, bolting his door — believing, as the guilty always believed, that twenty quiet years amounted to acquittal.

"They don't," Vane said aloud, to the empty room, to the ledger, to the dead man whose relief she would carry until it was explained. "They're just the sentence, deferred. And deferred isn't forgiven. Deferred is *owed*."

She rang for Brech.

"Double the Cindermarket detail tonight," she said. "Quietly. No uniforms on the lanes — plainclothes at the corners, watching doors. Anyone selling anything unusual, anyone receiving visitors after dark, anyone who looks at a hunter's shadow and flinches. I want eyes on that quarter before midnight."

"And tomorrow's sweep, Captain?"

"Tomorrow we stop being polite." She was already pulling on her coat, already feeling the coming snow in her teeth like a filing of iron. "Wake me if anything moves. Wake me especially if nothing does — nothing is often the loudest thing there is."

Brech went. Vane stood a moment longer at the window, looking out at the quay, where the river ran black and slow between walls furred with the first rime of the season. Far off, barely visible through the industrial haze, the mountain stood over the city like a creditor at a funeral, patient, immense, waiting to be paid.

She thought, not for the first time, that the Edict had always felt less like law than like a wall built hastily around something — and that walls built hastily were built to keep something *in*, not out.

She put the thought away. Thoughts like that were warmth, and warmth was the disease.

The ledger waited on her desk, open to page 411, the grey ribbon stirring slightly in the draft from the window, like a tongue about to speak.

* * *

They woke her at the fourth bell of the night, and it was Brech again, and he was trying not to look excited, which in Brech meant he looked constipated.

"Plainclothes on Tanner's Fold report a light," he said. "A tenement on the fold itself, third floor. Lamp lit past midnight, then shuttered fast when the watch passed. And Captain—" he consulted his slate, savoring it, "—the ground-floor tenant says the upstairs man keeps odd hours, buys little, sells at the Cindermarket stalls, and has hands, quote, 'too clever for a ribbon-seller.' Unquote."

"Name?"

"No one knows it. He pays cash, tips well, and asks no favors. The landlady calls him 'the quiet one.'"

Of course she did. Vane was already dressing, buckling the coat, checking the irons at her belt with the blind fluency of twenty years. Somewhere across the sleeping city a lamp had gone out behind shutters, and somewhere a man who had been quiet for twenty years was lying awake listening to the sound of his own heartbeat, wondering if tonight was the night the past knocked.

Let him wonder. Wonder loosened bolts; fear made men run, and running men chose bad doors.

"Rouse the squad," she said. "Six, no more. We do it at dawn, proper, with the warrant — I want paper on him before I want iron. Sorrel taught me that much: a hunter who arrests without paper is just a robber with opinions."

"And if he runs, Captain?"

Vane paused at the door of the cold room, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the desk, at the ledger lying open, at the little brass scales hanging level in the lamplight, weighing nothing, promising everything.

"Then we'll learn a great deal from the direction he runs," she said. "Men run toward their sins, Corporal. Get your coat."

Dawn came up the colour of pewter, and the first flakes of the season's first snow were drifting down over Cindermarket as the squad moved into position — soft, silent, settling on shoulders and warrants alike, as if the sky itself had decided to file its report in white.

Vane stood at the foot of the tenement stairs, adjusted her gloves, and gave the order.

"Knock."
