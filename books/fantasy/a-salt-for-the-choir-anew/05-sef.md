## 5. Sef

Sef had learned to follow a man the way her father taught her to follow a deer: not by looking at him, but by looking at what he touched.

Emrys Nystrom left signs everywhere, small and careless as spilled grain. A luck-knot sold here, its wool dyed the particular yellow that only came from one dyer's yard by the tannery. A fever-charm pressed into a beggar's palm there, and the beggar's cough gone quiet by morning — which meant real craft in it, not just knotted string and confidence. He bought bread from the woman on Coriander Stair. He drank at the Drowned Bell but never more than two cups. He walked home each night past the shrine of the Unnamed Saint without stopping, though his eyes flicked to it every time, the way a man's eyes flick to a door he has locked but does not trust.

She had been following him for nine days.

Nine days of Greyharrow's grey drizzle sliding down her collar, of sleeping in stairwells and haylofts with her knife against her forearm where she could feel its weight even through sleep, of watching this thin, tired man sell small mercies out of a market stall while pretending they were nothing but trinkets. Nine days since she had knelt beside her father's body in the snow behind a tenement on Vetch Lane and pried from his frozen hand the impression of a wax seal, half-melted into his palm like a brand.

*The charm-seller,* he had told her, three weeks before that, feverish in their rented room, his frostbitten fingers wrapped around a cup she kept refilling. *The apprentice. Maren's runaway. He'll know the roads. He has to.*

He had died with someone else's name in his mouth instead of hers.

That was the thing she could not put down, the stone she carried under her ribs. Not that he had died — she had known for years that the road he was walking ended somewhere cold. But that at the end, when the dark came up through him like water through a sinking hull, he had not said *Sef*. He had said *Maren Nystrom*, and *the debt comes due*, and *tell him*, tell him, tell him — and then he had said nothing ever again.

So she followed the man he had died trying to reach, and she sharpened her grief on a whetstone every morning until it had an edge she could use.

* * *

On the tenth day, the hunters came to the market quarter, and everything changed shape.

Sef was buying chestnuts she didn't want, an excuse to stand near the stall, when the bells began — not the hour-bells but the short, hard strokes of the raid-bell, three and three and three. The market didn't scream; Greyharrow markets never screamed. They simply *folded*, stalls collapsing inward like hands closing, vendors sweeping contraband into aprons and cellars with the smooth practiced motion of people who had done this a hundred times. Only the strangers froze.

And Emrys Nystrom froze too — just for a heartbeat, just long enough for Sef to see it. The mask of the bored trinket-seller slipped, and underneath was something young and terrified, a boy caught with his hand in the wrong drawer. Then he moved, fast, kicking over his own display table so that luck-knots and painted beads scattered across the cobbles, and in the chaos of a dozen greedy hands snatching them up he was gone, over a wall, through a gap between buildings no wider than a coffin.

The hunters came through the square in grey-and-iron, moving like a machine, and at their head walked a woman with a ledger under her arm and a face like a closed account. Sef shrank back against the chestnut brazier and watched the captain stop at the wreckage of Emrys's stall. She did not look at the fleeing crowd. She looked *down*, at the cobblestones, at the scattered charms, at the ground itself, reading it the way a priest reads scripture. Then she took out her ledger and wrote something, unhurried, while her squad tore apart the empty stall board by board.

Sef bought her chestnuts and walked away with her heart going like a drum in a procession.

Because she understood now, watching that woman read the ground, that she was not the only one tracking Emrys Nystrom. And whatever the hunters wanted with him, they would be slower than her or faster than her, but either way the window was closing. If she meant to do the thing she had carried nine days in her chest like a coal, she would have to do it soon, and do it herself, and be gone before the iron arrived.

That night she waited in the dark of his tenement stairwell, third floor, where the lamp burned behind frosted glass, and she held her knife, and she rehearsed the words she would say — *you have something of mine; my father died carrying it; give me the map and I'll let you keep your blood* — and she found that all the rehearsals sounded like a girl playing at being hard, and she hated herself a little for that, and she gripped the knife harder, and she waited.

* * *

He came home near midnight, smelling of river and panic, and she heard him stop on the landing outside his own door. Heard him breathe. Heard the long silence of a man who has noticed that the draught under his door is coming from the wrong direction.

"I can hear you," he said quietly, to the dark. "You've been behind me since the market. You breathe like you're counting."

Sef stepped out of the stairwell shadow with the knife low and her other hand open, the way her father had taught her — *open hand first, always, because a man who sees an open hand hesitates, and hesitation is all you need.* "Emrys Nystrom."

He went very still. In the lamplight leaking under his door she could see his face properly for the first time: younger than she'd expected, worn older than he was, with the particular pallor of someone who had spent years living indoors, in shadows, in the space between law and need. His eyes went to the knife, then to her hand, then to her face, and stayed there.

"You're not a hunter," he said.

"No."

"Then who—" and then she saw him see it: the resemblance, the jaw, the way she stood with her weight back like a fencer. She saw the recognition arrive in his face like ice cracking across a pond. "Oh," he said. "Oh, no. You're his—"

"My father died on Vetch Lane nine days ago," Sef said. Her voice came out flat and steady, which surprised her; she had expected it to shake. "Frostbite, exposure, and whatever else was chasing him. He was carrying a map sealed in wax. That map is yours now, because he gave it to you, and I want to know why a stranger's dying breath spent my father's last hours of warmth on *your* name."

"It wasn't my name," Emrys said. "It was Maren's. Maren Nystrom." He swallowed. "I'm the Nystrom. She was my mistress. My teacher. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before I ran." He said it simply, without decoration, and something about the plainness of it made Sef's grip loosen a fraction on the knife. "Twenty years ago. I was seventeen and I thought she was a coward, and I left, and I have spent twenty years selling luck-knots in a city where my craft is a hanging crime, and I never once asked myself why a hedge-witch as good as she was stayed somewhere that hated her. Until your father put a map in my hands and died saying her name."

"Give me the map," Sef said. "I'll finish what he started. You don't have to be part of it."

"And what exactly," Emrys said, very carefully, "did he start?"

And there it was — the question she had been avoiding for nine days, the hole in the center of her fury. Because her father had never told her. He had packed in the night with a map and a bag of salt and a knife, and he had kissed her forehead and said *if I'm not back by solstice, don't come looking*, and she had come looking anyway, and now she stood in a tenement stairwell demanding answers to questions she couldn't ask because she didn't know them.

"He was going under the mountain," she said. "To the court. To buy something back."

"Buy what back?"

"*Give me the map—*"

"Your mother," Emrys said quietly.

The word landed in the stairwell like a dropped glass.

Sef did not move. Could not. Somewhere below them a dog began to bark and was hushed. The lamplight under the door trembled, or her hand trembled, she couldn't tell which.

"What did you say?"

"Your father talked," Emrys said. "For three weeks, in a rented room, dying by inches. He thought I was a priest at the end, I think, or maybe just anyone. He told me things. He told me your mother went to the Court Beneath the Mountain nineteen years ago and signed herself over, soul entire, term of service perpetual, and that she did it to settle a blood-debt — a debt that belonged by rights to someone else in her family." His eyes were gentle and terrible. "He told me he'd spent nineteen years finding the roads down. And he told me the rule the court keeps, the one that makes the whole thing a trap: a sold soul can't be bought back. It can only be *inherited*. Someone has to take the bargain whole. Term, service, silence. Willingly."

"You're lying," Sef said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it. "You're lying to save your skin."

"I'm the worst liar in Greyharrow," Emrys said, with a ghost of a smile that had no humor in it at all. "It's why I'm poor. Look — look, I'll show you. Not tell. Show."

* * *

He unlocked his door and lit one candle, cupping the flame, and Sef followed him in with the knife still in her hand because putting it away felt like surrendering something she might need. The room was small and clean and sad: a cot, a shelf of jars labeled in a careful hand, bundles of dried herbs hanging from the beams like sleeping bats, and on the table, weighted under a river stone, the map.

Her father's map. She knew it before she saw the seal — knew the heavy paper, the ink mixed with ash that he favored, the little compass rose drawn as a snowflake because he had always said north was just the direction winter came from. It was spread open across the table, and it showed the mountain cut open like a dissected body: the old mine-shafts in brown ink, the bone-roads beneath them in white, winding down and down toward a blank space at the heart of the paper where the court should have been. Not torn out. *Blanked* — the parchment there gone smooth and featureless, as if the ink had been drunk out of it.

"He tried to mark the court's location," Emrys said. "Three times, different inks. Watch."

He dipped a pen, touched it to the blank space, wrote a single letter — *C* — and Sef watched the ink bead up and slide off the parchment like water off grease, leaving nothing. The candle flame leaned sideways in a room with no draft.

"The map won't show it," Emrys said. "The court doesn't permit itself to be seen. Your father got as far as the tolls — see these marks? Names crossed out along the lower road. He paid his way in names. That's how far he got before he turned back." He paused. "Or was turned back."

"Turned back by what?"

"By the rule." Emrys turned the map over, and there, crammed into the margins in a cramped, slantwise hand, were notes — her father's notes, she recognized the pressure of the pen, the way he bore down when he was afraid — and among them, copied out in fuller script, a passage that made Sef's stomach turn over:

*A soul sold to the court may be reclaimed only by one who freely assumes the seller's bargain entire: the term, the service, the silence, the debt whole and unbroken. No purchase. No rescue. No force. The court does not take hostages. It takes volunteers.*

Below it, in her father's hand, almost too faint to read: *Nineteen years to find this out. Nineteen years, and the answer is that I must become what she is. God forgive me. I am not brave enough. God forgive me, I am not brave enough.*

Sef sat down. She didn't decide to; her legs simply stopped holding her, and the cot received her like it had been waiting. The knife lay in her lap, suddenly absurd, a tool for a problem that knives couldn't touch.

"She did it for me," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "The blood-debt. It was mine. Our family owed it — my grandfather's doing, before I was born, a bargain gone bad, a life owed to a life. And when the collectors finally traced it to our house, she went to the mountain instead. She told me she was going to her sister's. She told me—" Her voice failed and restarted. "She told me she'd write."

"And she couldn't," Emrys said softly. "Silence is part of the terms. Your father wrote that too. *They sign away the right to speak of it, or perhaps the ability.* He wasn't sure which was worse."

Sef looked down at her hands. They were her mother's hands — she had been told that all her life, the long fingers, the burn scar on the thumb — and they were shaking. Nine days she had carried a knife and a plan and a hatred with nowhere to go, and now the hatred dissolved in her chest like salt in warm water and left behind something much heavier: the picture of her mother, nineteen years ago, kissing her forehead and walking up the mountain to be erased, and her father spending two decades and finally his life trying to find a way to follow that didn't end in the same signature.

And failing. Failing at the last step, because the step was becoming a thrall forever, and he had knelt in the snow on Vetch Lane and found, at the bottom of himself, that he was not brave enough — and had crawled instead to a stranger's door to ask a boy who hated him to be braver than he was.

"He wasn't a coward," Sef said, and didn't know who she was defending him to.

"No," Emrys said. "He was honest. There's a difference, and the mountain punishes the difference." He sat down across from her, folding his long frame onto a stool, and pushed the map aside so that nothing lay between them. "Sef. I need to say something, and you should hold the knife while I say it."

She looked up.

"Your father asked me to go down. Me, specifically. He said — his words — *the apprentice owes her; the apprentice is the only one who owes her enough.* And he was right. I ran from Maren Nystrom when she was keeping me fed and taught and safe in a city that burns witches, because I was seventeen and afraid, and everything I've done for twenty years has been hiding in the shape of the life she made possible." He exhaled slowly. "But I'm not going to lie to you and say I'll march to the mountain tomorrow and sign my soul away out of love. I don't know what I'll do. I know the rule now, and I know the price, and I know that whoever pays it doesn't come back. So I'm telling you plainly: I'm afraid. I've been afraid for twenty years. It's the only inheritance my mistress ever gave me that I kept."

Something in Sef's chest, wound tight for nine days, loosened by one turn. It was not forgiveness. It was not trust. But it was the truth, offered without varnish, and after the blankness of the map and the blankness of the court and the nineteen blank years where her mother's letters should have been, the plain ugly truth felt like bread after famine.

"My father died with your mistress's name in his mouth," she said. "Not mine. I've been angry about that for nine days. I think — I think he wasn't choosing her over me. I think he was choosing the one person whose guilt was big enough to spend." She picked up the knife, considered it, and slid it back into its sheath with a finality that surprised them both. "You owe her. Fine. But understand this, charm-seller: I'm not letting you go alone, and I'm not letting you decide anything without me. Whatever's down there — whatever the rule is, whatever the price is — my family started this chain, and my family is going to be the one that ends it. One way or another."

"That's what worries me," Emrys said quietly. "Your father said something else, near the end. He said the mountain doesn't want souls. It wants *lines*. Debts that pass down, generation to generation, because a debt that ends is a loss, but a debt inherited is an investment." He met her eyes. "If we go down there together, we're not two people. We're a lineage. And lineages are exactly what it feeds on."

Outside, far off across the rooftops, a bell began to ring — not the hour, not the raid. A slow, deep, single stroke, repeated, from a tower Sef had never heard ring before. The sound rolled over Greyharow like a stone over a well-mouth, and both of them went still, listening.

"What is that?" Sef whispered.

Emrys had gone pale. He was looking at the window, where the frost on the glass had begun, very slowly, to grow — ferns of ice spreading inward from the corners, delicate and deliberate, like something writing.

"Solstice is eleven days away," he said. "That bell means the mountain knows we're coming."
