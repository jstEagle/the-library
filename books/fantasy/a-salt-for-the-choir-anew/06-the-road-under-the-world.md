## 6. The Road Under the World

The raid came at the changing of the watch, because the Magistrate's men were nothing if not punctual.

Emrys heard it before he saw it — the particular rhythm of hobnailed boots on cobbles that meant not soldiers but hunters, spaced too evenly, moving in a sweep rather than a march. He had been selling luck-knots to a fishwife, three fingers of grey dawn light coming through the market quarter's canvas awnings, his breath steaming over the little brass pan where he warmed wax for seals. The fishwife was haggling over a knot meant for her son's boat, and Emrys was letting her win, because a customer who thought she'd beaten you down came back.

Then the boots. Then the fishwife's face going careful and blank as a shuttered window, her coin already back in her fist.

"Fire," someone shouted from the far row, which was the old code — *fire* meaning hunters, meaning run, meaning every charm-seller within four streets should scatter their stock and become, instantly, an honest merchant of nothing at all.

Emrys swept his arm across the stall. Luck-knots, fever-charms, the wax, the pan, the bundle of dried rowan — all of it went into the sack beneath the counter that was oiled and weighted for exactly this purpose, and the sack went into the culvert grate behind him that had never once, in six years, been found by anyone but him. By the time Captain Vane's people rounded the corner he was standing in the open with his hands folded, wearing the expression of a man who sold buttons and had always sold buttons and could not imagine why anyone would think otherwise.

It nearly worked. It had always nearly worked.

What undid him was Sef.

She came through the crowd like a stone comes through a window — fast, angled, aimed. He saw her before she saw him and had time to register three things: that she was the dead stranger's daughter; that she had a knife low along her thigh in the grip of someone who knew how to hold it; and that her eyes had found him with the terrible certainty of a person who had spent nine days doing nothing else.

"You," she said.

Every hunter in the market quarter turned toward the word.

Later — much later, on a road with no sky above it — he would have time to be angry about this, about how a single syllable from her had cost him six years of small careful living. In the moment there was only motion. He went over the stall, through the awning, into the mouth of Tanner's Lane, with her footsteps behind him matching his stride for stride, and the boots of the law fanning out behind them both like a hand of cards being laid down wrong.

Greyharrow in winter was a city built for exactly one kind of runner, and Emrys was that kind. He took the cut behind the chandlery where the drainpipe leaned at a climbable angle. He crossed the dye-yard where the snow lay thick enough to swallow footprints and thin enough to betray anyone who didn't know which flagstones were hollow. He went down the Stair of Nine Apologies, so named because everyone who used it owed someone an explanation, and came out in the shadow of the old Eastmine headframe, where the mountain rose up black against the bruised morning sky like something that had stood up out of the earth and decided to stay standing.

She was still with him. That was the unforgivable part. He'd shaken better pursuers than her off worse trails.

"The mine," she said, not even breathing hard, which he resented. "Your map says the mine."

"My map doesn't say anything. My map is a liar made of wax." But his hands were already pulling the boards off the adit, because his body had apparently voted without consulting him, and the body of a runaway apprentice knows: when the law is behind you and the mountain is ahead of you, you choose the mountain. The Magistrate could sentence a man. The mountain only kept him.

The collapsed shaft wasn't collapsed all the way through — that was the rumor, anyway, the kind miners' widows pass to each other over laundry, that the Eastmine's deepest gallery ran clean into the dark under Greyharrow and came out somewhere no map admitted to. Twenty years ago they'd sealed it after the cave-in that killed eleven men and, officially, ended mining under the city. Officially.

Emrys squeezed through the gap where the roof beams had buckled into an arch of splinters and shadow, and the cold changed around him. Not deeper cold. Older cold. The difference between a winter night and the inside of a locked cellar where something has been waiting.

Sef came through behind him, dragging a stolen storm-lantern, and the light swung across walls veined with something pale and greenish that seemed to contract at its touch.

"Lichen," Emrys said, grateful for a fact, any fact. "Glow-moss. Miners used to—"

"I don't care what miners used to do." She lifted the lantern higher. Behind them, faint through the rock, came the sound of the raid going on without them — whistles, shouts, a world getting farther away. "My father came down here alone. I intend to find out what killed him. You're coming with me until I do."

"And then?"

"Then we'll discuss it."

* *

The road began half a mile in.

They found it where the mine's worked galleries gave out and the passage opened like a throat — a road, an actual road, wide enough for four abreast, paved in slabs of bone-white stone worn smooth and slightly hollowed down the middle, the way cathedral steps are worn by centuries of pilgrims' knees. Except these hadn't been worn by knees. Emrys crouched and put his palm flat against the nearest slab and felt, or thought he felt, a grain to it like the long parallel fibers of a femur.

"This isn't stone," he said.

"No," said Sef. Her voice had gone quiet in a way he was beginning to understand she hated. "Look at the joins."

He looked. The slabs met edge to edge with the seamless intimacy of growth rings, of knuckles fitted to knuckles. The road had not been built. It had grown, or healed, or — he refused the third thought until the road itself refused to let him — calcified, the way a body will encase what it cannot expel.

The lichen covered everything past the last of the mine's timber. It grew in slow spirals up the bone-walls and in soft mats across the road, and it glowed without flickering, a steady blue-green light like the underside of ice, and walking in it felt like walking inside the moment before you speak a name you've almost forgotten.

"It's beautiful," Sef said, and Emrys heard the wonder in her voice and thought, distantly, *yes*, and thought nothing else, because the first mile took something from him and he did not notice what it was until two miles later.

They walked. The road descended in long easy grades that no mine ever followed, banking gently around pillars of fused bone where galleries must once have intersected, and the light came with them like a patient animal. They talked, at first — or Sef interrogated and Emrys answered, about Maren Nystrom, about the twenty years since he'd run, about the map with its self-blanking heart — and then they talked less, and then they walked in silence, because silence was easier and the road rewarded ease.

At some point Sef asked him how long he'd apprenticed under his mistress, and Emrys opened his mouth to say *from the age of eight, seven years, the best and worst years of my life*, and found that he could remember the eight and the seven and could not, anywhere in himself, locate the *best*.

There was a shape where the memory should have been. A smooth place, like a socket. He knew the cottage. He knew the smell of the tallow and the iron pot and the way frost feathered the inside of the shutters. He knew that he had loved it. He could produce the fact of love the way you can produce a receipt for something stolen from you. But the thing itself — the warm specific weight of it, whatever morning or evening had carried it — was gone, and the road under his feet glowed very slightly brighter than it had a mile ago.

He stopped walking.

"What," said Sef.

"The lichen." His voice came out wrong, flattened. "It eats memory. That's what feeds it. That's what the light *is*." He turned and looked back the way they'd come, at the glowing road unspooling behind them through the dark, and understood with a lurch that he was looking at himself — at miles of himself, spread out along the bone in luminous film. "Every step. Every mile. It takes a little. It's been taking since we came in."

"That's insane."

"Test it," he said. "Tell me your mother's face."

Sef opened her mouth. Closed it. He watched her go inward, watched her search, watched the lantern tremble in her grip — and then he watched the exact moment she found the memory still there, whole, and the relief hit her like a physical blow.

"She's there," she whispered. "She's—" And then, quieter, with dawning arithmetic: "My father. When they found him. The woman who identified the body said he kept saying names. Over and over. Names, like he was checking he still had them."

They stood in the blue-green light, two small warm animals on a road made of the dead, and above them and behind them and ahead of them the mountain held its silence the way a creditor holds a ledger.

"We can go back," Emrys said, though he heard the lie in it even as he offered it, because behind them now lay five miles of road that had already fed, and Vane, and the market quarter, and the whole small hidden machinery of his life.

Sef shouldered the lantern. "My father walked it alone," she said. "I'm not going to let it eat me slower than it ate him. Walk."

* *

By the sixth mile Emrys had lost the taste of bread fresh from an oven.

He noticed this one immediately — had trained himself, after the first loss, to audit himself at every bend of the road, running down the shelves of himself like a shopkeeper counting stock. Faces: all present, though his father's had gone strangely general, more portrait than person. Skills: intact; he could still feel the knot-work living in his fingers. Sensations were going next, it seemed — the road had taken the oven-bread and, somewhere around the fifth mile, quietly, the feeling of sun on the back of the neck, which he discovered while trying to recall what summer was like and finding he could describe it only in the way he could describe a country he'd never visited.

The horror of it was how painless it was. That was the trap's whole architecture. No tearing, no theft you could fight. Each memory simply ripened and detached, like fruit, and somewhere below his feet he could feel — or believed he could feel, which by the seventh mile was becoming a distinction without edges — the road digesting, the light growing richer, the whole vast patient organism of the under-mountain warmed and fed by everything he'd ever been.

"It takes the sweet ones first," Sef said. She was limping slightly. She had stopped talking about her mother's face and started, occasionally, saying her mother's name aloud at intervals, like a man calling into fog. "That's what's cruel about it. It doesn't take what you need. It takes what you *love*. It leaves the ledger-work. I can still remember every debt my family owed and to whom. I would trade every one of those for the smell of my mother's hair and it won't take the trade."

"How do you know it takes the sweet ones?"

"Because I've lost three, and all three were—" Her jaw set. "My father teaching me to gut a fish. My mother humming in the kitchen. The day I got my knife." She looked at him with eyes that dared him to comment on what those choices said about her. "Everything ugly is still here. Ugly weighs nothing to it. Love must be rich feeding."

The road bent around a pillar of bone so vast it had weathered into flutes and hollows, and the wind — there was wind down here, a slow tidal breathing from somewhere deep, smelling of snow and iron — moved through the flutes and made a sound. Not quite moaning. Not quite singing. A chord held almost too low to hear, felt in the teeth more than the ears, and Emrys stopped again, because he knew the note. He knew it the way you know a teacher's voice.

Maren had hummed it. Working charm-craft, stirring the pot, sealing wax over a luck-knot. A little rising-falling figure, three tones, that she'd claimed was nothing, just habit, just keeping time.

"It's the same," he said. "The road sings what she sang."

"Or she sang what the road sings." Sef's voice had gone flat and practical, the voice of a woman rationing her own dread. "Think about what that means and then stop thinking about it. We need to keep moving."

But Emrys stood one moment longer with his hand on the fluted bone, feeling the chord move up through his palm into the wrist, into the chest, and thought: *she knew this road. She walked it. Twenty years ago she walked it down and she has been walking it, in some fashion, ever since.* And the thought arrived attached to another thought, unwelcome as a bill: *and if the road fed on her for twenty years, what is left? What will we find at the bottom — my mistress, or just the light that ate her?*

He caught himself reaching for the memory of Maren's laugh — reached for it deliberately, greedily, wanting to hold it against the road's appetite — and found it still there, whole, ringing. And wept, briefly and silently and without entirely understanding why, before shouldering his empty pack and walking on.

* *

Somewhere past the tenth mile, the road began to show them other travelers.

Not living ones. The lichen grew in shapes alongside the verges — low mounds, long ridges, forms that might have been drifts of glowing mold and might have been figures lying at rest, shoulder to shoulder, facing the direction of travel. Emrys did not look closely at them. Looking closely at them felt like a toll he was unwilling to pay on top of the one the road was already collecting.

"They came down," Sef said quietly. "All of them. For twenty years, at least, people have been coming down this road to try what my father tried." She paused beside one of the longer ridges. Whatever it was, it had been there long enough for the lichen to have fully claimed its outline, gentle and complete, like snowfall finishing a sentence. "Nobody came back."

"People came back," Emrys said. "One came back. Barely. Far enough to die against my door." He looked down the endless glowing grade of the road, descending now more steeply, curving away into a darkness the lichen did not bother to enter, as if the light itself knew better. "And he gave the map to me. Why me? Out of the whole city. He was dying in a district full of hedge-witches and he pressed it into my hands and said her name. He knew who I was."

"Did he?"

"He said *Maren Nystrom* and looked at me like—" Emrys stopped. Tried to reach for the memory of the stranger's face, the frostbitten dying face at his door, and found it intact, vivid, every crack in the frostbitten lips preserved — except that when he tried to remember what the man's eyes had looked like, what expression had been in them when he'd spoken her name, there was nothing. A smooth socket. The road had taken that one too, and he had not noticed, had not audited, had walked miles past the theft.

"He knew," Emrys said slowly. "He knew who I was. He knew what she was to me. He chose me because—" Because the bargain needed a willing heir. Because grief makes people cruel. Because a dying man will spend anything, including a stranger boy's soul, to get one mile closer to his wife's.

Ahead, the road dropped through an arch of fused vertebrae tall enough to have swallowed the tenement Emrys had lived in his whole life twice over, and beyond the arch, for the first time since the collapsed mine, there was structure — walls, terraces, the suggestion of architecture grown rather than built, descending in lit tiers into a depth the lantern and the lichen together could not plumb. And threading up from that depth, faint but unmistakable, came sound. Many voices. One song.

The choir.

Sef raised the lantern. Her face in its light was the face of a woman standing at the edge of everything she'd wanted and fearing it. "Tollmen," she said. "Father's notes mentioned tollmen. Past the arch, the road charges."

"Charges what?"

She didn't answer, and the road glowed a fraction brighter between them, and Emrys understood that somewhere behind them, strung out along ten miles of bone, lay everything he had ever loved, feeding the dark — and that the dark, ahead, had not finished asking.
