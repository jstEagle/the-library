## 7. Tolls and Tollmen

The lichen thinned as we walked, and where it thinned the dark grew teeth.

I do not mean that kindly or metaphorically. The bone-road narrowed to a shelf of rib-white stone no wider than a coffin lid, and below it the mountain opened its throat into a dark so complete that my eyes ached trying to find a bottom in it. The air came up from that depth cold and mineral, tasting of iron and old water, and beneath those tastes — faintly, like a scent remembered from someone else's childhood — there was something sweet. Rot-sweet. Altar-sweet.

Emrys walked ahead of me with his hand trailing the wall. He had not spoken in an hour. Neither had I. Since the third mile, when I had lost the smell of our kitchen — tallow and barley and my mother's rosemary hung to dry above the stove — I had been afraid that speaking would shake loose other things, things I could not afford to lose yet. Memory, we were learning, was currency here. And like any currency, it leaked.

The road bent. Around the bend, light.

Not lichen-light, which was green and apologetic and seemed always on the verge of apology for shining at all. This was white. Hard white, the white of frost on a windowpane seen from the wrong side, and it fell from somewhere above us in a column so straight it might have been drawn with a rule. Where it struck the road, the stone glittered like salt.

And standing in the light, at a table of black wood set across the road's full width, was the tollman.

He was tall in the way that furniture is tall — built rather than grown, all planes and edges, his coat the grey of unwritten paper. His face was smooth as an egg. No eyes that I could see, only a shallow depression where eyes ought to be, and in that depression something moved, slow as oil. His hands rested flat on the table before him, long-fingered, patient, the hands of a man who has waited so long that waiting has become his trade.

Behind him, the road continued into a gate of black iron worked into shapes I did not want to look at too long — they resolved, when I tried, into letters; when I stopped trying, into hands; when I looked away entirely, I felt them on my back like weather.

"Travelers," said the tollman. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst of it, at first. It was the voice of a hospitable man, a man offering you a chair by a fire. "The Court Beneath the Mountain thanks you for your journey thus far. The road ahead is maintained at great expense. There is a toll."

Emrys stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into his back. I felt him go still in a way that was not calm. It was the stillness of a rabbit that has heard the hawk but cannot see it.

"What toll?" he asked. His voice came out thinner than he wanted it to, I think. He cleared his throat. "What toll?"

"All tolls here are paid in the same coin," the tollman said, and spread his hands, palms up, over the black table. "Names."

* * *

I had heard of this. Everyone from the deep mining families had heard of it, the way you hear of wolves — as a story told to keep children near the fire. Old Hollis Verrin, who'd spent forty years under the mountain before the collapse, used to say: *the court doesn't take your silver, girl. Silver it has. It takes what answers when you call yourself.*

I had thought it a fable. Fables are how miners survive their work.

But here stood the fable, wearing a coat of unwritten paper, asking for payment.

"How does it work?" Emrys asked. He had recovered some of his merchant's smoothness, the voice he used at market when a buyer squeezed him on price. "Is it — do we speak a name aloud? Whose name? Our own?"

"The mechanics," said the tollman, "are simple. The consequences are not." He tilted his smooth head, and the oil-dark thing in his eye-sockets shifted, and I had the sudden animal certainty that it was looking at me. Not at Emrys. At me. "You will give me a name. Any name you have the right to give. Your own, if you wish to pass through as yourself. Or another's — but only another's if it is truly yours to spend. A name you carry. A name you hold in trust. A name given to you by love or by blood or by grief." He paused. "A name inherited."

The word dropped through me like a stone through well-water.

Inherited.

My father's name was Anselm Corvane. Nobody had called him that in years — he'd gone by Ans, by Cor, by *that stubborn bastard* when the neighbors were feeling fond. But it was his, and it had been his father's, and his father's before him, back through seven generations of Corvanes whose graves I had weeded every spring since I was six years old because my father believed that a family that forgot its dead deserved to lose them twice.

Seven generations of names, and I carried them all. That is what daughters do, though nobody writes it down anywhere. We carry the names. We say them at the gravesides. We are the ledger the family keeps without knowing it keeps one.

"You're wondering," the tollman said softly, "what you have that you can spare."

"I'm wondering," I said, and my voice surprised me with its steadiness, "why you want them."

Something flickered across the egg-smooth face — not a smile, nothing so warm, but a rearrangement, the way ice rearranges when weight crosses it. "Why does any keeper of accounts want entries? The Court runs on debt. Debt must be written somewhere. Names are how debts are written into flesh." He lifted one long hand from the table. "Pass, and pay. Refuse, and turn back. The road behind you is longer now than it was. The mountain has been counting your steps."

Emrys made a small sound beside me, half laugh, half horror. "It's been *counting*—"

"They always count," the tollman said, with something almost like pity. "From the first step. Every mile you walked cost you something already. You simply haven't finished noticing what."

* * *

We argued ten feet away from the table, which the tollman permitted with the serenity of a creature that knows exactly how the argument will end.

"We pay it our own names," Emrys whispered. "Both of us. We walk through as — as whoever we become. Nameless. The choir is full of the nameless already; maybe that's all they lost, at the start. Maybe Maren—"

"And then what?" I said. "You walk into the Court with no name to bargain with, no name to sign, no name to reclaim anything *with*. You told me yourself — the whole architecture of that place is debts and signatures. Walk in nameless and you're not a negotiator. You're inventory."

He stared at me. In the hard white light his face looked older than it had in Greyharrow, older than it had that morning at his stall, older than it had the night I'd found him and put a knife to his throat over my father's map. Grief does that. It ages you in hours instead of years.

"My father," I said, and stopped, because saying it hurt, and then said it anyway, because I was beginning to understand that everything worth doing down here would hurt. "My father gave me his name the day I was born. That's what fathers do. They hand you the name like a coat — *here, wear this, it'll be too big for years, grow into it.*" My hands had gone cold in the mountain's cold, but colder still inside them. "He died carrying mine. Did you know that? On the road, dying, frostbitten through, he pressed that map into your hands and the last thing he asked about — the last thing — was whether I was safe. Whether I was found. He spent his last breath on my name. Spent it keeping it safe."

"Sef—" Emrys began.

"So I'll spend his." I turned back toward the table. "It's mine to spend. He gave it to me. That's the whole of inheritance, isn't it? Someone gives you something and trusts you to know what it's for."

The tollman's hands had not moved. But the oil in his eye-deepths had come forward, attentive, and I understood suddenly that this — this exact moment, this exact choice, repeated ten thousand times down ten thousand years — was the thing he existed to witness. The mountain did not merely eat memory. It ate the shape of giving. It wanted not just names but the *sacrifice of* names, the ceremony of it, the grief of it, grief being the richest ore in any soul.

So I would give it none.

I walked to the table. I did not kneel — the tollman's height suggested kneeling was customary, and I refused custom on principle. I put both palms flat on the black wood, mirroring him, and the wood was colder than riverbed stone, colder than the frost that had killed my father, and through that cold I felt something listening. Something vast and patient underneath the table, underneath the road, underneath the mountain's skin, with its ear pressed to the wood like a child eavesdropping through a floor.

"Anselm Corvane," I said.

Not loudly. Not with weeping. I said it the way you read a line from a ledger: level, clear, complete. First name, family name, no honorifics, no endearments, no *my father*, no *dear dead Ans who taught me to gut fish and hold a grudge*. Just the name, bare, the way it would appear in a record.

"That is the name I pay. Take it. And take nothing else."

The tollman inclined his smooth head. "Paid," he said, and it sounded almost gentle, and gentleness from that mouth was the most frightening sound I had heard since entering the mountain. "The gate is open to you. Walk through as yourselves. Your own names remain your own — for now. What the Court does with names once it holds them is the Court's affair." A pause, thin as paper. "It usually finds a use."

Somewhere behind the black iron gate, a mechanism I could not see released something I could not hear, and the gate swung inward a hand's width, exhaling air that smelled of candlewax and snow.

* * *

We walked through. And within twenty steps, I understood what I had done, in the way you understand a wound only after the arrow's flight, when the shock burns off and the true weight arrives.

I was telling Emrys about the fish-gutting. It seemed important, suddenly, to say things aloud while I still had them — my father taught me to gut a trout when I was five, standing on an overturned bucket at the river shallows, his big scarred hands closing over mine to guide the knife, *under the chin, up the belly, don't rush it, rushing it makes a mess and a mess wastes meat* — and I got as far as *under the chin* before the sentence went strange in my mouth. Under the chin. Up the belly. I knew the motions. I knew I knew them. But the hands guiding mine had gone — not forgotten. Absent. Like a room after the furniture's been sold. The shape of the lesson remained on the floor of me, outlined in dust where the memory had stood.

"Say his name," Emrys said quietly. He had stopped walking. "Right now. Say it and see."

"Anselm Corvane."

Nothing. The words came out shaped correctly, two syllables and four, properly pronounced, and they landed in my mind like coins into a dry well. I heard them. I recognized them as words. They referred to nothing. They were a label on an empty jar.

I tried again, reaching further. *Anselm. My father. Anselm Corvane, who was born in the winter of the bad harvest, who married Lira, who—*

Lira. My mother's name surfaced intact, bright and painful, and around it a hole the exact size and shape of my father. I could remember that my father had married her. I could remember *that* he had loved her — I remembered knowing it, the way you remember a proof of geometry, all steps accounted for. But the love itself, the texture of it, the sound of his voice saying her name across a kitchen — gone. Sold. Already ground into whatever grist the Court grinds, already singing somewhere in a throat that wasn't his anymore.

"He's fading," I said. My voice did something I didn't authorize. It stayed level. It stayed ledger-level, tollman-level, and I realized I was doing it deliberately, holding the grief off at arm's length the way you hold a snake, because if I let it reach me I would fall down in this road and the mountain would count that too. "He's fading while I walk. You said the choir sings the King's mass. Is that — is that where it goes? Straight there? Does it start singing immediately?"

"I don't know." Emrys's face had gone grey. "Sef, I didn't — I thought it would take the name and hold it. Escrow. A vault. I didn't think it would *spend* it—"

"It eats inheritance." I kept walking, because stopping felt like sinking. "That's what you said in the tunnel, an hour ago, about the lichen feeding on memory. But it's worse than eating. Look at what it took." I held up my fingers, counting, a merchant's gesture I'd learned from watching him at his stall. "It didn't take my memories of him wholesale. It took the *connection*. I still have fragments of him — the fish, the graves, the bucket by the river. But each fragment is orphaned now. Unowned. Floating loose in me with no thread back to their source." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you understand? It didn't steal the beads. It stole the string. Everything he ever gave me is still here, scattered, and nothing points to him. In twenty years I won't even know these orphan pieces are orphans. I'll think they're mine."

Emrys was silent for a long moment. Then, in a low voice: "That's what it does to bloodlines. That's what it's *for*. The Court doesn't just collect souls — it severs lines. Every soul sold here was somebody's parent or child, and every sale cuts the thread between them, and the children grow up with holes in them they can't name, and some of those children —" his voice caught — "some of them come looking. Down the bone-roads. With maps. And the mountain counts their steps, and takes its tolls, and by the time they reach the Court they've paid away half of what they came to save."

"Like my father," I said.

"Like your father." He wouldn't look at me. "He came down here, Sef. Before Greyharrow. Before the door of my tenement. He must have — the map shows the roads, the tolls, the sequence. He walked this same shelf of stone. He stood at this same table." A breath. "And he paid. He must have paid something. And then he reached the Court, or nearly, and whatever he found there broke him or emptied him or both, and he climbed back out with enough left to hate me and enough left to die of the climb, and the last thing he did with his remaining self was make sure the map reached someone who could finish it."

The road sloped downward. The white light of the toll-station fell away behind us, and the lichen-light rose ahead, green and dim, and between the two lights we walked through a band of ordinary darkness where, for a few yards, nothing fed on us at all. It was the closest thing to mercy the mountain had shown us. I hoarded those yards.

"Ask me something about him," I said suddenly. "Anything. While I still know what the fragments are."

Emrys considered. "What did he look like?"

"Tall. Broad. Hands like—" and there it was again, the hole, opening under me mid-answer. *Hands like spades,* I should have said. *Hands that could close around a hammer handle or my whole forearm, depending.* I knew there had been hands. I knew they had been large. The largeness remained as a fact; the hands themselves had gone to the choir. "Tall," I said finally. "Broad. I think dark-haired. I think — I *think*, Emrys, and thinking is all that's left. Thinking is what you do about strangers."

We walked. The lichen brightened by degrees, drinking from us as it lit, and I felt it taking in small sips now — the smell of bread, the color of a door, the tune I used to hum at the washline. Small coin. It didn't bother bargaining for the large pieces anymore; it had learned, somewhere in the last mile, that I would guard those, and it contented itself with skimming.

Ahead, the road widened. The lichen gathered itself into ropes and curtains, and beyond them, far down the slope, I saw it: a glow like a city seen from a ridge at dusk, pale gold, vast, humming. Sound reached us across the distance — not music exactly. Repetition. A mass of voices moving in patterns too regular to be alive and too mournful to be machine.

The choir.

"There it is," Emrys said, and his voice shook. "There she is, somewhere in that."

And I put my hand on his shoulder — the shoulder of the man my father died hating, the man I'd held a knife to, the man I now trusted more than anyone breathing — and I said, "Then we go down and get her back. And when we get there, and they ask us what we're carrying —"

I stopped. Because I had been about to say *we're carrying my father's name, we're carrying our grievance, we're carrying justice*, and even as the words formed I felt the tollman's oil-dark attention slide across the back of my neck like a cold finger, and I understood, with the clarity the mountain grants in place of kindness:

Everything I named down here, I fed.

So I said nothing. I kept my intent wrapped in silence, folded small, hidden even from myself. And beside me Emrys went quiet too, and we descended together toward the golden hum, two orphans of the mountain's appetite, carrying less than we'd arrived with and more than we knew.
