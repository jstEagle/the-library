## 8. The Hollow Choir

The golden hum grew teeth as we descended.

I don't know how else to say it. What had been a warmth at the edge of hearing, a sweetness in the stone, resolved by degrees into something with structure, with intent — voices, hundreds of them, thousands, braided into a single unbroken tone that never paused for breath. It was music the way a glacier is water. Technically true. Utterly beside the point.

Sef heard it too. I watched it move across her face as we picked our way down the last of the bone-road's switchbacks: first wonder, then recognition, then a grief so sudden and so naked that I had to look away, because looking at it felt like reading her letters over her shoulder.

"You know it," I said quietly.

"My mother sang." Her voice came out flat, planed smooth by effort. "Lullabies. Harvest songs. Nothing like this. But it's still her voice. Under all of it. You can hear where she—" She stopped. Swallowed. "You can hear where she is."

*Where she is*, not *where she was*. I turned that over as we walked and found I couldn't decide whether it was hope or horror.

The road had changed while we weren't attending to it. That was the thing about the bone-roads no one could have warned us about, because no one who knew had ever come back to tell it: the mountain did not stay still. It read you as you walked, the way the lichen read you, and the road ahead assembled itself out of what you feared and what you remembered and what you were quietly trying not to remember. The walls here were no longer ribbed and pale. They had become something closer to architecture — vaulted arches of grey stone veined with silver-green light, pillars carved — no, not carved, *grown* — into shapes that might have been caryatids or might have been people standing very still, waiting for someone to notice they were suffering.

The memory-lichen thickened. It grew in long curtains from the vaults above, glowing that deep grave-green, and as we passed beneath each curtain the light would lean toward us like wheat toward wind, tasting.

I had lost the smell of my mother's bread two miles back. I knew this the way you know a tooth is missing — by the hole, by the tongue going again and again to the absence. I could remember *that* there had been bread, that there had been a kitchen, that my mother had stood in it. But the smell itself, warm and yeast-dark and specific, had been lifted cleanly out of me, and something down here was wearing it now, or digesting it, or hanging it on a wall somewhere in the dark like a stolen coat.

Sef had lost more. She didn't say what. She walked with one hand pressed flat against her sternum, as if holding a door shut, and I did not ask, because asking would have meant naming, and naming fed the mountain.

"Emrys." Her voice, when it came, was careful. "The singing. Listen to the words."

"I can't make out words. It's just—"

"No. Listen." She caught my sleeve. "Underneath. There are words under the sound. It's a mass. A service. It's been going on so long the words have worn smooth, but they're there. *Salvum fac.* Save. Over and over. Save, save, save." Her hand tightened on my arm hard enough to hurt. "It isn't praise, Emrys. It's begging. They're all begging."

And once she said it, I could hear it — the way you can't unhear a name once someone points at its owner. Beneath the golden braid of sound, a substratum of language, worn nearly to nothing, syllables rubbed round as river stones. *Salvum fac. Salvum fac. Salvum fac populum tuum.* Save your people. An endless mass, celebrated by a congregation of the sold, for a salvation that had never come, addressed to a king who fed on the asking.

We stood in the dark under the lichen-curtains and let that settle over us.

Then we walked on, because there was nowhere else to go, and because the humming in our own blood had begun to pull.

* * *

The choir hall announced itself first by temperature.

All the way down, the mountain had held a strange temperate chill, the cold of cellars and caves, patient and dry. But as the road widened toward whatever waited at its end, a different cold moved out to meet us — a living cold, an exhaled cold, the cold that comes off a frozen lake at dusk. Sef's breath plumed white. So did mine. And the lichen brightened around us, feeding richly now, drinking in something the air itself carried, until we walked through a corridor of green fire and I understood with a lurch that the light was so strong because there was so much to eat here. Memory, shed like snowfall. Selfhood, sloughed off in flakes. Twenty years of it, five hundred years of it, drifting down through the dark from the hall ahead.

Then the corridor opened, and I stopped understanding anything, because the scale of what opened before us broke the part of me that did the understanding.

The hall — cathedral does it better, but even cathedral is too small a word — rose in tiers, hollowed from the mountain's living heart, and every tier was filled with people.

They stood in ranks upon ranks upon terraced galleries that spiraled up into darkness beyond the reach of the lichen-light. Hundreds visible. Thousands implied by the architecture, by the tiers that climbed and climbed until the eye gave out. They stood shoulder to shoulder, motionless as the pillar-figures on the road, and they sang. The sound I had taken for wind, for water, for the mountain's own voice, was theirs: the endless mass pouring up through the dark, *salvum fac, salvum fac*, worn smooth as glass, and every one of them singing it with their whole ruined being, because there was nothing left of them to sing with *but* being.

They were not dead. That was the first thing. I had prepared myself — without knowing I'd been preparing — for bones, for corpses arranged in pews, for the tidy horror of the graveyard. This was worse. They were alive. They breathed; I could see the rise and fall of ribs under thin clothes, decades out of fashion, some of them rotted to lace, some of them whole and ordinary as Sunday coats. Their skin moved over living muscle. Their eyes—

Their eyes were open. All of them. Every rank, every tier, every face in that ascending multitude had its eyes open and turned forward, toward the far end of the hall, where the light changed, and none of them saw anything at all. The eyes were the worst part. Not empty — empty would have been mercy, empty would have meant nobody home, nobody suffering. These were *frosted*. Glassed-over, lit faintly from behind by something cold, like windows in an abandoned house with a lamp still burning in a back room. Someone was inside those eyes. Someone was looking out through the frost and unable to touch the glass.

"Oh," Sef said. Very small. A child's syllable in an adult's mouth. "Oh, they're *waiting*."

"For what?"

"To be saved. That's the mass, don't you see? They're singing the prayer for themselves. Forever. Because it never gets answered, so it never ends." Her face was bloodless. "He makes them pray for their own deliverance and he harvests the praying. That's the engine. That's what runs the court. Hope, ground fine, forever."

And I thought of Greyharrow, of the amnesty, of the law that had outlawed magic and emptied the witch-houses and put hunters like Vane on the streets with their ledgers and irons — and I understood, in one cold drop sliding down my spine, what fed *that*. Every hedge-witch who signed the amnesty and lived small and hated themselves for living. Every charm-seller hiding craft behind trinkets. Every quiet surrender in every market stall across twenty years, each one a little hope deferred, a little *someday*, a little prayer ground fine. It all drained downhill. It all ran to the mountain.

I had been tithing him my whole life. We all had. He'd built himself a kingdom on the interest.

*Don't name it,* I reminded myself, hard. *Don't hold it up and look at it. Fold it small.*

We went down the outside stair along the hall's rim, close to the wall, far from the front ranks of the choir. Close enough, though. Close enough to see faces. An old man with a cooper's scarred forearms, mouth open mid-syllable. A girl who could not have been more than sixteen when they took her, in a wedding dress gone yellow as old tallow. A woman whose hands were a scholar's hands, ink-stained ghosts under frost-pale skin. None of them swayed. None of them blinked. The blinking, I realized, watching for it with a dread I couldn't account for, was collective — every eye in a rank closed and opened together, slow as a lighthouse, on some rhythm I did not want to understand.

"We're looking for one person," Sef whispered, and her whisper was a scream's volume in that place; I kept expecting the choir to falter, and it never did. "In all this. How do we find one person?"

"She'll find us," I said, before I thought. "Or I'll find her. I'd know her anywhere. She—"

The word died in my throat. Because the mass shifted.

Not ended — I don't think it has ever ended, I don't think it *can* end — but shifted, the way weather shifts, a pressure change rolling through the ranks. The nearest gallery turned its heads. All of them. Together, smooth as a field of wheat bowing, several hundred frosted faces rotating in perfect unison toward the stairs where we stood, and the mass flowed on without them, unworn, unbroken, sung by the thousands behind, while the hundreds in front stood facing us and *regarded* us with those lamplit windows and did not see us, and did not fail to see us either, in some third state of attention that made my skin try to leave my body.

Then, as one, they inhaled. And the exhale was not the mass.

It was my name.

"*Emrys Nystrom.*"

A thousand throats. Softly. In unison. My name pronounced by the hollow and rolled up the tiers to the dark above, and the dark above received it, and somewhere far off in the mountain's heights something vast turned over in its sleep and considered.

Beside me, Sef had gone rigid, one hand at her knife. "They know you."

"They shouldn't. Nobody knows I—" And then the second wave hit, and I stopped thinking altogether, because the choir spoke again, and this time it was not my name.

"*Little owl. You came the long way round.*"

One voice. One only, out of all the hundreds facing us — a woman in the third rank, middle-aged, grey-streaked hair cut practical-short, hands folded at her waist in the exact attitude of a teacher listening to a student botch a scales exercise. Her mouth shaped the words in time with the chorus of her neighbors, but her voice came alone, distinct, threaded through the mass like silver wire through wool. And her eyes—

Her eyes were still frosted. Still glassed, still lamplit, still terrible. But behind the frost, something had come close to the glass. Something was pressing its palms against the inside.

Maren.

Twenty years older than the woman who had taught me to knot luck into red thread, to salt a threshold, to read the weather in a hen's temper. Twenty years of standing in this cold, singing this mass, and it showed — she was thinner, sharper, worn like a coin rubbed between fingers — but it was her. It was her voice saying *little owl*, which she had called me at eleven years old when I fell asleep over my copywork, and the sound of it went through me like a hook through the gills, hauling twenty years of grief and rage and runaway's shame up out of the deep water where I'd sunk them.

"You're awake," I breathed. "Mistress, you're—"

"One bar." Her frosted eyes did not blink; the blink belonged to the rank, and the rank would blink when it would. "That's what he leaves me. When something new walks in carrying an old shape. One bar of the song, I get to be Maren Nystrom for one bar of the song, and then the music takes me back." Her voice was level, precise, and that precision was the most horrifying thing in the whole hall, because I recognized it. It was how she talked when she was conserving herself. When there was much to say and no time. "Listen fast, little owl. You should not have come. There is no reclamation here. Whatever the map promised, whoever died giving it to you — the door only opens outward if someone takes your place inside it. Understand? The debt doesn't die. It *changes hands*. That's all the court is. A ledger that never closes."

"I know."

"Do you?" And for a moment — half a heartbeat — the frost thinned, and what looked out at me was not calm at all. It was anguish, twenty years deep, and it broke over me like cold water. "Then look at me and understand what you're asking. I chose this. Do you hear me? I stood at his altar with my eyes open and I chose it, and there has not been one hour of these twenty years I have not known exactly what I am. If you take my debt, you buy that. Not freedom. *This.* Singing. Standing. Knowing. Until someone young and foolish follows you down these stairs, and then you get one bar of the song to warn them off, and God help you, you'll do it anyway, because I just did."

The rank blinked. Slowly. Lighthouse-slow.

When the eyes opened, the thing behind the glass had retreated. Her mouth was already moving with the mass again, *salvum fac*, worn smooth, and I lunged a step forward before Sef's hand closed on my arm like iron.

"Mistress—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "Maren. *Please*. Is there a flaw? Is there any flaw in it, anything he doesn't own, anything—"

Her head tilted. Just slightly. A teacher's tilt, the old tilt, the one that meant *think, boy, you know more than you're using*. And her lips, moving with the endless mass, shaped three extra words inside the prayer — smuggled them in between the worn-smooth syllables where only someone raised on her cadences could find them:

*He inherits too.*

Then the frost closed over, and the bar ended, and Maren Nystrom was gone back into the thousand-throated dark, singing salvation to the thing that ate it, and I stood on the stair with her last three words ringing in the marrow of me and no idea at all what they meant — only that she had spent her one bar of selfhood, her single hour of Maren in twenty years, not on pleading and not on farewell, but on *instruction*. Still teaching. Still spending herself on me.

Sef pulled me gently back from the rail.

"We can't stay," she murmured. "The lichen's feeding on both of us faster now — can you feel it? It knows we've found what we came for. It's pricing it." Her eyes were wet and furious. "Emrys. I'm sorry. About her."

"So am I," I said, and could not have said which of the hundred things I meant by it.

We turned up the stair toward a side gallery, toward the dark behind the tiers where the road went on toward whatever heart the court kept — and that was when the music stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Mid-syllable. A thousand mouths closed on the worn-smooth words at once, and the silence that crashed down was so total, so sudden, that I heard my own pulse, heard Sef's breath, heard — underneath everything, coming down through the vaulted dark from the top of the hall —

Footsteps.

Slow. Unhurried. The tread of someone who has never once in ten centuries needed to hurry. And threading down through the tiers, parting the choir like a comb through hair, a cold that was not temperature at all but *attention*, vast and old and pleased.

Somewhere above us, in the dark where the lichen did not grow, the Winter King had noticed that two new souls had come to his hall.

And one of them belonged to him already.
