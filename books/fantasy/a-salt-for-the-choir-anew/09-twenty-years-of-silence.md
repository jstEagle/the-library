## 9. Twenty Years of Silence

The song did not end. That was the first thing to understand about the Court Beneath the Mountain: the song did not end, and neither did the one who sang it.

Maren Nystrom stood in the third rank of the hollow choir, her mouth moving in time with a hundred other mouths, and somewhere beneath the frost-glass of her eyes a small and stubborn coal remembered that once she had hated singing.

Once she had taught it. Once she had told a runaway boy with ink-stained fingers that a charm was only a song you could hold in your hands — that if he learned to hear the shape of a working, he would never need to see it. *Emrys*, the coal whispered, in a voice that no longer belonged to any living throat. *The boy's name was Emrys.*

Then the mass turned its next page, and the coal dimmed, and she sang.

* * *

Twenty years earlier, in a cottage above the tannery row where the wind smelled of lime and rot, Maren had known the plague was coming before anyone else did.

She knew it the way hedge-witches know things: not by prophecy but by attention. The rats had gone quiet. The river gulls were flying inland in ragged, purposeful lines. And old Tam Wicker, who sold eels at the Saltgate market and had survived every sickness Greyharrow had thrown at him across seventy years, came to her door with a fever-charm he'd bought from a rival and asked her, in a small voice, whether she thought it would be enough.

She looked at the charm — clumsy work, a luck-knot tied left over right when grief-work wanted right over left — and she lied to him. She said yes. She gave him willow bark and elderflower and sent him home, and she sat down at her table afterward and did not move for a long while, because lying to old men about their deaths is a weight that settles into the bones and does not leave.

That was the autumn the Magistrate's men nailed the new edicts to every door in the city. MAGIC IS OUTLAWED, they said, in letters tall as a child. PRACTITIONERS WILL BE TAKEN FOR THE COURT OF QUESTION. The law had been coming for years, gathering like weather behind the hills; everyone in the trade had felt the pressure drop. But the plague gave it teeth. When the fever reached the wealthier quarters and took the son of a shipping magnate, and a licensed physician failed where a hedgewife's poultice might have succeeded, the Magistrate needed someone to blame who could not argue back.

Maren counted her people that night, sitting by the banked fire with a slate on her knee. Forty-one names she knew of in Greyharrow and the villages within a day's walk. Hedge-witches, root-doctors, knot-tiers, bone-setters, the woman at the lamplighters' hall who could coax flame to burn blue and slow and kind. Forty-one lives braided thin through the city like threads of silver wire, invisible until you pulled.

She wrote them all down. Then she looked at what she'd written and understood, with the cold clarity that comes before terrible decisions, that she was not looking at a list of names.

She was looking at a debt schedule.

* * *

Now, in the choir, the memory surfaced like a drowned body rising — slowly, turning, clothed in silt.

The hollow do not dream. But between the verses of the endless mass there are rests, and in the rests something like remembering leaks through, the way water finds the seam in a wall. The choir sang of the Winter King's patience, his hunger, his long accounting, and in the spaces between the notes, Maren's twenty-years-buried self floated up and gasped.

*The plague winter.* Yes. There it was. The smell of vinegar and burning rosemary. The carts going door to door collecting the dead, their wheels ringing on the cobbles like dull bells. Her own hands, cracked from lye water, packing poultices into every basket that came to her door, teaching the children of the tannery row which moss to gather, knowing all the while that the cure she could give was nothing beside the cure she was forbidden to give.

Because the plague that winter was not natural. She had seen that early — seen the way it burned cold instead of hot, the way the sick dreamed of snow and woke asking for someone named nothing, no one, the king under the hill. Something old and patient had put its thumb on the city, testing the flesh of it, tasting. The hedge-witches fought it with what they had. The physicians bled the patients paler. And the mountain beneath Greyharrow grew fat and attentive, and waited for someone desperate enough to come to the door it had so thoughtfully left unlocked.

Maren had lasted until the ninth week of the plague. She remembered the number the way you remember your own heartbeat.

Nine weeks. Two hundred and six dead, by her slate's count. Eleven of her forty-one taken by the hunters — dragged to the Court of Question in irons, and none returned, because the court beneath the mountain had bought the Magistrate long ago too, and the Court of Question was less a court than a delivery system. A chute. Souls went down it like coins into a slot.

And then, on a night of hard black frost, the summons had come.

Not a letter. Not a man. A knock at her door at the hour when even the watch slept, and when she opened it there was nothing on her step but a single line of writing in the snow, in a hand so elegant it looked typeset:

**THE MAGISTRATE OFFERS TERMS. COME ALONE. COME DOWN.**

* * *

The audience chamber had been smaller than she expected. That was her first thought, absurdly, walking into the dark beneath the world: *all this dread, and the room is the size of my kitchen.*

Her second thought was that the Winter King was beautiful, and that this was the most dangerous thing about him. He sat at a desk — a desk! — with ledgers stacked around him like ramparts, and he wore winter the way other kings wore ermine: a cloak of falling white that never touched the floor, a face like moonlight on ice, and eyes the color of the moment just before you freeze to death, when everything begins to feel warm.

"Maren Nystrom," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like it was being read from a very old document. "Hedge-witch. Teacher. Keeper of forty-one souls, or near enough." He turned a ledger toward her. Every name she'd written on her slate was there, in his hand, already. "You've been keeping accounts. So have I."

"You have my people," she said. Her voice did not shake. She would remember being proud of that, later, in the years when pride was all she had left to sift through.

"I have eleven," he corrected gently. "The rest are merely *available*. As you are available. Everything in Greyharrow is available; that is what your Magistrates have never understood about the ground they build on. They think they own the city. They rent it. From me." He folded his hands. "But I digress. You received my terms?"

"The snow said come alone and come down. It didn't say why."

"The why is simple." He opened another ledger, and she saw the plague in it — saw the whole winter laid out in columns, every death, every fever, each one marked with a small, tidy notation. "This season belongs to me. Your city's suffering belongs to me. It ends when I am paid. Or it continues until there is no city left to suffer. Those are the terms the snow did not say." His smile was the crack in a frozen river. "But I am given to understand you hedge-folk prefer bargains to surrender. Very well. Here is mine."

He slid a fresh page across the desk. Blank, except for a single line at the bottom, waiting.

"Your Magistrate wants his plague gone and his blame assigned. I want what I always want. Signatures. Give me the eleven I've already taken — they're spoken for, they stay. Give me the thirty who remain, and I'll lift the fever from Greyharrow by morning, and your Magistrate, grateful and relieved, will outlaw magic forever, and your little silver-threaded lives can go on hidden and safe and *small* until the last of you dies of old age. That is the offer on the table. Thirty souls for a city."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the fever continues, and when the city is dying past saving, I take everyone anyway. Refusal only changes the order of things. Never the sum." He tapped the blank page. "Or —"

There it was. She had heard it coming, the way you hear the third verse of a song you half-remember, the way you hear your own name called from a great distance. She had known since the snow. Perhaps since the rats went quiet.

"Or," the Winter King said, watching her with the delight of a fisherman watching the float dip, "one soul may stand surety for many. An old law. A generous law. One soul, freely offered, of sufficient quality — and quality, Mistress Nystrom, you have — may buy a term of amnesty. Twenty years, we'll say. Twenty years in which I touch no one in Greyharrow. In which your Magistrate's outlawry keeps the trade hidden, and the hidden trade keeps my table bare, and everyone keeps their souls where Nature put them." He leaned back. "One for thirty. You do the arithmetic. I'm told you're good at arithmetic."

Maren looked at the blank page for a long time.

She thought of Tam Wicker and his clumsy fever-charm. She thought of the lamplighter's woman with her blue and gentle flames. She thought of an apprentice — no. She stopped that thought deliberately, set it down outside the door of herself like a coat she refused to wear into this room, because if she thought of Emrys she would think of what he'd say, and what he'd say was *don't*, and what he'd say was *there's another way, let me help you find it*, and he was seventeen and brilliant and wrong, because she had spent three weeks looking for another way and there wasn't one. There was only the page, and the pen, and the difference between one voice silenced and forty.

"One condition," she said.

The Winter King's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Negotiating. How wonderfully base of you."

"When the twenty years end, the bargain dies with me. Whatever becomes of my soul after — it doesn't pass to anyone. No inheritance. No chain. My debt is mine and ends at my ending. Swear it."

Something flickered behind the King's eyes — she would understand it only much later, much too late, standing hollow-eyed in his choir: disappointment. Not because he minded the clause. Because he had hoped she wouldn't ask. Hoped she'd sign blind and trusting, the way nearly everyone signed, leaving the door of inheritance ajar for some future desperate hand. He had wanted her chain to be long.

But a bargain was a bargain, even for kings. Especially for kings.

"Sworn," he said, and the word fell into the room like a stone into a well.

She picked up the pen. Her hand was steady. She remembered being proud of that too.

*Maren,* said the coal beneath the frost-glass, twenty years later, in the rest between verses. *You were proud. That was the worst part. You were proud, and you never once thought to ask us.*

* * *

Because here was the thing the choir taught her, in its wordless way, across twenty years of singing without self: she had never once considered asking.

That was the abandonment. Not the signing — the signing had been noble, the signing had saved thirty lives, the signing had bought Greyharrow two decades of uneasy peace and its witches their hidden, small survival. If she could have stood before those thirty people and shown them the choice, some of them would have wept for her and most would have lived, and the nobility of it would have been real nobility, shared, witnessed, carried together.

Instead she had gone alone. Had told no one. Had walked out of the mountain at dawn with the plague already lifting from the streets, and had gone home and packed her apprentice's belongings herself — folded his shirts, boxed his inks, set his practice-charms in a row on the table like little soldiers awaiting dismissal — so that when the hunters came for the last of the hedge-witches under the new outlawry, hers would be the easiest house to raid and his would be empty. She had engineered his running away. She had made certain he fled angry, believing she had grown cold, believing she had chosen her secrets over him, because an apprentice who hates his mistress leaves quickly and stays away and lives.

It was the cruelest spell she ever cast, and she cast it on a boy who trusted her, and it worked perfectly, and she had stood at her window watching the road for three days after he was gone, and then the King's summons had come again — *come down, come sing* — and she had locked her cottage door behind her and left the lamp lit, out of habit, out of hope, out of the small human foolishness of leaving a light on for someone who is never coming home.

*You should have told us,* said the coal. *Thirty lives, and you couldn't spend the truth on them.*

*Truth has a price too,* answered the ghost of her, the her that still argued, the her that kept the coal alive. *They'd have refused. They'd have insisted on drawing lots, or fighting, or finding a third way that didn't exist, and the fever would have eaten the argument along with the city.*

*Then let them refuse,* said the coal, merciless as only love is merciless. *Their refusal was theirs to make. You stole it from them. You bought their lives with their consent and called it mercy, and it was the same theft the King commits, only kinder, only smaller, only yours.*

And there was no answer to that. There had been no answer for twenty years. She sang instead.

* * *

In the choir, time did not pass so much as pool. But pools reflect, and lately — these last few turnings of the mass, these last few weeks above where snow was falling on a city that had forgotten snow — the reflections had changed.

The King was hungry. She could feel it through the song itself, the way a house feels a change in weather through its walls. The twenty years were ending. The amnesty was a candle burning down to its socket, and the King had begun to extend his season over Greyharrow, sending his white heralds up through the cracks of the world, and the choir sang louder, and the rests between the verses grew shorter, and in the shorter rests the memories crowded thicker, like refugees at a gate.

She saw the stranger now, in the rests. The frostbitten man with the map. She knew him — had known him, twenty years ago, as one of the eleven, a knot-tier named Aldric Sef-something, whose wife had been taken the year before the plague and whose grief had been the quiet, patient kind that outlives everything except its object. He had found the door she'd found. He had come down alone, carrying a map inked in wax and stubbornness, meaning to reclaim his wife's soul by assuming her debt — not knowing, poor man, that the debt of a sold soul is not transferable downward, only sideways, only to a volunteer of matching weight, and that a grieving husband's love weighs heavy but not heavy enough. The tollmen had unwound him like thread. What crawled back up the bone-road to die against a tenement door had been mostly map.

Mostly map, and one message, delivered by a dying man to a name he'd heard whispered in the dark places: *Nystrom. Find the apprentice Nystrom. Tell him the debt comes due at solstice.*

She had heard the tollmen speak of it, in a rest, and the coal in her had flared so bright that for one full bar of music she had *felt* her own hands — felt them as hers, cracked and scarred and clever — and the choir had faltered, one voice out of a hundred missing its note, and the King had lifted his head from his ledgers like a wolf scenting blood on the wind.

She did not flare anymore. She had learned. Carry nothing. Want nothing. Be frost-glass, be a bell with no clapper, be a mouth for the mass and nothing else. Let the boy — her boy, her runaway, her unforgivable abandonment, her Emrys, if it was truly him coming, and the mountain's attention said it was — let him find whatever he was going to find without her treason lighting his way like a beacon for the thing that hunted him.

But oh, the coal was heavy to keep banked. Oh, the rests were short.

And then, on a turning of the mass that matched no turning she had counted in twenty years, the choir's resonance shivered — not from within, but from without. Footfalls on the bone-road far above. Many feet, iron-shod, marching in step, descending with a warrant and a certainty that had not yet learned to doubt. The tollmen stirred in the dark like spiders feeling rain. And behind the iron feet, fainter but nearer, another tread: one walker, unarmored, moving fast, and wrapped around that walker's passing like warmth off a hearthstone, unmistakable, impossible, alive —

— the shape of a charm-song she herself had taught, sung badly, sung bravely, sung in a young man's cracked voice as he came down into the dark with no intention she could read and a salt-white hole in the world's ledger where his future should be.

Maren Nystrom stood in the third rank of the hollow choir, her frosted eyes fixed on nothing, and inside the frost-glass a coal that had banked itself for twenty years began, very quietly, against every law of the mountain and every rule of her own survival, to glow.

The next verse began. She sang it.

But she was counting the rests now.
