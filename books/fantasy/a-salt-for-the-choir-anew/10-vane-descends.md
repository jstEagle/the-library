## 10. Vane Descends

The dead man's boots were good leather, and that was the first thing Captain Ilsa Vane noticed about him, because it was the only thing about him that was not wrong.

He lay in the alcove behind the tannery row where the frost came earliest and stayed longest, curled as if asleep against the cold, and the cold had taken him with a thoroughness she had rarely seen outside the deep winter years. No blood. No wound. His lips were blue as slate and his eyes were open, fixed on something above him that was not the sky, and when Vane crouched beside him and passed her gloved hand before his face, his gaze did not follow it. It did not waver at all. Whatever he had been looking at, he was looking at it still.

"Name?" asked Corporal Dree, at her shoulder, notebook already out.

"Unknown." Vane turned the man's hands over. Frostbitten to the wrist, black at the fingertips, but the palms were calloused in a way she recognized — rope-burn ridges across both, the marks of a man who had climbed things he should not have climbed, or been dragged. "Search him."

They found little. A knife gone to rust. Three coins, two of them foreign, stamped with a mountain sigil no mint in Greyharrow had used in living memory. And folded into the lining of his coat, sewn there by someone who had wanted it hidden and found too late that hiding is not the same as keeping — a scrap of paper bearing a wax seal, cracked down the middle.

Vane held it up to the grey light and felt something old move in her chest, the way a floorboard moves underfoot in a house you thought you knew.

She knew that seal. Every hunter in the Magistrate's service knew it, though none of them had seen it pressed into anything but ash and rumor for twenty years. The sigil of the Court Beneath the Mountain. The seal that appeared on no warrant, answered to no law, and had been officially declared a superstition by the very office that kept a locked drawer of its impressions in the cellar of the Hall of Records.

"Captain," Dree said quietly. "The district."

"I see it."

Tanners' Row sat at the edge of Cindermarket. Cindermarket was charm-seller country — luck-knots sold as friendship bracelets, fever-charms dressed up as herbal sachets, hedge-craft wearing the thinnest of mundane masks. And one name in her ledger had waited twenty years under that district like a knife under a floorboard.

*Emrys Nystrom.*

She closed her hand around the wax fragment until its edges bit through her glove.

* * *

The Hall of Records smelled of dust and lamp oil and, on the lower floors, of something older — the particular staleness of paper that has been forbidden to breathe. Vane went down alone. She always went down alone. The cellar archive was hers by seniority and by appetite; the other captains called it her crypt, and she let them, because a woman who enjoys her work frightens men who merely endure theirs, and fear was a currency she had never minded earning.

The drawer was iron, and the lock took her personal key, and inside lay a ledger bound in grey calfskin with a title burned into the cover in letters so shallow they might have been an accident of the binding:

*Debts of Record. Sealed by order of Magistrate Corvane. Year of the Amnesty.*

Twenty years ago. The year the law changed. The year magic became contraband in Greyharrow overnight — not gradually, not by debate, but by a single edict issued in midwinter with a speed that had always struck Vane, in her more honest hours, as less like legislation than like panic. She had been a young hunter then. She had believed the edict was wisdom. She had built her whole life on the belief that the law was a wall between the city and the dark, and walls do not ask why they were built.

She opened the ledger.

The first pages were administrative. Dates. Signatures. Payments recorded in sums that made her frown — payments from the Magistrate's discretionary fund, the one that answered to no audit, flowing out month after month, year after year, twenty years unbroken, to a recipient listed only as *the Steward Below*. Beside each payment, in a clerk's careful hand, a single word of purpose:

*Toll.*
*Toll.*
*Toll.*
*Toll.*

Vane turned faster. The tolls grew. In famine years they doubled. In plague years they tripled. And then, forty pages in, the handwriting changed — became larger, more deliberate, written by a different hand entirely, a hand that had signed itself without shame:

*Received of Magistrate Corvane, in settlement of account: one soul, freely offered. Witnessed and entered.*

And beneath it, another. And beneath that, another.

Magistrate Aldous Reeve. Magistrate Corvane himself. Magistrate Hetta Morrow, whose portrait hung in the Hall of Justice above the words *LET THE LAW BE BLIND AND THEREFORE JUST.* Magistrate after magistrate after magistrate, going back not twenty years but — Vane's breath went out of her slowly — going back generations, the ink fading and freshening and fading again like rings in a felled tree. Souls sold. Freely offered, every entry insisted, in the same formula, the same legal cadence, the same language the Magistracy used for property transfers and executions of warrant. The court below had not stolen from Greyharrow's rulers.

It had done business with them.

Her finger stopped on a page near the end, where the entries thinned and the last signature was freshest. Not a magistrate this time. A name she did not know, followed by a note in the margin, small and hurried, as if the clerk had written it against orders:

*Buyer claims kinship right. Attempted reclamation. Rejected — debt not assumable by force. Subject deceased on return. See attached.*

Attached was nothing. There was never anything attached. But Vane knew the shape of what she was reading, because she had spent twenty years learning the shapes of crimes, and this was the shape of a cover-up so old it had fossilized into procedure. The law that outlawed magic. The amnesty. The edict that had come down in midwinter like a stone dropped down a well.

It had not come from wisdom. It had come from *terms*.

She stood in the lamplight for a long time, the ledger open in her hands, listening to the building settle above her the way a body settles when it lies.

Then she closed the book, put it under her arm, and went upstairs to write a warrant.

* * *

"You can't take a squad into the mountain," said Undersecretary Pell, wringing his soft hands over his desk. "There is no mountain jurisdiction. There is no—"

"There's a dead man in Tanners' Row with a court seal in his coat," Vane said. "There's a ledger in your own cellar showing your predecessors selling souls like grain futures. And there's a fugitive apprentice carrying a map to all of it. I'm taking four hunters and I'm taking them tonight, before Nystrom reaches whatever's down there and before whoever's been paying these tolls decides the cleanest solution is burning the evidence. That evidence might include me."

Pell's mouth worked. "The Magistrate will hear of this insubordination."

"The Magistrate," Vane said, "can be heard of at my convenience, once I've established which of his predecessors' signatures he's been honoring." She let that sit. Pell went the color of tallow. She had not known, then. But he had known something; men who knew nothing did not go that color. "Squad assembles at the north gate within the hour. If anyone asks, we're pursuing a murder suspect into the old mine workings."

"And if they ask which murder?"

"The one that hasn't happened yet," Vane said, and left him to make sense of that, if he could.

* * *

The collapsed mine above the Hollow Vein seam had been sealed by Magistrate's order for thirty years, and the seal was broken now — timbers pried, stone shifted, the dark beyond breathing out air that smelled of wet iron and, beneath that, faintly, sweetly, of lichen. Vane knelt at the breach and touched the broken wood. Splinters still sharp. Hours old, perhaps less.

"He's ahead of us," said Sergeant Brakk, a slab of a man with a crossbow he treated like a child.

"He's ahead of someone," Vane corrected. She held up the wax fragment, turning it. "This seal was sewn into a dead man's coat lining. Dead men don't deliver their own mail. Someone gave him that map, and someone else may have given him to us." She did not say aloud the thought forming underneath, the one with teeth: *or the court let him die on the doorstep precisely so the map would travel.* Debts came due at solstice. The dying man had said so — no, he hadn't said anything; he'd been dead before she arrived. But the report from the watchman said he'd spoken. A name. *Maren Nystrom.*

A witch's name. A name from the amnesty year, from the purge lists, marked *deceased* in a file Vane had read a hundred times and never once questioned until this week.

Everything questioned easily, once you started. That was the horror of it. Twenty years of certainty, and it came apart like wet paper.

"Torches," she said. "Single file. Nobody touches anything, nobody eats anything, nobody answers any voice that doesn't belong to this squad. We go down, we find the fugitive, we come back up. This is a retrieval, not an exploration."

"And if we find more than a fugitive, Captain?"

Vane looked into the dark of the mine, where the torchlight reached and failed, and where — impossibly, absurdly, unmistakably — the rock itself seemed to continue downward farther than any mine in Greyharrow had ever gone, stairs carved in the living stone descending in long patient spirals toward a warmth that had no source.

"Then we find it," she said, "and we arrest it."

Brakk almost smiled. Almost. He knew better than to laugh at her, and she knew better than to pretend she'd meant it as a joke.

* * *

The bone-roads began three hundred feet down, where the mine shafts ended and the older passages began — passages that predated mining, predated the city, predated, Vane suspected, most of what she had been taught to believe about the world. The walls were veined with lichen that glowed a pale, cold green, and it grew thickest wherever the stone was carved with writing so old the letters had worn to suggestion.

Dree ran his fingers along one vein of lichen and snatched his hand back. "Captain. It's warm."

"It's hungry," Vane said. She had read the field reports. Everyone had read the field reports and filed them under folklore: the roads below fed on memory, took a toll of recollection from every traveler, mile by mile. She had filed those reports herself, once, under *unverified*. Now she walked among them with her hand on her sword hilt and made a private inventory of her own mind, itemizing what she could not afford to lose. Her mother's voice. The smell of the harbor in autumn. Her brother's laugh — quick, cracked, always half a syllable from trouble.

Tam. Executed eleven years ago for a theft he probably committed and a conspiracy he definitely didn't. She had signed the transport order herself, because the law required it and because refusing would have cost her everything, and she had told herself for eleven years that the law requiring it was enough. That the wall did not need to explain itself.

She thought of the ledger in her saddlebag. *Freely offered,* said every entry. *Freely offered, witnessed, entered.* How many of those souls had been offered by people standing exactly where she had stood — law on one side, love on the other, and the paperwork already drawn?

"Captain." Brakk's voice, low. "You want to look at this."

Ahead, the passage opened into a gallery where the lichen grew in curtains, and the curtains framed a door. Not a mine door. A court door — tall, black wood banded in white metal, and upon it, pressed into a plate of lead as if by a signet ring the size of a shield, the seal from the dead man's coat.

Beneath the seal, in lettering that glowed faintly with its own borrowed light, an inscription. Dree read it aloud, halting, as though the words weighed something:

*"All debts are inherited. All inheritances are debts. Enter freely, and be entered."*

"Charming hospitality," muttered Corporas, the youngest of them, and his joke died in the air the way jokes die in churches.

Vane drew her warrant from its oilskin case — properly drafted, properly sealed, *Emrys Nystrom, charges of unlawful craft and obstruction*, the ink still sharp — and held it up to the door as if the door might read it. As if the law were a thing that traveled. As if the wall extended this far down.

The door swung open at her touch, silently, without hinges she could find, onto a stairway of white stone descending into a glow like frozen moonrise, and from far below rose a sound that stopped every one of them where they stood.

Singing. Many voices. One song. Wordless, vast, patient — a mass that had been going on, Vane understood with a certainty that arrived whole and unwelcome, since before her grandmother's grandmother, and would go on after the last stone of Greyharrow fell, unless someone made it stop.

"Captain?" Dree whispered. "Your orders?"

Vane looked at the warrant in her hand. At the singing below. At the four faces turned to hers, waiting for the law to tell them what was real.

"We descend," she said, and her voice held, because her voice always held, even now — especially now, when holding it was the last piece of the old faith she had left. "Stay close. Count your memories as you spend them. And if any of you hears a voice offering you a bargain—"

She stepped through the door.

"—you tell me the price before you pay it. That's an order."

Below, the choir sang on, and somewhere in its endless weaving of voices, Vane thought she heard — though she would deny it to her grave — a single thread of melody that sounded almost like her brother's laugh, quick and cracked, calling her down.
