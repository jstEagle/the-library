## 11. The Bargain-Keeper

The steward came for them at the hour when even lichen forgets to shine.

Emrys heard him before he saw him — a sound like a ledger being closed, soft and enormous, echoing up the bone-road from a direction that had no direction. The road beneath the mountain did not bend so much as *agree* to turn, and where it agreed this time, it opened onto a chamber that had clearly been waiting for them longer than they had been walking.

It was round, and it was warm, and both facts were wrong.

Everything else in the deep places had been cold — a cold that lived in the teeth, in the marrow, in the spaces between thoughts. But this room held a hearth-fire that burned without smoke, without wood, without any color Emrys had ever seen flame wear. It burned the color of old debts: a dull, patient amber, like lamplight through honeyed glass, like the last coin in a miser's purse. The warmth it gave was real, which somehow made it worse. Warmth, here, was bait.

And in the center of the room stood a desk.

Not an altar. Not a throne. A *desk* — oak, scarred, ink-stained at the corners where a hundred thousand elbows had leaned while their owners signed away something they could not take back. Behind it sat the steward, and Emrys's first foolish thought was that he looked like every tax-clerk Emrys had ever cheated in Greyharrow's lower markets: gray-robed, thin-fingered, spectacles of smoked crystal perched on a nose like a knuckle. His skin had the waxy translucence of someone who had been indoors for a very long time. Centuries, perhaps. His eyes, when he raised them, were the amber of the fire.

"Sit," he said. "You'll want to sit."

There were two chairs. They had not been there a moment ago. Sef looked at Emrys — her jaw still set from the toll-road, her father's name gone out of her like a tooth pulled, leaving a socket of silence where forty years of memory used to live — and then she sat, because her legs had been carrying grief for three days and the chair offered no argument.

Emrys remained standing. It was a small rebellion and he knew it, and he suspected the steward knew it too, and filed it away with everything else.

"You're the bargain-keeper," Emrys said.

"I keep bargains." The steward drew a ledger toward himself across the desk's scarred face. The book was vast — thick as a tombstone, its cover the grey of mountain granite, its pages whispering against each other with a sound like wind through a charnel field. "I do not make them. That distinction matters, though no one who sits in that chair has ever believed me. They all think, at first, that I am the trap." He turned a page. "The trap is much older than I am. I merely keep the accounts."

"The dying man," Emrys said. "In Greyharrow. Corvin —" he caught himself; Sef flinched at the name, or at the ghost of it, since her father's name was no longer hers to say. He adjusted. "*Sef's father.* He came to reclaim a soul. My mistress told him nothing of how."

"No," the steward agreed mildly. "Your mistress could not have told him. That is part of the debt you are about to ask me to describe. Silence is not merely a clause, hedge-witch. It is a *load-bearing wall*."

He turned the ledger around.

The page he showed them was written in a hand Emrys recognized with a sickness that started in his stomach and climbed. He had spent six years copying that hand — the sharp ascenders, the way Maren Nystrom crossed her t's like small wounds. Here it filled an entire column, but the letters had degraded, row by row, as if the writer had been erasing herself mid-sentence: the early lines crisp and furious, the later lines thinning, spacing widening, until the final entries were barely more than frost-ferns on glass, the handwriting of someone forgetting what hands were for.

**MAREN NYSTROM. SOLD IN FULL. TERM: PERPETUITY. SERVICE: THE CHOIR. SILENCE: ABSOLUTE. PURCHASED AGAINST: AMNESTY, CITY OF GREYHARROW, ALL PRACTITIONERS THEREIN, IN PERPETUITY.**

Beneath it, in a different hand — older, formal, the hand of some long-dead clerk — the terms of redemption.

Emrys read them twice. Then a third time, because his mind kept sliding off them the way rain slides off oiled leather, refusing to believe what it understood.

"A soul sold to this court may be reclaimed by one means only," the steward said, in the tone of a man reciting something he had recited ten thousand times and would recite ten thousand more. "Another soul must assume the debt entire. Term, service, and silence. Not partially. Not in substitution. Entirely. The new debtor takes the full term remaining — in your mistress's case, perpetuity — the full service required — the choir — and the full silence, absolute and unbroken, forever."

"And the original seller?" Sef asked. Her voice was flat. She had learned on the bone-road what questions cost, and she rationed them now like bread in a siege.

"Freed," said the steward. "Whole. Their memory returns, their selfhood returns, their name returns if a name was paid. They walk out of here owing nothing to anyone." He smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, because it was genuinely kind. "That is not cruelty. That is the mercy built into the architecture. The court does not *want* souls to be unreclaimable. Unreclaimable souls are dead assets. The court wants souls to be reclaimable at a price no one can pay — which is a very different thing, and far more profitable."

Emrys stared at the page. At Maren's dissolving handwriting. Twenty years of it. Twenty years of service already rendered, and the term was perpetuity, which meant the twenty years had purchased precisely nothing except the city's amnesty and her own erosion.

"The stranger," he said slowly. "He assumed he could buy her back. Coin, or blood, or —"

"He attempted assumption," the steward said. "Yes. We have his file." Another page turned — the ledger seemed to turn its own pages when it wished, Emrys realized, and the steward merely indulged it. A new column: a cramped, hurried hand Emrys didn't know, though Sef went white beside him. **CORVIN ASHVALE. ATTEMPTED ASSUMPTION OF THE DEBT OF SELA ASHVALE. FAILED AT THE THIRD CLAUSE.**

"The third clause," Emrys repeated.

"Silence." The steward removed his smoked-crystal spectacles and polished them on his robe, an absurdly domestic gesture. "Term, most debtors can bear. Service, many can bear — the choir is terrible, but it is not pain, exactly. It is the absence of pain, and of pleasure, and of everything else. But silence." He resettled the spectacles. "Silence requires that you never tell another living soul what you have given up, or why, or for whom. Not a hint. Not a song lyric. Not a look held too long across a market square. And here is the elegant cruelty, hedge-witch, the detail your stranger did not survive discovering: *love makes silence impossible*."

The fire popped, softly, like a knuckle cracking.

"He found his wife in the choir," the steward continued, and beside Emrys, Sef made a sound like something tearing quietly. "One bar of her voice among ten thousand. And he broke. Of course he broke. He tried to sing her name into the mass — a single syllable, less than a syllable, a breath shaped like a name — and intention is contract, down here. The moment he intended her rescue rather than his service, the assumption voided itself. He was ejected from the debt. Ejected from the court. And the cost of a failed assumption is—" the steward consulted the ledger, unnecessarily, Emrys thought; the gesture was pure theater, or pure pity— "well. You saw what it was. The cold took him somewhere above ground, in a city he had walked home to for fifty years. He died with a map in his hands and his daughter's mother still singing."

Emrys did not look at Sef. Looking at her would have been a kind of theft, and he had stolen enough on this road.

Instead he looked at the ledger, and let the shape of the trap assemble itself in his mind, piece by piece, the way he'd once assembled lock-picks from bent nails in Maren's kitchen while she pretended not to watch.

Because it *was* a lock, and it was beautiful, and he hated it the way a craftsman hates a rival's flawless work.

"Anyone may attempt assumption," he said. "But only willingly. Only with open eyes. And the moment love enters — the moment you *care* whether it succeeds — the intention poisons the contract."

"Not quite." The steward raised one waxy finger. "You are close, and being close is how the trap feeds. Listen carefully, because I am permitted to explain the mechanism, and no one has ever listened carefully. Intention is not the poison. *Specificity* of intention is. The debt must be assumed for its own sake — accepted entire, as a thing in itself, without attachment to outcome. If you sign in order to free her, you have made the signing conditional, and conditionality voids the contract at the moment of testing. But if you sign because the debt *ought to be carried*, because the carrying itself is right, because you would carry it even if she walked out of this mountain and never learned your name —" he spread his thin hands — "then the contract holds. The court has been defeated that way exactly once in a thousand years, and the one who did it did not mean to, which tells you everything."

"And how many," Emrys said quietly, "have sat in that chair? How many have signed?"

The steward regarded him over the spectacles. For a moment the amber of his eyes flickered, and behind it — very deep, like a coin at the bottom of a well — Emrys thought he saw something tired. Anciently, unbearably tired.

"Four hundred and eleven attempts," the steward said. "Six successes. And of the six, three were persons so emptied by grief that love had nothing left to grip." He closed the ledger. The boom of it went through the chamber and up the bone-roads, and somewhere far above, Emrys fancied he heard the choir falter — half a note of discord, quickly smoothed. "You understand now what your stranger understood too late. The rule is not designed to prevent reclamation. It is designed to ensure that every reclamation costs exactly one additional soul, forever. The court does not lose a debtor when one is freed. It gains one. Compound interest, hedge-witch, compounded in flesh. Your mistress has been, for twenty years, not a prisoner but an *investment*. And whoever signs for her will become the same."

The fire burned its patient amber. Sef's hand lay on the desk, near the ledger, and Emrys watched her fingers and hated himself for watching them.

"There is a question you are not asking," the steward said to her, gently.

"I know what my father's file says," Sef answered. Her voice was ash and iron. "I've known since the toll-road. I know what's in it about my mother. And I know what I came down here to do."

"Sef." Emrys said her name before he could stop himself, and immediately understood his error, because the steward's eyes moved between them with the mild satisfaction of a clerk watching two figures reconcile in opposite columns.

"Yes," the steward said. "I imagine you do. And I imagine, hedge-witch, that you intend to prevent her. Which raises the only interesting accounting question this office has seen in a generation: which of you loves the other enough to lie about it?"

He reopened the ledger to a blank page. The paper gleamed. Somewhere in its fibers, faintly, Emrys could see lines already forming — waiting, like snow waiting for footprints.

"Now," said the bargain-keeper, dipping a pen in ink the color of the fire. "Shall we discuss what each of you intends to sacrifice, and whether either of you can afford the truth of it?"

* * *

They walked out of the chamber an hour later — or what felt like an hour; time under the mountain moved like syrup off a spoon — and neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was the loudest thing either had ever carried.

The steward's words went with them, down the bone-road, past lichen dimming as their memories thinned:

*A soul may be reclaimed only if another willingly assumes the debt entire. Term. Service. Silence. And silence is broken by love, and intention is tested, and the court always collects.*

Ahead, the road forked. One branch descended toward a distant glow — the choir-halls, where Maren's frosted eyes waited. The other climbed toward a crack of grey air that might, if the map could be trusted, and the map could rarely be trusted, lead toward the world of weather and warrant-hunters and a city buried in coming snow.

Sef stopped at the fork. She did not look at Emrys. She looked down the descending road, at the glow, at whatever she was deciding, and Emrys saw her shoulders settle into the particular stillness of a person who has stopped weighing options and begun carrying one.

"Sef," he said again.

"Don't," she said.

And she stepped onto the downward road alone.
