## 12. What Sef Carries

The fire in the waystation had burned down to a red eye by the time Sef finally spoke.

They had made it as far as the old toll-house at the mountain's knee — a squat stone shelter built by miners who'd never come back, its walls furred with frost that did not melt even when Emrys fed the hearth. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters like something patient. Inside, the lichen-light from their packs threw pale green shadows, and every shadow looked like a person deciding whether to approach.

Sef sat with her back to the wall and her father's knife across her knees. She had not sharpened it since the tollman's bridge. She wasn't sure she ever would again. A knife needed a hand that knew what it was for, and hers had been uncertain since the moment she'd watched a man bargain away his own name for safe passage — watched him do it *gladly*, the way you'd set down a weight carried too long.

Emrys was mending a luck-knot by firelight, his fingers moving through the old patterns without his seeming to notice. She had watched him do it for three days now, on the road, in the dark of the mine-mouth, always the same small compulsive craft: over, under, tuck, breathe. It was the only prayer he had left, she thought. The hedge-witch's apprentice who ran away and kept running, right into a city that outlawed everything he knew.

"You should sleep," he said, not looking up.

"I don't sleep well underground."

"We're not underground."

"We're in its shadow." She turned the knife over. "That's worse."

He said nothing to that. He was good at saying nothing, Emrys Nystrom. He had a face built for it — narrow, watchful, a face that had learned early how to be furniture. But there was something underneath it tonight, some question he was carrying the way she carried her knife, and she was tired of watching him pretend otherwise.

"Ask me," she said.

His hands stilled on the knot. "Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the bridge. Since I gave the tollman my father's name." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used on people she intended to rob. "You keep looking at me like I'm a debt you can't read the terms of. So ask."

The luck-knot hung forgotten between his fingers. For a long moment the only sound was the wind and the fire's low breathing, and then Emrys set the knot down carefully, the way you'd set down something that might still be alive.

"Why him?" he asked quietly. "Your father. Why would a man spend twenty years trying to walk into a place like this? Alone. With no craft, no protection, nothing but a map he couldn't even read all of."

"Because it was his fault."

She hadn't meant to say it that plainly. The words came out like a tooth coming free — sudden, bloody, and with an awful lightness after.

Emrys went very still. "What was?"

Sef looked into the fire. It was easier than looking at him. Easier than looking at anything.

"My mother didn't sell her soul to the court," she said. "*I* did."

* * *

The story came out of her the way water comes out of a cracked jar — slowly at first, then all at once, faster than she could stop it.

Her family had been salt-farmers once, before Greyharrow grew up around the marshes and swallowed them. Not rich. Never rich. But they had a trade, and a name, and a strip of white ground that fed them, and that had been enough. Her grandmother had been a cunning-woman of the old kind — not a hedge-witch like Maren Nystrom, all knots and fevers and small mercies, but something harder. Something that dealt in *obligation*. In what was owed.

"There's a magic older than any court or crown," Sef said. "Older than the Winter King, maybe. My grandmother called it the tally. You wrong someone badly enough — blood-wrong them — and the wrong doesn't stay put. It doesn't die with the wronged. It *travels*. Down the family line, generation to generation, until somebody pays it or somebody ends the line paying it." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile. "My grandmother ended a feud that way. A rival clan disputed our salt-grounds. She settled it. Permanently. Eleven people, Emrys. She killed eleven people over a marsh."

The fire popped. Neither of them moved.

"The tally passed to my mother. That's how it works — the eldest carries it, and the eldest's children inherit it if she dies unpaid. A blood-debt that size doesn't fade. It *grows*. Interest, the cunning-women called it. Every year unpaid, the wrong got heavier, hungrier. By the time my mother was thirty, the tally had started... reaching. Taking things. Our cows birthed dead. Our wells soured. My father's brother drowned in two inches of standing water, alone, in a locked shed." Her voice stayed flat, reciting, because feeling it all at once would have stopped her mouth entirely. "And then I was born. And the tally saw me."

"What does that mean," Emrys said, "the tally *saw* you—"

"It means I was marked from the cradle. It means every cunning-woman in three parishes told my parents the same thing: that girl will pay her grandmother's debt one way or another. Either she'll spend her whole life being collected from — sickness, ruin, death coming for everyone she loves until the arithmetic balances — or she'll end it herself, young, and let the line close over her grave." Sef finally looked up. "My mother couldn't bear either. So she found a third door."

"The court."

"The court." The word tasted like ash and cold iron. "Beneath the mountain. Where debts go to be held. She went down with my father weeping at the gate, and she signed herself over — soul, self, service, silence, whatever their terms were — and in exchange, the court *assumed* the tally. Swallowed my grandmother's eleven deaths into its ledger. Because that's what they do, isn't it? They're not just buyers. They're *holders*. Every debt in the world wants somewhere to sit, and the court beneath the mountain has room for all of them."

"And you were free," Emrys said softly. "Clean line. No inheritance."

"I was a child whose mother walked into a hole in the world and didn't come back." Sef's hands had gone white on the knife-hilt. "Do you know what my father became after that? Not a widower. A *debtor's husband*. He spent nineteen years telling himself he'd get her back. That there was a rule, a loophole, some door he hadn't found. He sold the salt-farm. He learned to read maps. He wrote letters to magistrates and scholars and one very patient archivist who finally told him about the assumption clause — that a sold soul can be reclaimed if another takes the seller's bargain entire." She laughed, short and terrible. "You understand? He wasn't trying to save her. He was trying to *substitute* himself. Take her place in the choir so she could come home to a daughter who'd never even known why she left."

"He died trying to carry your family's debt," Emrys said. "Not mine. Not Maren's. Yours."

"*Ours.*" The word cracked out of her. "Don't you dare make it noble. He died because of what my grandmother did, and what my mother chose, and what I *am*. The tally didn't vanish when the court took it, Emrys. Debts don't vanish. They just change hands. And somewhere in that ledger under the mountain is a page with my blood on it, waiting for me to come sign it properly — and my father walked into the dark carrying a map instead of a sword because he thought love was a currency the court would honor." She looked down at the knife. "It wasn't."

The wind screamed against the shutters. Somewhere far above, snow was beginning to fall on a city that hadn't seen true winter in twenty years, and neither of them knew it yet.

* * *

Emrys was quiet for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — stripped of its careful neutrality, laid open the way he must have been the day he ran from his mistress's cottage.

"When I left Maren," he said, "I told myself it was because she was cruel. She worked me half to death, taught me nothing gently, never once said a kind word I didn't have to dig for. And all of that was true. But it wasn't why I left." He turned the luck-knot over in his fingers. "I left because I found out what she was saving for. There was a box under her floorboards. Money. Twenty years of it, coin by coin. And a paper beside it with a name on it — the Magistrate's seal. I thought she'd bought her way into respectability. Sold us out, maybe. Sold the craft out. So I took what I could carry and I hated her for six years, and it turns out—" His breath caught. "It turns out the money was for *us*. For all of us. Every hedge-witch in Greyharrow. She paid the amnesty with herself, and I ran from her the week she signed."

Sef stared at him. "You're telling me your mistress is in the choir too."

"Maren Nystrom. Hollow-eyed. Singing." He said it like a man reporting his own execution date. "So no. I won't make your father noble. I know exactly what it costs to run from someone who saved you. I've been doing it half my life." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and clear. "But here's what I can't stop doing, Sef. Arithmetic. Your mother assumed the tally. The court holds it. Which means somewhere in that mountain is a debt with your family's name on it — and a rule that says it can be reclaimed by assumption. Someone willing to take it entire."

"No," Sef said immediately.

"You haven't let me finish."

"I know where you're going. I've been there since the bridge." She stood, and the knife slid from her knees and rang against the stone floor, and she left it lying there, which frightened her more than anything else that night. "You want to take Maren's debt. I've watched you do the same arithmetic since the toll-house. And I'm telling you *no*, because I already know how this ends. Someone has to assume my family's tally. My mother tried to make it herself and lost. My father tried to substitute himself and died in the snow outside a stranger's door. There is exactly one person alive whose blood that debt belongs to, and she is standing in this room, and she has known since she was nine years old what she was going to do about it."

"Sef—"

"Do you think I followed you to kill you?" She laughed, and it came out broken. "I followed you because I hoped you'd fail. I hoped the map would burn, or the road would eat you, or Vane would catch you, and then I could stop pretending I had a choice. But you're competent, curse you. You're brave in this small stubborn way, and you read her handwriting in the margins, and you keep *going* — and every mile closer to the court, I can feel it. The tally. Like a hook behind my sternum, reeling me in. It knows I'm coming home."

The lichen-light pulsed once, slow, like something breathing in its sleep.

"My grandmother killed eleven people," Sef said, quieter now. "My mother sold herself to bury it. My father died in the snow rather than pass it to me. Three generations, Emrys, each one trying to hand the chain to someone else — to the court, to God, to a stranger, to time. And the chain just gets heavier with every pair of hands." She knelt and picked up the knife, and this time she did not hold it like a weapon. She held it like an heirloom. "Someone has to be the last link. Not pass it. Not pay it forward. *End* it. And it might as well be me, because I am the only one in the world it actually fits."

"That's not resolve," Emrys said hoarsely. "That's just shame wearing armor."

"Maybe." She met his eyes. "But shame is what I *have*. My mother gave her selfhood. My father gave his life. All I was ever given was the bill. So yes — I mean to sign. At solstice, when the court renews its contracts, I will stand in front of whatever passes for a steward down there, and I will assume my mother's bargain entire, and the tally comes home to the only throat it was ever measured for, and my line's chain ends with me. That's not a plan, Emrys. It's a *vow*. I made it when I was nine years old, crying into my father's coat, and I have never once unmade it."

The fire had burned nearly to embers. In the green dark, Emrys looked at her for a long, long moment — and she saw him decide not to argue. She saw something else too, something that scared her: the same look her father used to get, late at night, over his maps. The look of a man doing arithmetic he intended to act on.

"Then we go down together," Emrys said finally. "And we both keep our hands to ourselves."

"Agreed," said Sef — and did not believe him, and knew he didn't believe her either, and let it stand, because some lies are load-bearing.

Outside, the wind changed. It came down off the mountain now instead of up from the valley, and it smelled of iron and old snow, and somewhere high above them the first flakes of the long winter were beginning to fall on Greyharrow — fat, silent, wrong-seasoned flakes, falling like signatures on a page.

Sef stood at the shuttered window and listened, and felt the hook behind her sternum pull tight.

"They know we're coming," she said.

Emrys rose to stand beside her. Through the slats, faint as breath, the snow was starting.

"Then we'd better not keep them waiting."
