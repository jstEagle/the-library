## 13. The Long Winter Falls

The snow began while they were still a day's walk from Greyharrow, and it did not fall so much as arrive — a white decision made somewhere above the clouds and executed without mercy or pause.

Emrys felt it before he saw it. They had camped in the lee of a fallen watchtower, he and Sef, with the mountain's bulk shouldering up behind them like a sleeping animal, and in the hour before dawn his charm-hand woke itching under the skin. It was an old signal, one Maren had taught him in the first winter of his apprenticeship: *when the air tastes of iron and your craft crawls, something great is moving overhead.* He had thought, in those days, that she meant storms.

He stepped out of the shelter of the tower and looked up, and the sky was full.

Not cloud — snow. Snow in sheets, snow in walls, snow falling with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had all the time in the world and intended to use it. It came down in fat, deliberate flakes that caught what little light the east offered and turned it to ash-grey pearl. Within ten breaths his shoulders were white. Within a hundred, the road south had vanished.

"Sef," he said, and could not finish.

She came out beside him, pulling her father's coat tight around her throat, and stood looking at the sky with an expression he was beginning to know well: the flat, narrowed look of someone counting costs. "That's not weather," she said.

"No."

"That's him."

"Yes." Emrys held out his palm and let a flake land on it. It did not melt. It sat there, perfect and six-pointed and cold as a coin, until he shook it off. "Twenty years I lived under his amnesty and never once saw true snow in Greyharrow. Frost, yes. Rime on the gutters. But the Magistrate's records say the last real snowfall was the winter Maren—" He stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and he was tired of walking off cliffs. "The last snowfall was twenty years ago. The year of the bargain."

Sef watched the flakeless sky the way other people watched a knife coming toward them. "He's stretching," she said. "Reaching for the city. Solstice is close enough now that he can put his hand out and touch it."

"And snow means signatures." Emrys heard his own voice go thin. "Cold drives people desperate. Desperate people bargain. Every refugee on those roads is a soul he hasn't got yet — and every one of them will be offered warmth, bread, a bed, a future, if they'll only sign their name in his book. That's how the court feeds. That's how it's always fed. Not with armies. With winters."

* * *

They walked south through it because there was nowhere else to walk.

The road to Greyharrow had been empty when they'd come up it a week past, a rutted brown seam through dead grass with the mountains rearing blue-white to the north. Now it was a river, and the river flowed the wrong way — out of the city, toward the lowlands, choked with everything that could walk.

Refugees. Hundreds of them, then thousands, a slow grey procession of carts and backs and bundled children, all of them moving away from the snow that followed them like a debt collector. Emrys and Sef pressed against the verges to let them pass and found there was no verge left, only more people, trampling the frozen fields into a single broad scar of mud and ice.

He saw a woman carrying a loom shuttle and nothing else — not food, not bedding, just the shuttle, gripped in both fists like a talisman. He saw a man leading three goats and weeping without sound, tears freezing in the creases of his face. He saw children asleep standing up, propped between adults who no longer had laps to set them in. And everywhere, threaded through the crowd like red thread through grey cloth, he heard the same words, passed mouth to mouth:

*The King's season has come. The King's season has come at last.*

"They're not running from the cold," Sef said quietly. She had gone very still, watching a family pry open a frozen gate to cut through a field. "Look at their faces. Half of them are frightened. But half of them—"

"Half of them are hopeful," Emrys finished. His stomach turned over slowly, like something settling in deep water. "Because they've heard the old stories too. Winter comes, the King wakes, and the King provides. Warm halls under the mountain. Bread that never runs out. A place where the law can't follow." He swallowed. "The amnesty kept magic out of Greyharrow for twenty years, but it never kept the stories out. Stories don't need permission. They just wait."

A cart ground past close enough to touch, its wheels wrapped in rags, piled with household goods lashed under oilcloth. An old man sat atop the pile holding a birdcage with nothing in it. As it drew level with them, the old man's eyes found Emrys's — found, specifically, the charm-knots braided into his collar where he'd stopped bothering to hide them, this far from any hunter's eye — and the old man's face changed. Hunger moved across it like weather.

"You," the old man said. "You're one of the cunning folk. You can read the signs. Tell me — is it true? Under the mountain? Is it warm there? Do they feed you?"

"Grandfather," Emrys said carefully, "what have you heard?"

"Heard?" The old man laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "Everyone's heard. There's a man going up and down these roads with a ledger and a lantern, signing folk. Says the King's steward sent him. Says there's room for everyone willing to put their name down honest. Says the snow won't touch anyone under the King's peace." He leaned down from his perch, and up close Emrys could see that his eyes were rimmed red, that his hands shook. "My wife's coughing blood, boy. My daughter's got two little ones. What would you have me do?"

Behind him, Sef had gone rigid. Emrys felt her coat brush his arm as she leaned in. "This man with the ledger," she said. "What does he look like?"

"Tall. Grey as frost. Smiles too much." The old man spat, missing in the wind. "Folk say he's not a man at all. Folk say a lot of things." He gathered his reins. "Well? Will you sign us through, cunning man? Work your charms? Give us a warm road?"

"I can give you this," Emrys said, and took the old man's shaking hand, and pressed into it a luck-knot — one of the last three he had, braided from the stock he'd rebuilt after the raid, each knot a small working of finding and fortune. "Keep south. Past the shrine at Marrow Ford there's a granary the Order of the Quiet Hand keeps open in hard years. Tell them Vesper Fairweather's charity sent you — they'll grumble, but they'll feed you. Don't go north. Whatever you're promised, whatever the cold does — don't go north."

The old man looked at the knot in his palm as though it might bite. Then he closed his fingers around it, nodded once, and whipped up his horse, and the cart groaned away south with the rest of the river.

Sef was staring at him. "Vesper Fairweather," she said.

"It's a name that opens doors." He shrugged, uncomfortable. "She endowed the granaries before the outlawry. Before—" He gestured vaguely northward, at the mountain, at everything. "Before she became a story herself. The Order never forgot. It's the only charity in the lowlands that still takes hedge-folk's names without spitting."

"You knew her," Sef said. It wasn't a question. It had stopped being a question somewhere on the bone-roads, and now it surfaced again, patient as ice. "Before. You knew her well enough to spend her name like coin."

"I knew her well enough to hate her," Emrys said, "and then well enough to understand her, and I haven't yet worked out which is worse." He turned south, into the flow of refugees. "Come on. If the King's recruiters are working the roads, the roads are the worst place we can be."

* * *

They found the recruiter at dusk, exactly where the roads forked below Marrow Ford, and Emrys understood immediately why the old man's description had been wrong in every particular except the smile.

It was not tall. It was not grey. It was, if anything, generous — a broad, comfortable figure in a good wool coat, rosy-cheeked, with a lantern hanging from one hand and a ledger open on a folding table before it, and around the table perhaps forty people were queued in the falling snow, stamping their feet, breathing steam, waiting their turn to be saved. The creature — because it was a creature, whatever glamour it wore — looked up as Emrys and Sef approached along the queue's edge, and its smile widened by exactly one tooth.

"Friends!" it called, warm as a hearth. "Come out of the cold! Names in the book, warmth under the hill, bread on the board — the King's peace for all who ask it!"

"We're just passing," Emrys said.

"Everyone's just passing," the recruiter agreed pleasantly, "until they stop." Its eyes — and here was the wrongness, the eyes were the color of a frozen pond seen from beneath — slid over Emrys's collar, over the exposed charm-knots, and lingered there with an appetite that had nothing human in it. "Though you, master hedge-witch, you might find special accommodation. The court honors craft. The court *collects* craft." It tapped the ledger with one soft finger. "Your line has history with us, I think. Nystrom, isn't it? We keep excellent records."

The queue had gone quiet. Forty faces turned toward them, and in most of those faces Emrys saw the same expression: hope curdling into fear, fear hardening into something that could, given a push, become a mob. *Witch,* said the faces. *Witch, and witch-hunters follow witches, and hunters bring the rope.*

"The lady and I are leaving," Emrys said loudly, and took Sef's arm, and walked.

They made half a mile before the voice caught up with them — not shouted, simply *present*, arriving inside the snow itself, inside the space between falling flakes, so that it seemed to speak from directly behind their ears no matter which way they turned.

*"Nystrom."* The warmth was gone from it. What remained was vast and patient and cold as the underside of a glacier. *"Little runaway. Little thief of maps. You carry my mistress's teaching in your hands and my choir's song in your ears, and you think to walk out of my season. The solstice is four days off. Everything falls toward me now — the snow, the hungry, the hopeless. Even you. Especially you. Bring me your intention, boy. Bring me your signature. I have waited twenty years to collect the interest on Maren Nystrom's account, and you have been carrying it north to me like a dutiful son."*

Then the presence withdrew, folding back into the storm, and the snow went on falling, gentle as ever, indifferent as ever, and Emrys realized he had stopped walking and was standing in the middle of the road with his fists clenched and his breath tearing out of him in ragged white flags.

"Four days," Sef said beside him. Her face was pale but steady, and her hand rested on the place beneath her coat where she kept her mother's ring on its cord. "Solstice is four days off."

"I know."

"He spoke to you by name. He knows what you intend."

"I know." Emrys started walking again, faster now, angling off the road toward the dark shape of Marrow Ford's shrine, where a single lamp burned in a niche above the frozen ford. "And that's the problem, Sef. That's the whole rotten problem. On the bone-roads, Maren warned me — one bar of song, one flicker of herself, and she spent it telling me: *the King reads intentions, so carry none.* He can't see what you'll do. Only what you mean to do. Every plan I've made since we came down off the mountain, he's been reading over my shoulder like a schoolmaster."

They reached the shrine. It was barely a shrine — a stone hood over a lamp, a shelf of offerings gone to ice, the names of the quiet-handed dead carved in rows along the base. But the lamp was lit, and the alcove behind it cut the wind, and for the first time since dawn Emrys stopped hearing the snow as a sound and started hearing it as silence.

Sef crouched in the alcove, unwrapping the heel of bread they'd been rationing, and did not offer it to him, because she had learned he wouldn't take it. "So make no plans," she said.

"I can't fight a war without plans."

"Then fight it without meaning to." She tore the bread and handed him half anyway, and he took it, because her look permitted no refusal. "You told me once — on the bone-roads, when the tollman took Papa's name — you told me the mountain eats inheritance. Debts pass down. Bargains pass down. Crowns pass down." Her jaw tightened around something. "Everything there moves by inheritance. So don't inherit anything. Don't mean anything. Walk in empty, Emrys, and let the mountain guess."

He chewed the stale bread and looked out at the road, at the river of refugees still flowing south under the pitiless snow, at the far-off smudge of Greyharrow's walls where the lamps were being lit one by one against a dark that was coming earlier every night. Somewhere in that city, Captain Vane was either hunting them or had already gone into the mountain after them — he couldn't know which, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed coal. Somewhere under that mountain, Maren sang in a choir of the hollow, and the Winter King counted the days to solstice the way a miser counts coins.

Four days. The sand was running, and every grain that fell was a soul signed in the book below Marrow Ford, a family turning north instead of south, a name added to the ledger of the long winter.

"Empty," Emrys said at last. "I can try empty."

"You'll do better than try." Sef settled back against the shrine's cold stone and pulled her father's coat around them both, because the night was coming and there was only one coat. "You were his apprentice before you were anything else. And apprentices," she said, with the ghost of a smile that had no warmth in it at all, "are very good at seeming to mean one thing while doing another."

Above the shrine, unbroken from horizon to horizon, the snow went on falling — and far to the north, faint beneath the wind, Emrys thought he could hear singing.
