## 14. Interlude in Iron

They took her at the third toll.

Captain Ilsa Vane had counted the tolls the way she counted everything — in ink, in margins, in the small disciplined columns of a mind that trusted arithmetic more than prayer. The first had been a bridge of black stone over a river that flowed uphill, and the tollman there had wanted a memory of her mother's voice. She had paid it because the squad was watching and because she had not heard her mother's voice in thirty years and could spare it the way a miser spares a coin he never intended to spend. The second had wanted a scar, and she had given them the one from Kettering, and the flesh of her forearm had gone smooth and unmarked as a girl's, and Corporal Denny had looked at her with something like awe, and she had written in her mental ledger: *one scar, surrendered, value unknown.*

The third toll was iron.

Not iron the way the Magistrate's cells were iron — riveted, honest, smelling of rust and old blood. This iron was a gate grown rather than forged, great fronds of black metal curling like fern fronds out of the mountain's throat, and set into it a door with no handle and no hinge and a keyhole shaped like nothing she had ever seen except, once, in a fever dream she had never reported. The tollmen who stood before it were not the withered gray things from the upper roads. These were tall, and their faces were smooth as poured wax, and they wore the livery of the Magistrate's own guard — her guard — rendered in tarnished silver, as if the mountain had dressed itself in her world's authority the way a thief dresses in a stolen coat.

"Captain Ilsa Vane," the foremost said. Its voice was her brother's voice.

She did not flinch. She had trained herself not to flinch at the sound of that voice years ago, when it had first begun visiting her in the space between waking and sleep, saying nothing, only breathing, only being present the way the dead are present — everywhere and nowhere and always slightly behind her left shoulder.

"You have my name," she said. "The Magistrate's ledger has the rest."

The tollman smiled. It was a well-made smile, copied from a living mouth, and it did not reach the wax.

"The ledger," it said, "is why we've been expecting you."

* * *

They took her weapons the way a priest takes a confession — gently, with both hands, with a kind of terrible reverence. Her sword they laid on a shelf of stone and did not touch again, as if it were an offering. Her warrant — the warrant, twenty years old, signed by three Magistrates dead and one living, the paper gone soft and brown as old skin — the tollman held it up to the lichen-light and read it aloud in her brother's voice, every word, including the marginalia, including the note in her own hand from her first year of service: *Find the Nystrom boy. He was there when it happened.*

"You were a child," the tollman said, folding the warrant along its worn creases. "When your brother burned."

"Executed," she corrected. "The Magistrate does not burn. The Magistrate tries, convicts, and executes. Burning is what hedge-craft does to itself when it's caught."

"Is that what you believed?"

"I believe what is written."

"Ah," said the tollman, and something in the wax of its face shifted, a hairline fracture of something almost like pity. "Then the court has a document for you."

* * *

The room they brought her to was a cell, because the mountain understood what she was and gave her the shape of her own life turned inside out. It was iron, but the iron was warm. There was a desk — her desk, the one from the Magistrate's third-floor office with the wobbly left leg and the ring-stain from a decade of mugs — and there was a lamp that burned without oil, and there was a chair, and across the desk, in the position she herself occupied ten hours of every day, sat a figure she had been dreading and longing to see since the moment she had entered the dark.

It wore her brother's face at nineteen. The age he had been. The face as it had been the last morning, before the trial, before the sentence, before she had stood in the square with her hand on her sword-hilt because a captain does not weep in public and a sister does not weep at all, not where the ledger can see.

"Tomas," she said.

"Not quite," said the thing in her brother's face. "But close enough to talk business. You'd want it that way. You were always the one who wanted it that way."

She sat. She did not let her hands shake, and they did not shake, because she had spent twenty years building a woman out of discipline the way a mason builds a wall, stone by stone, and the wall held. "The court wants something."

"The court always wants something. That's all the court is — wanting, wearing a crown." Her brother's face tilted, and the lamplight moved across it wrong, the shadow falling the opposite direction from the flame. "But this particular want is simple. Elegant, even. You'll appreciate the elegance."

"Tell me."

"Tomas Vane." The thing spoke the name the way a clerk reads a heading. "Convicted of hedge-craft, third degree, in the winter of his nineteenth year. Sentence carried out. Body given to the lime pit. Soul — " and here it paused, delicately, the way a jeweler pauses before the stone — "held in escrow."

The word landed in her like a blade between ribs. "Escrow."

"Every soul the Magistrate's courts have condemned in the last two hundred years. The executions are theater, Captain. The smoke, the pit, the bell. The soul itself comes here. The Magistrates have known for generations. It's in the founding charter — the one clause no one reads anymore, because no one reads anything anymore, they only sign." The thing leaned forward, and its breath, when it exhaled, was cold and smelled of snow on stone. "Your brother's soul has been in our keeping for twenty years. Safe. Intact. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to claim it." Her brother's smile — the real one, the one from the summer before everything went wrong, the one that had made her follow him into the orchard at night to watch him charm the apples ripe — "And here is the elegance. We can't just give him back. Debts don't work that way. Nothing here works that way. But a transfer — a transfer we can do. You sign one document, Captain. One page. You assume his account — his term of service, his silence, his place in the choir — and he walks free. Out the mountain's mouth. Into the world. Alive in every way that matters. And you take his seat."

The lamp hissed. Or perhaps it was her own blood in her ears.

"You're offering me a trade," she said. "My freedom for his."

"We're offering you his freedom for yours. Same trade. Better framing." The wax face was very kind now, and that was the worst thing yet, because she recognized the kindness. It was her own kindness, the one she used on the families of the condemned, the one that said *the Magistrate is merciful* with her eyes while her hands filed the paperwork that was not merciful at all.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then he stays. Another twenty years. Another fifty. The escrow doesn't spoil, Captain. That's the cruelty of it. That's the *mercy* of it, if you like. He'll wait as long as it takes. He's already waited longer than he lived."

* * *

She asked for the document. Of course she asked for the document. She was Ilsa Vane; she had never signed anything she had not read twice, and she would not begin with her brother's soul.

The page was vellum soft as skin. The ink was already on it, all of it, every clause, in a hand that kept shifting — now her brother's, now her own, now, briefly and horribly, the spidery ecclesiastical script of the founding charter itself. She read it the way she read everything: top to bottom, clause to clause, hunting the trap.

The trap was that there was no trap.

That was the thing about the court beneath the mountain, she was beginning to understand. It did not lie. Lying was for the living, for the Magistrates in their marble hall, for the charter with its buried clause, for the whole rotten architecture of a city that had outlawed magic by writing magic into its own law. The court beneath the mountain only asked. It laid the price on the table and waited, and the waiting was the trap, because the waiting let you do the lying to yourself. *It's only a memory. It's only a scar. It's only twenty years, and he's worth twenty years, he's worth a hundred, he was worth more than the whole city —*

Her hand was on the page. She watched it there, her own hand, steady as a surgeon's, and she thought: *I have wanted to die for twenty years and called it duty.*

The realization did not come like thunder. It came like the lamp — a small flame in a cold room, burning without fuel. She had walked into this mountain with a warrant and a squad and a certainty, and the mountain had taken her squad one by one — Denny to the second toll, the Brask twins to the river that flowed uphill, young Pell to something in the dark that had not even asked a price but only smiled — and she had filed each loss in her ledger as *cost of pursuit, acceptable,* because that was what the ledger was for. That was what the ledger had always been for. A place to write down what you had spent so you never had to look at what you'd bought.

She had bought a city's safety with her brother's smoke. That was the trade she had made at nineteen, standing in the square, hand on her hilt, believing the Magistrate's word over her brother's eyes. And the city's safety had turned out to be a clause in a charter, and the executions had turned out to be theater, and the ledger — her ledger, twenty years of names and dates and convictions, the work she had told herself was justice — was a delivery route. She had been the court's courier. She had carried souls to this mountain one signature at a time and called it law.

"Captain," said the thing wearing her brother, gently. "The page is waiting. He is waiting. You are the only one who has ever been offered this. Do you know how many come down here and would sign in a heartbeat, if only the mountain asked them? It asked your witch. It asked the stranger with the map. It asked the girl's mother. Everyone says yes, eventually. Everyone has something they'd burn the world to get back."

"I know," Vane said.

Her voice came out strange. Quiet. Not the captain's voice at all.

"I know," she said again, "because I'm the one who taught this city to say it."

* * *

She stood up.

The thing in her brother's face went still — the particular stillness of a machine encountering an input it has no column for.

"Captain Vane," it said. "You should understand what refusal means. The escrow remains. The debt remains. Nothing changes except — "

"Except everything," she said. "You said it yourself. Nothing here works the way the living think it works. So I'll tell you what I think, and you can file it however you like." She picked up the lamp. It weighed nothing, or it weighed everything; her arms had gone strange and light, the way they did on the morning after a fever breaks. "I spent twenty years believing that law was the thing that stood between people and the dark. I executed my own brother to keep that belief. I hunted children for it. I wrote names in a book for it. And the whole time, the law was a pipe, and the dark was at the other end of it, drinking."

"The document — "

"The document is ash." She said it before she knew she would say it, and the moment she said it she felt it become true, the way a verdict becomes true the instant the word *guilty* leaves the bench. "I'm not signing. Not for him. Not for anyone. You want to know why? Because if I sign, then every soul in your escrow stays exactly where it is, and every soul after mine gets harvested the same way, and the pipe keeps running, and the only thing that changes is which Vane is in the choir." She set the lamp down on the desk, deliberately, in the ring-stain, the way she had never once allowed herself to set a mug in twenty years of service. "I refuse. Do you hear me? I refuse the trade, I refuse the escrow, I refuse the ledger. Write it down. I know how much your kind loves a record."

For a long moment the cell was silent. The lamp burned. The wax face of the thing across the desk did not move, and then it did — a slow unraveling, the borrowed features sliding like candlewax, her brother's face and her own face and a hundred other faces passing through it like fish under ice, until what remained was neither. Something old. Something that looked, absurdly, like a clerk who has just been handed a form that does not exist in any drawer.

"No one," it said, and its voice was a thousand voices and none of them, "has refused in a hundred years."

"Then your ledgers are due for an amendment," Vane said. "I'm told the mountain keeps excellent records. See that this one is accurate: Captain Ilsa Vane, of the Magistrate's Third Company, declines. On the record. For the archive. For whoever comes after me — " and she thought, though she did not say it, of a boy with a stall of luck-knots and a dead woman's map, of the name in the margin of her warrant, *find the Nystrom boy* — "for whoever comes after me, let them know the pipe can be refused."

The thing rose. The cell rose with it, the iron walls breathing outward, the warm metal cooling by degrees until frost feathered across the desk, across the unsigned page, across her brother's face as it finished dissolving into something that was not her brother and had never been.

"You understand," it said, almost conversationally now, almost tired, "that refusal has a price."

"Everything has a price," Vane said. "I've spent twenty years paying other people's. I find I'd rather start paying my own."

The walls opened. Not into freedom — she did not imagine, even now, that the mountain meant to let her simply walk out — but into a corridor of black stone, lit by lichen that glowed the color of remembered snow, stretching down and down into the dark where, faint and far, she could hear something that stopped her heart and started it again.

Singing.

A choir. Hundreds of voices, thin and cold and perfectly, terribly in unison — and threaded through the mass of them, one voice rising, breaking the unison, singing *against* the current, a counterpoint, wild and small and alive.

She knew that discipline. She had heard it once, twenty years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of bread and fever-charms, a boy of fourteen singing his mistress's scales wrong on purpose because it made her laugh.

The lichen brightened along the corridor, mile by mile, as if the mountain itself were leaning in to listen.

Vane picked up her warrant — the paper soft as skin, the signature of four Magistrates, her own marginal note — and tore it in half, and in half again, and let the pieces fall into the dark behind her like snow.

Then she walked toward the singing.
