## 15. Choir Practice

The mountain kept its own hours. There was no dawn beneath the stone, no dusk, only a slow breathing of the lichen-light along the bone-roads — the pale green glow that brightened when someone remembered something and dimmed when they forgot. Emrys had learned to read it the way he'd once learned to read weather over Greyharrow's rooftops: a flush of brightness meant Sef was thinking of her father; a long grey dimming meant she'd lost another piece of him to the road.

They had been walking for what felt like nine days and might have been three. The tolls had taken their tithe of names and small mercies. Emrys no longer remembered his mother's face — only that he had once had a mother, and that the absence ached like a tooth pulled whole. Sef had given up more. She walked now with her jaw set and her hands fisted in her pockets, as if she could hold what remained by force.

And ahead of them, always ahead, through corridors of ribbed stone hung with frost-flowers that chimed when the air moved, came the singing.

It had been faint at first, a rumor in the rock. Now it filled everything. It came up through the soles of their boots and settled behind their teeth. A mass without end, voices braided into a single seamless thread — hundreds of them, thousands, worn smooth as river stones, singing words that had long since stopped meaning anything to the mouths that made them.

*The Hollow Choir,* the map's margins had called it, in Maren Nystrom's small, precise hand. *They sing the King's season into being. Do not listen with your heart open. It will think you are volunteering.*

Emrys listened anyway. He couldn't help it. But he listened the way Maren had taught him to listen, twenty years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of tallow and dried nettle, when he was eleven and furious at the world for being small.

"Magic isn't shouting," she'd said, stirring a pot with a spoon carved from rowan. "Anyone can shout. Shouting is what gets hedge-witches burned. Magic is listening until you know the shape of the thing better than it knows itself — and then singing one note against it. One note, Emrys. Small enough to hide inside a heartbeat."

He had been a child who wanted thunder. He had run away from a woman who taught whispers. And now, in the dark under the world, her whisper was the only weapon he had left.

* * *

They made camp in an alcove where the choir's sound pooled and eddied, a pocket of relative quiet, like the lee side of a wall in a storm. Sef slept badly, curled against the stone with her knife unsheathed across her knees even in sleep. Emrys sat awake, turning the wax-sealed map over in his hands though there was nothing left to read from it. The blank space where the court lay had begun, in the last day, to pulse — a slow white shimmer, like breath on glass.

The choir sang on.

And underneath the vast seamless braid of it, if he held very still and let his own breath fall into rhythm with the stone, he could hear the seams. Voices that wavered where the others held true. A soprano a half-breath late. An alto that cracked, every seventh bar, on a note that must once have been hers alone.

Every seventh bar.

Emrys closed his eyes. He thought about fever-charms, which were the first thing Maren had ever taught him to make: not a charm to drive the fever out — fevers fought back, fevers had their own momentum — but a counter-rhythm, a small cold note sung into the blood's roar until the body, confused, remembered its own temperature. *You don't fight the fire,* she'd said. *You remind the house it knows how to be warm without burning.*

He began, very softly, to hum.

Not a melody. A counter-melody. He took the choir's endless line and found the places where it thinned — the seventh bars, the cracks, the half-breath latenesses — and he laid his own note against them, low, almost subvocal, a vibration more felt than heard. Where the choir said *serve*, he hummed the interval that meant *rest*. Where it said *forever*, he found the falling third that meant *enough*. It was old craft, older than the Magistrate's laws, older than the mountain: the craft of women who had healed livestock and quieted storms and buried their dead with songs no church had sanctioned, passed hand to hand down generations of kitchens and birthing-rooms, all the way to a boy in Greyharrow who had sold luck-knots from a market stall and hated himself for how small his life had become.

His life had not been small. His life had been hidden. There was a difference, and the difference had a use, and the use was this.

The lichen around the alcove flushed bright — brighter than he'd seen it since the tolls — feeding, he realized, on what he was remembering. Not losing. Giving back. Somewhere in the walls of the world, the road itself seemed to lean toward him and listen.

And in the seventh bar, the alto voice cracked — and did not resume.

* * *

She came apart from the choir the way a thread comes loose from a hem: slowly, then all at once. Emrys felt it before he saw it, a wrongness in the great braid of sound, a gap opening where a voice should be. Then frost-cracked footsteps, uneven, coming up the corridor toward the light of their camp.

Sef woke with her knife already moving. Emrys put a hand on her wrist.

The figure in the corridor wore the grey robes of the court's thralls, hemmed in hoarfrost, trailing rime like a bride's train. Her hair had gone white as salt. Her hands hung at her sides, fingers blue-tipped, and they trembled — not with cold, Emrys thought. With effort. Like hands holding shut a door against wind.

Her eyes were frosted glass. He had prepared himself for that. He had told himself, walking these last miles, that whatever remained of Maren Nystrom would be gone, worn away by twenty years of the King's service, and that looking into those eyes would be like looking into a winter pond: surface only, nothing beneath.

But the glass was fogging. From the inside.

"Emrys." Her voice was rust and snowmelt. It was also, unmistakably, the voice that had scolded him for sloppy knotwork and fed him barley soup and never once, in six years, told him she loved him, because love was a debt and debts got people sold. "Emrys Nystrom. You always did hum flat."

Something broke open in his chest and he was eleven years old again, caught out, ears hot. "You said my intervals were fine."

"I lied. You needed the confidence." She swayed. Sef caught her other arm without being asked, and Maren looked at the girl with those fogging-glass eyes and said, "You have her chin. Your mother's. She argued with the tollman for an hour rather than pay full price. I remember her. I remember—" and her face did something terrible and brief, a flicker of grief crossing ice, "—I remember being able to choose what to remember."

"How long do you have?" Emrys asked quietly.

"A bar. Perhaps two." She gripped his wrists with shocking strength, her cold fingers finding his pulse as if checking it, the way she used to when he was sick. "Listen. We have no time for tenderness, so I will spend it all on warning, and you will forgive me, because you always forgave me, God help you."

"Maren—"

"The King reads intentions." The words came fast, ground out between clenched teeth, each one costing her visibly, the frost creeping back up her throat between syllables. "That is the whole of his power, do you understand? Not force. Force belongs to the tollmen, and the tollmen are only ledgers with legs. The King reads intention the way you read weather. Every soul that walks into his court carrying a plan — *I will free her, I will break him, I will sign and then strike* — he tastes it before they cross the threshold. That is how your stranger died. That is how everyone dies here. They come with hope, and hope has a shape, and he takes the shape and leaves the husk."

Emrys's mouth had gone dry. "Then how—"

"You don't come with hope." Her grip tightened. "You come with nothing. You walk into that court hollow as the choir, empty as the ledger, wanting nothing, planning nothing, intending nothing — and he looks at you and sees only another instrument waiting to be played. And while he is not looking — because he does not look at instruments — you sing your one note."

"One note," Emrys whispered. The kitchen. The rowan spoon. Twenty years collapsing into a single lesson.

"One note. Small enough to hide inside a heartbeat." Her breath fogged and crystallized. "But hear me, boy, because this is the part that will kill you if you get it wrong. To carry no intention, you cannot merely *pretend* to carry none. He reads the pretense too; pretense is just intention wearing a mask. You must genuinely surrender the outcome. Walk in willing to fail. Willing to lose her—" her eyes flicked to Sef, and back, "—willing to lose me, willing to leave this mountain having done nothing, and mean it, all the way down, the way you meant running away from me. Can you do that? Can you want a thing so much that you can set the wanting down and walk past it?"

Emrys thought of the market stall. Of the charms labeled as trinkets. Of twenty years of setting down, every morning, the one thing he actually wanted, so that he could survive to want it again tomorrow.

"Yes," he said. "That's the only skill I have."

For one moment — less than a moment, a fraction of a bar — Maren Nystrom smiled. It was not the thrall's blankness and it was not quite the woman he remembered; it was something new, something the mountain had never seen, a smile made of frost and fury and fierce, unbanked pride.

"There's my apprentice," she said.

And then the light went out of her. Not gradually — all at once, like a snuffed candle, like a door closing. The trembling stopped. The fog cleared from her eyes until they were only glass again, and her hands went slack in his, and she stepped back from him with the smooth mechanical grace of the choir, and turned, and walked back down the corridor toward the sound.

At the edge of the lichen-light she paused. Just a pause. Her head tilted, slightly, the way she used to tilt her head when he'd almost understood a lesson but not quite.

Then she was gone, and the choir swallowed her voice again, seamless, eternal.

* * *

Sef sat down slowly on the stone, knife across her knees, staring down the empty corridor. "She knew you," she said at last. "In there. Under all of it. She knew you."

"She knew me." Emrys found he was shaking. He pressed his palms flat against the floor until the stone's cold gave him something else to feel. "One bar of selfhood. Out of twenty years."

"That's more than my father ever got." Sef's voice was flat, but her knuckles were white around the knife. "He died at the threshold. Whatever the King took from him, he took it before Dad ever heard a voice answer back." She looked at Emrys sideways. "You sang her loose. One song, and she surfaced. What happens at solstice if you sing to the whole choir?"

"I don't know." It was the truth, and it frightened him more than any lie would have. "Maren said the King reads intentions. If I go in planning to sing the choir free, he'll strip me before I draw breath."

"So we go in with nothing." Sef laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it anywhere. "My father came with a plan and died. My mother came with a sacrifice and was eaten. Everyone who's ever faced this court came carrying something, and it cost them everything." She turned the knife over in her hands, watching the lichen-light slide along the blade. "Maybe the only way to beat a thing that eats intentions is to starve it."

"Starve it," Emrys repeated softly.

The plan assembled itself in his mind — and he caught it, deliberately, and set it down. That was the discipline, wasn't it? That was the whole terrible discipline. He was allowed to understand the shape of the thing; he was not allowed to hold it. Understand like water, hold like stone. He let the idea flow through him and out, and felt, absurdly, the lichen dim around him in approval, or perhaps in hunger. Down here it was hard to tell the difference.

"We should sleep," he said. "Solstice is close. I can feel it in the rock — the mountain's counting down. And tomorrow we walk into the court, and I need to practice being empty."

Sef snorted. "You've been practicing being empty your whole life, charm-seller. Shouldn't take you long."

He laughed despite himself — a real laugh, startled out of him, the first in longer than he could measure. Above them, miles of stone and frost and singing away, the Winter King's winter deepened over Greyharrow, and somewhere in the great braid of the Hollow Choir an alto voice waited in its seventh bar, keeping, against all law and season, one small cracked note of herself.

Emrys lay back against the stone, closed his eyes, and emptied himself — of hope, of plan, of everything except the memory of a kitchen, a rowan spoon, and a woman saying: *one note, small enough to hide inside a heartbeat.*

Tomorrow, he would carry nothing into the dark.

And in the morning, when they rose to follow the choir down into the court, they found the frost-flowers along the corridor had bloomed overnight into shapes none of the maps described: small, precise, unmistakable — written in ice, in a hand Emrys knew better than his own —

*a margin note. Maren's handwriting. Two words:*

***carry none.***
