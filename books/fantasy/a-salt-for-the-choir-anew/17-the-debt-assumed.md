## 17. The Debt Assumed

The ink was still wet when the mountain noticed.

Emrys felt it the way a man feels a hook set behind his breastbone—not pain, not yet, but *attention*, vast and cold and patient as the dark beneath a frozen lake. The quill slipped from his fingers and did not so much fall as be *dismissed*, clattering against the ledger's iron stand while somewhere far above them the whole court seemed to draw one long breath.

He had signed Maren Nystrom's name in his own hand. He had signed it before Sef could reach the book, before the King's stewards could arrange their elegant cruelties, before anyone—including himself—had time to think better of it. That had been the whole of the trick, the only lesson of hers he'd ever truly mastered: *carry no intention*. A blank mind, an open hand, a signature given like a gift to a stranger.

Now came the bill.

* * *

It began with cold.

Not the cold of the bone-roads, which had been a living thing, lichen-lit and hungry for memory. This was older. This was the cold of the first winter, the one that had taught men to huddle and worship and bargain. It poured into him through the wet ink on his fingertips, up through the soles of his boots where he stood on stone that was not stone, and Emrys understood with terrible clarity that he was standing inside a contract, that the floor beneath him was the contract, that the air itself was clause and condition and covenant.

*Twenty years,* said a voice that was not a voice.

The Winter King did not speak aloud. Speaking aloud would have required a mouth, lungs, the vulgar machinery of breath. Instead the words arrived fully formed in the marrow of him, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and they were courteous the way an avalanche is courteous—it does not hate you; it simply arrives.

*Twenty years of service. Term entire. Service entire. Silence entire.*

And then the debt began to pay itself out into him.

* * *

He saw the plague winter.

Not remembered—*received*, the way a body receives blood from another vein, foreign and vital and wrong. Snow falling grey over Greyharrow twenty years gone, snow that fell for forty days without ceasing, and under it the coughing, the cart-wheels, the doors marked with chalk crosses that meant *burn the bedding and pray*. He saw a young woman with iron-grey hair and hands stained to the wrist with charm-ink walking door to door through streets the Magistrate had sealed, carrying fever-knots in her pockets like contraband mercy. He saw her kneel in the slush outside a tenement and press a luck-charm into a dying child's fist because the child's mother could not pay, and he heard her say—the words arriving in Emrys's own skull in Maren's own cadence—*"Tell them I'll come back for the rest."*

She never came back for the rest. There were too many restings.

He saw her stand before the Magistrate's tribunal, three old men in furs who had outlawed magic in the same season the plague made magic the only medicine left, and he watched them offer her the bargain they thought was a trap: *one soul, freely given, and the city's hedge-folk go free. Amnesty entire. Your craft walks daylight or it burns—you choose which.*

They had expected her to refuse. They had wanted her to refuse; refusals justified pyres.

She said yes before they finished asking.

Emrys stood in the court beneath the mountain with his eyes open and let all of it pour through him, because closing his eyes would have been a kind of refusal, and refusal now would unmake everything. His knees hit the iron floor. Someone was shouting his name—Sef, Sef's voice, raw and furious and frightened—but the sound reached him as if through deep water, through ice, through twenty years of accumulating winter.

*Service,* said the King, and showed him service.

* * *

He saw what Maren had done for two decades.

He saw her at the loom of seasons, if it could be called a loom—vast wheels of frost turning in a hall with no walls, threads of cold drawn out and woven down onto the world above. He saw her hands, younger than they should have been, the court keeping its property in good repair, guiding winter's edge so it grazed Greyharrow gently, year after year, holding back the killing frosts her bargain technically permitted. She had spent her servitude softening the very thing she'd sold herself to. No one had asked her to. No one had known.

*She cheated,* Emrys realized, and something in his chest cracked open like river ice. *All this time she cheated them. Every mild winter was theft.*

Then he saw the rest, and the crack became a wound.

He saw the tolls she'd paid to do it. Each softened frost bought with a memory surrendered to the choir—her mother's face, the smell of her first teacher's kitchen, the name of the boy she'd loved at seventeen, the taste of bread on a summer morning, the sound of her own laughter. He watched her walk to the choir-loft night after night and give away pieces of herself to buy small mercies for a city that had outlawed her, until there was so little left that the hollow-eyed woman singing in the loft was less a person than a residue, a sediment of sacrifice, a shape that had once been Maren Nystrom and was now mostly absence wearing her posture.

That was why her eyes were frosted glass. Not punishment. *Expenditure.*

*Silence,* said the King, and took his voice.

* * *

It went out of him like a bird startled from a hedge—one moment perched in his throat, the next gone into a sky he couldn't see. He tried to cry out and produced nothing. He tried to say Sef's name, because she was kneeling beside him now, gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise, and he wanted—I want you to know I don't regret it, he thought, I want you to hear me say I'd do it again—and the thought stayed a thought, sealed behind teeth that would not obey.

*Term entire. Service entire. Silence entire.* The King's voice arranged itself comfortably in his skull, the way a landlord arranges furniture in a house just vacated. It did not shout. It did not need to. It simply *was*, ambient as cold, and Emrys understood that for the next twenty years every thought he thought would be thought in company.

*You are mine now, apprentice-of-Maren,* the voice said, almost gently. *As she was mine. As all things that bargain are mine. You will find the arrangement survivable. Most do.*

*Most.* The word had teeth in it. Emrys, mute, tasted them.

Through the flood of another man's—or another woman's—memories still draining into him, through the cold settling into his bones like silt, he felt the choir begin to call.

* * *

It was not a sound. That was the horror of it. It was a *pull*, a gravity, centered on the loft where the hollow ones sang their endless mass, and now that he belonged to the court, now that his soul carried the court's seal, the pull recognized him the way a current recognizes a leaf. Come sing, it said, without words. Come be emptied. Come be useful. You have given your term; why keep the self that occupies it?

His body began to rise. Not by choice—he was still on his knees, but his spine straightened, his head lifted, his feet found the floor as though strings had been taken up by some patient puppeteer. Twenty years, the pull whispered. Twenty years is long. Better to begin emptying now. Better to arrive at the end of your service already smooth, already glass, already song.

*Maren,* he thought, with the last of his own voice, inward, secret, the only country left to him. *Maren, look at me.*

* * *

She was looking at him.

The freed soul of Maren Nystrom lay crumpled on the iron floor six feet away, and Emrys watched the frost leave her eyes the way frost leaves a window when a hand is laid flat against the glass—a bloom of clarity spreading outward from the center, wet and human and astonished. She breathed. He hadn't known she'd been breathing shallowly for twenty years until he saw her take one full, greedy, ragged breath, and the sound of it was the most obscene, beautiful thing he had ever heard.

She pushed herself up on arms that shook. She was old. The court had kept its property functional but it had spent her like coin, and what remained was a thin, white-haired woman in a grey shift, blinking at the vaulted dark as though the concept of *darkness one could choose to move through* had been returned to her along with everything else.

Her gaze found him. Found him rising against his will, feet shuffling toward the choir-loft, mouth open around a silence.

"Emrys." Her voice was rust and honey. "Emrys, *no*—"

She crossed the distance between them faster than her body should have allowed. Her hands—charm-stained even now, the ink sunk too deep ever to wash out—took his face, and her eyes, brown again, ordinary again, *hers* again, searched his with a ferocity that had survived two decades of being fed piece by piece into a choir.

"You beautiful, stupid boy," she whispered. "You carried nothing. You carried *nothing*, didn't you. Blank as new snow. Where did you learn—" and then she stopped, because she knew. She had taught him. Ten years of apprenticeship, and the one lesson he'd swallowed whole while spitting out all the others: *a witch's best spell is an unreadable face.*

"I can't speak," he tried to tell her. Nothing came out. His feet took another step toward the loft.

"Yes you can," Maren said. "In here. In here you can." She pressed her forehead to his, and her thoughts poured into him along whatever thread the debt had strung between their souls, and oh, it was *warm*, it was the first warm thing the mountain had shown him. *Listen to me. The pull is strong now, strongest at the start, because it wants you emptied before you learn the shape of your chains. But chains have shapes, Emrys. Everything in this place has a shape, and a shape can be studied, and a thing that can be studied can be turned. I spent twenty years learning mine. You will spend twenty years learning yours, and you will cheat them every single day, do you hear me? Every mild winter. Every small mercy. We will rob this king blind from inside his own house.*

Tears were running down her face. Or his. He couldn't tell anymore whose they were.

"And when the twenty years are done," she thought at him, fierce as a struck flint, "you walk out of here, and you do not look back, and you live a long life selling luck to people who need it, and you grow *old*, do you understand me? Old and tiresome and free. That is the only payment I will ever accept from you."

*I love you,* he thought back, because it was true, and because it was the one intention the King could not tax—love given freely was the one coin the mountain had never learned to mint.

For one moment her presence wrapped around him like a cloak against the cold, and the choir's pull slackened, baffled, finding nothing in him to grip that wasn't already spoken for.

Then the King's attention turned upon them both like a lighthouse beam, and the moment broke.

* * *

*Tender,* observed the Winter King, and the word in Emrys's skull was amused, and the amusement was worse than any rage could have been. *How touching. The thrall comforts the new acquisition. Do continue, Maren Nystrom. Grief seasons the voice beautifully. You taught me that yourself.*

Maren's hands fell from his face. Not released—*compelled*, her own twenty-year servitude reasserting itself now that its terms had a successor, the court tightening its grip on what remained of its claim on her. Emrys saw her fight it. Saw her plant her feet on the iron floor and lean against the compulsion like a woman leaning into a gale, and for three heartbeats, four, five, she held—

"Sing," she gasped at him. "When it takes you—sing *counterpoint*, boy, sing anything, sing off-key, sing my teaching-songs, fill every corner of yourself with noise it didn't choose—"

And then her feet moved, bearing her back toward the loft whether she willed it or no, and Emrys's bore him forward too, and they passed each other going opposite directions down the same dark aisle, teacher and student, freed and bound, and neither of them could stop to touch again.

Sef was shouting. He registered it distantly—she had gotten between him and the loft, hands out, and the tollmen were converging, and somewhere above them all the great wheels of frost were turning faster, solstice coming, solstice coming, the longest night leaning down over the world like a lid.

The choir received him.

* * *

The loft was not a place. It was a *condition*. He stood among the hollow ones and the mass rose through him from below, entering through the soles of his feet, and he felt it begin its patient work: sifting him, sorting him, reaching for the memories the road had left him—his mother's kitchen, the smell of tallow, the first charm he'd ever tied, Maren's voice saying *knot it twice for grief, thrice for fever*—reaching for them with fingers of glass.

He sang counterpoint.

It was ugly. It was deliberate. He took Maren's teaching-songs and dragged them a half-step flat, mashed them against the mass's perfect cold harmony, filled his skull with the musical equivalent of broken crockery. The pull faltered around him, offended. Somewhere in the ranks of the hollow ones, faces turned toward him with expressions that might, in beings who still had expressions, have been hope.

And Maren—Maren, compelled to her place in the loft, frosted eyes beginning to glaze again as the court reclaimed its due—found the thread of his discordant song and, for a single bar, wove her voice into it. Off-key. On purpose. Smiling.

Then the King's voice rose through the mass like a depth-charge, vast and courteous and cold:

*Sing properly, little debtor. You have twenty years to learn the hymn. And you should know—your friend the huntress has entered my mountain. Captain Vane walks my bone-roads with iron and a warrant, and she is about to learn what my magistrates have been paying me since before her grandmother drew breath. Shall we watch together, you and I?*

The choir swelled. The frost-wheels turned. And Emrys Nystrom, bound, silenced, singing wrong notes into the dark with everything he had left, felt the King's attention slide past him downward into the deeps—toward torchlight, toward iron, toward a woman in a black coat opening a ledger that was about to break her heart—

—and he began, silently, furiously, to study the shape of his chains.
