## 20. Maren's Farewell

The mountain took three days to finish dying.

Emrys felt it in his teeth first — a loosening, a softening, as though the bone-roads beneath him were being unknit stitch by stitch. The lichen that had fed on memory along the descent now glowed a gentler color, something like dawn, and where it had drunk from them going down it seemed to give back on the way up: fragments surfacing unbidden. The smell of tallow candles in Maren's workroom. The weight of the first luck-knot he'd ever tied wrong and been made to unpick, thread by thread, until his fingers bled. His mother's voice, which he had not known he'd lost until it returned to him at the third tollman's bridge, whole and warm and saying his name.

Sef walked beside him in silence for most of it. She had given away more than he had — her father's name, her family's blood-debt, and finally, in that last terrible moment inside the unraveling court, whatever remained of her line's inheritance. She should have come out of the mountain hollow as a flute with its stops pulled. Instead she came out upright, gray-faced, her eyes dry and enormous, carrying an emptiness she wore like a coat that almost fit.

They did not speak of the ledger. They did not speak of what Emrys had signed, or what it had cost to refuse what he'd signed, or how Sef had arrived through the choir like a blade coming home to its sheath. There would be time. Or there wouldn't, and words spent early would be wasted.

On the morning of the fourth day they reached the mouth of the mountain, and Maren was waiting for them there.

* * *

She looked smaller than memory. That was the first cruelty of it. Twenty years of service to the Winter King had worn her down to something wren-boned and papery, her hands spotted like old fruit, her hair gone white as frost on a windowpane. But her eyes — her eyes were hers again. Brown, sharp, ringed with the fine lines of a woman who had squinted at small stitches by bad light for sixty years. Not frosted glass. Not the hollow choir-eyes of the thing that had sung the King's endless mass. When she looked at Emrys, she looked *at* him, all the way through, the way she had on the day he'd run.

"You've grown," she said. Her voice was rust and honey. "You were all elbows when you left. All mouth."

"You're old," Emrys said, because he had never learned to be gentle first, and because if he didn't say something cruel and small immediately he was going to weep in front of her, and he had not wept in front of anyone since he was nine years old.

Maren smiled. It transformed her face entirely, folding decades into it, and for one heartbeat she was the woman from the workroom again, laughing at his botched knots. "Yes," she said. "That happens. Even down here. Especially down here." She turned her face up toward the grey sky above the mountain's mouth, where snow was falling still — but softly now, without appetite, the last of the long winter spending itself in drifts that melted before they landed. "The King kept us outside of time. Now time is catching up with interest. I have perhaps a season in me. Maybe two, if I'm stubborn, and you know I am."

"Then come back to Greyharrow," Emrys said. It came out too fast, too young. "There are rooms above my tenement. The fever-charms will keep you—"

"No." She said it gently, but she said it the way she said everything: finally. "I'm staying here."

* * *

They walked while she explained it. The mountain's mouth opened onto a high valley that no map of Greyharrow's hinterlands had ever bothered to record, cupped between shoulders of black stone, and already — even in these first days — it was changing. The sold souls who had woken were coming up out of the deep roads blinking and bewildered, hundreds of them, generations of them: hedge-witches and cunning-men, midwives and rain-callers, people whose names had been struck from every register in the city below. Some wept. Some wandered. Some sat down in the melting snow and refused to move, having forgotten how to want anything.

"They'll need somewhere to go," Maren said. "And most of them can't go back. Greyharrow outlawed their craft; the law may be ash now, but twenty years of fear doesn't burn off overnight. Vane's warrant is dead paper, but the Magistrate's office isn't, and neither is the habit of hiding." She paused at a shelf of rock, breathing hard, and Emrys pretended not to notice her hand trembling against the stone. "So it becomes this. A refuge. A school, eventually, if any of us live long enough to teach. The court Beneath the Mountain ran on debts taken unwillingly. Let it run instead on debts paid freely. Salt and remembrance. That's all a choir ever needed to be."

"You're asking me to help build it," Emrys said.

"I'm asking nothing." She resumed walking, slow as syrup. "That's rather the point of everything that just happened, boy. Pay attention."

He wanted to argue. He wanted to grab her by those bird-thin shoulders and shake her and tell her that she owed him twenty years, that she owed him an explanation, that she had let him believe — all those years in Greyharrow, selling luck-knots behind a screen of mundane trinkets, flinching at every boot on cobbles — that she had simply abandoned him, that her silence had been indifference, that the woman who taught him letters and knotwork and the names of forty herbs had thrown him away like a failed charm. He had carried that belief for two decades. It had shaped him like water shapes stone. And now here she was, frail and mortal and dying on some season's schedule, offering him forgiveness he hadn't asked for and absolution he wasn't sure he could afford to accept cheaply.

Instead he said: "Why didn't you send for me?"

Maren stopped. The valley wind moved her white hair. For a long moment she said nothing, and Emrys watched her decide — watched her weigh the truth against kindness and choose the truth, because she had always chosen the truth, and it was the truest and cruelest thing about her.

"Because the bargain forbade it," she said. "Term, service, and silence. You know the terms now better than anyone living. But that's only half the answer, and you deserve the whole of it." She turned to face him fully. "The other half is that I was afraid you'd come. Twenty years ago, when they took me down, I told myself: he's young, he's angry, he'll grieve and forget. And then the years went on and I thought: he's stubborn, he's mine, someday he'll find the road. And I prayed to every god I'd ever slighted that he *wouldn't*. Because the debt can only pass to one who takes it willingly, and I knew you, Emrys Nystrom. I knew you'd take it. I lay in that choir singing the King's mass with frosted eyes and I thought: if he comes, he dies as me. Better he hates me alive than loves me dead."

"That's not your choice to make."

"No," she agreed. "It wasn't. It was arrogance, the same arrogance that made me think one soul could buy a city's mercy. We are a prideful trade, we hedge-witches. It's why they fear us." Her mouth quirked. "You took the debt anyway. Without intention, so the King couldn't read it. Sef tells me you sang counterpoint for a full day beforehand to learn how to carry nothing in your heart. That was my teaching. My tradecraft. You used my own lessons to unmake my master."

"He wasn't your master."

"He was my creditor. In the end it amounts to the same thing, unless someone breaks the account." She reached out and took his hand — her fingers cold, papery, impossibly light — and pressed something into his palm. "Which brings me to what I actually brought you here to give."

* * *

It was a knot. A simple one, worked in red thread gone dark with age, the kind of luck-knot a first-year apprentice might tie on their first day. He recognized it instantly, because he had tied its twin: the botched one, the one she'd made him unpick until his fingers bled, the lesson about patience that he'd resented for years and built his entire hidden livelihood upon.

"This is mine," he said stupidly.

"It's yours. I kept it. Down there, in the dark, when the King had scraped everything else out of me — my name, my craft, my self — this stayed. A little loop of red thread and a girl's mistake in it. Do you know why?" She closed his fingers over it. "Because it was freely given. I gave it to you the day you left. Threw it after you, actually, and missed, and you never saw it land in the snow. Everything else I owned belonged to the court. This belonged to you. Debts bind, Emrys. Gifts free. That's the whole of the craft, and I let them take twenty years of it from me before I remembered."

Emrys stood holding the knot and found that his eyes were burning and that he was, after all, going to weep in front of her, and that it didn't matter, and that perhaps it never had.

"I hated you," he said. "For years. I built a life on hating you. It was easier than missing you."

"I know." She said it without flinching. "Hate is a load-bearing wall. Take it out and the house has to be rebuilt, and rebuilding is slow, cold work." She patted his cheek with her small cold hand, brisk as a tutor correcting posture. "Start today. You've got spring coming, and spring is for building."

"And you? What do you build?"

"Gravestones," she said cheerfully. "Metaphorical ones. The court's ledgers don't burn, unfortunately — they're older than paper — but they can be rewritten, and I'm the only soul down there who read the original contracts before I signed one. Every thrall who woke has a right to see what was taken from them and what was returned. Someone has to hold the pen. I intend to hold it until my hands stop working, and then I intend to haunt this valley so thoroughly that my successors will beg for quiet." She glanced toward the settlement already taking shape below — cookfires, canvas shelters, a woman with a midwife's hands moving among the newly-woken like a lighthouse. "Besides. Somebody has to teach them the salt rite properly. Half of them are doing it backwards."

"The salt rite," Emrys said slowly. "Where did that even come from? You never taught me—"

"I didn't know it yet. I learned it down there, in the choir, from souls older than the King. Salt for remembrance, salt for release. The choir sang it once a year, on the solstice, and none of us knew what we were singing." Her eyes went distant, then returned. "You'll carry it up, when I'm done. That's not a request either. It's just how inheritance works when it's done right: not a debt passed down, but a gift handed over, hand to hand, with the giver watching."

* * *

They came down out of the rocks as the afternoon thinned, and walked together to the edge of the camp, where the road began its long climb back toward Greyharrow. Sef was waiting there with the packs, talking low with a broad-shouldered man who had woken that morning from forty years in the choir and was relearning the concept of hunger. She caught Emrys's eye and deliberately turned away, giving them the last stretch of path alone, and he loved her a little for it.

At the fork where the road split — one branch climbing east toward the city's smoke, one descending into the green-shadowed valley — Maren stopped.

"Well," she said.

"Well," Emrys said.

They stood there like two strangers at a market stall, each waiting for the other to name a price, and then Maren laughed — a real laugh, cracked and wheezing and delighted — and pulled him down into an embrace that smelled of woodsmoke and feverfew and the deep cold stone of the place she had chosen to end. He held her carefully, the way you hold a thing you have already begun to mourn, and felt her heart beating quick and shallow against his ribs like a bird deciding whether to stay.

"For what it's worth," she said into his shoulder, "you were the best thing I ever made, and I didn't make you. I just handed you thread and got out of the way. Go sell your charms in daylight, boy. Label them honestly. Charge the rich double." She stepped back, and her eyes were bright and dry and absolutely steady. "And next solstice, bring salt. The good coarse kind, not that market dust you used to palm off on tourists."

"Yes, mistress," he said, and the word — the old word, the fled-from word — sat in his mouth like a coin of pure gold.

She turned and started down into the valley without looking back, because she had never once in her life looked back, and because looking back was for people who doubted. Emrys watched her go: a small white-haired figure moving through the melting snow toward the fires and the waking and the work, growing smaller and smaller against the black shoulder of the mountain, until the dusk took her the way dusk takes everything — gently, and not forever, because he would see her again at solstice, and the one after that, for as many solstices as her stubbornness could purchase.

Sef appeared at his elbow. "Ready?"

He looked east, up the climbing road, toward Greyharrow. Smoke stood over the city in the distance, thin and ordinary, and beyond it the last of the winter clouds were tearing apart to show a sky the color of a washed wound beginning to close. Somewhere down in his pack, wrapped in oilcloth, were the first charms he would sell openly — labeled honestly, priced fairly, magic in daylight for the first time in twenty years.

"One thing first," Emrys said.

He knelt at the fork in the road, took out the little red-thread knot — the mistake, the lesson, the gift — and buried it in the thawing earth at the meeting of the two ways, pressing the soil down flat with both palms. A marker for travelers. A toll paid backward. When the grass came it would hide it, and that was fine; gifts were meant to be forgotten by the giver and found by strangers.

"What was that?" Sef asked.

"Nothing," Emrys said, standing, brushing the dirt from his knees, feeling lighter than he had since he was nine years old and the world still fit inside a workroom that smelled of tallow and herbs. "A debt I won't be collecting."

They took the eastern road as the first star came out, and behind them, faint on the wind, rising from the valley they had unmade and remade, they could hear singing — not the King's endless mass, not the hollow hymn of the sold, but something new, rough and unpracticed and joyful, a hundred broken voices finding a tune together for the very first time.

It sounded, Emrys thought, like the beginning of the world.

It sounded like salt.
