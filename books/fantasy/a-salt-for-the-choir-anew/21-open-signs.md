## 21. Open Signs

The thaw came to Greyharrow the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once, and never quite clean.

Emrys woke on the first morning of true spring to the sound of water. Not the polite drip of eaves but a full-throated roaring in the gutters, snowmelt running brown and eager down the tenement stairs, out into the streets, carrying the winter away piece by piece. He lay in his narrow bed for a long while just listening to it, the way a man might listen to a heartbeat he had not expected to hear again. Somewhere below, children were shouting. He could not remember the last time he had heard children shouting in Greyharrow. Under the Winter King's long season they had gone quiet as everything else, folded indoors, thinned by cold and hunger until their voices seemed a luxury the city could no longer afford.

He got up, dressed, and stood at his window with his hands flat against the sill. The square below was wet and shining, cobbles scrubbed pale by the melt, and the market stalls were already going up — real stalls, full ones, merchants hauling canvas and hammering pegs with an energy that bordered on recklessness. A woman was hanging strings of dyed eggs from her awning. A man was arguing cheerfully about the price of lamp oil. The air, when Emrys cracked the window, smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and, faintly, impossibly, of green things growing somewhere beyond the walls.

His own stall was still in pieces in the corner of the room where he had dragged it after the raid, all those months ago. He knelt beside it now and ran his thumb along the split in the counter-top where a hunter's boot had come down. He had meant, once, to sand it out. He found he did not want to anymore. Some marks deserved keeping.

He began to load it anyway: the luck-knots first, braided cord and river-clay beads, each one humming faintly under his fingertips like a held breath. Then the fever-charms in their little glass vials, willow-bark steeped in moonwater, the liquid catching the morning light and throwing it back gold. Then the rest — the salt-sachets, the sleep-wafers, the small carved doors that opened onto nothing and kept nightmares out all the same. Twenty years he had sold these things in disguise, buried inside trinkets, labeled as souvenirs, priced so cheaply that no magistrate would bother looking twice. Twenty years of lying with his whole craft.

Today he would write the truth on every sign.

His hand shook a little when he thought it. Not with fear, exactly. Fear he knew the shape of; fear fit him like an old coat. This was something stranger — the vertigo of a door left open after a lifetime of locking it.

* * *

Sef arrived before he did, which was her way. She had claimed the corner spot near the fountain, the good one, the one with the most foot-traffic, and she had already chalked their prices on the flagstones in her blunt, slanting hand. She looked better than she had in winter. The hollows under her eyes had filled in; there was color in her face again, or at least the memory of color. But when she turned toward him he saw what the mountain had left in her, the way frost leaves patterns on glass that no amount of warming quite erases: her name was only half-restored, and she wore the missing half like a scar she had decided not to hide.

They had argued about it, in the early weeks. He had wanted to give her back what the tollman took — he had tried, actually, sitting up three nights composing a new name for her out of old letters, offering it the way you offer bread to a starving stranger. She had refused with a flatness that ended the conversation. *A name given isn't a name earned,* she said. *I'll grow the rest myself.* And then, because she was Sef, she had gone back to inventorying charms as if nothing had been said at all.

"You're late," she said now, though he wasn't, particularly.

"The gutters are rivers. I nearly lost a boot."

"Tragic." She glanced at the crate in his arms, at the luck-knots rattling gently against the fever-charms, and something moved behind her eyes — the look she got sometimes, he had learned, when the past pressed close. Her father had carried a crate like this once, probably. Had died with a map instead of merchandise in his hands. "Sign's ready," she said, and stepped aside.

She had painted it herself, on a board salvaged from the flood-debris: **EMRYS NYSTROM — CHARMS, HONESTLY MADE**. Below it, smaller, in letters she had clearly taken care over: **LUCK. FEVER. SLEEP. GRIEF. ASK AND I'LL TELL YOU WHAT EACH ONE DOES AND WHAT IT COSTS.**

No euphemisms. No *keepsakes*, no *souvenirs of Greyharrow*. Just the words themselves, standing in the open air like people who had finally been let out of a cellar.

"It's too honest," Emrys said.

"That's the point." Sef took the crate from him and began setting out stock with quick, sure hands. "Half the city spent the winter wondering if magic was going to eat them. The other half spent it praying someone would use some. They're done with riddles." She set a fever-charm in its place, adjusted it a quarter-turn, adjusted it back. "Besides. You signed your name in the ledger freely. You don't get to be shy about a wooden sign."

He laughed — short, surprised at himself — and hung the board on its hook. It swung in the thawing wind, creaked once, settled. Passersby slowed. Some only stared and hurried on, crossing themselves or clutching their purses, twenty years of law living in their spines. But others stopped. An old woman with a cane read the sign twice through, lips moving, and then looked up at Emrys with an expression he couldn't parse — suspicion and hope fighting to a draw. A boy of maybe ten planted himself in front of the luck-knots and refused to be moved by his mother's tugging.

"What do they *do*?" the boy demanded.

"They hold your luck steady," Emrys said, kneeling to his level. "Not good luck. Steady. So the bad days don't knock you over."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My da fell off a roof in the winter."

"I know," Emrys said quietly. Half of Greyharrow had fallen off something that winter. "This won't bring him back. Nothing does. But it might keep you steady while you miss him."

The mother's hand went to her mouth. She bought two knots, and paid without haggling, and did not quite meet his eye, and thanked him in a voice that cracked on the second word. That was how it went, all that first morning. No crowds, no miracles. Just people coming one and two at a time, reading the sign, asking careful questions, buying small things for large reasons. Grief-charms went first. Then the sleep-wafers. By midday the fever-charms were half gone, and Sef was chalking new prices, and Emrys realized his face hurt from smiling and could not remember when he had started.

* * *

In the afternoon, the Magistrate's captain came.

Emrys saw her before she crossed the square — you learned to see Ilsa Vane the way sailors learn to see weather, in the set of other people's shoulders, the sudden hush moving ahead of her like a bow-wave. She wore no squad today. No warrant-case, no irons, no hunters flanking her in their grey livery. Just the woman herself, tall and straight-backed, her coat brushed clean, her boots finding the dry stones across the flooded cobbles as if the water made way for her out of professional courtesy.

Sef went still beside him. Emrys felt his own hands remember, unhelpfully, exactly how fast they could pack a crate.

Vane stopped at the far edge of the square. She did not approach the stall. She stood beneath the bare-limbed plane tree — budding now, he noticed, tiny green fists clenched at the ends of every branch — and she watched. Her face gave away nothing. It never had. But she watched the way a person watches a fire they have decided, provisionally, not to put out.

"She can't arrest me," Emrys said, low. "There's no law left to arrest me under. The amnesty—"

"The amnesty is Maren's bargain, and Maren's bargain died with the King. You told me." Sef didn't take her eyes off Vane either. "Which means whatever happens next is new law. Or no law." A pause. "Or her law."

The afternoon stretched. Vane did not move. Customers came and went around the edges of the square, giving the captain a wide berth, pretending very hard not to see her, and she let them pretend. Once, a clerk from the Magistracy hurried up to her, breathless, papers in hand, and she spoke to him briefly without turning her head. Whatever she said sent him away at almost a run.

When the light began to go gold and the market crowd thinned, Vane finally crossed the square.

She came to the stall and stood before it and read the sign top to bottom, twice, the way the old woman had. Her jaw worked once. When she spoke, her voice was the voice Emrys remembered from the ledger-room, from the raids, from every interrogation he had ever hidden from: level, precise, a voice that filed things away even as it said them.

"Honestly made," she read. "Ask and I'll tell you what each one costs." She looked up. "That last part is new. Charm-sellers never used to advertise the cost."

"Most of them didn't know it," Emrys said. "I do. I paid it."

Something flickered behind her eyes — not softness, nothing so simple, but the recalculation of a woman who had built her whole life on categories and was watching the categories fail. "Twenty years I hunted your kind," she said. "Do you know why?"

"Because it was the law."

"Because it was the *only* law I trusted." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "My brother swung for less than what's in those vials. I signed the warrant myself. I believed—" She stopped. Her hand rose halfway to her coat, to the place where the ledger used to sit against her ribs, and dropped. "I burned it, Nystrom. All of it. Every page. There are magistrates who will call that treason, when they find their records missing. Some of them already suspect. Some of them have reason to suspect." Her mouth twisted, the ghost of the court's corruption still bitter on her. "So understand me. I am not here because I've forgiven you. I'm not here because I believe in charms. I'm here because I watched a hundred years of bargains rot everyone who touched them, and then I watched you and that girl break the wheel by refusing to turn it, and I don't have a category for that yet."

"No," Emrys said. "Neither do we. We're building as we go."

Vane studied him for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and set something on the counter: a single coin, old silver, worn nearly smooth. Payment. For what, she didn't say. She took a grief-charm from the display — Sef's hand twitched but did not stop her — and closed her fist around it.

"For the families," Vane said. "Of the ones I took. I'm told these help with the missing." Her voice did not change at all, and that was how Emrys knew how much it was costing her. "If it doesn't work, I'll want my money back."

"It works," Sef said quietly. "It doesn't fix anything. It just holds you steady."

"Steady," Vane repeated, tasting the word. She turned to go, then paused, her back to them, the gold light catching the grey at her temples. "There's talk in the Magistracy. About writing new statutes. About licensing what you do." A beat. "Some of them mean it honestly. Some of them mean to build a new ledger with nicer binding. Watch which kind wins." And she walked away across the square, unhurried, her shadow long behind her, and did not look back.

* * *

They closed the stall at dusk. Sef counted the day's take twice, wrote it in her neat column, and sat back on her stool with an expression Emrys had learned to distrust slightly less than her scowls.

"We'll sell out by week's end at this rate," she said. "You'll need more willow-bark. And the clay for the knots — the river clay, not the pit stuff. Pit clay goes dead in a month."

"You've been paying attention."

"I've been paying attention since the bone-roads," she said. "Turns out watching someone work magic is different when you're not planning to kill them afterward." She said it dryly, but her hand drifted to her collarbone, to the place where her half-name sat like a key missing its mate, and her voice gentled despite itself. "My father would have liked today. He'd have hated the prices, and argued with every customer, and stood right there—" she pointed at the empty third of the stall, "—and told everyone within earshot that his daughter ran the whole operation."

"He'd have been right about the last part."

"Yes," Sef said. "He would." She was quiet a moment. Down in the square, the lamplighters were making their rounds, and the melting snow sang in every gutter, and somewhere a fiddler had started up — badly, joyfully, the first music Emrys had heard in the streets since autumn. "Maren writes that the eastern halls are clear. The choir's old dormitories. She says they could house forty families if the roof holds."

"Maren always did think in terms of sheltering people."

"She also thinks you should visit. She says—" Sef squinted at the letter she'd produced from her apron, the mountain's paper, faintly cool to the touch even now, "—she says 'tell my apprentice the kettle works again, and that a witch who hides from her teacher's tea has learned nothing worth knowing.'"

Emrys laughed, and it came out rougher than he intended. Twenty years of exile, one winter of horror, and still that voice could reach across the miles and find the boy in him, the runaway, the one who'd fled rather than watch her grow strange. He folded the letter carefully and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where the debt had lived and no longer did.

"Next rest-day," he said. "We'll both go. Bring salt."

"Always salt," Sef said, but she was smiling as she said it, and the last light came down through the budding plane tree and lay across the stall like a blessing neither of them had asked for, and the sign creaked overhead — *honestly made, ask and I'll tell you the cost* — swinging gently in the warm wind that came up from the south, carrying, on it, unmistakably now, the smell of green things growing.

And beneath the smell of spring, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, something else. Cold. Old. Patient.

From the mountain, a bell was ringing — one note, repeated, the remembrance-note the freed choir had chosen for their dead. Except this was not the hour for it. This was no hour anyone had assigned.

Sef heard it too. Her smile faded by degrees, like a lamp running low.

"That's wrong," she said. "That's not the schedule."

The bell rang on, and on, and did not stop.
