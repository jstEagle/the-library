## 22. A Salt for the Choir Anew

The salt came from the sea, as all true salt must.

Emrys had carried it the whole way from Greyharrow in a jar stoppered with wax and wrapped in wool against the jostle of the road — coarse grey crystals, each one a small rough star, harvested from the tidal flats below the cliffs where his mistress had once taken him to learn the names of waves. *Salt remembers,* Maren had told him that day, twenty-some years ago now, when his hands were still too small to knot a proper luck-charm and his patience was smaller still. She had held a pinch of it up to the light and let him taste it off her fingertip, sharp and clean and old. *It has been in the dark of the earth and the dark of the water and it comes back shining. Remember that, when you forget everything else I teach you.*

He had forgotten nearly everything else. But he had remembered that.

Now the jar rode against his hip on a sledge-rope, and the mountain rose ahead of him through the morning mist like a thought too large to finish, and Emrys Nystrom walked the last mile alone because some miles were meant to be walked alone.

* * *

A year. He kept counting it in small ways, the way you count a scar — by what no longer hurts and what still does. A year since the snow had stopped falling upward. A year since Sef's voice had rung through the hollow choir like a bell through fog, declaring the ledger void, void, void, until the words themselves wore grooves in the world. A year since the Winter King had come apart not in fire but in something quieter — a long exhale, a crown slipping from a head that had never truly been a head at all, only the shape a debt makes when it grows old enough to believe itself a person.

A year since Maren had stood at the mountain's mouth with frost melting out of her eyes and said, *Go home, boy. You've paid enough for one lifetime,* and meant nothing cruel by it, and everything kind.

The road to the threshold was better kept than it had any right to be. That was Vane's doing, mostly. The Magistrate's witch-hunters had been formally disbanded in the spring — their charter dissolved by a council that could no longer quite explain, in any honest language, what exactly they had been hunting or why — but Captain Ilsa Vane had found new work for her iron habits. She organized the road crews now. She kept the ledgers of the rebuilt way-stations. She had burned her warrant-book in the square outside the Magistrate's hall, page by page, in front of everyone, and then she had picked up a shovel, and somehow that had made more sense to the people of Greyharrow than any speech could have.

Emrys passed two of her crews on the lower switchbacks, mending the frost-heaved stone. They nodded to him without fear. One of them — a broad woman with lichen-grey dust on her knuckles — asked him if he'd bless her trowel, half-joking, half-not, and he did, a small thing, a charm for straight lines and honest work, and she laughed and said her grandfather would have called that witchcraft and she supposed her grandfather had been right, and they both let the word sit there in the cold air between them, unafraid.

*Witchcraft.* Once it had been a word you whispered. Now it was a word you handed back and forth like a tool, like bread.

The mountain took him in at midday.

* * *

The bone-roads had changed.

He had expected that, and still it caught at something behind his ribs. Where the passages had been lit by memory-eating lichen — pale, hungry, beautiful in the way of things that feed on you — there was now a softer glow, amber and low, like lamplight through honey. The lichen remained, but it fed differently now. It had learned, or been taught, or simply grown into its new nature the way a rescued animal slowly stops flinching: it drew lightness instead of loss. Travelers left memories here deliberately now, happy ones mostly, pressed into the glowing walls like flowers into books, and the roads gave them back changed — warmed, shared, made common property. You could walk this stretch and feel someone else's wedding day flicker past your shoulder like a guest at your own table.

Emrys touched the wall once, lightly, in passing. A stranger's memory bloomed under his palm: a kitchen, steam, a child laughing at something spilled. He smiled, and left a little of his own in exchange — the smell of Maren's workshop, cedar oil and hot wax — and felt it settle into the stone like a coin into a well.

The tollmen were gone. In their places stood wardens — former thralls who had woken when the King unraveled, souls returned to bodies that had waited hollow so long some of them chose to stay anyway, keeping the roads, keeping the doors, keeping the courtesy that the court had perverted into tolls. The first warden he met was a thin man with a voice like dry leaves, and when Emrys approached the old toll-gate the man simply bowed and stepped aside.

"No payment?" Emrys asked, though he knew the answer. He asked every time. It had become a kind of prayer.

"Passage is free," the warden said, in the formula they all used, in the words that had cost so much to make true. "What is freely given is freely kept."

*Freely given, freely kept.* It was carved now over every gate in the deep court. Sef had insisted on it. She said a law was only a scar you agreed to keep touching, and she wanted this one touched daily, by everyone, until the touching stopped hurting and started teaching.

* * *

The choir sang when he came into the great vaulted dark where the mass had once rung endless and joyless.

But oh, how it had changed.

Where once a thousand hollow voices had droned the Winter King's single unbroken note — a sound like ice settling over a lake, like a held breath that never ended — now the choir sang in parts. In *parts*. Emrys stopped on the gallery steps and let it wash over him: descant and ground, a slow round moving through the pillars, voices rising and falling out of time with each other and finding each other again, the way voices do when no one is forcing them. Some of the singers were the woken dead, souls restored to bodies that had aged in escrow. Some were those whose bodies had failed during the long captivity and who had chosen, freely, to remain as the court's resident spirits — singing because they wanted to, which was the whole miracle, the entire point, the thing Emrys suspected he would spend the rest of his life failing to fully comprehend.

They were rehearsing the remembrance rite. Tomorrow was the anniversary. Tonight they would sing the names.

Maren stood among them.

She had stayed, as she had said she would. Her hair had gone from frosted white to merely grey in the year since her release, as if color were seeping back into her the way dawn seeps into a valley. Her voice — he found it easily, the way you find one instrument in an orchestra, the way you find your mother's footsteps in a house full of company — her voice carried the ground line, steady and low, and there was nothing hollow in it. When the phrase ended she turned, as if she'd felt him arrive the way you feel weather change, and her eyes were brown again, ordinary, human, lined with the tiredness of a woman doing hard and chosen work.

"You're early," she said.

"You're surprised," he answered.

"I'm never surprised. I'm a teacher. I'm disappointed in advance." But she was smiling as she said it, and she came down the steps to him, and for a moment they simply stood in the choir's wash of sound, neither one certain of the grammar of embrace after everything that lay between them. Then she solved it the way she'd always solved things — practically, briskly, taking his face in both hands and looking at him for a long moment, reading him like a margin note.

"You've been sleeping," she observed. "Eating. Arguing with Sef about the shop's accounts." A pause. "You've been happy. Mostly. In the way of people who are learning how."

"Is that an accusation?"

"It's an inventory." She released him. "Show me what you brought."

* * *

They carried the salt down together to the threshold — not the deep court itself, but the place just before it, the antechamber where the old ledger had been kept. The ledger still existed; Sef had seen to that too, over Emrys's mild objection and Vane's stronger ones. *You don't burn a record of debts,* she had said, with her father's stubborn jaw set like a cornerstone. *You keep it so you can check the arithmetic. So no one ever gets to say it didn't happen.* But the pages had been struck through, every contract annulled in ink mixed with ash and salt-water, and the book now rested open on a stone lectern beneath the words carved in the arch above:

FREELY GIVEN, FREELY KEPT.

The rite was simple, because Maren had designed it and Maren distrusted ceremony the way other people distrust strangers. The choir would sing the names of the dead — those who woke into bodies too broken to keep, those who dissolved into peace rather than return, those like Sef's father who had died on the threshold of understanding. And the living would carry salt, sea-salt, the oldest remembering substance, and lay it on the ledger's open pages, and speak, if they wished, one line: what was lost, and what was kept.

Emrys opened his jar at the lectern while Maren withdrew a respectful distance, giving him the privacy of the act.

The salt caught the amber light and threw it back in small constellations. He thought about how much a year could hold. He thought about his stall in the market square, reopened in spring with the charms labeled honestly — *luck-knot, fever-charm, grief-ease, road-blessing* — and the first customer, an old woman who had stared at the sign and wept, and bought nothing, and come back three days later to buy everything. He thought about Sef beside him now as partner, her name half-restored — the tollman's price had taken her father's name from her memory forever, and part of her own with it, the syllables that had connected her to him — and how she had decided that the missing piece was not a wound but a room, and she would furnish it with new things. He thought about Vane across the square, watching, not intervening, and the day she'd finally crossed the street and bought a road-blessing and paid full price and refused the discount and said, *I don't take gifts from witches. I pay my debts.* And how Emrys had understood, then, that this was her version of forgiveness, and accepted it.

He took a handful of salt.

"For the ones who didn't wake," he said quietly, and let the grains fall across the ledger's struck-through pages, glittering in the furrows of the old ink. "For the ones who woke and couldn't stay. For the ones who are still deciding." The salt settled into the paper like snow into furrows. "And for the man who died at my door with a map in his hands, whose name I never learned because Sef had to spend it at a tollgate — I carry yours too. Both of you. Every year. Freely given."

Then he did the thing he had come to do, the thing he had turned over all the long walk from Greyharrow, testing it, doubting it, returning to it.

He uncapped the inkhorn at his belt, took up the quill that lay chained to the lectern — a formality now, chains without locks — and turned to the ledger's final page. The margin there was wide and blank, running down beside the ruined contracts like a road beside a battlefield.

And in the margin, in his own careful hand, the hand Maren had trained, he wrote:

*Emrys Nystrom. Apprentice of Maren Nystrom. Partner of Sef. Debtor to no one. Keeper of this book's lesson.*

Then, beneath it, smaller:

*Freely given. Freely kept.*

It was not a signature. Signatures bound. This was the opposite of a signature — a naming, a claiming, a line written into the record not as payment but as inheritance. Because that was what he had learned, walking down and coming back up: that inheritance was not the debt you were handed. It was what you chose to carry forward. His mistress had sold her soul to buy a city's mercy, and he had spent a lifetime calling that abandonment, and another season calling it nobility, and the truth, as usual, was larger and less tidy than either: it was a gift given badly, in a system built to break givers, and the gift itself had survived the system. What he inherited from her was not her bargain. It was her salt. The remembering substance. The thing that goes into the dark and comes back shining.

Maren's hand settled on his shoulder. He hadn't heard her cross the floor; the choir covered footsteps the way rain covers weeping.

"What did you write?" she asked.

"My name," he said. "In the margin. Where the debts used to live."

She read it. He watched her read it, watched her lips move around the words, watched the exact moment she understood — teachers always understand a beat before they admit it — and then her breath went out of her in a long, unsteady thread, and she pressed her forehead briefly to the back of his head, quick and fierce, the way she'd done when he was nine and had finished his first true charm and she'd been too proud to say so in words.

"Well," she managed. "The margins were always where the real lessons lived."

* * *

That night the choir sang the names, and the mountain listened.

Emrys stood among the living — Sef with her hand warm around his, Vane upright and uncomfortable in a crowd that kept thanking her, the woken dead in their borrowed years, the road-crews who had hiked down out of curiosity and stayed out of wonder — and the names rose through the vaulted dark like sparks off a bonfire, each one spoken by the choir and caught and carried, and the amber lichen along the walls brightened with each name as if the mountain itself were keeping count, keeping faith, keeping *everything*, now, instead of eating it.

When the last name faded, Sef leaned close to him, her breath a small cloud in the cool air.

"Same time next year?" she murmured.

"Every year," Emrys said. "As long as there's salt in the sea and a road to carry it."

Above them, far above, winter was turning toward solstice and turning away again, gentler now, a season instead of a hunger. Somewhere beyond the mountain's skin, Greyharrow slept under honest snow, and in the morning the market stalls would open, and the charms would be labeled, and no one would have to hide.

Emrys closed the ledger softly, leaving his name in the margin where the light could find it, and walked out with the others into the free and ordinary dark.
