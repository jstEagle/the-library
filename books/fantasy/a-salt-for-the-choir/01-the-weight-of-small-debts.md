## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The rain came to Vellmark the way debts came to the poor: slowly, then all at once, and never where you'd braced for it. Cyrus Galloway heard it before he saw it—a thousand small fingers drumming the slate roof of the guildhall—and he marked it in the margin of his ledger with a small notation, *rain, heavy, third bell*, because that was the kind of man he had made himself: a man who wrote everything down and felt nothing about any of it.

Or so he told himself.

He sat in the counting room at the top of the Guild of Impossible Things, in a chair worn to the shape of his spine, before a desk scarred by forty years of knives and chisels. The room smelled of tallow and whale oil and the particular mineral sweetness of fresh-cut bone. Three lamps burned against the grey morning, though the grey was more habit than necessity; Cyrus worked as often by touch as by sight, running his thumb along the grain of ivory the way a blind man reads a face.

Before him lay the day's first commission: a curl of whalebone the length of his forearm, yellowed like old teeth, taken from a finback that had beached itself at Saltrow two winters past. The sailor's widow had paid eleven coppers and a jar of plum preserves—Cyrus had accepted both, because the guild took payment in whatever form grief could afford—and now he owed her an hour of his hands and whatever words her husband had carried into the water.

He set the burin against the bone and began.

The dead spoke through the material they'd loved in life, or through the material that had held them at the end, and this sailor—Tomm Aldous, thirty-one years drowned, no, not drowned, the voices were specific even when the widows weren't—had gone down in the netting of his own boat, tangled, dragged, aware the whole way. His voice lived in the whalebone the way heat lives in a hearthstone long after the fire dies. Cyrus only had to listen.

*Tell her the preserves were her mother's recipe,* Tomm said, patient, waterlogged, unhurried in the way the dead are unhurried, having traded urgency for insistence. *Tell her I knew. Tell her I ate them anyway because she was watching me pretend.*

Cyrus carved. The burin whispered along the grain, lifting curls of ivory thin as fingernail parings, and the letters came out clean and small and true: *THE PRESERVES WERE YOUR MOTHER'S RECIPE. I KNEW. I ATE THEM ANYWAY.*

There were other voices, of course. There were always other voices. The guildhall was built on a foundation of older buildings, which stood on the bones of older ones still, and Vellmark had been burying its dead beneath its own streets since before anyone thought to write anything down. On quiet nights Cyrus could hear the whole city murmuring under him like a hive, like a sea, like a choir rehearsing in a locked cathedral. He had learned, over thirty years, to fold those voices flat, to press them into the background noise of his own skull the way a man who lives beside a waterfall stops hearing the water.

Mostly.

*Tomm,* said another voice, closer, and Cyrus's hand did not slip but wanted to. It was a woman's voice, dry as paper, and it did not come from the bone or from the streets below but from the ledger itself, from the paper, from the ink. Old ledgers remembered their entries. That was the trouble with writing things down. *You've balanced page forty wrong again.*

"I have not," Cyrus said aloud, to the empty room, because talking back kept the voices at the level of neighbors rather than ghosts. "The carryover from the salt-tithe is correct."

*Check it.*

He checked it. It was wrong. He corrected it, mouth pressed thin, and the paper-voice subsided with something like satisfaction, and this was the shape of his life: a man alone in a tower of accounts, arguing with dead clerks, carving love letters from the drowned, invisible as furniture.

He would not have called it comfort. But it was close enough to comfort that he had stopped looking for anything else, and that, he understood dimly, was what most people meant by the word.

* * *

The audit was Ossen's idea, and like all of Ossen's ideas it arrived dressed as a kindness.

"Routine," the guildmaster said, lowering himself into the visitor's chair across from Cyrus's desk with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. Ossen was seventy if he was a day, broad as a door, with a white beard trimmed square and hands like knotted rope. He had been carving souls' names into stone before Cyrus was born, and he was the only person alive who knew what Cyrus had paid, seven years ago, and what it had bought. "Every ledger audited twice yearly. Yours last. You complain every time."

"I don't complain."

"You mutter. It's worse." Ossen slid the master registry across the desk—an iron-bound tome thick as a paving stone, chained to nothing because no one had ever been fool enough to steal it—and tapped the cover with one cracked nail. "Page through. Sign the reconciliation. I'll take the preserves your widow sent, if she sent plum."

"She sent plum."

"Good woman. Dead husband, good preserves. There's a balance in that somewhere." But Ossen didn't leave. He stayed while Cyrus opened the registry, and stayed while Cyrus worked through the early pages—the memory-forge receipts, Sable's invoices for recollections bought and sold, Hesk's promises logged in his cramped collector's hand, each one witnessed, each one sealed—and Cyrus felt the old unease stir, the feeling he always got when the registry was open, the feeling of standing in a room where something was hidden behind the wall.

Seven years ago lived behind that wall. Seven years ago had a price and a signature and a seal, and Cyrus had signed it in his own hand because there had been no other hand, and no other price, and no other way to keep his sister breathing through the winter fever that was eating her alive. He did not look at that page. He had not looked at that page in seven years. He knew its number the way you know the location of a bruise—by avoiding it.

The morning wore on. Rain kept its rhythm on the roof. The dead murmured their hive-murmur beneath the floor. Cyrus reconciled column after column, and the columns balanced, because Cyrus's columns always balanced, and the work was warm and small and safe as bread.

Then he turned the page to tomorrow.

The entry sat in the middle of the leaf, centered the way important entries were centered, written in the registry's formal hand—the hand used only for soul-contracts and guild oaths, the hand that appeared on the page without any scribe touching pen to it, because the registry wrote its own gravest matters:

**CYRUS GALLOWAY — ONE (1) SOUL — RECEIVED IN FULL — PAYMENT DUE.**

Dated tomorrow.

Cyrus read it three times. The rain filled the silences between readings. Somewhere below, faintly, absurdly, he heard Tomm Aldous still talking about the preserves.

"That's not my handwriting," he said finally, and his voice came out level, which surprised him, and cost him more than he could say.

"No," Ossen agreed quietly. "It isn't."

"You said routine."

"I said routine because I hoped it was." Ossen's big hands lay flat on the desk, and Cyrus saw—for the first time in thirty years of knowing the man—that they were trembling. "It appeared last night. Between the second bell and the third. The registry writes its own entries when a contract comes due, boy. It has done it four times in the guild's history. Four times." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and fierce and old. "And every time, the name in that book has been collected."

The lamps guttered, though there was no draft. Beneath the floor, the murmur of the dead changed—not louder, not nearer, but *aligned*, hundreds of voices that had spent centuries talking over one another suddenly pausing, listening, turning their attention upward toward the counting room like a congregation toward the pulpit.

Cyrus looked down at the entry. At his own name. At *payment due*, dated tomorrow, in ink that gleamed wet as though it had been written moments ago and might still be drying.

"Collected by whom?" he asked.

Ossen didn't answer. And in the silence, for the first time in his life, Cyrus heard the dead of Vellmark not as a murmur, not as a choir, but as a single voice, vast and low and patient, saying his name.
