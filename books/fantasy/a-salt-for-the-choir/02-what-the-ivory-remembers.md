## 2. What the Ivory Remembers

Cyrus did not sleep. He told himself it was the audit — that any ledger-keeper worth his salt would sit up with an entry he could not account for, the way a physician sits up with a fever of unknown origin. But the truth was simpler and worse: his own name sat on tomorrow's line in his own handwriting, and every hour he spent not looking at it was an hour he could feel it looking back.

The guildhall at night had its own weather. Rain moved through the archive stacks like something breathing, and the whale-oil lamps threw long amber corridors between the shelves of bone. Cyrus walked them with a lamp of his own, past drawers of knucklebones and reliquary ribs, past the sealed cabinet where the guild kept the things it could not price — a key that opened only lies, a mirror that showed debts instead of faces. The dead murmured in their ivory all around him, the way they always did: not words, exactly, but the shape of words, the residue of speech soaked into the material the way wine soaks into wood. Seven years of carving had taught him to let it wash over him like rain on a roof. Tonight the roof leaked.

*Tomorrow,* said a rib from the third shelf, in a drowned man's burr. *Tomorrow-tomorrow-tomorrow.*

"Quiet," Cyrus said, and felt foolish, and kept walking.

The anomalous entry read, in full:

*Entry 4,417. One (1) soul, unpriced. Debtor: C. Galloway. Due: —*

No amount. No date of origin. Only *due*, and the dash after it, which in ledger-language meant *on demand*, which meant *already owed*. And beneath it, in the margin, in ink that matched the rest of the page perfectly — same batch, same age, same iron-gall bite into the paper — a single word he had not written, or did not remember writing:

*Wren.*

He reached the reading room at the archive's heart, a round chamber walled floor-to-ceiling with contract drawers, each one labeled in Ossen's crabbed hand. The Guild of Impossible Things traded in three currencies: relics, oaths, and souls. Relics went in the vault below. Oaths lived in memory, sworn aloud and witnessed. But souls — souls were paper. A soul-bond deed was the only instrument in the free cities that could hold something a person could not carry, and there were perhaps four hundred of them in this room, and Cyrus had catalogued every one.

He knew, before he touched the drawers, which one he was looking for. That was the horror of it. His hands knew the way the way a tongue knows a missing tooth.

Drawer 311. Contracts of sale, bonds against future labor, deeds of transfer — the oldest class, the shameful class, mostly dormant since Ossen had choked off the trade a generation ago. The drawer slid out with a sound like a held breath released. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay the folders in their strict alphabetical order by debtor surname. G.

His fingers stopped on the folder without his permission.

*Galloway, C.*

He set it on the reading table and did not open it for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, rain found a new seam in the roof and began ticking onto stone. In the walls, faintly, the dead had gone quiet — all of them, every shelf and drawer, as if the whole archive were leaning in to listen.

He broke the seal.

The paper inside was seven years old. He knew its age the way he knew the age of everything in the archive, by the color of the foxing at the edges, by the particular brown that Vellmark's damp pulled out of good rag stock. It was a standard deed of soul-transfer, Form Nine, the kind used for sailors selling passage-luck or miners selling their fear of the dark — small sales, survivable sales, the kind the guild had once brokered by the dozen before deciding they were not survivable at all.

But this was not a small sale.

*Be it known,* the deed began, in the formal hand the forms required, *that the undersigned does convey, wholly and irrevocably, the soul of Wren Ashlily of Vellmark, cordwainer's daughter—*

Cyrus read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way you touch a wound to learn its shape.

The buyer was listed as *the Court Beneath the Mountain, per bearer.* The consideration — what Wren's soul had been sold *for* — was left blank except for one line, filled in not in the formal hand but in his own: the quick, forward-slanting script with the crossed t's he made when writing fast, when writing scared.

*Consideration: one life, restored. Payment rendered in full.*

He remembered.

That was the thing — he had spent seven years building a wall around that night, brick by brick, and now the wall came down all at once, and he was standing in the middle of it, seventeen years old again, in a room that smelled of tallow and river water, holding his sister's wrist and counting her breaths.

She had been dying. Not sick — dying, the way the plague took people in Vellmark that winter, quickly and from the feet up, the cold climbing them like water filling a hull. The physicians had done everything physicians did, which was nothing, and the priests had done everything priests did, which was pray to gods who had been silent for three hundred years. And Cyrus — nineteen then, already half-drunk on the voices in the ivory, already knowing things about death that no living person should know — had gone down to the guildhall in the black hours and put his name to a form he barely understood, because a broker had told him there was a buyer who paid in miracles, and the price was not money.

The price was a soul. Any soul. *Yours or one you love,* the broker had said, with the terrible gentleness of someone quoting a rate card. *The court beneath the mountain is not particular. But it does prefer what is freely given.*

And Wren — Wren Ashlily, who was not his sister, who had never been his sister, who was the girl who had taught him to whistle through cupped hands on the breakwater and kissed him once behind the salt sheds when they were both too young to know what it would cost them later — Wren had found out. Had come to him the night before the deed was signed, fevered and furious and clear-eyed, and had taken the pen out of his hand.

"You're not selling yours," she'd said. "You hear the dead, Cy. You're the only one of us who isn't afraid of them. If anyone goes into the dark owing nothing, it should be me."

He had argued. He could not remember a single word of the argument. What he remembered was her hand steady on the pen, and the way she signed her name the way she did everything, quickly, like jumping off the breakwater into deep water, and how she'd laughed afterward — laughed — and said, "There. Now I'm impossible too. I'll fit right in with your guild."

Three days later the fever broke. Her skin warmed from the feet up. The physicians called it providence; the priests called it a mystery; the broker collected the deed and vanished into the mountain roads and was never seen in Vellmark again.

And Wren woke changed — quieter, slower to laugh, sometimes staring at her own hands as if she'd forgotten whose they were — and within the year she had gone east with a wool caravan and stopped writing, and Cyrus had told himself that was grief doing what grief does, people drifting, lives diverging. He had told himself she was alive somewhere, married maybe, whistling through cupped hands for someone else's children.

He had never let himself ask the question the deed answered now, in ink seven years dry: *what walks around wearing a body when the soul has been sold out of it?*

The lamp guttered. Cyrus realized he was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and that his eyes were wet, and that the dead in the walls had begun again — softly, all together, in no voice he recognized — singing. Not the scattered mutter of the archive's usual hours. A single line, sustained, like a choir holding one note under a cathedral dome. He could not make out the words. He was not sure he wanted to.

* * *

Guildmaster Ossen was awake, because Ossen was always awake; the man slept the way cats do, in shallow installments, and claimed that a guildmaster who slept deeply deserved to be robbed. Cyrus found him in his tower study at the top of the hall, a round room hung with instruments of verification — balances that weighed promises, a bell that rang false in the presence of forged documents — surrounded by the green-glass lamps he favored because they flattered nothing.

Ossen looked up from a column of figures, saw Cyrus's face, and set down his pen.

"Well," he said. "Either the building's on fire or you've finally read Drawer Three-eleven."

Cyrus stood in the doorway with the deed in his hand and could not make his voice work. Ossen waited. He was very good at waiting; it was half of what he'd taught Cyrus, that the second-most-important skill in the trade was silence.

"I sold her," Cyrus said at last. "Seven years ago. I sold—" His throat closed. He held out the deed instead.

Ossen did not take it. He looked at it the way he looked at all dangerous paper, from a slight distance, head tilted, as if it might go for his throat. Then he rose, crossed the room, and shut the door, and drew the bolt, and lit a candle beside the green lamps so that the light went hard and honest.

"Sit," he said.

"I don't want to sit."

"Sit anyway. You'll want the floor closer in a minute." Ossen lowered himself back into his chair, joints cracking like green firewood, and studied Cyrus across the desk with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk but missing nothing. "Tell me what you remember."

So Cyrus told him. The plague winter. The broker. Wren's hand on the pen. The fever breaking. All of it came out flat and fast, the way confession does when you've rehearsed it in the dark for years without admitting you were rehearsing. Ossen listened without moving except to feed the candle, and when Cyrus finished, the old man was quiet for so long that the rain became the loudest thing in the room.

"The broker," Ossen said finally. "Describe him."

"Tall. Grey coat. Gloves — he never took the gloves off. He knew the forms better than I did. He knew *I* knew the forms, that was the worst part, he talked to me like a colleague." Cyrus's hands had started shaking somewhere in the telling and had not stopped. "He said the buyer wasn't particular. He said the mountain preferred what was freely given."

"And you let her sign."

"She took the pen out of my hand."

"Yes." Ossen sighed, a sound like wind through a wreck. "That sounds like her."

Cyrus went still. "You knew her?"

"I knew a girl who used to haunt my archive asking questions no apprentice should ask, and who once reorganized my oaths cabinet alphabetically by *sincerity*, which remains the most blasphemous thing ever done in this building." Something moved behind the pale eyes — not quite a smile. "Yes. I knew her. Sit down, Cyrus."

This time Cyrus sat.

"Now listen to me, because I am going to say this once and then we are going to deal with it." Ossen leaned forward, and the candlelight carved his face into ridges. "A soul-deed witnessed by no god can be broken. Bought back, bargained, stolen, sued for in three jurisdictions — the guild has recovered forty-one souls in my tenure, and buried nine brokers doing it. It is ugly work but it is *work*, and it is possible." He tapped the deed without touching it. "Turn it over."

Cyrus turned it over.

On the reverse, in the lower corner, was a seal he had taken for a watermark — a spiral pressed into the paper, concentric rings closing inward like something falling forever down a well. Except watermarks did not gleam. Watermarks did not shift, very slightly, when you were not looking directly at them, the way deep water shifts over something enormous turning in its sleep.

"That is the sigil of the Mountain Below," Ossen said. "The eldest god. The one the shrines were built to before the shrines were built to anything else. And a deed sealed by a god is not a contract, boy. It is a *fact*. You might as well try to buy back the sunrise."

"The usurper's court holds it," Cyrus said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Ashkaval. The herald yesterday — the registry, the tithe —"

"— wants every soul-deed in the free cities delivered to the mountain, yes. And now you know why, or part of why. A throne built on borrowed divinity wants to own the collateral." Ossen sat back. "You are following this faster than I expected, which means you are about to do something stupid. So I will skip ahead."

"Burn the page," Cyrus said.

Ossen blinked. "What?"

"That's what you're going to tell me. Burn the deed. Forget the entry. Let the ledger lie." Cyrus heard his own voice go low and even, the voice he used with grieving clients, the voice that was not his. "Because the guild survived seven hundred years by being invisible, and a ledger-keeper who goes chasing a god's paperwork stops being invisible, and the armies coming down the pass will follow the smoke straight here. Because Wren has been gone seven years and the world didn't end, and mine won't either. Because it's *cheaper*." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Am I close?"

Ossen regarded him for a long moment. Then he reached across the desk, took the deed gently from Cyrus's hands, and held it over the candle.

Cyrus's whole body lurched forward — he was on his feet without deciding to stand, hand outstretched, some sound tearing out of him that had no word in it — and Ossen froze, the paper an inch above the flame, watching him with an expression of terrible satisfaction.

"There it is," the old man said quietly. He withdrew the deed and laid it on the desk, smoothing it flat with two fingers. "That is the answer to the question I was actually asking. You cannot burn it. Good. Now neither of us has to pretend."

"You *bastard*—"

"I am eighty-one years old and I have buried every friend I ever made, and I will not watch you spend thirty years becoming a ghost in your own archive because you thought grief was a debt you could service quietly." Ossen's voice cracked across the room like a ledger slammed shut. "Listen to me. That entry in your book — your name, dated tomorrow — that is not an error. Entries do not write themselves. Something *knows* you are holding this deed tonight, and it is telling you that the account has come due. The mountain is waking, boy. The gods are answering prayers again — you've heard it, don't look surprised, the whole city hears it if they have ears — and when old creditors wake, they call in old paper."

"So what do I do?" Cyrus's hands were fists at his sides. "You just said a god's seal can't be broken."

"I said it couldn't be bought back, bargained, or sued for. I said nothing about *broken*." Ossen picked up the deed and held it to the light, and for a moment the spiral sigil seemed to turn, slowly, like a stair descending. "Nothing is unbreakable. That is the first lesson of this guild and the reason it exists. Every contract is a story two parties agreed to tell, and stories—" he set it down, and looked at Cyrus over the green lamplight, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, "—stories have authors. Find out who wrote this one. Find out who paid the broker. Because a nineteen-year-old bone-carver did not stumble into a bargain with the eldest god by accident, Cyrus. Someone arranged it. Someone wanted Wren's soul in that vault seven years before anyone knew there would be a war over it."

The candle flame stood perfectly still. Outside, the rain had stopped, and in the ringing quiet Cyrus became aware of the archive below them — hundreds of drawers, hundreds of the dead, all silent again, all listening.

"And until you find them?" he asked.

Ossen pushed the deed back across the desk.

"Until then," he said, "you keep it where no one can take it, and you tell no one you have it, and you start copying the registry — every soul-deed, every seal, every signature — because if the usurper's herald comes back for our books, I intend for us to know more about them than they do." He turned back to his columns of figures, dismissal in every line of his shoulders, and then paused, pen hovering.

"Cyrus. The girl who reorganized my cabinets. What was her name?"

The question was gentle, and it undid him more than the shouting had. "Wren," he said. "Her name was Wren."

"Wren," Ossen repeated, tasting it. "Then I suggest you bring her home, boy. The guild has recovered forty-one souls." He bent to his figures. "Let's see if we can make it forty-two."

Cyrus carried the deed back down through the sleeping archive, past the shelves of murmuring bone, and locked it in the iron box beneath his workbench where he kept the things he could not price. As the lock clicked home, the dead in the walls began to sing again — that single sustained note, that choir holding its breath — and this time, faintly, underneath it, he heard something else.

A whistle. Cupped hands, a harbor wind, a tune from the breakwater.

Coming up through the ivory, like light through deep water.
