## 3. Prayers Answered

The dead had been singing for three days, and Cyrus Galloway could not make them stop.

It began, he thought later, on the morning the baker's ovens went cold. He had been at his desk in the guildhall's counting room since before dawn—the hour he liked best, when Vellmark's bells were still asleep and the only voices in the world were the ones in the ivory. A whalebone scapula lay pinned beneath his left hand, salvaged from a sailor drowned off the Serrated Coast, and his burin moved through it in slow, careful strokes, cutting the man's last words into the smooth curve: *Tell my daughter the gulls were kind to me at the end.* The sailor's voice hummed up through the bone and into Cyrus's knuckles as it always did, a low murmur like wind through a keyhole, familiar as his own breathing. He did not listen. Listening was the one thing he had never learned to do safely. He only carved.

Then, somewhere past the second bell, the sailor's voice changed.

It did not grow louder, exactly. It grew *wider*—as though a door had opened somewhere behind it and let in a chorus. Cyrus's burin slipped, gouging a white furrow across the whalebone, and he sat very still with his hand pressed flat against the desk, the way a man presses a hand to a wound.

Other voices had joined the sailor's. Dozens of them. Hundreds. They came not through any single piece of ivory but through everything—the bone dust in the floorboards' cracks, the old antler handles of the guildhall drawers, the little carved button Ossen kept loose in a dish on the mantel. They rose out of every scrap of the dead that the guild had ever handled or stored or sold, and they were singing. Not words. Not the fragmented last-thoughts and half-finished farewells that Cyrus had spent thirty years translating into careful lines of script. This was melody, long and rising and terrible, all of them together, as if every soul that had ever passed through a ledger in the Guild of Impossible Things had found the same note at the same moment and decided, with one will, to hold it.

Cyrus pressed his palms over his ears. It did nothing. The song did not travel by air. It traveled by *substance*—by calcium and memory—and his hands were on the desk, and the desk had been built from a wrecked ship whose keel still held three drowned men, and so the song poured into him anyway, through skin and sinew, until his teeth ached with it.

"Stop," he said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "I'm not listening. I've never listened."

They sang anyway. And for the first time in his life, Cyrus Galloway understood what it might feel like to go mad, and he gripped the edge of the desk and breathed and waited, the way he had taught himself to wait through every storm of grief that ever came through his door, for the thing to pass.

It did not pass. By noon, the whole city was ringing with answered prayers.

* * *

He heard about the baker first because everyone heard about the baker first. Old Marta Ellings, whose ovens stood two streets from the guildhall, had gone to the cracked shrine of Saint Audra—the one that leaned against the fish-market wall, its saint's face worn featureless by salt wind, its offering bowl full of rainwater and cigarette ends for longer than anyone could remember. Marta had knelt there because her son had knelt there, and her son was dead, and she had nothing left to lose. She had prayed for her blight-ridden flour to rise. She had promised Saint Audra her winter coat, her good candlesticks, whatever a faceless saint wanted.

By midmorning the smell of bread rolled down Cordwainer Street like a tide. Every loaf Marta pulled from her ovens rose pale and perfect, crusted gold, warm as a living thing. She wept into her flour. She gave loaves away until the street smelled like a festival. And the people of Vellmark, who had been raised on the doctrine that the gods were gone—that the shrines were monuments, the prayers were habits, and the only miracles worth believing in were the ones the guild could broker at a price—began, in ones and twos and then in dozens, to kneel at the cracked places in the walls.

By evening, the stories came faster than the bells could ring them.

A fisherman on the Gray Quay had prayed for calm water and gotten it—a flatness on the sea so complete that his boat seemed to rest on glass. A child with a fevered lung woke whole in the Lowmarsh district while her mother slept upright in a chair. A widow found her husband's lost wedding ring sitting on her windowsill, polished bright, though the house had been searched to its joists.

And then, on the third day, the drowned boy came back.

Cyrus heard it from Hesk, who arrived at the guildhall with his collar turned up against a rain that fell soft and gray and endless, and who told it in his flat collector's voice, the voice he used for debts, which somehow made it worse.

"Tamber boy," Hesk said. "Went under the ice off Tarrow's Bridge two winters back. His mother never stopped going to the shrine by the bridge—old river-shrine, some forgotten water-saint, cracked clean down the middle since before you were born. Tuesday night she prayed. Wednesday morning—" He paused, and even Hesk's flatness wavered, just slightly, at the seam. "Wednesday morning they pulled her body from the house and took the boy to the temple physicians. Because he came home. He walked in the door dripping riverwater, and he kissed her cheek, and he said, *Mother, the cold is over.*

"But it wasn't him." Hesk pulled off his wet gloves, finger by finger. "The physicians say the body is his. Grown two years, which is impossible, but the body is his—same scar on the chin, same crooked tooth. But it doesn't blink right. It doesn't sleep. It stands at the window at night looking toward the river, and when they ask it questions it answers in its mother's voice, not its own. Whatever came back wearing Tamber," Hesk said, "it isn't Tamber. Something heard her prayer. Something answered it. And the answer is standing at a window in Lowmarsh right now, smiling at the water."

Ossen said nothing all evening. He sat by the guildhall fire with his bad leg propped on a stool, turning his carved button over and over between thumb and forefinger—which meant he could hear them too, or suspected, or feared. The guildmaster was ninety-one years old and had buried three apprentices and two cities' worth of certainties, and when he finally spoke, it was to the fire, not to Cyrus.

"The trade's surging," he said. "Have you seen the books?"

Cyrus had. That was the other strangeness, the mundane strangeness, almost comforting beside the singing: the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things had begun to fill like rivers in thaw. For thirty years the guild had traded quietly—in relics authenticated by bone, in oaths witnessed by things older than law, in the small desperate commerce of people who needed what could not be bought. Business was steady the way rain was steady. Now it was a flood. People were coming to the guildhall doors asking for locks of hair that would bring their dead back lucid in dreams. Asking for contracts written in ash. Asking, twice this week alone, for the impossible thing itself—for their sons and daughters and mothers returned, whole and warm, the way the drowned boy had been returned, wrong.

"We don't deal in resurrection," Cyrus said. "We never have."

"No," said Ossen. "We deal in what people will pay for. There's a difference, and it has kept this guild alive for four hundred years, and I am too old to learn whether it still holds." He set the button down at last and looked at Cyrus with eyes gone pale as skimmed milk. "What are they saying? The dead. You've gone white as tallow, boy, and you've had your hands on that desk all week."

Cyrus hesitated. Thirty years of discipline held his tongue for exactly as long as it took to draw one breath.

"They're singing," he said. "All of them. Together. Like a choir that's been rehearsing somewhere I couldn't hear, and now the door's open." He turned his hands palm-up, studied them as though they belonged to someone else. "In thirty years I have never once heard two souls agree on anything. The dead are like us—frightened, selfish, scattered. They don't sing in unison. They *can't*. Unless something is leading them."

Ossen was quiet a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the rafters where the guild kept its oldest bones, the singing threaded faintly through the smoke-dark air.

"Or unless something has changed," Ossen said, "about the world they're in."

* * *

Maren Odell had kept the shrine of Saint Ferrow at the end of Pilgrim's Walk for nineteen years, and in nineteen years the saint had never once spoken to her, which was how she preferred it. Her god—the small, nameless river-god whose cracked stone face stood in the alcove behind her shrine, worn smooth by floods older than the city—had also never spoken. Maren swept the steps, refilled the oil lamps, fed coins to the poor-box, and maintained the great comfortable silence that was the whole architecture of faith in Vellmark: a silence built jointly by the living and the absent, each agreeing not to embarrass the other.

On the fourth night of the singing, the silence broke.

She had stayed late to trim the lamp wicks, and she was reaching into the alcove with the brass snuffer when she felt it—a warmth coming off the stone like breath, like the flank of a large animal. Maren froze. River-gods do not breathe. Stone does not warm. Nineteen years of doctrine stood in her chest like a wall, and then the crack in the saint's face—which she had swept around, prayed before, patched twice with lime mortar—widened with a sound like ice on a pond giving way, and light came out of it. Not lamplight. Not moonlight. A green-gray light, the color of deep water over stones, and it filled the alcove and ran down the wall and pooled across the flagstones where Maren stood, and she felt it move over her feet like the first cold inch of a rising tide.

She should have run. She had time to know that. Instead she did what she had done every dusk for nineteen years: she knelt.

And her god—who had never spoken, who owed her nothing, whom she had served in the perfect confidence of being unobserved—looked at her. She felt the attention settle on her the way a hand settles on a head. And it said, in a voice that came not through her ears but up through the wet stone, through the soles of her shoes, through the long bones of her legs:

*I am waking. Wake with me. Take this, keeper, so that you may read what is coming.*

Something entered her mouth then—not painful, but total, like swallowing the river—and when the light faded and Maren Odell picked herself up off the flagstones of her shrine and opened her mouth to cry out at the miracle, no sound came.

Her voice was gone. Taken whole, taken cleanly, like an offering lifted from a bowl.

In its place, when she looked at the cracked stone face of her god—at the sigils now glowing faintly in the fissure, spiraling marks she had never seen and yet understood the way one understands one's own name—she found that she could read them. Every one. Every god-mark on every shrine in Vellmark would be an open book to her now, and she knew, with the knowledge the river had poured into her, that this was the price and the purpose both. Her voice for their language. Her silence for their sight.

Maren knelt again in the dark, in the wet, and wept without sound, and laughed without sound, and did not know which she was doing.

* * *

Cyrus did not sleep that night. He sat in the counting room with every lamp lit and the registry open before him, because work was the only prayer he knew, and because the singing had grown so loud in the small hours that he had taken to holding a piece of raw ivory against his sternum just to give the chorus a single clear channel—one throat instead of hundreds, one drowned whaler's fragmentary death-hymn instead of the vast wordless accord that rose now out of every bone in the building. Even so, the harmony leaked around the edges of it. Even so, he found himself tapping his foot to it, which frightened him more than anything else: that some part of him already knew the tune.

Near the coldest hour, someone knocked at the guildhall's side door—three knocks, a pause, three more, the old sign of a member come home.

He found a woman on the step, soaked through, hood back, gray-eyed and hollow-cheeked, with river silt still drying in the seams of her sleeves. He knew her distantly: the shrine-keeper from Pilgrim's Walk, the quiet one who came to the guild once a year to buy lamp-oil in bulk and never haggled. She was shaking. Not from cold—or not only from cold. When she looked up at him, her face wore the expression he had seen a thousand times across his ledger desk, on the faces of people carrying news too large for one body.

"You're the bone-carver," she wrote on a slate she must have taken from her own shrine, chalk letters quick and sure. "They say you hear the dead."

"I hear something," Cyrus said. "Since three nights ago. All of them, singing together. I don't know why."

The woman—Maren, she wrote next, though he hadn't asked—stared at him for a long moment. Then she wiped the slate with her sleeve and wrote again, pressing hard enough that the chalk squeaked:

*I can read what's waking them. Or beginning to. The shrines are cracking open all over the city—all over the coast, I think—and there are signs in the stone, god-marks, old ones. Most say the same thing. But one of them, the big one, the one under the mountain—they'll want to see this.*

She turned the slate. Beneath her chalk rendering of a sigil—a circle coiled like a sleeping serpent, crossed by a line that descended downward through it, down and down, into depth—Cyrus felt the singing in his chest stutter and fall silent for the first time in four days.

Not silent. *Attentive.* Every soul in the guildhall, every dead sailor and whaler and pauper whose bones the guild had ever touched, went still at once, the way a congregation goes still when the doors open at the back of the church.

Because Cyrus knew that mark. He had cut its twin himself, seven years ago, with a steady hand and a heart full of desperation, into the corner of a contract he had signed in his own blood: the deed that had carried Wren's soul down into the court beneath the mountain.

The mountain was waking. And the first thing it would remember, Cyrus understood, looking at the serpent-coil drawn in a shrine-keeper's shaking chalk, was what it held.

Maren wiped the slate and wrote one line more, and held it up between them in the doorway, in the gray beginning of a rain that would not stop for a season:

*Whatever you sold to wake it—it's about to be called due.*
