## 4. The Usurper's Tithe

The morning the herald came to Vellmark, Cyrus Galloway was carving a drowned man's last words into a rib of whalebone, and the dead were singing.

Not the dead themselves—he had not heard them sing in unison before, not in thirty years of listening through ivory and bone—but something beneath them, a chord struck low and held, as if every voice in the world had been tuned to the same distant bell. He had woken with it humming in his molars. It had not stopped since.

He worked at his bench by the guildhall's east window, where the light came down gray and even off the harbor fog, good light for bone. The whalebone was old stock, yellowed like a page left too long in the sun, and it took the graver beautifully. *Tell Marta the nets were never her fault,* the sailor had said, or rather the sailor's ghost had said it, caught in the ivory the way a fly is caught in amber, still speaking its one sentence over and over into Cyrus's hands. *The sea took me. Not her. Tell her.*

Cyrus carved the words small and true, in the tight cursive the trade demanded, and did not think about how the singing underneath had grown louder each day this week, or how the shrines across Vellmark had begun to crack their seals like eggs hatching, or how the ledger on his desk—his own ledger, kept in his own hand for eleven years—still held that entry dated tomorrow.

*Galloway, C. One soul, received.*

He had not burned the page. Ossen had told him to. Ossen had stood over him in the archive with the lamp turned low and said, *Burn it, boy, and let the ink forget you,* and Cyrus had carried the ledger back upstairs instead and set it beneath his elbow like a stone he could lean against. Some part of him—the bookkeeper part, the part that believed every debt must be written down or it would only be written larger elsewhere—could not bring itself to destroy the record of what he had done. Wren's name was in that book. If he burned the page, she would be nowhere at all.

So he carved, and the dead sang under their breath, and the fog sat on the harbor like a lid on a pot.

The knock came at midmorning: three blows, spaced evenly, too loud for the door's thin oak. Not a client's knock. Clients knocked apologetically, twice, hoping no one was home. This was the knock of someone who knew exactly who was home and wanted them to know it too.

Cyrus set down the graver. In the whalebone, the sailor's sentence ended mid-word, and the ghost's voice frayed into silence—a small mercy, though it felt like one more thing interrupted that would have to be finished later, at cost.

Downstairs, the guildhall's front room smelled of tallow and old paper, as it always did. Ossen was already there, standing behind the counter with his hands flat on the wood, and Maren of the cracked shrine stood near the hearth with her shawl drawn close, watching the door. She had come an hour ago, Cyrus realized now—had come without being summoned, the way shrine-keepers came when their gods dreamed loudly—and her silence filled the room more thoroughly than any noise could have. She looked at the door the way sailors look at weather.

Ossen glanced at Cyrus once. *Invisible,* his eyes said, the old instruction, the first rule of the guild: stand in plain sight and be no one. Cyrus moved to the shadowed end of the counter and became furniture.

The door opened without further knocking.

The herald was tall in the way that made rooms feel smaller, robed in ash-gray wool stitched with a single mark over the heart: a circle bisected by a descending line, the tithe-mark, which Cyrus had seen before only in old engravings and on the wax of certain letters no one admitted to receiving. The herald's face was unremarkable, deliberately so, the face of a person whose job required being forgotten. Only the eyes were wrong—too steady, too dry, as if they had been open a very long time.

"Guildmaster," the herald said. The voice was pleasant and completely without warmth, a voice like a receipt. "I bring the regard of Ashkaval, rightful sovereign of the mountain court and heir of all oaths sworn beneath it."

"There is no guild here," Ossen said mildly. "This is a counting-house. We appraise curiosities."

The herald smiled without moving anything but the mouth. "Of course. A counting-house." From within the gray robe came a scroll, sealed in black wax, and the herald laid it on the counter between them with the care of someone setting down a blade. "Then this will be simple. My lord requires the registry of soul-contracts held by the Guild of Impossible Things. All deeds. All bonds. All names."

The fire popped in the hearth. Maren's hands tightened in her shawl.

"You mistake us," Ossen said. "There are no such registries. There has been no such trade in this city for a generation. Whatever my lord Ashkaval has been told—"

"He has been told everything." The herald's dry eyes moved—not to Ossen, but around the room, touching the shelves, the ledgers behind their cage of latticed wood, the long counter, and lastly, briefly, Cyrus himself, standing in his shadow. The gaze passed over him the way a hand passes over a coat checking for coins. "My lord's armies are nine days from the free cities. Vellmark will open its gates or it will learn why the mountain has no doors. The registry is the price of your invisibility, Guildmaster. You have kept it so long; surely you understand its value."

"And if we do not surrender it?"

"Then you will be found," the herald said, "and everything you have hidden will be read aloud."

* * *

Afterward—it could not have taken more than ten minutes, though it felt like the length of a funeral—Cyrus climbed back to his bench and found his hands were shaking, and made them stop, because a carver's hands did not shake, and because there was work.

But the work would not settle. The whalebone lay where he'd left it, the sailor's sentence broken mid-word, and when Cyrus picked up the graver the dead man's voice came back faint and frightened, *tell her, tell her*, and underneath it the great chord still hummed, louder now, as if whatever sang below the world had heard the knock at the guildhall door and leaned closer to listen.

He thought about the herald's eyes passing over him. He had spent his whole life being passed over; it was his craft, his armor, the quiet genius of his existence. Standing unseen in plain sight. And yet for one moment—one cold, precise moment—he had felt *seen*, felt catalogued, the way he himself catalogued relics: *one item, provenance unknown, value pending.* The usurper's people were auditors too. That was the thing he understood now, sitting in his own gray light with the fog pressing at the window. Ashkaval was not merely a conqueror. He was a collector. And collectors, Cyrus knew from the inside, never stopped at one acquisition.

He went down to the archive.

It was not forbidden; Ossen had never forbidden him anything, which was its own kind of trust and its own kind of wound. The archive lived two floors down, behind a door disguised as a wall, in long galleries of shelved bone and vellum and slate, organized by year, by debtor, by the nature of the impossible thing traded. Cyrus walked the aisles with the lamp held high, past oaths preserved in ambergris and promises knotted into hair, until he reached the vault-cage where the master registry slept in its iron cradle.

He did not touch it. He stood before it instead, hands clasped behind his back like a man at a graveside, and read the spines of the annual volumes, and thought about what the herald had said. *Everything you have hidden will be read aloud.*

Wren's deed was in there. Sealed with a god's sigil—he had found it three nights ago, and had not slept properly since. Nearly unbreakable, Ossen had said, and Ossen did not use nearly lightly. But nearly was not absolutely, and Cyrus was a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers knew that every contract, however divine its seal, was still an agreement between parties—and agreements could be renegotiated, voided, stolen, forged, or paid out. There was always a clause. There was always a clause; finding it was simply a matter of reading closely enough.

If Ashkaval took the registry, though—if the registry went beneath the mountain into the hands of a man who fed souls to waking gods like logs to a furnace—then Wren's deed would become not a contract but a weapon, and the clause would be read by someone who wanted her exactly where she was.

He heard footsteps on the stair and did not turn. Only Ossen and Maren knew the door.

"She would not want you to burn yourself for her," Ossen said quietly, coming to stand beside him among the shelves. The old guildmaster's voice was rough as unsanded wood. He was looking at the iron cradle, not at Cyrus. "That girl. She sold herself clean and asked nothing, and now you stand here at midnight planning wars over a page."

"It's not midnight."

"It's always midnight in this room." Ossen touched the cage with one knuckle, gently, the way he touched everything valuable. "You heard what the herald offered. The registry for our invisibility. Eleven years I've kept this guild small enough to be ignored, boy. Small enough that kings forgot our street existed. And in one morning—" He exhaled through his nose. "One morning, and a man in gray stands in my front room saying *we know what you are*."

"The tithe-mark," Cyrus said. "On the door."

"Yes." Ossen's jaw worked. "You saw it."

Everyone had seen it. When the herald left—unhurried, unhurried the way floodwater is unhurried—he had paused at the guildhall's threshold and pressed two fingers to the oak, and the wood had taken the mark the way paper takes flame: a slow bloom of black, the circle and the descending line, burned an inch deep and cold to the touch, colder than the door around it. By evening, Cyrus knew, every informer and tithe-agent and hungry neighbor in Vellmark would know precisely which building wore it. The guild's invisibility had not been surrendered. It had been *revoked*.

"We could pay," Cyrus said, and hated the words even as they left him. "Give them a copy. A false registry—Sable could forge one in a day, they'd never—"

"They'd know in a week." Ossen shook his head slowly. "A man who keeps gods does not keep bad accounts. No, boy. The moment we handed over even a forgery, we'd be confessing the true one exists. Refusal is the only honest lie we have left." He was silent a moment, and then, lower: "Besides. There are four hundred souls in that registry. Four hundred lanterns. You think I can sign my name to handing those to a man who hangs them in vaults?"

"No," Cyrus said. "Neither can I."

Ossen looked at him then, really looked, and something moved behind the old man's eyes—something that might have been pride, or might have been grief wearing pride's coat. "No," he said. "I didn't think you could. That's the trouble with you, Galloway. You were supposed to stay invisible. Instead you keep becoming visible in ways I can't carve back out."

* * *

That night Cyrus did not sleep. He sat at his bench with the lamp trimmed low and copied.

He had decided it somewhere between the archive and the second glass of bitter tea: if the registry could not be defended by refusal alone—and it could not, not against armies, not against a man who collected gods—then it would have to be defended by redundancy. Every deed, every bond, every clause, copied in his own small true hand onto slips of whalebone thin as leaves, bone being the one material in the world that remembered what was written on it even after burning. Let Ashkaval take the vellum. Let him burn the guildhall to its foundations. The bones would remember. Bones always told him the truth eventually; it was the one law of his life he had never once seen broken.

He worked until the candle guttered, copying contracts he barely understood, clauses in dead dialects, sigils that made his teeth ache to look at. And it was near the bottom of the third hour, copying a deed of servitude from the reign of some king whose name had been deliberately scratched out of history, that he noticed the pattern.

Every old contract—every one older than sixty years—carried the same countersignature. Not a human name. A seal: a circle bisected by a descending line.

The tithe-mark.

Cyrus sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below the floorboards, faint as breath, the chord of the singing dead shifted key.

The mark was not Ashkaval's. Or rather—it had never belonged only to Ashkaval. It was older than the usurper, older than his grandfather's grandfather; it was the mark of the mountain court itself, of whatever power sat beneath it and signed its name to bargains. Ashkaval had not invented the tithe. He had inherited it. He was collecting on debts the mountain had held for centuries—and the guild, invisible little guild, had been doing business with that power all along, selling and buying and witnessing, while the mark sat patiently at the bottom of every page like a hook waiting in dark water.

And Wren's deed bore the same seal. Witnessed by the same signer.

*Everything you have hidden will be read aloud.*

Cyrus pressed his palms flat against the desk until the shaking stopped. Then, in the margin of his working notes, in his smallest, truest hand, he wrote a single line:

*We were never trading with men.*

Outside, the fog had lifted off the harbor, and the sky above Vellmark was clear for the first time in a week—clear, and full of stars, and directly over the mountain road to the south, low on the horizon, a light burned that was not a star and did not move. Campfires. Thousands of them. Nine days away, the herald had said.

Cyrus blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with the bones, listening to the dead sing, and waited for morning to make the refusal official.
