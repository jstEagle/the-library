## 5. Ledger of the Lost

The guildhall slept the way old buildings sleep: in stages, with creaks and settling groans like an animal dreaming of its younger weight. Cyrus Galloway knew every one of those sounds the way a sailor knows the language of rigging. The third-floor joists spoke first, always—the archive's weight pressing down through two centuries of oak—and then the iron stove in the counting room ticked as it cooled, and then, if the wind came off the harbor from the northeast, the great ledger-doors downstairs would breathe against their frames with a sound like slow exhalation.

Tonight all three sounds came in their proper order, and after the last of them he counted to three hundred before he moved.

He had dressed without a candle. That was the trick of moving through a house you had served for nineteen years: your body memorized the rooms so that your conscience didn't have to watch itself doing what it was about to do. He wore his ordinary coat, the one with ink stains at both cuffs, because if anyone found him abroad in the dark, invisibility was his armor and he meant to wear every layer of it. He carried no lamp. He carried instead a satchel containing blank paper, a stoppered inkhorn, three quibs of different weights, and—wrapped in oilcloth against his heart, where a man might carry a locket—a single page folded twice.

Wren's contract. Or rather, the copy of the summary entry he'd traced two nights ago, the one line among ten thousand that read:

*Galloway, C. — sale of one (1) human soul, unencumbered, sound condition — buyer: court beneath the mountain — witnessed: [sigil] — price received: 400 marks & forgiveness of debt.*

*Forgiveness of debt.* He could still feel the way his stomach had dropped when he'd parsed that phrase. Not paid. *Forgiven*. Which meant that seven years ago, someone else had held a claim against him large enough to buy a soul with, and had traded it away for Wren's light. He had spent two days telling himself it was a clerical shorthand, a bookkeeper's abbreviation, nothing more. He was very good at telling himself things. It was, in a sense, his profession.

The stairs took him down past the second-floor dormitories where the guild's outcasts slept—Sable's door shut, Hesk snoring like a landslide in progress, the memory-forger's room smelling even through wood of hot wax and cut lavender. Past the counting room with its cold stove. Down to the archive floor, where the guild kept what it kept.

Most people in Vellmark believed the Guild of Impossible Things dealt in relics. Curiosities, mostly: saints' knuckles that were certainly pig bone, swords with better stories than edges, love philtres brewed by frauds with honest hands. And this was true, as far as it went. But beneath the relic trade, in ledgers that had never once been shown to a customs officer or a magistrate, the guild recorded its real business. Oaths brokered. Names bought and sold. Debts transferred between the living and the dead. And souls—souls above all—because a soul was the only collateral that could never be forged, never be stolen, never be devalued. You could burn everything else a person owned and they would still have themselves to spend.

Cyrus had entered perhaps six hundred such transactions into the master registry over nineteen years. He had always told himself that the work was clean. That he was only a hand, a pen, a column of figures. That the guilt belonged to sellers and buyers, and he merely recorded what they did to each other, the way a mirror records a murder without committing it.

Seven years ago he had stopped being a mirror. Seven years ago he had signed.

* * *

The archive was a long vaulted room lined floor to ceiling with deed-chests, each chest holding contracts sealed in wax, tallow, or—for the oldest ones—lead. No windows. One door, locked from the inside with a key that lived around Ossen's neck, except that Ossen, trusting his ledger-keeper beyond all reason, had given him a second key eleven years ago with the words *"You count faster than I do, and my eyes are going."*

Cyrus let himself in and stood a moment in the dark, listening.

He heard what he always heard here, and it was worse tonight than it had ever been. The dead did not rest quietly in the archive; they soaked into the paper like damp into plaster. A soul-contract is written on vellum, and vellum is skin, and skin remembers. For nineteen years he had heard them as a murmur, a tide-noise, the way you hear the sea through a closed window—names repeating themselves, prices repeating themselves, the small arithmetic of surrender murmured over and over. *Four hundred marks. Four hundred marks. My daughter's fever broken, that was the price. Four hundred marks.*

But since the shrines had begun cracking open across Vellmark—since the baker's blight had lifted overnight and the drowned child had come walking up out of the river wrong—the tide-noise had changed. Tonight the dead were not murmuring. They were listening.

He felt it as he lit the small hooded lamp he'd hidden behind the R-to-S deed-chest two days ago, during the audit, when he'd already known what he would have to do. The whole room had gone quiet in the way a forest goes quiet, the way a choir goes quiet between movements—not empty but attentive. Thousands of dead names, pressed into vellum, waiting to hear whether the man with the key had finally come to read them.

"Keep your voices," he said softly, to no one, to everyone. "I'm not here for you."

They didn't answer. They never answered directly. But the silence rearranged itself around him like water around a wading man, and he chose, as he had chosen for thirty-one years, to take it for comfort.

He started at the far end of the wall, where the deeds hung in chronological order by year of transaction, and he worked methodically, because method was the only prayer he trusted. Every soul-contract in the registry, copied in his own fair hand onto blank paper: seller, buyer, vessel, witness, price. Not the summaries—the full deeds, because summaries were how lies were laundered into history, and he had learned in the last week that he had been lied to, or had lied to himself, for seven years.

It was slow work. Each chest opened with a lead seal to crack and a smell like cold cellars. Each deed demanded to be read twice—once with his eyes, once with the part of him that heard the vellum speak. By the second hour his writing hand ached and his ears had begun to ring faintly, the way they always did when he listened too hard to too many dead things at once. He copied forty-one contracts before midnight. He copied sixty-three by the time the harbor bells rang two. His inkhorn ran dry and he refilled it from the reserve bottle in his satchel, and his fingers were so steady with habit that he noticed only afterward that his heart had been hammering the entire time.

Sixty-three contracts. Sixty-three souls sold out of Vellmark alone in the years since Wren's. He copied each one faithfully, and with each one something in him hardened and something else in him broke, and he understood that these were the same process viewed from opposite sides, the way grief and resolve are the same fire seen entering and leaving a room.

*You sold her,* said a voice that was not any of the dead ones. It lived behind his sternum and it kept guild hours. *You sat at this desk and you wrote the entry yourself and you went home and slept.*

*I saved my sister,* he answered it. *She was dying of the wasting debt. Four hundred marks or she went to the debtor's hospital, and no one comes back from the debtor's hospital with their hands.*

*And whose idea was the price? Yours? Or does the ledger remember differently?*

That was the question. That was the whole night distilled into one question, and there was only one way to answer it.

* * *

Wren's original deed was filed under the year of the sale, in the drawer for private sales—transactions conducted outside the guild's open auction days, between named parties, sealed under bond. Private sales were rare. They were also, Cyrus now understood, how the guild laundered its worst business: no bidding, no witnesses among the members, just a clerk, a seller, a buyer's agent, and God knew what standing quietly in the corner where only the vellum could see it.

The drawer stuck, as it always stuck, and he eased it with the little rocking motion Ossen had taught him—*"persuade, don't fight; that's true of drawers and debtors both"*—and lifted out the deed.

He knew it before he unrolled it. Of course he knew it. He had signed it. But memory is a forger's apprentice, and it had softened the thing across seven years, rounded its corners, blurred the hand. Laid flat under the hooded lamp, the deed was brutal in its clarity. His own signature sat at the bottom right, unmistakable, the *y* in *Galloway* looping exactly the way it looped still. Above it, in a hand he recognized with a fresh lurch of sickness—Ossen's hand, younger, less palsied—the terms of the sale, transcribed and countersigned. And above *that*, in a third hand, cramped and hurried, the seller's attestation.

His own name again. Twice. Once as seller's agent, once as guarantor.

*I, Cyrus Galloway, do attest that the soul herein conveyed is mine to convey, being held in surety against debts contracted by the undersigned...*

He read it four times. The words did not change. Words never changed; that was their cruelty and their mercy. He had attested that Wren's soul was his to sell. Not hers. His. As though she had been a chest, a deed-house, a column in a ledger. And below the attestation, the price clause, the one the summary had abbreviated until it looked like mercy:

*...in consideration whereof the buyer shall forgive unto the undersigned all debts outstanding against his name, whencesoever contracted, and shall further hold the conveyed soul inviolate against use, consumption, or expenditure, save only as collateral, until such time as the principal debt be discharged...*

*Collateral.* Not consumed. Not spent. Kept—as a lender keeps a ring, as a pawnshop keeps a violin, untouched and gleaming in a dark drawer, valued precisely because it is not used. She was alive down there, then. Whatever the court beneath the mountain was, whatever Ashkaval's ancestors had built in the deep places, Wren's soul hung somewhere in it whole, waiting for a debt to be discharged that Cyrus had never known he owed.

He sat back on his heels in the cold archive and laughed once, briefly, horribly, and the sound frightened the silence into stillness.

Then he turned the deed over to copy the seal.

Every contract bore a seal. Wax, usually, stamped with the guild's sigil—an open hand holding a closed eye—or with a buyer's private mark. He expected the Mountain Below's mark, whatever form it took; some heraldic arrangement of roots and stone, perhaps, some emblem of the deep courts. What he did not expect was the sigil that met him when the lamplight crossed the wax.

It was not a stamp. Stamps press *into* wax. This seal stood *out* of it, raised a hair's breadth above the surface, as though the wax had grown upward around something invisible to hold its shape. It was circular, and within the circle was a mountain drawn in a single unbroken line—one line, beginning at the base, climbing to the summit, descending again, and closing where it began without ever lifting from the wax. And along that line, if he tilted the lamp, ran characters. Tiny ones. Older than any alphabet he knew, older than the guild's founding, older than Vellmark's walls. They were not letters. Letters are agreed-upon sounds. These were agreements themselves—each character a small binding, a knot tied in the substance of the world.

Maren's voice came back to him unbidden, from three nights ago, in the cracked shrine by the tanneries, when she had shown him the sigils scratched into her god's broken altar: *These aren't prayers, carver. Prayers go up. These came down. These are the gods' own handwriting, and I can read them the way you read your ledgers—badly, slowly, and only because I've lost everything else worth reading.*

He had thought, then, that she meant the shrine-gods. The small ones. The hearth-gods and river-gods, the waking handful stirring in Vellmark's neglected corners.

This sigil on Wren's deed was not small. He could feel its size the way you feel the size of a cathedral by the draft. The single unbroken line hummed against his hearing—not in the ears, which had begun their long dull failure, but in the bones behind them, in the ivory-handled knife at his belt, in his teeth. The dead in the archive had gone absolutely silent, every name on every shelf of vellum holding its breath, and Cyrus understood with the calm of total catastrophe that he was looking at a god's signature on a piece of paper he himself had signed.

A divine witness. The sale had been witnessed—notarized—*underwritten* by something old enough that mountains were its middle age. And a contract sealed by a god was not a contract the way men understood contracts. Men broke contracts; that was half the guild's business, arbitrating the breakage. Gods did not break what they signed. Gods *kept* accounts. The Mountain Below had held Wren's soul for seven years not out of malice but out of exactitude, the terrible patient exactitude of a creditor who has forever.

Nearly unbreakable, then. Nearly. He clung to the *nearly* the way a drowning man clings to a plank with a crack in it. Because nearly unbreakable meant there was a seam. Because Maren could read the handwriting. Because the god who had signed was waking—he had felt it in the singing of the dead, in the cracked shrines, in the wrongness of the returned drowned child—and a waking god might be reasoned with, bargained with, presented with its own ledger and asked to settle accounts. That was all he had. That was everything he had: a crack in a plank, a woman with no voice who could read, and nineteen years of knowing how to find a number in a book.

He copied the sigil three times, pressing hard, tracing the unbroken line until his hand knew its shape blind. Then he rolled the original deed closed, replaced it in the drawer, persuaded the drawer shut, and sat for a moment in the dark with the copies spread before him—sixty-three souls and one god's signature, the whole terrible architecture of his life laid out in his own fair hand.

Determination and despair had stopped being distinguishable hours ago. He noted this the way he noted all entries now: accurately, without comment, in the ledger behind his sternum.

* * *

He was folding the copies into his satchel when he heard the stair outside the archive door creak.

Not the building's voice. The building settled downward and inward, always toward its foundations. This creak rose, foot by foot, from below. Someone was climbing.

Cyrus killed the lamp with his palm and stood motionless in the absolute black, and the dead in the vellum around him woke all at once—not murmuring now, not listening, but *whispering*, a thousand parchments rustling with soundless urgency, and whether they warned him or betrayed him he could not tell, because for the first time in his life he couldn't separate their voices from the ringing in his own skull.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

A key slid into the outer lock—slowly, expertly, the rattle muffled by a sleeve. And through the door, in a voice pitched low and pleasant and utterly unfamiliar, a man said:

"You needn't hide, Master Ledger-keeper. I've read your hand for years. I'd know it anywhere." A pause; the lock turning. "Besides—we share a creditor, you and I. And I've come about the balance."
