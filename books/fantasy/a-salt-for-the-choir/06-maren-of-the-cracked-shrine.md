## 6. Maren of the Cracked Shrine

Maren had kept the shrine of the Unnamed for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once spoken to it aloud.

This was not piety. It was arithmetic. A prayer was a coin, and coins spent carelessly bought ruin. She had learned that lesson at nineteen, kneeling in this same shrine with her mother's fever burning through the house behind her, and she had prayed with everything she had—*take anything, take whatever you want, only let her live*—and the god beneath the cracked flagstone had considered the offer with the patience of stone and accepted it.

Her mother lived. Maren's voice did not.

She had walked out of the shrine the next morning into a world gone silent at the edges, her own breath the loudest thing she owned. The priest who found her there wrote in the temple ledger: *Answered. Cost: the suppliant's voice.* He wrote it the way one recorded a hailstorm or a good harvest, without comment, and Maren understood even then that the gods had never stopped being real. They had only stopped being *asked*, because asking was expensive and people had grown poor.

So she stayed. Someone had to sweep the floor of a god who took payment in throats. Someone had to keep the wick trimmed and the offering bowl clean and the crack in the flagstone—the long jagged seam where the god's attention seeped up through the world like cold through a window—swept free of dust. Eleven years of small maintenance. Eleven years of silence, which was not emptiness but a kind of listening, because when you cannot speak you learn to hear everything else: the rats in the wall, the wind worrying the shutters, and underneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the slow breathing of the thing below the stone.

For eleven years it breathed evenly.

Three weeks ago, it began to breathe faster.

* * *

She noticed first as warmth. The flagstone over the crack, always cool even in high summer, grew warm enough that bare feet flinched from it. Then the offerings began to vanish—not eaten by cats or stolen by the desperate, which she could have forgiven, but *consumed*: the bread gone to dry husks, the milk soured black in the bowl, the honey crystallized into something like amber teeth. Things were being taken down. Things were being paid upward, or paid across, and Maren, who understood ledgers if nothing else, recognized the motion of debt changing hands.

Then the dreams started.

In them she stood in the shrine, whole-voiced, singing the old hymns she had never learned, and every note she sang fell through the crack like a coin into a well, and something far below caught each one and said *thank you* in a voice like a mountain settling. She woke each morning with her throat aching though she had made no sound, and with the unshakable sense of having been *used*—of having been, in her sleep, a spout through which something poured.

On the ninth night of dreams, the crack widened.

It happened at dusk, while she knelt scrubbing the flagstones. No sound preceded it—or none she could hear; sound had never been hers to trust—but the candle flames all leaned toward the crack at once, like iron filings finding north, and the stone split with a groan she felt in her knees and her back teeth. Not much. A finger's width more. Enough that when she leaned over it she felt air moving *upward*, warm and sweet-smelling, like breath from an open oven, like the exhalation of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

And threaded through that breath, so faint she might have imagined it, came voices.

Not the dead—she knew the dead by their texture, thin and papery, the residue of lives. These voices were thick, wet, alive, thousands of them overlapping, and they were all saying the same thing in a hundred tongues and dialects and stammering half-learned prayers: *help me, help me, help me, give me back my son, heal my fields, let me win, let her love me, let me live.* Prayers. A river of prayers, pouring downward past her like water finding a drain, and somewhere far below, something was drinking.

Maren sat back on her heels among her candles and understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first: the old gods were waking all across Vellmark and beyond, cracking their shrines open, answering prayers that hadn't been answered in centuries—and they were waking not of their own accord but because someone was *feeding* them. Herding prayer like cattle down chutes. Stoking gods like furnaces.

The second: whoever was doing the feeding was doing it from beneath a mountain.

* * *

She should have run then. She knew that too. A sensible woman would have folded her hands, bowed to the crack, and walked out of Vellmark before the thing below finished waking and noticed the mute girl who had been keeping its doorstep for a decade. Maren had survived nineteen years of voicelessness and eleven years of poverty by being sensible.

But she had also spent those years learning everything a voiceless woman could learn about what people said when they thought no one was listening, and lately, in the market, in the taverns where she delivered the shrine's tallow, she had been hearing the shape of the city's fear. The baker whose blight lifted overnight and whose bread now rose weeping. The drowned child pulled from the canal after three days, breathing, *wrong*—eyes too still, hands too cold, smiling at his mother with someone else's smile. The shrines opening like boils. And threading under all of it, the other rumor, the political one, the one men spoke of in low voices over beer: Ashkaval's armies marching on the free cities, and the usurper's heralds carrying sealed writs, and the word *mandate* spoken as though it were a physical object, a deed, a chain of title.

Gods waking. Armies marching. And between them, like a keystone, the court beneath the mountain.

Maren could not speak. But she could read—sigils best of all, the old god-marks that priests had let fall out of use because the gods themselves had gone quiet and reading them became a curiosity rather than a necessity. She had taught herself from the marginalia of a burned hymnal, and she had spent eleven years staring at the sigils worn into her own shrine's lintel until she dreamed in them.

And three days ago, a man had come to her shrine. Bone-carver, the neighbors called him. Galloway. Quiet as she was, almost—he wasn't mute, merely practiced at silence, the way certain animals are. He had stood before her cracked altar holding a scrap of whalebone like a man holding a dying bird, and he had looked at the god-sigil carved above it and she had watched him fail to read it. Watched him trace the lines with his eyes the way an illiterate man traces writing, hungry and locked out.

He had left without speaking. But he had left knowing things she needed, and needing things she had, and Maren had spent three days deciding, and sensibility had lost.

* * *

She went to find him on a morning of hard frost, when Vellmark's chimneys stood up straight against a white sky and the canals steamed like kettles. Finding him was not difficult. The guild of Impossible Things kept no sign above its door—that was the point of it, invisibility as institution—but everyone in the tanners' quarter knew which building swallowed strange goods and stranger questions and gave back coin. She walked there through streets where she counted, without meaning to, four fresh cracks in four different doorways, hairline seams in the thresholds of houses, as though the waking beneath the world were pushing up through everything, through stone and wood and bone alike.

The guildhall door was scarred with something new: a brand, huge and deliberate, burned into the oak in the shape of a circle quartered by a blade. Tithe-mark. She had seen the shape once before, in the margins of her burned hymnal, next to a passage about kings who bought their crowns from gods on credit. She stood looking at it for a long moment, feeling the frost creep up through her boot-soles, and understood that the clock she had been hearing tick beneath the world was ticking here too, on this door, in char.

She knocked. When no answer came, she knocked again, harder, and then tried the handle, and found it unlatched—which for a guild that traded in souls was either arrogance or catastrophe.

Inside smelled of ash and whale oil and old paper. The great room was a shambles of ledgers, some stacked, some scattered, some scorched at the edges. A dead fire slumped in the hearth. And at a table near the window, lit by grey light, sat Cyrus Galloway with a quill in his hand and a page before him, copying—she saw at a glance, from the columns and the careful abbreviations, contracts, soul-deeds, the guild's whole terrible inventory—copying with the frantic steadiness of a man bailing a boat.

He looked up when her shadow crossed the page, and his face did the thing faces did around her: flickered through politeness, confusion, the dawning awkwardness of a man realizing his visitor cannot say good morning. He opened his mouth to ask who she was.

Maren set her satchel on his table, took out chalk and slate—her voice, her substitute voice, eleven years practiced—and wrote:

**You couldn't read the sigil at my shrine.**

His eyes went to the slate, then to her face, then to her throat, and she watched him assemble it: the mute shrine-keeper, the cracked altar, the connection arriving behind his eyes like weather. "You're the one who keeps the Unnamed," he said. His voice was rough, unused-sounding, a voice that belonged to a man who spoke mostly to the dead. "I didn't—I wasn't sure anyone still tended that shrine."

**Someone has to sweep the doorstep,** she wrote. **Even when the god starts breathing.**

Something moved through his expression at that—recognition, and grief, and the particular relief of a man discovering he is not the only one who has heard the world change pitch. "You've heard it." Not a question. "The singing. The prayers going down."

She nodded, and wrote: **They're being fed. Driven. Like beasts to market. All of them, every waking god—they're answering because someone is funneling prayer to them. I've felt it through my floor for three weeks. It comes from below. From the mountain.**

Galloway set down his quill very carefully, the way men set down things when their hands want to shake. "Ashkaval," he said. "The usurper. Everyone says his mandate is divine—everyone says the old gods back him now. I thought the gods had simply... chosen." He laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it. "But chosen is just another word for *paid*. If someone beneath the mountain is feeding the gods prayers like kindling—"

**Then the fires aren't miracles,** Maren wrote. **They're purchases. And the drowned child who came back wrong, and the bread that rises weeping—those are what bad coin buys.**

She turned the slate and wrote again, faster now, the chalk squeaking, because she had carried this alone for three weeks and the weight of it had grown teeth: **There's more. The sigil above my altar—the one you couldn't read—I know it. It's the mark of the Mountain Below. The eldest. The one the oldest hymns say sleeps at the root of the world. Every god-sigil I've found on the newly opened shrines in this city is a lesser mark, a servant-mark, but they all answer to it. Whatever is waking the gods is waking them in the Mountain Below's name. Or in its despite. I can't tell which. That's why I came.**

She hesitated. Then, because alliance required honesty, she added:

**I can read what you can't. Sigils, deeds, seals. God-writing. And I think you have documents that need reading. I think—" **and here her chalk slowed, because the next words were the ones she had turned over for three nights, the ones that had finally dragged her out of her shrine and across the frozen city—**I think you're hunting a specific contract. You copied these pages like a man looking for one entry among thousands. I've kept a god's doorstep for eleven years. I know what it looks like when someone is trying to buy back a soul.**

The silence after that was long. Frost ticked in the window-glass. Somewhere in the walls, a rat moved, and beneath even that—did he feel it too? she watched his face and decided he did—the faint, quickening pulse of the thing below the world, drinking its river of prayers.

"Wren," Galloway said at last, and the name came out of him like a splinter coming out of flesh, relief and agony in the same motion. "Her name was Wren. Is Wren. Seven years ago I sold her soul to the court beneath the mountain to save my sister, and I signed it myself, and I told myself for seven years that the ledger balanced." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "And now the deed turns out to be sealed with a god's sigil I can't read, and the usurper's armies are coming, and the man who kept this guild died last week holding the master registry so I'd have it, and I am sitting here copying contracts by daylight like a clerk because clerk is the only courage I have ever known."

He looked at her then—really looked, past the slate and the silence, at the woman rather than the inconvenience—and said, "Why? Why come to me? You could take what you know to the temples. To Ashkaval's people, even. They'd pay a mute shrine-keeper very well for what you just wrote on that slate."

Maren met his eyes. Then she wiped the slate clean with her sleeve and wrote, slowly, so there could be no mistake:

**My god answered my mother's life with my voice. I have spent eleven years being the cost of a miracle. I know exactly what these gods' answers are worth, and so do you—we're the only two people in this city who do. The temples will use me. Ashkaval will spend me. You—" **and here the chalk paused, and she hated how long the pause was, how it betrayed her, how it showed him the thing she had not planned to show—**you looked at my god's name like a man looking at a locked door he actually wanted opened. Everyone else looks at it like furniture.**

She underlined the last line twice, hard enough to snap a chip off the chalk.

**I'm broken in the exact shape of your problem. Let's be broken together.**

Galloway read it twice. And then he did something she had not expected: he laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, rusty and cracked and human—and pushed the stack of copied contracts aside and cleared a space on the table as if setting a place for her.

"The guildmaster used to say the impossible trade runs on broken instruments," he said. "That a whole thing can't hear what a cracked thing can." He pulled a chair out for her, and gestured, and for the first time in eleven years Maren sat down at a man's table as a colleague rather than a curiosity. "I have a registry with four hundred god-sealed contracts and a head full of dead men's whispers. You have the alphabet. Between us we might manage a sentence."

She smiled—her first in weeks, she realized; her face had gone out of the habit—and reached for the topmost page.

The seal at its foot stopped her hand mid-air.

She knew the sigil. Of course she knew it; she had slept beneath its cousin for eleven years. The Mountain Below's mark, the coiled root-and-stone glyph of the eldest god—but this one was *altered*. Quartered. Divided by a blade.

The same alteration that scarred the guildhall door outside.

Maren looked up and found Galloway already watching her face, and whatever he saw there drained the color from his own. "What," he said quietly. "What is it. What does it say."

She took up the chalk. Her hand, for the first time since her voice had been taken, was not entirely steady.

**It says the god who holds your Wren's soul,** she wrote, **is the same god whose mark Ashkaval is burning on doors. Your contract and his crown are sealed by the same hand. Whatever happens beneath that mountain—your debt and his throne are the same debt.**

She turned the slate toward him and let him read it, and read it again, and watched the last comfortable lie of his seven quiet years die behind his eyes.

Outside, far beneath the frost and the cobbles and the cracked flagstone of a small neglected shrine, something drank deep of the world's prayers and turned over in its sleep—and all across Vellmark, in a hundred doorways, the hairline cracks grew a finger's width wider.
