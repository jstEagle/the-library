## 7. The Guild of Impossible Things

The guildhall had not held a full convocation in eleven years, and the room remembered it the way old rooms remember everything—in the dust that lay thicker where chairs once stood, in the candle-sconces blackened at heights no one had reached since, in the long scar across the floorboards where someone, long ago, had dragged an anvil out into the rain and never brought it back.

Cyrus arrived first. He always arrived first; it was the habit of invisible men to be early, to be already present when others entered so that their arrival seemed less like an event and more like furniture. He set the ledgers on the master table—Ossen's registry wrapped in oilcloth beneath his arm, heavier than any book had a right to be—and then he did what he could not stop himself from doing: he listened.

The dead were quieter here than anywhere else in Vellmark. The guildhall sat on ground that had been paid for, properly paid for, in oaths and bone, and whatever slept beneath it slept content. But not silent. There was always a murmur, like wind through a keyhole, like the sea heard through a wall. Tonight it sounded almost expectant.

He lit the lamps one by one. Seven lamps for seven chairs, though only four would be filled. The fifth chair belonged to Ossen, who would sit at the head or not at all. The sixth had been empty so long that moss grew in its joints. The seventh was Wren's—not that anyone but Cyrus knew it, not that anyone had ever known it. She had never been a member. She had only ever been the reason there was a guild at all, though the founding charter said otherwise, and the charter was written in Ossen's hand, and Ossen's hand did not lie.

*Except once,* whispered something in the whalebone at Cyrus's breast pocket. *Except once, ledger-keeper.*

He pressed his palm flat against the bone until the voice subsided. It had been speaking more often since the shrines cracked open. All the dead spoke more often now. They spoke in unison, in choirs, in harmonies that no choir of the living could match, and every day the singing got a little louder, as if somewhere far below the world a conductor had raised his baton.

The door opened at the third bell.

Sable came in the way smoke comes into a room—not through the door so much as *with* it, so that afterward you couldn't quite swear the door had moved at all. She was small and grey and precise, with the kind of face you forgot while looking at it, which was of course the point; a memory-forger who left strong impressions was a memory-forger out of work. She carried a leather case that clinked softly, and she set it down beside her chair with the tenderness other people reserved for infants.

"Galloway." Her voice was dry paper. "You look terrible."

"I've been reading."

"Reading does that. I've seen men read themselves into graves." She looked at the registry under his arm, and something flickered behind her forgettable face—the first true expression she'd worn in his presence in years. "That's why we're here, isn't it. Not the tithe-mark. The book."

"The book," Cyrus agreed.

Hesk arrived at the fourth bell, because Hesk was constitutionally incapable of arriving early—he said punctuality was a promise made to time, and he'd stopped making promises to anyone, even time, twenty years ago. He filled the doorway the way Sable emptied it: big, loud, unmistakable, a debt-collector built like a door itself, with knuckles like drawer-pulls and a voice that had ended marriages and started tavern brawls in equal measure. He wore his collection on a cord around his neck: small tokens, each one a promise extracted and kept, each one humming faintly with the pressure of a word that could not be taken back.

"Ledger-keeper." He nodded to Cyrus, then to Sable. "Smoke-and-mirrors." Then he looked at the empty chair at the head of the table, and his voice dropped, and the drop cost him visibly. "Is the old man coming?"

"He called the convocation," Cyrus said.

"That's not what I asked."

Cyrus didn't answer, and Hesk's face did the thing it did when he understood a silence better than most men understood speech. He sat down heavily. His tokens clicked against the chair-back.

Maren came last, hooded, hands folded, and took the seat nearest the window where the light would fall across her mouth—she still gestured as she thought, shaping words her throat could no longer make, and Cyrus had begun, without meaning to, to read them. *Where is everyone else?* her hands asked. *You said the guild.*

"This is the guild," Cyrus said aloud, and hated how small it sounded. Four people, a ghost of a fifth, and a hall full of dust. Once there had been forty members, fifty, spread across the free cities like a web too fine to see. Now the web had burned away strand by strand, and what remained sat around a table wondering if it was still a thing at all.

Ossen came down the stairs at the fifth bell.

He came slowly, one hand on the rail, and Cyrus saw immediately what he had been refusing to see for days: the Guildmaster was dying. Not aging—dying, the way a candle dies, all at once and obviously, the flame guttering in a body that had decided to spend what remained of itself in a single direction. His eyes were still sharp. That was the worst part. The fire behind them hadn't dimmed at all; it had simply stopped bothering to warm anything else.

He did not go to the head chair. He stood at the table's end instead, leaning on it, and looked at each of them in turn, and Cyrus felt the room change the way a room changes when a judge enters it.

"You received my letters," Ossen said. "You came anyway. Good. That saves me the trouble of being surprised by your cowardice later." A dry pause. "Ashkaval's herald burned a mark into our door. You've seen it. You know what it means. The usurper wants the registry—all of it, every soul-contract the guild has ever signed—and he wants it before his armies reach the walls of Vellmark. Give it to him, and the guild continues, fat and safe and owned. Refuse, and we burn."

"Then we give it to him," Hesk said flatly. "Old man, with respect—I've collected promises from kings' chamberlains. Ashkaval doesn't lose. Every city that's fought him has a tithe-mark on its gate now, and the marks don't wash off. They *grow.*"

"They grow," Ossen agreed. "Because they're fed. Because every prayer his priests steal goes into the mark like coin into a purse. Which brings us to the real matter." He turned, deliberately, to Cyrus. "Tell them."

Cyrus had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it in the bath, on the stairs, walking the night streets, and every rehearsal had collapsed, because there was no way to say it that wasn't either madness or confession, and he could not decide which was worse.

So he simply opened the registry to the page and laid it on the table.

"A soul-contract," he said. "Signed seven years ago. Sealed with a god's sigil—the Mountain Below, if Maren's reading is right. The soul sold is—" His voice caught, and he let it catch; hiding it now would be worse. "Her name was Wren. She was a singer. And I sold her."

The silence that followed had texture. Sable went very still, the stillness of a woman memorizing the moment for later forgery. Hesk's hand drifted, without his seeming to notice, to the cord of tokens at his neck.

"I know that contract," Hesk said quietly.

Every head turned.

"I collected on it," Hesk said. "Not the sale itself—a side-payment. Silence. Someone paid me, years back, to keep quiet about a soul-sale signed by a guild bone-carver. I never knew whose. Anonymous buyer, anonymous terms. I took the coin because taking coin is what I do, and I told myself the silence was harmless because the deal was already done." He looked at Cyrus with something that was not quite apology and not quite pity. "It was your name on the deed, Galloway. I didn't know until tonight. I swear that on my cord."

"And who paid you?" Ossen asked.

"No name. A courier. Paid in old mint—coin that hasn't circulated since before the war." Hesk's jaw worked. "Which means whoever bought my silence has been planning this a long time. Longer than Ashkaval's armies. Maybe longer than Ashkaval."

*Longer than the usurper,* sang the bone against Cyrus's chest, soft and terrible. *Longer than the living.*

Maren's hands moved, quick and urgent, and Cyrus translated before he fully understood he was doing it: "She says it doesn't matter who paid. She says the contract is sealed with a god's sigil, and god-sealed contracts can't be broken by men. Only unwritten. Only returned."

"Returned," Sable repeated. Her fingers had begun, unconsciously, to tap the lid of her case. "You want to steal her back. Out of the mountain. Out of the vaults of a god, from under the nose of the man who holds the god's leash." She laughed—one short, airless note. "Galloway, I forge memories. Beautiful ones, convincing ones. But no forgery survives contact with a god. Gods remember *everything.*"

"Then we don't forge," Cyrus said. "We return. A contract unwritten is a debt unpaid, and debts are language every power understands—even gods. Especially gods. The Mountain Below has been bound for generations. Bound things want release. We offer the one currency it can't refuse: we void the oldest contract the guild ever signed, and we take Wren's light out with it."

"And the oldest contract?" Ossen asked, very softly. He already knew. Cyrus could see that he already knew, had perhaps always known, and had spent seven years hoping the boy would never find the page.

"It's in the registry," Cyrus said. "I haven't read it yet. I was waiting for—" He gestured at the room, at all of them. "For this."

Ossen closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked older than the hall, older than the city.

"Then hear the terms of convocation," he said. "This is not a command. The guild has never commanded; we trade. So I put it to you as a trade. Cyrus Galloway proposes an impossible thing: the theft of a soul from the court beneath the mountain. Those who join will be marked, hunted, and very likely dead within the year. Those who refuse may walk out that door with my blessing and a share of the archive to buy their silence elsewhere. Speak plainly. No one will think less—"

"I'll think less," Hesk said.

Everyone looked at him. He was staring at the cord around his neck, turning the tokens over one by one, and his great blunt face had gone strange.

"I'll think less of anyone who walks," he said, "because I've been sitting here doing sums, and the sums are bad. Whoever bought my silence bought it in old mint. Old mint means old money, old money means the court, the court means this goes back further than any of us. And I have spent thirty years collecting promises, and I know exactly one thing about them: a promise kept for the wrong person rots in your chest like a swallowed hook." He lifted his head. "I'm in. Not for the girl. For the hook."

Sable said nothing for a long moment. Then she opened her case, and inside, nested in wool, lay dozens of small glass vials, each holding a curl of something that might have been smoke, might have been hair, might have been light. Memories. Forged and genuine, hers to plant, hers to sell.

"If I come," she said, "I want it understood that I am not brave. I am curious. There's a difference, and the difference is that cowards survive." She selected one vial and turned it in the lamplight. "Also—I owe a debt to a dead woman. A singer, years ago, in Corvess. She bought a memory from me once: the memory of being loved. She couldn't afford the real thing, so she bought the forgery, and she died smiling, and I have wondered ever since whether I sold her comfort or a lie." Her forgettable face was, for once, entirely memorable. "If your Wren was a singer, Galloway, then perhaps the accounts balance. I'm in."

They all turned to Maren.

She looked at each of them in turn, and then her hands rose, and moved, and Cyrus found that his translation came slow, because what she said was not simple.

"She says," he began, and stopped, and started again. "She says her god took her voice when it answered her, and she has been waiting seven years to learn why. She says the waking gods are being fed like fires, kindled by someone beneath the mountain, and that the only way to understand a fire is to walk into the house that's burning." Her hands stilled. "She says: *I gave my voice to a god once. I can give the rest.*"

No one spoke. In the silence, the dead beneath the floor began—very faintly—to sing.

And half the guild walked out.

Not Sable, not Hesk, not Maren. The others—the ones Cyrus had forgotten were even present, the shadow-members who had come in with the last bells, the archivists and appraisers who had sat listening from the dark of the gallery above. They came down the stairs quietly, gathering cloaks, avoiding one another's eyes. One of them, an old appraiser named Vetch who had taught Cyrus to weigh a soul by its weight in the hand, paused at the door.

"You were the best of us, boy," Vetch said. "Invisible, incorruptible. Don't ruin it by becoming visible. Visible men die."

Then they were gone, and the door boomed shut behind them, and the convocation was five souls in a hall built for fifty, and the lamps burned lower, and outside the tithe-mark on the guildhall door pulsed once, softly, like a heart beginning to count.

Ossen lowered himself at last into the head chair.

"Five," he said. "Well. The guild has been five before. Five founded it, in a cellar in Corvess, with a stolen god-bone and a debt no one believed could be paid." He looked around the table, and the fire in his eyes leaned forward. "Tomorrow we open the registry to its oldest page. Tonight—" and here the old man smiled, and it was the smile of a man lighting a fuse, "—tonight, we make our preparations. Sable, you'll need three days and every vial you own. Hesk, start collecting. Maren—" he bowed to her, formally, terribly, "—prepare yourself. What you may have to give, no one has given in a thousand years."

"And me?" Cyrus asked.

Ossen looked at him for a long moment, and the singing beneath the floor rose and fell like breathing.

"You," he said, "will carve. There's a block of god-bone in the cellar, boy, older than the guild, older than the city. I've kept it locked for forty years because I was afraid of what it remembers. Tomorrow you're going to need to hear it. And Cyrus—" his voice dropped, gentle as a closing ledger, "—whatever it tells you about the oldest contract, however it names the hand that wrote it... try to sleep tonight anyway. Because after tomorrow, none of us will."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the tithe-mark on the door began, very softly, to hum.
