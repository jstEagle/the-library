## 9. Road Beneath the Pines

The pines began three miles north of Vellmark, and Cyrus counted every one of them as they passed.

It was a habit of ledgers, this counting. Seven years behind a desk had taught him that anything uncounted was already lost. So he counted trees—two hundred and eleven by the time the light failed—and he counted their company: four souls, one mule named Debt (Hesk's joke, and Hesk did not make many), and the master registry wrapped in oilcloth against Cyrus's chest like a second heart. He counted the things he could not count, too, because grief is an auditor of a different kind. One guildhall, burned. One guildmaster, dead. One pair of ears, half-ruined, ringing with the after-sound of gods.

Ossen's voice had been the loudest thing in the burning. *You were never invisible.* The words had gone through Cyrus like a chisel through green wood, splitting him along a grain he hadn't known he carried. And then the roof beam came down, and the old man's voice stopped being a voice at all and became only a weight in Cyrus's pocket—a curl of whalebone he'd torn from the lintel of Ossen's own desk, still warm when he grabbed it, or perhaps warm only in memory.

They made camp in a hollow where the pines grew thick enough to swallow firelight. Maren built the fire without a sound, as she did everything without sound; her god had taken her voice whole, and what remained of her speech lived in her hands, quick and eloquent as birds. She signed to Sable across the flames—*how far*—and Sable, who forged memories the way other men forged coins, answered aloud for both of them, because Sable had learned Maren's hands during three nights on the road and wore the knowledge lightly, the way she wore everything.

"Two weeks if the road holds," Sable said. "Three if the tithe-marked hold us."

"They'll hold us." Hesk crouched by the fire, turning his promise-coins over in his fingers. He collected promises the way moneylenders collected interest—each one sealed in a small lead token, each one redeemable. He had forty-one tokens in his purse and Cyrus knew, now, that one of them bore his own name. "Tithe-marked don't tire. They're not men anymore, exactly. They're debts wearing boots."

"Then we don't stop," Cyrus said.

"We always stop," Hesk said gently. "Mules stop. Bladders stop. Grief stops you whether you permit it or not."

Cyrus looked into the fire and said nothing, because the dead were saying plenty.

They had been quiet since Vellmark—the dead, I mean, the chorus he'd heard all his life through ivory and bone and the fillings of strangers' teeth. Since the shrines cracked open and the old gods began answering, the dead had sung in unison, a vast chord beneath the world, and Cyrus had learned to live inside it the way a man lives inside a bell. But the fire at the guildhall had changed the song. Now, whenever he held bone, the dead spoke singly, intimately, one voice per relic, as though the burning had taught them manners. Or taught them urgency.

He took the curl of whalebone from his pocket.

It was poor stock—salt-brittle, stained amber at the curve where some harpooner's rope had worn it—but it was Ossen's, and that made it scripture. Around the fire, his companions pretended not to watch. Sable was bad at pretending; her eyes tracked his hands the way a cat tracks a moth. Hesk was excellent at it, which was its own kind of kindness.

Cyrus drew his carving knife and set the blade against the bone.

For twenty years he had heard the dead through his work, but always at a remove, always through water, always as though the voices came from the far side of a wall he himself had built. He'd told himself it was the nature of the gift. Distance was safety. A man who hears clearly becomes a door, and doors get walked through. He had spent his whole life being a wall with a crack in it, and he had called the crack a talent.

Now he set the knife and carved the first letter of Ossen's last words, and the wall came down entire.

*You were never invisible.*

The voice arrived not as sound but as presence—warm, dry, smelling of ink and pipe-smoke and the particular dust of old ledgers. It was so clear that Cyrus's hand shook and the knife skidded, gouging a flaw across the W. He didn't care. He carved on, weeping without noise, because he had learned weeping-without-noise from Maren and because any louder grief would bring the tithe-marked down on them like wolves on a bleating thing.

*You were never invisible. You thought the ledgers were your hiding place. Fool boy. The ledgers were your training. Every soul you recorded, you recorded because someone had to see them. That was never bookkeeping. That was witness.*

"I know," Cyrus whispered. "I know."

*You don't. You think you're going to the mountain to steal back a girl. You're going to the mountain to settle accounts. There's a difference, and the difference will keep you alive.*

The knife moved. Cyrus let it move, guiding it less than following it, and the words rose out of the whalebone letter by letter, Ossen's dying testament taking shape in the old guildmaster's plain, unforgiving hand—not the words as Cyrus remembered hearing them but the words as Ossen had meant them, the full text, the parts that had been drowned under the roar of the fire:

*I leave the registry to my clerk, who was never my clerk. I leave the guild to whoever is brave enough to be owed something by a god. I leave my regrets to no one, because regrets are just entries left unclosed, and I closed mine years ago—all but one. Find the oldest contract. It isn't Ashkaval's deed you should fear. It's ours.*

The knife stopped.

Cyrus sat very still. Around him the fire popped; Maren had gone motionless, watching his face; Hesk's coins had stopped turning.

"It's ours," Cyrus repeated aloud. His voice sounded strange to his own half-deaf ears, flattened, as though spoken underwater. "The oldest contract. Ossen says it's *ours*. The guild's."

Sable leaned forward. "That can't be right. The usurper holds the deed to the Mountain Below. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what heralds say." Hesk's voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when he was doing arithmetic with souls. "But I've collected promises for thirty years, and I'll tell you what I've learned: nobody sells a god into bondage. You can't. What you *can* do is buy a god's freedom and then lose the receipt." He looked up, and firelight moved in his eyes like water. "Someone in the guild sold something once. Something enormous. And everyone since has been paying the interest."

Maren's hands moved, quick and grave. Sable translated, her voice hollower than before: "She says the waking gods are singing the same song. She's heard it in the cracked shrines. She says they aren't waking angry. They're waking *hungry*. Like creditors."

Cyrus wrapped the registry tighter against his chest. He thought of the entry in his own hand, seven years old, that he did not remember writing. *Wren. One soul. Sold to the court beneath the mountain.* He thought of the unknown buyer Hesk had confessed to—the silence purchased, the trail leading inward, toward the guild itself. He thought of Ossen telling him to burn the page, and Ossen dying to save the same page, and how both things could be true if a man loved a sin and hated it in the same breath.

"He knew," Cyrus said. "Ossen knew what the oldest contract was. He died holding the proof because he couldn't fix it alive." He looked around the fire, at the forger and the collector and the mute saint, these outcasts who had followed a ledger-keeper into the dark because there was nowhere else to go. "So we'll fix it dead."

Nobody cheered. That wasn't the guild's way. But Hesk put another log on the fire, and Maren signed something short and fierce that Sable refused to translate, and the mule Debt sighed like an old man asked to carry more than mules should.

* * *

They traveled north by day and hid by dusk, and on the fourth day the tithe-marked found them.

Cyrus heard them first—or rather, he heard the absence of them. The dead went silent along the whole stretch of forest ahead, the way birds go silent before a hawk. He raised a hand and the party froze among the pines, breath smoking in the cold.

They came up the game trail in single file: six figures in gray, each with the tithe-mark burned into their foreheads—a small circle bisected by a line, the sigil of Ashkaval's exchequer, the brand that meant *this body has been entered into the ledger as payment.* Their eyes were the worst part. Not empty—full. Full of arithmetic. They looked at the world the way auditors look at discrepancies.

Hesk stepped forward before anyone could stop him, palms open, showing his purse of promise-tokens.

"Gentlemen," he said pleasantly. "You're hunting four fugitives and a mule. I'd like to discuss a settlement."

The lead hunter's head tilted. When it spoke, its voice was many voices pressed flat, like flowers in a book. *"The registry. The clerk. The mute. The forger. The collector may pass. His accounts are with another office."*

"You see my problem," Hesk said. "I'm attached to all of them."

*"Attachment is not recognized."*

"No," Hesk agreed. "It never is. Which is why I deal in promises instead." He drew out a single lead token and held it up between two fingers, and even from thirty feet away Cyrus felt it—the pressure of it, like a change in weather. "This one is fresh. Collected at a burning guildhall, from a dying man. Guildmaster Ossen, may his ledger balance. His final promise. Would you like to know what he promised?"

The hunters did not answer, but they did not advance, and in the grammar of debts that was a question.

"He promised," Hesk said, "that the registry would reach the mountain. And here is the beautiful part, friends: a promise made by a dying man cannot be transferred, assigned, or extinguished except by fulfillment. Your master's own law. If you take the registry now, you break a binding oath sworn under a god's waking sky—and the gods are awake, gentlemen, and they are *collecting*."

Silence in the pines. Snow began to fall through the branches, fine and dry, ticking softly on the needles.

The lead hunter stared at Hesk for a long moment with its full, terrible eyes. Then it reached up and touched the brand on its forehead, almost tenderly, the way a man touches a wound he has decided to keep.

*"We will report the obstruction,"* it said. *"Others will come. More than others."* And it turned, and the six of them filed away down the trail, gray shapes dissolving into gray trees, and the dead breathed back in around Cyrus like a tide returning.

Sable sat down abruptly on a root. "How much of that was true?"

"The promise is real. Ossen swore it while I held his hand." Hesk returned the token to his purse, and his hand, Cyrus noticed, was not entirely steady. "The rest was theater. Good theater buys hours, not days. We have hours."

Maren touched Cyrus's arm and signed, rapid and urgent. Sable watched her hands and translated slowly, reluctantly: "She says... she says you should carve the rest of the bone tonight. All of it. She says the dead don't speak twice."

* * *

That night they camped in the ruin of a charcoal-burner's hut, a low stone shell with a hearth still black from some abandoned fire, and Cyrus took out the whalebone and turned it in the light and saw that Ossen was right, Ossen was always right: there was more. The bone was long, and he had carved only the beginning. The rest lay waiting in the grain like a letter half-read.

He worked until dawn while the others slept, and the voice came clear as cold water, and he wrote nothing down because the bone was writing itself:

*You'll want to know why I never told you about Wren. Here is the truth a guildmaster can afford and a man cannot: I suspected the sale before you made it. I saw the shape of the debt coming for you and I did nothing, because stopping it meant revealing the oldest contract, and revealing the oldest contract would have burned the guild a generation early. I chose the guild over you. Write that on my stone, boy. I chose the ledger over the living.*

*But hear the rest. The buyer who bought your silence—Hesk will have told you by now, or he's a worse collector than I credit—that buyer was not Ashkaval. Ashkaval wasn't crowned yet. The buyer was the guild. A faction of it. Men who believed the Mountain Below must stay bound at any cost, because if it wakes, every prayer ever answered gets called due, and the free cities drown in their own wishes. They sold Wren's soul to the court because a soul freely given by love anchors better than any chain. They used you, Cyrus. They used your love as mortar.*

*And they are still in the guild. What remains of it. Wherever you run, whatever you rebuild—they will be there first, and they will smile, and they will call themselves protectors.*

*So: you were never invisible. Now stop trying to be. Walk into the mountain in plain sight, ledger open, and make them look at what they've done. Witness is the only weapon our trade ever truly held. Everything else was sleight of hand.*

*Last entry. Balance the books, Cyrus. All of them. Even yours.*

The knife slipped from his fingers as the sun came up through the pines, striking the bone gold, and Cyrus sat in the ruined hut with the finished carving in his hands and understood, at last, the shape of the road ahead. Not a rescue. A reckoning. Wren at the end of it, yes—Wren always—but behind her, older and darker, the guild's own name written in the ledger of the world's great debt.

Hesk appeared at the doorway, grey-faced, sleepless. In his hand he held one of his lead tokens, and Cyrus had never seen him look at one of his own coins with fear before.

"Cyrus," Hesk said quietly. "I need to show you something. This token—it's not one of mine. It was in my purse this morning. Someone put it there while we slept." He turned it over. Stamped into the lead, in a hand Cyrus knew better than his own heartbeat—in the hand from the ledger entry dated tomorrow—was a single word:

*SOON.*

"And there's more," Hesk said. "It's not addressed to me."
