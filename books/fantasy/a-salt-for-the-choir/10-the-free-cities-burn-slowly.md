## 10. The Free Cities Burn Slowly

Sable had spent forty years learning how to disappear, and she knew every road out of a life the way other people knew the backs of their hands. So it was a particular kind of humiliation to find herself walking in a line.

The refugee column stretched from the pine ridge down to the river flats and back up again on the far side, a grey rope of humanity six miles long, knotted with carts and cattle and the occasional stubborn donkey that had decided, somewhere around noon, that it would go no farther. Sable counted exits out of habit: the drainage culvert under the old toll road, the game trail behind the shrine of some forgotten orchard god, the ford where the guards weren't looking. She could have taken any one of them by nightfall. She could have taken Cyrus with her, if she'd wanted to—slipped him a new face, a new name, a past stitched so seamlessly he'd believe it himself within a week. It was what she was for. The guild called her a forger of memories, but that was too clean a phrase for what she actually did. She unmade people and remade them, gently, like turning a shirt inside out so the stains didn't show.

She looked at Cyrus Galloway trudging ahead of her in the churned mud, shoulders hunched against a rain that hadn't quite committed to falling, and thought: *he doesn't even know what I could give him.*

That was the trouble with bone-carvers. They lived so deep inside other people's dead that they forgot they had a self worth saving.

The column moved at the speed of its slowest grief. There were families from Vellmark's outer wards who had left with nothing but what they could carry and their guild-debts, which they carried anyway, because debts were heavier than furniture and harder to abandon. There were farmers from the river valleys whose fields now fed Ashkaval's armies instead of their own children. There was a whole village of salt-boilers from the coast who had walked eleven days on rumor alone—the rumor being that the free cities still held, that Vellmark's walls still stood, that anywhere was better than the road behind them.

The road behind them burned slowly. That was the thing nobody said aloud, though everyone thought it: the free cities did not fall the way cities fell in songs, all at once, in fire and glory. They burned the way wet wood burns. Slowly. From the edges inward. First the tithe-marks appeared on doors, and then the tithe-collectors appeared behind the marks, and then the men who had paid their tithes disappeared into the usurper's columns wearing uniforms they hadn't chosen. A city could take a year to die and call most of that year peace.

Sable had watched it happen twice before, in her previous lives. She remembered both of them perfectly, because remembering was her trade and forgetting was her sin.

"Cyrus." Maren's voice came from beside him—her written voice, since her spoken one had gone wherever voices go when gods take them; she signed as she walked, quick gestures half-swallowed by the press of bodies, and Cyrus read her hands without seeming to look at them, the way he read everything, sideways and without being seen reading. "There's a woman back three carts. She's been watching you since the ridge."

"People watch me," Cyrus said. "I have an honest face."

"You have an invisible face," Maren signed. "That's different. She's not watching you. She's working up to something."

Hesk, limping along on the cart rail with his purse of lead tokens clinking like a funeral bell, snorted. "Everyone works up to something around a bone-carver. Half this column probably wants their dead asked after. The other half wants them told to stay dead." He said it lightly, but Sable saw his hand close over the purse, over the token stamped SOON that he still hadn't explained, and she filed that away with everything else she'd been filing away since the guildhall burned. Hesk owed someone a silence. She'd collected enough promises in her time to know the shape of a man carrying one.

Cyrus didn't answer. He was looking at the sky, or pretending to, and Sable recognized the pretense for what it was. He was listening. Even half-deafened by whatever the gods' answering had done to him at the shrines, he was listening—and lately, when he listened, his face went through small terrible changes, like weather moving across a plain.

* * *

They made camp that night in a harvested barley field, and the field filled with fires the way a skull fills with dark thoughts—one here, one there, then everywhere, each one ringed by people who had nothing left to burn except the wood they'd carried and, eventually, the wood they hadn't.

Sable sat apart, as she always did, sharpening a knife she didn't need sharpened, and watched the column become a city of the dispossessed. A child two fires over was teaching another child a skipping rhyme about the Mountain Below, the words garbled through generations of mouths until the eldest god had become a bogeyman who ate naughty tithe-payers. An old man was showing anyone who'd stand still a scrap of embroidery—a wedding cloth, Sable guessed, from the pattern—that he'd pulled from his burning house and nothing else. Somewhere a baby cried with the tireless outrage of the very young, and no one told it to hush, because everyone understood that crying was the only honest work left.

It was near full dark when the woman found Cyrus.

Sable saw it happen the way she saw everything: from the edge, at an angle, with time to intervene and no intention of doing so. The woman was maybe fifty, built like a woman who had carried water and children and grief in equal measure for decades, and she crossed the firelight toward Cyrus with the deliberate tread of someone who had rehearsed. Maren saw her coming and touched Cyrus's arm. Cyrus turned. And the woman stopped three feet away and simply stood there, all her rehearsal gone, holding her cap in her hands like a bird she was afraid to crush.

"You're the bone-man," she said. "The one who hears them."

"I'm a carver," Cyrus said. "Sometimes the ivory remembers things."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't be modest with me. I've got no use for a modest man tonight." She swallowed. "My name is Odile Brant. My son was Tam. He was a fisher off the Vellmark shallows, and when the conscription wagons came he ran, because he was seventeen and sensible, and they caught him at the salt-works, and—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He wrote me one letter. From the training camp at Harrow Ford. Then the camp marched east, and then there was a battle at a place I can't pronounce, and then there was a list, and his name was on it, and that's all I know. That's everything I know about how my son died. A name on a list."

Around them, the nearest fires had gone quiet. Not deliberately—no one had shushed her—but the way a room goes quiet when grief walks in wearing its good coat. People turned their faces toward their own flames and pretended not to listen, and listened with everything they had.

"I'm sorry," Cyrus said, and Sable heard how inadequate it was, and so did he, and so did she.

"Sorry's a coin," Odile Brant said. "I'm not here to beg. I'm here to pay." She fumbled at her neck and pulled free a cord, and on the cord hung a single small object that caught the firelight: a whale-tooth, worn smooth, carved with waves. "His father cut this tooth when Tam was born. It's the only piece of him I have that ever touched him while he breathed. I've been told—I've been told by people who know—that if a carver holds a piece like this, the dead can speak through it. That you can carry a question down to them and carry the answer back up."

"That's not—" Cyrus began.

"I'm not asking if he suffered," she said, louder now, and her voice carried down the whole dark field, fire to fire to fire. "I've imagined every way a boy can die for a year and a half; suffering is a country I have citizenship in. I'm asking—" and here her voice finally broke, all the way through, into the raw place beneath, "—I'm asking if he forgave me. For letting them take him. For being poor. For not running faster, or fighting harder, or dying in his place, which is what a mother should do, isn't it? Isn't that what a mother is *for?*" She thrust the tooth out at arm's length, hand shaking. "Ask him. Whatever it costs, I'll pay it. Ask my son if he forgives me, and tell him his mother is sorry, and tell him—tell him the dog still sleeps on his bed, God help me, tell him that, he'll laugh—"

Cyrus took the tooth.

Sable watched his hands do it—watched them move before his face agreed to anything, the way a drowning man's hands move before his mind consents—and she felt something twist in her chest that she resented deeply, because she had spent forty years removing exactly those twists from other people and had assumed hers were long since excised.

Cyrus held the tooth in both palms. Closed his eyes. And Sable, who had sat across from him through seven years of ledgers and lies and quiet guildhall suppers, saw for the first time what he actually looked like when he worked. He looked like a man standing at the edge of a well in winter. He looked cold. His lips moved once—*Tam*—and then stopped moving altogether, and the fire cracked, and the baby cried, and the field waited.

Then Cyrus Galloway began to weep.

Not gracefully. Not the way heroes weep in songs, one bright tear down a noble cheek. He wept the way the column had traveled all day: slowly, heavily, from somewhere deep in the load-bearing part of him. His shoulders shook. Tears cut lines through the road-dust on his face. And Odile Brant stood frozen with her hands over her mouth, because she could see—as everyone within ten fires could see—that the answer had already arrived, and that it was arriving through him, and that whatever a seventeen-year-old dead fisher-boy had to say to his mother, it was passing through this stranger's body like a current through wire, and the current was love, and the current was forgiveness, and it was burning him on the way through.

"He says—" Cyrus's voice came out wrecked, half-swallowed. "He says it wasn't your fault. He says—he says you carried him before he was born and after, and both times uphill, and he never thanked you for either." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh, might have been the boy laughing through him. "He says tell the dog—tell the dog he's doing his job." And then, softer, in a register that belonged to no one living: "*Mam. Go home. Plant the spring beds. I'll be in the beans.*"

Odile Brant made a noise Sable had never heard a human make and hoped never to hear again, and folded down onto her knees in the mud, and pressed the whale-tooth to her forehead, and wept with her whole ruined body while the fires crackled and the column pretended, kindly, badly, not to watch.

Cyrus stayed kneeling across from her until her crying slowed. Then he set the tooth gently in her hands, closed her fingers over it, and stood, and walked away from the firelight into the dark beyond the carts, because he was Cyrus, and he would rather bleed alone than be thanked.

Sable followed him. Of course she followed him. Discretion was her profession, but so, in the end, was rescue.

* * *

She found him sitting on the overturned wheel of a broken cart, facing away from the camp, his face in his hands. She sat down beside him without asking. Forty years of practice had taught her that the first minute after a haunting belongs to the silence, and the second minute belongs to whoever loves you enough to break it.

"You could stop," she said, when the minute was up.

He lowered his hands. His eyes were red and swollen and very tired. "Stop what?"

"This." She gestured back at the fires, at the whole grey rope of the column, at the country burning slowly behind them. "All of it. The mountain. The registry. Ossen's ledger weighing on your back like a second spine. You're not a soldier, Cyrus. You're not a hero. You're a bookkeeper who hears ghosts, and the war has no use for you except as fuel, and neither does the god, and neither does Wren, honestly, wherever she is—she loved you, and loving you means she wouldn't want this." She let that sit, then leaned closer, and lowered her voice to the register she used for the delicate work, the intimate work, the work of taking a person apart and putting them back together someone new. "I can make you no one. You understand that? Not hidden—*no one*. A name with roots. A childhood with scars you'll swear are real. I've done it nine times, to myself, and I'm excellent company in every one of them. We walk north tomorrow, you and I, out of this story entirely. By midsummer you're a cooper in a town that's never heard of the Guild of Impossible Things, and the dead are just dead, and no mother ever finds you in a barley field and breaks your heart with a fish-tooth. You'd sleep. You'd eat. You'd forget her face—" she caught herself, because everyone in the party knew about the cartographer's door, about the memory of Wren's face that Cyrus had already paid, "—what remains of her. And the mountain can keep its lanterns, and Ashkaval can keep his stolen crown, and none of it will ever again cost you a single tear."

The silence after that was long. Out in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Maren gave her voice to a god," Cyrus said at last, quietly. "Hesk is carrying a token with my handwriting on it that neither of us understands yet. Ossen died with a registry in his arms and my name in his mouth. And Wren—" his voice caught on the name, steadied. "Wren has been hanging in the dark beneath a mountain for seven years because of something I did, or something I am, or something the oldest contract says I owe. And you're offering me a workbench and a nap."

"I'm offering you a life," Sable said. "It's the only thing I know how to forge that always holds."

"And it's generous," Cyrus said. "And it's kind. And if I were the man I was a month ago—the man who kept the ledgers and stayed invisible and told himself invisibility was humility instead of cowardice—I'd take it." He turned and looked at her, and in the distant firelight his eyes were steady, and Sable saw with real regret that the boy who had once sold his first love's soul to save his sister and the man sitting on the broken wheel were, at last, the same person. "But I heard that woman's son tonight, Sable. And he wasn't angry. He wasn't even sad. He was *planting beans*, in whatever garden the dead keep, and he was worried about his dog. And there are thousands of Tams in the columns behind us and the vaults below us, and every one of them is somebody's bean-planter, somebody's letter-writer, somebody's name on a list. If I run, I don't stop hearing them. I'd just hear them from farther away, through better walls, and call the muffled version peace." He shook his head slowly. "You can't forge me an identity strong enough to hold a man who's already decided who he is. I'm the guild's ledger-keeper. The guild is ash. So now I'm the ledger."

Sable looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, rough, defeated sound—and took out her knife and began cleaning her nails with it, because her hands needed something cynical to do.

"For the record," she said, "in four of my nine lives, I was happy."

"In how many were you brave?"

She considered. "One. And it didn't stick."

"No," Cyrus said, rising, brushing the mud from his coat, turning back toward the fires and the column and the mountain waiting at the end of all roads. "It never does."

They had almost reached the firelight when Hesk came stumbling between the carts to meet them, and Sable saw at once that something had broken loose in him—something long dammed, now flooding. He grabbed Cyrus's sleeve with a hand that shook, and glanced wildly at the fires, at the sleeping column, at Sable, and dragged them both into the shadow of the grain wagon.

"The token," he whispered. "The one stamped SOON. I lied to you in the guildhall, Cyrus—or half-lied, which is worse, and you know it's worse, you've audited me for seven years." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "I didn't just find it this morning. I know who sent it. Because I remember the promise I collected—the one I told you about, the silence about Wren's sale. And I remember *who paid me*. And Cyrus—" his eyes were white-rimmed in the dark, "—the buyer signed the receipt. And the signature is the same hand that's on the entry dated tomorrow. It's someone who's been inside the guild longer than either of us has been alive."

Behind them, unnoticed, the last fire in Odile Brant's ring burned low, and the column slept, and far to the east the free cities went on burning slowly, patiently, like a wick trimmed for a long night.
