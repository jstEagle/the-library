## 11. Hesk's Price

Hesk had collected promises for forty years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: everyone lied about what they'd given away.

They came to him with their bargains half-remembered, softened by time into gifts freely offered, favors asked and granted between friends. They forgot the desperation, the trembling hand on the contract line, the moment when they'd traded something they could not afford for something they could not live without. It was Hesk's trade to remember for them. He kept each promise he collected folded inside his chest like letters in a coat pocket—warm, close, and heavy—and when a debt came due, he unfolded them and read them aloud, word for word, in the debtor's own voice, so there could be no argument.

He was good at his work. He had never once lost a promise.

Which is why, sitting in the dark of a pine shelter with rain hammering the shingles and Cyrus Galloway sleeping fitfully ten feet away, Hesk finally admitted to himself that he was going to have to break the only rule of his long career.

He was going to have to give a promise back.

* * *

The trouble had started three nights before, at the crossroads shrine where the hunters had found them—or rather, it had started long before that, decades before, but it had *surfaced* three nights before, the way drowned things surface: slowly at first, then all at once.

They had buried Hesk's blood in the shrine's scorched earth—no, that was wrong, that was the ambush, that hadn't happened yet, would happen soon, he could feel it coming the way an old debt-collector felt a payment date approaching. Before the ambush. Before. Three nights ago, then, or four—the days blurred on the road—they had made camp in the ruins of a waystation inn, its roof half-collapsed, its hearth still bearing the black handprint of some traveler's desperate fire. Maren had signed her evening hymn to no god in particular, her hands moving in the firelight with a grace that hurt to watch, and Sable had been mending the false faces she carried in her pack, and Cyrus—

Cyrus had taken out the bone.

Hesk had seen him do it a hundred times since Ossen's death. The bone-carver sat apart from the rest of them, always, turning a curl of whalebone in his fingers, carving with a knife small as a fingernail, listening. That was the thing about Cyrus that people who didn't know him never understood: the man did nothing but listen. His whole life was an ear pressed to a door the rest of the world couldn't find. He heard the dead through ivory and bone, through teeth and antler, through anything that had once been part of something living. He heard them the way other men heard rain.

That night, the dead man in the bone had been a soldier. Hesk hadn't meant to listen—he made a practice of not listening to Cyrus's dead; a collector learned early which doors were not his—but the soldier's words had carried anyway, because Cyrus had gone pale as tallow reading them, and because he'd said, aloud, in a voice stripped down to the studs:

*"The tithe-marks aren't hunting the registry. They're hunting the trail of a single sale."*

Hesk had known, in that instant, exactly which sale.

And worse—far worse—he had known something else, something he had carried sealed and silent for seven years, paid for in full, non-negotiable, ironclad: *a promise of silence concerning the soul-deed of Wren Halloran, collected from Hesk Thorne, promise-collector of the Guild of Impossible Things, by a buyer whose face he had never been permitted to see.*

He had told himself, on the road, over and over, that the promise didn't matter. That whoever had bought his silence all those years ago had bought it to protect Wren, or Cyrus, or the guild itself—that silence was a kindness in this case, a mercy, a wall around a grave. He had told himself that a collector who broke faith was no collector at all, that his whole self was built on the keeping of promises, and if he let one crumble, everything he was would come down with it.

But he had watched Cyrus carve the soldier's last words into that curl of bone with hands that shook, and he had watched Maren watch him with her voiceless devotion, and he had watched Sable pretend not to care about any of them while stitching new faces by firelight, and he had thought: *these people are going to die walking blindfolded toward a thing I can see.*

A promise-collector's promises were his livelihood. But they had never been supposed to be his conscience.

* * *

Rain again the next day. The road beneath the pines had turned to a brown river, and they walked it hoods up, single file, Sable in front reading the country like a forged signature, looking for the places where the world's writing didn't match its hand. Hesk brought up the rear, as he always did, because a collector walked last—it was tradition, or superstition, or both. The one who held the debts walked behind them all, so nothing owed could slip past uncollected.

He spent the day's march taking inventory of his chest-pocket of promises, the way a miser counts coin. There were hundreds in there now. A miller's vow to never again water his flour. A widow's oath to name her daughter after the sea. A king's—well. Not a king. Not anymore. Some promises went stale when their makers died; those he carried to the grave-markers and read aloud, releasing them like birds. Others outlived their owners, passing down bloodlines like curses, and those he kept until someone with better standing than he could judge them void.

And there, folded deepest, wrapped in seven years of careful not-looking: *the Wren promise.*

He unwrapped it now, walking, and examined it properly for the first time since he'd accepted it.

Here was what he remembered of the night he'd collected it—and memory, for a man of his trade, was not a soft thing but a hard one, chiseled and dated. He remembered the guildhall at night, the great ledger-hall with its ten thousand drawers, each drawer holding a deed or a debt or a death. He remembered being summoned—not asked, *summoned*—by a note slipped under his door in a hand he did not recognize, though the wax seal had been guild-wax, senior grade, the kind only the inner circle used.

He remembered the buyer waiting among the shelves, robed and hooded, and here his memory went strange, the way memories did around certain kinds of magic. He could recall the figure's presence, its height, the smell of old paper and cold stone that clung to it. He could recall its voice—low, level, genderless, the voice of someone who had practiced being no one. But the face above the robe's edge simply wasn't there. Not hidden. Not shadowed. *Absent*, like a word rubbed off a page, and when he tried now, walking, to force his mind back to that gap, his thoughts slid off it the way rain slid off oiled leather.

He remembered the terms, though. Terms were his scripture; he never lost terms.

*A promise of silence, absolute and perpetual, upon the subject of the sale of the soul of Wren Halloran, sold by Cyrus Galloway, bone-carver, to the court beneath the mountain. You will neither speak nor write nor sign nor gesture this knowledge. You will not lead another to discover it by your conduct. In exchange: your standing debt to this office, forgiven entire.*

His standing debt. Hesk had owed the guild's inner circle a considerable sum in those days—nothing sordid, only the ordinary ruin of a man who had once, in his youth, promised more than he possessed. Seven years ago, forgiveness of that debt had seemed a fair price for silence about a sale that was, after all, already done, already grieved over, already carved into the past where no one could reach it.

Now he understood what he had not understood then: that the buyer had not purchased his silence about the past.

The buyer had purchased his silence about the *future*—about the day the sale would matter, the day a bone-carver would read a strange entry in a ledger and begin to dig. Someone had known that day was coming. Someone had wanted Cyrus digging blind.

And someone had known enough to come to Hesk specifically. Not Ossen, who could be refused. Not the registry keepers. Hesk—the one man whose profession made him the perfect vault for a secret, the one man bound by craft to hold what he was given forever, the one man whose silence could be bought not with coin but with absolution.

Whoever it was had known the guild the way a surgeon knows a body. Whoever it was had been *inside*.

Hesk stopped walking.

Ahead, the others straggled on through the rain, unaware. Behind him the pines stood in ranks, dripping, patient, indifferent. He stood in the middle of the road like a man who has just realized the floor of his own house is hollow.

Because there was one more thing, and he had been circling it for three days without letting himself land on it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The buyer's absence-of-face. He had assumed magic—a veil-charm, an anonymity working, the sort of thing the memory-forger's craft could do and undo. But Sable, when he had cautiously described the sensation to her two nights ago—framing it as a hypothetical, a collector's curiosity, nothing more—had frowned and said that veils left seams. A worked memory had edges, little scars of unreality, and she could usually find them. What he described—thoughts sliding off, a smooth absence with no seam at all—wasn't a veil.

"It sounds," she'd said, threading her needle without looking up, "like the memory was never fully formed. Like whatever you saw, you weren't permitted to perceive it in the first place. That's not forgery, Hesk. That's authority."

Authority. The kind that lived in seals and sigils. The kind that had witnessed Wren's deed and made it nearly unbreakable. The kind that belonged to gods—and to whatever served them.

Someone inside the guild had gone to the Mountain Below, or been sent by it, or worn its power like a borrowed coat. And they had arranged, seven years ago, for a boy's grief to be converted into collateral, for a promise-collector's mouth to be sewn shut with his own craft, and for the whole arrangement to lie dormant until the precise hour it was needed.

Needed for what? That was the question that gnawed him. A sale that anchored a usurper's claim. A soul hung in a vault like a lantern. None of it accidental. All of it—every step, every year—*planned*.

And Cyrus Galloway, who trusted Hesk, who had wept over a stranger's dead son in a refugee column, who slept at night with a piece of bone under his pillow the way children sleep with knives—Cyrus had been the instrument of it all along. Not the author. The pen.

*You were the pen too,* Hesk reminded himself grimly, resuming his walk. *Don't flatter yourself. We're all pens. The question is who holds us.*

* * *

He waited three more days, because a collector knew the value of timing, and because cowardice dressed in patience's clothing is still cowardice, and he was honest enough with himself to know which it was.

The chance came at dusk, in the lee of a granite outcrop where they dared a small fire—Sable's craft could kill its smoke, and the hunters, she judged, were a valley behind. Maren cooked barley and dried fish without complaint, as she did everything without complaint, her silence a reproach and a comfort both. Cyrus sat sharpening his carving knives, the wet stone whispering. And Hesk, watching the fire, felt the folded promise in his chest grow hot as a coal.

"Cyrus," he said.

The whispering stopped.

"I have something to return to you. Or—to give back, I should say. Promises aren't mine to return. They're only mine to break." He swallowed. "And I am about to break one."

Maren's hands stilled over the pot. Sable looked up sharply, needle poised, her mismatched eyes—for she wore a borrowed face tonight, and its eyes did not match—fixed on him with the flat attention of a woman who dealt in secrets professionally and smelled one dying in the air.

Cyrus set down his knife. Very carefully. The way a man sets down a blade rather than throw it.

"Say it," he said.

Hesk had rehearsed this a hundred times in three days, and every rehearsal fell apart now, because there was no clean way to say it, no angle that spared them both. So he did what his trade demanded: he unfolded the promise and read it aloud, word for word, in the buyer's voice, which was also his own, which was how a collection was witnessed.

"A promise of silence, absolute and perpetual, upon the subject of the sale of the soul of Wren Halloran—"

He got no further. Cyrus had risen without seeming to move, the way weather rises, and his face in the firelight was terrible—not angry, worse than angry, *understanding*, arriving all at once at the shape of a betrayal seven years deep.

"You knew." Not shouted. Spoken with a bookkeeper's precision, each word entered in its column. "Seven years. You sat across from me in that hall. You ate at Ossen's table. When I found the entry in my own ledger—when I came to the members, asking, begging anyone to remember the night I sold her—you knew, and you said nothing, and you let me dig with my hands tied."

"I was bound," Hesk said. "By craft, by contract, by—"

"By *coin*. Whose?"

"That's the thing." Hesk's voice cracked, an old hinge. "I don't know. I never saw the face. Not hidden—*gone*. And Sable says—" he gestured helplessly at the forger, who had put down her needlework and was watching Cyrus with the wariness of a woman near a lit fuse— "Sable says what happened to my memory isn't a veil. It's authority. God-grade, or near enough. Which means the buyer wasn't merely protected. The buyer was *serving*."

Silence, except the fire and the rain beginning again somewhere above the outcrop.

Maren moved first. She crossed to Cyrus and touched his arm—two fingers, light, the touch she used instead of speech—and then she signed, quick and fluid, and Hesk read her hands the way he read contracts, because he'd had weeks to learn her alphabet: *Ask him what else he remembers. Every detail. Details are doors.*

"He's right to tell us now, whatever his reasons were," Sable said quietly. "For what it's worth. A secret held that long, by a professional, against payment—I've forged men's entire lives, Hesk, and even I don't know if I could have done differently." She tilted her head. "But that's not the interesting question. The interesting question is why he's telling us *now*."

"Because of the soldier," Hesk said. "In the bone. Three nights ago. The dead said the tithe-marked aren't hunting the registry. They're hunting the trail of a single sale. And I knew which sale, and I knew I was one of the stones in that trail, and I—" he spread his hands, empty, the emptiest gesture of his life— "I could not collect my own silence forever. Not from you. Not when the price of keeping it is your throat."

Cyrus sat back down. Slowly, like an old man, though he was not old. He looked into the fire for a long time, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and cold and terribly steady, the voice Hesk imagined he used with ledgers when the numbers refused to balance.

"The buyer summoned you at night. Senior guild wax on the seal. Knew your private debt to the inner circle to the copper. Knew my sale before most of the guild knew it existed. Could erase a face from perception without leaving a seam." He counted them off on scarred fingers. "There are eleven people alive with access to senior wax. I have audited every one of their accounts for twenty years. I know their hands, their vices, their handwriting." His jaw tightened. "I'm going to write out that list tonight, and you're going to walk me through that room again, shelf by shelf, sound by sound, smell by smell, until something matches."

"And if it does?" Hesk asked. "If it's someone we named at Ossen's table? Someone whose ashes are in the guildhall?" Because that was the thought that had kept him pacing the road behind the others for three days: the buyer had come to him *before* the fire. The orchestration was old. Older than Ashkaval's armies. Older, perhaps, than the usurper himself. Someone had laid these stones years ago—and if they had laid one trail, they had laid others, and every step this company took toward the mountain might be walking a road designed for them, paved by a hand they had trusted, ending wherever that hand wished.

Sable, softly, finished the thought for him, because forgers and collectors thought alike in the end: "Then we're not following Cyrus's deed to the mountain. We're being *driven* along it. Like debtors."

Maren signed something short and fierce that needed no translation.

Cyrus stared into the fire, and Hesk watched him arrive at the place Hesk had lived for three days—the place where trust turned in the hand and showed its other face. Then the bone-carver reached into his shirt and drew out the curl of whalebone, the soldier's last words still half-carved along its curve, and held it to his ear as though it were a shell, listening to the dead, who perhaps knew more about the guild's sins than any of the living.

When he lowered it, his eyes were wet and his voice was iron.

"Shelf by shelf, then," he said to Hesk. "Start with the wax."

And far below them, beneath miles of pine and stone and rain, something in the dark of the mountain turned a page.
