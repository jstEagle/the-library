## 13. Footholds of the Old Gods

Maren had not prayed in four years. She had thought, in the beginning, that the silence would be the worst of it—that waking each morning to a throat that opened on nothing would be a small dying repeated daily until it finished her. But she had learned what every mute thing learns: that listening is its own kind of speech, and that the world is louder than anyone with a voice can afford to notice.

So it was Maren who heard the mountain first.

They had climbed for six days out of the shrine valley, up through pine that thinned to birch and birch that thinned to nothing, following a road that existed only because Sable insisted it did. The air grew thin and clean and cold enough to bite through wool. Cyrus walked ahead with his collar up and his head bowed, one hand cupped behind his ruined ear as though he could warm the hearing back into it. Hesk was gone—burned into all of them now, a promise-shaped absence—and Cyrus carried his last collection the way other men carry ashes.

Maren walked at the rear, because the rear was where the listening was best.

It began as a rhythm under her boots. Not the ordinary pulse of stone settling, not water moving in some deep vein, but something slower and vaster—a breath drawn over minutes and released over more, so long between beats that a lesser listener would have called it silence. She stopped. The others went on three paces before Cyrus turned, reading her stillness the way he read everything now, with his whole body.

*What is it?* he signed. His hands were clumsy still; hers were fluent. Four years of sign had made her hands quicker than most people's tongues.

She pointed down. Then, after a moment's thought, pressed her palm flat against the frozen ground and held it there, eyes closed, letting her face tell him what her voice could not.

Cyrus crouched beside her. He could not hear it—his gift ran through bone and ivory, through the carved and the dead, not through living stone—but he watched her face the way he watched ledgers, line by line, and whatever he saw there made him go pale in the particular way he went pale when an entry refused to balance.

"That's not a heartbeat," he said aloud, for Sable's benefit, though his eyes stayed on Maren. "That's breathing."

Sable stood with her arms crossed against the wind, her memory-forger's eyes narrowed at the ridge above them. "Then we've found what the maps wouldn't show me," she said. "I charted this pass twenty years ago. There was nothing here. No cave, no hollow, no weight in the rock worth marking." She paused, and Maren saw the rare thing happen: Sable doubting her own craft. "Something has changed the geography by being in it."

Maren signed, slowly, so they could both follow: *Not changed. Revealed.*

Because she knew what breathed beneath them. She had known since the second day of climbing, when the rhythm first brushed the edges of her attention like a sleeper turning over. Her god—the cracked-shrine god, the nameless hearth-thing who had answered her prayer for rain by taking her voice as payment—had gone quiet the closer they came to the high passes. Not absent. Attentive. The way a servant goes quiet in the presence of a master's master.

The eldest was near. And the eldest was waking.

* * *

They found her on the seventh day, in a cirque of black stone where no snow lay though snow fell into it and never touched ground.

Maren felt her before any of them saw anything: a pressure like the moment before thunder, a fullness in the ears she no longer used for hearing. Then the fog that had dogged them all morning tore along a seam no wind had made, and there she was.

A giantess of granite and glacier, half-risen from the mountainside as though the mountain were a bed she had slept in for a thousand years and was only now sitting up in. She was vast past sense—her shoulder a cliff-face lichened silver, her hair a cascade of icefalls frozen mid-tumble, her face tilted toward a sky she seemed to be remembering rather than seeing. One eye was open. It was the size of a chapel window, and it was the grey-green of deep water over stone, and it was looking directly at them.

Maren's knees went. Not from fear—from recognition. Every god she had ever tended, every cracked shrine and forgotten altar in forty years of keeping, had been a finger pointing at this. The hearth-god, the river-mothers, the small bitter saints of doorways and crossroads: sparks. Embers carried up from a fire banked somewhere far below. And here was the fire—or here was the fire's sister, its kin, one of the old ones who had lain down when the new prayers went elsewhere, and was now, joint by joint, century by century, rising.

Cyrus had gone utterly still. Maren had learned to read his stillnesses. This one was awe with terror threaded through it, the stillness of a man standing in a ledger-hall and realizing the ledger is reading him back.

"She's looking at us," Sable whispered. "Gods don't look. They attend. They preside. They—" She stopped herself, because the eye moved then, tracking Cyrus as he took one careful step forward, and there was nothing presiding in it at all. There was attention. There was something almost like hope, which was worse.

Maren stepped up beside him. If the eldest wanted to look, let her look at two broken people who had come anyway.

The giantess spoke. Maren felt it rather than heard it—a vibration through the soles of her boots, through her teeth, through the bones of her hands, and she realized with a strange, distant clarity that this was how Cyrus lived all the time now, a man of pure vibration, and that perhaps his half-deafness was not a wound but a tuning.

"Little carriers," the mountain said. Her voice was an avalanche slowed ten thousand times, each word arriving whole and patient. "You smell of ink and ash and given things."

Cyrus bowed. Actually bowed, low, the ledger-keeper's bow he must have made ten thousand times to merchants and magistrates, offered now to a hillside with an open eye. "We're traveling to the court beneath the mountain," he said. "Under Ashkaval. We carry a debt that needs unwinding."

The great eye blinked. Snow shivered loose from an eyelash the size of a fallen pine.

"Ashkaval." The name rolled through the cirque, and where it rolled, the frost on the stones bloomed and died, bloomed and died, seasons in miniature. "The thief in my brother's house. The man who prays with a knife." Something moved in the granite face that might have been distaste, or grief; with a face that old and that still, Maren could not tell where the mountain ended and the expression began. "You are going to him. To the Mountain Below."

"Yes," Cyrus said.

"Then you are going to a prison," she said, "and calling it a court. Come closer, little carrier. Closer. My sight is long but my patience is shorter than it was, and I have been asleep so long that waking feels like drowning, and I would spend some of my first clear hours on the truth."

They came closer. There was no alternative Maren could imagine; refusing a waking god felt like refusing weather. They climbed the tumbled apron of stone at her base and stood in the lee of a hand large enough to roof a barn, and up close Maren could see that she was not entirely stone—that green things grew in the seams of her, moss and stunted pine and one improbable flower, and that her open eye wept a slow meltwater that fed a stream none of their maps had ever shown.

"You know the sigil," Cyrus said, and drew from inside his coat the copy of Wren's deed, wrapped in oilcloth against the cold. He unrolled it with the reverence he gave only to documents and to Wren's memory, and held it up.

The effect was immediate and terrible. The giantess went rigid—all of her, a thousand tons of waking stone seizing at once—and the temperature of the cirque dropped so fast that Maren's breath cracked in the air. When the voice came again it was very quiet, and quiet, from something that size, was the loudest thing Maren had ever not-heard.

"Where," said the giantess, "did you get that."

"It was sealed over my friend's soul seven years ago," Cyrus said. His voice did not shake. Maren marveled at that, and understood it: this was the steadiness of a man who had already lost everything once and found it did not kill him. "Sold to the court below. Witnessed by—" he tapped the sigil, a spiral wound around a descending line, cut into the parchment in ink that gleamed faintly even now, even in shadow, like water at the bottom of a well. "By this."

"I know this mark." Each word fell separately, like stones into a well. "I taught it to him. My brother. Before your cities had names, before your guild had ledgers, before the first prayer was ever whispered into the dark and answered—we were two, and we marked our bargains thus, so that neither could lie to the other." The meltwater from her eye quickened. "And now his mark is on a soul-deed. On collateral. Tell me, keeper of accounts. What does your registry say the Mountain Below is?"

Cyrus hesitated. Maren signed the answer before he could find words gentle enough, because there were no words gentle enough, and gentleness was a luxury the truth could not afford: *Prisoner.*

The word landed. She saw it land, in the stillness of the giantess, in the way the pines growing from her shoulders bent as if in wind.

"Yes," the mountain said at last. "Yes. I dreamed it, these hundred years, and told myself the dream was rot. That he had gone down into the deep places willingly, as he always did, to keep the roots of the world. But the prayers—" Her gaze went distant, up and away, toward Vellmark and the free cities and all the cracked shrines opening across them. "You cannot imagine what prayers taste like from inside a sleep. For centuries they thinned. Men forgot us, and the forgetting was restful, and I did not mind. And then—seven years ago? Eight?—they began again. Thick. Sweet. Pouring downward like grain into a silo. Someone below was drinking them. Someone below was being *fed*." The eye refocused on Cyrus, and Maren saw the awful arithmetic complete itself behind it. "A god does not answer prayers, little carrier. A god *is* answers, the way a river is wet. Unless he is bound. Unless someone holds the deed to him and forces him to pour out his nature on command—and takes a tithe of every miracle, and grows fat on divinity that was never his."

"Ashkaval," Sable said softly. "His mandate. His armies march and don't break, his claim holds against every rival, because every prayer in the free cities gets answered in his favor. We thought he'd bargained with the Mountain Below. We thought it was alliance."

"Alliance." The giantess laughed, and the laugh was a rockfall far away, sad and enormous. "You do not ally with your plow. Your guild sold my brother, keeper. Sold him the way your kind sells everything, and someone has been holding his leash ever since, and wearing his sigil like a stolen crown. And now his mark sits on the soul of a girl, because souls are the chain-links, aren't they? Souls are what deeds are nailed with." Her gaze dropped to the parchment, to the name written there in Cyrus's own hand seven years ago, and softened in a way that frightened Maren more than the rage had. "*Wren.* A small name. A bird's name. What did she cost you?"

"Everything," Cyrus said simply. "And she paid more."

The giantess was silent a long time. Wind moved through the cirque. Somewhere below them, Maren felt her own god stir—her cracked-shrine god, her voice-thief—and press close to the surface of her awareness like a child at a mother's knee, listening to its elder.

"Then hear the scale of what you climb toward," the mountain said. "My brother lies beneath that court in chains of contract, older and tighter than iron, and every year the chains are renewed with souls. Free the girl and you snap one link. Snap one link and the deed frays. Fray the deed and Ashkaval falls—but my brother rises, and he has been milked of his nature for a lifetime of your years, and Maren-of-the-cracked-shrine"—the eye turned to her, and Maren's heart slammed against her ribs, because of course the god knew her, of course they all knew her, every keeper of every cracked shrine was known—"you of all of them should understand what a god is like when the taking stops. You lost a voice. He will have lost everything, and he will wake angry, and the anger of the eldest will not distinguish between the thief and the thieves' neighbors."

Maren lifted her hands. They did not shake, though everything in her shook. *Then someone must be there when he wakes,* she signed. *Someone who knows what it is to have a god take from you and survive it. Someone who can teach him that the taking can stop without the world stopping.*

The giantess regarded her for a long moment. Then, slowly, with a care that made the mountainside groan, she extended one finger—the size of a felled oak—and touched it to the ground before them, palm-up, the way a human offers a hand to a sparrow.

On the granite pad of her fingertip, frost crawled into lines. Into a spiral, wound around a descending line. The sigil—but reversed, unmade, a key where the deed bore a lock.

"When you stand in front of my brother," she said, "show him this. Show him his sister remembers the bargain we made before bargains had names: that neither of us would ever be the other's property. He will have forgotten. They will have made him forget. Remind him." The eye closed, then opened, wetter. "And keeper—if you trade that girl's freedom for anything, if you let them price you, if you so much as *balance* against her—I will feel it here, in my sleep, and I will finish waking, and I will come down, and no one in that court will enjoy what follows. Do you understand the terms?"

"He already swore it," Sable said quietly. "To a dying man. Hesk collected the promise himself."

"Good," said the giantess. "Debts witnessed are debts kept. Now go. The pass beyond my shoulder descends to the drowned mines, and the mines remember the way down even when men forget. And little carrier—" the eye fixed on Cyrus one final time, and the tenderness in it was unbearable, "—when you see my brother, tell him his sister is awake. Tell him he is not alone down there. He has believed he is alone for a very long time, and belief is the strongest chain of all."

They turned to go. Maren was last, as always, and as she passed beneath the great shadowed jaw she felt the giantess's attention settle on her alone, brief and warm as a hand on the head.

*Thank you,* Maren signed, not knowing if she could be seen, not caring.

The mountain's breath moved over her like a thaw. *Keep listening, voiceless one,* the elder god said, in the marrow of her bones. *When you reach the vaults, it will be the listening that saves us all.*

Beyond the giantess's shoulder, the pass descended into darkness, and far below—so far that Maren felt it only as a tremor in her teeth, a single link of a chain shifting somewhere in the deep—the Mountain Below stirred in his sleep, and dreamed, for the first time in a century, of morning.
