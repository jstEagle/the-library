## 14. Tithe-Marked

The shrine appeared out of the snow like a wound in the world.

Cyrus saw it first as a smudge of grey against the white pines, then as a shape—low, hunched, built of river stones stacked without mortar by hands that had trusted nothing to last. Its roof had half-collapsed under seasons of snow. A wooden lintel leaned where a door should have been. And above it all, cracked down the middle like an egg struck once and abandoned, stood the shrine-stone itself: a pillar of black granite furred with lichen, humming faintly in the way that waking things hummed.

Maren felt it before any of them. She stopped mid-stride on the frozen track, her bare throat working around words she no longer owned, and pressed two fingers to the stone's cold surface the way another woman might touch a sleeping child's brow. Her eyes went wide. Her hand began to shake.

"She says it's one of hers," Sable translated quietly. The memory-forger had learned Maren's language of gestures faster than any of them, which Cyrus supposed made sense—a woman who traded in false recollections would be quick to read true ones. "One of the Mountain Below's way-shrines. Old trade road, before the mines drowned. She says—" Sable paused, and something flickered across her face, some unease she smoothed away too quickly. "She says the god is listening here. More than elsewhere."

"Then we rest somewhere it can hear us," Hesk said cheerfully, dropping his pack into the snow. "Good. I've been talking to myself for nine days and even I'm bored of my company."

They made camp in the shrine's lee because there was nowhere else. The pass had narrowed to a throat of pine and shadow; the track ahead climbed through drifts past a man's waist, and behind them the tithe-marked hunters had been a rumor at their backs for three days now—a broken bird snared wrong, a cairn toppled, once a distant horn-note flattened by wind until none of them could swear they'd heard it at all. Rest was not a choice so much as a debt coming due.

Cyrus built the fire while Hesk rigged the tarps and Sable walked the perimeter with her hood up, reading the snow the way she read anything: for what had been written over. Maren sat inside the shrine itself, cross-legged before the cracked stone, her palms flat against it, her lips moving in silence. Praying, Cyrus thought. To a god who had already taken her voice as payment for one answered prayer. He could not decide if that was faith or grief or both braided tight enough to be indistinguishable.

He sat apart, as he always did, with a scrap of whalebone in his lap and his knife moving in small, patient strokes.

He was carving Ossen again. Or rather, he was carving the words Ossen had died saying—you were never invisible—and each night the letters came truer, deeper, more obedient to his hand. It was strange, this new clarity. All his life the dead had come to him muffled, as voices heard through a wall, through water, through the ivory's own dense forgetting. But since the guildhall burned, since Ossen's weight had sagged against him with the registry clutched between them like a child passed from a sinking ship—the dead spoke plainly. Too plainly. As though the gods' answering had worn his hearing open like a boot worn through at the sole.

*You were never invisible,* the bone said, in Ossen's dry rasp, whenever Cyrus's thumb found the letters.

"I know," Cyrus murmured. "You said. You keep saying."

*Because you keep needing to hear it.*

Hesk dropped down beside him with a grunt, close enough that their shoulders touched, and produced from some inner pocket a twist of paper containing four shriveled plums. He offered one. Cyrus took it. This was, in its entirety, the ritual of their friendship: Hesk appearing beside him, food changing hands, neither of them obliged to speak.

But tonight Hesk spoke.

"You know what a promise weighs?" the collector asked, gnawing his plum. "Properly weighed, I mean. On the scales we used to keep at the guildhall, before your registry went up in smoke."

"Heavier than gold," Cyrus said. "Lighter than bone."

"Wrong." Hesk held up a finger. "A promise weighs exactly what the promiser can't afford to lose. That's why I never collected from poor folk. Nothing to hold. That's why the rich hated me." He smiled his gap-toothed smile, but it didn't reach his eyes, and Cyrus—who had spent thirty years reading ledgers, who knew every way a column could lie—saw it. "And that's why I've been thinking about you, Galloway. About what you'd put on the scales, if it came to it."

"It won't come to it."

"No," Hesk agreed easily. "Course not."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the pines shed snow in soft, irregular sighs. Cyrus looked across the flames at Maren's bowed back, at Sable's silhouette pacing the tree line, and felt the particular tenderness he had begun—without noticing when—to feel toward all of them. Four broken instruments, Ossen might have said, tuned to different losses, somehow making a music.

"Hesk," he said. "What did you collect from me? Back then. Before Wren."

The collector went very still. It lasted only a moment—he was too good a trader to let stillness linger—but Cyrus caught it, the way you catch a misprinted figure in a long column: instantly, and forever.

"Another time," Hesk said gently. "When we're somewhere warm."

"That's what you always say."

"And I always mean it." He rose, brushing snow from his knees, and paused. For a heartbeat his face held something Cyrus had never seen on it—not the merchant's mask, not the rogue's grin, but something older and barer, the face beneath both. "Galloway. If it ever comes to scales—if you're ever standing there weighing one thing against another—remember what you told me you'd never do. Whatever else you forget. Remember that."

"I don't remember telling you anything."

"I know," said Hesk. "That was rather the point."

He walked off toward the horses before Cyrus could ask what in the god's name that meant, and Cyrus sat alone with his carving and the fire and the slow dread rising in him like water in a locked hold.

* * *

It was Sable who heard them first—or rather, it was Sable who noticed the absence.

"The birds," she said, materializing at the fire's edge near midnight, her voice stripped of all its usual silk. "There are no birds. There have been no birds since dusk. In a pine forest. In winter." She looked at Cyrus. "Birds go quiet for owls. They go quiet for weather. They go silent as the grave for men."

They woke Maren with a touch, gathered the gear with hands made quick and quiet by fear, and had the horses saddled when the horn sounded—close, closer than any of them had guessed, close enough that the note needed no wind to carry it. It was answered by a second horn to the east. A third to the south.

"They've been waiting," Sable breathed. "Not following. *Waiting.* They knew where the only shelter in the pass was."

"They knew where the shrine was," Cyrus corrected, and the understanding arrived cold and complete: the tithe-marked didn't hunt. They herded. Someone—Ashkaval's people, or worse, someone who read god-sigils the way Maren did—had known the party must stop at the last waking shrine before the high snows, and had simply closed the trap around it and waited for the cold to do the work.

The first arrow took Cyrus's horse through the neck.

After that, the night came apart.

He remembered it afterward in fragments, the way he remembered everything since the answering began—in pieces too sharp to fit together. Firelight and snow spinning orange. Sable screaming words that were not words, forging something in the air itself, and three hunters reining hard away from a bridge that wasn't there, plunging into a ravine that was. Maren standing before the cracked stone with her arms flung wide and her mouth open on soundless fury, and the granite pillar *humming*, actually humming, a note that made the hunters' horses scream and balk. Hesk—

Hesk was everywhere at once. He fought the way he collected debts: cheerfully, shamelessly, with no honor whatsoever and total commitment. He threw snow into a man's eyes. He broke a bow across another's back. He laughed the whole time, that terrible gap-toothed laugh, and when a blade opened his side he laughed harder, and when they cut him down he was still laughing, and Cyrus reached him on his knees in the bloodied snow with a hunter's sword raised over him and put his knife into the hunter's throat without ever remembering deciding to.

Then it was quiet. The remaining horns sounded retreat—Sable's forged ravine had convinced them the pass held soldiers, or Maren's god had frightened them, or both—and the snow drank the blood and the fire burned low and Cyrus knelt in the ruin of the night holding Hesk's head off the ground while the promise-collector's breath came in short, bright, failing bursts.

"Don't," Cyrus said. "Don't you dare. You owe me an answer. You owe me—"

"Aye," Hesk whispered. His grin was still there. Blood in his teeth couldn't dislodge it. "I do. And I pay my debts. Always have." His hand crawled across the snow, found Cyrus's wrist, gripped it with shocking strength. "Listen. The sale. Wren's sale. Seven years back. A buyer came to the guild—veiled, paid triple, wanted no name in the ledger. Only a mark. I collected the silence from you myself—I was told to, Galloway, I was *told*, by someone whose word I couldn't refuse, and I've hated myself seven years for taking the fee—"

"*Who?*"

"Never learned the face. Learned the *voice*, though." Hesk coughed, wet and long. "Learned it well enough that when I heard it again, years later, from a throne-room herald's mouth, I knew it at once. Ashkaval's own chamberlain speaks with the tongue that bought your silence. Which means—" his grip tightened, "—means the usurper's court arranged Wren's soul before Ashkaval ever claimed the mountain. Before anyone knew there'd be a claim to make. They *built* their mandate on her, Cyrus. She's not collateral. She's the cornerstone."

The world tilted. Seven years of ledger-lines rearranged themselves in Cyrus's mind with a sound like ice cracking. Not an accident. Not a desperate sale gone astray. An architecture. His guilt, his grief, Wren's captivity—all of it laid brick by brick by hands in the dark, and he had signed his name to the mortar.

"Why didn't you tell me on the road?"

"Because you'd have turned back. Or turned reckless. Both the same thing." Hesk's breath was slowing. His eyes, fixed on Cyrus's face, were terribly clear. "Now you can't turn back. Now you can only finish it. So. My fee. My last collection." He pulled Cyrus closer, and his whisper was the steadiest thing about him. "Promise me. Promise me you will not trade her freedom for your life. Not your death I'm buying—your *life*. Your surrender, your bargain, your clever ledger-keeper's trade where you give yourself up and call it love. Ashkaval will offer it. The god may offer it. Someone *will* offer it, because it's the one trade that undoes everything. Promise me, Cyrus Galloway, on whatever you still hold holy, and I'll die lighter than any man the mountain ever buried."

Cyrus's throat closed. Every argument rose in him at once—that he couldn't promise, that the future was unwritten, that a dying man had no right to bind the living—and every argument dissolved against the clarity in Hesk's fading eyes.

"I promise," Cyrus said. "By the bone and the ledger and her name. I will not trade Wren's freedom for my life."

"There." Hesk's smile gentled into something young. Something almost boyish. "Witnessed. Collected. Paid in full." His gaze drifted past Cyrus's shoulder, to the cracked stone and the humming dark, and his voice dropped to a wondering murmur. "Well. Look at that. Somebody's keeping accounts after all."

And then the promise-collector of the Guild of Impossible Things closed his eyes, having settled his final account, and the snow fell on him softly, as snow falls on everything, keeping its own kind of ledger.

* * *

They buried him at dawn where the shrine-stone's shadow fell longest.

There was no ground to dig—the mountain was iron and ice—so they built him a cairn, stone upon stone, and Maren sang over it in the sign-language hymns she had begun teaching them, her hands shaping grief into grammar. Sable laid a forged memory on the cairn, a small one, freely given: a warm room, a full table, friends laughing, a life Hesk had never gotten and deserved. "So wherever he is," she said, wiping her face with an angry hand, "he'll believe he was loved properly. Because he was."

Cyrus carved through the morning. He worked without stopping, without weeping, his knife moving in the old rhythms, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge he had finished a plaque of antler-bone bearing Hesk's name and the only epitaph that had ever fit him:

*HE PAID ALL HIS DEBTS.*

When he set it atop the cairn, the dead spoke to him—not muffled, not through walls, but plain as a bell—and for the first time since the guildhall burned, Cyrus did not flinch from the sound. He listened to Hesk's ghost chuckling somewhere in the stones, light-hearted even in death, unburdened even beyond the grave, and he understood at last what Ossen had tried to tell him: that invisibility had never been his gift. Hearing had been. Bearing witness had been. Carrying the names of the dead into the places the living feared to speak of them.

"We go on," he said aloud, to Maren, to Sable, to the cairn, to the humming god in the cracked stone. "Today. Now. While the hunters think us scattered."

Maren caught his sleeve and gestured rapidly, urgently, her hands spelling out what her stolen voice could not: *the god showed me something while they fought. The vaults below the mountain. The lanterns. One of them gutters, Cyrus. Hers. Time is shorter than we thought.*

Cyrus looked north, where the peaks climbed black and white into a sky the color of unsullied bone, and beneath them, unseen, the drowned mines ran down and down to a court where souls hung like lanterns and a usurper sat enthroned on a contract written in his own hand.

Seven years ago he had sold a soul to save his sister and called it the worst day of his life.

He picked up his pack. He tested the knife in his grip. And Cyrus Galloway, ledger-keeper, bone-carver, widower of a woman not yet dead, began to walk toward the mountain with a dead man's promise burning in his chest like a coal he would carry the rest of the way.

Behind him, faint and clear, the cracked shrine hummed—one long note, like a god drawing breath to speak.
