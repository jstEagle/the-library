## 15. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The water came up to their knees and then their waists and then, for one terrible hour, to their chins, and Cyrus learned that a drowned mine has a voice of its own—not the voices of the dead he had carried all his life, but something older and wetter, a groaning in the stone like a giant turning over in its sleep.

"Keep your hand on the guide-rope," Maren signed, her pale fingers catching the light of Sable's lamp-oil flame. She could not speak—had not spoken since her god took her voice as payment—but her hands were eloquent as scripture. "And do not drink it. The dead who drowned here are still thirsty."

Cyrus kept his hand on the rope. It was slick with something that was not quite slime and not quite memory, and through his palm he felt them: the miners, hundreds of them, generations of them, still walking their shifts in the dark below the waterline, still swinging their picks at veins that had gone barren centuries ago. They did not notice him. They were too tired even for that. He had heard angry dead and grieving dead and vengeful dead, but never before dead so *weary*, and it frightened him more than any of the others ever had.

Sable swam ahead with the lamp held high, her borrowed face—today she wore the countenance of a Vellmark fishwife, broad and unremarkable—set in an expression of grim concentration. Hesk's absence walked beside them all, though none of them said it. Three days since the shrine ambush. Three days since the promise-collector had stood in the narrow door of that crumbling waystation with his ledger-book raised like a shield and told them, in the flat merchant's cadence he used for everything, including dying: *This one is on my account.*

Cyrus carried the promise in his breast pocket now, written on a scrap of Hesk's own paper in Hesk's own hand: *That Cyrus Galloway will not trade Wren's freedom for his own life.* A debt-collector's final collection. A vow with teeth in it. Some promises were gentle things; this one would bite him if he broke it, and Hesk had made sure of that from beyond whatever lay beyond.

The water began to recede. They climbed.

* * *

The mine shafts above the floodline were dry as old sermons, and the dark there was different—not empty but *furnished*, hung with the residue of what had been done in it. Cyrus felt it against his skin the way a sailor feels weather. His hearing had been failing since the gods woke; the world's ordinary sounds arrived muffled, wrapped in wool, but the dead came through clearer than ever, and down here they came through like a choir rehearsing in the next room.

"They bargained here," Maren signed, stopping at a junction where three tunnels met and the walls were covered in markings. Not graffiti. Contracts. Rows upon rows of them, cut into the living rock in script older than any alphabet Cyrus knew, each clause sealed with a sigil that hurt slightly to look at—the same sigil that burned on Wren's deed, the mark of the Mountain Below.

"Ashkaval's ancestors," Sable murmured. She ran her fingers over the carvings without touching them, reading by nearness alone. "Every generation renewed it. Look—here's the original. *One soul in ten, rendered upward, in exchange for the mountain's favor in war and harvest.* And here—" she moved along the wall, "—*amended: one soul in five.* Here: *one in three.* Each generation a little greedier, a little more afraid. Fear makes excellent negotiating conditions."

"And the last entry?" Cyrus asked. His voice came out strange in the dry dark, half-swallowed.

Sable went quiet. Then she lifted the lamp higher, and they all saw it: the final contract, larger than the rest, carved deep enough to cast its own shadow. The signature line bore a name Cyrus recognized from a hundred guild documents, from the founding charter Ossen had kept under glass in the archive that was now ash: *Ashkaval, called the Undying Claim.*

"He didn't renew it," Sable said softly. "He *assumed* it. Took the whole debt onto himself. Which means—" she turned, and behind her borrowed face her real eyes were bright with the particular excitement she got from terrible discoveries, "—which means every soul owed under these contracts is now collateral for *his* claim. Break the contracts, break the claim. Or—" she glanced at Maren, "—wake the creditor, and let Him break it Himself."

Maren's hands moved slowly, deliberately, the way she signed when the words mattered most. *"Then we go down and knock."*

They went down.

* * *

The court announced itself first by warmth. After days of cold water and colder stone, the air turned soft as breath, smelling of candle-tallow and incense and something sweeter underneath, something like ripe fruit left too long in the sun. Then light—not lamplight, not firelight, but a diffuse golden glow rising from below, as if they were descending into the heart of a lantern.

The tunnel opened without warning into a cavern so vast that Cyrus's failing ears registered it as pressure rather than sound, a fullness in the skull. Stalactites the size of cathedral spires hung overhead, and from each one—this was the thing he would remember until he died, the thing he already knew he would be carving into bone someday, if his hands still worked, if he still lived—from each stalactite hung a chain, and from each chain hung a soul.

They looked like lanterns. That was the horror of it, that they looked *beautiful*: motes of gathered light, gold and white and one or two a deep bruised violet, swaying gently in air that had no wind, casting pools of radiance on the polished floor far below. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The whole cavern glittered like a night sky that had been taken down and stored.

And they were singing.

Not aloud. Not with voices. But Cyrus had spent forty years listening to the dead through ivory and bone, and he heard them the way he heard everything now—through his skin, his teeth, the small intact chambers of his inner ear that the gods' answering had spared. They sang without words, a vast slow chord, and every soul in that hanging constellation sang the same note of longing, and the chord rose through the cavern and out through the mountain, and Cyrus understood with sickening clarity that this was what the old gods had been feeding on. This was the kindling. Answered prayers needed fuel, and the fuel was *this*—souls hung up like lamps, burning slow, burning forever, their longing harvested as light.

"Oh," Sable whispered beside him. For once she had nothing clever to add.

Maren pressed both hands flat over her heart, then extended them outward—an old shrine-keeper's gesture, *all of this belongs to someone*. Her eyes were wet.

Cyrus stood at the edge of the gallery—a ledge of dressed stone where clerks must once have sat, taking inventory—and looked out over the vault of lanterns, and did the thing he had always done, the only thing he knew how to do. He counted.

It steadied him. It always had. Numbers were the wall he had built between himself and the world's noise, and he built it now, brick by brick: rows of twelve, chains of nine, vaults of sixty. He was perhaps four hundred souls into the eastern galleries when he stopped counting.

Because one of the lights had flickered.

It was small, that flicker, no more than a stutter in the glow, but Cyrus had spent a lifetime noticing what others overlooked—a misfiled entry, a forged seal, a name dated tomorrow. He found the chain again. Third gallery, east vault, seventh chain. The soul hanging there was dimmer than its neighbors, guttering like a candle in a draft, and its light was the wrong color—not the warm gold of the others but a paler shade, silver-gold, the color of—

Of dawn through a workshop window. Of the first frost on the reed-beds outside Vellmark. Of a girl laughing on a bridge with her sleeves rolled up, showing him the tiny bones of a wren she'd found, saying *everything that dies leaves something worth keeping, Cy, you just have to know how to listen.*

"Wren," he said. His ruined ears barely caught it. His whole body heard it.

He was moving before he decided to move—down the gallery stairs two at a time, past a bored-looking clerk in Ashkaval's livery who shouted something Cyrus couldn't hear and did not try to, along a walkway of black iron that trembled under his boots. Sable and Maren followed; he heard their footsteps through the floor, felt Sable grab his sleeve once and him shake it off. The vaults rose around him now, chains descending from the dark above to anchors set in the floor, and the souls hung close enough to touch, and he did not look at them, he *could not* look at them, because each one was a person, each one had a mother somewhere in the free cities lighting a candle to a god who was burning their child for lamp-oil—

Seventh chain.

She hung at the height of his heart.

Up close, the lantern-light resolved into something almost like a figure, a suggestion of shoulders and a bowed head, wrapped in its own glow. Dim. Flickering. Seven years of being collateral, seven years of burning slow, and she was dim as a coal nearly spent.

But aware. God help him, she was aware. As he reached out—as his scarred carver's fingers, stained with whalebone dust and Ossen's ashes and three countries of road mud, rose toward the light—the soul stirred. Turned, in the way light turns. And he felt her feel him: a shiver down the chain, a ripple through the glow, the way a sleeping woman stirs when someone says her name in the next room.

*Cyrus?*

It came through his bones, faint as a footstep in snow. Seven years since he had heard her voice except in memory, and memory had worn it smooth and wrong, and this—this thin, guttering, astonished whisper—was more hers than anything his remembering had managed to keep.

"I'm here," he said. Aloud, though he could hardly hear himself. "Wren. I'm here. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took me seven years, I didn't know, I didn't *know*—"

*I know,* she said. The light steadied a little, brightened, the way a fire leans toward a draft. *I felt the ledger open. I felt you find the page. I've been waiting for you to come read me.*

"I'm going to take you down from here." His voice cracked on it. He was crying, he realized distantly, the tears cold on his face in the warm golden air, and he did not care, he had spent his whole life invisible and anonymous and careful and he was done, done, done. "I don't know how yet. There's a contract, there's a god, there's a usurper with a claim built on your—on all of you—but I'm going to unhook you from this chain if I have to carve the anchor-stone apart with my bare hands. Do you hear me?"

A pause. Then, and he felt her smile the way you feel sun through a window:

*You always did balance your books late into the night. Come to collect me, then, bookkeeper?*

"Yes," Cyrus Galloway said, to the girl he had sold to save his sister, the girl who had let herself be sold to save him, the light in the dark that he had crossed a kingdom to reach. "Yes. I've come to settle the account."

The light flared—just once, just briefly, bright enough to throw his shadow huge and trembling across the vaulted stone—and somewhere far above them, deeper in the mountain, in whatever chamber held the throne of the man who called himself the Undying Claim, a bell began to ring.

Maren's hand closed hard on Cyrus's shoulder. Her signing was sharp, urgent:

*"They know we're here."*

From the darkness at the far end of the gallery, torchlight bloomed. And with it, unhurried, came the sound of boots—many boots, descending—though whether Cyrus heard them with his broken ears or felt them through the iron floor, he could no longer have said.
