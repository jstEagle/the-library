## 16. Wren, Lantern-Bright

The bone in his pocket had gone warm.

Cyrus noticed it the way a man notices a fever—gradually, then all at once. He stood at the edge of the vault of lanterns with Maren's hand still pressed to his sleeve, her mouth open on a silence that was no longer hers alone, and the whalebone he had carried for seven years—the small carved token Wren had given him the last morning of her life—was warming against his thigh like a living thing.

Around him the souls hung in their thousands, suspended in the vaulted dark of the Mountain Below's keeping: lights the color of drowned suns, of banked coals, of winter stars seen through ice. They had hung there since before Ashkaval's grandfather's grandfather had signed away his name. They were collateral. They were accounts receivable. They were, Cyrus understood now with a sickness that reached all the way down into his marrow, people.

And one of them was flickering.

He saw it without meaning to—a lantern-light near the vault's far wall, dimmer than its neighbors, guttering in a rhythm that was not random. Not random because it repeated. Four quick pulses, a pause, three slow ones. The rhythm of a knock. The rhythm Wren used to rap on his workshop door when she came to fetch him for supper, when she wanted to tease him out of his ledgers and into the evening air.

Four quick. A pause. Three slow.

"Cyrus." Maren could not speak anymore—not with sound—but her hands moved, and he had learned enough of her signing on the road to catch the shape of her words. *Your pocket. It's singing.*

He drew out the bone.

It was only a sliver of whalebone, worn smooth by seven years of his thumb, carved with a pattern of interlocking waves that Wren had made one idle afternoon while he pretended to work. He had never been able to hear anything through it before. Some bones were mute; he'd told himself that for years, told himself that the silence in this particular piece meant nothing, meant only that some dead things kept their own counsel.

But now the bone was warm as a held hand, and when his fingers closed around it, the voices came.

Not the dead. Not the chorus of the departed that had murmured at the edges of his hearing since childhood, that had grown deafening since the gods began answering prayers. This voice was singular. This voice was alive in the way that souls are alive even after bodies fail. This voice said his name.

*Cyrus.*

He did not fall to his knees, though his knees asked him to. He gripped the vault's iron railing instead, and the cold of it bit through his palms, and he let it bite, because he needed something real to hold against the impossible warmth of a voice he had buried with its owner seven years ago.

"Wren," he said aloud, and his voice cracked like green wood in a fire.

*You're older,* the voice said, and there was laughter in it, faint and wondering, laughter stretched thin across seven years of dark. *I can feel it through the bone. Your hands are heavier. You've been carrying too many ledgers, you fool, wonderful man.*

"I've been carrying—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, because there were ten thousand things to say and only one of them mattered. "I came for you. I sold everything, I burned everything, I walked half the world under the pines and through the passes and I gave up—" His hand went reflexively to his breast pocket, where the absence lived where his memory of her face used to be. He had traded that memory to the cartographer's door, weeks ago, and he had thought the price paid in full. But hearing her voice now, he learned that grief has annexes he had never mapped. "I don't remember your face," he whispered. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I paid it for the road down here, and I'd pay it again, but I want you to know—I want you to know that I know what it cost."

*Oh,* said the soul in the lantern-light, and the light flared, guttering bright. *Oh, Cyrus. You beautiful, terrible bookkeeper. You think you bought this road?*

* * *

Wren had counted the days differently down here.

There was no sun beneath the mountain, so she had made her own arithmetic: each time the great god in his chains turned over in his sleep, that was a month. Each time the tithe-carts rattled up from the world above with their cargo of new lantern-souls, that was a season. She had been counting for seven years, and she had arrived, somewhere in the fourth year, at an understanding that had settled over her like snowfall: that she was not being punished. That she was not even, strictly speaking, imprisoned.

She was collateral. And collateral, if it is patient, can learn everything about the debt it secures.

She had drifted—souls do not walk, they drift, the way smoke drifts, the way silt drifts in deep water—along the vault walls, among the thousands of other lights. Most of them slept. That was the mercy of the Mountain Below's keeping: the god, chained as he was, wrapped the souls in something like sleep, because a soul left awake in the dark too long begins to fray. Wren had refused the sleep. She had reasons.

Her reason had a name, and heavy hands, and a habit of talking to bones.

She had watched the tithe-carts come. She had listened—souls hear strangely, everything at once and nothing clearly—to the negotiations conducted in the court above the vaults, between Ashkaval's stewards and the waking fragments of the eldest god. And slowly, in the long dark, she had assembled the truth the way Cyrus assembled ledgers: entry by entry, cross-referenced, balanced.

The guild's oldest contract. Older than Ossen. Older than the guildhall. A bargain struck generations ago between the founders of the Guild of Impossible Things and the god they called the Mountain Below: the guild would feed him prayers, worship, the slow drip of answered hopes—and in exchange, the god would be free.

Except the contract had been written wrong. Or written right, depending on whose hand had held the pen. Because the clause that bound the god also bound the guild, and the guild's half of the bargain had been defaulted upon within a generation, and a defaulted divine contract does not simply lapse. It accrues. Interest compounds. And the interest on a broken promise to a god is measured in souls.

Someone had to stand surety. Someone had to hang in the vault as living proof of the debt, so that the god, in his rage, would have something to hold instead of tearing loose and drowning the world above in answered prayers until nothing living remained.

For six generations, the surety had rotated. A soul every twenty years or so, quietly taken, quietly hung, quietly forgotten. The guild's invisible ledger-keepers had recorded each transfer in a hand so small and neat that no auditor ever questioned it.

And seven years ago, the rotation had come due again, and the name drawn from the ledger had been Cyrus Galloway's.

*That's what you don't remember,* Wren had told him, would tell him, was telling him now through the warm bone across miles of stone. *You never knew because Hesk collected the promise of your not-knowing before you ever learned to look. There was a debt against your name, Cyrus. Your life, pledged against the guild's oldest sin. And I found out first.*

She had found out because she had gone looking. She had always gone looking—that was the thing about Wren that the ledgers never captured, the thing that made her laugh at locked doors and pick arguments with priests. She had pried into the archive on a night when the lamps burned low, and she had read the entry dated for the following year, and she had understood that the man she loved was scheduled, like a shipment, like a tithe.

So she had done the arithmetic no one else would do. One soul for one soul. A substitution of parties, perfectly legal, perfectly binding, witnessed—she had insisted on this, had demanded it—with a god's sigil, so that no clerk, no usurper, no clever-handed forger could ever undo it without unraveling the whole ancient web.

*I chose it,* she said now, and her voice through the bone was fierce, was blazing, was the voice of a woman who had never once in her life let someone else pay her debts. *Do you hear me? I didn't die for you. I didn't sacrifice myself like some martyr in a shrine-song. I made a trade. I was good at trades. I learned from watching you. And I got the better end of it, because I knew exactly what I was buying.*

"What could you possibly have bought," Cyrus said, and his voice broke apart on the words, "that was worth this?"

*Time,* she said. *Seven years of time. Every morning you woke up free. Every stupid audit, every dead sailor's last words, every cup of tea gone cold on your desk—you lived them. You were supposed to hang in this dark before you'd ever really lived at all. I bought you a whole life, Cyrus Galloway. Don't you dare stand there apologizing for spending it.*

* * *

In the vault, the lantern-light that was Wren pulsed in the old rhythm—four quick, a pause, three slow—and if souls could weep, hers would have been weeping, because she could feel him through the bone, feel his heartbeat stuttering, feel the shape of the memory-shaped hole in him where her face had been.

She had expected that. She had not expected how much it would hurt to be forgotten by the one person she had done everything to protect.

*Tell me what I looked like,* she said. *No—wait. Tell me what you remember, even without the face. Tell me one thing. Anything.*

And Cyrus, gripping the railing, gripping the bone, spoke into the dark of the vault of lanterns the way a man confesses: "You hummed when you cooked. Badly. You hummed songs that didn't exist, made them up as you went, and when I pointed out that you'd changed the tune halfway through you said that was the point of songs. You had ink on your left ring finger most days because you helped me copy the ledgers even though you claimed to hate them. You sat on the roof of the guildhall during storms. You said lightning was just the sky doing arithmetic." His breath hitched. "I don't have your face. But I have all of that. I have all of that, and I kept it, and I will keep it until I die."

The light flared so bright that nearby lantern-souls stirred in their sleep, drifting toward it like moths.

*The sky doing arithmetic,* Wren repeated, wonder-struck. *I said that? I'd forgotten I said that.* A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gentled into something almost shy. *You kept me better than I kept myself. Down here, in the dark, I lost pieces. Souls fray, even refusing the sleep. But you kept the parts that mattered. You were my ledger too, all along. I just never audited you.*

"Maren is going to wake him," Cyrus said, turning to look at her—at Maren, who stood with her hands folded and her throat bare, who had given her voice to the vault's threshold and was already beginning the signs that were less language than prayer. "The Mountain Below. She's giving what's left of her voice to wake him. When he wakes, the vault shatters. The souls go free. You'll go free. You'll go back—to your body, if it still—"

*It doesn't matter if it does,* Wren said simply. *Free is free. Even if I come back as rain. Even if I come back as nothing but the last note of a badly hummed song.* Then, softer: *But Cyrus. Listen to me. There's something you have to know before he wakes, because once he wakes there won't be time, and because you will be offered things, and you are the worst man I have ever known at telling the difference between a gift and a trap.*

"Tell me."

*When the god wakes, he will owe accounts. Seven generations of accounts. And the one who holds the registry—the one who holds the oldest contract—gets to decide how those accounts are settled. Ashkaval wants that power. But the god might offer it to you instead. To the man who came all this way. To the keeper of the ledgers.* Her light pulsed, urgent now. *They will dress it up as justice. They will say: you of all people deserve to make the guilty pay. And you will be tempted, because you have spent your whole life invisible, keeping everyone else's accounts, and no one has ever once offered to put the pen in your hand.*

"And if they do?"

*Then you remember Hesk,* she said. *Then you remember what he bought with his life. Promise me. Here, now, through the bone, where I can witness it the way I witnessed my own sale. Promise me you won't trade yourself for power. Promise me you'll choose the small thing. The living thing. Promise me you'll come back up into the daylight and be a man who hums badly and sits on roofs, and not a god with a ledger for a heart.*

Cyrus looked at the bone in his palm. At the little carved waves, interlocking, endless, the pattern she had made on an ordinary afternoon while he pretended to work and actually watched her hands.

"You know," he said, "I've heard the dead speak through ivory my entire life. Thousands of them. Sailors and kings and children and thieves. And not one of them—not one—ever managed to make me promise anything. I carve their words. I keep their accounts. But I never once let them give me orders."

*And?*

"And you're not dead," he said. "You're collateral. Which means you're not subject to my rules." He lifted the bone to his lips, an absurd gesture, a kiss given to a piece of whalebone in a vault beneath the world, and said: "I promise. Witnessed. Entered into the record. Let it be binding."

The light that was Wren blazed like a small dawn.

*Witnessed,* she echoed, and the word rang through the vault, and every sleeping lantern-soul stirred at the sound, and far above them—or perhaps below them, or perhaps everywhere at once—something vast and ancient shifted in its chains, and the first crack appeared in the ceiling of the world.

*Now go,* Wren said. *Go stand next to the shrine-keeper. Go watch a god wake up. And Cyrus—* four quick pulses, a pause, three slow, *—when I come back, whatever I come back as, find me. I'll be the one humming.*

* * *

Maren's final signs rose like birds taking flight.

The Mountain Below opened one eye the size of a harbor, and the vault of lanterns began, with a sound like the first syllable of a word seven thousand years long, to break.
