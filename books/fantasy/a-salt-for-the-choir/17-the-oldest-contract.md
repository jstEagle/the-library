## 17. The Oldest Contract

Sable had forged a thousand memories in her life—wedding days that never happened, childhoods stitched from borrowed cloth, griefs softened at the edges like old coins—and she had learned one truth above all the others: *every forgery has a seam.* The trick was never to hide it. The trick was to make the seam look intentional, so that when the mind brushed against it, it flinched away, believing the flaw to be its own.

The master registry of the Guild of Impossible Things had no seams.

She had been reading it for two days in the drowned-mine camp where they'd sheltered after the vaults of lanterns, and every page she turned felt like pressing her thumb against a scar that had healed too perfectly. Ossen's hand for the first century of entries. Before him, a spidery script signed only with an initial: *V.* Before that, entries in a language Sable recognized but could not read—the tongue of the first guildmasters, dead now longer than most nations.

She sat cross-legged on a bedroll near the mine's flooded lower gallery, the registry open across her knees, a stub of tallow candle guttering beside her. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a metronome. Above her, faint through the stone, came the sound of Cyrus Galloway talking to Wren's lantern-light—or trying to. His voice kept failing. Hers kept flickering. It was the saddest conversation Sable had ever overheard, and she had once forged a widow's memory of forty years of marriage down to the smell of the bread.

"You're frowning at it again," Maren said.

Maren moved quietly for a woman who had given her voice to a god; she'd learned to announce herself by touch, a brush of fingers on the shoulder, and Sable still startled every time. The shrine-keeper crouched beside her, sleeves rolled, forearms still dusted white from the god-sigils she'd chalked along the gallery walls as wards. She looked at the registry the way she looked at everything now—with the particular hunger of someone who had lost the ability to ask questions aloud.

"It's not lying to me," Sable said. "That's what worries me."

Maren made a small gesture: *explain.*

"A forgery wants to be believed. This—" Sable turned a page, and the candlelight caught the vellum's grain, fine as skin. "This wants to be *understood*. Look at the marginalia. Whoever kept this ledger wanted whoever came after to follow the thread. Every soul-contract in here is cross-referenced, dated, witnessed. Except this section." She flattened her palm against the final third of the book, where the pages grew older, stiffer, their edges browned like autumn leaves. "Except the oldest contract. Which isn't cross-referenced at all. Because there's nothing to cross-reference it *against*. It's the root. Everything else grows out of it."

Maren leaned closer. Her eyes moved across the archaic script, and Sable watched her lips shape silent syllables—she still mouthed words sometimes, out of habit, though no sound came. Then her hands began to move, quick and precise.

*It's not a language I know. But the sigil repeats.*

"I know," Sable said grimly. "It repeats forty-one times."

She had counted. Of course she had counted; counting was half of forging, the other half being the patience to let a lie grow its own roots. Forty-one repetitions of the same sigil, each one larger than the last, until the final instance filled an entire page—a seal pressed into the vellum so deep it had embossed the three pages beneath it, a mark like a mountain seen from below, or a mouth seen from inside.

The sigil of the Mountain Below. The eldest god. The prisoner-patron whose waking had shaken the vaults loose and sent souls raining upward like reversed snowfall.

"The deed is signed in god's blood," Sable said quietly. "Or something that serves for blood, in something that old. And the counterparty—" She traced the line of names at the foot of the oldest entry, names worn nearly smooth by centuries of thumbs. "The counterparty is us. The guild. Not any single guildmaster. *The Guild of Impossible Things*, in perpetuity, its heirs and assigns, world without end."

Maren went very still.

Above them, Cyrus's voice rose and cracked and fell silent again. Sable closed her eyes briefly. She had spent three days watching that man carry the registry Ossen died for, and he carried it the way mules carry loads—head down, jaw set, refusing to feel the weight because feeling it would stop him. She knew that posture intimately. She had invented it for clients more times than she could count.

"We need to tell him," Maren signed.

"Not yet." Sable held up a finger. "Not until I'm sure. If I'm wrong about what this says, I'll have handed a drowning man a stone instead of a rope."

*And if you're right?*

Sable looked at the embossed sigil, at the mountain-mouth pressed into the flesh of the book, and said nothing, because there was no sign for what she was thinking, and she was glad of it.

* * *

She worked through the night-watch while the others slept, Hesk's absence a cold spot in the camp where his snoring used to be. That was the thing about the dead, Sable thought—they left holes shaped exactly like themselves. She had forged memories of the dead for grieving families for thirty years, and she had never once managed to fill the hole honestly. You could give a widow back her husband's laugh, but you couldn't give her back the way the laugh changed the acoustics of a room.

Hesk had collected promises the way other men collected debts, and he had died collecting the largest one of all: *that Cyrus would not trade Wren's freedom for his own life.* Sable had heard him say it, had watched the promise settle onto Cyrus like a yoke. Promises were heavier than debts. Debts you could pay off. A promise lived in you and paid itself out in installments of dread.

By candlelight, she translated.

It went slowly, because the old script was a merchant-tongue, all clauses and considerations, and the guild's founders had written contracts the way siege engineers built walls—every sentence load-bearing, every loophole bricked shut. But Sable had spent her life finding the seams in things, and even a perfect wall had a foundation, and foundations were poured in haste.

The contract ran to eleven pages. She copied the key clauses into her own shorthand on scraps of birchbark, her stylus scratching in the dark:

*Item the First: That the party of the second part [the Guild] shall hold in trust the freedom of the party of the first part [the Mountain Below], such trust to endure until the debt herein described be discharged.*

*Item the Fourth: That in consideration thereof, the Mountain Below shall serve as the wellspring of answered prayer throughout the world, its power flowing through whatever vessels the Guild shall appoint, unto all generations.*

*Item the Ninth: That the deed of this trust may be held by any who possess the master registry, and that possession shall constitute authority entire.*

Sable set down her stylus and stared at the birchbark scraps for a long time.

They hadn't sold the god's freedom. They'd done something worse. They'd taken it *into custody*. Generations ago—she counted backward through the regnal dates, through the wars and plagues referenced in the margins, and arrived at a number that made her stomach turn—nearly nine hundred years ago, the guild had bound the Mountain Below. Had made the eldest god the engine of every answered prayer in the world. Every blight lifted, every drowned child returned, every miracle great and small had flowed through the guild's appointed vessels, and the god itself had lain beneath the mountain, chained by its own signature, its freedom held *in trust* by a guild of merchants.

And then, at some point in the long centuries—she found the amendment, a later hand, Ashkaval's dynasty's hand, ink still sharp—the custody had passed. *Possession shall constitute authority entire.* The usurper's ancestors had acquired the registry. Had acquired, with it, the deed to a god.

That was why Ashkaval wanted the master registry back. Not for legitimacy. Not for the soul-contracts, not for Wren, not for any of the thousand souls hanging in those vaults like fruit. He wanted it because whoever held the book held the leash of the oldest power in the world, and he had held it unchallenged until a bone-carver walked out of Vellmark carrying it under his arm.

"Oh, you fools," Sable whispered—to the founders, to Ossen, to the whole long parade of guildmasters who had guarded this book like a treasure without ever asking what it *was*. "You beautiful, mercenary fools."

* * *

Dawn came down the mine shaft as a gray suggestion rather than a light. Sable heard the others stirring—Cyrus's uneven footsteps, the scrape of the cook-pot, Maren's soundless comings and goings. When Cyrus descended the ladder to her alcove, she was waiting with the birchbark scraps arranged in order and the registry open to the eleventh page.

He looked terrible. Three days of talking to a flickering light had carved new lines into his face, and his hearing—the hearing he'd already half-spent trading for his audience with Ashkaval—seemed worse each morning; she'd had to repeat herself twice at breakfast. But his eyes were clear, and clear eyes were dangerous. Clear eyes meant a man had decided something.

"You've been reading all night," he said.

"I finished." She patted the bedroll beside her. "Sit down, Galloway. What I have to tell you, you'll want to be sitting for."

He sat. He always did what she told him, which she suspected was less deference than exhaustion, but she'd take it.

"Tell me about Wren's contract," she said. "What you know."

His jaw tightened. "Sold seven years ago. Sealed with a god's sigil—the Mountain Below's. Witnessed divine, which makes it near-unbreakable. She was collateral for a debt I never—" His voice snagged. He cleared his throat. "For a debt the guild owed. Something old. That's all anyone knows."

"That's what everyone believes," Sable said. "Because that's what the ledger was arranged to make people believe. But Wren wasn't sold as collateral, Cyrus. She was sold as *leverage*. There's a difference. Collateral sits in a vault. Leverage walks into the room and makes you do things." She tapped the eleventh page. "Read this clause. Aloud, if you can manage it."

He bent over the ancient script. His lips moved. Sable watched comprehension arrive in stages, like weather coming over a ridge—first confusion, then dawning horror, then the flat gray stillness that comes after.

"*Item the Eleventh,*" he read slowly. "*That should the trust be voided—by discharge, by breach, or by destruction of the instrument—the Mountain Below shall stand released of all bonds entire, owing neither debt nor duty to the Guild, its heirs, or any holder of the deed. And whatsoever power the god hath lent unto the world through the vessels of the Guild shall revert unto the god, to keep or to withhold, to give or to take, according to its own will alone.*"

Silence. The water dripped its patient count.

"When we free Wren," Sable said, "when we void the contract that holds her—we don't just free a soul. We dissolve the mechanism. Every prayer answered since before your grandmother's grandmother was answered *through* that contract. Through the god, channeled by the guild's vessels, and lately by Ashkaval, who holds the deed. Void it, and the god wakes fully free. Free to answer prayers. Free to ignore them. Free to be angry about nine hundred years of captivity." She met his eyes. "Free to decide what the world owes it. And Cyrus—the last clause. Read the last clause."

He read it. She watched his face as he did.

"*Item the Twelfth,*" he said, barely above a whisper. "*That the party of the second part acknowledges the peril herein described, and accepts it willingly, for the alternative is the death of the god, which death shall unmake all prayer ever answered, all miracle ever wrought, and all soul sustained thereby.*"

"Sustained thereby," Sable repeated softly. "Do you understand what that means? Every soul in those vaults. Every soul ever bought or sold or blessed through the guild's trade. Nine hundred years of impossible things, all of them standing on this one foundation. We don't get to choose between freeing Wren and keeping the world safe. The contract was written so that those are the same act. Either we void it and unleash a god with nine centuries of grievance—or someone destroys the deed, and everything built on it comes down with it. Including her." She nodded upward, toward the place where Wren's light waited. "Including every soul in the vaults. Possibly including every soul, period. The founders weren't sure. Neither am I."

Cyrus sat very still. Sable had seen men receive mortal wounds with more movement.

"And Ashkaval holds the original deed," he said at last.

"Held. Until Ossen gave us the registry." She allowed herself a thin smile. "Congratulations, bone-carver. You're carrying the deed to a god under your arm, and the most dangerous man in the world wants it back. That's why the hunters won't stop. That's why he'll burn every free city between here and the sea. It was never about legitimacy. Legitimacy is what you tell the mob. The registry is the *throne*."

He put his head in his hands. For a long moment the only sounds were the water and his breathing—careful, controlled, the breathing of a man holding a door shut against something enormous.

"Wren chose this," he said finally, into his palms. "In the vault. She told me. She chose to be sold, to shield me from a debt I didn't know I owed. She knew. Somehow she knew what the contract was, and she tied herself to it anyway, because as long as she hung in that vault, the contract had a face. A reason to be honored." He lifted his head. His eyes were wet and furious and terribly calm. "So if I break the contract to free her, I also break the thing she sacrificed herself to protect. And if I honor it, she hangs in the dark forever."

"Yes," Sable said. She didn't soften it. Softening was for forgeries; the truth deserved plain timber.

"And the god?"

"Is awake. Angry. And about to learn that the people who chained it for nine hundred years are sitting in its own mines, holding the key." She gathered the birchbark scraps and squared them. "There's one more thing. Item the Ninth. Possession constitutes authority entire. Which means the contract can't just be voided by tearing a page. It has to be *discharged*—the debt paid, the terms fulfilled, by the authority of the deed-holder. By you, Cyrus. Or surrendered to Ashkaval, and then by him. One of you is going to hold a god's leash by the end of this. The other is going to be holding nothing at all."

Cyrus stood. He stood the way he did everything now, heavily, like a man wading upstream. At the ladder he paused, one hand on the rung, and looked back at her.

"Ossen told me I was never invisible," he said. "I think I finally understand what he meant. Nobody who carries this book gets to be invisible. Not even for a day."

Then he climbed up toward the gray light, toward the flickering soul of the woman he loved, and Sable sat alone with the registry and the candle and the mountain-mouth sigil staring up at her from the embossed page like an eye that had finally opened.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, she permitted herself one long shudder—the private earthquake of a woman who had just read the end of the world in eleven pages of merchant-script—and then she smoothed her face into its working expression, because upstairs there was a man who needed her to have a plan, and forging hope was the oldest trade she knew.

But as she climbed the ladder, one thought followed her up through the stone, patient as the dripping water:

Ashkaval didn't know they had the deed yet. And there was exactly one chance—one narrow, closing window—between the moment he learned it and the moment he stopped sending hunters and started sending armies.

They would have to go to him first.
