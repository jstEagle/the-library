## 18. Audience with Ashkaval

The herald's summons came at dusk, written on vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, sealed with the tithe-mark that had burned itself into the guildhall door a season ago. Cyrus read it twice by candlelight while Maren watched from across the table, her hands folded, her throat scarred where her voice had gone to feed a god.

*Ashkaval, Third of That Name, Heir of the Mountain, Sovereign of the Answered Prayer, commands the presence of the Keeper of the Registry, bearing the master ledger entire, at the court beneath the mountain. Refusal will be answered as refusal has always been answered.*

"He knows you have it," Maren signed. Her hands were quick and precise; Cyrus had learned to read them the way he once read bone. "Ossen died for it. Now he wants an audience."

"Then I'll give him one." Cyrus set the letter down. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Seven days out from Hesk's grave in the shrine-shadow, seven days of walking with the registry wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart, and his hands had finally stopped shaking. "A bookkeeper grants audiences all the time. It's called an audit."

Sable laughed from the corner where she sat mending a cloak with thread pulled from its own hem—a forger's habit, making something pay for itself. "You're going to walk into the usurper's court and *audit* him."

"I'm going to stall him." Cyrus looked at each of them in turn: Sable with her needle, Maren with her silent hands, the cartographer's door still faintly warm behind them where it had spat them into the footholds of the old gods. "Maren needs time to reach the god-vaults. The vaults are beneath the throne—that much the stone giantess told us, and she doesn't lie, she only sleeps. So I stand in front of Ashkaval and I talk. Ledgers. Tithes. Provenance. The most boring things in the world, delivered by the most boring man alive. No one interrupts an auditor. It's the one law even kings obey."

"And when he tires of you?" Sable asked.

"Kings don't tire of ledgers. They *need* them." Cyrus touched the oilcloth bundle through his shirt. "He can't legitimize the conquest without the registry. Every soul-contract, every oath-deed, every impossible thing this guild ever sold—he needs my signature under his name or his claim is just another warlord's. He'll wait. He'll listen. He'll ask me to explain an entry from two hundred years ago, and I'll explain it slowly, and then he'll ask about another one."

Maren's hands moved, hesitant now. *And what will you pay?*

Cyrus had known this question was coming since the night he'd made the offer, and he had not let himself think it through, because thinking it through was how courage died. He thought it through now.

"The Mountain Below is waking," he said. "When it wakes fully—when Maren gives it the last of what she carries—it will answer every prayer in the world at once. Every voice, every plea, every dead man singing. I hear the dead through ivory and bone. What do you suppose happens to a man who hears *everything*, all at once, forever?"

Sable's needle stopped.

"I trade him my hearing," Cyrus said. "My gift. The one thing in me that's worth anything to a man who rules by answered prayers. A soul-hearer, given up freely, witnessed before the court—I'd wager there are precedents in the registry. There are precedents for everything. That's rather the point of it."

"You'd be deaf," Sable said. "To them. To Wren."

"To everything." He said it lightly, the way Ossen had taught him to say hard things: flat, unadorned, a number entered in a column. "It's a fair price. Cheaper than some I've seen paid."

Maren crossed the room and took his face in her small scarred hands, and for a moment she simply looked at him—the look of someone who had already paid the price he was only now agreeing to, who had given a god her voice and lived on the far side of it. Then she signed against his chest, slow enough for him to follow:

*You are not invisible anymore. Remember that. Walk in there like a man who is seen.*

* * *

They went down through the drowned mines, because there was no other way. The cartographer had drawn them a road that ended at a flooded shaft, and beyond the water the way belonged to the mountain's oldest veins, tunnels dug before the free cities had names, before the guild, before perhaps anything. The water was black and utterly still, and their lanterns did not reflect in it so much as sink into it.

Cyrus carried the registry. Maren walked beside him with her jar of gathered silence—the last of her voice, hoarded in glass the way misers hoard salt. Sable had refused to come further. "Forgeries work best at a distance," she'd said, and pressed a folded paper into Cyrus's hand: a letter of introduction, perfect in every particular, naming him *Lior Vess, Under-Auditor Extraordinary, Guild of Impossible Things, empowered to negotiate conveyance of the master registry.* It was true except for the name, and names, Sable said, were the cheapest things to lie about and the first things kings checked.

At the last gallery before the court, the water ended and the air began to hum. Not sound exactly—pressure, the way a struck bell keeps ringing in the teeth long after the ear gives up. The walls here were veined with gold that moved, very slowly, like sap. And everywhere, hanging in niches cut into the living rock, were the lanterns.

Souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They hung in the vault-galleries above and below and receding into dark beyond counting, each one a soft gold light with a flicker at its heart like a struggling moth. Some burned steady. Some guttered. One, near the path, flared wildly as Cyrus passed, straining toward him, and he felt it the way he felt all the dead—not words, not yet, but a pressure, a reaching, a *knowing me*.

He did not stop. If he stopped he would never move again.

Maren caught his sleeve and pointed down the final corridor, where the gold-veined rock opened into a vastness that swallowed their lantern-light whole. Somewhere in that darkness sat a throne, and on the throne sat a man, and behind the man, filling the cavern wall like a mountain seen through a window, slept—or half-slept, or raged in dreams too deep to wake—the eldest god.

*I'll find the vaults,* Maren signed. *Count three hundred breaths after you begin. Then count again. Stall him until the mountain wakes.*

"How will I know?"

Her hands hesitated. Then: *You'll know. The dead will sing.*

She kissed his cheek—her mouth cool and silent—and turned down a side gallery where the lanterns burned thickest, and Cyrus Galloway stood alone at the threshold of the court beneath the mountain, holding a forged letter and a stolen ledger, about to commit the largest fraud of his life: pretending to be nobody, in front of everyone.

He walked in.

* * *

The throne room was not a room. It was a cavern the size of a cathedral's ambition, its ceiling lost in darkness where more lanterns hung like constellations, its floor of black stone polished by centuries of kneeling. Courtiers lined the walls—thin men and women in ash-grey silk, their faces smooth as eggs, their eyes fixed on nothing. Servants of the tithed cities, Cyrus guessed. Or the tithe-marked. It was hard to tell where obedience ended and ownership began.

And on the throne of veined gold sat Ashkaval, Third of That Name.

Cyrus had expected a monster. What he saw was a man of perhaps fifty, broad and grey-templed, dressed plainly in black, with the tired hands of someone who had done real work once and resented having stopped. Only his eyes gave him away: they were the color of the flooded mine-water, black and still, and they did not reflect the lanterns any more than the water had.

"You are not Lior Vess," Ashkaval said. His voice filled the cavern without effort, the way the god's hum filled it. "Vess died eleven years ago. I collected his debts myself."

Cyrus bowed, low and unhurried, the bow of a man with nothing to fear because he had already priced everything. "No, Your Radiance. Vess is dead. But the guild's authority isn't, and authority is transferable—which Your Radiance knows better than anyone, since that is rather the basis of your claim." He straightened and produced Sable's letter. "I am the Under-Auditor Extraordinary. The name on the letter is a formality. Names are the cheapest things to lie about. You'll find that entry in the registry too."

Something moved at the corner of the usurper's mouth. It might have been the ancestor of a smile.

"The registry," Ashkaval said. "You brought it."

"I brought it." Cyrus did not produce it. "Before I surrender the master ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things to the Heir of the Mountain, standard procedure requires an audit of the receiving party. Chain of custody, Your Radiance. Provenance. If I simply hand you the book and your claim later fails, the guild's liability is—" he paused, savoring it— "*unlimited.*"

The egg-faced courtiers stirred. One of them made a small sound, quickly suppressed, that might have been horror or delight.

"Audit," Ashkaval repeated.

"Of course, if Your Radiance prefers to simply take the book, we can proceed that way instead. Seizure voids warranty. Anything goes wrong afterward—any prayer unanswered, any mandate cracked—the failure is yours alone, unwitnessed, undocumented." Cyrus let the silence stretch. "Whereas a properly audited conveyance protects us both."

For a long moment the only sound was the hum of the sleeping god, and the soft, terrible fluttering of ten thousand souls in their niches.

"Sit," Ashkaval said at last, gesturing to a stool beside the throne. "Auditor. Begin."

And Cyrus began.

He opened the registry on his knees between them, and he talked. He talked about the guild's founding contract and the three clauses no one had ever enforced. He talked about a disputed oath from the reign of Ashkaval the First, and the precedent it set, and the counter-precedent that complicated it. He explained interest calculations on borrowed courage. He described, in exhaustive detail, the difference between a soul sold and a soul pawned, and why the distinction mattered enormously to anyone planning to hold one long-term. The courtiers' eyes glazed. Time in the cavern was hard to measure, but Cyrus counted his breaths as Maren had taught him, and at three hundred he began counting again, and at six hundred Ashkaval raised a hand.

"You are stalling."

"Your Radiance," Cyrus said, "stalling is merely auditing performed at a defensible pace."

This time the smile arrived in full, and it was worse than a monster's would have been, because it was patient. "Then let us skip to the entry that interests me. The one dated tomorrow." The black eyes fixed on him. "In your own hand, Auditor. Your own name. You've read it. Everyone who touches that ledger reads their own page eventually. What did yours say?"

Cyrus's blood went cold and then, strangely, warm. Because he had read it, months ago in Vellmark, the entry that had started everything: *Cyrus Galloway — one hearing, freely given — received.* Dated tomorrow.

"It says," Cyrus heard himself reply, "that I am the payment. Witnessed, Your Radiance, by the same sigil that seals your own deed. Which means if you accept me, the Mountain Below accepts me—and the god is party to the contract whether you like it or not." He leaned forward over the open ledger, a small grey man offering himself up like a line item. "So. Shall we conclude the conveyance? I believe the term is *payment rendered.*"

Ashkaval reached out—and the whole cavern shuddered.

Far below, deeper than the vaults, deeper than the drowned mines, something vast turned over in its sleep. The gold veins in the walls pulsed. The lanterns flared all at once, thousands of them, straining upward like flames in a draft—and through the roar of pressure in his skull, through the humming of the eldest god beginning to open one ancient eye, Cyrus heard it rising up through the black stone, through the soles of his feet, through the bone-carver's hands that had listened their whole life:

The dead were singing.

Maren had reached the vaults.
