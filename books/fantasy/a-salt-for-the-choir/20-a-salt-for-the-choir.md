## 20. A Salt for the Choir

The mountain was still shaking when Cyrus climbed out of the vault of lanterns.

The stair behind him had been stone once; now it was something more like a throat, ribbed and warm and breathing in slow tides. Dust fell upward. Somewhere far below, the Mountain Below was stretching limbs it had not moved in a thousand years, and the whole world above the vaults groaned like the hull of a ship coming off a reef.

Cyrus held the master registry against his chest with both arms, the way Ossen had pressed it into them in the burning guildhall. The leather was scorched at one corner. It smelled of ash and old ink and, faintly, of whale oil—the same smell as every ledger he had ever balanced, every bone he had ever carved. His hands were steady. He noticed that with a kind of distant surprise, the way he noticed everything now: half through his ears, which were failing him, and half through his skin, which had begun to listen instead.

Maren had gone down into the god-vaults an hour ago—or a year ago; time moved strangely in the deep places—and she had not come back up. She had given her voice entirely to wake the sleeping god, all that remained of it after her own shrine had taken the first portion, and Cyrus understood, with the certainty of a man reading a column of figures that finally sums, that she was not coming back up as the woman who went down.

He climbed anyway. Because Wren's light had rained free with all the others. Because somewhere above him, in the world of weather and bread and morning, a soul was falling home into a body that had waited seven years empty.

And because Hesk had died on a mountainside making him promise—not to save Wren, not to save himself, but this: *You will not trade her freedom for your own life.* And Cyrus had a sick, cold suspicion, born in the marrow the way all his certainties were, that the mountain was about to offer him exactly that trade.

The stair opened onto a gallery of black basalt, and the gallery opened onto the court.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain had been built by men who wanted to impress a god and ended by imprisoning one. Its pillars were carved with bargains—Cyrus could read them now without touching, the god-sigils glowing faintly in the dark like banknotes left too long in a drawer. Generations of Ashkaval's ancestors had stood in this hall and sworn oaths to the eldest god, and then, contract by contract, clause by clause, had bound the god's freedom into their own legitimacy. Every crown Ashkaval wore was a lien. Every army that marched under his banners marched on credit drawn against a prisoner.

Ashkaval himself stood at the center of the hall, alone.

That was the first thing Cyrus truly saw about him. No guards. No heralds. No courtiers. The usurper was a tall man in plain iron, graying at the temples, with the face of someone who had done arithmetic all his life and never once liked the total. He was looking up—at the ceiling, where cracks had begun to spider outward from a single point, and through those cracks came light that was not torchlight, not daylight, but the slow gold of something vast opening its eyes.

"You're the bookkeeper," Ashkaval said, without turning. "I can smell the ledgers on you."

"I'm the bookkeeper," Cyrus agreed. His voice sounded strange in his own ears—thin, half-swallowed. He was going deaf. He had known it since the gods began answering prayers in unison, since the singing of the dead had cracked something delicate in him forever. He raised his voice slightly, the way one does in a wind.

"Then you know what's happening." Ashkaval turned. Up close his eyes were ordinary brown, tired, human. That was somehow worse than if they had burned. "My patron is awake. My mandate is a stack of paper in your arms. And my armies—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the world, toward Vellmark and the free cities and the columns of smoke Cyrus had walked through, "—are marching on the strength of that paper. Tell me, Galloway. What happens to an army when its debt is called?"

"It falls," Cyrus said. "Armies are just promises wearing armor."

Something flickered across the usurper's face—not anger. Recognition. "Yes," he said quietly. "That's exactly what they are. I've known it for thirty years. Do you know why I want your registry? Not for power. Power I have. I want it because it's the only honest account of what this world owes and to whom. Every soul-contract. Every oath. Every prayer ever sold. I looked at my own deed once—all those clauses binding the Mountain Below—and do you know what I felt?"

"Guilt," said Cyrus.

"Fear," said Ashkaval. "Guilt is for men who think they can still pay." He stepped closer, and the light from the cracking ceiling caught the iron at his shoulders. "But that's not why we're here, you and I. We're here because the god is awake, and awake, it will need an anchor. A point of contact in the world of the living. Someone to hold the weight of every answered prayer, so the answering doesn't tear the world open like wet cloth."

The hall shuddered. Dust sifted down through the golden light. And Cyrus felt it then—not heard it, *felt* it, rising through the soles of his boots, through the bones of his legs, into the hollow places where the dead had always spoken to him:

*Bookkeeper.*

The voice did not come through his ears at all. It came through the registry itself, through the vellum and the ink, through seven years of guilt and twenty years of small careful anonymity. It was old the way bedrock is old. It was patient the way debt is patient.

*I have slept a thousand years inside other people's bargains,* said the Mountain Below. *Every prayer answered in this age has passed through me like water through a locked mill. Now the lock is broken, and the water is everywhere, and it will drown the living unless there is a hand upon the sluice.*

"What are you saying?" Cyrus asked, though he knew. He had known since the stair. His heart was doing something complicated and painful in his chest, and beneath the pain was a temptation so clean and enormous it made his teeth ache.

*Choose,* said the god. *Become the anchor. Hold the sluice. Every prayer in the world will pass through you, and you will hear them all—you who already hear the dead. You will be the salt in the choir, the note under every song, and no prayer will go unanswered again, and no soul will hang in any vault while you keep the books.*

"And the cost?" Cyrus whispered.

*Your ears first,* said the god, gently, terribly gently. *Then your solitude. Then your self, a little at a time, the way a river wears a channel. You will become the answering. You will not remember being the answerer.*

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Cyrus had ever not-heard.

And in that silence, unbidden, unkillable, Wren rose in him. Not her face—he had traded that memory to the cartographer's door, paid it gladly, and grieved it every night since—but the shape of her presence, the way she used to hum off-key while she worked, the specific warmth of her shoulder against his in a cold room. Seven years he had believed her dead. Seven years he had carried the sale like a stone swallowed whole. And now her light was falling home through the cracked ceiling of the world, and she would wake mortal and changed and remembering nothing, and she would need—

She would need someone who was *there*. Not a god. Not a sluice-hand for the prayers of ten thousand strangers. A man. Half-deaf. Ordinary. Holding a ledger.

*You will not trade her freedom for your own life.* Hesk's last words, collected like a coin, spent now at the exact moment they were minted for.

"No," Cyrus said.

The word fell into the hall and rang there.

*No?* The god's attention settled over him like snowfall—vast, unhurried, neither offended nor surprised. *Few refuse me twice.*

"You asked once before," Cyrus said. "Seven years ago. Through a sigil on a contract. You witnessed the sale of Wren's soul, and I think—I've thought for a long time—that you were testing whether anyone would come for her. Whether anyone would climb down into the dark and try to settle an account no one else even knew existed." He lifted the registry higher against his chest. "I refused then, too. I just didn't know it yet."

*And what do you offer instead, little bookkeeper?*

"This." He held out the master registry with both hands, arms trembling now, not from weight but from the enormity of letting go. "Every contract. Every oath. Every soul-deed, including yours—including the oldest one, the one that sold you your own freedom. Take it. Settle your own accounts. You're awake now; you don't need a middleman, and you never should have had one. Let the world owe *you* directly, and let the world hear *your* terms. But don't pour yourself through one person again. That's how Ashkaval happened. That's how all of this happened—one anchor, one chokepoint, one pair of hands holding what should belong to everyone."

The god was silent for a long moment. Above them, the cracks in the ceiling widened, and golden light poured through in widening sheets, and somewhere very far away Cyrus felt—as a tremor in his failing ears, as a pressure behind his eyes—the sound of armies stopping.

Not dying. Stopping. Men lowering spears and looking at each other with the sudden bewildered clarity of sleepers waking mid-march, asking themselves, *why am I here, whose war is this, what was I promised?* Mandates dissolving like salt in rain. The tithe-mark on the guildhall door fading to a scar, then to nothing.

*You understand,* said the Mountain Below, *that if I take this, I will void the oldest contract. I will free myself. And a freed god answers prayers according to its own judgment, not its jailers'. Some will be answered cruelly. Some will be answered wrong. The drowned child returned wrong—that was my jailers' work, but mine will make mistakes too. You are offering the world a wilder mercy than it has known.*

"Yes," said Cyrus. "Freedom usually is."

*And you give this freely? Your guild's whole inheritance? Ossen died for these pages.*

"He died telling me I was never invisible," Cyrus said, and his voice broke on it, and he let it break. "Invisible men hide things. I'm done hiding. Take it. Please. Before I'm tempted again, because I will be, every day, forever—I'll want to be the sluice, I'll want to hold every prayer in my two hands and fix the world one answer at a time, and I'd lose myself doing it, and there's a woman waking up right now who needs me to stay *found*."

The registry left his hands.

It did not fall. It rose—slowly, page by page, the covers opening like wings, and every contract in two hundred years of the Guild of Impossible Things lifted out of the binding and hung in the golden air, thousands of them, a blizzard of vellum and ink and sealed souls, turning slowly around the pillar of light. Cyrus watched Wren's deed spin past him, the god-sigil flaring bright and then, gently, going dark—witness discharged, debt dissolved. He watched the oldest contract rise highest of all, generations of clauses peeling off it like bark, until only a single line remained, and the line read, in a hand older than writing:

*Paid in full.*

The Mountain Below laughed. It was not a sound Cyrus heard so much as one he became briefly part of—a joy in the bedrock, a settling of shoulders, a thousand-year exhale moving through the roots of the world. The pillars of the court stopped looking like prison bars and started looking like trees.

*Well kept, bookkeeper,* said the god. *Go up. Go be small. It is the harder office, and rarer.*

* * *

He woke on the mountainside, three days later, in the smell of pine and woodsmoke.

Sable told him afterward that he had walked out of the mines on his own feet, guided by Maren—or what remained of Maren, who no longer spoke at all but whose hands had learned, in the dark of the god-vaults, a language of light. Sable had carried him the last mile. Hesk's body they had buried at the shrine where he fell, with a promise-marker on the cairn that read, in the collector's own shorthand, *All debts honored.*

The armies had stopped. That was the word traveling down the refugee roads like fire along a fuse: *stopped, stopped, stopped.* Soldiers wandering home in ones and twos, their officers' warrants blank, the usurper's banners suddenly just cloth. Ashkaval himself, they said, had abdicated in a single sentence written on the door of his own empty throne room: *The account is closed.* No one knew where he had gone. Cyrus suspected he had finally found a ledger he couldn't balance and had done the only honest thing left.

Cyrus sat up slowly. The world was very quiet. Not silent—he could still hear wind, and Sable humming tunelessly over the cookfire, and birds, distantly, like voices through a wall. But the dead were gone from him. All of them. The choir had disbanded, or perhaps had simply moved on to a hall where he could no longer follow. For the first time since he was a boy pressing his ear to his grandmother's whalebone combs, Cyrus Galloway's head contained only his own thoughts.

He wept for that, a little, privately, facing the trees. And then he stopped, because Sable touched his shoulder and pointed down the slope.

Wren was standing at the edge of the camp.

She was thin, and pale as new birchwood, and dressed in a refugee's coat someone had given her, and she was looking at Cyrus with an expression he had imagined ten thousand times and never once gotten right: not recognition, not love, not gratitude. Curiosity. The frank, tilted-head curiosity of a woman meeting a stranger who seems, inexplicably, familiar.

"They said you carried me out," she called down. Her voice was rough from disuse. Off-key, somehow, even speaking. He loved it immediately and completely. "They said you climbed down into the mountain and carried me out."

"I did," Cyrus said. His half-deaf ears strained for the words, and got most of them.

"Why?"

He thought about lying. About some story, some forged memory Sable could have built for him, some gentle fiction. Instead he said, "Because I owed you. Seven years of interest. And I'm a bookkeeper—I always settle my accounts."

Wren considered this for a long moment. Wind moved through the pines. Far below, in the valleys, the free cities were lighting their evening lamps one by one, and in the high passes, if you knew how to look, the stones had begun, very faintly, to glow.

"Well," she said at last, and started up the slope toward him, awkward on legs that hadn't borne her in seven years, catching herself, laughing at herself, catching herself again. "Then I suppose you'd better teach me the ledger."

She reached him. She took his arm to steady herself, and her hand closed around his wrist, warm and alive and mortal, and through his skin—through touch alone, the only sense the gods had left him—Cyrus heard her heartbeat, clear and unmistakable, singing.

It was enough. It was, he understood, going to be enough for the rest of his life.

Behind them, unnoticed, at the mouth of the abandoned mine, something small and bright had begun to grow: a single shoot of green pushing up through the shattered stone, reaching for a sun it had never seen, the first sprout of a garden no one had planted yet.
