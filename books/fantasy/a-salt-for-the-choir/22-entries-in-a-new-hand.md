## 22. Entries in a New Hand

The ledger was new leather, still stiff as a winter hide, and it smelled of tannin and cedar and the faint smoke that clung to everything Cyrus Galloway owned now—everything in Vellmark, really, everything within a week's ride of the guildhall's bones. He had bought it from a stationer on Tallow Row who did not know his name and had not asked for it, which was itself a small miracle, a small mercy of the year that had passed. The man had wrapped the book in brown paper and said, *Good weight to this one. Will hold ink a long time.*

Cyrus had almost laughed. He knew better than anyone what a book could hold.

He sat at the window of the house on Lanternmaker's Lane—the house was Wren's, technically, inherited from an aunt whose name she was still learning to say without flinching—and he opened the ledger to its first page and let his pen hover above the ruling line. Morning light came through the glass at a slant, catching the dust motes, warming the paper to the color of old ivory. Below, the street was waking: a cart of spring onions, two children racing a hoop, the baker's boy hauling yesterday's ash to the midden pile. Ordinary sounds. He heard them at half their true volume now, as though the whole world spoke through a wall of wool, but he heard them, and hearing them had become a kind of prayer.

His right hand rested on the desk beside the ledger, palm down, fingers spread against the wood.

*Say it again,* he thought toward the grain. *Slowly this time.*

The desk answered—not in sound, not anymore, but in pressure, in the faint tremor of the wood against his skin, the way a plucked string is felt in the floorboards of the room above. Ossen's voice lived in the desk. Not all of him; only the words Cyrus had carved into its underside the night after the burning, when his hands shook too badly to hold a chisel steady and he had used a knife meant for bread. *You were never invisible.* Four words, cut into oak, and now whenever Cyrus laid his palm flat and listened with his skin instead of his ears, the dead guildmaster said them again, patient as rain.

It was not the same as before. Before, the voices had come unbidden, a choir always singing whether he wanted it or not; the ivory had merely been their loudest pulpit. Now the dead spoke only through touch, only when he asked, only slowly, word by word like a man hauling water up a deep well. Half-deaf, the physickers called it. Cyrus had stopped arguing about what to call it. The gods had answered him once, at the top of the world beneath the world, and their answering had taken the fine bright registers of his hearing as payment—or as gift; he had never decided which. What remained was enough. What remained was, he suspected, exactly as much as a man needed.

He dipped the pen. At the head of the first page, in his careful ledger-hand—the hand that had balanced the Guild of Impossible Things' accounts for fifteen years, every soul weighed and priced and entered—he wrote:

*Ledger of Gifts Returned. Begun the first day of Thawmonth, in the year after the mountain woke.*

Below it, the first entry:

*Item the first: To the widow Sarelle of Cooper's Yard, returned the last words of her son Tam, drowned off the Saltspit. Carved in whalebone, given freely. She wept. She thanked me. I did not charge her. — C.G.*

He sat back and read it twice. Fifteen years of entries, and never once had he written the words *given freely*. In the old ledgers everything had a price because everything had a price because the guild's oldest contract had made price the shape of the world itself—a god sold into bondage so that prayers could be bought and sold like salt. He had spent his life keeping those books, invisible behind them, telling himself that a ledger-keeper who only recorded was not responsible for what the numbers did. He knew better now. Everyone who had survived the mountain knew better. That was why the new guild—the guild in exile, meeting in Maren's cracked shrine and in rented rooms and once, memorably, in a hay barn while hail hammered the roof—had written its first new law in blood-warm ink: *No more soul-trade. Nothing that breathes shall be entered as debt.*

Some of the old members had walked out over that law. Hesk would have walked out too, Cyrus thought, and then smiled sadly at the thought, because Hesk had died before he ever got the chance to argue, buying time at a roadside shrine with a promise-collector's last trick, and his final collected promise still lived in Cyrus's chest like a swallowed coal: *that Cyrus will not trade Wren's freedom for his own life.* A promise kept. A promise that had nearly killed him, standing before Ashkaval with the registry under his arm and the usurper's eyes like coins. But kept.

A knock at the door below. Then, because knocking had never been quite enough between them, three sharp raps followed by two soft ones—Wren's code, invented in the second month, when she had grown tired of him not hearing her at the door.

He went down and let her in, and as always there was the half-second where he looked at her face and searched for something that was not there anymore and would never be there again, and then let it go, and loved what remained.

Wren, restored, mortal, changed. Her body had been found the way the Mountain Below had promised it would be—sleeping in a hospice bed in Vellmark, breathing, seven years older than the girl who had been sold, worn thin by whatever slow dream her flesh had dreamed while her soul hung lantern-bright in the vaults. She had woken with no memory of him at all. No memory of the sale, of the debt she had shielded him from, of the years they had loved each other in the guildhall's shadow when they were young and stupid and certain the world would make room for them. She remembered her aunt's house. She remembered her own name. Everything else was a page torn out, and the Mountain Below—who owed Cyrus a debt larger than any ledger could hold, and knew it, and was uncomfortable about it in the way that eldest gods are uncomfortable about gratitude—had refused to say what the torn page had held.

*She chose,* Wren's soul had told him through the bone in his pocket, in the dark beneath the mountain. *I chose. Do not carry my choice like a stone, Cyrus. Carry it like a seed.*

So he carried it like a seed. And he waited at her door, as he had promised himself he would, asking nothing, explaining nothing beyond what she asked to hear—which, over the long winter, had become more and more. She liked his stories of the guild. She liked the way he talked about bone, the reverence in it. Last week she had caught his hand turning a whalebone comb in the market and said, "You love things that remember," and he had nearly wept into the fishmonger's ice.

"You're up early," she said now, stamping snow off her boots in the doorway. She was carrying a sack of seed potatoes and a coil of twine, and there was dirt already under her fingernails, which meant she'd been to the nursery on Bellows Street before dawn like a woman smuggling contraband. "And don't tell me you weren't. There's ink on your finger."

"I'm always up early."

"You're always up early *now*," she corrected, hanging her cloak on the peg. "Ossen's letters say you used to sleep until the bells."

"He tells you that?"

"He tells everyone that." She grinned. It was a good grin, crooked, familiar in a way that made his chest ache pleasantly rather than painfully—she had not recovered his memories, but some things lived deeper than memory, in the body, in the tilt of a smile. "Your dead mentor gossips, Cyrus. Through the furniture. Maren says the shrine pews repeat everything you carve."

"Maren exaggerates."

"Maren has no voice and perfect hearing. Trust her." She set the potatoes down and looked at him with the expression she wore when she was deciding something. He had learned to wait through these silences. They were how she rebuilt herself, one decision at a time. "The garden," she said finally. "Over the guildhall. I want to start today. While the ground's still soft."

Cyrus looked at her. The ashes of the Guild of Impossible Things lay on a hill above the city, a black rectangle of foundation stones that the city had, in the year since the armies faltered and the mandate dissolved, quietly begun to treat as a monument. People left things there. Flowers, mostly, but also shoes, letters, a child's carved horse. The place where the impossible trade had died, and where the free cities had inexplicably not.

"It's ash-ground," he said. "Nothing grows well in ash the first year."

"Then we'll be the first thing." She picked up the potato sack and thrust it at his chest. "Take the other end. You owe me labor—I'm told you're rich in debts."

He took the sack. It was warm from her arms.

* * *

Maren stood before the cracked shrine of the Mountain Below in the high quarter of Vellmark, where the old temple district was slowly filling again with people who had nowhere else to put their hope, and taught forty strangers to sing with their hands.

Cyrus watched from the back, Wren beside him, both of them holding seed-dirty gloves in their laps. The shrine had been a granary until Thawmonth last year; the god's return had cracked its eastern wall open in a single night, splitting the stone along a seam of white quartz that now ran floor to ceiling like a vein of light, and the granary's owners had donated the building with the particular speed of merchants who had heard what happened to those who haggled with waking gods.

Maren could not speak. Her god had taken her voice as the price of her prayer—one voice for ten thousand answered, she had said once, in writing, with a shrug that forgave everything—and so she had built a language out of the wreckage. Her hymns were signed, not sung: both hands rising like wings for the opening line, palms turning outward for the verse of waking, fists over the heart for the verse of debt forgiven. The congregation copied her clumsily, forty pairs of hands moving out of rhythm, and the sight of it made something tighten in Cyrus's throat. A choir with no sound. A salt for the choir, Ossen would have said, and laughed at his own joke, and been right.

Halfway through the third hymn, Maren paused. Turned. Found Cyrus at the back of the room with the unerring accuracy of a woman who had once been deaf in all the ways that mattered and now missed nothing.

She beckoned.

He went forward through the kneeling congregation, Wren trailing, and Maren took his hands in hers—her hands were strong, a stonemason's daughter's hands—and signed directly into his palms, letter by letter, the private shorthand they had developed over the winter. *Show them,* she signed. *The carving. Show them what listening looks like.*

"They won't understand it."

*They understood nothing six months ago either. Look at them now.* Her eyes were bright and impatient. *You are the only man alive who has spoken with the Mountain Below and lived polite about it. They need to see that the god answers touch. That it isn't only me.*

So Cyrus knelt at the front of the shrine, before the quartz seam, and laid both palms flat against the cold stone, and closed his eyes, and listened with his skin.

The god was dreaming. He felt it immediately, the way you feel a sleeping animal in a dark barn—the vast slow respiration of something enormous just beneath the surface of the world. Since the vault had shattered and the Mountain Below had woken angry, then woken free, then woken—gradually, with Maren's help—something gentler, the god had slept most days, digesting a captivity of centuries. But it dreamed toward the surface sometimes. It dreamed toward the shrines. And when Cyrus touched the stone, the dream leaned toward him like a great cat acknowledging a known hand.

*LEDGER-KEEPER,* the stone said, in pressure and warmth and the faint taste of iron at the back of his teeth. *YOU HAVE THE BOOK WITH YOU.*

"I do," Cyrus murmured. Behind him he heard the congregation go very still.

*READ ME THE FIRST ENTRY.*

He hesitated. Then he drew the new ledger from his coat, opened it on the quartz seam, and read aloud—half-deaf, too loud, uncaring—in his flat accountant's voice: "To the widow Sarelle of Cooper's Yard, returned the last words of her son Tam, drowned off the Saltspit. Given freely."

The silence afterward was total. Even the street noise outside seemed to hold its breath.

Then the quartz seam warmed under his palms, and a tremor moved through the floor, gentle as a purr, and every person in the shrine felt it—felt the god acknowledge the entry, felt the oldest contract in the world being rewritten one gift at a time. Several of them began to cry. One woman signed the wing-hands of the opening hymn, alone, trembling, and then another joined her, and then forty pairs of hands rose together in silence, and Cyrus knelt among them with his forehead nearly touching the stone and let himself be part of a choir for the first time since the mountain.

Afterward, outside, Wren slipped her arm through his. "You're crying," she said.

"The wind," he said.

"There's no wind."

"There's always wind somewhere."

She laughed, and squeezed his arm, and did not press, and he loved her for the thousandth time for knowing exactly when not to ask. But at the bottom of the hill, as they turned toward the ash-field above the old guildhall, she stopped, and looked at him with the sun full on her face, and said quietly: "One day you'll tell me what I chose. Won't you? When I'm ready."

"When you're ready," he said. "Not before. Seed, not stone."

"I don't know what that means."

"You will," he said. "It's in the ledger."

* * *

The ash-ground took the potatoes badly and the herbs well, and by midsummer the hill above the ruined guildhall was a ragged green quilt that the neighborhood children had already claimed as territory. Cyrus and Wren worked it every morning that wasn't raining. She directed; he dug. She had discovered she was imperious about gardens, and he had discovered he liked being ordered around by her, and neither of them examined this too closely.

On the last day of summer, they planted the tree.

It was a rowan, grown from a cutting Maren had brought down from the high passes—from the foothold where the stone giantess still half-slept, her shoulders become hills, her breathing weather. The cutting had rooted in Maren's shrine in a pot of broken granite, and when Maren pressed it into Cyrus's hands she had signed, with great ceremony: *Plant it where the books burned. Gods grow back from ash faster than men do. Give them a head start.*

They dug the hole together in the footprint of the old archive, where Ossen had died shielding the master registry, where the heat had been hot enough to fuse the ledger-leather to the stone. Cyrus's spade struck something at the depth of a forearm. He knelt and brushed the soil away and lifted out a scrap of fused brass—the corner plate of a shelf, warped into a wave-shape by the fire, stamped with the guild's old mark: the impossible thing, the knot that ties and unties itself.

He held it a long moment. Then he set it in the bottom of the hole, beneath the rowan's roots, where it belonged—an entry in the earth's own ledger, a debt buried so a gift could grow above it.

Wren packed the soil around the roots with her bare hands. When it was done they sat side by side in the grass, dirty to the elbows, watching the tree stand absurdly small against the sky.

"One day it'll be tall enough to see the sea," Wren said.

"Rowans don't get that tall."

"This one will. It's got an impossible thing buried under it." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Tell me a story. One of yours. About the guild, before."

So he told her one—about the cartographer of places that don't exist, and the door that cost a memory, and the fool who paid it gladly. He changed nothing. He left his own name in it. She listened with her chin on her knees, and when he finished she was quiet for a moment, and then she said, "That fool sounds like someone worth waiting for."

"He's hoping so," Cyrus said.

That evening, home at the window on Lanternmaker's Lane, he opened the ledger of gifts returned and wrote the day's entries in the failing light, his pen scratching loud in his half-silence. *Item the forty-first: planted a rowan over the old archive... Item the forty-second: taught the widow Sarelle's grandson his letters, using carved bone... Item the forty-third:* — and here he paused, pen hovering, because the last entry of the day had happened on the walk home and he was not sure it fit any category the old guild would have recognized.

Wren had taken his hand at the corner of Bellows Street, in front of everyone, and laced her fingers through his, and said, "Tomorrow, the north bed. And bring your knife—that comb you're making, I want to watch you carve it. I want to watch you listen to it."

An invitation. An ordinary evening, requested in advance. A small life, asked for out loud.

He wrote: *Item the forty-third: was invited to stay. Accepted.*

Then, because the habit of fifteen years dies hard, he ruled the line beneath it, and dated it, and initialed it—*C.G.*—and sat looking at the page until the light failed. Somewhere under his palm the desk murmured its four words, patient as rain. *You were never invisible.* No, he thought. And never would be again. Not hiding in a ledger, not vanishing into a guild's shadow, not trading himself away piece by piece to powers that priced everything. Just a man at a window in a city that had held, with ink on his fingers and a garden going to seed and a woman downstairs humming tunelessly over the supper fire, waiting for him to come down.

He blew out the lamp and went down to her.

Outside, far to the north, beneath the hills where a giantess breathed in her sleep, the mountain kept its own new ledger now—kept it in quartz and root and the signed hymns of forty clumsy hands—and every gift freely given anywhere in the world was an entry in it. The accounts were settling. Slowly, entry by entry, the oldest debt in the world was being paid down by the smallest possible payments: a returned word here, a planted tree there, a hand taken at a street corner in front of everyone.

There would be hard years. There would be letters from the exile guild demanding guidance, shrines cracking open in cities that feared them, questions from Wren that would take all his courage to answer. But that was tomorrow's column. Tonight the ledger was balanced, the lamp was out, and Cyrus Galloway—bone-carver, ledger-keeper, once the invisible man of the Guild of Impossible Things—went down the stairs toward the light and the noise and the living, and stayed.
