## 1. The Date in the Margins

The rain came sideways off the Thornfell passes, the way it always did this time of year, and Jude Dunmore hated it with the particular, comfortable hatred of long acquaintance. Nine years walking these roads had taught her that the mountains did not want travelers. They tolerated them, the way an old dog tolerates children — patiently, until the moment it doesn't.

She stood at the head of the caravan with her hood down because she'd learned that too, that the hood did nothing and the rain on her bare scalp was at least an honest misery. Behind her, six wagons groaned under tarps of false wool and true salt-brandy, and eleven smugglers who trusted her more than they trusted their own mothers, which in Jude's experience was the only kind of trust worth anything.

"Road forks ahead," called Doss from the second wagon, a squat man with a voice like gravel in a bucket. "You sure about the high path, Dunmore?"

"I'm sure the low path has a toll gate on it," she said, "and the toll gate has Voss's colors on it, and the colors have inquisitors under them. So yes. High path."

Doss muttered something about the high path having weather on it instead, and Jude let him. Weather was honest. Weather only wanted to kill you; it didn't want to ask you questions first.

The Silent Tide had not asked her a question in nine years.

That was the thought that lived under her ribs like a swallowed stone. She was salt-sworn — had been since she was seventeen, since the night her whole village drowned in a storm that came from a sea with no storm in it, and she had stood on the bluff and screamed her grief at the water and the water had answered. Not in words. In stillness. The waves had gone flat and quiet around the drowned houses, and she had understood, in the way you understand things in dreams, that something vast had heard her and was sorry. She had sworn herself to it on the spot. Salt on her tongue, salt in her palm-cuts, salt in the name she took for her god: the Silent Tide, the god who listened.

And it had listened, for a while. Little answers. A flat calm when she needed a crossing. A fish shoal where there should have been none. Once — only once — a word, rising up through her bones like cold water through a boot: *wait.*

Then, nine years ago, nothing. Not silence the way a listener is silent. Silence the way a house is silent when everyone in it has died. She had prayed at every shrine, every waystone, every salt-pool from Brack to the coast, and the Tide had not so much as shifted a current for her. The other salt-sworn said the god was deep in its own affairs. The priests said faith was faith precisely when it went unanswered. Jude, being a practical woman, had mostly stopped praying, and had felt the stopping like an amputation that never quite finished hurting.

Tonight, though — tonight she had prayed. She didn't know why. Habit, maybe. Or the way the mountains had felt wrong all day, a pressure behind the weather, like a held breath. She'd crouched at the waystone at the pass mouth, wet her thumb with rain, touched it to her tongue, and said the old words: *Hear me, Tide. I'm coming down the mountain with eleven lives behind me. If you're still in there, tell me which road.*

Nothing. Rain, and rock, and the groan of the wagons.

She was still tasting the nothing when the waystation came into view.

* * *

It crouched in the saddle of the pass like a thing that had given up: a low stone building, roof half-caved, walls furred with lichen the color of old bruises. Smugglers called it the Widow's Rest, though no one living remembered why. It had been abandoned since before Jude started running the Thorn roads — or so every map and every rumor agreed.

But the front door was open.

Not broken. Open. Swung inward on oiled hinges, and that was wrong in a way that made the hair on Jude's arms lift. Oiled hinges meant use. Use meant people, and people in the Widow's Rest meant either smugglers who hadn't told her — which would be its own kind of insult — or something else.

"Hold here," she told Doss, and left the caravan in the lee of the rocks.

She went in with her knife out and her weight on the balls of her feet, rain dripping off her into the dark. Inside smelled of stone and old smoke and — she paused — of salt. Fresh salt. The floor of the main room was scattered with it, a thin crust, deliberate, laid down in lines. She knew those lines. Every salt-sworn did. Wards. Someone had warded this room, and recently, and the wards were the old kind, the kind that predated the free cities, the kind the priests said hadn't been drawn in a hundred years.

The lines converged on the hearth.

The hearthstone was raised, and under it was a cache — that much was smuggler's common sense, hearths were always caches — but this one was not a smuggler's cache. The stone came up on a bronze pivot, smooth as a well-hinge, and beneath it a lead-lined box sat in a hollow that had been cut with care and lined with oilcloth that was not yet rotted. Whatever this was, it had been hidden by someone who expected it to be found by the right person and never by the wrong one.

Jude looked at the box for a long moment. The rain hammered the broken roof. Behind her, the wards on the floor seemed, in the grey light, almost to glow.

"You're not mine," she said aloud, to the box, because saying things aloud to objects was a habit of a woman who'd spent nine years talking to a god that didn't answer. "You're not Doss's. You're not anybody's I know."

She opened it anyway. She was a smuggler, after all. Curiosity was the whole trade.

Inside, wrapped in waxed linen gone amber with age, was a book.

It was large — the size of a ship's chest-lid — and bound in something that was not leather. It was grey-white and faintly translucent at the edges, and when Jude's fingers brushed it, it felt warm, which no book had any business being in a cold cache under a cold mountain. Pressed into the cover was a sigil: a wave curled around a crown, the crown broken at its center.

Jude knew that sigil the way she knew her own hands. Every child in the salt villages knew it, though the priests had spent a century scraping it off doors and grinding it off coins. The mark of the drowned court. The mark of the covenant — the old bargain between the land and the sea beneath Thornfell, the one that had broken a hundred years ago in a night of storms, the one nobody would talk about except in whispers, the one whose breaking had ended the age of wonders and started the age of walls.

Her mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, sitting in a room full of rain.

She opened the book.

The pages were not paper. They were thin sheets of something like horn, like shell, and they were covered in writing that shifted as she looked at it — no, not shifted. *Resolved.* Letters that had been drifting like silt settled into place as her eyes touched them, as if the book had been waiting to decide what language to be. She read the first page. It was a chronicle. A list. A hundred years of entries, one to a year, in a hand that changed and changed again — dozens of hands, generations of hands, all writing the same kind of thing: small portents, small sorrows, the tide-marks of a world.

*Year ninety-one. The passes snow early. A child in Brack born with gills that close by morning.*

*Year ninety-four. The sea withdraws from the salt villages and does not explain itself.*

*Year ninety-nine.* — and here the writing changed, became cramped, urgent, pressed hard enough to score the page — *It is nearly done. We have hidden it as long as we can. The gods have gone silent to keep it. Forgive us.*

Jude turned the page, and the world narrowed to a point.

The entry for the hundredth year was not a chronicle.

It was a name.

*In the hundredth year, the Saltbride comes. She walks the Thorn roads with the dead on her back and the sea in her mouth. She was sworn to the Silent Tide and heard it go quiet. She carries the covenant's cost and does not yet know her own name in the bargain.*

And beneath it, in the same scored, desperate hand, as if the writer had been weeping or laughing:

*Jude Dunmore.*

Her own name. Not a name like hers — her name. The letters resolved under her eyes the way all the letters had, but these settled with a terrible finality, each stroke clicking into place like a lock turning. She read it again. And again. The rain filled the silence of the room, and her own breath sounded loud and stupid in her ears.

Below the name, one more line:

*She finds this book on the last night of the ninety-ninth year, in the Widow's Rest, in the rain.*

Jude looked up. Through the broken roof, through the door, she could see the light going out of the sky. She counted days the way smugglers counted coin, automatically, without thinking — and tonight was the last night of the ninety-ninth year. Tomorrow the century turned. Every city in the free coast would be lighting turn-of-the-year fires, and she had planned to be in Brack for it, drinking Ossa Vane's terrible brandy.

She looked back at the page. At the date. At her name.

"No," she said.

It came out steady, which surprised her. She said it again, louder, to the book, to the wards, to the god who wasn't listening. "No. I don't know who wrote this. I don't know who knew my name. But books don't — books don't *do* this."

The book, being a book, said nothing. But as she watched, the ink of the final line seemed to darken, to deepen, the way wet stone darkens, and she had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that it had not finished writing itself. That somewhere ahead of her were pages not yet resolved, waiting to settle around choices she hadn't made.

She should burn it. That was the sane thought, the smuggler's thought. Books that named you were books that owned you; every runner on the coast knew that proverbs were just epitaphs with better manners. She got as far as holding the cover near the dead hearth, as far as thinking about kindling.

She could not make her hands do it.

It wasn't reverence. It was the stone under her ribs, the nine-year silence, and one thought arriving with the weight of a wave: *the Tide went quiet nine years ago. This book says the gods went silent to hide something. What if those are the same silence?*

What if her god hadn't abandoned her? What if her god had been *hiding* — from her, for her, because of her — and this book was the first crack in the hiding?

She wrapped the Codex back in its linen, and her hands were shaking now, and she hated that. She was Jude Dunmore, and she had talked her way past three toll guards and a tax-farm and the widow of a pirate king, and her hands did not shake. She put the book inside her coat, against her chest, where the warmth of it sat like a second heartbeat.

That was when she heard the horses.

* * *

They came up the low path fast — too fast for weather, too fast for smugglers. She was out the door and waving the caravan into the rocks before the first torchlight broke the ridgeline, and by the time the riders drew up at the Widow's Rest, her people were shadows among boulders and the wagons were cold.

There were eight riders. Their torches burned blue-white, and Jude's stomach dropped through the wet stone of the mountain, because blue-white fire meant *chantry-work*, and chantry-work meant inquisitors. Not Voss's common soldiers, who could be bribed. Inquisitors of the Usurper's own commission, who could only be survived.

They dismounted at the waystation. Their leader — a tall figure whose armor was grey as the rain — stood in the doorway of the building Jude had just left, and even at two hundred paces Jude saw the leader's head turn, slowly, reading the room. Reading the wards. Reading the raised hearthstone with its hollow empty of everything but rain.

The tall figure went very still.

Then the blue-white torches began to go out, one by one, and the riders spread out into the rocks with the unhurried competence of hounds that have found the scent, and Jude pressed herself and the Codex into a seam of stone and understood, with a dread that came on like the tide, that the book had made her the hunted thing on this mountain — and that the hunt had already started.

Behind her, deeper in the rocks, she heard a sound she would remember for the rest of her life: someone else hiding in the dark, trying not to breathe, and failing.
