## 2. Inquisitors at the Salt Gate

The waystation at Salt Gate had been a smuggler's cache for longer than it had been anything else, and everyone in the caravan knew it except, apparently, the dead man who'd built the fire.

Jude Dunmore crouched at the edge of the treeline with her hood up and her breath held shallow, watching orange light climb the station's timber walls like something alive and hungry. The flames moved wrong for a kitchen fire — too fast, too eager, climbing sideways against the wind as though they'd been given a name to hunt and were impatient about it.

"Inquisitor work," said Maren beside her, and spat into the frost.

"I can see that."

"Can you? Then tell me why we're not already running."

Because of the book. Because of the date. Because nine hours ago she'd pried up a floorboard in that same burning building and found a codex bound in grey hide with her own name written in its margins alongside tonight's date, and she hadn't been able to make her legs carry her away from it until the sound of hooves came down the pass. She didn't say any of this. Maren was a good hand and a better friend, but names were currency on the Thornfell roads, and Jude had just learned hers was worth a pyre.

"The master's still inside," she said instead.

Caravan Master Hesk had gone in at dusk to settle accounts with the waystation keeper — which meant to drink, argue about tolls, and slip a few unmarked crates into the cellar cache. He'd been doing it for eleven years. He'd been doing it when the riders came: a dozen of them, black-lacquered armor under grey cloaks, each cloak pinned at the shoulder with a badge Jude knew from bad dreams and worse news — a crown above crossed waves, the sigil of Kaelen Voss, who called himself Warden of the Reaches and whose inquisitors called themselves nothing at all, because what they did didn't bear naming.

Hesk had come out with his hands up. They'd taken him anyway.

Now the station burned, and the keeper's family stood in the yard in their nightclothes while a tall figure in a bone-pale mask walked the line of them, asking questions Jude couldn't hear over the crackle but could imagine word for word. *Where is the book. Who has touched the book. When did you last pray, and to whom, and did it answer.*

"They'll work inward from the village," Maren murmured. "Then the cache cellars. We should be three ridges gone by dawn."

"We will be." Jude's eyes stayed on the cellar door, half-visible through smoke, still shut. "Give me ten minutes."

Maren turned and looked at her the way people look at someone they've decided not to argue with yet. "Ten minutes for what?"

"For Hesk."

"Hesk is in an inquisitor's cart, girl. You want to steal him back from Voss's own hounds with—" she gestured at the two of them, two knives and a mule "—*this*?"

"No," Jude said. "I want what he went in for. And then I'm going to feel less like a coward when I ride past his cart and do nothing."

Maren stared at her. Somewhere behind them, a beam collapsed in a gout of sparks, and neither of them flinched; they were salt-sworn, both of them, hired muscle for a god who never spoke, and you learned early that flinching cost time you wouldn't get back.

"You've been strange since Brindle Ford," Maren said finally. "Since before. Whatever you found in there—"

"Ten minutes," Jude said, and slid down into the dark.

* * *

Smoke lay along the ground like a second night. Jude went through it low and fast, counting her steps the way the Silent Tide's rites had taught her — or rather, the way the rites' ruins had taught her, because the Tide itself had been silent for nine years now, and a mercenary sworn to a silent god learns to count on herself alone.

Nine years ago she had prayed at a drowned shrine off the Greywater coast, the proper rite, salt in the palm and the old words in her mouth, and asked the god for one thing only: to know whether her brother had died quickly, out on the water, when the storm took his boat. The Tide had given her nothing. Not silence-with-comfort, not the warm blank that other gods offered their faithful. Just absence, vast and cold, like shouting into a well and hearing your own voice die. She'd kept the vows anyway. Habit, mostly. And the small stubborn hope that a god who ignored you might one day be sorry.

She reached the cellar door and put her shoulder to it. It gave with a groan she felt in her teeth.

Inside, the cache was exactly as she'd left it — crates of untaxed brandy, bundles of salted eel, a strongbox Hesk thought she didn't know the combination to — and above it all, the smoke was starting to seep down through the floorboards in grey ropes. She knelt where the loose board was, worked it up with her knife, and pulled out the Century Codex.

It looked no different by firelight than it had by lanternlight. Grey hide cover, cracked and sea-stiff. No title. But pressed into the leather at the center, so worn it was nearly invisible unless the light caught it sideways: a sigil. A circle broken by a descending line, like a crown sinking beneath a wave.

Her name was still in the margins. *Jude Dunmore.* Still dated tonight. Still followed by words in a script she couldn't read but that made her fingertips ache when she traced them, a deep-sea ache, pressure-ache, like the book remembered drowning and wanted to share.

She wrapped it in oilcloth, shoved it inside her coat, and was turning for the stairs when she heard the breathing.

It came from behind the brandy crates — wet, ragged breathing, the kind that had blood in it. Jude drew her knife and moved around the crates with her weight forward, and found a stranger crumpled in the corner between a cask and the wall.

Young, maybe. Hard to tell. Her face was a ruin of bruises and dried salt-crust, as if she'd been pulled out of the sea and beaten on the way. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, prison-style. She wore the remains of a fine coat, once grey silk, now stiff with something darker, and around her neck on a cord hung a small pendant: a circle broken by a descending line.

The same sigil as the book.

The stranger's eyes opened. They were pale grey-green, tidepool-colored, and they fixed on Jude with startling focus for someone half-dead.

"You have it," the stranger rasped. Her voice sounded like it had been underwater for years. "I can smell the pages. Give it—"

"Quiet." Jude glanced at the ceiling. Above them, boots crossed the burning floor. The inquisitors weren't leaving; they were searching the wreckage, which meant they'd find the cellar, which meant she had perhaps sixty heartbeats. "Who are you?"

The stranger laughed, and it turned into coughing, and the coughing brought red to her lips. "Does it matter? You're the one in the book." Her tidepool eyes flicked to Jude's coat, to the shape of the oilcloth bundle. "*Saltbride.*"

Every hair on Jude's arms rose. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No. You know exactly. That's why your hands are shaking." The stranger tried to push herself up the wall and failed, sliding back down with a sound that was mostly pain. "They took me off a wreck off Thornfell Point three days ago. Inquisitor Dane's hounds. They wanted me to read the sigil for them. I wouldn't. So they left me here to burn with the rest of the evidence." A ghost of a smile. "Sloppy of them."

Boots on the stairs. Two sets, heavy, unhurried.

Jude made a decision in the space of one heartbeat, the kind of decision she'd later understand had been made for her years ago, in margins written by hands long dead. She sheathed her knife, hauled the stranger's arm across her shoulders, and took the stranger's weight — more of it than there should have been, dense as driftwood — and went for the cellar's back wall, where the cache's smugglers' exit ran out into the root cellar of the keeper's house next door. Every waystation on the Thornfell roads had one. Hesk had shown her the first week, grinning: *the empire builds doors, love, and we build ways around them.*

Behind them, the cellar door burst open. Light bloomed. A voice like a blade being drawn slowly called out in the formal cadence of interrogation:

"In the name of Kaelen Voss, Warden of the Reaches — halt, and be counted."

Jude did not halt, and was not counted.

* * *

They came out through a tangle of onion beds into the cold clean night, and Jude got maybe forty paces before her legs informed her that carrying a drowned woman uphill through frost was beyond their contract. She lowered the stranger behind a drystone wall, checked the fires behind them — the waystation was fully involved now, a bonfire visible from three valleys, a message written in flame for anyone who knew how to read it — and looked down at her burden.

The stranger had passed out. Or was pretending to. With her, Jude suspected, the distinction would always be negotiable.

"Maren's going to kill me," she told her, and whistled, low, twice — the caravan's signal for *trouble, come quiet.*

Maren came quiet. She took one look at the stranger, one look at Jude's face, and one look at the smoke, and said, "That's not ten minutes."

"It was ten minutes. The extra ones weren't my fault."

"Whose are they?" Maren crouched, prodded the stranger's ruined coat, and went very still. "Jude. This is court silk. Drowned-court silk. Where in all the hells—"

"Off a wreck, she says. Inquisitors had her." Jude hesitated. Everything in her wanted to keep the Codex private a little longer, the way you keep a wound covered. But Maren had just helped her steal a prisoner out from under Voss's hounds; she'd earned the truth, or some of it. "There's a book, Mare. Out of the cache. It has my name in it. Tonight's date. And it has this woman's sigil on the cover."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. The firelight painted half her face orange. "Your name."

"My name."

"And the date."

"Tonight."

Maren sat back on her heels. She was fifty, had buried two husbands and one company, and did not spook easy, but Jude watched the color leave her face anyway, watched her mouth form a word she didn't say aloud. Everyone on these roads knew what a named date in a prophecy-book meant. Everyone knew what happened to places where such books were found. The waystation behind them was the current answer to that question.

"We ride for Brack," Maren said at last, flat and fast, decision-mode, sergeant-mode. "Ossa Vane owes you, and the Saltcloaks are the only wall in the free cities thick enough to hide behind. We take the high smuggler road past Widow's Tooth, we say nothing to anyone, and you show that book to no one until Ossa sees it. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And her?" Maren jerked her chin at the stranger.

Jude looked down. The stranger's eyes were open again, watching them both with that unnerving stillness, and when she spoke her voice was barely a thread, but it carried.

"Brack won't hold," she said. "Nothing holds once they start reading. I've seen it." She coughed, wet and long. "My name is Tam. And before you ask — yes, the sigil is mine. My mother's line. The drowned court beneath Thornfell." She smiled with bloody teeth. "Which makes me either the most valuable thing you've ever stolen, Jude Dunmore, or the reason you die. Possibly both."

The name landed like a stone in still water. Jude felt the ripples go out through everything she'd believed about the last nine hours — about the book, the date, the margin that bore her name in a dead language's handwriting. Prophecies didn't usually come with witnesses. Witnesses didn't usually come wearing the sigil of a court every mapmaker swore was legend.

"How do you know my name?" she asked.

Tam's tidepool eyes closed. "You're in the book," she whispered. "I told you. I've read it. I read it a hundred years ago, in a library that's under the sea now." A pause, and then, softer, almost tender, almost cruel: "You should sleep sometime soon, Saltbride. Tomorrow starts costing."

Behind them, the waystation's roof fell in, and the sparks went up like a swarm of dying stars, and somewhere down the valley an inquisitor's horn blew three notes — the signal for *found*, or for *hunted*, Jude couldn't tell which, and suspected it didn't matter anymore.

She got Tam's arm over her shoulders again, nodded to Maren, and started up toward Widow's Tooth with a prophecy against her ribs and a drowned heir's blood soaking into her sleeve, and understood, with the first real clarity of her life, that the Silent Tide's nine-year silence had never been absence at all.

It had been a held breath.

And tonight, something had begun to exhale.
