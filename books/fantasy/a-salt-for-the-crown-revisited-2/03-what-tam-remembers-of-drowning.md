## 3. What Tam Remembers of Drowning

The boat smelled of tar and old fish and the particular sourness of a man who had been dead for six hours before the tide gave him back.

Tam lay in the bilge of the smuggler's skiff with her cheek against wet planking and listened to Jude Dunmore row. The oars creaked in their locks with a rhythm so steady it might have been a heartbeat — pull, breathe, pull, breathe — and Tam fixed herself to that rhythm the way a drowning woman fixes on a rope. Above them Thornfell's cliffs went by in the grey morning light, black stone streaked white with salt, and somewhere behind them the waystation burned, its smoke smearing the sky like a thumbprint on wet ink.

Her shoulder was still bleeding. She could feel it in the slow, patient way of wounds that mean to be taken seriously: not sharp anymore, but deep, a heat spreading through her ribs with every breath. The inquisitor's blade had been silver-edged. Of course it had. They always were, when they came for things like her.

*Things like her.* She almost laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough hurt enough to whiten her vision.

"Stop moving." Jude's voice came low over the water, roughened by smoke and sleeplessness. "You'll open it again."

"It's already open."

"Then stop making it worse." The oars dipped, pulled. "We're putting in at the sea caves past Gullwatch. There's a chapel of your silent god there — half-ruined, nobody tends it. You can bleed where no one's watching."

"You know the coast," Tam said. It wasn't a question.

"I grew up on this coast." A pause. The oars kept their rhythm. "You knew that sigil on the Codex cover. You touched it like you'd touched it before. So before I decide whether I've saved a woman or made the worst mistake of my life, you're going to tell me what you are."

Tam closed her eyes. Against her eyelids, unbidden, the water rose.

---

She remembered drowning the way other people remembered their wedding day: in fragments, all of them bright, none of them entirely trustworthy.

She had been ninety years old, which among her people was barely grown, barely more than Jude's twenty-some years would be among the dry-kind. Ninety years old and standing in the Pearl Hall in a gown of woven kelp-silk, watching her mother — *not* her mother, her aunt, Nerissa, though grief had worn the distinction smooth long ago — watch her with eyes like chips of green ice. The court had gathered for the Rite of Naming, when an heir of the drowned blood first heard the Tide speak her true name aloud. Every heir got one hearing. One word from the god beneath the god, spoken into the marrow.

Tam had knelt on the pearl floor. The water of the hall had gone still around her, stiller than water should be, and she had waited for her name.

And the Silent Tide had said nothing.

Not silence the way the dry-kind imagined it — absence, emptiness. This had been silence with weight, silence like a hand pressed over a mouth, silence *grieving*. She had felt it pass over her like the shadow of something enormous swimming overhead, and she had understood, in the bone-deep way children understand things they cannot explain, that the god was not refusing her.

The god was hiding.

Three days later the covenant broke. She remembered that too: the shudder that ran through the drowned court like an earthquake underwater, the pearls of the Hall dimming all at once, the wardens' horns sounding the note that hadn't sounded in a hundred generations. The gates between realms, which had stood open since before her people had written history, sealed themselves with a sound like a heart stopping. And above — she had seen it through the Gate's last clear moment, the image burned into her forever — above, on the cliffs of Thornfell, the free cities' crown flared white-hot in its vault, drinking something, taking something in, and every priest of every creed fell to their knees at once without knowing why.

The court needed someone to blame. Courts always do.

They chose the heir whose naming the god had refused. *Ill-omened*, they called her. *Tide-forsaken*. Nerissa, who had raised her from the cradle after her parents died in the border wars, who had braided her hair with her own hands and taught her the old laws — Nerissa signed the decree of exile with a steady pen and did not look at her while she did it.

"They made me walk out through the Drowned Gate," Tam said now, to the grey morning, to the creaking oars, to the stranger who had dragged her out of a burning building. "On foot. Through the toll-water. They let me keep my life because killing me would have made a martyr, and martyrs are inconvenient. But they took my name off the rolls. In the court's memory, I was never born."

Jude didn't answer right away. The oars pulled, breathed, pulled.

"And the sigil," she said finally. "On the Codex."

"The seal of the covenant. Two courts joined — see how the wave curls around the crown? That's the old mark, from before the break. Nobody living has ever seen it whole." Tam pushed herself up against the skiff's bench, slowly, because her shoulder had opinions about haste. "Where did you find that book, Jude Dunmore?"

"In a smuggler's cache under a waystation floor, wrapped in oilcloth, next to forty years of unpaid debts." Jude's jaw tightened. "It has my name in it. My name, and tonight's date, written in a margin in a hand I swear I've never seen. It says—" She stopped rowing. The skiff rocked. Her eyes, when they found Tam's, were the flat grey-green of the sea before weather. "It says there's going to be a Saltbride. Someone who reforges the broken covenant between the dry world and yours. Or Kaelen Voss takes both."

The words landed in Tam's chest like stones dropped into deep water. She watched them sink, and she watched what they stirred up rise.

Because she had heard the prophecy before. Not the dry-kind's version — theirs was garbled, secondhand, passed down through a century of priests guessing at a closed door. She had heard it in the drowned court, in the voices of the old ones, the elders who still remembered the covenant's terms. *When the crown drinks the sacrifice*, the elders chanted on the dark tides, *a bride shall come up from the salt to make it whole again. Saltbride, seal-bride, heart given for the gate.* She had thought it a lullaby, a comfort-story, the way exiles tell themselves the door will open someday.

She had never once imagined the bride would be the woman rowing her away from a fire.

"Say it," Jude said. "Whatever you just realized. Say it out loud, because if you keep it behind your teeth I'm going to assume the worst, and I've had a very bad night for assuming the worst."

"The prophecy is real," Tam said. "I've known it since I was a child. The elders sang it. *A bride shall come up from the salt.*" She watched Jude's face as the words arrived, watched the mercenary's careful blankness strain and hold. "I thought it was a story. A hope. Something we told ourselves so the exile wouldn't be forever."

"And now?"

"Now I think the Codex isn't a book that predicts the future." Tam looked down at the bundle in the bottom of the skiff, the oilcloth-wrapped weight that had cost a caravan master his freedom and nearly cost them both their lives. "I think it's a book that *makes* one. And it's chosen you."

Jude picked the oars back up. Pulled. Breathed. For a long time the only sounds were water and wood and the far cry of gulls off Gullwatch, and Tam used that time to hate herself efficiently and thoroughly, because she had just watched hope die in another person's face and recognized the expression, because she'd worn it for a century.

* * *

The sea cave swallowed them in darkness that smelled of stone and cold. Jude grounded the skiff on shingle and hauled Tam out with a gentleness that sat strangely on her — everything else about the woman was edges, but her hands were careful, always, as if she'd learned early that the world breaks easily and someone ought to mind.

The chapel was exactly as promised: a hollow in the rock where some forgotten congregation had set a tide-altar, long since cracked, its basin full of rainwater and its walls furred with salt-bloom. No god lived here. Tam could feel the absence the way she could feel the absence of her own name — a shaped hole, waiting.

Jude built a small fire of driftwood, laid Tam down near it, and cut the ruined shirt off her shoulder with a knife that moved like it had done this a thousand times. Then she cleaned the wound with seawater, which was either cruelty or competence, and stitched it with gut-thread from her pack, and her hands never shook once.

"You've done soldiering," Tam said, to keep from making a sound.

"Fifteen years. Salt-sworn company, the Saltcloaks, out of Brack." Jude tied off the last stitch and sat back on her heels. "We prayed to the Silent Tide before every contract. Standard rite. Salt in the water, name in the salt." Her voice went carefully level. "You're of the drowned court. Tell me why my god stopped answering nine years ago."

The question hit closer than the inquisitor's blade had. Tam turned her face toward the firelight and let the truth come up, because this woman had earned it with a needle and gut-thread and a burning building, and because lying to Jude Dunmore suddenly seemed like the most exhausting thing in the world.

"I don't know," she said. "I swear it on the water I was born in. The Tide went quiet to us too. Quieter, maybe — we at least had the memory of its voice. The elders said the silence began the night the covenant broke, that the god withdrew to... to hide something. That's the only teaching that survived. *The silence is a hiding place.*" She shook her head. "A god doesn't go silent for a century out of spite, Jude. Gods go silent out of grief. Or shame."

"Shame." Jude laughed, short and bitter, and fed a piece of driftwood to the fire. "Nine years ago I prayed for my brother's fever to break. I was nineteen. I did the rite perfectly — Ossa checked it herself, she was strict about rites. He died anyway. And the Tide never said a word to me again. Not that night, not since. I kept praying anyway, because that's what salt-sworn means, you pray whether or not anyone's listening." She stared into the flames. "Now I find out the god's been grieving this whole time. Over what?"

"I don't know." Tam reached out — slowly, giving her time to refuse — and put her fingers on Jude's wrist, on the pulse there, steady as oar-strokes. "But I know this much. The Codex named you. Tonight's date. Your name. And the elders' song says the bride comes *up from the salt* — meaning the dry world, meaning a woman of the shore-blood, meaning—"

"Meaning a mercenary who can't even get her god to say hello." Jude didn't pull her wrist away. That small fact settled into Tam's chest warmer than the fire. "And Voss wants the covenant reforged under his crown. Which means he needs what the Codex names. Which means he needs me."

"He'll burn the coast looking for you."

"He already started." Jude's jaw set. "So we run. You and me and this cursed book. And along the way you're going to teach me everything you remember about the drowned court, the covenant, the prophecy — all of it. Because if I'm going to be somebody's Saltbride, I want to know exactly what's being bought."

Something in Tam flinched at the word — *bought* — and she saw Jude catch the flinch, because the woman missed nothing.

"What aren't you telling me?"

"A great deal," Tam said honestly. "Most of it I don't understand yet myself. But there's one thing I do know, and you should hear it now, while you still have the option of throwing me back in the sea." She drew a breath that pulled at her stitches. "In the elders' song, the covenant isn't reforged with vows. It's reforged with a *giving*. The bride's part in it isn't ceremonial. The song says her heart becomes the seal. I always thought that meant devotion. Love. Willingness." Her voice dropped. "Standing in that hall as a child, feeling the god's silence press down on me like the whole ocean's weight — I don't think it means willingness, Jude. I think it means the altar."

The fire popped. Outside, the tide was turning; Tam could feel it in her bones the way the dry-born never could, the great slow breath of the world drawing in.

Jude Dunmore was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, in the flat, decided tone of a woman signing a contract she intends to survive:

"Then we find out what the fine print says before anyone signs anything. There's a blind archivist in Brack — Mother Quill — reads anything, deciphers anything, sells nothing. If anyone can take that prophecy apart and show me its seams, it's her." She stood, shouldering her pack, and offered Tam her hand. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk."

"Then walk. We move before dawn." At the mouth of the cave, Jude paused, silhouetted against the greying sea, and said — not turning around, as if the words cost less that way — "For what it's worth. Whatever you are. Exile, heir, ill-omened, whatever your court wrote you off as. You bled for a stranger's book tonight. That's more than my god's done in nine years."

Tam took her hand and stood, and did not say that no one in a hundred years had called her worth anything, because if she said it she would weep, and she had spent a century learning to hold the ocean inside her face.

Behind them, in the bottom of the skiff, the Century Codex lay in its oilcloth — and neither of them saw the fresh line surfacing in its margins, ink rising through the page like a bruise through skin, in a hand that was becoming, stroke by stroke, unmistakably Jude Dunmore's own:

*The Saltbride walks east with the exile. The road to Brack opens. Let the reading begin.*
