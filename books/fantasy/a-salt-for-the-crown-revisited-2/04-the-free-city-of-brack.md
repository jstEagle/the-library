## 4. The Free City of Brack

They came down off the salt flats at dusk, when Brack's lamps were just beginning to argue with the fog.

Jude had walked this road a hundred times in her soldiering years — the causeway of packed shell and crushed coral that ran between the tidal pools like a spine laid bare — but she had never come to it running, never come with blood on her boots that wasn't her own and a stranger leaning on her shoulder who weighed less than her pack and shivered like a wet gull. Tam had not spoken in an hour. Her lips had gone the color of drowned things.

"Hold on," Jude said, for the fourth time or the fourteenth. "The city's close. You can smell it."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were something a person could do with cracked ribs. "I smell fish," she managed. "And tar. And something burning."

"That's Brack." Jude shifted the stranger's arm higher across her shoulders. The Codex rode against her chest inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and it seemed to warm with every step, as though it knew where they were going. She tried not to think about that. She had spent nine years learning not to think about things her god did, and she was out of practice at being thought *about* instead.

The fog thickened as they came under the first gate — no wall, really; Brack had never believed in walls, only in the maze of stilted houses and rope bridges and canal-streets that could swallow an army whole and spit out its boots. Lanterns hung from every eave, glass globes crusted with salt bloom, and their light turned the mist the color of weak ale. Somewhere ahead a bell rang the tide-change, three slow strokes, and the whole city seemed to lean toward the water and then away again, breathing.

"Where are we going?" Tam whispered.

"To find out if I still have friends."

* * *

The Saltcloaks kept their hall above a chandlery on Sable Row, in the crook of the city where the canals ran narrow and the neighbors minded their own business because their own business was mostly smuggling. Jude remembered the stairs — forty-one of them, warped, the seventh one rotten — and she took them carefully with Tam's weight dragging at her, and she stood at the top breathing hard and looking at the door she'd walked through a thousand mornings of her old life.

The sigil had changed. It used to be a plain grey cloak stitched over crossed oars. Now someone had added a third element: a small white wave, breaking beneath the oars, worked in thread that caught the lamplight with a faint, wrong iridescence. Pearl-thread. Or something pretending to be pearl-thread.

"They've gone heretic," Tam murmured, reading it over her shoulder.

"They've gone *honest*," Jude said, and knocked.

The door opened on a knife. Of course it did. The knife belonged to a boy of maybe fifteen with a shaved head and a salt-oil brand on his jaw — new mark, newer than the hall — and he looked at Jude the way you look at weather you've heard about but never seen.

"I'm here for Ossa Vane."

"She doesn't see people after dark."

"She'll see me." Jude let her hood fall back. "Tell her Dunmore's home."

The boy's eyes went wide, then narrow, then he shut the door in her face. Boots hammered away inside. Tam slid slowly down the wall until she was sitting, one hand pressed to her ribs, watching Jude with an expression she'd been wearing since the waystation — the look of someone cataloguing facts about a person for later use.

"You're known here."

"I was known here."

"And this Ossa Vane?"

"My sergeant. When I had a sergeant." Jude crouched, checked the bandage at Tam's side, found it soaked through and tacky. Inquisitor steel left wounds that didn't want to close; everyone knew that, nobody said why. "She taught me everything I know about staying alive and half of what I know about wanting to. Then I took my salt-oath and went quiet-service, and she stayed loud. We didn't part badly. We just parted the way tides part."

The door opened again before Tam could answer. Not the boy this time.

Ossa Vane filled the doorway the way she always had — broad as a barrel, iron-grey braid coiled at her neck like a rope ready for work, a scar through her eyebrow that Jude had watched her earn outside Corren's Ford and never let her forget. She looked older. Everyone would, after six years. But her eyes did the same thing they'd always done: went over Jude once, quick, counting weapons and wounds, and then softened by exactly nothing that anyone else would notice.

"Well," Ossa said. "The silent sister comes home at night, hood up, carrying something wrapped in oilcloth, with a dying stranger on her arm and inquisitor steel-smell all over her." She stepped back and held the door wide. "Get in before the fog gets ideas."

* * *

Inside, the hall smelled of lamp oil, stew, and tallow — the same smells, but layered now over something else, something mineral and cold, like the air off deep water. The long table had been pushed aside and the floor chalked with diagrams Jude didn't recognize: concentric rings, tide-marks, a spiral of words in a script that hurt to look at sideways. Half a dozen Saltcloaks sat around it, and every head turned when they entered, and every face did the arithmetic of *Dunmore, alive, why*.

Ossa cleared a bench with her boot. "Down," she told Tam, and then, to a wiry woman by the hearth, "Marette. Needle and the good thread. And the grey bottle." A pause. "Not the black bottle."

"The black bottle works faster."

"The black bottle stops hearts, Marette. Grey."

While Marette worked — and Tam bore the probing of the wound with a silence that Jude recognized as aristocratic rather than brave — Ossa drew Jude into the alcove behind the stair, out of earshot, and poured two cups of something plum-dark from a jug that lived there.

"Talk," she said.

So Jude talked. The caravan. The cache under the waystation floor, the smuggler's strongbox no list had ever recorded. The book. She watched Ossa's face go through disbelief, then hunger, then a careful blankness that Jude liked considerably less than either of the others.

"It named you," Ossa said at last. "Your name. Today's date."

"In ink that hadn't been there before. I checked the page twice. Three times."

"And your god?" Ossa's voice gentled in a way that was worse than mockery. "The Silent Tide. Did it say anything?"

Jude looked into her cup. Nine years. Nine years of prayers dropped into silence like stones into a well with no bottom, and she had made her peace with it, or told herself she had — the quiet service demanded faith without feedback, salt given without return, and she had worn that vow like armor right up until a book had spoken her name in a dead man's handwriting.

"No," she said. "It never says anything."

"Mm." Ossa drank. Behind them, Marette's needle flashed, and Tam made no sound at all, which Jude was beginning to understand was simply how she was built. "Then listen to me, girl, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to feed you, because you look like a winter fence post." Ossa set down her cup and gripped Jude's wrist, hard, the old grip, the parade-ground grip. "That book is worth more than this city. More than any city on the coast. Every crown from here to the Thorn passes will pay armies for it, and every creed will burn cities for it, and neither will care whether it's in your hands or your ashes when they take it. Do you understand what you're carrying?"

"I carried it here, didn't I?"

"You carried it *into my house*." Ossa's eyes flicked toward the chalked floor, the pearl-worked sigil upstairs, the half-heretic company drinking around a diagram of the deep tides. "Which is either the smartest thing you've done since I've known you or the last stupid thing, and I genuinely don't know which yet."

"What does that mean? The floor. The sigil. What are you all doing, Ossa?"

For a long moment her old sergeant said nothing. Then she laughed — short, tired, real — and rubbed her scarred brow with the heel of her hand.

"We're praying," she said. "Half this company got tired of gods who don't answer. So we started asking ones who might. Old rites. Drowned rites. Things the temples burned the books for, back when burning books was fashionable." She nodded at the chalk spirals. "You wouldn't believe what washes up on the flats these days. What the tide leaves. What it seems to be *leaving for us*."

Jude felt the Codex shift against her ribs — or imagined it did — warm as a sleeping animal.

* * *

They fed her stew and dark bread and salt-butter, and Tam slept in the bunk room under three blankets with Marette's stitching holding her together, and for perhaps an hour Jude sat among her old company and let herself forget, almost, what was folded in oilcloth against her heart.

It was easy, that hour. That was the treacherous thing about it, and she knew it even as she sank into it. Rell — who'd been a raw recruit when she'd left, now a corporal with a wife and a missing tooth — retold the story of the goose incident at Corren's Ford with embellishments that had calcified into legend. The boy with the shaved head, whose name was Pip, asked her eleven questions about the quiet service and visibly lost his religion about halfway through her answers, which she suspected was Ossa's plan all along. Someone put another log on the fire. The fog pressed white against the windows, and the city breathed its slow tidal breath outside, and Jude sat in the warm middle of it with her shoulders finally down from around her ears, and thought: *this is what it was. This is what I gave up when I took the vow. Not comfort — belonging.*

She had missed it so much it frightened her.

Ossa appeared at her elbow with the jug and refilled her cup unasked. "You're doing the thing," she said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you enjoy something and start writing its eulogy. You always did that. Even at nineteen." She sat, joints cracking like green wood. "The stranger. Who is she?"

"Her name is Tam."

"That's not an answer."

"She says she's from under the water, Ossa. Exiled heir of the drowned court. Banished when the covenant broke." Jude watched her sergeant's face very carefully as she said it. "And before you decide I've been drinking the black bottle after all — she knew the sigil on the Codex cover on sight. Knew what the binding was. Knew things about the deep roads I couldn't have invented with a week and a bottle of plum wine."

Ossa was quiet for a long time. Around them the hall roared softly on, Rell reaching the goose story's climax, Pip laughing too loudly, the fire settling.

"A century ago," Ossa said at last, "the sea stopped answering everybody. Not just your order. Everybody. The temples called it the Silence and built doctrine on it. My grandmother called it something less polite." She turned her cup slowly on her knee. "If the drowned court has an heir walking around dry-shod, and a book that names dates, and inquisitors burning waystations over both — then the world's tilting, Dunmore. And I've got sixty-three people in this hall who follow me because I keep them fed and pointed at the enemy. So I need you to tell me true: are you bringing a storm into my house, or are you bringing the eye of one?"

Jude thought about lying. She thought about how easy it would be, and how Ossa would see through it in the time it took to blink, and how the lie would sit between them forever after, like a stone in a boot.

"I don't know," she said. "I honestly don't. But I know Voss's inquisitors took our caravan master alive, and Master Corvo knows my face and my routes and that I had the book. They'll work him. He's tough, but everyone's workable. And when they've worked him, they'll know exactly where I'd run." She met Ossa's eyes. "Brack is the only place I'd run. So the storm's coming here regardless. I'd rather it found us ready."

Ossa drained her cup and stood, and for a moment she was pure sergeant again, and Jude felt nineteen and terrified and safe all at once.

"Then here's my price, and it's not negotiable," Ossa said. "You sleep under my roof, you eat at my table, and my people bleed for yours if it comes to that. In return, tomorrow morning, that book goes in front of Mother Quill."

Jude's stomach dropped. "The archivist. The blind one."

"The blind one who reads anything put in front of her, including things that aren't written in any alphabet living men use. If anyone in this city can tell us what that book actually wants — not what it says, what it *wants* — it's her." Ossa paused at the edge of the firelight, silhouetted, broad and immovable. "One more thing, Dunmore. Whatever Quill finds in there — and it'll be worse than you're imagining, it's always worse — you remember that you knocked on my door tonight and I opened it. Whatever the prophecy says you are, in this house you're Saltcloak first. Don't let a book convince you otherwise."

She went. Jude sat alone by the fire with her cup going cold, listening to her company laugh, feeling the warmth of them soak into her like sun into stone — fragile, borrowed, already half-grieved for.

Against her heart, through oilcloth and wool and skin, the Century Codex beat once, softly, like a second pulse. Like something patient. Like something that had all the time in the world, and knew exactly what tomorrow morning held.

Upstairs, in the bunk room, Tam cried out once in her sleep — a short, drowning sound, in a language Jude had never heard and somehow understood anyway.

The word was *soon*.
