## 5. Reading the Prophecy Backwards

Mother Quill had been blind for forty years, and in that time she had learned that the world was better read with the fingers than the eyes.

The Codex lay open beneath her hands on the reading table in the undercroft of Brack's Chantry of the Drowned Tide, and it did not feel like any book she had ever touched. Most books were patient things — paper and ink, waiting to be understood. This one *breathed*. The vellum was warm, faintly damp, like the flank of a living animal, and when she ran her fingertips across the lines of script they prickled against her skin, fine as fishhooks, as though the words resented being read.

"Careful," she murmured, not to anyone in particular. The dead archivists whose bones lined the undercroft walls had never minded her talking to books. "I've unraveled older secrets than you, love."

The lamp above the table burned low. She had no use for lamps, but Jude Dunmore had insisted on lighting one anyway — a courtesy, Quill suspected, more for the mercenary's own comfort than the archivist's. Jude stood somewhere to her left; she could hear the salt-sworn woman breathing, slow and controlled, the way soldiers breathed when they were pretending not to be afraid. The other one — Tam, the half-drowned stranger with the voice like water over stones — sat nearer, close enough that Quill could smell the sea on her. Not the harbor-smell of Brack, all tar and fish-rot. This was deep water. Cold water. Water that had never known a sun.

"You've read this page three times now," Jude said. Her voice was rougher than usual. Fear did that to people; it sanded them down. "What does it say?"

"It says," Quill said, "that whoever bound this book wanted very much for no one to read it backwards."

She turned another leaf. The parchment resisted, then yielded with a sound like a sigh.

Most people believed prophecy ran forward — that a seer looked ahead and wrote down what she saw, plain as a merchant recording a debt. But Quill had spent her life among texts, and texts lied in their own characteristic ways. A forgery flattered. A translation smoothed. And a prophecy written by something that wished to *hide* a truth would bury that truth at the beginning and dress the ending in glory, trusting that readers, like all mortal creatures, would start where they were told to start.

So she had started at the end and worked backward, the way you unwound a fishing net from a drowned man: carefully, from the knots.

"The last page is the one everyone reads first," she said. "The Saltbride. The reforging. The crown and the covenant renewed. It's beautiful writing. Rhythmic. It wants to be memorized." Her fingers found the line again, traced its elegant curves. "*And she shall go down gladly, and the two realms shall be one beneath her.*" She snorted. "Gladly. Mark that word. When a text insists too hard on gladness, someone is lying about tears."

Behind her, Tam made a small sound. Quill catalogued it without turning: grief, or recognition.

"Now go back a page." She moved her hands. "Here the language changes. Still lovely, but the grammar tightens. Conditions appear. *If the bride comes freely, the seal holds a hundred years. If she comes unwilling, the seal holds but a season.* Unwilling. Freely. Do you notice what's missing from both?"

Silence. Then Jude, flatly: "No."

"*Whether*," Quill said. "There is no page on which the bride may decline. The book offers her choices about how she'll be given, never whether. That's not prophecy, child. That's a contract. And contracts are written by the party who benefits."

She heard Jude shift her weight. The creak of leather armor. "Keep going."

"I intend to." Quill turned back another leaf, and another, moving upstream through the Codex toward its source. The script grew older here, stranger, the letters leaning like reeds in a current. Her fingers knew these letterforms. They predated the free cities. They predated, if the chantry's oldest records were honest, the crowns themselves.

"Here." She stopped. "This is the oldest page. The first page, really, though it's bound last — someone reversed the quires, long ago, so that a casual reader would begin at the ending and never think to question it. Clever. Wickedly clever." She pressed her palms flat against the vellum and let herself simply *feel* it for a moment. Warmth pulsed beneath the surface, slow as a heartbeat. Whatever had been sacrificed to make this book, it was not entirely finished being sacrificed.

"Read it aloud," Tam said quietly. It was the first time the stranger had spoken since they'd descended into the undercroft. Her voice carried that strange doubled quality, as though two throats spoke in unison a half-beat apart. "Please. I knew parts of this once, in the old tongue. I want to hear it in yours."

Quill nodded, though of course no one could see her nod, and that was the joke of her whole life. She bent close to the page, close enough that the fishhook letters seemed to catch at her lips, and read:

"*In the first age, before the crowns, the sea and the shore were wed by bargain: breath for brine, memory for memory, each realm giving freely to the other. But the bargainers grew proud, and the shore-king said: why should we give year upon year what we may give once and be done? And he took his own daughter to the tide-line, and gave her wholly, and with her death he sealed the covenant so that no further price need ever be paid. And the gods of the sea wept, for they had not asked for her. And the gods of the sea fell silent, so that neither realm would ever learn what the crown of the shore was truly forged from.*"

The undercroft was very quiet after that. Somewhere above them, Brack went on with its evening — gulls, gulls always, the clatter of the fish-market closing, a drunk singing three streets away — but down here among the bones and the breathing book, the silence had weight.

"That's it?" Jude said at last. "That's the whole of it? A king killed his own daughter and called it a bargain?"

"A king killed his own daughter," Quill corrected gently, "and called it an *ending*. That's worse. Look at the structure of it, child. The covenant was meant to be renewed — breath for brine, memory for memory, freely given, over and over, forever. A living thing. A marriage, if you like, between equals, renewed each season with vows spoken fresh. And then some shore-king decided renewal was *inconvenient*, and paid everything at once, and the price was a person." She shook her head. "The gods didn't accept it. Read the line again — *they had not asked for her.* The sacrifice wasn't holy. It was theft. And the silence isn't reverence or mystery. It's shame. The Silent Tide has been silent for a thousand years because it cannot bear to tell either realm what was done in its name."

"And the Saltbride," Tam said. Her doubled voice had gone very soft. "The rite at the end. The reforging."

"Is the same theft," Quill said, "performed again, with better manners. Go back to the final page with me. *She shall go down gladly.* Gladly — because a willing victim makes a stronger seal, doesn't it, and the book's authors learned that much from their mistake. The first covenant broke because it was built on a single stolen death. So the new one will be built on a single *unstolen* one. A girl who walks into the sea smiling." She closed her hands slowly into fists atop the page. "They've dressed a bride-price up as a coronation. The honor, the destiny, the chosen-one language — it's all fine print, child. All of it. You're not being asked to save the world. You're being asked to *be the payment*."

* * *

Jude Dunmore did not shout. Quill had expected shouting, honestly. She'd served this chantry through four wars and had watched a great many soldiers receive news they didn't like, and most of them shouted, or laughed, or smashed something small and blameless.

Jude went still. Perfectly still, the way people do when the floor has opened under them and movement feels like admitting it.

"No," she said finally. Quietly. "No. I'm not doing it. I don't care what the book says. I don't care what the god says — the god hasn't said anything in nine years, so it can keep its opinions to itself a while longer." A short, harsh breath. "I'm a salt-sworn mercenary, Mother. My vows are to my company and my own two hands. Nobody bought me. Nobody *gets* to spend me."

"I know," Quill said.

"I mean it."

"I know you do, child. I believe you completely." And she did — that was the terrible thing, the thing that sat in Quill's chest like swallowed ice. She believed Jude Dunmore would refuse. Any sane creature would refuse. And the Codex did not care.

Because here was what she had not yet said aloud, what she had been circling for the past hour like a gull over a corpse: the book was not merely describing the future. It was *negotiating* with it. Every refusal fed it. She had felt it under her fingers as she worked — each time Jude spoke a denial, each time her heart hardened against the fate described, the vellum had warmed, the script had shifted, faint new lines surfacing like bubbles rising through dark water. She had told herself she was imagining it. Archivists imagined things; it was an occupational hazard.

But now, as Jude declared her refusal for the third time, Quill laid her fingers on the final page and felt the words move.

"Stop," she said sharply. "Both of you. Be quiet a moment. Don't — don't argue with it. Don't even think loudly near it."

Tam drew an audible breath. "It's rewriting itself."

"It's *responding*," Quill said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the worst part." She traced the fresh lines rising through the old ones, letters surfacing like drowned things coming up for air, and read them aloud because someone had to, because horror unspoken grows teeth:

"*Though she refuses the altar, the tide comes for its own. What is named may be declined; what is owed may not. The debt stands. The bride-price gathers interest.*"

"Gathers interest," Jude repeated. Her voice had gone very level, which in a soldier Quill had learned was far more frightening than screaming. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning," Quill said, and hated every word as she weighed it, "that your refusal isn't an escape, child. It's a term of the contract. The book anticipated it. Perhaps the book *required* it — a bride dragged unwilling makes a weak seal, remember, so the authors wrote a prophecy designed to produce a bride who refuses first and yields later, freely, having understood the cost. Your 'no' is a scene in their play. It may even be the most important scene." She lifted her hands from the page and wiped them on her robe, though the warmth clung. "Whoever wrote this didn't just see the future. They built a trap with a door in it shaped exactly like your defiance."

The lamp guttered. Above them the drunk had stopped singing.

"So what do I do?" Jude asked. And there it was — the question underneath all questions, the one Quill had heard from dying men and desperate queens and once, long ago, from her own younger self, back when she still had eyes to weep with. *What do I do.*

Quill was eighty-one years old. She had spent her life learning that texts lie, that gods grieve, that kingdoms are built on buried crimes. She had no comfort to give and would have insulted the girl by pretending otherwise.

"You do what the book hasn't predicted," she said. "Which means first we find out what it *has*. There are six copies of the Century Codex fragments scattered through the free cities — I've spent thirty years tracking them. If the book rewrites itself around your choices, then somewhere, in some vault or smuggler's cache, another copy is rewriting too. And if we can read what it becomes elsewhere—" she tapped the table, "—we learn the shape of the trap. And a trap, child, once mapped, can be walked around. Or broken. Or fed something else instead of you."

"Other copies," Jude said slowly. "Where?"

"One is in the private collection of Kaelen Voss himself, which is out of reach until we're all corpses. One sank with the *Merciful Wake* off Sallow Point. One—" Quill paused. The next location was not one she shared lightly; it belonged to a chapter of her life she had paid dearly to close. "One is held by the ash-priests at Cinderhold, in the Thorn passes. Which Voss's army crossed three days ago."

She heard the silence change quality — the specific silence of people realizing the clock was already running.

"There's another way," Tam said suddenly. Her doubled voice had sharpened, and beneath it Quill caught something new: fear, yes, but also a fierce, reluctant hope, like a coal refusing to die. "You said the covenant was meant to be renewed. Breath for brine. Memory for memory. *Freely given.* If the original bargain still exists — if it was only buried, not destroyed — then it could be honored as it was written. Without a bride. Without a price." A pause. "I know where the first terms were kept. I knew them when I was a child, before the exile. Before the drowning."

The warmth of the Codex surged under Quill's hands — sudden, eager, hungry — and fresh script boiled up through the final page faster than she could follow, and she read what she could, snatching phrases as they surfaced:

"*Let her not seek the old terms. Let her not wake what sleeps beneath—*"

Then the page went blank. Not empty — *blank*, deliberately, furiously blank, the vellum smooth and warm as skin, as though the book had clapped a hand over its own mouth.

Quill sat back. Her heart was hammering like a novice's.

"Well," she said dryly, into the dark. "It seems we've asked it a question it would rather not answer. Which, in forty years of archival work, tells me it's the right question."

She reached for the lamp to trim the wick, and that was when the bells began.

Not the chantry bells — those she knew, lazy and cracked. These came from the harbor quarter, urgent, overlapping, the alarm-pattern every Brack child learned before their prayers: *gate, gate, fire, gate.* And beneath the bells, thin with distance but unmistakable, a sound Quill had hoped never to hear again in her lifetime: war-horns. Many of them. Coming down the coast road from the north.

Voss's army was not three days behind them in the passes.

It was at the gates.

"Child," Quill said calmly, folding her hands over the warm, silent, treacherous book, "I believe our reading session has been interrupted. Fetch my staff. And whatever else you do tonight — whatever you choose, whoever you choose to be — do not leave this book where the wrong hands can read it. Because I promise you, the wrong hands already know exactly what it says."

The bells rang on. And beneath her palms, faint as a pulse, the Codex began, very softly, to laugh.
