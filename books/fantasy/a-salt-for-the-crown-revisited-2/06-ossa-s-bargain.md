## 6. Ossa's Bargain

The news came up the mountain on the backs of crows.

Jude saw them first from the wall of Brack's salt-yard, three black shapes riding the wind off the Thorn passes, and she knew before the watchman shouted that they were not carrion birds. Crows followed armies the way gulls followed fishing boats, and no army had crossed the Thorn in a generation. The passes were supposed to be death — scree and ice and the ghosts of every fool who'd tried them — but Voss had spent a winter buying mountain clans with grain and gold, and now his banners came down through the high cols like a wound opening across the white slopes.

By dusk the yard was full of rumor. Ten thousand men, said some. Twenty, said others. Inquisitors in grey walking at the column's head, burning shrines as they came, feeding the Silent Tide's little altars to their own clean fire. Jude stood at the edge of the cookfire listening to it all and felt the Codex's weight against her ribs where she'd bound it beneath her coat, a second heartbeat made of calfskin and prophecy.

Tam found her there. She always knew how to find her now, which was its own kind of dangerous.

"They're faster than anyone guessed," Tam said. Her voice was low, pitched for Jude alone. In the firelight her drowned-court coloring showed — that faint pearl-grey beneath the skin, the eyes like deep water over slate — and Jude had learned to stop staring at it, mostly. "Voss doesn't march. He *arrives*. Someone told him exactly where you'd be."

"Ossa knows," Jude said. "Ossa's known since we walked through her gate."

"And yet we're still here."

Jude didn't answer that. She watched the fire instead, and thought about nine years of silence from her god, and about the way Ossa Vane had looked at her the night they arrived — not with warmth, exactly, but with the particular hunger of a woman counting what a thing might be worth.

* * *

They came for her an hour past midnight, when the salt-yard was dark and the sentries changed. Not soldiers — Ossa wouldn't insult her that way. Just Ossa herself, wrapped in her old sergeant's cloak, standing outside the lean-to where Jude slept with one boot on and her knife already drawn.

"You're awake," Ossa said.

"I'm always awake when someone stands over me breathing like a bellows."

A grunt that might have been a laugh, once. Ossa Vane had been Jude's sergeant back when the Saltcloaks took contracts honestly, back before half the company went heretic and the other half went dead. She was broad as a door and weathered like a pier post, and she had taught Jude everything worth knowing about staying alive, which made what she said next feel like a knife going in slow.

"We need to talk about the book."

"There's no book."

"Jude." Ossa stepped into the lean-to's shadow, and her face was unreadable, which was worse than anger. "I've known you since you were seventeen and stupid enough to take a blade for a stranger. Don't make me do arithmetic in front of you."

Jude sat up slowly. The Codex pressed cold against her side. "Say what you came to say."

"Ten thousand men are coming down the Thorn passes, and Brack has four hundred Saltcloaks and a wall older than anyone's grandmother. You've done the sums. I can read the outcome on your face same as mine." Ossa crouched, forearms on knees, close enough that Jude could smell woodsmoke and old leather. "But there's another sum. A man like Voss pays for what he wants. And what he wants is in your coat."

"No."

"You haven't heard the offer."

"I don't need to. I know how it ends. You sell me out, he takes the book, and everyone who helped me gets to keep breathing." Jude stood, and the lean-to was low enough that she had to hunch, which made the moment feel smaller than she wanted it to. "I've been sold before, Ossa. It was a cheaper price and I survived it."

"This isn't selling you. This is saving forty families." For the first time something moved behind Ossa's face — not guilt, Jude realized. Arithmetic again. The terrible honesty of a woman who had never learned to lie to herself. "You think I don't love you? I buried eleven of ours beside you. But love doesn't hold a wall. Gold holds a wall. Terms hold a wall. Voss gives terms — that's the horror of him, he actually keeps them. I give him the Codex, he leaves Brack standing, and you walk away with your girl and your archivist and your life. That's the bargain."

"The bargain is a bride-price," Jude said, and heard Quill's voice in her own words, the old blind woman's reading of the prophecy echoing up from two nights around the archive fire. "*The Saltbride's role is a price paid, not an honor won.* Everything in this rots down to the same trade, Ossa. Somebody's heart for somebody's safety. I'm done paying it."

"Then you're done being protected." Ossa rose. She didn't reach for a weapon; she never needed to. Her whole body was one. "Walls cost, Jude. Always have. I'm sorry it's you the bill lands on."

"So am I," Jude said. "That's the difference between us. You're sorry and you're doing it anyway."

* * *

She woke Tam and Quill without lighting a lamp. That was the smuggler's craft in her — nine years of moving other people's contraband through the Thorn country had taught her hands to work in the dark, packing in silence, choosing in silence. Two cloaks. Dried fish. The flint and the little iron salt-cellar she'd carried since her first oath, because a salt-sworn mercenary without salt was a lie told badly.

Quill came awake the way she did everything: completely, instantly, as if sleep were just another text she'd finished reading. "The army?"

"Three days out. Maybe less."

"And Ossa?"

"Selling us."

The old woman sighed, and it was the sigh of someone closing a beloved book at a bad chapter. "I wondered which way she'd break. They always break toward the many over the few. It looks like virtue from a distance." Quill's blind eyes turned toward Jude with unsettling accuracy. "And you? Which way will you break, child, when the arithmetic finally corners you?"

"That's why we're leaving before it does."

Tam was already dressed, already armed, standing at the flap with her head cocked toward the yard. "Sentries," she murmured. "Two at the gate. One on the wall above the storehouse. They're not looking for us to leave, though. They're looking outward." A pause, and then something flickered across her face that Jude had learned to read as drowned-court humor, dry and cold as tide-pools. "Your sergeant has posted guards to keep Voss's scouts from arriving early and spoiling the sale."

"Can you get us past them?"

"I grew up in a court where every corridor had ears and half of them had teeth." Tam smiled, thin and sharp. "Two sleepy mercenaries are nothing."

She was right. They went over the wall by the tannery drain, where the stones were slick and the smell covered everything, and Tam moved through the dark like she'd been born to it — which, Jude reflected, in a palace under the sea, perhaps she had. No one called out. No horn sounded. Brack fell away behind them with its fires banked and its walls waiting for an army, and Jude did not look back, because she was afraid of what her face would do if she did.

* * *

They walked until the grey hour, when the world goes colorless and even grief gets tired, and then Jude took them off the road entirely.

"Here?" Tam looked at the hillside — bracken, scree, and a wall of gorse with nothing visible behind it. "There's nothing here."

"There's everything here. There just isn't a road to it." Jude pushed into the gorse, found the gap by touch, the one her body knew the way it knew its own heartbeat. "Every kingdom builds roads so armies can use them. Smugglers build none, so armies can't follow. Welcome to the Vein."

The Vein was a network of paths that existed nowhere on any map, stitched together over centuries by people whose survival depended on being unfound: goat tracks widened by hand, ledges cut below cliff trails, fords that only ran shallow three weeks a year, a hundred shortcuts between the free cities that no tax collector or soldier had ever walked. Jude had spent nine years learning it, carrying salt and silk and sometimes people, and she had never once thought of it as anything but work. Now it was the only home she had left.

She led them down a slot canyon barely shoulder-wide, along a ledge above a river that thundered in the dark, through a hollow in the hills where someone long dead had built a smokehouse into the rock. By midmorning they were ten miles from Brack as the crow flew and a hundred as the soldier marched, and Quill, who was sixty-some years old and blind, had kept pace without complaint and without once asking where they were.

"How?" Tam asked her, watching the old woman step sure-footed over scree that would have broken a younger ankle.

"Jude describes the ground three steps ahead," Quill said placidly. "She's been doing it since we left the wall. She thinks I haven't noticed." Her mouth curved. "It's how she loves, that one. Quietly, usefully, while pretending it's just habit."

Jude felt her ears go hot and kept walking.

They made camp that night in the smokehouse hollow, a fire small enough to hide, fish broth in a tin cup passed around. And it should have been grim — fugitives, cold, hunted — but instead Tam was telling a story about her aunt Nerissa that was apparently funny, and Quill was laughing in her dry rustling way, and Jude sat with the cup warming her hands and felt something crack open in her chest that she didn't have a name for.

This. This was the thing the vows never covered. She had sworn salt-oaths to gods and companies, and both had failed or been sold, and here in a hole in the earth with a heretic archivist and an exiled princess she felt more sworn-to than she ever had kneeling at any altar.

"We could keep going," Tam said quietly, later, when Quill slept. She sat close, shoulders almost touching. "South, past the Saltmarshes, onto a ship, gone. Let Voss have the Thorn country. Let the prophecy rot."

"You'd give up your court?"

"I gave it up a century ago. It gave me up first." Tam's hand found hers in the dark, deliberate, warm despite everything. "But you — you'd be running forever. The Codex wrote your name, Jude. Books like that don't forget."

"No," Jude agreed. "They don't." She turned her hand and laced her fingers through Tam's, and it felt like a decision even though she hadn't decided anything. "But there's a place we can go where the book can't follow. Under the mountain. The wardens' roads. If the drowned court still remembers you—"

"It remembers me." Bitterness, and underneath it, hope, which was worse. "That's the problem."

"Then we ask it anyway." Jude squeezed her hand. "Together. That's the whole plan. Together, and refuse to let them split the bill."

Far off to the north, faint as a scar on the horizon, the sky over the Thorn passes glowed dull orange. Army fires. Voss settling in for the night, ten thousand men warm while three women hid in a hole in the hills with a forbidden book and half a plan.

Jude looked at the glow a long time.

Then, in the morning, they went down.
