## 7. The Road Under the Mountain

The deep roads did not forgive.

Tam had told them that at the mouth of the tunnel, standing in the cold breath that poured out of the mountain like the exhale of something sleeping, and Jude had only grinned — that crooked, salt-cracked grin of hers — and said, "Good. I'm tired of roads that pretend to be kind."

Now, three days in, Tam wondered if the grin had been a kind of prayer. The smuggler ways beneath Thornfell were older than smuggling, older than Thornfell itself, cut by hands no chronicle named and lit by nothing but the pale fungus that clung to the ceilings in constellations, glowing faintly blue-green, like drowned stars seen from below. The air tasted of iron and old water. Every footfall came back to them twice: once off the stone, once off something deeper, as though the mountain were repeating their steps to someone else.

Jude walked point with a lantern she'd shuttered to a knife-slit of light. Quill walked behind her, one hand on the guide-rope they'd strung from the entrance, her blind face tilted up toward the fungal stars as if reading them. Tam took the rear, because rear was where exiles belonged, and because from there she could watch both of them — the mercenary who had become a compass she could not stop following, and the archivist who had become, without ever saying so, the closest thing to a mother Tam had known since the sea closed behind her.

"You're brooding," Quill said softly, without turning around. Blind eyes saw more than most. It was infuriating.

"I'm counting exits," Tam said.

"Ah," said Quill. "Brooding with numbers."

Tam almost smiled. Then the Codex spoke.

It did not speak in words. It never had. But Tam carried it slung against her spine in its oilcloth wrap, and she felt it the way you feel a change in current before your eyes confirm it — a pressure, a warmth, a wrongness blooming through the canvas like ink through wet paper. She stopped. Shrugged the pack down. Jude's lantern swung back toward her immediately, the light-slit finding Tam's face.

"What is it?"

"It's warm." Tam unwrapped the book with hands that remembered, unhelpfully, how to hold a scepter. The Century Codex lay in its cradle of cloth, and the sigil on its cover — the drowned court's mark, the spiral shell broken by a straight line — was moving. Not the metal. The *line*. It was unbreaking itself, millimeter by millimeter, the way a scar fades, the way a wound decides to be whole again. "It's doing this because of her."

"Because of me?" Jude crouched beside her. Up close, in the fungous light, her face was all angles and exhaustion and stubbornness, and Tam hated how much comfort she took from it.

"The Codex doesn't record prophecy," Tam said. "I told you it was written. I didn't tell you it *writes*. My people made it so it could not be owned — it rewrites itself around choices. Around refusals." She looked at Jude. "You refused Mother Quill's reading. You refused the Saltbride's terms. And now the book is deciding what you are instead."

Jude touched the cover the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't trust. Under her fingers, the broken line sealed another hair's width. "And what's it deciding?"

"I don't know." That was the truth, and the truth was worse than any lie, because Tam's people had built this thing a century ago and even then it had frightened them. "But books that rewrite themselves don't serve their readers, Jude. They serve the ending."

Quill had come close, her hand hovering over the pages without touching. "Then we'd best learn to read faster than it writes," she said. "Come. Standing still in the deep roads is how the deep roads win."

They walked on. Behind them, in the dark, the Codex hummed like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

* * *

On the fourth day they found the bones of the last people who'd tried to hurry.

The passage opened into a vaulted gallery where the ceiling rose into darkness and the floor was paved — actually paved, fitted stone worn smooth by feet long dead. Pillars stood in ranks, each carved with the same spiral-shell sigil, and each pillar bore niches, and the niches held bones arranged with terrible tenderness: seated, hands folded, skulls bowed forward as if in endless quiet prayer. Drowned-court dead. Wardens who had come up when the covenant broke and never gone home, who had died in the dark rather than live in a world without their sea.

"They were waiting for the covenant to be reforged," Tam said quietly. Her voice echoed and the echo sounded younger than she was. "A hundred years. They sat down here and waited."

"And died waiting," Jude said. She moved among the pillars with a soldier's respect, not touching anything, her lantern light sliding across bone. "Is that what we're walking toward? Sitting down somewhere and waiting?"

"No." Tam heard the hardness in her own voice and couldn't soften it. "We're walking toward an altar. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes." Tam turned away, because Jude's face in that light made lying impossible and honesty unbearable. "Altars have knives."

They made camp in the gallery despite everything, because the pillars broke the wind and the dead, whatever else they were, kept no watch. Quill slept first, curled against a pillar base with her hand resting, deliberately, on the folded stone fingers of one of the warden dead — a gesture Tam could not parse and could not stop watching. Jude took second watch. Tam should have slept, but sleep under the mountain meant dreams, and dreams under the mountain meant the court, and the court meant—

"Tide take it," she muttered, and sat down beside Jude instead.

Jude didn't look at her. That was one of the things Tam had learned about her: Jude Dunmore gave people the gift of not looking. "Bad dreams?"

"Dreams of home." Tam pulled her knees up. "Which are worse."

"Tell me about it. Home. The real one."

So Tam told her. About the court of salt and pearl beneath the waves off Thornfell's coast, where the water was warm as blood and lit from within, where the nobles wore their griefs as jewelry because drowning had taught them that sorrow keeps. About her mother, Queen Maren, who had ruled the tides with a patience like bedrock. About the day the covenant broke — not with thunder but with silence, the prayers of the surface simply ceasing to arrive, a hundred voices going out at once like lamps snuffed in a corridor — and how her mother had sent Tam herself, thirteen years old, heir to the deep throne, up through the Drowned Gate to learn why.

"And the gate closed behind me," Tam finished. "And stayed closed. And the wardens who escorted me looked at me the way you look at a letter that says someone has died, and they left me on the shore, and I have been on the shore for eighty-seven years."

Jude was quiet for a long moment. The fungal stars drifted overhead in their slow, rootless way.

"My god went silent nine years ago," she said at last. "The Silent Tide. I prayed every night for a year after. Then every week. Then on holy days. Now I do it the way you crack your knuckles — habit, not hope." She glanced sideways at Tam, and the lantern-light caught the corner of her mouth. "Maybe we're both letters about someone who died."

"Maybe we're both still being delivered," Tam said, and immediately wanted to drown herself for saying something so naked.

But Jude laughed — low, brief, real — and leaned until her shoulder rested against Tam's shoulder, and neither of them moved away, and the dead of the drowned court prayed on around them in the dark, and Tam thought: *this is the cruelest thing the mountain has done yet. It let me be happy for eleven minutes.*

That was when the wardens came.

* * *

Tam heard them before anyone — of course she did; she had been raised to the sound of her own people moving through water, and these moved through air the same way, weightless, patient, inevitable. She was on her feet with her knife out before the first shadow detached from the gallery's far mouth.

"Lights," she breathed. "Kill the lights."

Jude shuttered the lantern without question. Darkness dropped over the gallery like a lid, leaving only the blue-green starlight of the ceiling, and in that light Tam saw them come: seven figures in lacquered scale the color of deep water, helms shaped like breaking waves, tridents held low and easy. Drowned-court wardens. Her father's guard. Her mother's honor. Men and women whose names she had known, whose children she had taught to swim, come up out of the deep roads like ghosts wearing faces she'd mourned.

They fanned out between the pillars with a discipline that made her chest ache. They did not see well in darkness — that was the mercy of the surface years, her eyes had changed — but they did not need to. One of them raised a trident and struck the pavement twice, and the sound rolled through the gallery like a bell through a body, and Tam knew the signal. *Halt and be counted.*

She stepped out of the dark with her empty hands raised.

"Hold," she called, in High Tidal, the language of the court, the language she hadn't spoken aloud in decades and which came out of her mouth whole, unbroken, as if she'd never left. "You stand in the Gallery of the Waiting. I know your rite. Count me."

The lead warden went very still. Then he crossed the pavement, unhurried, and stopped an arm's length away, and lifted his helm.

Tam's heart stopped and restarted wrong. *Corvin.* Corvin Saltmark, who had carried her on his shoulders through the kelp-courtyards when she was small enough to be carried, who had wept openly at her mother's coronation, who had been third through the Drowned Gate the day it closed and had looked back at her with a face like a letter about someone dead. He was older. His scales were gone grey at the gills. But his eyes were the same, and they were looking at her now with an expression she had spent eighty-seven years learning not to hope for.

"Say your name," he said. His voice was gravel dragged through surf.

"You know my name."

"Say it. The roads play tricks. The roads wear faces."

Tam lifted her chin the way her mother had taught her, the way that had once made entire courts go silent. "I am Tamara of House Deepwell, daughter of Maren, heir of the drowned throne, exiled by the closing of the Gate and the breaking of the covenant. I am the last of my line above the water. Count me, Corvin Saltmark, or strike me, but do either quickly, because I am tired, and armed, and grieving, and I have no patience left for rites."

For a moment the gallery held nothing but the drip of water and the hum of the Codex against her spine.

Then Corvin Saltmark, warden of the deep, sank to one knee on the ancient pavement, struck his trident-butt to the stone, and bowed his head. "The heir is found," he said, and his voice cracked on it, cracked like ice, like a dam, like eighty-seven years arriving all at once. "The heir is found. Blessed be the tide that returns her."

One by one, the other six knelt. The sound of it — lacquered scale folding down onto consecrated stone, seven times, deliberate as tide — filled the gallery, and Tam stood in the middle of it feeling as though she were being buried alive in something warm.

Behind her, she heard Jude shift. Heard the soft scrape of a knife being — not drawn, exactly. Just remembered.

"Don't," Tam said quietly, without turning. "Please. Not this time."

* * *

They sat by a warden-fire afterward — the wardens burned driftwood they'd carried up from the sea, which was either devotion or madness, and Tam suspected both — and the gallery filled with light and the smell of salt-smoke, and Tam learned what the century had done to her people while she'd been learning what it had done to hers.

The court had not forgotten her. That was the wound of it. She had assumed exile meant erasure, and erasure would at least have been clean. Instead: Corvin telling her, in the flat voice of a man reporting casualties, that her name was still spoken at the turning of every year. That her mother — dead now, forty years gone, and Tam learned it here, by firelight, from a man who knelt while he said it — had ordered a chair set at the high table every feast-night, empty, facing the closed Gate. That when the regent Nerissa had taken power, she had kept the chair, and the keeping of it was spoken of as mercy, and Tam understood with a sick lurch that Nerissa kept it the way a jailer keeps a cell swept.

"They love you," Corvin said. "That is not in question. It has never been in question. The question is what the regent loves, and whether the two can share a room."

"And the regent wants the Saltbride," Tam said. "The rite. The bride-price."

Corvin's eyes flicked — just flicked — toward where Jude sat apart, cleaning her knife with the focused violence of someone pretending not to listen. "The Codex has reached the court's ears. Fragments. The regent believes the covenant can be reforged, and that the surface-born woman named in it is the price of admission back into the world's good graces." He paused. "There are those of us who believe otherwise. Who believe the covenant broke for reasons the regent does not say aloud. Who came up into these roads not to hunt, but to wait — as our brothers in the niches waited — for the heir to come home."

"And if the heir comes home refusing the rite?"

"Then the heir comes home," Corvin said simply, "and the court will have to decide which it loves more: its princess, or its prophecy." He rose, and knelt again — the kneeling seemed to be reflexive now, and each time it landed on Tam like a blow — and said, "We will escort you to the Drowned Gate. All three of you. On the heir's word."

"On my word," Tam said, and heard, in her own voice, the queen her mother had died believing she would be, and hated how easily the voice came, and hated more that some starving part of her had missed it.

Later, when the fires burned low and the wardens kept watch at the gallery mouths, Jude came and sat beside her. She didn't ask about the empty chair. Tam loved her ferociously for that.

"They knelt to you," Jude said.

"They knelt to a title."

"They knelt to *you*. I watched their faces." Jude turned the knife over in her hands, not looking at it. "Back at Brack, everyone looked at me like I was a coin they were arguing over. Bride-price, target, asset. You know what nobody's looked at me like since? A person. Except you." She finally turned her head. "And now they're doing it to you. Kneeling. Naming you. Deciding what you are." A pause, and then, quieter, with that terrible directness of hers: "How does it feel? Being loved by the place that threw you away?"

Tam opened her mouth to give the correct answer, the diplomatic answer, the answer an heir gives.

What came out instead was: "Like drowning in warm water. Like it feels wonderful right up until you realize you can't breathe."

Jude nodded slowly, as if Tam had said something wise instead of something true. "Then we go in breathing on purpose," she said. "Both of us. Whatever's waiting at that gate — the regent, the rite, the book rewriting itself in my pack — we walk in as people, not prices. Deal?"

Against Tam's spine, the Codex pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat, and she felt — distinctly, unmistakably — the broken line on its cover seal shut. Whole. Unbroken. Rewritten.

Somewhere ahead of them, miles down through the dark, water began to move where water had been still for a hundred years.

"Deal," Tam said, and did not tell her yet.
