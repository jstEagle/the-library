## 8. Salt Prayers

The deep road gave them a hollow to sleep in — a collapsed wayshrine older than the free cities, its walls still bearing salt-scarred niches where travelers once left offerings for gods who no longer answered. Jude had known a hundred places like it across her nine years of silence. She had prayed in all of them.

Tonight she meant to pray properly.

She told no one at first. She banked the fire low, checked Tam's bandages where the wardens' barbs had torn through her sleeve, and waited until Quill's breathing went slow and even against the far wall and Tam lay curled with her back to the coals, one hand still loosely closed around the hilt of a knife she'd never need in this company. Then Jude took out the things she carried for this purpose alone: a twist of grey cloth, and inside it, a palm-sized stone of raw sea salt she had cut from the cliffs below Brack the day she'd sworn her first oath.

The Silent Tide did not want much. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she'd told herself a thousand times on a thousand cold nights. The god of the deep wanted almost nothing — only attention, only the old words spoken into water, only a mortal willing to stand at the edge of the world's hearing and say *I am here* without demanding anything back. Most gods of the surface pantheons were tollkeepers. The Tide was supposed to be a listener.

For nine years it had not listened.

Jude knelt at the shrine's central basin, a shallow depression worn smooth by centuries of rain that no longer fell this far underground. She poured the water from her flask — the last of the clean stuff, saved from the wardens' camp — and watched it pool in the dark.

*Old words,* she thought. *You remember them or you don't.*

She remembered. Her mother had taught them to her kneeling on a beach at low tide, when Jude was small enough that the sea seemed like a country you could walk into and come back from. The rites weren't complicated. They were just precise, the way knots are precise, the way salt-measures are precise. A mercenary's prayer and a fisherman's prayer were the same prayer; the Tide didn't care what you'd done, only that you came honest.

She spoke the opening. Her voice sounded strange in the hollow — too loud, too human, scraping against stone that hadn't heard language in generations.

*"Salt to salt. Water to water. What is given to the deep returns changed."*

Nothing. Of course nothing. Nine years of nothing had taught her not to flinch at nothing.

But she kept going. That was the whole discipline of it, the thing Ossa had never understood about her faith even back when they'd shared bedrolls and rations and the particular intimacy of people who expect to die together. Ossa thought prayer was a transaction that had stopped paying out, so she'd stopped paying in. Jude had never been able to make her understand that the silence wasn't an unpaid debt. It was more like standing outside a house where someone you loved had died, knocking on a door that would never open, and knocking anyway — because the alternative was admitting the house was empty, and she could not admit that. Not yet. Possibly not ever.

*"I am Jude Dunmore,"* she said, in the second movement of the rite, the naming. *"Salt-sworn. Sworn at Brack, at the age of fourteen, by my own hand and my own blood."*

She opened the old scar on her palm as she said it, the line the knife had left a decade ago, and let three drops fall into the basin. The water took them without comment. Salt dissolved into salt.

*"I do not ask for power,"* she said, moving into the third part, the offering. *"I do not ask for victory. I ask—"*

And there she stopped, because this was the part that had changed.

For nine years the third movement had been the same: *I ask to know why you are silent.* It was the question that had kept her faithful through every ambush and every winter and every night she'd woken sure the god had never existed at all. But tonight, kneeling in a dead shrine under a mountain with a prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth six feet away and a drowned-court heir breathing softly by the fire, she found the question had rearranged itself while she wasn't looking.

*I ask you to tell me if I am already lost.*

Because that was the truth under everything since Thornfell. Not *why have you abandoned me.* Something worse. *Did you know? Did the silence mean you knew — about the Codex, about the Saltbride, about tonight's date written in margins a century dead? Have I spent nine years praying to a god who looked at me and saw a sacrifice waiting to be collected?*

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the wet stone.

*"Tell me if I am already yours,"* she whispered. *"That's all. Tell me if the choosing was ever mine."*

The flame guttered.

Not from wind — there was no wind down here, the air hung dead as the air in a tomb. It guttered the way a candle gutters when something vast moves past it, displacing what it passes through, and Jude felt it too, felt it against her skin before she heard anything: a pressure change, a depth-change, as though the little basin had suddenly become the mouth of a trench and the room had become the sea floor.

Tam sat up fast. Jude heard the whisper of her knife clearing its sheath, heard Quill stir and murmur, but none of it reached her properly. All of it reached her as if through water.

The water in the basin was moving.

It moved wrong — no ripple spreading inward from the edges, but a slow turning from the center, a rotation like something beneath the stone floor was stirring a bowl. And in the turning, the surface began to carry light it had no business carrying: a green-grey luminescence rising out of the depths of a half-inch of water, the light of the deep sea, the light of the drowned court, the light of places where the sun had never been invited.

Jude did not move. Later she would not be able to explain why she didn't move. Fear should have driven her back. Faith should have driven her forward. She did neither. She simply stayed, the way you stay at a graveside, because some moments are not about what you want.

A sound rose out of the basin.

It was not a voice. She had imagined, over nine years, what the Tide's voice might be — a booming, a whisper, a chorus. It was none of those. It was the sound of a wave withdrawing over shingle, drawn out and slowed until it shaped itself around something like consonants, something like meaning, and what it shaped was a single word.

*"SOON."*

Then the light sank, the turning stopped, and the water in the basin was just water again — a half-inch of her own drinking supply, reflecting firelight, holding nothing.

* * *

"Soon," Quill repeated. She had turned the word over perhaps forty times since Jude finished telling them what happened, and each time she turned it, her blind face grew a little more grim. "Soon. Not *well met, daughter.* Not *be at peace.* Not any of the ten thousand consolations a god might offer a servant who has begged at its door for nine years. Just — soon."

"Maybe it doesn't console," Tam said. She was sitting close to the fire now, knees drawn up, and she hadn't touched her knife since the light went out. "Maybe the deep court doesn't console. My aunt certainly doesn't."

"It isn't a question of consolation." Quill's hands moved as she spoke, the restless gestures of a woman accustomed to reading texts she could no longer see, shaping the argument in the air. "It's a question of grammar. A blessing has a shape. A warning has another. *Soon* is a promise made about someone else's schedule. It says: something is coming, and it is coming whether you are ready or not." She turned her face toward Jude, and though the eyes were white as pearl, Jude felt seen down to the bone. "Child, when you prayed — what exactly did you ask?"

"You know what I asked."

"I know what you think you asked. Say it anyway."

Jude looked at the basin. The water was gone now; she'd dumped it out onto the stones rather than drink it, which Tam had found hilarious and Quill had found significant. "I asked it to tell me if I was already lost. If the choosing was ever mine."

"And instead of answering, it said *soon.*"

"Instead of answering, it said soon."

Quill exhaled slowly through her nose. "Then here is what I believe, and I warn you it is not comfortable. A god that answers a question with a date does not intend to answer the question. A god that answers *am I lost* with *soon* is not reassuring you. It is counting."

The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the tunnels, water dripped with the patience of stone.

"That's mad," Tam said quietly. "She's served it her whole life. It wouldn't—"

"Wouldn't it?" Quill's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "What do we actually know of the Silent Tide, Tam of the drowned court? We know it went silent nine years ago. We know your court broke from the surface a century ago. We know the Codex names a bride-price and calls it a covenant. And we know that when a woman finally breaks through that silence — a woman the book itself has marked — the god spends its single word not on her question but on its own timing." She spread her hands. "I have spent fifty years among archives. In my experience, when a powerful party refuses to answer a direct question and instead tells you *when*, the answer to the question is one they prefer you learn after the fact."

Jude stood. Her legs felt distant, borrowed. "So what are you saying? That my god is going to feed me to whatever's coming and call it devotion?"

"I'm saying," Quill said, "that hope arrived tonight wearing exactly the face you've been praying toward for nine years, and I am asking you to notice that it arrived *after* the Codex named you, *after* the wardens knelt to Tam, *after* every step of a prophecy has begun arranging itself around your feet like a road building itself under a walker. Either your god has finally chosen to speak after nearly a decade of silence — or something has learned to imitate the voice you most wanted to hear, and it wants you walking confidently."

Silence took the shrine. Jude became aware, distantly, of Tam watching her — not with pity, which she could have borne, but with the careful blankness of someone deciding how much truth a person can hold.

"There's another possibility," Tam said at last.

"Name it," Jude said.

"That it answered because you're close. Because the Drowned Gate isn't far now, and the deep things can reach further near their own doors." Tam's jaw tightened. "And that *soon* means the court knows you're coming. Nerissa knows. The regency knows. Whatever they're preparing at the tide-peak — they're preparing it for your arrival, and the Tide just told you so to your face."

Jude laughed. It came out wrong, thin and sharp, the laugh of someone finding the trapdoor under the trapdoor. "So the options are: my god is lying to me, something else is lying to me, or my god is telling me the truth and the truth is that I'm expected."

"Yes," Quill said simply. "Those are the options. I'm sorry."

* * *

She took the middle watch because she didn't trust herself to sleep.

The others went under eventually — Quill first, muttering half-lines of scripture she'd committed to memory against blindness, then Tam, whose breathing found a rhythm only after long stillness, the rhythm of someone who had learned to sleep in exile and never fully trusted shelter again. Jude sat with her back to the wall and the Codex on her knees, unwrapped, its cover sigil faintly luminous in the dark: the drowned crown, the broken circle, the salt-line that ran through both.

She had read it four times since Thornfell. Each time, the margins had shifted a little. Tonight, for the first time, she noticed the shift happening to the page she'd memorized best — the page with her name.

*On the eve of the third moon, the Saltbrine rises,* the old text had said, in the archivist's crabbed hand. Now it read:

*On the eve of the third moon, the Saltbride comes willingly.*

Willingly. The word had not been there yesterday. She was certain it had not been there yesterday.

"Stop it," she whispered to the book. To the god behind the book. To whatever was listening. "I haven't agreed to anything. I don't agree to anything. Write that down, if your ink is so clever. *She refuses.* Put it in your margins. Let me watch you choke on it."

The pages did not move. But as she stared at them, in the space below the altered line, in an area that had been blank an hour ago — she would swear it had been blank an hour ago — new letters were surfacing the way bubbles surface in deep water, rising slowly through the vellum itself:

*and yet she goes.*

Jude closed the book hard enough to sting her own palms.

She sat in the dark afterward with her scarred hand pressed flat against the cover, feeling her heart hammer against the bone of her wrist, and understood at last what the feeling was — the thing underneath the dread and the anger and the tired ache of nine unanswered years. It was the feeling of a door closing that she had mistaken for a door opening. Every sign she'd taken as grace — the answered prayer, the word, the warmth of being *known* after so long unseen — pointed the same direction as the Codex, the wardens, the prophecy, the tide. Everything in her life had begun converging on a single point, and convergence felt like destiny, and destiny felt like meaning, and meaning felt like the thing she'd starved for since she was fourteen years old with a knife in her hand.

But a net also converges, she thought. A net converges beautifully, right up until it closes.

In her sleep, Tam murmured something in the drowned tongue — soft, unguarded, the accent of home leaking through exile. Jude listened to it the way you listen to rain, and hated herself a little for how much comfort it gave her, and how much danger that comfort was worth to anyone clever enough to price it.

Far off in the deep roads, down passages no map had charted in a hundred years, water was moving where water should not move — rising, patient, keeping a schedule. Jude couldn't hear it yet.

But *soon*, the dark seemed to say, in a voice like a wave drawing back over stone. *Soon.*
