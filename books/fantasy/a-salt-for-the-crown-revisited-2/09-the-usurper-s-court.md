## 9. The Usurper's Court

Serah Dane had burned a hundred heretics and never once doubted the fire.

She had been nineteen when the Inquisition found her, an orphan of the Salt Wars with nothing to her name but a talent for reading and a hunger for certainty. Kaelen Voss's inquisitors did not recruit the faithful; they recruited the frightened. Serah had been both. They gave her grey robes, a ring of cold iron, and a doctrine clean as a blade: the old gods were liars, the covenant was superstition, and the free cities would only be safe under one crown, one law, one truth. She had believed it the way drowning people believe in air.

Nine years later she stood in Voss's war camp beneath the Thorn passes, holding a leather-wrapped fragment of the Century Codex as though it were a live coal, and for the first time she felt the ground tilt beneath her certainty.

* * *

The camp sprawled across the high valley like a wound in the mountain's flank — ten thousand tents, black banners stitched with Voss's new sigil, a crown over crossed chains. Smoke from ten thousand cookfires rose straight into the windless sky, a grey pillar visible from leagues away, as if the usurper wanted the whole world to know where he slept. Serah had always admired that about him. Secrecy was for thieves. Voss intended to be a king, and kings announced themselves.

The command pavilion stood at the valley's heart, taller than any tent had a right to be, its poles carved from the masts of captured ships. Two inquisitors flanked the entrance, motionless as salt pillars. Inside, the air was warm and close, lit by braziers that burned something sweeter than wood — sea-coal, imported at ruinous cost, because Voss liked the smell of the ocean he intended to own.

He sat behind a table of black oak, and he was not what strangers expected. The broadsheets painted him as a giant, a warlord hewn from granite. In truth he was a lean, precise man with a scholar's hands and a voice like a door closing slowly. His hair was going grey at the temples, and he wore it long, bound back with a cord of woven silver. Only his eyes betrayed him — pale, almost colourless, eyes that had looked at too many burning things without blinking.

"Inquisitor Dane." He did not rise. Kings-in-waiting did not rise for anyone. "You've brought me a book."

"A fragment of a book, Your Radiance." She set the leather wrapping on the oak and unwound it with the care of a surgeon. Beneath lay the Codex fragment Ossa Vane had sold them — three gatherings of pages, water-stained, their edges crusted white with salt that no amount of handling had ever removed. "Purchased through intermediaries from the mercenary captain of the Saltcloaks. She claimed it came from a smuggler's cache at Thornfell. She claimed it was incomplete." Serah paused. "I believe her on both counts."

Voss leaned forward. He did not touch the fragment. He never touched relics himself — that was what inquisitors were for, hands that could be washed or burned if they soured. Instead he studied it the way a man studies a snake: head slightly tilted, weight on his back foot.

"Read me the passage," he said.

Serah had read it forty times on the road south, and each time the words had sat differently in her mouth. She opened to the marked leaf, where the vellum had gone translucent with age, and translated from the Old Tongue as she had been taught — though who had taught the teachers, none of them ever asked.

"'When the tide turns the hundredth year, a bride of salt shall walk between the courts. Her name is written in water. Her date is written in blood. What she binds, no crown may hold. What she refuses, every crown will burn.'"

Silence held the pavilion for the space of six heartbeats. Serah counted them out of habit. Counting kept the fear orderly.

"And the name?" Voss said.

"Jude Dunmore." Serah watched his face as she said it, the way she had learned to watch faces over kindling. "A mercenary of the Silent Tide. Salt-sworn. And the date—" she hesitated, because the date was the part that had kept her awake for three nights running, "—the date given in the margin is eleven days past. The night the waystation at Thornfell burned."

"Your raid," Voss said. It was not a question.

"My raid, Your Radiance. We burned the cache. We took the caravan master. We did not find the Codex entire, because the woman named in it had already carried it away." Serah's voice tightened. "Which means either the prophecy is false, or it has already begun."

* * *

Voss rose then, finally, and walked to the pavilion's eastern wall, where a map of the known world hung in dyed leather — the free cities along the coast, the drowned court marked beneath the sea in faded ink, and above it all, freshly painted, the crown-and-chains. He stood before it with his hands clasped behind his back, and Serah knew better than to speak until spoken to. Nine years in the grey robes had taught her the geometry of power: you waited in the silence until the powerful shape it for you.

"Do you know why I burned the temples, Dane?" he said at last, without turning.

"Because the gods were instruments of the old houses. Because faith breeds faction."

"Because they are real." He turned, and there was something in his face that Serah had never seen there before — not the cold satisfaction of the true believer, but something hungrier and more afraid. "Every priest I burned screamed names at me as they died. Not curses. Names. Invocations. And in nine years of burning, not one of those gods ever answered them. Do you know what that told me?"

"That they were empty rituals," Serah said, because that was the doctrine.

"It told me they were *silent*." Voss came back toward the table, each step deliberate. "Not absent. Silent. There is a difference, and the difference is the most important fact in the world. A god that does not exist needs no reason for quiet. A god that exists and chooses silence is hiding something." He laid one scholar's finger on the Codex fragment, not quite touching the vellum. "And this book knows what."

Serah felt the first crack then, thin as a hairline fracture in glaze. Because the doctrine said the gods were lies, and here was the man who had built the Inquisition saying they were real — real enough to hide, real enough to fear, real enough to want.

"You believe the covenant is genuine," she said carefully. "The old bond between the surface realms and the drowned court."

"I believe it broke a century ago, and everything since has been rot spreading from the break." Voss's colourless eyes fixed on her. "The seas have been rising wrong. The tides come unscheduled. Fishermen drown in calm water. You've read the reports; you wrote half of them yourself, filing them under omens and portents and *superstition*. But superstition doesn't have a schedule, Dane. A broken treaty does."

"And your solution—"

"Is to reforge it." He said it simply, the way other men said *harvest* or *tax*. "Under my crown. Sealed by my authority. The free cities made safe by binding the deep court to a throne that can hold it, instead of leaving it loose beneath us like a knife in the dark."

It was, Serah had to admit, coherent. That was the trouble with Voss. Every horror he ordered arrived wrapped in logic so tight you had to cut yourself free of it, and the cutting always left scars. She had burned a midwife in Brackwater because her logic had been equally tight: one woman's death against a plague's spread. She had not slept well after. She had done it anyway.

"But the prophecy," she pressed, "names a Saltbride. Jude Dunmore. Not a king."

"Yes." Voss smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the pavilion. "The prophecy is very specific about the instrument and entirely vague about the purpose. Read it again. 'What she binds, no crown may hold.' The old scribes meant it as a warning — whatever covenant she makes will belong to no throne. But prophecies are like contracts, Dane. They bind the letter, not the spirit. If the Saltbride performs the rite, the covenant is reforged. Full stop. The book does not say whose seal sits upon it afterward." He tapped the map, on the crown-and-chains. "Mine will."

"And Dunmore herself?"

"What do the margins say happens to the bride?"

Serah had read the margins forty times too. She recited them now and heard, for the first time, how much they sounded like a butcher's ledger. "'Her heart becomes the seal. Her breath becomes the tide. Her name is given to the water, and the water keeps it.'"

"She dies," Voss said, when she finished. "Of course she dies. Covenants of this age were always sealed in blood — look at the founding rites of any of the free cities, once you scrape the festival paint off them. The Saltbride is not a queen. She is mortar." He folded his hands on the black oak. "I don't need her willing. I need her present, and alive until the rite completes, and then the sea can keep what the sea is owed. My inquisitors are very good at keeping people alive exactly as long as required. You taught half of them that skill yourself."

There it was. The crack widened, and cold light came through it.

Serah Dane had always understood that her work involved killing. She had made her peace with it in the specific, transactional way that soldiers and surgeons make peace: each death weighed, justified, filed. But this was different. This was a woman — a mercenary, yes, a heretic-sworn servant of a silent god, but a woman with a name and a date written in a century-old book — being discussed as *mortar*. As material. And the man discussing her was the man Serah had given her life to, whose grey cause she had believed was the last clean thing in a dirty world.

She thought of the waystation at Thornfell. The caravan master they had taken — Marta Ellik, grey-haired, who had spat at Serah and called her the Crown's broom and been thrown in a cage wagon anyway. Serah had ordered the cage locked herself. She remembered thinking, that night, that the old woman would probably hang, and that this was acceptable, because order was acceptable, because order was everything.

Now she heard Voss say *mortar* in the same tone she used for *acceptable*, and felt the floor of her certainty tilt like a deck in weather.

"Your Radiance," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "if the rite requires the Saltbride's death, and the covenant reforged under your seal requires the rite — then your crown will be founded on a sacrifice. Exactly like the old crowns you burned the temples to overthrow."

Voss went still. For a moment he looked genuinely surprised, as though she had said something no one had ever said to him before — which, Serah realized with a chill, might actually be true. Everyone around him was too frightened or too ambitious to point at the foundation and ask what it was made of.

"The old crowns were founded on *chaos*," he said finally. "On bargains struck in the dark by priests and fish-kings. Mine will be founded on design. On intention. That is the difference, Dane, and it is the only difference that has ever mattered in history." He straightened. "But you raise a practical concern, and I reward practical concerns. Find her. Jude Dunmore and whoever carries her — the exile, the archivist, whichever traitors the Saltcloaks' captain failed to mention. Bring them to me intact. The rite must happen at the Drowned Gate at tide-peak, and the tides are turning early this season. We have weeks, not months."

"And if she refuses?" Serah asked. "If the prophecy can be refused?"

Something moved behind Voss's pale eyes — a flicker, quickly buried, of the same question asked and answered in the dark hours of some sleepless night. "Then we will discover," he said softly, "whether destiny is stronger than iron. And either answer serves me. Go. Take the fragment to the scriptorium tent. I want every marginal note transcribed by morning."

Serah gathered the Codex fragment, bowed the precise depth required, and turned for the pavilion door.

* * *

Outside, the mountain air hit her like cold water, and she stood between the salt-pillar guards and let it scour her.

Below the command rise, the camp went about its business — foragers stacking brushwood, smiths hammering, a line of prisoners from the pass villages digging latrines under whips. Somewhere a chaplain of the new creed was preaching the gospel of the One Crown to a circle of bored soldiers, his voice bouncing off the tents: *one law, one truth, one throne.* Serah had preached that sermon herself, twice, in cities that later burned.

She looked down at the Codex fragment in her arms. Salt-crusted vellum, older than the Inquisition, older than Voss's ambition, patient as tide. *Her heart becomes the seal.*

For nine years she had believed she was sweeping the world clean. Now she saw the broom's other end: a woman named in water, hunted by iron, destined for an altar — and a king who called her mortar and smiled.

Serah Dane began walking toward the scriptorium tent, and her steps were steady, and her face was the correct blank mask of a good inquisitor, and inside her chest a crack was widening with every stride, and she did not yet know what she would do when it broke all the way open.

She only knew that she had started counting heartbeats again. And that somewhere north of the passes, a woman with her name in a dead book was running out of road — and Serah was the one they would send down it.
