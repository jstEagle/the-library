## 10. The Drowned Gate

Mother Quill had been blind for forty years, and in that time she had learned that the world spoke most honestly through its floors.

Stone told her the truth of a place. The flagstones of Brack's archive had been worn smooth by a thousand years of scholars' feet, each hollow a small confession of patience. The smuggler roads beneath the mountain had spoken to her of haste and fear, their surfaces cracked where boots had run, patched with newer stone where someone had tried to hide the passing of many feet. And now, as the three of them — Jude ahead with her salt-knife drawn, Tam behind with her hand resting light between Quill's shoulder blades — descended the last stair of the deep road, the floor beneath Quill's sandals said something she had never felt before.

It said *ocean*.

Not the memory of ocean. Not salt crust or tide-marked stone. The rock itself was porous here, riddled with channels fine as veins, and through those channels came a cold that was not the mountain's cold. It breathed. Slowly. Rhythmically. The whole staircase was breathing with something vast below it, and every exhalation carried the mineral ghost of water so old it had forgotten the sun.

"Stop," Quill said.

Jude stopped. Tam's hand lifted from her back.

"The gate is close," Quill said. "I can hear it."

"You're blind," Jude said, not unkindly. She had never quite learned how to say such things gently; she said them the way she did everything, blunt as a hammer, and Quill had come to love that about her the way one loves a storm's honesty.

"I am blind, girl, which is why I can hear it. You two keep looking at things with your eyes and mistaking what you see for all there is." Quill turned her face toward the sound. "There is water moving below us. A great deal of it. Moving the way a congregation moves when it has been waiting a very long time for the service to begin."

Tam made a low sound in her throat. When she spoke, her voice had that doubled quality it took on near drowned-things, as if another voice swam just beneath her own, an echo a half-beat late. "The Drowned Gate doesn't open from this side," she said. "I told you that. I told you it opens when it decides you've paid."

"And we've discussed the toll." Quill smoothed her robes with both hands, a habit from a lifetime of lecterns. "Memory. One memory, freely given, per soul seeking passage. Yes. You've told me four times now, child, and each time you've looked at me like a woman about to watch a dog be put down. I wish you'd stop. It makes me feel sentimental, and sentiment before a transaction is how merchants get robbed."

Jude laughed — a short, sharp bark with no humor in it. "She's going to do it. She's already decided. Look at her."

"I don't need to look at anything," Quill said, "to know that you are both planning to stand in front of that gate and argue with it on my behalf, and that the argument will cost us time we do not have, and that Voss's inquisitors are perhaps two days behind us on the deep road, because I counted their boots in the echo at the last junction. Forty-two pairs, marching in cadence. Inquisitors march in cadence. Smugglers don't. Soldiers only mostly do." She folded her hands into her sleeves. "So we will not waste our remaining hours arguing. We will walk down to the gate, and we will pay, and we will go through, and you will both be furious with me, and I will find it very restful to be furious at for once instead of furious at the world."

The stair went down. They followed it.

* * *

The Drowned Gate revealed itself slowly, the way truth does.

First there was only the widening of the stair into a cavern so vast their footsteps lost all echo, swallowed by distance. Then the air changed — thickened, took on weight, pressed against the skin like a wet hand. Then the smell: brine and pearl-dust and something floral and rotten underneath, the scent of a garden grown in the dark. And then, at last, the light.

It came from below. That was the thing Quill could not have imagined and did not need to; the light simply arrived against her ruined eyes as warmth, faint and green-gold, rising out of the earth like heat off summer stone but cooler, patient, tidal. Bioluminescence, Tam called it later — the drowned court's gardens growing up through the threshold, pale fronds of glowing weed draped over the gate's frame like funeral garlands.

The gate itself stood at the cavern's heart: two pillars of black coral, grown — not carved, *grown* — into arches that interlocked without touching, and between them, where a door should have been, hung the sea.

Not water. The sea. Vertical, upright, a wall of deep water standing in the air as if the ocean had been asked to stand at attention and had obeyed for a hundred years. Fish moved in it. Far down within its green depths, shapes drifted that might have been kelp and might have been banners. The surface of it rippled though no wind touched the cavern, and each ripple sent a sound across the stone like a whispered syllable in a language with no living native speakers above the waves.

Quill stood before it and listened with her whole self.

"It's reading us," she murmured. "Do you feel it? Like an archivist going through pockets."

"I feel it," Jude said grimly. Her knife-hand hadn't relaxed since the stair. "It's deciding."

"It's remembering," Tam corrected softly. "The Gate keeps the ledger of everything that ever passed through. Every memory ever paid into it is still in there. That's the price and the treasury both." Her doubled voice caught. "My mother paid hers. To leave. I've wondered my whole exile what she chose to forget. Whether it was me."

No one answered that. There was nothing to answer it with.

The water-wall stirred. A tendril of it extended — a rope of seawater thick as a man's arm, hanging in the air, its tip quivering like a scrying pendulum. It swung toward Jude first, hovered before her face. She flinched; the drop of brine that fell from its tip struck the stone and hissed.

"*Salt-sworn,*" said the Gate. Its voice came from everywhere, from the stone and the water and the bones of the mountain, vast and unhurried and old past reckoning. "*The Saltbride. The Codex-bearer. Your toll was paid before you were born. Pass.*"

"I didn't pay anything," Jude said.

"*You will.*" The tendril withdrew from her face and swung toward Tam, and Quill heard the heir's breath catch. "*Little tide-that-left. Your name is written in the ledger in your mother's hand. The court remembers what it banished. Pass — and be unwelcome.*"

"That's fair," Tam whispered, and stepped forward, and the wall of standing sea opened around her like a wound closing, and closed again behind her, leaving only ripple-sound and the fading print of bare feet on wet stone.

Which left Quill alone before the Gate, and the Gate alone with Quill, and both of them knowing exactly what came next.

"No," Jude said.

Quill smiled. She knew he could see it even if he could not see it — some people's smiles carry, the way bells do.

"Girl. Jude. Come here." She held out her hand and Jude took it, and Quill guided that rough, callused mercenary hand up to her own face, pressed it flat against her cheek the way her novices used to when they were small and frightened. "Feel that? Ninety-one years of face. I have outlived my library, my order, my eyes, my knees, and every person who ever called me by my first name — and I will tell you what my first name was, since I'm the only one left who knows it. It was Maren. Maren Quillon. My mother named me after a saint who drowned saving fishermen, which everyone agreed was either prophetic or cruel."

"Don't," Jude said. Her voice had gone rough. "Quill—"

"Listen to me. I spent my life reading other people's destinies and telling them what they must do, and it was easy, because I never had to do any of it. Destiny was always a book on someone else's shelf." Quill squeezed the hand against her cheek. "Now destiny is standing in front of me wearing the face of a frightened mercenary girl, and I find, at the end, that I finally understand my own life's work. It was never the books, child. It was this. Being the one who stands in the doorway so that others can pass through it."

"The Gate takes memories. Your life's work — the Codex translation, everything—"

"Then it may have my life's work." Quill said it lightly, lightly, the way one sets down something heavy so no one hears the strain. "What use have I for it now? I read the prophecy backwards and learned its ugly fine print, and I passed that knowledge to you, and you carry it now. Knowledge given away cannot be taken. Only the having of it goes. Let the Gate have the having." She turned her blind face toward the standing sea, and her voice gentled. "And let it leave me whatever it leaves. I'll make a life of it. I always have."

"There has to be another way—"

"There is always another way, and it is always worse, and it is always later." Quill drew her hand free and patted Jude's cheek, twice, briskly, the way she had patted a thousand frightened students. "Two days' march behind us are forty-two men in cadence who want the book in your pack and your heart on Voss's altar. There is no version of the future where I cross that threshold with my memories intact and you live long enough to refuse your prophecy properly. So. Choose which refusal you want: mine, or yours. Because yours is the important one."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in it, Jude Dunmore — who had sworn vows to a silent god and broken faith with companies and buried friends in shallow graves along smuggler roads — bowed her head over an old blind woman's hands and wept, briefly and furiously, the way soldiers weep, hating every second of it.

Then she kissed Quill's brow, salt on salt, and stepped back.

* * *

Quill faced the Gate.

The tendril of standing water rose before her and did not swing like a pendulum this time. It waited. Courteous. An usher at a door.

"*Old one,*" the Gate said, and its vastness bent around something almost like respect. "*You have carried more dead words than any soul in the ledger. Name your toll.*"

"How does it work?" Quill asked. "Truly. I've read secondhand accounts all my life and they disagree."

"*A memory is weighed, not chosen entire. You will offer. The Gate will take what balances.*"

"Then I offer my life's work." She spread her empty hands. "Every page of the Century Codex as I read it. Every cipher I broke. Every hour in the archives of Brack. The prophecy, the fine print, the sacrifice, the crown — take the whole of my scholarship, freely given, and open your door."

"*And what will remain, if the weighing takes it all?*"

Quill considered. This was, she reflected, the first honest examination she'd faced in ninety-one years, and there was something clean about that too.

"Whatever I am underneath the work," she said. "I suppose we'll find out together."

"*Freely given?*"

"Freely given. Witnessed by the Saltbride and the tide-that-left, and by whatever god is listening, silent or otherwise. Freely given, and gladly, and without regret." She paused, and allowed herself one dishonesty, small and human: "Mostly without regret."

The tendril touched her forehead.

Cold — then not cold. Light poured backward through her, or she poured forward through light; it was impossible to say which. And Quill felt her life's work lift out of her the way silt lifts off a riverbed: the long nights in the Brack archives, the smell of vellum and lamp-oil, the cipher keys unfolding in her mind like flowers, the Codex's pages under her fingertips, the horror of the fine print, the bride-price, the seal, the sacrifice — gone, gone, gone, each memory sliding out smooth as a splinter drawn from flesh, and stranger still, it did not hurt. It felt like being forgiven.

What remained was smaller and somehow sturdier. A mother's voice saying *Maren*. Salt wind on a harbor wall. The weight of a novice asleep against her shoulder. A blind old woman's hands learning a new room by its floors. Two young women somewhere nearby whose names she was suddenly, urgently certain she loved, though she could not for the life of her have said why, or how she knew them, or what peril they were walking into together.

She blinked eyes that had seen nothing for forty years and saw — nothing still, nothing yet, but a *warmth* where the world would be, green-gold, tidal, breathing.

"Oh," Mother Quill said, to the Gate, to the sea, to whoever she now was. "Oh, that's what I was for."

The wall of standing water parted. Warm light rolled out over the stone like a welcome home. And Quill walked through it unafraid, her empty hands open, her ledger wiped clean, carrying nothing across the threshold of the drowned world except the only things the Gate could not weigh:

her faith, and her love, and the strange new certainty — wordless, sourceless, absolute — that the two women following behind her were going to need her, very soon, for something no book had ever predicted.

Behind her, Jude Dunmore watched the sea close over the old woman like a curtain, and swore into the silence of her silent god that whatever waited on the other side, it would be sorry it had asked for a toll from her family.
