## 13. Nerissa's Tide

They came for Tam in the hour before dawn, when the pearl halls were dimmest and the tide-songs of the court had thinned to a single low drone, like a held breath.

She woke to the sound of coral shackles closing — a wet, grinding click that she knew from childhood, from stories told to frighten heir-children into obedience. *The traitor's bracelet. The collaborator's kiss.* She had thought those stories fables. She had been wrong about so much, lately, and this was only the newest item on a long and lengthening list.

Four wardens stood around her sleeping-couch in the guest chamber Nerissa had assigned her — a chamber she now understood had never been a courtesy but a cage with softer walls. Their armor was nacre and deep-green kelp-weave, their faces hidden behind masks of smoked glass through which their eyes showed as pale smudges. She knew two of them by voice. Warden Sef, who had taught her to read the pressure-currents when she was small enough to ride her father's shoulders through the market-tunnels. And the youngest one, Brine-adopted, whose name was Liss and who had wept openly at Tam's presentation before the court three nights past, when the exile was revoked and the circlet of heirship set upon her brow.

*Liss, who is here now, holding the other end of my chains.*

"You come armed," Tam said, sitting up slowly, "into an heir's chamber."

"We come on the regent's writ," said Sef, and his voice was careful, flattened, the voice of a man walking across ice he did not trust. "Tam of the drowned court, styled heir, you are accused of collaboration with surface powers against the security of the realm. You will be held in the Salt Cells until the rite is concluded."

"The rite." Tam laughed, and it came out ugly. "You mean the rite that was to be held at the next full tide. Six days hence."

Sef's mask did not move. But behind the smoked glass, his eyes did.

"The tide has been moved," he said. "By regent's decree. It rises tomorrow."

*Tomorrow.* The word went through her like a harpoon through the gill-slits. Tomorrow. Which meant Nerissa had decided before the presentation, before the circlet, before the homecoming — perhaps before Tam had even passed the Drowned Gate. All of it, the kneeling wardens, the ancestral hall, the singing of the old names — theater staged around an altar already dressed.

"Then take me," Tam said, standing, letting them bind her wrists in coral that bit cold and slow, drinking warmth from her skin the way the deep places drank light. "But know that I will say what I have to say, loudly, in whatever cell you put me in, and the walls of this court are thin with grievance."

"Save your breath," said Sef quietly, as they marched her out. And then, so low only she could hear it, in the old cadence of the nursery-tunnels they had both grown up speaking: *"The cells face the tide-well, little heir. Sound carries up the well. We will listen."*

* * *

The Salt Cells were cut into the bone-white foundations of the Pearl Hall itself, below the waterline of the court's great cisterns, where the pressure made the stone sing faintly at all hours. Tam had seen them once as a child, on one of her father's grim tours of the realm's instruments of justice. He had hated them, she remembered now. He had stood in that corridor with his hand on her head and said, *Never let them use these rooms while I live, Tam. A court that jails its own blood has already begun to eat itself.*

Her father had been dead eleven years. The rooms were very much in use.

They put her in the third cell, which had a barred window onto the tide-well shaft — a vertical throat of black water rising through the heart of the palace, up which the tides climbed twice a day to fill the cisterns above. Through it she could hear the whole court breathing: the shift-change drums, the market-noise of the lower terraces, the warden barracks' evening prayers to the Silent Tide, prayed, as always, in silence.

And through it, in time, she began to make herself heard.

She did not scream. Screaming was what Nerissa wanted; a screaming prisoner confirmed the charge of instability, of surface-corruption, of a mind unmade by exile. Instead Tam sat with her back to the bars and spoke — clearly, steadily, in the cadence of the old storytellers — about her father. About the covenant as it had actually been, before it broke. About the first sacrifice, the name the Codex had shown Jude, the truth the gods had gone silent to bury. About a bride-price dressed up as destiny, and a regent who would pay it with another woman's heart rather than risk her own grip on the throne.

At first, no one came. Then the guards outside her door stopped changing shifts at the usual intervals — because they were lingering, listening. Then someone left food that was better than prison food, wrapped in oilcloth, with no note. Then, on the second night, a folded scrap of kelp-paper slid under the door bearing a single line in a warden's cipher she hadn't seen since childhood:

*The Third Cohort remembers your father. Say more.*

So she said more.

* * *

Jude, meanwhile, was being prepared.

They told her it was preparation. They used gentle words for it — *anointing, attuning, the washing of the bride* — and they performed it with the terrible tenderness of people who believe cruelty and kindness cannot share a room. Wardens of the regent's own house, women with kind faces and salt-grey robes, bathed her in water drawn from the covenant-well. They combed her hair with combs of white coral. They fed her foods she did not ask for, rich with deep-sea sugar, meant to plump the offering. When she refused to eat, they waited. When she refused again, they fed her by other means, and apologized while they did it.

"They are not monsters," Quill had once said of institutions, back in Brack, in another life. *"That's what makes them dangerous. Monsters know they're monstrous."*

Jude fought them the only way left to her: with stillness. She gave them nothing. Not tears, not rage, not pleas. She lay where they put her, walked where they led, and inside the fortress of her skull she kept hold of two things only: Tam's face, and the single drowned word the Silent Tide had given her on the road — the word Quill had called a warning.

*Soon,* the word had meant. Or possibly *beware*. In the drowned tongue, the archivists admitted, the two shared a root.

On the eve of the rite, Nerissa came to her chamber personally.

The regent filled the doorway the way deep water fills a trench — not entering so much as arriving, pressure preceding her. She was beautiful the way a shipwreck is beautiful, all sweeping lines and patient ruin, and her crown was a circlet of black pearl that drank the lamplight.

"You hate me," Nerissa said. It was not a question, and she did not seem to require an answer. "Good. Hate keeps the heart loud. A loud heart seals better than a quiet one. The last Saltbride was serene, and look how long her covenant lasted."

"The last Saltbride," Jude said, "was murdered."

"She was *consecrated*." For just a moment something moved behind the regent's composure — something old and tired and horribly like grief. "You think I do not know what I do? I have read the same pages you have, mercenary. I have sat with the arithmetic of it longer than you have been alive. One heart, freely or otherwise, against two realms drowning in war. I would pay that price myself if the covenant would take me. It will not. It wants what it always wanted." Her eyes, pale as fish-belly, settled on Jude with something almost like respect. "It wants a bride who chose the sea over the land. That is why the Codex named you. That is why the god went quiet nine years ago and woke when you prayed. You are not its victim, Jude Dunmore. You are its answer."

"And if I refuse at the altar? Before everyone?"

Nerissa smiled, and it was the saddest thing Jude had seen since Quill died.

"Then everything my sister's line built drowns, Voss takes the Gate, and I will spend eternity knowing I flinched. So you will not refuse. You will be bound, anointed, and carried if need be. The rite does not require your consent, child. Only your presence." She turned to go, then paused. "Your warden-lover sings to the tide-well at night. Did you know? Half the Third Cohort stands her watch outside your door out of love for her, and the other half out of love for you. My court is splitting down its seams, and I am out of time to mend it. Pray your god is worth it."

* * *

Tam heard the rite had been moved to dawn — true dawn, the surface dawn, the hour when the drowned court's oldest laws said no binding may occur — and understood finally the shape of Nerissa's desperation. The regent was not merely hurrying. She was breaking her own law, and she knew it, and she was doing it anyway because the alternative was Voss at the Gate with a fragment of the crown in his fist.

That was when the Third Cohort came for her.

They did not come with trumpets or decrees. They came the way water comes — through cracks, all at once. Sef opened her cell with his own seal-ring. Liss pressed a warden's cloak into her hands, still warm from her own shoulders. A dozen masked faces crowded the corridor, and beyond them, in the dark of the tide-well shaft, more shapes gathered — wardens off-shift, armor half-buckled, some carrying the old banner of Tam's father's house that had not flown in eleven years.

"No one is ordering anyone," Sef said. "No decree. If the court learns of this, every one of us hangs in the kelp-gardens. We choose it. Understand? We *choose* it." His mask tilted toward her, and behind the smoked glass his eyes were bright and afraid. "Your father said a covenant is only real if it can be refused. We are refusing, little heir. Take us somewhere useful."

Tam looked at them — her father's soldiers, her mother's language, her homeland's stubborn, grieving heart — and felt something in her chest crack open that was not despair for the first time in days.

"Then we go to the Pearl Hall," she said. "We go to stop a wedding."

Above them, far up the black throat of the tide-well, the first grey light of surface-dawn was beginning to fall like snow through the water — and with it, muffled by fathoms, came a sound none of them had heard in a hundred years.

The Silent Tide, clearing its throat.
