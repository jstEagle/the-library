## 15. Voss at the Gate

The sea went wrong before anyone in the pearl hall understood why.

Jude felt it first as a pressure in her teeth, the old salt-sworn ache that had been her only conversation with the Silent Tide for nine years. Then the tide itself stuttered. She was standing at one of the high windows of the court, watching the deep water beyond the drowned terraces, when the currents that had run in patient silver ribbons for a century simply *stopped*—hung motionless like a held breath—and then reversed, dragging inward, toward the Drowned Gate, as though the whole ocean had been told to inhale and could not refuse.

Around her, the court's music faltered. The pearl-bell choristers in the lower galleries let their song trail off note by note, the way a congregation goes quiet when the doors bang open at the back of the temple.

"They're here," Jude said, though no one had asked her. Her voice sounded small in the vast hall. "The Gate. They're at the Gate."

Tam was across the chamber, deep in argument with the wardens who had knelt to her in the mountain roads—wardens who now stood in the pearl hall like exiles twice over, loyal to a princess their regent kept under guard. Tam looked up. Even across that gulf of marble and murmuring courtiers, Jude saw the moment the wrongness reached her too: the slight widening of her dark eyes, the hand that went instinctively to the salt-charm at her throat, the one Jude had braided for her on the road beneath the mountain from strands of her own hair and a knot of blessed cord that had cost them a night's argument and a kiss neither of them had named.

Then the horns began.

They were not horns of shell, like the court's own, which sang in long mournful harmonies that seemed to rise out of the water itself. These were iron horns, human horns, and they should not have carried underwater at all. But they did. They came through the stone and the sea and the marrow of Jude's bones, each blast a hammerfall, and with every note the pressure in the water grew, and the pearl-light of the great lamps dimmed as if something vast had passed between the drowned court and its sun.

*The crown fragment,* Jude thought, and the thought was ice water. *He's brought the crown fragment.*

She had heard the whispers for days—Nerissa's regent-council murmuring over maps of the Gate, wardens reporting that Voss's dredge-ships had been lowering cages into the shallows above, divers in black lacquer armor descending with something wrapped in lead and chained to a palanquin of iron. A fragment of the crown of the free cities. The crown that, if the Codex's rewritten pages were true—and every day Jude believed them more—had been forged from the covenant's first sacrifice, from a heart given to the Tide a thousand years ago and hammered into gold.

Old covenant-metal. It remembered the way in.

* * *

The defenses of the drowned court were beautiful, and they failed beautifully.

Jude watched from the Gate terrace with Tam at her side—Tam, whose guards had finally, grudgingly, allowed her to move freely now that the court needed every heir it had—and what she saw would stay with her the rest of her life, however long the tides let that be.

The wardens sang the Gate closed. Hundreds of them, ranked on the terraces and in the water beyond, their voices braiding into the old harmonies, and the Drowned Gate itself—a vast arch of black coral and covenant-stone, crusted with a century of silence—began to glow with a light like moonrise seen from underwater. The sea between the arch and the invading host thickened, turned to something between water and glass. Jude felt the prayer of it in her salt-sworn bones: *not through here, not through here, not through here.*

Voss's answer was the fragment.

They brought it forward on its iron palanquin, and even wrapped in lead Jude could feel it, a wrongness like a toothache in the world's jaw. When they unwrapped it—when the divers bore it up to the Gate's threshold on a bier of chains—the glass-sea *screamed*. Not the water. The covenant itself. Jude heard it with the part of her soul that had been salt-sworn since she was sixteen years old, and she doubled over the terrace rail and was sick into the deep water, because the fragment sang a note that the Gate knew, that the Gate had been built from, that the Gate had *missed* for a thousand years.

The black coral began to crack.

It did not shatter. That would have been almost merciful. It cracked the way ice cracks on a frozen river—slow, branching, singing—and through each crack came the light of Voss's lanterns, the shadows of his dredge-ships, the black lacquer gleam of his vanguard swimming through the breach with the fragment borne before them like a relic, like a god, like a wound.

"They're through," Tam said. Her voice was flat with a grief Jude recognized: the grief of watching a home be hurt and being unable to stand in front of it. "Jude. They're through the Gate."

The vanguard did not rush the terraces. That was the worst of it. They came slowly, in perfect ranks, and they *knelt* at the Gate's inner threshold—knelt to the fragment, to the crown-piece, to the old sacrifice it embodied—and when they rose they advanced with the unhurried confidence of an army that knows the battle is already a matter of bookkeeping.

Behind them, filling the Gate arch from side to side, came the inquisitors in their grey, and behind the inquisitors, far back in the dark of the breach, Jude saw the banners. Black salt-crowns on white. The sigil of Kaelen Voss, come at last to claim both worlds.

* * *

The pearl hall had become a war council, and a bad one.

Nerissa stood at the center of it, and Jude, watching from the edge of the chamber with Quill's memory-toll still a hollow ache in her heart—the old archivist had given her life's work to open this road, and Jude still caught herself turning to say something and finding no one—saw the regent with new eyes.

Nerissa had been iron since Jude arrived. Iron in her decrees, iron in her hatred of Tam's exile-revoked presence, iron in her certainty that the prophecy was a door and the Saltbride was the key. Jude had hated her for it, quietly, the way you hate weather.

But now the iron was bending. The regent's hands, clasped before her on the war-table, were trembling. Her voice, when she gave orders, cracked on the third word and she had to begin again. Reports came in from the outer terraces—vanguard holding the Gate's inner court, inquisitors advancing up the Processional, casualties among the wardens, casualties, casualties—and each report landed on her like a stone on wet clay.

"The Processional will fall within the hour," said the warden-captain, a scarred old eel-blooded soldier named Vashen who had knelt to Tam on the mountain road and had not straightened all the way since. "The inquisitors carry salt-fire. It burns covenant-stone. We cannot hold the Pearl Hall and the Gate both."

"Then hold the Pearl Hall," Nerissa said. "Fall back. Seal the lower galleries."

"And after that, Regent?"

Nerissa did not answer. She looked at the war-table, at the map of her court, at the small carved figures representing everything she had governed in exile's shadow for a hundred years, and Jude watched the moment the arithmetic came out wrong. Watched her understand that there were no more terraces after the Pearl Hall. That the drowned court was a single room, in the end, and Voss was already at the door.

Then Nerissa turned, and found Tam across the chamber, and the look on her face was the most terrible thing Jude had seen since the waystation burned.

"Aunt," Tam said. Careful. Neutral. A word used as a shield.

"You knew this would happen." Nerissa's voice was low, meant for their corner of the hall, but the chamber had gone quiet enough that every ear caught it. "You and your smuggler and your heretic archivist. You came back from exile with a prophecy and a Saltbride, and now the usurper's armies stand in my Gate, and you tell me, Tam—*tell me*—what you thought would happen."

"I thought we'd reach the rite on our own terms," Tam said. "I thought we'd have until the tide-peak. I thought—"

"You thought like an exile. You thought you had time." Nerissa crossed to her, and Jude's hand went to the knife at her belt before she could stop it, and Tam's fingers brushed her wrist—*stand down*—without either of them looking. "The rite is in three days. Voss is here in three hours. There is one way this ends with my court alive, and it is the way the prophecy wrote, and you have spent every day since you returned teaching these people to hope for something else."

"Aunt—"

"*Complete the rite.*" It came out of Nerissa not as an order but as a plea, and that was what made Jude's blood run cold. The regent of the drowned court, the iron woman, took her niece by both shoulders in front of the whole assembled court and said, in a voice that broke on every syllable: "Bind the Saltbride at the next tide. Tonight. Now. The covenant will rise to defend its own—the old covenant, the true one—and Voss's fragment will be a trinket against it. I was wrong to wait for the tide-peak. I was wrong to give you time to *negotiate*. There is nothing to negotiate with. There is only what survives."

The hall was silent. The wardens looked at Tam. The courtiers looked at Tam. Jude looked at Tam, and saw her standing in the wreckage of her homecoming—crowned heir, prisoner, niece, traitor, last hope, all in one body—and felt the terror of the whole chapter of her life converge on this single marble floor.

Every road had led here. The waystation, the burning, the mountain roads, Quill's sacrifice, the Gate, the war. Every road, and all of them ended at the same altar, and the only question left in the world was whether the woman she loved would walk her to it.

"Regent." Tam's voice was very quiet. She reached up and took her aunt's hands from her shoulders and held them, the way you hold the hands of someone drowning. "I will not bind her."

"You will—"

"I will not bind her," Tam said again, louder, and the hall heard it, the whole court heard it, and Jude felt something in her chest crack open that was not fear. "Not by force, not by fear, not to save the walls. If the covenant can only be defended by a bride-price, then let it fall, and let us build something from the rubble that doesn't eat its daughters. That's what I came home to say. That's what I *should* have said from the throne instead of letting you crown me and hope I'd learned obedience in exile."

Nerissa stared at her. And Jude saw—the whole hall saw—the exact moment the regent understood that her last arithmetic had failed too. That the niece she had crowned as an instrument had become, in the crucible of the road, something the prophecy had no column for.

"Then we die," Nerissa said. Simply. Almost gently. "We die in the Pearl Hall, and Voss takes both worlds, and a thousand years of tide comes to nothing."

"No," Jude said.

Every face turned to her. The Saltbride. The smuggler's guide with the salt-sworn scars and the knife and the Codex in a waterproof satchel against her heart. She had not meant to speak. But the words were out, and they were true, and she was so tired of true words dying in her throat.

"We don't die," Jude said. "And she doesn't bind me. There's a third road. There's always been a third road—I've been walking it since the waystation, I just didn't know its name yet. The Codex rewrote itself around every choice I made. Every refusal. It's been trying to tell me something this whole time." She pulled the satchel around, her hands steady now, steadier than they had any right to be. "The final page. I haven't read it yet. I was afraid to. I'm not afraid anymore, because the alternative is standing here listening to the two of you do arithmetic with my heart, and I'm done with that."

She drew out the Century Codex.

The book had changed. Jude had known it, in the way you know a face across years, but seeing it fresh—the cover's drowned sigil now bright as a living eye, the pages within shifting like water when the lamplight touched them—was still a blow. Tam's hand found hers. Nerissa watched with the expression of a woman watching a fire start in a room full of oil.

Jude opened the Codex to the final page.

And read.

* * *

The words were not what she expected. They were worse, and better, and they were the truth, and the truth came off the page like cold water over a burn:

*The crown of the free cities was forged from the covenant's first sacrifice. The Silent Tide went silent not in wrath, but in grief—hiding from both realms the truth of what their crowns were made of, because the truth would unmake them.*

*The Saltbride is not the covenant's fulfillment. She is its correction.*

*The bride-price was never the Tide's demand. It was the crown's.*

Jude read it aloud. She made herself read it aloud, every word, into the silence of the Pearl Hall, while the iron horns sounded again from the Processional and the pearl-lamps guttered and Nerissa's face went through grief and rage and a terrible dawning understanding, and Tam's grip on her hand tightened until it hurt, and hurt was good, hurt was *real*, hurt was an anchor.

"The crown," Nerissa whispered, when Jude finished. "The usurper's whole claim—his mandate—he carries a fragment of it into my court, and it's—" She laughed, a short broken sound with no humor in it at all. "It's a piece of a corpse. A piece of the first sacrifice. He's marching an army behind a relic of the very crime the Tide went silent to bury."

"And if the crown is broken," Tam said slowly, and Jude felt the idea take shape in her as it took shape in the hall, in the court, in the turning tide itself, "the claim breaks with it. The old covenant breaks with it. Everything Voss is, everything the old bargain was—"

"Ends," Jude said. "At the altar. At the tide-peak. Where every road was always going." She closed the Codex. Her heart was slamming against her ribs, but her voice, when it came, did not shake. "So we don't run. We don't bind. We don't die. We go to the rite, all of us, and when Voss comes to take his covenant, I give him the only thing left to give a crown made of a stolen heart."

The horns sounded again, closer now. Somewhere below, salt-fire bloomed against the lower galleries, and the whole Pearl Hall shuddered like a struck bell.

Nerissa looked at her for a long moment. Then the regent of the drowned court did the last thing Jude ever expected: she knelt. Not to the prophecy. Not to the crown. To Jude, on the cracked marble, with her armies failing and her court burning, and said:

"Then the tide-peak is in six hours, Saltbride. And I will spend every warden I have left making sure you reach the altar alive."

Above them, through the high windows, the breached Gate glowed with the light of the fragment, and the first ranks of Voss's inquisitors began the final climb toward the Pearl Hall.

Jude gripped the Codex against her heart, took Tam's hand, and turned to face the doors.
