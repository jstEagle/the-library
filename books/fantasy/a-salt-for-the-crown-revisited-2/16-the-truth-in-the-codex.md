## 16. The Truth in the Codex

The tide-peak was coming, and the drowned court smelled of it.

Jude had felt weather like this before—on the Thorn passes, before a rockfall; in Brack's harbor, the night the sea wall cracked—but never underwater, if this could be called underwater. The Pearl Hall's vaulted galleries held a pressure that was not quite water and not quite air, something older that pressed against her eardrums and behind her eyes, and tonight it pulsed like a second heartbeat. Outside the high windows, past the pearl-grown coral, the deep dark of the open trench turned restlessly, as though the whole ocean were an animal turning over in its sleep.

Two days. Nerissa had moved the rite up once already. There would be no third delay.

Jude sat alone in the guest chamber they had given her—a cell with better furniture—and read the Century Codex by the light of a lamp that burned without flame, only a slow silver glow, like moonlight kept in glass.

She had not meant to read it again. She had told herself she was done with it, that the book was Voss's weapon now as much as anyone's, that every word in it was a hook dressed as a promise. But sleep would not come in a place where the ceiling drifted like the surface of a pond seen from below, and so she had opened the Codex to the Saltbride passage one more time, the way you press a bruise to learn its shape.

And the words were wrong.

Not corrupted. Not damaged. *Wrong*—which is to say, they were not what she remembered.

Jude knew the prophecy by heart. She had carried it through burning waystations and mountain roads and a gate that ate memories; she had heard Mother Quill recite it syllable by syllable while her blind fingers walked the margins like a woman reading braille carved in bone. *When the tide turns thrice without a moon, the Saltbride comes, born of salt-sworn silence, to wed the covenant and seal the realms beneath the crown.*

That last phrase. *Beneath the crown.* It had been there since Brack. Quill had underlined it three times, muttering about scribes who couldn't resist editorializing.

It wasn't there anymore.

Jude sat very still, listening to the lamp hum. Then she read the line again, tracing each glyph with a fingertip, half expecting the ink to shift away from her touch like fish. It didn't. It simply said what it now said: *to wed the covenant and seal the realms.* No crown. No mention of any crown at all.

"Quill," she whispered, to no one. "What did you see that I'm only seeing now?"

Because that was the thing about the Codex—the thing Tam had tried to explain on the road under the mountain, when the book first began rewriting itself around their choices. It was not a record of the future. It was an argument with the future. The prophecy had been written by someone who wanted a particular ending, and every refusal of Jude's scraped another layer off the page, the way tide scrapes paint off a hull. What lay underneath had been there all along.

She turned the pages faster now, hunting. The Saltbride rite. The bride-price. The heart that becomes the seal. All still there, but thinner somehow, the letters grayer, like text viewed through fogged glass. And beneath them, between them, in the spaces where the old ink was failing—

New writing was surfacing.

It came up slowly, the way drowned things come up: reluctantly, and changed by the depth they'd risen from. The glyphs were older than anything else in the book, angular where the prophecy's script was flowing, cut rather than written, as if the original author had chiseled these lines into vellum with a stylus of bone. Jude did not know how a book could contain two hands' work in the same ink at the same age. She suspected the answer was that the Codex was not entirely a book.

The new lines began mid-sentence, as though she had opened the page in the middle of a confession:

*—and so the covenant was made, and it was good, and the gods spoke plainly in those days. The Tide spoke loudest of all. I set this down so that none who come after will believe the lie we are about to tell.*

Jude's mouth went dry. The lamp's silver light seemed to dim, or perhaps that was just her vision narrowing, the room contracting to the size of the page.

She turned the page.

*The first sacrifice was not given freely, whatever the priests will say afterward. Her name was Ysolde, and she was a warden of the Tide, and she loved a king of the shore cities, and when the realms went to war over the tolls of the Gate, the two of them went to the altar together and offered themselves as the price of peace. One heart for the land. One breath for the sea. The covenant took them both, and it held, and the war ended.*

*But a covenant needs a token, something both courts can swear upon, and there was nothing left of Ysolde and her king but what the altar returned: a single circlet of fused bone and pearl and gold, quenched in the blood of the offering. We called it a miracle. We called it the Crown of Accord. We set it upon the head of the first High King of the free cities and told ourselves the sacrifice had been sanctified.*

*We lied. Or rather—we chose not to look too closely at what we carried out of that altar-house. The crown is not a symbol of the covenant. The crown IS the covenant. Every oath sworn beneath it has been sworn on Ysolde's bones. Every king who wears it renews her death without knowing it. And the Silent Tide, who loved her warden best of all its children, saw what we had done and could not bear to speak of it.*

*So the Tide went silent. Not in anger. In grief. In shame. It sealed the truth behind nine years of silence, then ninety, then a hundred, because to speak it would be to tell every soul in the free cities that the crown they kneel to is a grave-stone, and to tell the drowned court that their beloved covenant was bought with a murder dressed as a marriage.*

*If you are reading this, then the silence has done its work too well. The realms have forgotten each other. The usurper wears the grave-stone and calls it a birthright. And some poor girl is being told she must die to patch what was never broken—only buried.*

Jude stopped reading.

Her hands were shaking. She set the Codex down on the pearl-inlaid table, carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and stood, and walked to the window, and looked out at the restless dark, and found that none of it helped.

*The crown is the covenant.*

She thought of Kaelen Voss's army crossing the Thorn passes under banners stitched with the crown sigil. She thought of the inquisitors burning a smuggler's waystation to ash over a book. She thought of Ossa, weighing a friend's life against a fragment of vellum, choosing survival, as sergeants do. Everyone in this war—every soldier, every priest, every courtier in Voss's camp and every warden in Nerissa's hall—was fighting over a piece of jewelry. Fighting and dying for it. Burning cities for it.

Except it wasn't a piece of jewelry. That was the vertigo of it, the thing that made the floor feel less solid than the drifting water beyond the glass. It was a person. It had always been a person. The free cities hadn't been ruled by kings for a thousand years; they'd been ruled by a dead woman's remains, polished and gilded and passed hand to hand, and nobody had known—not the kings, not the priests, not the Saltcloaks marching under it, not even the god whose warden she had been.

Nobody except the Silent Tide.

Nine years. Nine years of prayers thrown into silence. Nine years of Jude kneeling on wet stone at dawn, whispering her salt-oaths into nothing, wondering what she had done wrong, whether faith was a muscle she'd let atrophy, whether the god had simply looked at her and found her wanting. She had built her whole understanding of herself around that silence. A mercenary's god was a distant god; fine. A silent god tested you; fine. She had made the silence mean something, the way soldiers make everything mean something, because the alternative was admitting it meant nothing.

And the truth was worse than nothing. The Tide hadn't been testing her. It had been drowning. A god, gagging on a secret too heavy to speak, while a child of its own faith prayed into the void and got nothing back and blamed herself for it.

"You should have told someone," Jude said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the pressure-light air, flattened, close. "Anyone. You could have ended this a hundred years ago."

The lamp flickered. The water beyond the window pressed closer, or seemed to.

And that was when the second horror arrived, walking quietly behind the first, the way grief follows shock down a corridor.

If the crown was the covenant—if the covenant lived in the metal and bone of that circlet—then what happened when the Saltbride's heart became the seal? What was Nerissa actually asking for, at the next tide? Jude had assumed the rite would renew the old bargain, stitch the realms back together along the old seams. But the old bargain was anchored to the crown. To Voss's crown. If Jude gave her heart to the covenant as it stood, she wouldn't be sealing a peace between equals.

She'd be pouring fresh mortar into Voss's foundation. Legitimizing the grave-stone. Handing the usurper a mandate written in her own blood: *the covenant recognizes the crown, therefore the crown's wearer is the covenant's keeper, therefore Kaelen Voss speaks for both worlds.* The prophecy had never been a warning about what would happen if she failed.

It had been the mechanism of his victory all along.

"Oh," Jude said softly. "Oh, you absolute bastards."

Both of them. Both courts. Nerissa, who thought she was saving her realm. The Codex's original author, whoever he or she had been, who had hidden this confession inside a prophecy designed to produce a bride. Even the Silent Tide—she caught herself, gentled the thought. The Tide hadn't designed anything. The Tide had simply gone quiet and let the machinery run, and a hundred years later the machinery was grinding toward the worst possible outcome, and the god was still too ashamed to say stop.

Someone had to say it for them.

Jude picked up the Codex again. Her hands had steadied, mostly. She read the final surfaced lines, the ones rising slowest of all, barely legible, like words seen through fathom-deep water:

*There is one road out of this, and it is narrow, and it will cost everyone who walks it something they love. Do not reforge what was forged before. Break it. Break the crown, break the bargain, break the silence—all three together, for they are one thing wearing three shapes—and then, with the pieces, build something no god or king has ever dared to build: a covenant that asks instead of takes. It cannot be prophesied. It can only be chosen.*

*Choose.*

The last glyph was different from all the others. Jude leaned close, and the lamp-light caught it, and she understood why it looked familiar: it was the mark stamped on the cover of the book, the sigil Tam had recognized in the smoke of the burning waystation—the drowned-court heir's seal.

Ysolde's seal.

The first sacrifice had written the Codex. Or part of it had. Or her voice had survived in it the way a bell keeps the shape of the note long after it stops ringing. Jude ran her thumb over the mark and felt—she would swear this later, and Tam would believe her, because Tam had felt it too—a pulse. Faint. Patient. The pulse of someone waiting a very long time at a door for someone on the other side to open it.

Footsteps in the corridor. The particular cadence of a warden's patrol, trident-butts clicking against coral tile. Jude closed the book and slid it beneath the mattress of woven kelp, heart hammering, feeling suddenly like the smuggler she'd been three months and one lifetime ago, hiding contraband from a customs man.

The footsteps passed. The pressure in the halls pulsed again, stronger. Two days. Maybe less.

She needed Tam. She needed Tam now, tonight, needed to put this book in her hands and watch her face as she read it—because Tam was the heir, Tam's aunt was sharpening the altar-knives, and Tam had grown up on drowned-court histories that surely, *surely*, mentioned Ysolde somewhere in a footnote, a saint's-day, a name worn smooth by repetition until no one asked who she'd been. Tam needed to know that her homeland's founding peace was a crime scene. Tam needed to know before Nerissa put her in irons again, before the wardens who loved her were scattered, before the tide turned thrice without a moon.

And there was something else. Something smaller and larger.

The Codex said the Silent Tide's silence was grief. Quill had read the drowned word Jude received at the salt-prayer as a warning, and she'd been right—but incomplete. *Ysolde.* The word the god had finally spoken after nine years hadn't been a threat or a blessing. It had been a name. The god hadn't been answering Jude's prayer at all. It had been talking in its sleep, saying the name of its dead, and Jude had happened to be standing in the dream.

Unless it hadn't been an accident. Unless the Tide had spent a century looking for exactly one kind of listener—a salt-sworn mercenary with no court to please and no doctrine to protect, someone who might hear a dead woman's name and ask the dangerous question instead of kneeling harder.

The lamp glowed. The trench-dark breathed against the window. Somewhere far above, past a hundred fathoms and a Drowned Gate and a memory-toll that had eaten her archivist mother whole, the free cities slept under a grave-stone and called it a crown.

Jude pulled the Codex out from under the mattress. She wrapped it in the grey salt-cloak she hadn't been able to make herself throw away, tied the bundle with cord, and slung it across her back where a mercenary carries the things she cannot afford to lose.

Then she opened her door and went to find the heir of the drowned court, walking fast through the pulsing pearl-lit corridors, past wardens who saluted her without knowing why, carrying a dead woman's testimony toward whatever came next—

—and did not see, folded small and slipped beneath her door while she read, the black-edged summons bearing Nerissa's regent-seal, commanding the presence of the Saltbride at dawn.

Dawn. Not two days.

One.
