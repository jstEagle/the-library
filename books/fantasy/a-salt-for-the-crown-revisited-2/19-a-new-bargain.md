## 19. A New Bargain

The dark fragment woke the way drowning men wake — all at once, and angry about it.

Jude felt it before she saw it move: a pressure in her back teeth, a taste like a rusted coin sliding down her throat. The shard of the old crown lay among the rubble of the eastern arch where Tam's wardens had dragged Voss's broken siege-engine, and it was drinking. She could see it now that she knew what to look for — the way it pulled at the light, at the salt spray, at something finer and more terrible than either. It was pulling at the grief of everyone in the drowned hall. Grief, she understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water, was only another kind of salt.

"Jude." Tam's hand found hers. Her knuckles were split from the fight, her pearl-and-iron crown of heirship knocked askew, and there was blood on her jaw that might have been her own or anyone's. "Tell me you feel that too."

"I feel it." Jude did not let go of her hand. Around them the drowned court was settling into the stunned quiet that follows a battle — wardens dragging their wounded toward the pearl hall, water running in thin silver sheets down the great stair, and everywhere the sound of the sea itself, which had stopped its long groaning the moment the crown shattered and now made a noise Jude had never heard it make before. It sounded almost like breathing.

Behind them, across the flooded causeway where Voss's armies had breached the Drowned Gate, the usurper's host stood in ranks ten thousand strong, torches guttering in the sea-wind, waiting. They had come to seize a covenant. The thing they would have seized lay in pieces at Jude's feet: the crown of the free cities, forged — as the Codex's last page had confessed — from the covenant's first sacrifice, from the heart of a person the gods had loved enough to take and too much to speak of afterward. For nine years the Silent Tide had kept that silence inside Jude's own prayers like a swallowed stone. Now the stone was out. The god was speaking. And the last piece of the old bargain was still hungry.

"It will not stay dormant," said Nerissa.

The regent came limping through the wreckage, her regalia torn, her face gray beneath the kohl of office. An hour ago she had begged Tam to complete the rite; an hour ago she had been prepared to bind a living woman's heart into a seal and call it duty. Now she looked smaller than her title, and older than the tide-line, and she stopped three paces away because no one invited her closer.

"The fragments are not dead," Nerissa went on. "They were never dead. A sacrifice does not end when the knife is cleaned. It endures. That is what makes it powerful, and that is why your grandmother's grandmothers chose it, and why every realm since has chosen it, and why—" her voice caught, and she mastered it with visible effort, "—and why I was willing to do it again. Because the fragment is waking, Saltbride, and if it wakes fully it will make its own covenant. Not with us. With whatever will feed it."

Tam stepped forward, putting herself between the regent and Jude, which Jude noted — even now, even exhausted to the marrow — was a thing she would remember until she died.

"And what feeds it?" Tam asked.

"Grief," said a voice from the stair.

Serah Dane descended through the spray, her inquisitor's white coat scorched black along one side, the stolen Codex fragment cradled against her chest like an infant. Behind her came what remained of Quill — not Quill herself, who had given her memories to the Gate and her life to the escape from the pearl hall, but the blank book she had carried, its pages now filling with writing no one had set there. Serah laid it open on a slab of fallen masonry, steadying it against the wind.

"It's writing," she said. "It started when the crown broke. I thought you should—"

Jude leaned over the pages. The script was Quill's. Of course it was Quill's; the archivist had poured everything she was into the Gate's toll, and the Codex had caught her the way a river catches rain. The words came in her careful blind-woman's hand, letters formed by touch alone:

*the fragment remembers being whole. the fragment remembers being fed. it does not know it was ever anything but hunger.*

Below that, fresher, the ink still gleaming:

*ask her what she offers instead.*

Jude read it twice. Then she straightened, and looked at the fragment in the rubble, and looked past it — up, through forty fathoms of darkening water, through the Drowned Gate and the drowned courts and the long cold shelves of the deep, to the place where a god had gone silent for nine years out of grief for a murder committed in the name of peace.

She was so tired. She had been tired since the waystation, since the date in the margins, since the first inquisitor's torch. Every road of the last year had converged here, on this slab of drowned stone, and every version of the prophecy had asked her for the same payment dressed in different clothes: her heart, her freedom, her love, her self. The Codex had rewritten itself around each refusal and shown her, page by page, that destiny's fine print always read the same.

But the Codex had also taught her how bargains were written. And she had spent her whole life among smugglers, who knew better than priests that any contract, however ancient, however sacred, however soaked in blood, could be renegotiated — if both parties were still alive to sign.

"Everyone," Jude said, and her voice cracked on the first word, so she took a breath and tried again. "Everyone stop fighting. Everyone listen."

* * *

They gathered in what had been the covenant-hall, before the battle broke its dome and let the sky in. Wardens of the drowned court stood shoulder to shoulder with Saltcloaks who had followed Serah Dane down from the surface; inquisitors who had knelt to Voss stood beside smugglers who had never knelt to anyone. Tam took her place at the head of the court, crowned and bleeding. Nerissa stood apart, hands folded, watching her niece the way a woman watches weather she can no longer argue with. And Jude walked to the center of the hall, to the place where the altar had been, and stood on the stone where her heart was meant to have been cut out, and spoke.

"You've all heard the prophecy," she said. "Some of you were raised on it. Some of you were sent to kill for it. A Saltbride reforges the covenant between the realms, or Kaelen Voss takes both worlds. That was the deal. That was always the deal." She turned slowly, letting them see her face — the salt-scars, the mercenary's flat eyes, the exhaustion she wasn't bothering to hide anymore. "Here's what none of you were told, because a god went silent to keep it hidden, and a crown was forged to make the silence look like wisdom. The covenant was sealed with a murder. A person was taken — freely loved, freely grieving, and taken anyway — and their heart became the seal, and their death became the crown, and every realm above and below has been standing on that grave for a hundred years and calling it law."

Silence. Even the sea, coming down through the broken dome, seemed to fall quiet to listen.

"I was supposed to be next," Jude said. "That's what bride-price means. That's what the fine print said, and Mother Quill read it to me, and I refused, and the prophecy kept trying to collect anyway. Well." She held up her hands, empty. "I'm done paying into a contract written in someone else's blood. But I'm not done with the covenant. Hear me. I am *not* done with the covenant."

She turned to face the deep water beyond the hall's far wall, where the dark pressed in like a congregation.

"Tide," she said. "You silent, grieving, stubborn old god. You hid the truth because you couldn't bear to say it. You let a hundred years rot rather than confess what the crown was made of. I understand that. I've gone silent myself, over things I couldn't say." Her voice shook, and she let it. "But silence isn't protection. Silence is just the debt getting bigger. So I'm going to speak for both of us, and you can correct me if I'm wrong — and Tide, please, *please* correct me if I'm wrong, because I have missed your voice for nine years like a wound that won't close."

She knelt on the altar-stone. Not as a bride. As a negotiator kneeling to show she came unarmed.

"Here is my offer," Jude said. "No more sacrifice. No hearts, no brides, no chosen ones fed to the deep so the tides behave. Instead: exchange. Memory for memory. Salt for salt. Freely given, freely returned, and *revocable* — do you hear me, all of you — revocable, by either party, because a bargain you can't walk away from isn't a bargain, it's a cage. The realms give the sea their dead's names, their stories, their grief — freely offered, not harvested — and the sea gives back its calm, its fish, its mercy in the storm. When either side fails, the other may withdraw. No seal made of anyone's heart. No crown made of anyone's death." She paused. "And the old crown stays broken. All of it. Every fragment. Including yours," she added, to the dark fragment sleeping in the rubble, "especially yours, and if that offends you, you can take it up with me directly."

Tam made a small sound beside her — half laugh, half sob.

"You'd write a treaty with a god," Nerissa said, "on terms a smuggler wouldn't offer a rival crew."

"A smuggler would offer exactly these terms," Jude said, without turning around, "because a smuggler knows that the contracts that last are the ones nobody's forced to keep. Force breaks, Regent. Every time. You watched it break tonight." She spread her empty hands wider. "So. Tide. Last chance to tell me I'm a fool. Say something."

For a long moment there was nothing. The hall held its breath. Somewhere in the ranks of Voss's army beyond the gate, ten thousand soldiers waited for a sign of which world they would be marching home to.

Then the water moved.

Not a wave. Not a surge. The whole sea, from the broken dome to the drowned horizon, exhaled — and in the exhale was a voice. It did not come through the ears. It came up through the stone, through the salt in the blood, through nine years of unanswered prayers suddenly answered all at once, and it was vast and tired and so full of grief that half the hall wept without knowing why, and it was also — unmistakably, impossibly — *relieved.*

**FREELY,** said the Silent Tide, and the word rolled through them like the groundswell under a storm. **GIVEN. FREELY TAKEN. FREELY RETURNED. WRITTEN AND WITNESSED.**

Then, softer, in a register only Jude seemed to hear, a voice like her mother's, like the sea at night, like every prayer she had whispered into empty water finally finding its shore:

*Daughter of salt. You are no one's bride. You are my hand extended across the water. Rise.*

Jude rose.

* * *

What happened next happened quickly, the way avalanches happen quickly after a whole winter of hanging on.

The new covenant needed signing, and the Codex — Quill's Codex, the Codex that had rewritten itself around every choice Jude made — provided the page. It appeared between one wave of the book and the next, in ink that gleamed like wet pearl, and Tam read it aloud in the formal cadences of the drowned court while Jude signed her name with a stub of salt-pencil and the Silent Tide countersigned in a script of drifting foam that hovered on the paper and refused, permanently, to dry.

The moment the last line settled, the seas calmed.

There was no other word for it. The long fury that had ridden the ocean since the covenant broke — the storms, the drowned roads opening and closing without warning, the tides gone mad along every coast of the free cities — simply ended. Through the broken dome they could see it happening: the water smoothing like a sheet drawn tight, the distant thunder of the outer swells fading to a murmur, moonlight lying down flat and easy on a sea that had forgotten, for a century, how to rest. In the drowned court itself the pressure eased. Wardens straightened as though weights had lifted from their shoulders. Old wounds of the water — the slow poison that had been seeping into the deep places for a hundred years — began, visibly, to recede.

And Voss's army broke.

It happened without a single sword drawn. The mandate that had driven them — the prophecy, the claim, the promise that the usurper would inherit both worlds through a bride's spilled blood — dissolved the instant the crown's last legitimacy died with the covenant it was forged from. Soldiers felt it leave them. Later, survivors would struggle to describe it: a certainty lifting, a fever breaking, the sudden embarrassed awareness of how far from home they were and why. Whole companies lowered their banners. Others simply turned around and started walking, at first in ones and twos, then in hundreds, then in a rout that flowed back up the causeway and through the Drowned Gate and away into the passes like water finding its level.

Kaelen Voss himself did not run. From the high causeway his banner could still be seen, and his personal guard around it, a hard black knot of maybe two hundred who had given him more than orders. But even from this distance Jude could read the shape of him — the man who had built a war on the promise of a crown that no longer existed anywhere in the world except in four shards, three of them dark, one of them dust on a drowned floor.

"He'll rally them somewhere," Serah Dane said quietly, beside her. "He'll tell them it isn't over. Men like him always find a way to keep the war warm."

"Let him," Jude said. She was watching the sea. "Wars need fuel. He just lost his."

Tam came to stand at her other side, close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while none of the three of them said anything at all. Below them the drowned court was beginning — hesitantly, disbelievingly — to celebrate. Someone had found drums. The wardens' low singing rose through the broken dome, harmonizing with the Saltcloaks' rougher voices, and Nerissa stood apart from all of it with her hands still folded, looking at her niece's crown with an expression that was not yet surrender but was, at least, listening.

"You did it," Tam said at last. Her voice was rough. "Nine years of silence and a hundred years of rot, and you talked a god into a fair contract in the space of a prayer."

"We talked it into it together." Jude turned to her. Up close, in the moonlight coming down through the ruined dome, Tam looked young and ancient at once, crowned and unarmored, her exile revoked, her homeland hers, her future suddenly a blank page instead of a prophecy. "Don't you dare give me all of it. I saw you fight for this court tonight when it hadn't earned you. I saw you refuse the rite when refusing cost you everything you'd just gotten back." She reached up and straightened the crooked crown of heirship, an intimacy that made several nearby wardens look away with enormous diplomatic care. "Besides. There's still work. The fragments. Three left, and one of them was *waking*, and gods know where Voss's piece went when his army scattered."

"One problem at a time," Tam murmured. "Right now there's singing. And my aunt is watching us like she's composing a eulogy. And you haven't slept in two days, and neither have I, and the tide—" she glanced out at the smooth, resting sea, and something in her face went soft and wondering, "—the tide is calm, Jude. Do you understand? It hasn't been calm in a hundred years. My people can go home to the shallows again. The fishermen of Brack won't drown this winter. You did that with an empty-handed bargain and a smuggler's nerve."

"*We* did it," Jude said again, and this time Tam didn't argue.

They stood together while the singing swelled, two women on the edge of a remade world, and if the exhausted peace in Jude's chest felt fragile as sea-glass, it also felt — for the first time since a date in a margin had turned her life inside out — *hers.* Not the prophecy's. Not the god's. Not the crown's. Hers, and Tam's, and theirs together, freely given, freely returned, revocable by either party.

A perfect bargain, in other words, except for one thing.

Because when the celebration finally crested and the wardens brought food and the Saltcloaks started a song about Ossa Vane that was either a ballad or a threat depending on the verse, Serah Dane came pushing through the crowd with the Codex in her arms and an expression that killed the music wherever she passed.

"Jude." She opened the book with hands that weren't quite steady. "The Codex is writing again. It's been writing since the tide accepted. I thought — I thought it would be finished now, I thought the prophecy was done with us, but—"

She turned the book around.

On the fresh page, in ink that gleamed like wet pearl, in Quill's patient blind-woman's hand, new lines were still appearing, letter by letter, as they watched:

*three fragments remain. the first sleeps in the wreck of the first ship, where the first sacrifice was made. it dreams of being whole. and it is not the only one dreaming — for far to the north, in a war camp that should be empty, a man who lost his mandate has found the second fragment in the ashes of his banners, and the fragment has told him a secret:*

*a crown need not be given. a crown can be taken. and there is a third fragment still, and it lies closer than anyone thinks — carried all this way in a book, in a coat, against the heart of a true believer who is starting to wonder what it weighs.*

Jude looked at Serah Dane.

Serah Dane looked down at the scorched white coat, at the shape pressing against her chest that she had carried out of Brack and across the passes and through the breach of the Drowned Gate because it had seemed important, because it had seemed *safe* with her, because she had never once thought to ask—

"What have I been carrying?" she whispered.

Far to the north, in the ruins of a war camp, Kaelen Voss lifted a fragment of the old crown above his head, and the sea — the calm, the newly peaceful, the freely-bargaining sea — shivered from horizon to horizon.
