## 21. Two Courts, One Tide

The Drowned Gate had never looked like this.

Tam stood on the threshold stone where her ancestors had demanded blood and memory, and watched the tide come in gentle as a hand offered palm-up. A year of work lived in that gentleness. A year of wardens unlearning kneel-and-fear, of pearl halls thrown open to air and lamplight, of courts that had spent a century drowning in silence learning, awkwardly, to speak.

Below the Gate, the sea had been carved into stairs. Not drowned ones — *built* ones, wide and shallow, descending into the green water in long switchbacks so that a person could walk from the free cities' shore into the drowned court without paying a toll in anything but patience. Fishers from Brack came down them now with baskets of bread and iron nails. Drowned-court children — pale, dark-eyed, gill-slit at the throat like their queen — climbed up to trade pearls for candied ginger and lie outrageously about the size of the leviathans they'd seen.

Her realm. Open. Breathing.

*Inheritance rewritten,* she thought, and the old grief stirred, the grief for the girl banished into exile a century after her own birth, and then settled, the way sediment settles in still water.

"You're standing on the ceremony stone," said a voice behind her, dry as old paper. "The archivists will have your gills for that."

Tam turned. Serah Dane climbed the last of the shore stairs with a courier's satchel across her chest and the sea-wind pulling at her inquisitor's grey coat — no longer grey, actually. She'd had it re-dyed a deep tide-green, over Tam's objections that green was *their* color, and Serah had said, with the first smile Tam had ever seen on her, "Exactly. I'm declaring allegiance with my laundry. It's the most honest thing an envoy can do."

"You're early," Tam said.

"Envoys are always early. It's how you know they're not assassins anymore." Serah reached the stone and stood beside her, looking down at the stairs, at the fishers, at the small drowned girl who was currently convincing two Brackish boys that she had personally wrestled a kraken and only let it go because it cried. "Voss's remnant sent another letter. Third one."

"Let me guess. He demands recognition."

"He demands recognition, restitution, and the return of his 'rightful crown,' which I believe is currently in approximately four hundred pieces at the bottom of the tide-peak pool." Serah's mouth twisted. "His inquisitors are down to eleven. Two of them defected to us last month. One tried to assassinate me and then apologized, which I found more disturbing than the attempt."

"What did you do with him?"

"Sent him to Mother Quill's archive to alphabetize. He wept for three days. It was very educational." Serah's voice softened, just slightly, on the archive's name. It did that, sometimes, for both of them. The blind archivist who had traded her life's memory to open the Gate, who had died in the Pearl Hall naming strangers her family — her archive still stood in Brack, tended by the Saltcloaks, and every envoy who passed through the Drowned Gate now carried a copy of her cipher-key the way pilgrims once carried saints' bones.

Tam looked at the letter in Serah's satchel and did not ask to read it. She knew what it said. She had known since the day the crown shattered and Voss's mandate dissolved like salt in rain, and he had stood on the drowned stairs screaming that the covenant was *his*, that the prophecy had promised it to *him*, while his armies walked backward out of the sea with their banners dragging.

Prophecies, Tam had learned, were like inheritance. Everyone assumed they were owed one. Almost no one read the fine print.

* * *

Jude found her at midday, down on the third landing of the stairs, where the water came to the waist at high tide and the drowned court's lantern-light bled up through the green like a second sky.

Jude Dunmore swam the way she did everything now — like a woman who had stopped apologizing for taking up space. Her salt-oath scars had faded to pale lines on her forearms. She surfaced beside Tam, pushed wet hair from her face, and said, "The Brackish delegation is asking whether the treaty includes fishing rights north of the Teeth."

"Does it?"

"I don't know. Does it? You're the queen."

"You're the covenant-keeper. It's your treaty."

"Then it includes fishing rights north of the Teeth," Jude said, "because I've met the fishers of Brack, and a treaty that doesn't include their fish won't survive the first winter." She grinned, and Tam felt the familiar treacherous warmth of it, the warmth that had started in a smuggler's cache with a dead man's book and a date written in the margins, and had survived inquisitors and regents and gods and the end of the world as both their courts had known it.

"You're enjoying this," Tam said.

"I'm enjoying *you*. The treaty is a side effect." Jude treaded water, considering her. "Nerissa sent a gift, by the way. For the signing."

Tam went still. Her aunt — the regent who had moved the rite up, who had ordered Jude bound to the altar at the next tide, who had knelt in the flood-waters when the Silent Tide spoke and the old covenant broke like a bone — had been quiet since. Quiet and far away, in the deep courts, doing whatever penance regents did.

"A gift," Tam repeated.

"A pearl the size of your fist. And a note." Jude reached into the oilskin pouch she wore at her neck — Tam's old exile-token, repurposed, because Jude had a habit of keeping things that should have been thrown into the sea — and handed over a scrap of deep-paper, water-proof, written in the old court hand.

Tam read it twice.

*To my queen. I was wrong about what we were owed. I am told the new bargain requires gifts freely given. Consider this the first from a debtor who intends to keep paying. — N.*

"She's learning," Tam said at last.

"She's *trying*. It's different, and it's harder, and it counts for more." Jude took the note back, folded it with the care of a woman who had once kept a prophecy-book dry through a burning waystation, and tucked it away. "Come on. The tide turns at dusk. Everyone's waiting."

* * *

The signing was held at the Gate itself, at slack tide, because Jude had insisted — "If we're making a bargain of equals, we make it where neither realm is above the other. The threshold. The in-between." The archivists had written it down as *the Doctrine of the Threshold* and Tam privately suspected Jude had just liked the way it sounded, but it had become law, so.

They gathered on the wide stone: Tam in the regalia she had redesigned herself — the old court's pearl-and-mourning replaced with pearl and *salt-white*, because mourning was for things that stayed dead, and her court was not staying dead. Jude in Saltcloak grey with the covenant-sigil stitched over her heart, which the Silent Tide had marked there itself, a year ago, in a moment neither of them spoke of casually because it still made them both go quiet.

Serah Dane stood at the center with the treaty scroll, first envoy of the free cities to the drowned court, her tide-green coat bright against the stone. The Brackish delegation stood to the west — Ossa Vane among them, at the edge, where exiles stood, her face carved from the same stone it had been carved from the day Jude had said *exile, not execution* and the company had re-founded itself on salt-oaths that named their failures out loud. Ossa did not speak. She had asked, through Serah, only to be allowed to witness. Tam had said yes. Forgiveness that did not forget could still make room for a witness.

And below, in the water, the drowned court had come up to see. Hundreds of them, heads above the surface for the first time in a century without wardens forcing them back down — old courtiers and young wardens, the pearl-hall children, Tam's aunt's own household, all of them treading the green water and looking up at the Gate where their queen stood with a salt-sworn mercenary and a scroll.

Serah unrolled the treaty and read it aloud, in both tongues, her inquisitor's voice trained for verdicts repurposed for promises:

"*Let there be no bride-price. Let there be no sacrifice. Let the covenant between the free cities and the drowned court be this: memory for memory, salt for salt, freely given and freely returned. What one realm takes, it pays for. What one realm gives, it is credited. No crown shall be forged from any person. No god shall be asked to hide a truth in silence. And when the tide turns against either realm, the other shall turn with it.*"

She looked up. "The drowned court speaks first. Queen Tam, do you seal it?"

Tam stepped forward. The old rite would have had her kneel. She stood.

"I seal it," she said, and her voice carried down the water and up the shore stairs both. "Not as a debt paid, not as an exile forgiven, but as a queen who inherits a court that was taught to drown quietly — and who refuses to teach it that lesson one day longer. This is my realm's gift: the Gate stays open. The stairs stay lit. And any soul of the free cities who is lost at sea shall be brought home, not kept."

The water stirred. The drowned court made a sound Tam had never heard it make in her whole life — a sound like a hundred voices exhaling at once, relief given shape.

Serah turned. "The free cities speak. Jude Dunmore, covenant-keeper, do you seal it?"

Jude stepped up beside Tam, close enough that their shoulders touched, and Tam thought: *a year ago she stood on an altar to be a seal. Now she stands here to make one.* Jude looked out at the fishers, at the Saltcloaks, at Ossa Vane at the edge of things, and then at the water full of her once-enemies, and Tam watched her do the thing Jude did — take all of it in, weigh all of it, refuse all of it that deserved refusing.

"I seal it," Jude said. "And I'll add one line, because I'm told covenants are living documents now." She raised her voice. "The Silent Tide is no longer silent. It answers prayers. So let this be the last covenant either of our realms ever makes in the dark — and if any crown, any court, any god asks one of you to be a price, you come to the Gate, and you tell the tide. It's listening. *We're* listening. That's the whole bargain."

She pressed her scarred palm to the stone. Tam pressed hers beside it, and where the two prints met, the tide rose — gently, deliberately, the way it had learned to — and washed over the scroll and the stone and their joined hands, and the salt dried into the granite in a thin bright line that the archivists would later measure and find ran exactly the width of both realms' borders, as if the sea itself had countersigned.

The drowned court roared. The fishers on the stairs threw their caps in the air. Somewhere in the Brackish delegation, someone began to sing an old salt-working song, and half the drowned court, who had spent a century forbidden surface songs, picked it up in two-part harmony that shouldn't have worked and did.

Serah Dane rolled up the scroll with hands that were not entirely steady, and Tam caught her eye, and the former inquisitor said, "I have served three crowns and two gods, and this is the first document I've ever sealed that I'd defend with my life rather than my career," and then she went to be sick behind a rock, because true belief, Tam was learning, always cost something when it finally changed its object.

* * *

Dusk. The crowd had thinned to stragglers and singers. Tam and Jude sat on the threshold stone with their feet in the water, passing a flask of Brackish rum between them, watching the lantern-light of the drowned court rise through the sea like a city of stars that had decided, after a hundred years, to come home.

"Serah's taking the treaty to the other free cities next month," Jude said. "Saltmere first. Then the Iron Coast. Voss's remnant will hate every mile of it."

"Voss's remnant is a letter and eleven inquisitors."

"Eleven inquisitors and a grievance," Jude said. "Grievances are the most renewable resource in the world. I've met smugglers who built whole careers on less." She took a drink, handed the flask over. "You did well today. Your court — they sang, Tam. A year ago they didn't sing."

Tam leaned her head on Jude's shoulder. "I keep waiting to feel like I've finished something. Like the exile's finally over. It doesn't come."

"That's because it's not a thing you finish. It's a thing you keep." Jude's voice was quiet, sure, the voice of a woman who had refused a god's script and been confirmed by the god anyway. "Covenants, realms, us. You don't arrive. You keep."

Out in the water, a drowned-court warden surfaced near the stairs and raised a hand — not a salute, exactly, something newer, something they'd invented this year: palm out, fingers spread, the sign that meant *open*. Tam returned it.

Then the warden's other hand came up, and Tam went cold, because the sign the warden was making was the old one, the emergency one, the one that meant *stranger at the deep gate*.

"Jude."

"I see it."

The warden swam closer, and Tam could see now that she was one of Nerissa's household, and that she was not alone — behind her, at the edge of the lantern-light, the water was dark in a way that had nothing to do with dusk.

"Queen," the warden said, breathless. "A boat came down the deep current from the north. No banner. One passenger. He asked for the Gate by its *old* name — the name from before the covenant, the name only the first court knew." She swallowed. "He's waiting at the deep gate. He says his name is Voss. And he says—" her voice faltered, "—he says he's not here to take anything. He says he's here to *give something back*. And the tide — Queen, the tide went flat when he said it. Flat as glass, all the way to the deep gate. Like it's holding its breath."

Jude stood, and Tam stood with her, and below their feet the sea lay still as a held secret.

"He gave something up," Jude said slowly. "A year of letters and eleven inquisitors, and now he gives something up. What does a man like that have left to return?"

Tam looked north, where the water lay glass-flat and waiting, and felt the old vertigo of prophecy stir — the sense that no bargain, however freely given, ever quite closed the book.

"Only one way to find out," she said. "Get Serah. We're going to the deep gate."
