## 1. The Weight of Uncounted Things

The ledgers of the Gildhall of Impossible Things did not balance themselves, though there were nights Zephyrine Yarrow wished they would.

She sat in the counting room beneath three lamps turned low, for ink dried faster under a hot flame and the Gildhall's ink was precious—ground from slate and grave-moss and one other thing Corvane refused to name even to his own clerks. Around her rose the shelves, floor to vaulted ceiling, row upon row of them, each holding volumes bound in leather that had never been cowhide and clasped in metals that had never been mined. The air smelled of tallow, dust, and old bargains. It was a smell she had stopped noticing years ago, the way a fishwife stops smelling the harbor.

Zephyrine dipped her pen and wrote: *One grief, bottled, amber glass, stoppered in wax. Seller: the Widow Anselm of Harrow's Bend. Buyer: unnamed, payment rendered in forty years of unremembered Sundays.*

Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady; that was the first thing they taught you at the Gildhall, before they taught you anything else—that a clerk whose hand shook was a clerk who had begun to feel, and feeling, in a place like this, was how ledgers caught fire.

*Condition on receipt: full. Grief verified as authentic by two assessors. Weight: heavier than its vessel, as is customary.*

She blotted the line and sat back, and the silence of the Gildhall settled over her shoulders like a coat cut for someone larger.

Thirty years tonight. She knew it without consulting any calendar, the way she knew rain coming in her knee and lies coming in her teeth. Thirty years since the last word of Vethmar, the Quiet God, the Unspeaking, the god whose oath-mark lay coiled around her left wrist like a sleeping serpent of pale scar-tissue. Thirty years since every sworn blade of his order—from the great temples down to hedge-priests and mercenary companies like the one she'd once marched with—had felt the covenant go out between one heartbeat and the next, like a lamp pinched by fingers that were never seen.

They called it the Silence now. Priests had written treatises on it. Kings had declared holidays of mourning and then quietly un-declared them when the mourning proved bad for trade. And the mercenaries who bore his mark—the ones who hadn't cut it out of themselves in despair or drunk themselves dead waiting for an answer—had gone on working, because a contract was a contract, and Vethmar had never required their faith to be loud. Only kept.

*Kept,* Zephyrine thought, and looked at her wrist, and felt nothing, which frightened her more than grief would have.

* * *

The trouble began, if you could call it trouble, with the widow's grief.

It had come in that morning in its little amber bottle, no bigger than a thumb, and the assessors had passed it twice over. Authentic grief always had a weight to it—a drag on the hand, a coldness through the glass, a faint pressure behind the eyes of anyone who held it too long. This one had all of that. Widow Anselm had wept beautifully during the assessment, the way people did when they finally set down something they'd carried too far, and the buyer's agent had paid in memories of Sundays, which was a common enough currency among those who had too many days and not enough reasons for them.

Zephyrine's job was only to enter it. Verify, record, shelve. The bottle went to Shelf Nine, Row Twelve, between a stolen lullaby and a man's regret at never learning his father's trade. The ledger received its lines. Everything balanced.

And yet.

She took the bottle down again after the clerks had gone—Corvane permitted her small liberties, or pretended not to see them—and held it in her palm above the lamplight. Inside, the grief moved. They always moved, the bottled ones, slow as smoke underwater, turning over on themselves. This one curled and uncurled like a fist clenching in sleep. Somewhere inside it was a funeral, probably. A house gone quiet. A coat still hanging by a door. Forty years of marriage distilled to an ounce of amber sorrow, priced fairly, traded honestly, entered in a fair hand.

Zephyrine waited for it to hurt.

That was the ritual she had never admitted to anyone, not even to herself in so many words: whenever she handled a grief, she let it touch her, just barely, just through the glass, the way you might warm your hands at someone else's fire without stealing from it. It was her private proof that she was still a person and not merely a machine for recording what people lost. Every bottled sorrow had ever reached her, even faintly, even secondhand—even the griefs of strangers whose names meant nothing—had found some small door in her and knocked.

This one knocked, and nothing answered.

She turned the bottle. The grief turned with it, patient, beautiful, terrible. The lamplight came through it gold as honey. And Zephyrine Yarrow, veteran of eleven campaigns, keeper of the Gildhall's deepest rows, sworn sword of a god thirty years silent, felt precisely nothing at all.

Not numbness—she tested for that, pressed her thumbnail hard against her palm until the pain sang sharp and clean. Not exhaustion, though she was tired in the way stone is tired of being a wall. It was worse than either. It was distance. As if the grief in the bottle and the grief in her chest had been weighed on the same scale and found to be the same weight, and therefore canceled each other out, and what remained was only arithmetic.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, this is what Corvane feels all the time.*

She put the bottle back on Shelf Nine, Row Twelve, exactly where it belonged, and her hand did not shake, and that was somehow the most frightening part.

* * *

The Gildhall of Impossible Things stood at the crook of the river where Saltcrown City forgot to be holy and remembered how to be rich instead. From the outside it looked like what it pretended to be: a countinghouse, a merchant concern, a place where unusual goods changed hands at fair prices. The sign above the door read simply GILDHALL, and below that, in smaller letters, ALL TRADES FINAL. Pilgrims sometimes came to the door by mistake, looking for the temple district, and were given directions with perfect courtesy and sent on their way.

What actually traded within its walls could not be posted on any sign. Forgotten names, sold back to men who had paid whole fortunes to lose them. Bottled griefs, bottled joys, bottled last words. Debts owed across realms—those were the dangerous ones, the ones that required the deep ledgers and Corvane's personal seal. A farmer's gratitude owed to a river spirit. A king's promise made to something under a mountain, three dynasties back, now come due. Once, before Zephyrine's time, a whole winter had been traded north by a city that couldn't afford it, and the ledger recording that sale was kept in a locked case and never opened, because opening it, the old clerks said, might make the terms due again.

Rules governed all of it. The Gildhall had many rules, but one law, and every clerk learned it their first day with the same lesson Corvane had given Zephyrine twenty years ago, standing in this very room with a candle in his hand:

"No memory may be traded freely given," he had said. "If a soul offers a memory freely—without price, without coercion, without trick—it cannot be bought, sold, recorded, or shelved. We refuse such trades absolutely. Do you understand why?"

She had been young and arrogant and fresh off the roads, and she had said something clever about sentiment corrupting markets.

Corvane had looked at her for a long moment with his gray, patient eyes. "Because freely given memory is how covenants are sealed," he said. "It is the oldest currency there is, older than coin, older than blood. A gift freely given binds. That is not our rule, Clerk Yarrow. That is the law underneath all laws. We do not deal in it for the same reason sensible merchants do not keep dragons in the cellar."

She had thought it an odd speech then. She thought of it often now, though she could not have said why—only that lately the words came back to her at strange hours, the way a tune comes back, half-remembered, from somewhere very far away.

* * *

Near midnight, Corvane appeared in the doorway of the counting room.

He was not a tall man, Gildmaster Corvane, but he filled doors the way cold fills a cellar. He wore gray, always gray, and his hair was the same gray, and his eyes were the gray of a ledger page before anything is written on it. He carried no lamp; he never seemed to need one. Some said he had traded for that. Others said he had simply never had a shadow to lose.

"The Anselm entry," he said.

"Balanced," Zephyrine said. "Verified twice. The grief is genuine, the payment cleared, the shelf is correct."

"I didn't ask about the entry." He came into the room, and the lamps leaned toward him slightly, the way candles lean toward a draft, or perhaps toward authority. "I asked about the grief. You took it down after hours. You do that. I have watched you do it for years." He paused. "You're allowed. Assessors need practice holding weight. But you've been holding them longer each month, and returning them later each night, and tonight—" his gaze flicked to Shelf Nine, Row Twelve, then back to her, "—tonight you held it for the length of a bell and put it back untouched."

"You watch me closely, Gildmaster."

"It is my hall. Watching is the whole of my trade." He folded his hands. "Tell me what you felt."

And here was the moment where a wiser woman lied. Zephyrine had survived eleven campaigns and twenty years of Corvane precisely because she knew when to lie. But the silence in her chest was thirty years wide tonight, and it ached like a missing tooth, and she heard herself say, honestly:

"Nothing. I felt nothing. It was a true grief, master—one of the truest I've handled. Forty years of love in an ounce of glass. And I held it and it was like holding a stone. Like reading about rain in a book." She looked down at her own steady, traitorous hand. "I've audited four hundred griefs. I used to feel every one. Something has gone wrong with me."

Corvane was quiet for a while. The lamps burned. Somewhere above them, in the shelves, the Gildhall's inventory breathed its slow, impossible breath—the sound, if you listened long enough, of ten thousand traded things dreaming of their former owners.

"No," Corvane said at last, and there was something in his voice she had never heard before, and it took her a moment to recognize it as care, worn thin as old cloth. "Something has not gone wrong with you, Clerk Yarrow. Something has gone quiet *around* you. There is a difference, though I grant it feels the same from inside." He turned toward the door, then stopped. "Thirty years tonight, isn't it. Since the Silence."

She said nothing. Her wrist, beneath its cuff, throbbed once, faintly, like a bruise remembering itself.

"When I was a young clerk," Corvane said, not turning around, "the old Gildmaster told me that the Silence was not a death. Gods do not die the way men die, he said. They go where their covenant carries them, and if the covenant breaks, they go where broken things go." His voice flattened. "I asked him what a broken covenant could be mended with, and he looked at me as if I'd asked him to unmake the world, and he never answered. I have run this hall for forty years on the principle that the question was unanswerable." Now he did turn, and his gray eyes rested on her with an expression she could not read at all. "Lately I find myself wondering whether I was wrong to stop asking."

"Gildmaster—"

"Go home, Zephyrine. Balance comes tomorrow. Tonight, even ledgers should sleep."

He left. The lamps straightened slowly, like soldiers standing down.

* * *

She did not go home. Home was a room above a chandler's shop with a bed she slept in and did not rest in, and tonight the Gildhall's shelves felt closer to her than any hearth. So she walked the long aisles instead, past the bottled griefs and the forgotten names, past the case of cross-realm debts with its three locks, her boots silent on floors that had absorbed a century of hushed negotiations. The Gildhall at night was a different country. The inventory murmured. If you pressed your ear to certain shelves—she had done it exactly once, years ago—you could hear the traded things being themselves: a lullaby humming, a regret sighing, a name repeating itself over and over like a man calling for someone who no longer answered.

Vethmar, she thought. Are you in a place like this? Shelved somewhere? Traded? Or are you in the dark where broken things go, and does the dark know your name?

Her oath-mark gave no answer. It had given no answer for thirty years. It was only a scar now, a serpent-shaped paleness on her wrist that the priests of other gods called a relic and the faithless called a brand and she herself, on the worst nights, called a joke told at her expense by a universe that had better material available.

She stopped at the window at the end of Row One, the tall one that looked out over the river road, because it was the last thing she always checked—not because it needed checking, but because the view was the closest thing the Gildhall had to a prayer. Below, Saltcrown sprawled black and silver under a thin moon, its thousand chimneys breathing smoke into the dark, its temples dark, its gildhalls lit. The river ran seaward, carrying salt up from the flats and secrets down to the sea, impartial as a ledger.

Thirty years, she thought. I have kept every term of my oath. I have spoken no false contract, broken no sworn word, abandoned no post. I have been faithful to a god who does not answer, and I am so tired, and tonight I held a widow's grief in my hand and felt nothing at all, and I do not know anymore whether I am a priestess or just a very good clerk.

The moon slid behind cloud. The river road lay empty.

Except—

She leaned closer. At the far bend of the road, where the lantern on the corner post threw its weak circle, something small was moving. Not walking—staggering. Small and slight and mud-colored, a shape that resolved as it came nearer into something that made her breath catch: a child, a girl, barefoot in the mud of a autumn night, filthy to the knees, moving with the blind determination of someone who had walked much farther than a child should.

Zephyrine told herself it was none of the Gildhall's business. Foundlings were the city's problem; the Crown had orphanages, of a sort. She told herself to turn away, finish the night, go home to her narrow bed.

Then the child stopped directly beneath the window, in the circle of the corner lantern, and lifted her head, and looked up—at the Gildhall, at the dark glass, at Zephyrine—with eyes that caught the light wrong, too old, too steady, and spoke aloud into the empty street in a language Zephyrine had never heard and yet understood in her bones the way one understands thunder.

Around her wrist, for the first time in thirty years, the oath-mark of Vethmar began to burn.
